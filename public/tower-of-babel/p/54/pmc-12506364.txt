Association of lipid accumulation product with the prevalence and incidence of sarcopenia: a nationwide study in Chinese
Authors: Zhiyuan He, Jiangping Zeng, Wenquan Ding, Rui Xie, Ya Qian, Shenghao Wang, Wu Xu, Lixin Huang, Liyu Zhou, Dongqing You, Zijie Pei, Qian Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLipid accumulation product (LAP) has recently gained attention as a novel indicator of metabolic dysfunction. However, the association between LAP and sarcopenia, a metabolic condition characterized by loss of muscle mass, strength, and function, remains unclear. This study aimed to explore the relationship between LAP and both the prevalence and incidence of sarcopenia using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS).MethodsSarcopenia was defined according to the criteria established by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia in 2019. LAP was calculated using waist circumference and triglyceride levels. A cross-sectional analysis was performed with 7,004 participants from the baseline survey, utilizing logistic regression models to evaluate the association between LAP and sarcopenia prevalence. Additionally, a longitudinal cohort analysis involved 4,484 individuals who were free of sarcopenia at baseline and followed from 2011 to 2015. Cox proportional hazards models were employed to assess the longitudinal association between baseline LAP levels and incident sarcopenia. Furthermore, restricted cubic spline regression (RCS) and subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential nonlinear relationships and differences across various subgroups. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves was used to evaluate the discriminatory ability of LAP for identifying sarcopenia.ResultsCross-sectional analyses and RCS revealed a significant inverse linear relationship between LAP and the prevalence of sarcopenia [odds ratio (OR) = 0.95, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.94–0.96]. Participants within the highest LAP tertile demonstrated substantially lower odds of sarcopenia compared to those in the lowest tertile (OR = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.14–0.31). Longitudinal analyses similarly indicated that elevated LAP levels were associated with reduced sarcopenia incidence, with the highest LAP tertile associated with notably decreased risk (HR = 0.17, 95% CI: 0.11–0.27). The nonlinear pattern identified through RCS analysis indicated significant risk reductions up to a LAP threshold of 27.577. Furthermore, subgroup analyses consistently supported this inverse association across various demographic and clinical subgroups. Finally, diagnostic performance of LAP was assessed using the ROC curve (0.763 ([CI]: 0.744–0.783) in the longitudinal study).ConclusionsElevated LAP levels are inversely associated with both the prevalence and incidence of sarcopenia among middle-aged and elderly adults in China. These findings suggest LAP could serve as a useful metabolic indicator for predicting reduced sarcopenia risk, warranting additional studies to confirm and further elucidate these relationships.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-025-01016-w.

Introduction

Sarcopenia is a multifactorial degenerative condition characterized by a progressive decline in skeletal muscle mass, strength, and physical function [1]. Various factors, including physical inactivity, inadequate protein intake, hormonal changes, and chronic catabolic diseases, contribute to the onset and progression of sarcopenia to varying degrees [2]. In recent years, increasing attention has been paid to the role of obesity in the development of sarcopenia. On one hand, obesity-related mechanisms-such as chronic systemic inflammation-may accelerate muscle degradation and promote sarcopenia. On the other hand, the excess body weight associated with obesity may increase absolute muscle mass, potentially exerting a protective effect against sarcopenia [3–5]. Despite these opposing effects, the clinical relationship between obesity and sarcopenia remains complex and not fully understood. In this context, identifying more precise obesity-related indicators may help clarify their role in sarcopenia.

Lipid Accumulation Product (LAP) is a novel anthropometric-metabolic index developed to assess obesity more precisely. It combines waist circumference (WC)-a surrogate marker for visceral adiposity-and fasting triglyceride (TG) levels, both of which are key indicators of metabolic dysfunction [6, 7]. Compared to traditional obesity measures, LAP better reflects abdominal fat distribution and lipid metabolism abnormalities [8]. Previous studies have demonstrated strong associations between elevated LAP levels and increased risks of metabolic disorders, such as metabolic syndrome and type 2 diabetes [9, 10]. Wang et al. [11] reported that every 10-unit increase in LAP was associated with a 22% increase in the risk of diabetes and prediabetes, while Li et al. [12] demonstrated that LAP exhibits high diagnostic sensitivity for metabolic dysfunction–associated fatty liver disease, yielding area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.812 and 0.809 in the training and validation cohorts, respectively. Notably, these metabolic abnormalities are also closely linked to sarcopenia [13]. It is worth noting that distinct nutritional patterns in China, historically characterized by lower obesity prevalence and notable malnutrition, have led to unique obesity and sarcopenia profiles in its aging population compared with Western cohorts [14, 15]. This distinct nutritional context may significantly impact the interplay between lipid accumulation and the development of sarcopenia. However, the potential relationship between LAP and sarcopenia within Chinese populations remains underexplored, warranting further detailed investigation.

In this study, we hypothesized that moderate elevations in LAP could potentially serve as a protective factor against sarcopenia by supporting metabolic processes essential for muscle maintenance and conducted cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses utilizing data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative cohort study, focusing on the relationship between LAP and sarcopenia among middle-aged and older adults in China.

Methods

Study population

This study adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines and complies with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The CHARLS is a nationally representative longitudinal survey focusing on individuals aged 45 and older in China. It captures a diverse population across urban and rural areas, reflecting differences in demographic characteristics, health status, functional abilities, socioeconomic variables, and retirement-related information [16]. In recent years, studies on the etiology, diagnosis and treatment of sarcopenia in middle-aged and elderly patients in China using the CHARLS database have been gradually reported, which has attracted the attention of scholars [17–19]. As depicted in Fig. 1, participants included in the cross-sectional analysis were drawn from the baseline survey (Wave 1) of CHARLS in 2011, with an initial total of 17,705 individuals. A total of 8,447 participants were excluded at the first stage: 4,779 due to unavailable data on sarcopenia status, including unavailable handgrip strength data (n = 4,117), weight data (n = 119), height data (n = 93), age data (n = 64), and physical performance information (n = 383); and 3,996 due to unavailable data on LAP, including unavailable TG data (n = 3,668) and WC < 65 cm for men or < 58 cm for women or unavailable WC data (n = 328). Then, 2,227 participants with unavailable cystatin C data were further excluded, resulting in a final cross-sectional analysis sample size of 7,004 participants. For the longitudinal analysis, an additional 849 participants with sarcopenia at baseline (2011) and 1,670 participants with unavailable sarcopenia data in follow-up waves (CHARLS 2013 [Wave 2] and 2015 [Wave 3]) were excluded. Therefore, the final analytical cohort for longitudinal analysis comprised 4,484 individuals without sarcopenia at baseline, successfully followed through 2015.

Assessment of sarcopenia

This study assessed sarcopenia status according to the 2019 Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) consensus [20], involving the evaluation of appendicular skeletal muscle (ASM) mass, muscle strength, and physical performance. Participants with low muscle mass plus low muscle strength or low physical performance was diagnosed sarcopenia, which is consistent with the diagnostic criteria used in previous studies on sarcopenia utilizing the CHARLS database [21, 22]. ASM was calculated using a previously validated formula tailored to the Chinese population, which showed strong agreement with dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) assessments [23, 24]. The detailed ASM estimation formula is as follows:

ASM = 0.193 × weight (kg) + 0.107 × height (cm) - 4.157 × gender (1 represented for men and 2 represented for women) − 0.037 × age (years) − 2.631.

Low muscle mass was defined using the lowest 20% of height-adjusted muscle mass (ASM/height2) specific to each sex within the study population (ASM/height2 < 7.00 kg/m² for men and < 5.28 kg/m² for women) [25]. Low muscle strength was defined as grip strength < 28 kg for men and < 18 kg for women [16]. Poor physical performance was classified by a gait speed < 1.0 m/s, a five-time chair stand test duration ≥ 12 s, or a Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) score < 9 [20]. In the CHARLS dataset, data on the 10-second side-by-side stance, a component of the SPPB balance test, was nearly completely missing. Therefore, the standing balance score was derived from the other two tasks: semi-tandem and tandem stance. Participants were considered to have poor performance if they met the threshold on any one of the three physical performance tests. If data were available for only one or two tests, classification was based on available data; participants were excluded only if all three tests were missing or inconclusive.

LAP index calculation

Fasting venous blood samples were collected by qualified medical staff at the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention according to standard protocols and analyzed in a central laboratory. TG and fasting blood glucose levels were measured using an enzymatic colorimetric method. WC was measured at the midpoint between the lower costal margin and the iliac crest using a non-retractable tape measure while participants stood upright and breathed normally. The LAP index was calculated as follows [26]:

LAP = [WC (cm) − 65] × [TG (mmol/L)] for men and LAP = [WC (cm) − 58] × [TG (mmol/L)] for women.

Assessment of covariates

Consistent with previous studies [27–31], covariates assessed included sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle behaviors, current health status, and laboratory indicators. Sociodemographic variables comprised age, sex, residential location (urban or rural), educational attainment (illiterate, primary school, middle school, college, or higher), and marital status (married, separated/divorced, unmarried/widowed). Lifestyle behaviors assessed were smoking status (smoker, or non-smoker) and alcohol consumption frequency (frequent, occasional, or none). Health conditions were identified through self-reported medical diagnoses or ongoing treatment and included cancer, arthritis, dyslipidemia, hepatic disease, kidney disease, hypertension, and hyperglycemia. Body mass index (BMI) was classified according to Chinese adult standards into four categories: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–24 kg/m²), overweight (24–28 kg/m²), and obese (≥ 28 kg/m²) [32]. Laboratory diagnostic indicators measured from participants’ blood samples included white blood cell (WBC) count, platelet count, glucose, C-reactive protein (CRP), creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), uric acid and total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol and cystatin C.

For covariates, mean/median imputation was applied if missingness was < 5% of the total sample, following normality assessment via the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. For missing covariates exceeding 5%, participants were excluded to mitigate imputation-related bias.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.3), with statistical significance defined as a P-value less than 0.05. Consistent with the previous studies [28–30, 33, 34], the normality of continuous variables was assessed, with missing values imputed by means or medians accordingly. Missing categorical variables were grouped separately. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation if normally distributed, or median and interquartile range if skewed. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentage (%). The baseline characteristics of participants in both the cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses were compared across LAP tertiles using appropriate statistical tests, such as chi-square tests and analysis of variance. To assess potential multicollinearity among covariates included in the regression models, we calculated the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) for each variable. A VIF value < 10 was considered indicative of negligible multicollinearity, in Line with conventional statistical standards. As shown in Supplementary Table 1, all covariates included in the final multivariable models met this criterion, suggesting that multicollinearity was not a significant concern. Logistic regression was employed in the cross-sectional analysis to examine the association between LAP and sarcopenia prevalence, while Cox regression was used in the longitudinal analysis to investigate the relationship between LAP and sarcopenia incidence. The nonlinear relationship between LAP and sarcopenia was explored using the restricted cubic spline (RCS) curve and potential inflection points were found. ​Four knots​ at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the LAP distribution were selected. Subgroup analysis further explored the differences in the association between LAP and sarcopenia in different populations. To evaluate the discriminatory ability of LAP for identifying sarcopenia, we constructed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculated the AUC.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study

In the cross-sectional analysis of 7,004 participants, the median age was 59 (53–68) years, and the prevalence of sarcopenia was 12.12% (n = 849). As shown in Fig. 2A, participants with sarcopenia exhibited significantly lower LAP values (19.31 ± 16.81) than those without sarcopenia (41.81 ± 43.66). Further stratification of participants by LAP tertiles revealed that the prevalence of sarcopenia decreased progressively across groups, with rates of 22.79% (n = 532) in the lowest tertile, 9.90% (n = 231) in the middle tertile, and 3.68% (n = 86) in the highest tertile (Fig. 2B).

The baseline results, further divided into groups with or without new-onset sarcopenia, were shown in Supplementary Tables 2 and Fig. 3A. The LAP values of participants with new-onset sarcopenia were significantly lower than those without new-onset sarcopenia (P < 0.001). According to Tables 1, 4 and 484 participants were included in the longitudinal analysis and stratified according to LAP tertiles. The media age of the study cohort was 58 (52–65) years, with 53.72% of participants being female. Significant differences were observed across LAP tertiles regarding demographic and clinical characteristics. Higher LAP tertiles were associated with increasing proportions of females, rural residence, illiteracy, and higher BMI categories (overweight and obesity), whereas smoking and regular alcohol consumption rates decreased with increasing LAP tertiles (all P < 0.001). Participants in higher LAP tertiles exhibited significantly higher prevalence rates of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes (all P < 0.001), but no significant differences were found in the prevalence of cancer, liver disease, or kidney disease across tertiles. As shown in Fig. 3B, the prevalence of sarcopenia decreased significantly with increasing LAP tertiles: 31.97% in the low tertile, 11.58% in the middle tertile, and 4.68% in the high tertile (P < 0.001). For laboratory indicators, glucose, TC, LDL, uric acid, platelet counts, and WBC counts all increased significantly with higher LAP tertiles, while HDL and cystatin C levels decreased significantly (all P < 0.001). BUN and serum creatinine levels showed minor but statistically significant decreases across tertiles (P < 0.001), whereas CRP levels did not differ significantly among groups (P = 0.479).

Cross-sectional associations between LAP and the prevalence of sarcopenia

Table 2 summarized the association between LAP and the prevalence of sarcopenia. Consistent findings across all three models indicated an inverse relationship between continuous LAP values and sarcopenia risk. Specifically, in the fully adjusted model (Model 3), each one-unit increase in LAP corresponded to a 5.0% reduction in the odds of sarcopenia [odds ratio (OR) = 0.95, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.94–0.96]. Furthermore, participants were categorized into LAP tertiles to further investigate this inverse association. Results from all models consistently demonstrated that higher LAP tertiles were significantly associated with a decreased risk of sarcopenia (P for trend < 0.001). Notably, participants in the highest LAP tertile (Tertile 3) had only 21.0% of the odds of sarcopenia compared to those in the lowest tertile (Tertile 1), even after adjusting for all covariates.

Longitudinal association between LAP and incidence of sarcopenia

Table 3 presented the longitudinal associations between LAP and the incidence of sarcopenia. Over the 4-year follow-up period, a total of 721 cases of sarcopenia incidents were identified. As a continuous variable, each unit increase in LAP was associated with a 5.0% reduction in the risk of developing sarcopenia, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 0.95 (95% CI: 0.95–0.96, P < 0.001) in Model 1, 0.94 (95% CI: 0.94–0.95, P < 0.001) in Model 2, and 0.95 (95% CI: 0.95–0.96, P < 0.001) in Model 3. When LAP was categorized into tertiles (Tertile 1, Tertile 2, and Tertile 3), individuals in the higher LAP groups consistently exhibited a lower risk of sarcopenia. In Model 1, those in the second tertile (Tertile 2) had a 72% reduced risk of sarcopenia compared to the reference group (Tertile 1) [HR = 0.28 (95% CI: 0.23–0.34), P < 0.001]. Similarly, the third tertile (Tertile 3) was associated with a 90% reduced risk of sarcopenia [HR = 0.10 (95% CI: 0.08–0.14), P < 0.001]. After further adjustments for age, gender and residence (Model 2), the associations were even stronger, with Tertile 2 showing an 80% reduction in risk [HR = 0.20 (95% CI: 0.16–0.25), P < 0.001] and Tertile 3 showing a 94% reduction [HR = 0.06 (95% CI: 0.06–0.08), P < 0.001]. In the fully adjusted Model 3, which controlled for a wide range of covariates, the inverse association between higher LAP and sarcopenia remained significant, with individuals in Tertile 2 having 63% lower risk [HR = 0.37 (95% CI: 0.29–0.48), P < 0.001], and those in Tertile 3 having 83% lower risk [HR = 0.17 (95% CI: 0.11–0.27), P < 0.001].

Figure 7 illustrated the nonlinear relationship between LAP and sarcopenia using an RCS curve. The nonlinear association was observed (P - nonlinear < 0.001), with an identified inflection point at a LAP value of 27.577. According to the threshold effect analysis in Table 4, below 27.577, LAP showed a significant negative correlation with sarcopenia, with the HR of 0.94. Above the threshold of 27.577, the negative correlation between LAP and sarcopenia tends to be flat, with the HR of 0.98. Discussion

In this national study of Chinese adults, cross-sectional analyses demonstrated that higher LAP levels were associated with a lower prevalence of sarcopenia. A four-year follow-up longitudinal analysis confirmed that elevated LAP was causally associated with decreased risk of sarcopenia after adjusting for demographic factors, lifestyle behaviors, comorbidities, and laboratory biomarkers. Moreover, our analysis suggested a non-linear relationship between them, identifying LAP = 27.577 as the inflection point beyond which the protective effect reached a plateau.

Recent advancements in sarcopenia clinical research have focused on the risk factors and the effectiveness of biomarkers in diagnosis of sarcopenia. Xu et al. [35] found that, in nonelderly populations, sarcopenia is positively correlated with prediabetes, particularly with impaired glucose tolerance. Tao et al. [36], confirmed, in a study of 6,677 subjects, that obstructive sleep apnea was an independent risk factor for sarcopenia, possibly mediated by unhealthy dietary habits, elevated BMI, chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and vitamin D deficiency. In our previous study, we have developed diagnostic models leveraging ALT activity and the ALT1/ALT2 ratio, presenting the AUC of 0.789 and 0.830 separately [37]. Other research also indicated AUC values of 0.726 for the neurofilament Light chain and 0.70 for fibrin degradation products [38, 39]. In this study, we found that after adjusting for demographic factors, lifestyle behaviors, comorbidities, and laboratory biomarkers, the AUC values ​​of LAP for predicting the prevalence and incidence of sarcopenia also reached 0.730 and 0.763. This provides effective support for a comprehensive understanding of the risk factors of sarcopenia, as well as the relationship between sarcopenia and obesity.

Moreover, our analysis also suggested a negative non-linear relationship between LAP and incidence of sarcopenia. Recent longitudinal studies have uncovered what is known as the “obesity paradox”- a phenomenon in which moderate obesity is statistically linked to reduced all-cause mortality in certain elderly populations [40]. Additional investigations have shown that obesity is associated with improved five-year overall survival following immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy [41], and Liu et al. [42] observed a linear inverse relationship between LAP and both all-cause and cardiac mortality in patients with osteoporosis. These findings collectively support our observation that higher LAP levels are paradoxically associated with a lower prevalence of sarcopenia. This counterintuitive phenomenon not only challenges traditional conceptions regarding the risks posed by visceral adiposity but also provides a novel framework for exploring the role of lipid metabolism in the pathogenesis of sarcopenia.

Recent studies can offer potential mechanisms. Adipose tissue is an essential energy reservoir, providing free fatty acids and triglycerides to support muscle function, particularly in aging individuals who may experience progressive energy deficits [43]. Additionally, adipose tissue secretes bioactive molecules such as leptin and adiponectin, which regulate muscle metabolism, mitochondrial function, and protein synthesis. Leptin, for instance, has been shown to enhance muscle anabolism by activating the AMP-activated protein kinase and mammalian target of rapamycin pathways [44, 45]. Furthermore, although chronic systemic inflammation is widely recognized as a key mediator of muscle degradation, physiologically regulated levels of adipose-derived cytokines may exert context-dependent effects. For example, interleukin-6 is implicated in muscle catabolism under pathological conditions, yet under normal physiological circumstances, it may facilitate muscle repair and hypertrophy [46, 47]. Lastly, mild adiposity has been linked to improved insulin sensitivity, crucial for muscle protein synthesis and glucose uptake, whereas extremely low adipose tissue levels may lead to metabolic stress, insulin resistance, and muscle wasting [48, 49]. It’s worth noting that we identify LAP = 27.577 as the inflection point beyond which the protective effect reached a plateau. This may be related to the fact that fat toxicity caused by excessive fat accumulation offsets some of the protective effects. Indeed, excessive fat accumulation can promote muscle atrophy through increased inflammatory responses or a variety of other pathways [50].

In the cross-sectional analysis, although the negative association between LAP and the prevalence of sarcopenia remained consistent across most subgroups, this relationship appeared more pronounced among rural residents and widowed/unmarried individuals. Environmental and social factors may modify the impact of LAP on muscle health. Compared with urban residents, rural populations often have more limited access to healthcare resources and preventive services, as well as lower awareness of sarcopenia, which may make them more vulnerable [51]. Similarly, widowed or unmarried individuals may receive less psychosocial support, have poorer nutritional intake, and engage less in daily health-related behaviors, all of which could further exacerbate the detrimental effects of LAP on skeletal muscle metabolism [52]. In the longitudinal analysis, the protective effect of higher LAP on the incidence of sarcopenia was stronger in several subgroups, including younger adults (< 60 years), males, obese individuals, divorced participants, and those with smoking or drinking behaviors. Younger adults and men may exhibit more dynamic metabolic characteristics and greater muscular responsiveness to lipid storage status, making the association between LAP and sarcopenia more evident [53]. In obese individuals, excess energy reserves may delay muscle catabolism but could also interact with LAP-related metabolic pathways, resulting in stronger effects [54]. Divorced participants may experience chronic stress and lifestyle instability, which increase the sensitivity of muscle health to metabolic disturbances [55]. Likewise, smoking and alcohol consumption can induce systemic oxidative stress and inflammation, potentially aggravating muscle catabolism [56, 57]; therefore, LAP may act as a more sensitive marker of the balance between lipid metabolism and muscle preservation in these subgroups. These subgroup differences highlight that LAP interacts not only with intrinsic biological pathways but also with lifestyle, environmental, and psychosocial factors. Understanding these multifactorial interactions may help develop personalized prevention and management strategies for sarcopenia in vulnerable populations.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it is based on a large, nationally representative cohort with a robust study design that integrates cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses. Second, using LAP, a validated marker of lipid accumulation provides a more nuanced perspective on the relationship between adiposity and muscle health compared to traditional anthropometric measures.

This study has several limitations. First, despite rigorous adjustments for a wide range of potential confounders, residual confounding by unmeasured or unknown factors cannot be entirely excluded. Second, our findings are based exclusively on a Chinese population cohort, which may limit generalizability to other ethnic or regional populations; validation in diverse cohorts is warranted. Third, the four-year follow-up period may be insufficient to fully capture the long-term trajectory and progression of sarcopenia. Fourth, in assessing physical performance, we did not include all three balance components of the Short Physical Performance Battery, potentially leading to misclassification bias regarding sarcopenia status. Fifth, there are a large number of missing data for cystatin C in the CHARLS dataset, which may affect the robustness and precision of our estimates. Although we employed appropriate imputation strategies for variables with minimal missingness and excluded participants with extensive missing data to mitigate bias, the possibility of residual bias remains.

Clinical and public health implications

Our study suggests that moderate lipid accumulation, as reflected by LAP, may be beneficial in reducing sarcopenia risk among middle-aged and older adults. These findings challenge the notion that adiposity is uniformly harmful and highlight the need for a more individualized approach to sarcopenia prevention. From a clinical perspective, LAP could serve as a practical screening tool to identify individuals at risk of muscle loss. Public health strategies aimed at optimizing adiposity levels rather than indiscriminately reducing body fat may be more effective in maintaining muscle health in aging populations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates a significant inverse association between LAP and sarcopenia risk in middle-aged and older Chinese adults. These findings provide novel insights into the complex role of adiposity in aging and underscore the potential utility of LAP as a marker for sarcopenia risk assessment in the Chinese population.
