Adherence to the planetary health diet index and risk of glycemic progression or regression in prediabetic individuals: a longitudinal study
Authors: Farzam Kamrani, Elahe Etesami, Matin Ghanavati, Fatemeh Zare Nejad, Nazanin Zahra Nourian, Jalaledin Mirzay Razzaz, Reza Homayounfar, Ali Nikparast
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhether adherence to the Planetary Health Diet Index (PHDI)—a dietary pattern jointly promoting human and environmental health—influences glycemic trajectories among individuals with prediabetes remains unknown. This study examined associations between PHDI adherence and odds of regression to normoglycemia and progression to type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) in prediabetic adults.MethodsWe conducted a prospective cohort analysis among 4,143 prediabetic adults (age range 19–70 years, 46.2% men, mean age 43.3 ± 10.1 years) enrolled in the Tehran MMRT study between 2017 and 2018 and followed for 5 years. PHDI scores were derived from validated food-frequency questionnaires. Multinomial logistic regression estimated odds ratios (ORs) for glycemic regression and progression across PHDI quartiles, adjusting for demographic, lifestyle, metabolic, and dietary covariates. Component‑level and subgroup analyses, as well as restricted cubic spline modeling, were conducted.ResultsOver follow‑up, 1,288 participants regressed to normoglycemia; 775 progressed to T2DM. In fully adjusted multinomial logistic regression models, the highest versus lowest PHDI quartile exhibited 28% greater odds of regression (OR 1.28; 95% CI: 1.04–1.58; P‑trend = 0.003). Each standard‑deviation increase in PHDI conferred a 10% higher likelihood of regression (OR 1.10; 95% CI: 1.02–1.18). This association was significantly stronger among men than women (P‑interaction < 0.001). Higher intakes of legumes, vegetables, dairy, vegetable oils, and poultry were independently associated with regression, whereas red meat intake was inversely associated. This relationship was attenuated after HOMA‑IR adjustment, suggesting partial mediation by insulin sensitivity. No independent association was observed with diabetes progression after full adjustment (OR 1.04; 95% CI: 0.82–1.31; P‑trend = 0.840).ConclusionsGreater adherence to the PHDI was prospectively associated with higher odds of returning to normoglycemia, but not with reduced diabetes progression, in prediabetic adults. Insulin sensitivity may partly mediate this benefit. These findings underscore the importance of considering glycemic directionality when evaluating sustainable dietary interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12986-026-01176-3.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) represents an escalating public health challenge in Iran, where the prevalence of prediabetes increased from approximately 23% to 30% between 2016 and 2021 [1]. Prediabetes—defined by impaired fasting glucose (IFG), impaired glucose tolerance (IGT), or elevated glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) [2]—is a reversible intermediate state; regression to normoglycemia occurs in 33–59% of affected individuals and confers lasting protection against future T2DM [3] and confers lasting protection against future T2DM [4]. Consequently, identifying modifiable determinants of glycemic recovery is a public health priority.

Dietary patterns emphasizing whole grains, fruits, vegetables, legumes, nuts, fish, and limited red and processed meats are associated with lower T2DM risk [5, 6] and, among individuals with prediabetes, with a higher probability of returning to normoglycemia [7]. In 2019, the EAT–Lancet Commission proposed the Planetary Health Diet—a plant‑forward reference diet designed to promote both human health and environmental sustainability [8]. A recent meta‑analysis demonstrated that higher PHD adherence was associated with a 26% lower risk of incident diabetes [9], and two systematic reviews confirmed consistent inverse associations between PHD adherence and T2DM incidence [10, 11]. Individual prospective studies have reported similar protective associations in Danish [12], Swedish [13], Mexican [14], and British populations [15, 16].

However, all prior studies have been conducted in general populations free of diabetes at baseline; none have examined the role of PHD adherence specifically among individuals with established prediabetes. Moreover, previous investigations have focused solely on progression to T2DM, overlooking the potential for dietary factors to actively facilitate regression to normoglycemia—a distinct endpoint reflecting metabolic recovery rather than merely the absence of deterioration. The aim of the present study was, therefore, to prospectively examine the association between adherence to the Planetary Health Diet Index and bidirectional glycemic trajectories—regression to normoglycemia and progression to T2DM—in a cohort of prediabetic adults. We further evaluated individual PHDI components, potential non‑linearity, and effect modification by key population characteristics.

Methods and materials

Study design and population

This prospective cohort analysis was nested within the Tehran Monitoring of Metabolic Diseases Risk Factors (MMRT) study—an ongoing, population‑based longitudinal initiative designed to identify determinants of non‑communicable disease among adults aged 20–70 years residing in Tehran, Iran [17, 18]. Participant recruitment was conducted through convenience sampling at a major urban referral health center. Between January 2017 and December 2018, 8,760 eligible individuals were approached, of whom 7,836 provided written informed consent and completed baseline assessments (89.4% response rate). Each enrollee received a unique de‑identified code. Baseline evaluations included structured interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory investigations covering sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle, medical history, dietary intake, and anthropometric indices. Participants were invited to a follow‑up assessment approximately five years later (January 2022–December 2023), during which identical measurement protocols were re‑applied; 7,600 individuals were successfully reassessed (95% retention).

For the present analysis, we sequentially excluded participants diagnosed at baseline with cancer (n = 16), chronic kidney disease (n = 87), gallstone disease (n = 98), or rheumatoid arthritis (n = 206), as well as those reporting biologically implausible total energy intakes (defined as ± 2 standard deviations from the sex‑specific mean, i.e., < 1,534 or > 2,970 kcal/day; n = 86). After these exclusions, the analytic sample comprised 4,143 adults with baseline prediabetes and complete longitudinal data. The participant selection cascade is depicted in Fig. 1.

Ethical considerations

The protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (Ethics Code: IR.SBMU.NNFTRI.REC.1404.003). All procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki; written informed consent was obtained from every participant.

Assessment of non-dietary covariates

Demographic information, socioeconomic status (SES), and medical history were elicited using a standardized questionnaire. SES was operationalized via a Wealth Score Index (WSI) derived through multiple correspondence analysis of household asset holdings and service access [19]. Habitual physical activity was assessed using the validated Modifiable Activity Questionnaire, with energy expenditure expressed as metabolic equivalent task hours per week (MET‑h/week) [20]. Sleep duration was self‑reported and categorized as short (< 7 h), normal (7–9 h), or long (> 9 h) [21].

Anthropometric and body composition assessments were performed by certified health professionals adhering to strict, standardized protocols. Body weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using a calibrated digital scale with participants attired in light clothing and footwear removed. Standing height was recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm using a wall‑mounted stadiometer. Body mass index (BMI) was computed as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters. Waist circumference was determined at the midpoint between the lowest palpable rib margin and the iliac crest using a non‑extensible anthropometric tape. Total body fat mass was quantified by dual‑energy X‑ray absorptiometry (DXA) (Lunar iDXA, GE Healthcare, Wisconsin, USA). The fat mass index (FMI) was subsequently calculated as total fat mass (kg) divided by height squared (m²), yielding a height‑normalized adiposity metric [22].

Venous blood specimens were obtained following an 8–12 h overnight fast. Fasting blood glucose (FBG), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), and high‑density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‑C) concentrations were assayed via enzymatic colorimetric methods on an automated clinical chemistry analyzer (Selectra E, Vitalab, Holliston, The Netherlands) using commercially available reagent kits (Pars Azmoon, Iran). Low‑density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‑C) was derived using the Friedewald equation. Fasting serum insulin was quantified by electrochemiluminescence immunoassay, and insulin resistance was estimated using the Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA‑IR), defined as [fasting insulin (µU/mL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL)] / 405. Hepatic enzyme activities—alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and gamma‑glutamyl transferase (GGT)—were measured employing standard kinetic enzymatic techniques.

Assessment of adherence to the EAT–lancet diet (planetary health diet index)

Dietary intake over the preceding year was evaluated using a validated 125‑item Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) adapted from the Willett framework and tailored to capture Iranian dietary patterns [23]. For each food item, participants reported their habitual consumption frequency over the preceding year, selecting from daily, weekly, monthly, or annual response options. Portion sizes were standardized according to the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) food database, with household measures converted to gram equivalents where applicable. Nutritional composition values were primarily sourced from the USDA Food Composition Table, supplemented by the Iranian Food Composition Table for region‑specific items. Adherence to the EAT–Lancet reference diet was operationalized using the Planetary Health Diet Index (PHDI), originally developed and validated by Cacau et al. [24]. This index is anchored to the reference diet proposed by the EAT–Lancet Commission, which specifies recommended intake ranges for major food groups within a 2,500 kcal/day dietary framework. To facilitate comparability across individuals with differing total energy intakes, all food group recommendations were expressed using an energy‑density approach, defined as the proportional caloric contribution of each food group to total energy intake. Individual food items from the FFQ were aggregated into predefined food groups aligned with the EAT–Lancet classification; the specific FFQ items assigned to each PHDI component are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The PHDI comprises 16 components distributed across four scoring domains: adequacy, optimum, ratio, and moderation, each scored proportionally against recommended energy‑adjusted intake ranges. Total scores range from 0 to 150, with higher scores indicating greater alignment with the planetary health diet. Full details of FFQ items assigned to each component and scoring cut‑offs are provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2.

Ascertainment of glycemic outcomes

Both baseline and follow‑up glycemic status were classified according to the American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria [25], using FBG, 2‑hour plasma glucose (2‑h PG) following a 75‑g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), and HbA1c. Participants were considered to have prediabetes at baseline if they had IFG (FBG 100–125 mg/dL), IGT (2‑h PG 140–199 mg/dL), or elevated HbA1c (5.7–6.4%), provided no value exceeded the diagnostic threshold for diabetes. At follow‑up, three mutually exclusive outcome categories were defined:

Regression to normoglycemia: defined as FPG < 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L), 2-h PG < 140 mg/dL (7.8 mmol/L), and HbA1c < 5.7% (39 mmol/mol).

Persistent prediabetes: remained within prediabetes ranges without meeting diabetes criteria.

Progression to type 2 diabetes (T2DM): defined as meeting one or more ADA diagnostic criteria—FPG ≥ 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L), 2-h PG ≥ 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L), or HbA1c ≥ 6.5% (48 mmol/mol).

Thus, all outcome classifications were based on repeated biochemical measurements and not on self‑reported diagnosis or medication use alone.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics and dietary intakes are presented across PHDI quartiles as means ± SD and frequencies (%). Linear trends were tested using the median value of each quartile as a continuous variable in linear regression for continuous characteristics, while the Mantel–Haenszel χ² test was applied for categorical variables.

The primary analyses used multinomial logistic regression to simultaneously model the three possible follow‑up glycemic outcomes with persistent prediabetes as the reference category. This approach directly addresses our two distinct research questions—which factors facilitate regression to normoglycemia and which prevent progression to T2DM. A sequential adjustment strategy was implemented across three nested models: Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), physical activity, Wealth Score Index, educational level, smoking status, and family history of T2DM. Model 2 incorporated additional adjustment for short and long sleep duration, non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), hypertension, and the triglyceride‑to‑high‑density lipoprotein cholesterol (TG/HDL‑C) ratio. The fully adjusted model (Model 3) further included dietary total energy intake. All covariates were selected a priori based on prior studies investigating dietary patterns and glycemic outcomes in prediabetic populations, as well as known risk factors for diabetes [9–11].

To investigate potential effect modification, pre‑specified stratified analyses were conducted according to age (< 45 vs. ≥45 years), sex, BMI (< 25 vs. ≥25 kg/m²), smoking status, socioeconomic status (Wealth Score Index < mean vs. ≥ mean), and physical activity level (< mean vs. ≥ mean). In these analyses, the stratifying variable was included in the model as a main effect together with a product (interaction) term, and the corresponding continuous covariate was omitted from the adjustment set for that specific subgroup to avoid over‑adjustment. The significance of effect modification was assessed using the P‑value for the interaction term derived from the continuous PHDI variable. Only variables with a statistically significant interaction (P < 0.05) are presented in main stratified analyses; full subgroup results for progression are available in Supplementary Table S6.

The robustness of the primary findings was evaluated through a series of sensitivity analyses. These entailed the exclusion of participants who experienced less than 10% weight loss from baseline, those with baseline hypertension, those with baseline NAFLD, and those with baseline cardiovascular disease. Additional models were constructed with further adjustment for baseline Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA‑IR), C‑reactive protein (CRP), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), antidiabetic medication use, and anti‑inflammatory medication use. To elucidate the contribution of individual dietary components, the association between each PHDI component (per SD increment) and both glycemic outcomes was examined.

To assess the dose‑response relationship and identify potential non‑linear patterns, we employed multivariable‑adjusted restricted cubic spline regression with four knots located at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the PHDI distribution [26]. These analyses were performed within the fully adjusted model (Model 3) to evaluate potential departures from linearity in the dose‑response relationship.

Missing data were handled by complete‑case analysis; participants with missing values on key covariates were excluded during data cleaning, resulting in the final analytic sample. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics version 26 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) and R software version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two‑tailed P‑value < 0.05 was considered the threshold for statistical significance.

Result

Participants with higher adherence to the PHDI tended to be younger and had higher levels of physical activity compared with those in the lowest quartile (P‑trend < 0.001 for both). The proportion of men differed across quartiles (P‑trend < 0.001), whereas smoking status, educational level, and family history of diabetes did not vary significantly.

Anthropometric measures showed a favorable profile among individuals with greater adherence to the PHDI. Body weight, waist circumference, and body mass index progressively decreased across increasing quartiles of the index (all P‑trend < 0.001). Similarly, several cardiometabolic markers improved with higher PHDI scores. Participants in the highest quartile exhibited lower fasting insulin concentrations and lower HOMA‑IR values compared with those in the lowest quartile (P‑trend < 0.001). Favorable trends were also observed for lipid parameters, including triglycerides, total cholesterol, and LDL cholesterol, while HDL cholesterol increased across quartiles (all P‑trend < 0.001). In addition, inflammatory and liver function markers, including C‑reactive protein, ALT, AST, and GGT, were significantly lower among participants with higher PHDI adherence (all P‑trend < 0.001). The prevalence of non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease also declined markedly across increasing quartiles of the index (P‑trend < 0.001).

Of the 4,143 prediabetic participants, 2,878 (69.5%) met the FBG criterion, 546 (13.2%) met the 2‑h PG criterion, and 1,999 (48.3%) met the HbA1c criterion. The proportion with IFG increased across PHDI quartiles (Q1: 65.4% → Q4: 72.2%), whereas the proportions with IGT (16.4% → 10.8%) and elevated HbA1c (55.2% → 44.6%) decreased.

Dietary intakes across quartiles of the PHDI

Dietary intakes across quartiles of the PHDI are shown in Table 2. Higher PHDI scores were accompanied by lower total energy intake, a slight shift toward more carbohydrate and protein and less fat, and greater consumption of plant‑based foods, including whole grains, legumes, fruits, vegetables, and nuts (all P‑trend < 0.001). Intake of refined grains, added sugars, animal fats, and red meat tended to decrease across quartiles (all P‑trend < 0.001).

Association between PHDI and regression to normoglycemia

During follow‑up, 1,288 participants regressed from prediabetes to normoglycemia and 775 progressed to type 2 diabetes mellitus (Table 3). In the crude analysis, higher PHDI adherence was associated with greater odds of regression to normoglycemia. Participants in the third and fourth quartiles had 71% and 75% higher odds of regression, respectively, compared with those in the lowest quartile (Q3: OR 1.71; 95% CI: 1.40–2.08; Q4: OR 1.75; 95% CI: 1.44–2.13; P‑trend < 0.001). These associations remained significant after adjustment for demographic and lifestyle factors (Model 1: Q4 vs. Q1, OR 1.34; 95% CI: 1.09–1.65; P‑trend < 0.001) and after additional adjustment for metabolic conditions and sleep duration (Model 2: Q4 vs. Q1, OR 1.41; 95% CI: 1.15–1.73; P‑trend < 0.001). In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), the highest versus lowest quartile conferred a 28% higher odds of regression (OR 1.28; 95% CI: 1.04–1.58; P‑trend = 0.003). Each one‑standard‑deviation increase in the PHDI score was associated with a 10% higher likelihood of regression to normoglycemia (OR 1.10; 95% CI: 1.02–1.18).

Analysis of individual PHDI components (Supplementary Table S3) indicated that higher intakes of legumes (OR 1.35; 95% CI: 1.23–1.48), total vegetables (OR 1.24; 95% CI: 1.12–1.38), dairy products (OR 1.32; 95% CI: 1.19–1.45), vegetable oils (OR 1.15; 95% CI: 1.07–1.24), and poultry (OR 1.11; 95% CI: 1.03–1.20) were significantly associated with greater odds of regression to normoglycemia. In contrast, higher intake of red meat was associated with lower odds of regression (OR 0.88; 95% CI: 0.82–0.95). Tubers and starchy vegetables were also inversely associated with regression (OR 0.92; 95% CI: 0.86–0.99). Other components, including whole grains, fruits, nuts and seeds, fish, eggs, animal fats, added sugars, and vegetable ratio components, were not significantly associated with regression.

As shown in Fig. 2a, higher PHDI scores were generally associated with higher odds of regression from prediabetes to normoglycemia. The spline curve suggested a gradual increase in the likelihood of regression with increasing PHDI values, particularly at higher levels of adherence. However, the overall association did not reach statistical significance (P‑overall = 0.22), and there was no evidence of a nonlinear relationship (P for non‑linearity = 0.11).

Association between PHDI and progression to Type 2 diabetes

Regarding progression to type 2 diabetes, in the crude analysis a borderline inverse association was observed (Q3: OR 0.69; 95% CI: 0.54–0.88), but the trend across quartiles was not significant (P‑trend = 0.064) (Table 3). After adjustment for demographic and lifestyle factors (Model 1), the association was no longer significant (Q4 vs. Q1, OR 0.99; 95% CI: 0.78–1.25; P‑trend = 0.743). Further adjustment (Models 2 and 3) yielded null findings. In the fully adjusted model, the OR for Q4 versus Q1 was 1.04 (95% CI: 0.82–1.31; P‑trend = 0.840), and each SD increase in PHDI was not associated with progression (OR 1.01; 95% CI: 0.92–1.10).

Evaluation of individual PHDI components in relation to diabetes progression (Supplementary Table S4) showed that higher intakes of whole grains, legumes, fruits, total vegetables, vegetable oils, and poultry were significantly associated with lower odds of developing type 2 diabetes only in minimally adjusted models; after full adjustment, these associations were attenuated and no longer significant.

For progression from prediabetes to type 2 diabetes (Fig. 1b), the spline analysis indicated a general inverse trend, with higher PHDI scores corresponding to lower odds of developing diabetes up to moderate levels of the index, followed by a modest increase at the highest scores. Nevertheless, neither the overall association nor the nonlinear component was statistically significant (P‑overall = 0.69; P for non‑linearity = 0.48).

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses for regression to normoglycemia are presented in Table 4 and Supplementary Table S6. A significant interaction was observed for sex (P‑interaction < 0.001). The positive association between higher PHDI adherence and regression to normoglycemia was more pronounced among men (Q4 vs. Q1 OR 1.48; 95% CI: 1.06–2.05; P‑trend = 0.004) than among women (OR 1.22; 95% CI: 0.93–1.61; P‑trend = 0.090), although the confidence intervals overlapped. No significant interactions were observed for body mass index, age, smoking status, wealth score index, or physical activity (all P‑interaction > 0.05).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses further supported the robustness of the primary findings (Supplementary Table S5). The positive association between higher PHDI adherence and regression to normoglycemia persisted after excluding participants with substantial baseline weight loss (Q4 vs. Q1: OR 1.46; 95% CI: 1.13–1.88), hypertension (OR 1.55; 95% CI: 1.23–1.95), non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (OR 1.37; 95% CI: 1.09–1.73), and cardiovascular disease (OR 1.33; 95% CI: 1.08–1.65) (all P-trend ≤ 0.002). The association remained significant after additional adjustment for baseline HbA1c (OR 1.26; 95% CI: 1.03–1.54), C-reactive protein (OR 1.20; 95% CI: 0.98–1.47), antidiabetic medications (OR 1.27; 95% CI: 1.04–1.55), and anti-inflammatory medications (OR 1.27; 95% CI: 1.04–1.56), indicating that the observed benefit was independent of baseline glycemic severity, systemic inflammation, and medication use. However, additional adjustment for HOMA‑IR attenuated the association and rendered the continuous estimate marginally non‑significant (Q4 vs. Q1: OR 1.17; 95% CI: 0.95–1.44; continuous: OR 1.06; 95% CI: 0.99–1.15), suggesting that insulin resistance partially mediates the relationship between PHDI adherence and glycemic improvement.

Discussion

In this longitudinal study of 4,143 prediabetic adults, greater adherence to the PHDI was prospectively associated with higher odds of regression to normoglycemia, with participants in the highest quartile demonstrating a 28% greater likelihood of glycemic recovery compared with those in the lowest quartile after comprehensive adjustment. Each standard‑deviation increment in PHDI score conferred a 10% higher odds of regression. In contrast, PHDI adherence was not independently associated with progression to type 2 diabetes after full covariate adjustment, a null finding that persisted across all subgroup analyses.

Our findings extend the emerging evidence base linking the EAT‑Lancet dietary pattern with favorable glycemic outcomes, while adding novel insights specific to prediabetic populations. Two recent systematic reviews have synthesized the association between planetary health diet adherence and type 2 diabetes risk and consistently reported protective associations [10, 11]. Our results for glycemic regression align directionally with these observations, demonstrating that participants in the highest PHDI quartile had 27% greater odds of returning to normoglycemia. The magnitude of this association is broadly comparable to reported effect estimates in general populations. For instance, Xu et al. reported that a one-point increase in the EAT-Lancet diet pattern score was associated with a 6% decrease in type 2 diabetes risk (HR 0.94; 95% CI: 0.91–0.97) among 59,849 UK Biobank participants [16], while López et al. observed a 10% lower incidence of type 2 diabetes with higher adherence to the EAT-Lancet healthy reference diet among Mexican women (HR 0.90; 95% CI: 0.75–1.10) [14]. Zhang et al. further demonstrated that higher adherence to the EAT-Lancet diet index was associated with reduced type 2 diabetes risk across all levels of genetic susceptibility in Swedish adults [13], and Langmann et al. confirmed a lower risk of type 2 diabetes with greater EAT-Lancet diet adherence among middle-aged Danish adults [12]. However, our null finding regarding progression to type 2 diabetes contrasts with the protective associations reported in these prior studies conducted among normoglycemic populations. This discrepancy may reflect the advanced metabolic deterioration inherent to prediabetes, where pancreatic β-cell dysfunction and established insulin resistance may render dietary interventions less effective for preventing further deterioration, even as they facilitate metabolic recovery. As Xu et al. noted, approximately 44% of the association between the EAT-Lancet diet and type 2 diabetes was mediated by body mass index, and 40% by waist circumference [16], suggesting that adiposity-related pathways are central to the diet-diabetes relationship. Our observation that HOMA-IR adjustment attenuated the regression association provides complementary evidence that insulin sensitivity represents a key mechanistic pathway, consistent with findings from Cacau et al., who reported that higher PHDI adherence was cross-sectionally associated with lower values of blood pressure, total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, and non-HDL cholesterol among Brazilian adults, although no association with HOMA-IR was observed in that cross-sectional analysis [27].

Our finding that participants in the highest PHDI quartile had 28% greater odds of returning to normoglycemia extends the existing evidence, which has predominantly focused on diabetes incidence rather than the bidirectional nature of glycemic change. The two systematic reviews by Ojo et al. [10] and Lin et al. [11] summarized studies that uniformly assessed progression to type 2 diabetes as the primary outcome, leaving the question of whether planetary health diet adherence could actively facilitate metabolic recovery unexplored. Our study addresses this critical gap by demonstrating that shifting toward a plant-forward dietary pattern may not merely delay deterioration but may actively promote restoration of normoglycemia. The convergence of evidence across different levels of dietary exposure assessment substantially strengthens the biological plausibility of our findings. Supporting this, Jalali et al. followed 1,586 individuals with prediabetes and identified three major mineral patterns, reporting that the chromium-selenium pattern was associated with a 26% higher likelihood of returning to normoglycemia (HR 1.26; 95% CI: 1.02–1.55), while the iron-manganese pattern conferred a 42% increased chance of regression (HR 1.42; 95% CI: 1.14–1.76) [28]. Importantly, the food group correlates of these protective mineral patterns—whole grains for the Cr-Se pattern, and green leafy vegetables, total vegetables, whole grains, nuts, and legumes for the Fe-Mn pattern—are strikingly concordant with the PHDI components we found to independently predict regression, suggesting that the micronutrient composition of the PHDI, including higher intakes of magnesium, potassium, iron, and folate as documented in our baseline analyses, may partly underlie the observed metabolic benefits. In contrast, Mirmiran et al., in a 9-year follow-up of 334 Iranian adults with prediabetes, found that neither PCA-derived dietary patterns—including Western-style, healthy, and processed-foods patterns—nor the DASH or Mediterranean diet scores were significantly associated with regression to normoglycemia [29]. This comparison suggests that the EAT-Lancet diet, as an a priori index capturing a specific globally recommended dietary composition, may offer advantages over empirically derived or other established dietary patterns for facilitating glycemic recovery in prediabetic patients.

The sequential attenuation observed from crude to Model 2 indicates that metabolic factors—NAFLD, hypertension, and the TG/HDL‑C ratio—accounted for a substantial portion of the unadjusted association. This finding is consistent with the strong links between these conditions, insulin resistance, and T2D risk. NAFLD, in particular, was highly prevalent in the lowest PHDI quartile (39.5% vs. 23.4% in Q4) and is a well‑established confounder in diet‑glycemia relationships due to its reciprocal associations with insulin resistance and dietary quality. Its inclusion in Model 2 contributed to the marked reduction in the OR for regression. Beyond the sensitivity analysis excluding NAFLD participants, the observed attenuation highlights the importance of controlling for hepatic steatosis when evaluating diet–diabetes associations, and suggests that PHDI may partly exert its benefits through improvements in liver fat and related metabolic dysfunction.

Higher PHDI adherence in our study was characterized by greater consumption of plant-based foods—including whole grains, legumes, fruits, vegetables, and nuts—and lower intake of refined grains, added sugars, animal fats, and red meat, accompanied by higher intakes of magnesium, potassium, iron, calcium, vitamin C, and folate, and lower intakes of saturated, monounsaturated, polyunsaturated, and trans fats. These dietary profiles are consistent with those reported across PHDI quartiles in other cohorts [6, 15]. The component-level findings in our study also resonate with prior evidence. López et al. reported that adhering to recommendations for red meat (HR 0.79; 95% CI: 0.63–0.99), legumes (HR 0.92; 95% CI: 0.84–0.99), and fish (HR 0.92; 95% CI: 0.85–1.00) was associated with lower type 2 diabetes incidence [14]. partially paralleling our observation that higher intakes of legumes, vegetables, and poultry were associated with glycemic regression, while red meat intake was inversely associated. However, the finding by López et al. that meeting dairy recommendations was associated with higher diabetes incidence contrasts with our observation of a beneficial association between dairy intake and regression, potentially reflecting differences in dairy food composition, fat content, or consumption patterns across study populations. Importantly, no previous study has examined the association between PHDI adherence and bidirectional glycemic trajectories—specifically regression to normoglycemia—in prediabetic individuals, making our findings a novel contribution to the literature. Sowah et al. further demonstrated that the inverse association between PHD adherence and type 2 diabetes was partly explained by plasma vitamin C and carotenoids, which collectively accounted for approximately 32% of the association, suggesting that antioxidant and anti-inflammatory pathways may underlie the metabolic benefits of this dietary pattern [15]. Riera et al. also showed that individual PHDI components including vegetables, fish, red and processed meat, saturated and trans fats, and added sugars were independently associated with type 2 diabetes risk [6].

The significant sex-specific heterogeneity observed in our study—where higher PHDI adherence was more strongly associated with regression to normoglycemia in men than in women—warrants attention. This pattern is consistent with findings from Vallejo et al. [30], who reported that inverse associations between the EAT-Lancet dietary index and anthropometric markers such as BMI and body weight were more robust in male participants from the DONALD cohort. Men typically consume less healthful dietary patterns at baseline, with lower intakes of fruits, vegetables, and whole grains, which may result in a more substantial improvement in diet quality when transitioning toward higher PHDI adherence. Additionally, men exhibit greater visceral adiposity and hepatic insulin resistance, both of which are particularly responsive to the dietary composition emphasized within the PHDI framework, including higher plant protein and lower saturated fat intake [30].

In contrast, we did not observe statistically significant interactions for BMI, physical activity, or any of the other examined covariates (age, smoking status, wealth score index). This suggests that the beneficial association of the PHDI with regression to normoglycemia was broadly consistent across these strata. While some prior studies have reported that measures of adiposity mediate a substantial proportion of the association between plant-based dietary patterns and diabetes risk [15, 16], our subgroup analysis did not detect a differential effect by BMI category. It is possible that the PHDI confers benefits across the full range of body weight through multiple pathways—improved diet quality, reduced inflammation, and favorable changes in gut microbiota—that are not solely dependent on weight status. Similarly, the lack of a significant interaction with physical activity may reflect the dominant metabolic benefits of diet quality in a prediabetic population, independent of exercise levels. No meaningful interactions were observed for progression to type 2 diabetes, consistent with the overall null association between PHDI and diabetes progression in our fully adjusted models.

The absence of significant effect modification by age or socioeconomic status supports the potential applicability of the PHDI for glycemic health promotion across diverse demographic strata. However, the relative homogeneity of our cohort in terms of educational attainment and age range may partially explain these null interactions, and caution is warranted when extrapolating to more diverse populations.

The robustness of our primary findings was supported by multiple sensitivity analyses. The positive association between PHDI adherence and regression to normoglycemia remained significant after excluding participants with substantial baseline weight loss, hypertension, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, or cardiovascular disease, indicating that our results were not driven by individuals with pre-existing metabolic comorbidities that might confound or mediate the diet-glycemia relationship. The association also persisted after additional adjustment for baseline HbA1c, C-reactive protein, and use of antidiabetic or anti-inflammatory medications, suggesting that the observed benefit of PHDI adherence on glycemic recovery was independent of baseline glycemic severity, systemic inflammation, and medication use. These findings align with those from Sowah et al., who reported that the inverse association between PHD adherence and type 2 diabetes incidence in the EPIC-Norfolk cohort remained materially unchanged after excluding participants who developed diabetes within the first two years of follow-up, those with HbA1c exceeding 6.5% at baseline, or those with prevalent cardiovascular disease or cancer [15]. Similarly, Riera et al. demonstrated that the inverse association between PHDI and type 2 diabetes in the UK Biobank was robust in sensitivity analyses excluding participants who developed diabetes during the initial two years of follow-up and when restricting the sample to those with at least three dietary assessments [6]. The consistency of our sensitivity analyses with these prior studies reinforces the reliability of the observed association. Notably, in our study, additional adjustment for baseline HOMA‑IR attenuated the association and rendered the continuous estimate marginally non‑significant, suggesting that insulin resistance partially mediates the relationship between PHDI adherence and glycemic improvement. This finding is consistent with the well-established mechanistic role of plant-based dietary patterns in enhancing insulin sensitivity through multiple pathways, including reduced visceral adiposity, lower systemic inflammation, and improved lipid profiles—all of which were favorably distributed across PHDI quartiles at baseline in our study [31].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. The longitudinal prospective design examining both regression to normoglycemia and progression to type 2 diabetes captures the bidirectional nature of glycemic trajectories in prediabetes, an analytical approach superior to studies focusing solely on incident diabetes. The use of the validated PHDI enables assessment of whole‑diet patterns rather than isolated nutrients, reflecting real‑world dietary behavior while addressing both human and environmental health dimensions. Comprehensive adjustment for demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, metabolic, and dietary covariates, combined with extensive sensitivity analyses excluding participants with major comorbidities and additionally adjusting for HbA1c, C‑reactive protein, medications, and HOMA‑IR, strengthens internal validity and identifies insulin resistance as a potential mechanistic mediator. Additional strengths include the relatively large sample size, evaluation of both continuous and categorical PHDI exposures, and restricted cubic spline modeling of nonlinear relationships.

Several limitations warrant consideration. The observational design precludes causal inference, and residual confounding from unmeasured factors—including genetic predisposition, psychosocial stress, food processing levels, dietary timing, and neighborhood food environment—cannot be excluded. Dietary intake was assessed at a single time point using self‑reported instruments susceptible to recall and social desirability biases; repeated dietary assessments and objective intake biomarkers were unavailable. This single baseline assessment cannot capture dietary changes following prediabetes diagnosis or during follow‑up, introducing potential reverse causation. The PHDI, derived from the EAT‑Lancet global reference diet, may not perfectly capture regionally appropriate dietary patterns, and index construction involves subjective decisions regarding component scoring and weighting. Glycemic outcomes relied on clinical assessment using FBG, 2‑h PG from OGTT, and HbA1c at both time points, but the precise timing of glycemic status changes relative to dietary exposure could not be determined. Generalizability may be limited beyond the study population’s demographic and geographic characteristics. The modest magnitude of the fully adjusted association with regression suggests PHDI adherence alone may be insufficient to normalize glycemia, emphasizing the multifactorial nature of glucose regulation. The null association with diabetes progression may reflect insufficient power, limited dietary variability within this prediabetic cohort, or genuinely distinct etiological pathways governing regression versus progression. Furthermore, the high prevalence of prediabetes in the cohort (56% after exclusions) reflects the use of three diagnostic criteria and the urban referral‑center setting, which may limit generalizability but is consistent with recent national surveys reporting prediabetes rates exceeding 35% in Tehran [32].

Conclusion

In this longitudinal study of prediabetic adults, greater adherence to the PHDI was prospectively associated with higher odds of regression to normoglycemia, with evidence suggesting partial mediation by insulin sensitivity. Higher intakes of legumes, vegetables, dairy products, vegetable oils, and poultry emerged as key dietary drivers of glycemic recovery, while red meat intake was inversely associated. The positive association with regression was stronger among men, indicating potential effect modification by sex. In contrast, PHDI adherence was not independently associated with progression to type 2 diabetes after comprehensive covariate adjustment, highlighting the importance of distinguishing glycemic directionality in prediabetes research. These findings provide evidence that shifting toward a plant‑forward dietary pattern aligned with planetary health principles may facilitate glycemic recovery in high‑risk populations, offering a strategy with co‑benefits for both metabolic and environmental health. Future studies with repeated dietary assessments and interventional designs are warranted to establish causality and inform sustainable dietary recommendations for prediabetes management.
