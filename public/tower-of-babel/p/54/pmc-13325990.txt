Trajectories of blood pressure and influencing factors across menopause: A longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Xuanjin Yang, Gaifen Liu, Feiling Huang, Ruiyi Tang, Penghui Feng, Yubo Fan, Zhuolin Xie, Jingbo Huang, Xiaonan Ma, Keyang Yang, Rong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo identify the trajectories of blood pressure (BP) across menopause in Chinese women and analyze influencing factors.MethodsA total of 526 participants, contributing 4759 measurements, from the Peking Union Medical College Hospital Aging Longitudinal Cohort of Women in Midlife were included in this analysis. Group-based trajectory modeling was used to identify distinct trajectories of systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP) and mean arterial pressure (MAP) changes across menopause. Logistic regression and mixed-effects models were used to analyze the associations between sex hormones, body mass index (BMI), waist circumference(WC) and BP trajectories. Sensitivity analyses were conducted on participants who still have natural menstrual cycle at enrollment.ResultsThree distinct trajectories were identified for SBP, DBP and MAP. Overall, SBP and MAP exhibited an upward trend, while DBP showed a downward trend. For all three parameters, lower estradiol was correlated negatively to BP in the “high” group. But BMI and WC had a significant positive correlations of changes in BP in all three groups. BMI and WC exhibited a negative interactions in influencing BP. Additionally, SBP, DBP and MAP were higher in the “high” BMI trajectory group compared to the remaining two groups. Sensitivity analysis also showed similar findings as that from all the participants.ConclusionChinese women across menopause experience distinct trajectories for SBP, DBP and MAP, influenced by BMI and WC. This further underscores the necessity for enhanced BP monitoring and weight management among perimenopausal women to promote long-term cardiovascular health.

Introduction

Hypertension is one of the most common chronic diseases [1], with a notable sex disparity in its prevalence. Before age 50, women have a higher prevalence of hypertension than men, but this pattern reverses in individuals over 60 [2]. Similarly, the longitudinal trajectory of blood pressure (BP) with age also shows gender differences. Before midlife, men experience faster BP increases than women, but women overtake men after midlife [3]. This shift may be related to physiological changes across menopause in women [4]. The Study of Women’s Health across the Nation (SWAN) revealed the impact of the menopausal transition on BP by analyzing BP trajectories during this period [5]. These findings collectively suggest that the menopausal transition may present a critical window for preventive interventions targeting accelerated BP rise and subsequent cardiovascular disease risk in later life. Due to differences in genetic background and lifestyle habits, variations exist in trends of BP between Eastern and Western women. Although some studies have analyzed changes in BP among postmenopausal Chinese women [6], there remains a lack of research on identifying and analyzing BP trajectories specifically among Chinese women across menopause.

The most prominent feature of menopause is the elevation of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and the decline of estradiol (E2) [7]. However, findings regarding the relationship between sex hormones and BP remain heterogeneous. Among Chinese postmenopausal women, FSH has been found to be negatively correlated with systolic blood pressure (SBP) [8]. In contrast, a community-based study in Finland suggested no association between changes in FSH and BP [9]. Some studies considered E2 as a key protective factor against hypertension in women [10], yet others have found no relationship between changes in E2 and BP [5]. Additionally, perimenopausal women commonly experience accumulation of abdominal fat and an increase in body mass index (BMI) [[11], [12], [13]], all of which are risk factors for hypertension. Nevertheless, the relationship between changes in obesity and BP trajectories across menopause has not been thoroughly evaluated. The Peking Union Medical College Hospital Aging Longitudinal Cohort of Women in Midlife (PALM) is the first population-based prospective cohort study designed to comprehensively investigate the natural changes across menopause in Chinese women [14]. Thus, this study aims to delineate BP trajectories anchored to the final menopausal period (FMP) using the data from the PALM cohort. Based on this, we further seek to investigate the associations of adiposity measures and sex hormones with the trajectory and within-trajectory BP change, thereby providing scientific evidence for BP management in middle-aged Chinese women.

Methods

Participants

The data for this study were derived from the PALM, a prospective, open-cohort, community-based longitudinal study initiated by Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH) in 2005. The PALM cohort originally enrolled women aged 35–64 years who met the following criteria: (1) intact uterus with at least one ovary; (2) no severe systemic or reproductive endocrinologic disorders, including hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, stroke, malignant tumors, severe hepatic or renal dysfunction, autoimmune disorders; (3) no use of hormone medications in the previous 3 months; (4) no pregnancy or lactation in the preceding 6 months. Detailed methodology of the PALM cohort has been previously published [14]. Follow-up was conducted by trained and qualified nurses from the Department of Gynecology at PUMCH. The study protocol was approved by PUMCH Medical Ethics Review Committee (No. 1–23PJ1328), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

To examine BP trajectory in Chinese midlife women, we set up a sub-cohort based on PALM cohort. To ensure the reliability of trajectory identification and model convergence, we selected participants with at least 3 valid BP measurements during follow-up. Valid BP measurement were defined as those with complete data for both SBP and DBP, with no missing values for either measurement. And the exclusion of sub-cohort included: (1) premature ovarian insufficiency (POI), defined as exhaustion of normal ovarian activity in patients younger than 40 years; (2) induced menopause due to surgical removal of the ovaries or chemotherapy-/radiotherapy-induced ovarian failure; (3) not having reached natural menopause (≥12 months of amenorrhea) at the most recent follow-up; (4) missing essential co-variate data. The final sub-cohort comprised 526 participants with 4759 observations (Fig. 1). Time anchored to FMP

At each visit, participants were required to provide the exact date of their most recent menstrual period, along with detailed records of menstrual changes (including cycle length, duration, and volume). FMP was defined as the date of the last menstrual period prior to 12 consecutive months of amenrrohea. Participants who had not yet reached menopause or who underwent induced menopause were excluded due to the inability to obtain an accurate FMP. Menopausal age was calculated by subtracting birth date from final menstrual period (FMP) date.

BP measurement

The primary outcome was BP trajectory groups. BP measurement at baseline and during follow-up were obtained by professionally trained nurses using regularly calibrated electronic sphygmomanometer. Participants rested quietly in a seated position for 5–10 min before measurement. BP measurements were taken with the participants’ arm supported at heart level, with the cuff placed 2–3 cm above the antecubital fossa and secured snugly (allowing two-finger space). Participants were instructed to remain still and avoid coughing during measurements. Two consecutive values were recorded at 2-minute intervals, and the average value was used for analysis. Mean arterial pressure (MAP) was calculated as: MAP=(SBP-DBP)/3+DBP.

Hormone assays

Fasting blood samples were collected during each visit for hormone tests. Given that FSH and E2 are more commonly used in clinical practice to assess ovarian function in women, radioimmunoassay was employed as the primary method to determine their levels. All laboratory assessments were performed in the laboratory of the Gynecological Endocrinology and Women’s health Center of PUMCH. If participants were still menstruating, fasting blood samples were obtained during the early follicular phase of spontaneous menstrual cycles (day 2–4). In case where participants experienced amenorrhea exceeding 60 days or menopause, fasting blood samples were collected randomly during scheduled follow-up visits.

Study covariates

At each visit, participants self-reported demographic characteristics including age, education and income. Anthropometric measurements (height and weight) were obtained by trained staff, with body mass index (BMI) calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). BMI was classified into four categories: underweight (BMI <18.5kg/m2), normal weight (18.5kg/m2 ≤ BMI <24.0kg/m2), overweight (24.0kg/m2 ≤ BMI < 28.0kg/m2) and obese (BMI ≥ 28.0kg/m2) [15]. Waist circumference (WC) was measured by placing the measuring tape over the navel and horizontally circling the waist. According to the Healthy Adult Weight Determination in China (WS/T428–2013), central obesity was defined as the WC ≥85cm [16]. Participants self-reported whether they smoked (excluding secondhand smoke exposure).

Statistical analysis

This study primarily examined the impact of menopause on BP. To account for chronological aging effects, time (in year) was defined as follow-up date minus FMP date. Data points15 years before or after the FMP were dropped from the analysis due to limited observations. We used group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) to identify distinct trajectories of SBP, DBP and MAP over this period, separately. GBTM use finite mixture modeling to detect distinct groups sharing similar longitudinal patterns while accounting for classification uncertainty. The optimal number of trajectory groups (ranging from 2 to 5) was determined according to the following criteria: (1) the lowest Bayesian information criterion (BIC) and Akaike information criterion (AIC); (2) no <5 % of the participants within each trajectory group; (3) higher average posterior probabilities (APP) for each trajectory group (> 0.70) [17] . To flexibly estimate the shapes of BP trajectories, we testes linear, quadratic, and cubic terms of time. The quadratic terms of time was ultimately selected for trajectory fitting because it achieved a favorable balance between model fit and parsimony, avoiding overfitting while providing clinically interpretable trajectory shapes. We used locally weighted scatterplot smoothing (LOWESS) to visualize the BP trajectory curve and the 95 % confidence interval (CI) for each group.

Between-group differences in participant characteristics were assessed. Mean ± SD or median (IQR) were used for presenting continuous variables. Differences between multiple groups were analyzed by ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis test based on distribution normality. Categorical variables were reported as proportions and compared via chi-squared test.

After adjusting for baseline age, educational level and smoking status, multinomial logistic regression was used to evaluate the relationship between different BP trajectories and menopausal age, baseline BMI, WC, FSH and E2, with results expressed as odds ratios (OR) and 95 % confidence intervals (CI). Mixed-effects models were used for elevating associations of changes in BMI, WC, FSH, and E2 and changes in BP among different BP trajectories, with adjustment of age, educational level, and time-varying anti-hypertension medication. Based on the aforementioned multinomial logistic regression models and mixed-effects models, we further isolated the interaction effects of BMI and WC, as well as FSH and E2. Given the significant interaction between BMI and WC, we additionally analyzed the average marginal effects of baseline and time-varying BMI and WC.

Given that BMI changes were significantly associated with SBP, DBP, and MAP for each group, we used LOWESS to visualize BMI trends in each of different BP trajectory groups. To further explore the association between BMI changes across menopause and changes in BP, we identified distinct BMI trajectories during this period and similarly applied LOWESS to investigate how BP changed across different BMI trajectory groups.

As the PALM enrolled both premenopausal and postmenopausal women, we performed sensitivity analyses by replicating the entire analytical procedure specifically for the subset of women who maintained natural menstrual cycle (premenopausal) at baseline enrollment. All analysis were performed using R (version 4.3.0), with statistical significance set as p < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 526 participants were included, with follow-up assessments every 1 to 2 years over a median follow-up period of 11.5 years. Combined with AIC, BIC, proportions and APP (Table S1-S3), we identified three distinct trajectory groups of SBP, DBP and MAP (Fig. 2). Because AIC and BIC showed diminishing improvements beyond three classes, all classes had APP > 0.97, and class proportions were well-balanced. For SBP, the “high” group (46.58 %) exhibited a pronounced increase before menopause with a slower rise after menopause, whereas the “low” group (19.58 %) showed an initial decline followed by a period of relative stability, and then a gradual increase after menopause. The “medium” group (33.84 %) demonstrated a linear and gradual increase over time. For DBP, all groups showed an overall declining trend, though the “high” group (30.23 %) experienced a slight increase before menopause followed by a gradual decline, while the “low ” group (29.28 %) showed a rapid decline before menopause and a very slow decline after menopause.. The “medium” group (40.49 %) remained relatively stable throughout. As for MAP, the “high” group (36.69 %) showed a rapid increase before menopause and remained relatively stable after menopause, whereas the “low ”group (23.95 %) showed a rapid decline before menopause followed by stabilization. Menopausal characteristics and BP trajectories

Compared to the “low” group, after adjusting for baseline age, educational level and smoking status, women with overweight (OR=3.09, 95 %CI: 1.79∼5.34), obese (OR=5.75, 95 %CI: 2.58∼12.79) and central obesity (OR=2.93, 95 %CI: 1.54∼5.61) were more likely to follow the “high” group for SBP trajectories across menopause (Table 2). Although weak, the negative interaction between BMI and WC was present. Further marginal effects analysis (Table 3) showed that at the 25th and 50th percentiles of WC, a one-unit increase in BMI was associated with a 2.7 percentage point increase in the probability of belonging to the “high” group. For DBP and MAP, the effects of BMI and WC on trajectory group were largely consistent with the findings described above. Overweight, obesity, and central obesity significantly increased the probability of belonging to the “high” group, and there was a negative interaction between BMI and WC (Table 2). Marginal effects analysis (Table 3) also indicated that a higher BMI increased the probability of belonging to the “high”group, but this increase became smaller with higher WC levels. Interestingly, for SBP, each 1-SD increase in baseline log FSH levels was associated with a significantly reduced risk of transitioning to the “high” group (OR=0.70, 95 %CI: 0.52∼0.92) versus “low” group, the pattern also seen for DBP (OR=0.74, 95 %CI: 0.57∼97) and MAP (OR=0.71, 95 %CI: 0.54∼0.94). However, no such effect was observed for E2 and menopausal age. Sensitivity analysis

The pre-menopausal cohort consisted of 334 participants, with a total of 3478 observations and a median follow-up period of 13 years. As the 334 participants were at different menopausal status ay enrollment, we first classified them into reproductive stage, early menopausal transition, and late menopausal transition based on their menstrual status at baseline, and plotted their changes in BP across menopause (Figure S1). Regardless of their menopausal stage at enrollment, the three groups showed largely consistent trajectories of SBP, DBP, and MAP across menopause. Based on this, we conducted subsequent sensitivity analyses.

Table S8–10 present the statistical modeling features of BP trajectory groups. As shown in Figure S2, the trajectories of SBP, DBP and MAP during this period in the pre-menopausal cohort were similar to those of the overall participants. For all three BP measurements (including SBP, DBP and MAP), participants in the “high” group exhibited higher BP, BMI and WC, as well as a higher incidence of overweight, obese, and central obesity (p < 0.05) (Table S11–13). And there were no statistically significant differences in baseline menopausal stage between the groups.

Compared to “low” group, women with overweight, obese or central obesity were more likely to follow the “high” trajectory for SBP, DBP and MAP. Similar to the findings in all participants, the negative interaction was observed between BMI and WC. Notably, premenopausal women at enrollment showed no significant difference in baseline FSH and E2 levels between the “high” and “low”groups. And no significant interaction was found between FSH and E2 (Table S14). The marginal effects analysis of baseline BMI and WC was largely similar to that of the overall population (Table S15).The association between BMI increase and elevated BP remained consistent with the overall study population. However, in both the “high” DBP and MAP trajectory group, the inverse relationship between declining E2 and rising BP were still evident (Table S16). And the marginal effects analysis of time-varying BMI and WC was consistent with that of the overall population (Table S17).

Similarly, for SBP, DBP and MAP, the “high”group showed an accelerated upward trend in BMI before menopause, which was significantly higher than the other two groups from 5 years before menopause to 10 years after menopause (Figure S3 A-C). Consistent with the overall study population, three distinct BMI change trajectories across menopause were identified (Figure S3 D, statistical modeling features in Table S 18, baseline characteristics comparison in Table S19). Within 10 years before and after menopause, both the “high” and “medium” groups had significantly higher SBP, DBP and MAP levels compared the “low” group (Figure S3 E-G).

Discussion

Using data from PALM, the first Chinese prospective cohort study based on a community population to explore ovarian aging, we identified three distinct trajectories of SBP, DBP and MAP across menopause. It not only suggests that there is heterogeneity in BP changes among middle-aged Chines women, but also highlights the important need for regular monitoring BP during this period. Importantly, both baseline and time-varying BMI and WC were associated with trajectory groups or within-trajectory BP changes. These associations further reinforce the critical importance of weight management during the menopausal transition as a key window for maintaining stable BP and promoting long-term cardiovascular health. What’s more, within the “high” group, lower E2 was associated with higher SBP, DBP and MAP. However, given the observational nature of this analysis, these hormone-related associations should be interpreted as hypothesis and require further investigation.

The PALM cohort excluded women with hypertension at baseline. In the present analysis, to ensure the robustness of trajectory modeling, we further included only women with at least three BP measurements, which introduced a certain degree of selection bias. This inclusion criterion shifted the analytic sample toward healthier women with potentially higher adherence, which may lead to an underestimation of the actual magnitude of BP changes across menopause. Nevertheless, we identified three distinct trajectory patterns for SBP, DBP, and MAP, underscoring the critical importance of long-term BP monitoring for promoting cardiovascular health. Different BP trajectory patterns are closely related to cardiovascular health. Utilizing data from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), it was found that among adults older than 60, a long-term high BP trajectory was closely related to higher all-cause mortality, especially cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality [18]. Another longitudinal study focusing on Chinese elderly also revealed that individuals with higher levels of both SBP and DBP trajectories over time had a significantly increased risk of CVD [19]. Analysis based on the China Health and Nutrition Survey (CHNS) demonstrated that for individuals aged 20–50, an accelerated increase in SBP over life-course was associated with a significantly elevated risk of CVD [20] and diabetes [21]. Additionally, a study using the data from the English Longitudinal Study of Aging (ELSA) found that older adults with high-stable SBP trajectory faced a higher risk of all-cause mortality and dementia [22]. Therefore, despite the presence of selection bias, the primary objective of this study remains valid. We identified inter-individual differences in BP changes across menopause and analyzed their associated factors. These findings may inform risk stratification in clinical practice.

Compared with the findings from the SWAN [5], the trends in SBP and MAP trajectories identified in both studies were broadly similar. In the SWAN study, the SBP trajectory groups included high-slow decline group (rise before FMP and slow decline after FMP), medium-linear group (monotonic rise across menopause) and low-accelerated rise group (slow rise before FMP with accelerated rise after FMP). The MAP trajectories showed largely similar patterns across menopause. However, the DBP trajectories identified in our study and SWAN study were not entirely similar. Although both studies observed that the “high” DBP trajectory group showed a premenopausal rise followed by a postmenopausal decline, which may be related to vascular stiffening. Because some studies have suggested that the DBP peaks in midlife and then declines due to arterial stiffening [3,23]. But the remaining DBP trajectory groups differed. In the SWAN study, the other two groups showed and overall increasing trend, whereas in our study, they exhibited a declining trend. The SWAN primarily focused on Western women, whereas our study concentrated on Chinese women. Significant difference exist between the two populations in genetic background, dietary habits and body composition, which may account for the differing results. Additionally, we speculate that the progression of arteriosclerosis may differ between Chinese and Western women as they age, which could also serve as a contributing factor.The results of our study align with results from two studies using CLHLS, which found a decreasing trend in DBP among Chinese adults aged 60 and above [18,24]. In contrast, another analysis based on CHNS reported an increase in DBP over follow-up years [25],. Similarly, a CHNS-based analysis of BP trajectories in postmenopausal women also found an upward trend in DBP [6]. We speculate that these discrepancies may arise because these three studies used follow-up year rather than age as the x-axis, and the CHNS included participants with a wide age range at baseline. Besides, in China, isolated systolic hypertension (ISH) is the most prevalent and high-risk type, particularly common among older adults [[26], [27], [28]], and accounts for approximately 30 % of hypertension cases in young and middle-aged individuals [29]. Additionally, since SBP is more difficult to control than DBP, it remains a predominant feature of uncontrolled hypertension [30]. Therefore, considering our findings, SBP monitoring and management may be of greater importance for Chinese women across menopause.

We found that changes in SBP, DBP and MAP were nonlinear in both the overall participants and the premenopausal enrollment group, suggesting that menopause-related characteristics exert a certain influence on the changes in BP.

Abdominal fact accumulation is a characteristic feature of menopause. Our study found that at women with baseline overweight, obesity, or central obesity had a significantly increased risk of belonging to “high” trajectory groups for SBP, DBP, and MAP. Obesity and central obesity have long been recognized as risk factors for hypertension and cardiovascular diseases [[31], [32], [33]]. Furthermore, we observed a negative interaction between BMI and WC. Marginal effects analysis further revealed that, for SBP, DBP, and MAP, the probability of belonging to the “high” trajectory group increased with rising BMI. However, this increase in probability was attenuated when WC increased. We hypothesize that this negative interaction may be attributable to a saturation effect, whereby the impact of BMI on BP reaches a plateau in individuals with established obesity, thereby diminishing the additional contribution of WC. This can also explain the negative interaction between time-varying BMI and WC on BP values. Anyway, these findings suggest that weight management during the menopausal transition should address both general and central obesity, se neither alone is sufficient.

Our results demonstrated that, regardless of whether for SBP, DBP, and MAP, time-varying BMI was positively correlated with BP values. Consequently, we conducted a dual validation of the impact of BMI gain during the menopausal transition on BP elevation by analyzing BMI changes across different BP trajectories and BP changes across different BMI trajectories. During this period, factors such as estrogen decline leading to fat redistribution, increased central adiposity, and reduced energy expenditure contribute to BMI elevation in perimenopausal women [34]. Elevated BMI is a well-established high-risk factor for multiple diseases including CVD, metabolic syndrome and other condition [13,35]. A data analysis based on CHNS found that middle-aged women in the group with the highest baseline BMI and a gradually increasing trajectory also had the highest risk of developing hypertension, along with higher SBP and DBP [36]. These findings are consistent with ours although we focus on the menopausal transition. Our study provides an innovative dual validation of the effect of BMI changes on BP changes. This again underscores that maintaining an optimal weight during the menopausal transition, a key window period, is of great significance for long-term cardiovascular health.

For sex hormones, FSH and E2 don’t exhibit an interaction effect. And in the “high” group, time-varying E2 was negatively correlated with SBP, DBP and MAP. Previous study has reported that compared with age-matched premenopausal women, women with premature ovarian insufficiency (POI), induced menopause and postmenopausal women exhibit a higher prevalence of hypertension [10]. Additionally, decreased E2 during pregnancy is associated with an increased risk of pre-eclampsia [37]. However, we didn’t observe the relationship between baseline E2 and BP trajectory groups. We hypothesize that the effect of E2 on BP changes may be closely related to the timing of measurement, specifically depending on different menopausal stages. However, the relation between BP and E2 requires further studies to be validated. Furthermore, adipose tissue expresses estrogen receptors and can influence E2 levels [38], a mechanism that may partially explain the findings of this study.

Another characteristic of menopause is elevated FSH. BP trajectory analysis based on the SWAN study found that higher FSH over time predicted a lower BP level [5]. However, in our study, no association was observed between the time-varying FSH and BP, which may be attributed to differences in genetic backgrounds among populations. We only found that, for SBP, DBP and MAP, each 1-SD increase in baseline log FSH significantly reduced the risk of entering the “high” group compared to the “low”group. A multi-center cross-sectional study in China revealed that FSH was inversely associated with 10-year atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk in postmenopausal women, with a significant correlation with SBP but no clear relationship with DBP [8]. Another study based on the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) reported a negative correlation between FSH an BP in postmenopausal women, but this association was absent in premenopausal and perimenopausal women [39]. Meanwhile, we also observed that the inverse relationship was only present in the overall participants but not in the premenopausal enrollment group. Anyway, the opposing effects of baseline versus time-varying FSH suggest it may reflect menopausal stage rather than acting as a direct causal factor.

Previous study found that women who experienced menopause at 40–44 had a higher multivariable-adjusted risk of CVD, compared to those who underwent menopause at 50–55 [40]. Additionally, women with early menopause (before age 45) exhibited higher coronary heart disease mortality and all-cause mortality [41]. A large-sample study from China revealed that, even after adjusting for confounding factors such as age, BMI and smoking status, women who experienced menopause before 45 still had a significantly elevated risk of hypertension compared to those who underwent menopause after 53 [42]. A meta-analysis further confirmed that women with early menopause (age at menopause <45 years) had an increased risk for arterial hypertension compared with those of normal age at menopause [43]. However, we didn’t find an impact of menopausal age on BP trajectories. This discrepancy may be related to differences in analytical approaches. Previous studies have primarily been cohort studies comparing disease incidence between women with early menopause and those with normal menopause, whereas this study aimed to identify patterns of BP trajectories. We would like to highlight, however, that women who have early menopause should be encouraged to increase their BP surveillance for cardiovascular disease prevention.

Finally, misclassification in the trajectory modeling process may result in some individuals being assigned to inappropriate trajectory groups. Such misclassification may arise from a limited number of BP measurements and uneven follow-up intervals, which could lead to biased estimates of true differences between groups. Nevertheless, through repeated analytical explorations of the effect of BMI on BP trajectories, we believe that our findings still support the significance of weight management for cardiovascular health in middle-aged women.

Strengths and limitations

Firstly, we comprehensively examine the trajectories of SBP, DBP and MAP across menopause in Chinese women, along with their influencing factors, which can provide scientific evidence of BP management in perimenopausal women. Secondly, we find that there is a negative correlation between E2 and BP, consistent with previous studies [10]. Thirdly, we thoroughly explore the relationship between BMI and WC variations and changes in BP across menopause, reaffirming the importance of weight management during this period. However, our study also has several limitations.

Firstly, including premenopausal and postmenopausal women into the PALM might have affected the mapping of BP trajectories across menopause. Nevertheless, sensitivity analysis conducted on premenopausal women enrolled in the PALM yielded results consistent with the overall participants, enhancing the robustness of the findings to some extent. Secondly, the other study suggests that MHT exerts some effect on BP [44], as PALM is a community-based cohort with low MHT use, the small number of participants receiving MHT in our study precludes us from evaluating whether or not it had an impact on BP. Thirdly, we included only women with at least three blood pressure measurements, which may have introduced selection bias by enrolling healthier individuals. What’s more, we did not incorporate physical activity or lipid levels into the multinomial logistic or mixed-effects models, which may have influenced the results to some extent. Nevertheless, we further analyzed changes in blood pressure across different BMI trajectory groups, which can partially reflect the impact of BMI on blood pressure. Lastly, PALM is a single-center study, and the generalizability of these results needs to be confirmed by multicenter data.

Conclusion

For Chinese women, distinct trajectories of SBP, DBP and MAP across menopause are observed. We recommend more attention should be paid on BP surveillance during this period to promote cardiovascular health. Additionally, our findings concerning the impact of BMI and WC changes on BP emphasize the critical importance of maintaining appropriate weight during the menopausal transition, particularly for those with central obesity. Finally, time-varying E2 is negatively associated with changes in BP. More studies can be done to explore the relationship between them.Central Illustration.Unlabelled image dummy alt text
