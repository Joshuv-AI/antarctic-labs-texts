Laparoscopic Surgery in Recipients of Intra-abdominal Solid Organ Transplants: A Systematic Review
Authors: Eva Suarthana, Lynn Doan, Neda Mohammadi Danesh, Togas Tulandi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Laparoscopy is a minimally invasive surgical approach that can reduce morbidity compared with open surgery in appropriately selected patients. However, patients with a history of intra-abdominal solid organ transplantation may have postoperative adhesions and altered anatomy, leading to more complex and variable anatomy. It remains unclear whether prior transplantation increases the risk of complications during laparoscopy. This study aimed to evaluate the incidence of complications in patients undergoing laparoscopy following intra-abdominal solid organ transplantation.Materials and Methods:A systematic search of PubMed, EMBASE, and Google Scholar was conducted through October 2023 to identify studies on laparoscopy in postintra-abdominal solid organ transplant recipients. Two independent reviewers identified 30 relevant articles, of which 19 (including case reports, case–control, and cohort studies) met the inclusion criteria. Risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa scale by two independent reviewers. The study protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023477254).Results:Four studies included direct comparisons between transplant and nontransplant patients. The mean postoperative length of stay was 3.5 days among 1402 transplant patients, compared with 2.3 days among 2,640,340 nontransplant patients. Two studies reported a lower rate of wound infection in nontransplant patients compared with posttransplant patients (1.7% vs. 4.8%). Rates of other infections, seroma formation, ileus, and disease recurrence were comparable between groups, although each was reported by only one small study. Two of the four studies were assessed as having a low risk of bias.Conclusion:Direct comparisons between patients with and without a history of intra-abdominal solid organ transplantation suggest that laparoscopy is generally safe, with a modest increase in hospital stay and wound infection risk in posttransplant patients.

INTRODUCTION

Laparoscopy has transformed surgery by providing a minimally invasive approach to conventional laparotomy, resulting in reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stays, faster recovery, and decrease risk of surgical infection.[12] Within the context of solid organ transplantation, particularly kidney and liver transplants, laparoscopy is increasingly being utilized to manage postoperative complications and perform diagnostic or therapeutic procedures.

However, concerns about altered anatomy, postsurgical adhesions, and immunosuppressive therapy have restricted the use of laparoscopy in transplant recipients. With surgical proficiency and advancements in laparoscopic technology, this approach is now feasible in selected posttransplant patients. Yet, intra-abdominal solid organ transplantation can result in more complex and unpredictable anatomy.

The aim of our study was to evaluate the incidence of complications in patients undergoing laparoscopy after intra-abdominal solid organ transplantation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023477254), and the analysis followed the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses. A systematic search was conducted across PubMed, EMBASE, Medline, and Google Scholar for studies related to laparoscopic surgery and its associated complications from inception until October 2023. The search used the following terms: “Bowel injury” AND “Laparoscopy” AND “Intra-abdominal organ transplant” OR “Complications” OR “Injury.” We did not restrict our search based on specific organ transplants or laparoscopic procedures. The search was limited to studies involving human subjects and articles published in the English language. Since our systematic review involves no new data collection, no access to confidential or identifiable information, and no secondary use of restricted datasets, IRB approval to conduct it is not required, according to the requirements laid out in the Tri-Council Policy Statement (TCPS 2, 2022).

Articles flagged for retraction notices or expressions of concern in Retraction Watch were excluded from the study. We also excluded case reports that included three or fewer cases. After removing these articles and duplicates, abstracts were independently screened for relevance by two co-authors (LD and ND) on Rayyan platform (https://www.rayyan.ai/). Out of 1035 articles screened based on titles and abstracts, disagreements occurred for 35 (3.4%) articles, and these were resolved by ES as the adjudicator.

LD extracted data from each selected article, including the type of procedure (laparoscopy or open surgery), patient age and sex, length of hospital stay, and the types of complications (e.g., hospital readmission, wound infection, other infections, seroma, ileus, fever, disease recurrence such as lymphoceles or hernias, hemorrhage, hematoma, and reoperation). LD evaluated the risk of bias using the Newcastle–Ottawa scale.

Statistical analysis

We grouped the studies by the type of comparators: (1) case series of laparoscopic procedures in intra-abdominal organ transplant recipients, (2) controlled studies comparing laparoscopic versus open procedures in intra-abdominal organ transplant recipients, (3) controlled studies comparing laparoscopic outcomes in organ transplant recipients versus nontransplant patients. The pooled frequencies and percentages were presented for categorical variables. The pooled mean and standard deviations or median and interquartile range were calculated for continuous variables when appropriate.

We included all case reports, case–control studies, and cohort studies that described laparoscopic procedures and associated complications, when applicable. Overall, the reported complications were relatively low. Among the eleven case series (n = 182) of patients who had previously undergone intra-abdominal organ transplantation, the length of stay was reported in eight studies with a mean of 7.3 (standard deviation 0.4) days. Ileus (7.3%), reoperation (5.0%), and wound infection (4.2%) were each reported by three studies [Table 1b]. Ulrich et al.[6] reported two cases of lymphocele recurrence after laparoscopic fenestration that required laparoscopic reintervention. Varga et al.[5] reported two patients who underwent laparoscopy to treat biliary peritonitis post liver transplants who required reoperation; one was due to a deep wound infection and one to a bile leak. Cascales Campos et al.[7] reported two patients who underwent laparoscopic cholecystectomy postkidney transplants, who required reoperation due to biliary anastomosis complications. All but one case series described well the population, outcomes, and follow-up [Table 4].

DISCUSSION

Direct comparison between patients with and without a history of intra-abdominal solid organ transplantation shows that laparoscopy appears to be a safe surgical approach with a modest increase in length of hospital stay and wound infection in postorgan transplantation patients.

Three small studies by Doublet et al., Yannam et al., and Moris et al. reported that the length of hospital stay after laparoscopic procedures in transplant recipients was comparable to that of nontransplant patients.[181921] However, one large administrative data of 1336 kidney transplant (KTR) and 2,640,247 non-KTR recipients who underwent laparoscopic appendectomy showed that posttransplant patients treated at transplant centers had significantly longer length of stay (ratio 1.37, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.21–1.55) and greater hospital-associated costs (ratio 1.29, 95% CI 1.19–1.41) after adjusting for patient and hospital level factors.[20] The same study reported greater wound infection (odds ratio 3.54, 95% CI 1.54–8.09) in posttransplant patients.

As expected, in our study, the length of stay following open laparotomy in transplant patients was longer than after laparoscopic procedures (13.3 vs. 6.8 days, respectively). One study reported an average postoperative stay of 4.6 days for all laparoscopic cholecystectomy patients compared to 8.7 days for those undergoing open cholecystectomy, with prolonged hospitalization associated with the treatment of comorbid conditions and surgical complications.[22] Another study similarly observed that the average hospital stay after retroperitoneal laparoscopic nephrectomy was substantially shorter (3.8 days) than after open surgery (7.9 days).[18] In our institution, most patients after a laparoscopic procedure are discharged on the same day.

Common complications across studies included wound infections, fever, urinary tract infections, seroma formation, ileus, hemorrhage, hematoma, need for reoperation, and recurrence of the initial disease. In our cohort, the overall complication rate, including wound infection, was low at 5%. Morris et al. reported a 6.7% complication rate, including one case requiring intensive care unit monitoring due to intraoperative blood loss and one case of surgical site infection.[21] DiBrito et al., in their evaluation of 599 laparoscopic appendectomies in kidney transplant recipients, found no difference in overall complication rates compared to nontransplant patients.[20] However, kidney transplant recipients were more prone to wound infection and pulmonary complications. In a study on laparoscopic hernia repair in liver transplant recipients, the reported complications included seroma formation (8%) and wound infection (2%).[3]

To date, there is limited literature on laparoscopic surgery for gynecologic disorders in transplant recipients.[232425262728] However, available case reports suggest that laparoscopic procedures in recipients of solid intra-abdominal organ transplants are both feasible and safe. Our experience with laparoscopic hysterectomy in kidney transplant recipients supports these findings.

Special perioperative considerations in liver and kidney transplant recipients include modified trocar placement (particularly in cases involving pelvic kidney grafts), the use of prophylactic antibiotics, and thorough evaluation of graft function. The use of an open laparoscopic technique for abdominal entry may also help minimize the risk of injury to transplanted organs.[29]

The limitation of our study was the small number of included articles, many of which were case reports or case–control studies. However, our analysis included a relatively large cohort of postlaparoscopic patients and allowed for direct comparison of complication rates between transplant and nontransplant patients.

CONCLUSION

Accumulating evidence from previous studies and case reports supports the safety and feasibility of laparoscopic surgery in transplant recipients. Complication rates are generally low, and the minimally invasive approach offers distinct advantages, including reduced morbidity and faster recovery, that are particularly beneficial in immunosuppressed patients.
