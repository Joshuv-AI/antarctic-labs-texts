Aneurysmal Veins in Free Flaps: A Rare Case Report and Discussion
Authors: Manisha Anbudurai, Louis Choon Kit Wong, Christelle Guillon, Yishan Valerie Tuang, Priya Tiwari, Yan Lin Yap, Thiam Chye Lim, Jane Lim, Vigneswaran Nallathamby, O-Wern Low
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Microvascular anastomosis is a fundamental technique in reconstructive surgery, where careful selection and meticulous handling of vessels are critical in preventing thrombosis—the leading cause of flap failure. Despite the extensive literature surrounding the topic, aneurysmal veins—typically identified incidentally on imaging or occasionally presenting with thrombosis when located in the deep veins of the extremities—are seldom encountered in the context of microsurgery due to their overall rarity. In this case report, we describe our intraoperative encounter with aneurysmal veins during free flap reconstruction. We outline our attempts to use and salvage these aberrant vessels for microvascular anastomosis, describing the technical difficulties that were encountered along the way. Serving as a cautionary tale, we explain what should be done when such unusual veins are encountered.

Successful free tissue transfer in reconstructive surgery relies on well-executed microvascular anastomosis. Precise vessel selection and handling are key in preventing thrombosis, the leading cause of flap failure.1 Thrombosis often results from blood stasis, endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability, as defined by the Virchow triad. In flaps, factors that predispose to thrombosis include pedicle kinks, vasospasm, poor blood flow, and extrinsic vessel compression due to tight closure, tissue edema, or hematoma.2 Accordingly, extensive literature guides vessel preparation and handling.

Aneurysmal veins, defined as 2–3 times the diameter of normal veins,3,4 are rare. Typically seen as incidental findings on imaging or presenting with deep vein thrombosis in the extremities, neither their presence in free flaps nor their suitability for microvascular use has been reported. This case report describes our encounter with aneurysmal veins during free flap surgery, our attempts to use them for microvascular anastomosis, and the challenges faced.

CASE REPORT

A 54-year-old man had sustained a Gustilo 3A trimalleolar ankle fracture in a motorcycle accident. He underwent urgent debridement and external fixation of the fracture by the orthopedic team. After failed primary closure, a 3 × 5 cm wound with an exposed ankle joint prompted a referral to plastic surgery for wound coverage. An initial posterior tibial artery perforator-based adipofascial flap had failed within a week due to poor perfusion and had to be taken down, leaving behind a 6 × 15 cm defect. A contralateral free medial sural artery (MSA) perforator flap based on 2 musculocutaneous perforators was then harvested for wound coverage.

End-to-side anastomoses were performed between the posterior tibial artery and the MSA. Interestingly, one of the venae comitantes identified had an extraordinarily large vein caliber, approximately 4-fold the size of the largest recipient in the vicinity—the great saphenous vein (GSV)—and had the advantage of offering tension-free anastomosis compared with other deep veins. The GSV offered a tension-free anastomosis compared with other recipients; hence, an end-to-side anastomosis was performed between the GSV and vena comitans 1 of the medial sural vein (VC1) to overcome the size mismatch. An end-to-end anastomosis between one of the posterior tibial venae comitantes (PTV) and vena comitans 2 of the medial sural vein (VC2) was done to establish a dual venous outflow system (Fig. 1). Surgery concluded uneventfully with an ischemic time of 1 hour 13 minutes. The flap had a regional oxygen saturation (rSO2) of 67% measured via the INVOS system.

Over the next 6 hours on the ward, however, the flap rSO2 had steadily dropped to 40%, and upon review, the flap was congested. The patient was wheeled in for urgent exploration of the flap.

Both venous outlets had been compromised—the VC1–GSV anastomosis had extensive thrombosis that nearly occluded the vein, whereas the VC2–PTV anastomosis was kinked under the MSA. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays an actual specimen of an aneurysmal segment of VC1 that was narrowed using ligating clips, with the blood clot found within it, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E670.) To address the VC1–GSV mismatch, a proximal VC1 branch with a 1:1 size match was identified and reanastomosed to the GSV, and ligating clips were applied to taper its aneurysmal lumen. The VC2–PTV anastomosis was revised and reoriented to prevent kinking (Fig. 2), resulting in improved flap color and turgor, and a promising rSO2 of 66%. Unfortunately, this restoration was short-lived. Six hours later, the flap rSO2 had dropped to 50%, prompting urgent reexploration.

Reanastomoses of VC1–GSV to a more proximal VC1 branch with a 1:1 size match. Ligating clips were applied as well to narrow its aneurysmal lumen. Revised VC2–PTV anastomosis with a change in orientation to prevent kinking, resulting in improved flow. PTA, posterior tibial artery.

This time, both venous outlets were extensively thrombosed. Adventitiectomy and complete dissection of the vascular pedicle were performed for better visualization, and this revealed that VC2 was a branch of VC1—likely explaining the simultaneous thrombosis—and that VC1 had multiple aneurysmal segments with thrombi within (Fig. 3).

Adventitiectomy done to better delineate the vessels revealed multiple aneurysmal segments along VC1. PTA, posterior tibial artery.

To address this, a nonaneurysmal portion of VC2 was harvested as a vein graft to reconstruct the VC1–GSV and VC2–PTV anastomoses. But thrombosis recurred, and we decided to abandon dual venous drainage altogether. All aneurysmal segments of VC1 and VC2 were excised, and the main vein proximal to their bifurcation was anastomosed to the GSV using the healthy segment of VC2 harvested earlier as an interpositional vein graft. Flap turgor and color improved postoperatively, with a good flap rSO2 of 77% (Fig. 4).

Final revision in which VC2–PTV anastomosis was foregone, and a vein graft was used to anastomose the MSV to the GSV. PTA, posterior tibial artery.

The flap remained healthy and viable on the general ward, and the patient was started on non-weight-bearing ambulation and discharged well 2 weeks later. As of his latest clinic review, the patient is well, and the flap remains healthy.

DISCUSSION

Despite multiple attempts to salvage the aneurysmal vein for anastomosis, all aneurysmal segments had to be dissected out, and a vein graft had to be used to save the flap. The initial perceived benefits of preserving pedicle length and dual venous drainage were outweighed by recurrent thrombosis.

Aneurysmal veins are rare,3 with limited guidance on their management. Techniques such as tangential aneurysmectomy with lateral venorrhaphy, complete resection with end-to-end anastomosis, and interpositional vein grafting have been described, mainly for large caliber veins such as the popliteal.4 Their use for small-caliber vessels is unclear. In this case, we tried tapering the aneurysmal segments using ligating clips to create a uniform lumen, but the veins were prone to stasis in their dilated lumens,5 promoting recurrent thrombosis and flap failure.

What should be done, then, if an aneurysmal vein is encountered during microsurgery? We suggest that one should first perform a complete adventitiectomy to fully visualize the vessel. This helps to assess pedicle length and determine whether a vein graft is needed before dissecting nonaneurysmal veins that can be used. Next, all aneurysmal segments should be resected to avoid areas prone to stasis and thrombosis. If this limits pedicle length, a vein graft should be used to ensure a tension-free anastomosis—a well-established technique.6 Nonaneurysmal vein segments of good quality, caliber, and length should be harvested before dissection.

In summary, aneurysmal veins are uncommon in microsurgery. If encountered, they should be avoided for anastomosis; all aneurysmal segments should be resected, and only healthy veins should be used for vein grafts, to reduce the risk of thrombosis.
