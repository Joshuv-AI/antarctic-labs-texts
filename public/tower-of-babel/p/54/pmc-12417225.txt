Increased serologic reactivity with automated CMIA testing and its implications for deceased donor eligibility determination in the United States
Authors: Sara O. Dionne, Hillary C. S. R. Akana, Michael Real, Kimberly A. Elliott, Brenden R. O’Neale
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In the United States, the use of Food & Drug Administration (FDA)-licensed, approved, or cleared tests is required for infectious disease screening and determining the eligibility of deceased donors for all Human Cells, Tissues, and Cellular and Tissue-Based Products (HCT/Ps). With the discontinuation of two manual enzyme-linked immunoassay (EIA) tests, automated Chemiluminescent Microparticle Immunoassay (CMIA) technology was introduced as the primary alternative. This study compares serologic reactivity rates between manual EIA and automated CMIA methods. Serology testing was performed on blood specimens from deceased tissue and cornea donors using either manual EIA assays or automated CMIA platforms. A retrospective analysis of over 160,000 donors was conducted to evaluate assay-specific reactive rates. Concordance between serology and Nucleic Acid Testing (NAT) results was also assessed. Reactive rates for HBsAg, HIV-1/2 antibodies, and HTLV-I/II antibodies increased following the transition to CMIA-based testing compared to manual EIA methods. However, these increases were not associated with a corresponding rise in NAT reactive results, indicating a potential increase in false-positive or non-viremic results. Automated testing offers improved quality control and reduces variability associated with manual techniques. However, the shift to CMIA assays resulted in a higher number of reactive serology results, leading to increased donor ineligibility despite negative NAT results. The data presented here highlights the potential impact on donor deferral rates for tissue and cornea donors due to assay platform changes.

Introduction

Donor screening assays are designed to be more sensitive than specific compared to diagnostic testing to minimize the number of false negatives (missing true positive cases) (Screening Donated Blood for Transfusion-Transmissible Infections 2009). They are performed to detect potential health disorders or diseases in people who do not have symptoms of disease. In the context of Human Cells, Tissues, and Cellular and Tissue-Based Products (HCT/Ps), false-positive results, while potentially excluding eligible donors, are generally considered acceptable to maximize recipient safety.

In the United States, screening of deceased tissue and cornea donors is regulated by the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) Title 21 (§ 1271.80) which mandates the use of Food & Drug Administration (FDA)-licensed, approved, or cleared tests for deceased donor screening to prevent the transmission of communicable diseases (eCFR:: 21 CFR Part 1271 2025). The Association for Advancing Tissue and Biotherapies standards (D4.220) further stipulates that, when available, test kits must be specifically labeled for use with specimens collected after the donor’s heart has stopped beating (Testing Human Cells, Tissues, and Cellular and Tissue-Based Product (HCT/P) Donors for Relevant Communicable Disease Agents and Diseases|FDA). These screening requirements aim to ensure the safety and quality of HCT/Ps and are guided by the FDA's published list of approved donor screening assays (eCFR:: 21 CFR Part 1271 Subpart C 2025).

An upcoming change in the availability of screening assays, namely, the discontinuation of enzyme immunoassays (EIAs) for detecting hepatitis B core antibodies (anti-HBc) and hepatitis C antibodies (anti-HCV), has effectively resulted in laboratories needing to transition to chemiluminescent microparticle immunoassays (CMIAs) for these assays. In response to these technological changes, we performed a retrospective analysis of screening results for over 160,000 deceased Tissue and Cornea donors to assess the impact of transitioning to CMIA-based assays. Our analysis revealed an increase in reactive results for several infectious disease markers. Under current U.S. regulatory guidelines (§ 1271.50) (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2025), confirmatory testing for reactive screening results is neither approved nor accepted for determining donor eligibility. As a result, any reactive antibody analytes, regardless of negative nucleic acid test results, mandate donor deferral. These findings raise important concerns about the potential for increased donor disqualifications and a subsequent reduction in the availability of tissue and cornea donations.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study analyzed infectious disease screening results from 161,159 deceased tissue and cornea donors. Donor specimens were collected across the United States by various Tissue Recovery Organizations and tested at four independent laboratories. The EIA results had been generated earlier as part of routine deceased donor screening evaluation, while the CMIA assays were conducted later, post-validation of the platform and methodology.

According to FDA regulations, for cadaveric (non-heart-beating) donors, blood for donor screening must be collected within 24 h of death (Guidance for Industry 2025). This is specified in the FDA’s guidance for tissue and organ donation to ensure the accuracy and reliability of infectious disease testing. Deceased donor specimens were collected by either Tissue or Cornea Banks within the timeframes and temperatures specified by the manufacturers’ package inserts. The most stringent of conditions for testing was Nucleic Acid Testing (NAT); 24 h of death if the cadaver was refrigerated (1°–10°C) within 12 h of death or collected within 15 h of death if the cadaver was not refrigerated (1°–10°C), therefore this applied to all tests for donor specimens. Samples were shipped to the laboratories using validated shippers designed to maintain specimen integrity during transport. The time between sampling and centrifugation was determined by manufacturers’ package inserts and documented in the Laboratory Information Management System (LIMS). The LIMS was programmed to automatically calculate and assess sample acceptability based on collection time, centrifugation time, transport duration and temperature, and storage conditions, as per manufacturer guidelines. Specimens that did not meet these criteria were rejected and excluded from testing.

Upon receipt, specimens were stored at the temperature at which they were shipped until testing. All samples included in the analysis met the manufacturers’ specimen requirements and were tested according to the respective package inserts. Specimens that did not meet these requirements were rejected for testing.

All four laboratories were certified by Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA) and held FDA registrations, and each laboratory was audited on a routine basis by these agencies. Assay and instrument validations were designed to meet the requirements of the FDA and included installation, operational and performance qualification studies. Testing protocols were standardized across laboratories and followed identical Standard Operating Procedures (SOP), which were derived verbatim from the manufacturers' package inserts, in accordance with FDA regulations. Per the FDA, test protocols cannot be modified, changed, or altered. Quality control was built into each test run and results were documented in the LIMS. In addition, the LIMS was programmed to review both the QC criteria and result values to indicate a passing or failing test run. Comparisons were performed on instrumentation (automated testing platforms, plate washers, plate readers) in instances in which laboratories have platform redundancies. These comparisons took place on a semi-annual basis to ensure that results were identical despite which instrument was being used. All manufacturer preventative maintenance for instrumentation was performed as prescribed and documented. All laboratories participated in Proficiency Testing surveys from external agencies to demonstrate competency in all testing areas.

Serological testing was performed using either manually performed Enzyme-Linked Immunoassays (EIAs) or automated Chemiluminescent Microparticle Immunoassays (CMIAs). EIAs were sourced from BioRad (1000 Alfred Nobel Drive, Hercules, CA) and Quidel Ortho (9975 Summers Ridge Road, San Diego, CA) while CMIAs were run on the Alinity s platform (Abbott, 100 Abbott Park Road Abbott Park, IL); refer to Table 1 for assay descriptions. Data were extracted from the LIMS for the period 2021–2025. During this time, panels of tests were ordered by Tissue and Cornea Banks for each potential deceased donor. As this was a retrospective analysis, EIA and CMIA assays were not performed concurrently but during distinct and sequential timeframes. Manual EIA testing was performed between 2021–2024, prior to the implementation of CMIA. Following the transition to CMIA testing in 2024 and the discontinuation of EIA as a primary test method, results from CMIA assays (2024–2025) were included in this study. It is noteworthy that fewer specimens were tested for HBsAg using the automated CMIA platform compared to other analytes. This was due to the laboratories’ decision to revert to manual EIA testing for HBsAg after observing elevated reactivity rates with the CMIA method.

When CMIA testing was implemented, increases in the reactive rates of certain analytes were observed. The historical EIA data provides context for these changes and shows the continuity of reactive rate surveillance efforts.

In the United States, both NAT and serology tests are required for HBV, HCV and HIV to determine deceased donor eligibility. All serological results were paired with corresponding NAT results for HIV-1/2, HCV, and HBV to assess assay concordance, except for HTLV-I/II, for which no FDA-approved NAT exists.

Ethical approval was not required for this study, as all data were collected retrospectively and derived from routine infectious disease screening conducted as part of the standard donor evaluation process. Informed consent for such testing was obtained at the time of donation. The dataset contained no identifiable information, and there was no risk of compromising donor identity.

Results

Reactive Rates for EIA and CMIA Serology Methodologies in Post-Mortem Specimens

The implementation of CMIA technology enabled automation of anti-HCV, anti-HBc, HBsAg, anti-HTLV-I/II, and HIV-1/2 serologies, replacing previously used manual EIAs. Historical reactive rates for EIA-based testing had been well established for laboratories in this study. After the transition to CMIA testing, HBsAg reactivity increased significantly from 2.15% (3,192 reactive donors out of 148,423) with EIA to 6.35% (811 reactive donors out of 12,765) with CMIA. Pre-Analytic Factors and Reactivity

To determine whether pre-analytical processing could reduce HBsAg reactivity, a subset of post-mortem blood specimens was centrifuged prior to transport to testing laboratories. This intervention, however, had no measurable effect on the HBsAg reactive rate, suggesting that centrifugation alone does not mitigate the observed increase in reactivity.

To evaluate the potential impact of hemolysis on automated serologic assay performance, we assessed the degree of hemolysis in post-mortem specimens using the Hemolysis Reference Palette (Reference Tool to Determine Hemolysis Status 2025). Hemolysis can introduce intracellular components from red blood cells into serum potentially interfering with assay performance. Among 150 specimens tested, no correlation was observed between the degree of hemolysis and the frequency of reactive results, suggesting that hemolysis did not contribute significantly to reactivity in this sample set tested.

Discussion

As traditional manual EIA assay options dwindle for deceased donor screening, laboratories performing testing in this clinical setting must adapt to newer, automated testing methodologies. EIAs, while historically effective, have several limitations in clinical testing. Their manual nature increases the risk of human error and variability, particularly in tasks such as pipetting, assay timing, and plate washing. EIAs also offer longer turnaround times compared to automated platforms. Automation offers clear advantages, including increased productivity, quality, and safety, and positions laboratories to meet growth demands while maintaining high standards of performance. Automated platforms enhance quality control by minimizing tedious, repetitive tasks and reducing the likelihood of errors caused by manual intervention. Automated systems provide consistent, reproducible results, enhancing the overall reliability of the testing process. Additionally, automated systems improve safety by minimizing technician exposure to potentially infectious specimens.

CMIA offers superior analytical performance compared to traditional EIA (CLSI 2018; McPherson and Pincus 2016). Multiple studies have confirmed this advantage. For example, a comparative evaluation of the automated Abbott CMIA and conventional EIAs for syphilis screening in Chinese blood donors demonstrated higher sensitivity and specificity with CMIA (Fu et al. 2020). Similarly, a head-to-head comparison for α-fetoprotein detection found that microparticle-based chemiluminescence immunoassays provided a broader dynamic range, enhanced sensitivity, and improved precision compared to colorimetric ELISA (Zhang et al. 2012). These performance differences have important clinical implications, particularly in the context of deceased donor screening. The trade-offs between sensitivity and specificity have important clinical implications, particularly in deceased donor screening. CMIA detects light emission, which allows for greater sensitivity than EIA colorimetric detection. This heightened sensitivity improves the ability to detect early seroconversion. However, it can also result in the detection of non-specific antibody reactivity, especially in post-mortem samples whereas hemolysis is more common. Although CMIA generally provides higher specificity due to optimized antigen presentation and lower background noise, specificity can be compromised due to pre-analytical variables in the deceased donor setting.

Our findings demonstrate a significant rise in reactive serology results, particularly for HBsAg and HIV-1/2, following the implementation of CMIA-based automated testing. These increased reactive rates were not mirrored by corresponding NAT positivity. The advent of NAT decreased the window period for HIV, HBV, and HCV (White et al. 2019). HIV NAT decreased the window period to 6–11 d compared to 17–22 d with serology testing. The window period for HCV NAT is 3–5 d compared to 70 d for serological tests. HBV NAT window period is 20–22 d versus 35–44 d for antibody tests. As a result, the residual risk of window-period transmission is exceedingly low. Nonetheless, current United States regulations still rely heavily on serology, and lack of confirmatory testing in the United States limits the ability to differentiate true positives from false positives. This loss of donors due to potentially false negative serology results is significant for the Tissue industry and more importantly, donor families are impacted who, in honoring their loved ones’ wishes to donate, are ultimately denied that opportunity.

In our dataset, a sizable portion of donors who tested reactive by CMIA were non-reactive by NAT, specifically for HBsAg, representing a substantial loss of transplantable tissues. It is likely that a substantial number of these cases represent non-viremic individuals or biologically false-positive results, resulting in the unnecessary exclusion of viable donors. While it is valid to compare serology and NAT results, it is critical to recognize that these assays detect different markers; serologic assays identify antigens or antibodies indicating immune response or past exposure, whereas NAT detects viral nucleic acid indicative of active infection. As a result, discrepancies between these tests are not inherently unexpected. However, in the context of deceased donor screening in the United States, such discrepancies carry substantial consequences due to the regulatory framework governing Human Cells, Tissues, and Cellular and Tissue-Based Products (HCT/Ps). Under FDA regulations (21 CFR Part 1271), the initial result from an FDA-licensed donor screening assay is considered final for determining donor eligibility. Donors with reactive results, regardless of NAT findings or the availability of confirmatory testing, must be deemed ineligible. Retesting the same sample is not permitted, and once a test is reactive or invalid, the associated tissues must remain ineligible for transplantation (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2007). This policy, while designed to maximize safety, does not allow for clinical interpretation or resolution of potentially false-positive results.

Supplemental or confirmatory tests may lack sensitivity in early infection stages. Historically, the HCV recombinant immunoblot assay (RIBA) served as a confirmatory test for anti-HCV seropositivity (Getchell et al. 2013). Its discontinuation in 2013 left a significant gap in diagnostic specificity for HCV screening. The absence of FDA-licensed confirmatory assays complicates the interpretation of reactive results, particularly in deceased donor testing where follow-up samples are not available. As a result, false-positive findings carry greater consequence, leading to the unnecessary deferral of otherwise eligible donors.

This study had some limitations, inherent to its retrospective nature. Several potential confounding variables, including post-mortem interval, time to specimen centrifugation, and donor age, could not be controlled. The laboratories are not required collect the time between circulatory death and blood collection for specimen testing requirements, which limits the ability to evaluate the impact of post-mortem interval on test reactivity or sample integrity. Additionally, while centrifugation is required prior to nucleic acid testing (NAT), there is no standardized requirement for centrifugation of post-mortem blood specimens before serologic testing by EIA or CMIA methods. Variability in sample collection protocols, and the timing of centrifugation may influence assay performance. The lack of standardized pre-analytical specimen handling practices across tissue recovery organizations adds complexity to the assessment of reactivity rates and highlights the need for further investigation into the impact of pre-analytical variables on deceased donor infectious disease screening.

Of the estimated 40,000 tissue donors in the United States annually (NTRUS Results Reveal Trends in Tissue 2018), positive infectious disease results are historically one of the leading causes of post-donation rejection of deceased donor tissues and corneas. In the absence of a national tissue/cornea donor data repository, the exact number of donors lost due to reactive results is unknown. Based on our experience, approximately 5.2% of tissue donors are ineligible to donate annually due to infectious disease findings. This occurs despite extensive pre-recovery review of donor records and questionnaires to ensure freedom from risk factors and clinical symptoms for relevant communicable disease agents or diseases (Kohmer et al. 2022). The increase in reactivity rates observed with CMIA testing further exacerbates this challenge. For example, the rise in HBsAg positivity alone could eliminate an estimated 680 additional donors annually, translating to over 71,000 tissue grafts lost from potential transplantation. This represents a major loss to both patients and the donation community.

While this is the first study to report on the use of CMIA for deceased donor screening within the United States, previous studies outside the U.S. have examined CMIA performance in post-mortem specimens, primarily by comparing pre- and post-mortem sample concordance. Kohmer et al. (2022) evaluated deceased donor screening reactivity using the Abbott Alinity i and observed an 8.8% HBsAg discordance rate between pre- and post-mortem samples from cornea donors spiked with positive serum. Notably, several post-mortem positive results could not be confirmed by supplemental testing and were attributed to sample artifacts or autolysis, suggesting a potential for false positivity in post-mortem settings. Among the various CMIAs tested, the HBsAg assay showed the lowest agreement (91.2%) between sample types. Other studies using the Abbott Architect platform found no significant differences in reactivity between pre- and post-mortem samples for HIV Ag/Ab, anti-HCV, anti-HBc, HBsAg, HTLV I/II, and Syphilis assays when samples were spiked with positive serum (Baleriola et al. 2012; Kok et al. 2020). However, these studies did not evaluate CMIA performance against historical EIA results in real-world donor screening scenarios. Furthermore, the platforms evaluated are not currently approved for deceased donor screening in the United States. In contrast, our study provides a comparative analysis of EIA and CMIA in the post-mortem clinical setting, highlighting the practical consequences of increased CMIA reactivity on donor eligibility under current U.S. regulations.

While the transition to automated CMIA platforms represents a necessary evolution in the U.S. laboratory setting, the unintended consequences, specifically, increased donor ineligibility due to reactive serology results, must be carefully addressed. As testing technology continues to advance, regulatory frameworks and clinical guidelines must also evolve to ensure they reflect current capabilities, including the sensitivity of NAT and the limitations of serology in post-mortem specimens. A collaborative approach involving laboratories, regulatory bodies, and the Tissue community is critical to developing strategies that protect recipient safety while honoring the intent and generosity of donors and their families. Ultimately, striking a balance between test sensitivity, specificity, and clinical relevance will be essential to preserving the integrity of the donation process and ensuring that valuable allografts reach the patients who need them most. The goal continues to be ensuring patient safety and maintaining this at the highest standards, while maximizing the gift of donation.
