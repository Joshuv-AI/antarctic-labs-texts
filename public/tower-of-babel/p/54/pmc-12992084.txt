Improvement of the quality and flavor profiles of persimmon vinegar by bioaugmented inoculation with Lactobacillus acidophilus, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and Wickerhamomyces anomalus
Authors: Ningbo Wan, Mengyang Wang, Lijuan Wang, Dahong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNatural fermentation of persimmon vinegar often results in limited flavor profiles. To address this, a bioaugmentation strategy was employed by co-inoculating two strains of lactic acid bacteria and one aroma-producing yeast. This approach led to progressive increases in the contents of total acid, vitamin C, polypeptide, and polyphenols. Compared to the traditionally fermented control group (TF), the bioaugmented group (BF) exhibited a significant increase of 11.54% in lactic acid content. Additionally, levels of total free amino acids and umami amino acids rose by 18.71% and 27.85%, respectively. HS-SPME-GC–MS analysis showed that the total concentrations of aroma compounds and ester compounds in the BF group increased by 25.19% and 24.42%, respectively. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) identified seven key compounds responsible for flavor differentiation. Sensory evaluation and electronic tongue analysis further confirmed that bioaugmentation significantly enhanced umami taste, richness, and overall acceptability of the vinegar.Keywords: Persimmon vinegar, Bioaugmentation fermentation, Dynamic changes, Nutritional components, Aroma substances

Introduction

Persimmon (Diospyros kaki Thunb.) is an economically important fruit widely cultivated around the world. It is rich in flavonoids, polyphenols, and soluble dietary fiber, which contribute to blood lipid-lowering and antioxidant effects. The peel contains abundant tannins and can serve as a source of alpha-glucosidase inhibitors to help manage postprandial blood glucose levels (Zhao et al., 2025). In addition, persimmons provide substantial amounts of sugars, various vitamins, and essential minerals and trace elements. Current applications of persimmons include the production of wine, dried fruit, and leaf tea. Notably, persimmons also serve as an excellent raw material for vinegar brewing. The fermentation of persimmon vinegar not only enables the efficient utilization of persimmon resources and diversifies vinegar products, but also enhances the economic value of the fruit.

Persimmon vinegar is an acidic condiment that offers the combined health benefits of both fruit and vinegar (Bordiga et al., 2023). Currently, persimmon vinegar is typically produced through spontaneous fermentation, and its quality is influenced by multiple factors, including raw materials, brewing environment, and fermentation conditions. Among these, microbial diversity plays a critically important role (Bordiga et al., 2023; Zhang, Wang, et al., 2024). During fermentation, microorganisms convert the nutrients present in raw materials into volatile and non-volatile compounds, such as alcohols, aldehydes, phenols, organic acids, and amino acids, thereby imparting vinegar with its characteristic flavor (Wang et al., 2024). Yeast metabolizes to produce alcohols and esters, while acid-producing bacteria such as acetic acid bacteria (AAB) and lactic acid bacteria (LAB) generate acetic acid, lactic acid, and various other organic acids. These compounds contribute to a buffering system that balances the taste profile of vinegar and reduces its pungency (Bordiga et al., 2023). However, natural fermentation presents several drawbacks for persimmon vinegar production, including uncontrollable microbial sources that may introduce biotoxin risks, imbalances in the ratio of functional microorganisms, poor acidity control leading to fluctuations in acid content, monotonous and unbalanced flavor profiles, extended fermentation periods and low production efficiency, and inconsistent quality across batches. These severely restricts the development of the modern persimmon vinegar industry (Zhang, Wang, et al., 2024).

The quality of vinegar can be modulated through various strategies, including microbial inoculation, optimization of production parameters (e.g., raw material selection and aging time), and the application of physical processing technologies such as sonication and microwave treatment (Zhang, Zhang, et al., 2024). Among these, bioaugmentation has emerged as an effective approach to improving the quality of traditional fermented foods (De Vuyst et al., 2023; Sengun et al., 2022). For instance, sequential inoculation with dominant microorganisms such as Lactobacillus helveticus and Acetobacter pasteurianus improved both fermentation efficiency and flavor of cereal vinegar (Zhang et al., 2025). Similarly, bioaugmentation with AAB and LAB significantly increased acetoin concentration and improved the overall flavor profile of Zhenjiang vinegar (Lu et al., 2018). In Shanxi aged vinegar, bioaugmentation together with Pichia manshurica boosted the production of acids and esters, further enriching its sensory characteristics (Zhang et al., 2017).

In our previous study, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum CGMCC 24035 and Lactobacillus acidophilus R2 were isolated from persimmon vinegar fermentation broth (Jiao et al., 2023). Additionally, Wickerhamomyces anomalus ZX-1, an aroma-producing yeast, was obtained from Daqu which serves as a traditional starter culture used in the solid-state fermentation of foods such as baijiu and vinegar (Wang, Wang, Wang, et al., 2022). In this study, these three strains were co-inoculated as a bioaugmentation strategy in persimmon vinegar fermentation to improve product quality. The dynamic changes in physicochemical properties and flavor profiles during fermentation were systematically investigated. This work contributes to a deeper understanding of the role of bioaugmentation in persimmon vinegar production and provides a foundation for quality improvement and industrial application.

Material and methods

Microorganisms, cultivation conditions and seed broth preparation

Lactiplantibacillus plantarum CGMCC 24035, Lactobacillus acidophilus R2 and Wickerhamomyces anomalus ZX-1 were used for the persimmon vinegar bioaugmentation fermentation. For the preparation of the seed broth, L. plantarum CGMCC 24035 and L. acidophilus R2 were each inoculated into 500 mL flask containing 250 mL MRS medium (Aobox, Beijing Province, China) and cultivated at 37 °C for 24 h, respectively. W. anomalus ZX-1 was inoculated into 500 mL flask containing 250 mL YPD medium (peptone, 20 g/L, glucose, 20 g/L, yeast extract, 10 g/L), and cultivated at 28 °C for 24 h. Microbial cells were collected by centrifugation at 9600g for 10 min and 4 °C, and washed twice with sterile water. The resulting cell pellets were resuspended in sterile water to prepare a bacterial suspension, and the concentrations of L. plantarum CGMCC 24035, L. acidophilus R2, and W. anomalus ZX-1 were adjusted to 1 × 109 CFU/mL, 1 × 109 CFU/mL, and 1 × 108 CFU/mL, respectively.

Fermentation of persimmon vinegar

The persimmon vinegar fermentation was carried out at Luoyang Persimmon King Vinegar Industry Co., Ltd., located in Luanchuan County, Henan Province, China. Approximately 100 kg of crushed persimmons were transferred into sterilized 300 L fermentation vats, a total of six vats were prepared. The vats were disinfected with 75% alcohol prior to use. To initiate fermentation, 0.01% (w/w) of highly active dry Saccharomyces cerevisiae powder (Angel Yeast Co., Ltd., China) was added to the vats. The vats were then covered with the breathable straw lids and placed in an open-air environment for natural fermentation. Additional acrylic lids were prepared to protect the fermentation broth in case of rain or snow. After 20 days of fermentation, three vats were inoculated with a mixed suspension of L. plantarum CGMCC 24035, L. acidophilus R2, and W. anomalus ZX-1 at a ratio of 0.25%:0.25%:0.5% (v/w) to perform bioaugmented fermentation, designated as the BF group. The remaining three vats received an equal volume of sterile water and served as the traditionally fermented control group (TF group). After the fermentation for three months under static conditions, manual stirring was performed once per week. The fermentation process of persimmon vinegar spans from November to May of the following year. The average local temperatures during the six months of fermentation were 9.8 °C, 5.1 °C, 10.2 °C, 16.5 °C, 19.2 °C, and 28.6 °C, respectively. At 30, 60, 90, 120, and 150 days after bioaugmentation, 100 g samples were taken from the upper, middle, and lower layers of each vat, and mixed in sterile sampling bags for further analysis. The resulting samples from the bioaugmented group were labeled BF-30, BF-60, BF-90, BF-120 and BF-150, respectively, while those from the control group were designated TF-30, TF-60, TF-90, TF-120 and TF-150, respectively.

Determination of pH, the titratable acidity (TA), reducing sugar (RS), soluble solids, vitamin C (Vc), and soluble protein content

The samples were centrifuged at 9600 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was directly used for pH measurement with a pH meter. TA was determined with 0.1 mol/L NaOH titration, and expressed as acetic acid equivalents (Andreson et al., 2022). RS content was assayed using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method. The soluble solids content was determined using a refractometer. The content of Vc was determined using 2,6-dichlorophenolindophenol method. The polypeptide concentration was measured by Bradford method.

Determination of ethanol content

The ethanol content in persimmon vinegar was analyzed by a Gas Chromatography (GC, 7890 A, Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA). The GC system was equipped with an AT.LZP-930 capillary column (25 m × 530 μm × 1 μm, Lanzhou Institute of Chemical Physics, China) and a flame ionization detector (FID) maintained at 280 °C. The gas flow rates were set as follows: hydrogen (H₂) was 35 mL/min, air was 300 mL/min, and helium carrier gas was 2 mL/min. The temperature program was initialized at 50 °C (held for 3 min), then increased at a rate of 10 °C/min to 90 °C (held for 2 min). A sample volume of 1 μL was injected with the inlet temperature set to 280 °C. The ethanol content in the samples was calculated based on the ethanol standard curve.

Determination of total polyphenols, total flavonoids, and antioxidant capacity

Total polyphenols content was determined by the Folin-Ciocalteu method. Briefly, 100 μL of sample was mixed with 4 mL of 60% (v/v) ethanol and 0.3 mL of 5% (w/v) NaNO₂. After 5 min of reaction, 0.3 mL of 10% (w/v) Al(NO₃)₃ was added, and the mixture was allowed to react for an additional 6 min. The reaction was then terminated by adding 4 mL of 1 mol/L NaOH, and the volume was adjusted to 10 mL with 60% (v/v) ethanol. Absorbance was measured at 510 nm using a spectrophotometer (UV-2100, Unico Instrument Co., China). The total polyphenols content was quantitatively expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per mL. Total flavonoids were analyzed by aluminum nitrite colorimetric method. The sample (100 μL) was mixed with 0.65 mL of 0.8 mol/L Folin–Ciocalteu reagent and 4.0 mL of 20% (w/v) Na₂CO₃ solution. The mixture was then diluted to 25 mL with distilled water and incubated in a water bath at 60 °C for 60 min. Absorbance was measured at 720 nm. and the content was quantitatively expressed as mg rutin equivalents (RE) per mL. The determination of superoxide anion, DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging activity were according to the previous method (Wang, Wang, Cao, et al., 2022).

Sensory evaluation

Sensory evaluation was performed by a trained panel of 20 panelists (10 male and 10 female, aged 20–30). They were from Food Fermentation laboratory of Henan University of Science and Technology. Prior to the evaluation, the panelists underwent specific training on persimmon vinegar sensory to ensure consistent and accurate evaluation. During the formal assessment, each 10 mL sample was placed in a clear plastic cup, and the panelists were asked to assess the taste, flavor, texture, umami and overall acceptability. Each attribute was scored on a scale from 10 (highest acceptability) to 0 (lowest acceptability). The detailed criteria used for sensory evaluation are provided in Table S1 of the Supplementary Materials. To minimize carry-over effects, panelists rinsed with water and rested for 5 min between samples. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Henan University of Science and Technology (IRB numbers: 4103050043305). The informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the study.

Electronic tongue detection

An Electronic Tongue Analyzer (TS-5000Z, Insent Inc., Japan) was used to evaluate the sensory differences of six basic tastes (sour, sweet, bitter, salty, umami, astringency) between BF-150 group and TF-150 group. Before each measurement, the sensors were equilibrated by a solution containing 30 mmol/L KCl and 0.3 mmol/L tartaric acid. 30 mL of persimmon vinegar were placed in the taste analyzer for each sample. Each sample was performed with three replications.

Determination of organic acids (OAs) and free amino acids (FAAs)

The samples were centrifuged at 6750 ×g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. OAs was determined by an HPLC system (Agilent Technologies, USA) with a TSK gel ODS-100 V column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm), following a previously described method (Healy et al., 2024). For the FAAs determination, the samples were mixed with 10% sulfosalicylic acid at a 4:1 ratio and allowed to react for 60 min. The mixture was first centrifuged at 6750 ×g for 15 min. After removing the pellet, the supernatant was subsequently centrifuged at 11400 ×g for an additional 5 min. The supernatant was diluted at a 1:4 ratio, and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. The contents of FAAs were analyzed by an Amino Acid analyzer (A300, Membrapure GmbH, Germany).

Headspace solid-phase microextraction gas chromatography-mass spectrometer (HS-SPME-GC–MS) analysis

A 6 mL sample was transferred to a headspace vial, followed by the addition of 10 μL of 2-octanol (100 mg/L) as an internal standard and 1 g of sodium chloride. The vial was equilibrated at 40 °C for 10 min, and volatile compounds were extracted by HS-SPME using a 50/30 μm DVB/CAR/PDMS microextraction fiber (Supelco, USA) for 40 min. After extraction, the fiber was thermally desorbed in the injector port of a Gas Chromatograph-Triple Quadrupole Tandem Mass Spectrometer (TSQ9000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) for 5 min at 250 °C. GC was carried out on a TG-WAXMS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with helium as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1 mL/min. The oven temperature program was set as follows: initial hold at 35 °C for 5 min; ramped to 180 °C at 4 °C/min and hold for 2 min; then further increased to 220 °C at 5 °C/min with a final hold of 5 min. Mass spectrometric analysis was conducted in electron ionization (EI) mode at 70 eV, with the ion source maintained at 280 °C. Full-scan mass spectra were acquired over a range of m/z 33–450. Volatile compounds were identified by comparing their mass spectra with those in the NIST 2017 library. Compounds with a matching score greater than 80% were considered tentatively identified. Identification was further confirmed by comparing the retention index (RI) calculated using a series of n-alkanes (C7–C40) with those reported in the NIST Chemistry WebBook, and compounds with RI deviations exceeding ±50 were excluded. Relative quantification of volatile compounds was performed based on the peak area ratio of each compound to the internal standard (2-octanol). Odor activity values (OAVs) were calculated by dividing the concentration of each compound by its corresponding odor threshold reported in the literature (Burdock, 2010).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with three replications, and the results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Significant differences between the data were determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Differences with p < 0.05 and p < 0.01 were considered statistically significant. The statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS (version 18.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). SIMCA14.1 (Umetric, Umea, Sweden) was used to establish principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) models.

Results and discussion

Changes in total acidity, pH, RS, ethanol, polypeptide, and soluble solids

Acidity is a very important indicator of vinegar, typically assessed through pH and total acidity. In this study, the changes in total acidity and pH during the fermentation of persimmon vinegar were shown in Fig.1A and 1B. During the initial fermentation stage, both pH and total acidity changed slowly. As fermentation progressed, total acidity increased slowly during the initial stage, followed by a sharp rise from the mid- to late-fermentation stage. By day 150, the final total acidity reached 42.97 ± 2.08 g/L in the BF group and 37.49 ± 2.37 g/L in the TF group, with a statistically significant difference between the two groups (p < 0.01). The total acidity was improved by 14.62% after bioaugmentation fermentation. Both groups met the Chinese standard for total acidity in vinegar (≥3.5 g/100 mL). The pH gradually decreased to 3.19 in BF group and 3.28 in TF group, with no significant difference observed (p > 0.05). These results may be attributed to the inoculation of W. anomalus ZX-1, which likely increased the abundance of yeast in the microbial community, thereby enhancing ethanol production and subsequently promoting acetic acid synthesis. Meanwhile, the introduced L. plantarum CGMCC 24035 and L. acidophilus R2 possessed inherent acid-producing capacities, contributing to the rise in total acid content and the reduction of pH in the fermentation broth.

Dynamic changes of total acid(A), pH(B), reducing sugar content (C), ethanol content (D), soluble solids content (E) and polypeptide content (F) during persimmon vinegar fermentation. BF: bioaugmentation fermentation group; TF: traditional fermentation group.

The changes in RS and ethanol content during the fermentation were presented in Fig.1C and 1D. The RS content initially increased, likely due to low temperature slowing yeast metabolism, resulting in the release of sugars from persimmons exceeding microbial consumption. As fermentation proceeded and the proliferation of yeast, RS was rapidly utilized to produce ethanol, resulting in the increase of ethanol content (Qian et al., 2023). Subsequently, AAB further oxidized ethanol to produce acetic acid, leading to a rapid decrease in ethanol content and a rapid increase in acetic acid content. Meanwhile, LAB utilized RS to grow and produce flavor compounds such as organic acids and amino acids (Liu et al., 2025). In the later stage, rising temperature and the growth of microorganisms led to further consumption of carbon sources, resulting in a decrease in RS content. Ethanol, mainly produced through yeast metabolism, increased to 4.68% (60 d) in BF group and 4.32% (60 d) in the TF group, then declined to 0.39% and 0.32% at the end of fermentation. Throughout the fermentation process, no significant differences were observed in RS or ethanol content between the BF and TF groups (p > 0.05). Based on the research results, the core metabolic relationship centers on the conversion of sugars to ethanol and then to acids. Initially, RS is utilized by yeast to produce ethanol, which subsequently rises. AAB then oxidize this ethanol into acetic acid, causing a sharp increase in total acidity and a corresponding decrease in pH as the ethanol declines. LAB also contribute to acid production directly from RS.

Soluble solids, encompassing all water-dissolved substances such as sugars, organic acids, proteins, and amino acids, are a key indicator of food quality. In this study, the contents of soluble solids in the persimmon vinegar were shown in Fig.1E. The soluble solids content exhibited an initial decrease followed by an increase, ultimately reaching 3.47 ± 0.13 mg/L in the BF group and 4.40 ± 0.39 mg/L in the TF group, with a significant difference observed between the two groups (p < 0.05). Throughout the fermentation process, the soluble solids content in the BF group remained consistently lower than that in the TF group. This may be attributed to the inoculation of L. plantarum CGMCC 24035, L. acidophilus R2 and W. anomalus ZX-1, which enhanced microbial consumption of sugars during the early stage. In the later stage, accelerated microbial growth and the secretion of enzymes such as glycosidases contributed to the breakdown of plant tissue structures, leading to the release of intracellular sugars. As the release rate exceeded the microbial utilization rate, the soluble solids content consequently exhibited a trend of initial decline followed by an increase. During persimmon vinegar fermentation, proteases secreted by yeasts, LAB, and AAB break down proteins in the persimmon to generate small molecular peptides, while the lysis of microbial cells themselves also releases peptide substances, and some of these small peptides exhibit beneficial bioactivities (Zhang, Wang, et al., 2024). The Fig.1F showed the dynamic change of polypeptide content throughout the process. In the early and middle stages, the content was with a slow rise, and a more rapid increase in the later stage, reaching final values of 33.24 ± 2.31 g/L in the BF group and 30.89 ± 1.51 g/L in the TF group, respectively. No significant difference (p > 0.05) in polypeptide content was detected between the two groups during the entire fermentation period. The soluble solids content reflects a balance between microbial consumption and the later enzymatic breakdown of plant tissues. Polypeptide accumulation results from the microbial breakdown of proteins and from microbial cell lysis. Fig. 1 also showed that the variation ranges of above determined indicators in persimmon vinegar from the TF group were all smaller than those in the BF group, indicating that the bioaugmentation process successfully reduced the quality fluctuation of persimmon vinegar, leading to a more consistent fermentation process compared to the traditional fermentation group.

Changes in Vc, total flavonoids, total polyphenols, and antioxidant capacity

Persimmon contains are rich in various bioactive compounds, including flavonoids, polyphenols, VC, and polypeptide. The fermentation process facilitates the release of these compounds from plant cells and promotes their biotransformation into smaller molecules, thereby enhancing their bioavailability. Fig.2A showed that the Vc content increased continuously throughout the fermentation process. At the end of the fermentation, the content reached 32.2 ± 1.47 mg/L in BF group and 25.27 ± 0.73 mg/L in TF group, with a statistically significant difference between the two groups (p < 0.01). Bioaugmentation fermentation resulted in a 27.43% increase in Vc content. The VC in persimmon vinegar is primarily derived from the raw persimmon material. The enhanced Vc content in the BF group can be attributed to the combined effects of organic acid accumulation and microbial enzyme activity, which more effectively disrupted plant tissue and cellular structures, thereby promoting the release of Vc from the raw material into the fermentation broth.

Dynamic changes of VC (A), total polyphenols (B), total flavonoids (C), DPPH (D), ABTS (E) and superoxide anion (F) during persimmon vinegar fermentation. BF: bioaugmentation fermentation group; TF: traditional fermentation group.

The changes in total flavonoids and total polyphenols contents were presented in Fig.2B and 2C. Microbial metabolism plays a key role in the formation or the release of phenols. In the early fermentation stage, polyphenols leached from the persimmon peel were naturally degraded due to oxidation. However, with the initiation of alcoholic fermentation, enzymes such as β-glucosidase and amylase produced by yeast metabolism partially degraded the persimmon peels. This led to the release of bound phenolics from the cells and the subsequent degradation from large polymeric molecules into smaller phenolic forms by various enzymes, resulting in a gradual increase in total polyphenol content (Huang et al., 2024). Concurrently, the substantial production of alcohols and esters by yeast also facilitated the extraction of phenolic and flavonoid compounds (Li et al., 2024). During the acetic acid fermentation stage, OAs generated by AAB cleaved the glycosidic bonds of some flavonoids, releasing additional bound flavonoids from the cellular matrix (Liang et al., 2024). Moreover, in the later stages of fermentation, a marked rise in environmental temperature, combined with vigorous microbial activity, accelerated the breakdown of persimmon tissue and intensified microbial utilization of the pulp. These factors collectively contributed to a substantial rise in the contents of both flavonoids and polyphenols, reaching final concentrations of 0.38 ± 0.02 g/L (BF) and 0.43 ± 0.02 g/L (TF) for total flavonoids, and 0.78 ± 0.03 g/L (BF) and 0.69 ± 0.034 g/L (TF) for total polyphenols. The polyphenol content differed significantly between the two groups (p < 0.05), whereas no significant difference was observed in flavonoid content (p > 0.05). During fermentation, microorganisms produce some enzymes, including cellulase, xylanase, pectinase, and glucosidase, and they can reduce large molecular compounds to smaller polyphenols, thus increasing the total polyphenols content, and a large number of acids, alcohols, and esters from microbial metabolism can result in the release of insoluble bound phenols (Zhang et al., 2020).

Natural flavonoids and phenols in persimmon possess significant antioxidant properties. Furthermore, it has been reported that traditional persimmon vinegar contains higher levels of phenolic compounds and consequently demonstrate superior ABTS and DPPH radical-scavenging activities compared to other vinegars, suggesting that the fermentation process can enhance the bioactivity of products (Jang et al., 2015). In this study, the changes in antioxidant capacity were illustrated in Fig.2D, 2E and 2F. The DPPH radical scavenging activity increased slowly in the early fermentation stage, exhibited a pattern of initial decrease followed by an increase during the mid- to late-stage, and reached 91.87 ± 1.34% in BF group and 92.75 ± 1.66% in TF group by the end of fermentation. These values were markedly higher than those reported in balsamic, apple, lemon, and malt vinegars, and the specialty vinegars derived from hot pepper, dandelion, pine needle, green tea, and pine mushroom (Yun et al., 2016). The ABTS radical scavenging activity decreased slowly in the early stage, and showed an increasing trend in the later stage, with the final values reaching 71.37 ± 5.75% (BF) and 77.31 ± 5.3% (TF). The change in superoxide anion scavenging activity followed a trend similar to that of ABTS, peaking at the end of fermentation at 87.95 ± 2.43% (BF) and 83.81 ± 3.27% (TF). Throughout the fermentation process, no significant difference was observed in the antioxidant capacity between the BF and TF groups (p > 0.05).

Based on the results, the contents of bioactive substances, including Vc, total flavonoids, and total polyphenols, gradually increased in both the TF and BF groups as fermentation progressed. Correspondingly, the overall antioxidant capacity, as measured by ABTS, DPPH, and superoxide anion scavenging activities, also exhibited an upward trend, suggesting a positive correlation between the levels of these bioactive compounds and antioxidant activity. However, during the later stages of fermentation (at 90 or 120 days), the antioxidant capacity of the persimmon vinegar declined significantly, even as the content of bioactive substances continued to rise. This finding implied that other components in the fermentation broth (e.g. organic acids, vitamin and other natural products) in the fermentation broth may exert a notable influence on antioxidant capacity.

Analysis of organic acids (OAs) and free amino acids (FAAs) changes

The flavor substances in fruit vinegar can be broadly categorized into volatile compounds and non-volatile compounds. Volatile flavor components include esters, alcohols, acids, aldehydes, ketones, and heterocyclic compounds. Non-volatile components mainly include nitrogen-containing compounds (including peptides, free amino acids, nucleotides, etc.) and non‑nitrogen compounds (including reducing sugars, organic acids, metal ions, etc.). Among these, OAs are key determinants of vinegar taste and are mainly classified into volatile acids (e.g., acetic acid) and non-volatile acids (e.g., lactic acid, citric acid, malic acid, and tartaric acid). These acids are primarily formed through the cyclic metabolism of various microorganisms. The changes in the content of nine OAs during the fermentation of persimmon vinegar were shown in Fig.3. Among these nine OAs, acetic acid, lactic acid, and malic acid were present at relatively high concentrations, while the other six OAs were detected at lower levels. The total content of the nine OAs increased by 305.64% in BF group and 239.61% in TF group. After bioaugmentation, the OAs was improved by 13.64%, with a statistical difference between the two groups (p < 0.05). The increase in OAs content primarily stems from the synergistic action of the inoculated yeast and LAB. Specifically, yeast metabolism enhanced the supply of precursor substances for organic acid synthesis, while LAB directly contributed to organic acid production, collectively driving the accumulation of organic acids in the fermentation system (Sun et al., 2023).

Dynamic changes of organic acids contents during persimmon vinegar fermentation. BF: bioaugmentation fermentation group; TF: traditional fermentation group.

Due to the substantial proliferation of AAB during acetic acid fermentation, acetic acid was accumulated significantly from 3.79 ± 0.46 g/L and 3.89 ± 0.44 g/L to 32.81 ± 1.01 g/L and 27.47 ± 1.66 g/L in the BF and TF groups, respectively (Fig.3A). The BF group showed a 19.46% higher than the TF group, with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.05). The contents of oxalic acid, α-ketoglutaric acid, tartaric acid, and pyruvic acid increased in the early stage, then decreased in the later stage of the fermentation. Citric acid and succinic acid levels decreased in the early and middle stages, then increased in the later stage. Malic acid content increased slowly initially, rose sharply in the middle stage, and then decreased significantly. At the end of the fermentation, the contents of oxalic acid, tartaric acid, pyruvic acid, and malic acid in BF group were 14.89%, 31.24%, 9.30%, and 7.45% lower than those in TF group, respectively. In contrast, the contents of α-ketoglutaric acid, succinic acid, and citric acid in BF group were 20.88%, 18.38%, and 17.94% higher than those in TF group, respectively (Fig.3B-3I). Lactic acid content increased initially due to the addition of LAB which played an important role in flavor formation, including lactic acid and malic acid, resulting in the vinegar taste softer (Leal Maske et al., 2024). However, in the mid- to late-stage of the fermentation, the extensive accumulation of acetic acid from AAB growth inhibited LAB activity. Furthermore, as ethanol and sugars were gradually consumed, lactic acid began to be utilized as a carbon source, leading to a decline. Specifically, lactic acid content was 3.19 ± 0.19 g/L (150 d) in BF group and 2.86 ± 0.21 g/L (150 d) in the TF group, with an increase of 11.54% after bioaugmentation fermentation (p < 0.05). It can alleviate the pungency imparted by acetic acid in fermented vinegar, contributing a mellow, prolonged, and soft sensation with a lingering aftertaste (Liang et al., 2019). Malic acid provides a rich and persistent taste, while succinic acid, a metabolite of yeast, imparts a fresh and pleasantly sour flavor. Apart from acetic acid and lactic acid, most other OAs are intermediate metabolites of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and often utilized as precursors for other metabolic pathways, thus they do not accumulate extensively during fermentation (Wang, Wang, Cao, et al., 2022).

FAAs have diverse physiological functions and exhibit rich taste profiles with low threshold values. They not only contribute significantly to the flavor presentation of foods but also enhance the nutritional richness of vinegar (Wang et al., 2025). Different types of FAAs can undergo a series of reactions through the synergistic action of various microbial enzymes, yielding corresponding flavor characteristic amino acid derivatives. These metabolic pathways are a significant source of flavor in many fermented foods. The changes in FAAs content during the fermentation of persimmon vinegar were presented in Fig.4. The total content of FAAs in both groups exhibited a similar trend of initially decreasing and then increasing. The content of FAAs in BF group decreased from 1033.01 mg/L (BF-30) to 922.73 mg/L (BF-150). In the TF group, the content decreased from 865.18 mg/L (TF-30) to 773.31 mg/L (TF-150). The bioaugmentation improved FAAs content by 18.71%, with a statistically significant difference between the two groups (p < 0.01). The contents of Tau, Asp, Thr, Gly, (Cys)₂, and γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) increased significantly during fermentation. In contrast, the levels of Ser, Glu, Ala, Val, Met, Leu, Tyr, Phe, Arg, and Pro decreased markedly. At the final fermentation stage, the BF group exhibited significantly higher contents of Tau, Asp, Asn, Glu, Phe, GABA, and Arg compared to the TF group, with the increase of 14.64%, 21.94%, 13.95%, 96.20%, 253.99%, 91.01%, and 40.90%, respectively. In contrast, the contents of Gln, Gly, Ala, Val, Leu, His, and Trp were significantly lower in the BF-150 group, decreasing by 22.09%, 10.83%, 100.00%, 14.04%, 17.03%, 94.47%, and 100.00%, respectively, comparing to TF-150 group (Fig.4A). It is worth noting that the contribution of FAAs to the flavor of persimmon vinegar is not solely determined by their concentration, but is also significantly influenced by their respective threshold values. In this study, the OAVs of Asp, Met, GABA, Lys and Arg in the BF-150 group were greater than 1, whereas the OAVs of Asp, Met and Lys in the TF-150 group were greater than 1. Arg is considered an essential amino acid for infants, and its content reached 11.41 mg/L in the BF group. GABA, which reached 19.74 mg/L in the BF group, is known for its various physiological functions, including antihypertensive and antidepressant effects, diabetes prevention, and pain relief. In food flavor, Asp, Met, and Lys play distinct roles. Asp balances umami and sourness, while Met serves as a key precursor to volatile sulfur compounds, which can be converted into sulfur-containing substances such as methional and methanethiol through microbial metabolism. Lys is a typical bitter-tasting amino acid and is prone to develop an astringent taste (Zhu et al., 2025). These free amino acids exerted a noticeable influence on the flavor profiles of persimmon vinegar.

It is universally acknowledged that essential amino acids (EAA) play a critically important role in human physiological functions. After bioaugmentation, the EAA content increased by 21.79% (p < 0.01). Based on their taste characteristics, FAAs can be categorized into umami amino acids (UAA), sweet amino acids (SAA), and bitter amino acids (BAA), among which UAA and SAA are important contributors to the flavor of fermented foods (Shi et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 4B, although the proportional distribution of different amino acid categories was relatively similar between the two groups, their absolute contents differed markedly (Fig.4B). In the BF group, the proportions of BAA, SAA, and UAA changed from 36.84%, 29.20%, and 17.72% to 27.77%, 22.79%, and 19.87%, respectively. In the TF group, the proportions changed from 37.88%, 22.80%, and 29.33% to 28.31%, 21.40%, and 19.11%, respectively. The results showed that the proportion of BAA decreased. Compared with the TF-150 group, the BF-150 group exhibited increases of 20.83%,16.25%, and 27.85% in BAA, SAA, and UAA contents, respectively, with statistically significant differences between the two groups (p < 0.01). The previous report showed that the content of UAA and SAA in citrus vinegar was improved after biofortified fermentation (Yao et al., 2021). Thus, the nutritional value of persimmon vinegar can be improved after bioaugmentation fermentation.

A systematic analysis of the dynamic changes in OAs and FAAs during the fermentation of persimmon vinegar was conducted, and an OPLS-DA model was constructed. As shown in Fig.4C, the abundance of OAs and FAAs exhibited significant dynamic trends over fermentation time. The contents of acetic acid, lactic acid, Tau, Asp, Thr, Gly, (Cys)₂, and GABA gradually increased, while the levels of Ser, Glu, Ala, Val, Met, Leu, Tyr, Phe, Arg, Pro, and tartaric acid decreased progressively. Fig.4D showed the variable importance in projection (VIP) scores derived from the OPLS-DA model, where variables with VIP > 1.0 are typically considered significant contributors. Due to the substantially higher absolute content of OAs compared to FAAs in persimmon vinegar, FAAs contributed relatively less to the overall model. The results indicated that acetic acid, lactic acid, malic acid, succinic acid, and glutamic acid had the most significant impacts on the model. To assess model validity, 200 permutation tests were conducted (Fig. 4E), which confirmed a good fit with R2 = 0.956 and Q2 = −1.41, indicating no overfitting. The OPLS-DA biplot (Fig.4F) visually illustrated the relationships between samples and variables. Samples from 30, 60, and 90 days of fermentation clustered closely together, while those from 120 and 150 days were more distantly distributed, indicating significant evolution in the volatile flavor profile of persimmon vinegar. Early fermentation stages were strongly associated with amino acids such as Ile, Try, His, Tyr, and tartaric acid. Mid-fermentation (120 days) was primarily correlated with lactic acid, citric acid, α-ketoglutaric acid, Val, Leu, and pyruvic acid. Late fermentation stages showed high correlations with acetic acid, malic acid, Tau, Asp, GABA, and Lys.

Flavor composition analysis by HS-SPME-GC–MS

In this study, a total of 70 compounds were identified in persimmon vinegar using HS-SPME-GC–MS combined with mass spectral library matching and RI comparison. These included 20 alcohols, 26 esters, 12 acids, 9 aldehydes & ketones, and 3 other compounds (Fig.5A). Their corresponding RI were listed in Table S2 of Supplementary Material. Relative quantification of flavor compounds was performed using 2-octanol as an internal standard. The changes in the variety and content of these flavor substances at different fermentation stages were shown in Fig.5B. Among them, esters constituted the most abundant chemical class in terms of the number of compounds identified, followed by alcohols, acids, and aldehydes and ketones.

Changes in the quantity of each type and the contents of flavor substances, and bubble diagram of those substances during the fermentation of persimmon vinegar. (A): changes in the quantity of each type of flavor substances. (B): changes in the contents of flavor substances. (C): bubble diagram of acids and alcohols. (D): bubble diagram of esters, aldehydes and ketones.

As shown in Fig.5B, alcohols were the most abundant volatile compounds at the initial stage of fermentation, accounting for 52.45% in the BF group and 48.37% in the TF group, followed by esters (36.44% in BF group and 39.27% in TF group) and acids (9.64% in BF group and 11.17% in TF group). As fermentation progressed, the total flavor content increased. The proportion of alcohols gradually decreased, while esters increased and became the dominant category. By the end of fermentation, the relative content of total flavor compounds further increased to 25,466.11 (BF-150) and 20,341.75 (TF-150)，with an increase of 25.19% due to bioaugmentation (p < 0.01). Esters remained the most abundant category, comprising 16,039.89 (62.99%) and 12,896.257 (63.40%) in the BF and TF groups, respectively, followed by acids at 5657.32 (22.21%) and 4750.35 (23.35%). Overall, compared to the TF group, the BF group exhibited higher total flavor content, indicating that the bioaugmentation fermentation significantly influenced the metabolism of flavor compounds. Throughout the fermentation process, the total content of flavor substances showed an increasing trend, while the proportion of alcohols gradually decreased. In contrast, flavor metabolites such as acids and esters demonstrated a significant upward trend, particularly during the mid- to late-stage of fermentation.

Alcohols in persimmon vinegar primarily originate from ethanol fermentation, amino acid conversion, and the oxidation of linolenic acid degradation products. The changes in alcohols content during fermentation were visualized using a bubble chart after data normalization, as shown in Fig.5C. At the end of fermentation, the most abundant alcohols in BF group and TF group were 2-butanol, followed by 3-methyl-1-butanol, ethanol, and phenethyl alcohol. Throughout fermentation, the overall alcohol content exhibited a declining trend. Specifically, the levels of ethanol, 2-methyl-1-propanol, 3-methyl-1-butanol and 1-hexanol decreased. The content of phenethyl alcohol decreased in the BF group but increased in the TF group. The concentrations of ethanol, 2-methyl-1-propanol, 3-methyl-1-butanol, and 1-hexanol in the BF group were consistently higher than those in the TF group. In BF-150 group, the OAVs of 3-methyl-1-butanol, linalool, and phenylethyl alcohol were greater than 1, indicating their significant contribution to the overall aroma profile. Among these, 3-methyl-1-butanol is produced by yeast through the metabolism of branched-chain amino acids and is characterized by a fruity, banana- and apple-like sweetness, accompanied by a mellow alcoholic note (Fischer et al., 2022). Linalool is a monoterpene alcohol that typically originates from raw materials or secondary metabolism of yeast. Its aroma is characterized by fresh floral and citrus notes, with slight woody and spicy undertones. In persimmon vinegar, linalool imparts an elegant fruity-floral character, effectively balancing the sharpness brought by acetic acid and improving the freshness of the aroma as well as the overall pleasantness. Phenylethyl alcohol is mainly generated via the Ehrlich pathway through yeast metabolism of phenylalanine. This compound exhibits a rich floral rose aroma, honey-like sweetness, and complex hints of honey and spices (Bai et al., 2023). In vinegar, phenylethyl alcohol highlights floral and sweet notes, mitigates the sharpness of acidity, thereby enhancing the harmony and persistence of the flavor. It is commonly used to improve the mellowness and aftertaste of fermented foods. Therefore, the observed differences in alcohol profiles between the two groups can be attributed to the inoculation with the aroma-enhancing yeast W. anomalus ZX-1, which contributes to higher alcohol production and imparts a richer sensory character to the persimmon vinegar.

The changes in the content of acid compounds during fermentation were shown in Fig.5C. The acid substances in vinegar mainly originate from two sources: acetic fermentation and the decomposition of ester compounds. During fermentation, the total acid content, especially the acetic acid content, increased slowly in the early stage and showed a significant upward trend in the mid- and late-stage. Acetic acid accounted for 97.04% of the total acids in BF-150 group and 96.95% in TF-150 group, respectively. The high concentration of acid can inhibit the growth of acid-intolerant microorganisms. Acid-producing bacteria such as Komagataeibacter and Acetobacter play important roles in vinegar fermentation, enabling continuous growth and further accumulation of acetic acid (Ren & Tan, 2026). Among the acids, only the OAV of 3-methyl-butanoic acid was greater than 1 in the TF-150 group. The compound is primarily produced through the microbial degradation of leucine. Non-Saccharomyces yeasts (such as Wickerhamomyces and Pichia) and certain LAB are the key microorganisms responsible for its production. In some fermented foods, it serves as an essential component of their characteristic flavor profile (Jia et al., 2025). In the present study, the introduced microorganisms may have promoted the synthesis of this compound.

Esters are an important kind of flavor compounds in the fermented foods due to their elevated volatility and their profound impact on human olfactory perception. In vinegar, esters are by-products of yeast metabolism and are often characterized by fruity and floral aromas. The changes in ester content during persimmon vinegar fermentation were shown in Fig.5D. At the end of fermentation, the ester content in persimmon vinegar increased by 24.42% compared to the control group (p < 0.01). Among these esters, the concentrations of acetic acid methyl ester, ethyl acetate, butanol 3-methyl-acetate, heptanoic acid ethyl ester, octanoic acid ethyl ester, and sec-butyl acetate presented concentrations exceeding their odor thresholds, with OAVs greater than 1, indicating their significant contribution to the overall flavor profile of persimmon vinegar. These esters are primarily formed through esterification by microorganisms such as yeast and LAB during fermentation. They generally impart pleasant fruity, floral, and sweet aromas to foods, serving as the core of flavor complexity in fermented foods. Ethyl acetate, ethyl octanoate, and ethyl heptanoate together constitute the main fruity backbone, working in synergy with alcohols and acids to create multi-layered aromatic profiles. Additionally, isoamyl acetate contributes to the aged, mellow character in fermented foods (Rios-Reina et al., 2020). Among them, the most abundant ester was ethyl acetate, and the relative content was respectively 11,037.58 in the BF group and 8865.31 in the TF group, accounting for 68.81% and 68.74% of the total ester content, respectively. The BF group showed an increase of 24.5% in ethyl acetate compared to the TF group, with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.01). Ethyl acetate is considered the most important aroma compound in vinegar after acetic acid, contributing fruity notes and a softening effect that balances the sharp acidity (Guneser et al., 2022). Certain esters, such as ethyl acetate and isoamyl acetate, are predominantly produced by non-Saccharomyces yeasts. W. anomalus is a representative non-Saccharomyces yeast known to improve the aroma profile of fermented foods, particularly by increasing esters content (Cai et al., 2024).

The changes in the content of aldehydes and ketones during fermentation were shown in Fig.5D. Only a limited variety of aldehydes and ketones were detected in persimmon vinegar. Among the ketones, the most abundant compound was 3-hydroxy-2-butanone (acetoin), with an increase of 101.30% in the BF group compared to the TF group. Among these compounds, only the OAV of nonanal was greater than 1. Nonanal exhibits a complex aroma profile combining notes of citrus peel, rose, fat, and grass. At low concentrations, it presents a fresh fruity character. It contributes to the formation of a mellow and intricate aftertaste, serving as a natural byproduct of lipid oxidation and microbial metabolism. In addition, acetoin is a key precursor for the synthesis of tetramethylpyrazine, which contributes to the creamy odor of vinegar, and produced through microbial enzymatic catalysis (Pelicaen et al., 2020). The changes in other volatile flavor compounds were shown in Fig.6. The varieties and concentrations of phenols, pyrazines, and terpenes detected in persimmon vinegar were relatively limited, primarily including creosol, 1-methyl-4-(1-methylethyl)-1,3-cyclohexadiene, dill ether, etc., all showing no significant trends in their variations. Among them, creosol and 1-methyl-4-(1-methylethyl)-1,3-cyclohexadiene are derived from persimmon fruit. Their concentrations were low, and their OAVs were less than 1, indicating only a minor influence on the overall aroma profile of persimmon vinegar.

(C): permutation test plot. (D): V + S plot of OPLS-DA, the square was marked V-plot and the circle was marked S-plot. (E): flavor wheel of 11 key aroma-active compounds in persimmon vinegar. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed on the volatile flavor compounds to investigate the clustering patterns of metabolites and identify the differential volatile flavor substances. The score plot and loading plot of the PCA model were shown in Fig.6A and 6B. The results indicated that the samples from the early fermentation stage were mainly clustered in the lower-right region of the score plot, corresponding to a relatively large number of flavor compounds in the loading plot, suggesting a higher abundance of flavor substances associated with the initial stage. The samples from the mid-fermentation stage were distributed in the central area of the score plot and were associated with fewer types of flavor compounds in the loading plot. Late-stage samples were relatively concentrated in the upper-left region of the score plot, with a moderate number of associated flavor compounds, greater than the mid-fermentation stage but fewer than the early stage. To further screen for differential flavor compounds, an OPLS-DA model was constructed. The permutation test plot (Fig.6C) showed that the PLS-DA model fitted well (R2 = 0.837, Q2 = −0.341). V + S plot of OPLS-DA (Fig.6D) identified 7 volatile flavor compounds with VIP value≥1 as critical classifying variables, including ethyl acetate, acetic acid, 2-butanol, 3-methyl-1-butanol, 1-butanol 3-methyl-acetate, sec-butyl acetate, and phenylethyl alcohol, indicating their significant contribution to the flavor of persimmon vinegar. These substances are primarily derived from the metabolic activities of yeast and LAB. The bioaugmented strains introduced in this study likely established a distinct ecological niche within the persimmon vinegar fermentation system, thereby playing a significant role in shaping its flavor profile. To further elucidate the correlation between microorganisms and changes in flavor substances, it is necessary to analyze the microbial community structure at different fermentation stages. Therefore, in subsequent research, we will focus on investigating the dynamic relationship between microbial composition and the production of metabolites, so as to clarify the intrinsic mechanism by which bioaugmentation enhances the quality of persimmon vinegar.

Understanding the aroma-active compounds with OAVs ≥1 is essential for comprehending the overall flavor profile of persimmon vinegar. Based on the OAVs data presented in Table S3 of the Supplementary Material, a total of 11 aroma-active compounds were identified in persimmon vinegar, including seven esters, three alcohols, and one acid. A flavor wheel was used to visualize the aroma profiles of these aroma-active compounds (Fig.6E). The results showed that flowery was mainly associated with linalool, nonanal and octanoic acid ethyl ester. Fruity was associated with acetic acid methyl ester, ethyl acetate, hexanoic acid ethyl ester, heptanoic acid ethyl ester, octanoic acid ethyl ester and sec-butyl acetate. Spicy included nonanal, 3-methyl-butanoic acid, 3-methyl-1-butanol. Alcohol was affected by 3-methyl-1-butanol, hexanoic acid ethyl ester and heptanoic acid ethyl ester. Several studies have shown that esters, ketones and aldehydes are the primary sources of floral and fruity aromas in fruit vinegar (Ozdemir & Budak, 2022).

Electronic tongue response characteristics and sensory evaluation of persimmon vinegar

To evaluate the impact of bioaugmentation fermentation on permission vinegar sensory characteristics, the taste attributes of samples from the BF-150 and TF-150 groups were analyzed using an electronic tongue and compared their differences in the response values of different taste attributes. The radar plot was illustrated in Fig.7A, BF-150 group exhibited significantly stronger responses in umami, richness, aftertaste-A, and aftertaste-B. The BF-150 group had significantly higher total FAAs, particularly UAA like Asp and Glu. This directly correlates with the enhanced umami and richness responses. The higher contents of UAA in BF-150 group provided a chemical basis for the enhanced umami perception. Although the BF-150 group had higher total acidity and organic acid content, no significant difference in sourness response was observed between the two groups. This may be that acetic acid was the dominant acid, but the higher proportions of lactic acid and malic acid in the BF group mitigated acetic acid's pungency, resulting in a softer, more rounded sourness despite higher total acidity. This explains the similar electronic tongue sourness response between groups. Additionally, the presence of pleasant aromatic compounds derived from both the fruit and microbial metabolism may modulate the perception of basic tastes. The persimmon vinegar contained a lot of pleasant flowery, spicy and fruity aroma substances, such as linalool (lavender), ethyl acetate (pineapple), heptanoic acid ethyl ester (grape), and 3-methyl-1-butanol (pungent). The flavor compounds not only enhanced the flavor complexity of persimmon vinegar, but also diverted sensory attention away from the sourness, thereby contributing to a more harmonious overall flavor profile (Chen et al., 2025). Accordingly, the BF-150 group exhibited higher response values in the richness, aftertaste-A and aftertaste-B.

Radar charts of sensory evaluation and electronic tongue sensory analysis of persimmon vinegar. (A): sensory evaluation of BF group and TF group. (B): electronic tongue sensory analysis of BF group and TF group.

Sensory evaluation was subsequently conducted to further validate these fingdings. Fig. 7B showed that the score in the BF-150 group was higher than that in the TF-150 group. Moreover, the BF group received higher scores in all terms of taste, flavor, texture, umami, and overall acceptability, indicating that the persimmon vinegar exhibited a softer acidity with mild pungency, a distinct vinegar flavor, and a pronounced ester aroma accompanied by the characteristic fruity notes of persimmon. Although umami scores were slightly higher in the BF group, the difference was not statistically significant. The observed sensory differences between the BF-150 and TF-150 groups can be collectively attributed to variations in the content of aroma compounds, organic acids, and free amino acids. Bioaugmentation significantly increased total volatile compounds, especially esters (e.g., ethyl acetate) and specific alcohols (e.g., 3-methyl-1-butanol). These compounds, with OAVs >1, are directly responsible for the fruity, floral, and spicy notes identified in the flavor wheel, leading to higher sensory scores for flavor and overall acceptability. Both non-volatile and volatile compounds interact through synergistic and inhibitory mechanisms to shape the integrated flavor profile of the persimmon vinegar. In summary, both sensory evaluation and electronic tongue analysis confirmed that the bioaugmentation effectively enhanced both the quality and sensory characteristics of persimmon vinegar.

Conclusion

A bioaugmentation strategy using two LAB strains L. plantarum CGMCC 24035 and L. acidophilus R2, which were isolated from persimmon vinegar fermentation broth and showed strong lactic acid-producing capability, along with the aroma-producing yeast W. anomalus ZX-1, was employed to regulate fermentation and enhance the flavor profile of persimmon vinegar. Dynamic analysis revealed that bioaugmentation significantly increased the contents of non-volatile acids and FAAs, with a notable accumulation of umami amino acids. In addition, the strategy effectively improved the levels of volatile flavor compounds, particularly esters. Electronic tongue analysis and sensory evaluation further confirmed the enhanced flavor characteristics of the bioaugmented vinegar. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the role of bioaugmentation in persimmon vinegar fermentation and demonstrate the potential of integrating traditional fermentation practices with precision biotechnology to regulate flavor development in fermented foods. However, the main limitations of this study lie in the absence of dynamic monitoring of microorganisms, which hindered the clarification of interaction mechanisms between microbial strains, thereby affecting precise control over fermentation stability and flavor regulation. Additionally, fluctuations in environmental parameters and inherent variations in persimmon raw materials significantly influenced the fermentation outcomes, constraining the reproducibility and continuity of the results. Future studies should address these factors to further optimize and stabilize the bioaugmentation strategy for industrial application.
