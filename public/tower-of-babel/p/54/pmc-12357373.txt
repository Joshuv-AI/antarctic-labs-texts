Roles of inflammatory cytokines in the pathogenesis of hepatitis B virus-related acute-on-chronic liver failure and CAR-T therapy
Authors: Yan Wang, Jing Gu, Guanghua Chen, Yanfeng Jiang, Ying Xu, Xiaoping Huang, Wei Sun, Jianhe Gan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the association between inflammatory cytokines and liver function indices in hepatitis B virus-related acute-on-chronic liver failure (HBV-ACLF) patients and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T therapy recipients, with the aim of identifying prognostic biomarkers and elucidating the pathophysiological roles of inflammatory cytokines in HBV-ACLF.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed clinical data from three groups: 68 patients with confirmed HBV-ACLF, 30 patients with pre-HBV-ACLF, and 372 hematologic malignancy patients receiving CAR-T therapy with preserved liver function at the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University.ResultsSerum interleukin (IL)-10 levels demonstrated a progressive increase across the groups [healthy controls: 0.15 (0.10;2.18) pg/mL; pre-HBV-ACLF: 3.80 (2.38;11.83) pg/mL; HBV-ACLF: 5.95 (3.90;14.75) pg/mL; p < 0.001]. Patients with clinical improvement exhibited significantly lower IL-10 concentrations and higher IL-6/IL-10 ratios compared to those with disease progression (p < 0.05). Notably, IL-6 levels remained stable across clinical stages, with HBV-ACLF patients without secondary infection showing lower IL-6 levels than pre-HBV-ACLF patients (p < 0.05). Following CAR-T therapy, hematologic patients displayed significantly elevated IL-6 levels, accompanied by increases in AST and INR prolongation, whereas TBIL and ALT remained stable (p > 0.05). Consistent with HBV-ACLF observations, improved CAR-T recipients demonstrated significantly lower IL-6/IL-10 ratios than progression patients (p < 0.05).ConclusionsIL-10 exhibits stage-dependent dynamics in HBV-ACLF pathogenesis and progression, closely mirroring hepatic functional deterioration. The IL-6/IL-10 ratio serves as a prognostic biomarker for both HBV-ACLF and CAR-T therapy-related liver injury, with lower ratios indicating better clinical outcomes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: HBV-ACLF, Inflammatory cytokines, Cytokine release syndrome, Liver function, Chimeric antigen receptor T therapy

Background

Cytokine release syndrome (CRS), also referred to as uncontrollable systemic inflammatory response syndrome, represents an acute systemic inflammation condition [1–4]. This syndrome is primarily driven by the excessive release of proinflammatory cytokines, particularly interleukin (IL)-2, IL-6, IL-10, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ). These cytokines can trigger a spectrum of clinical manifestations ranging from pyrexia and hypotension to severe systemic inflammation and multi-organ dysfunction [5–7]. Although viral infections (e.g., hepatitis B virus [HBV] infection) are classical triggers, accumulating evidence suggests that immunotherapies, particularly those involving chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells or other activated immune cells, may also initiate CRS pathogenesis. CAR-T therapy has emerged as an effective form of cellular immunotherapy for hematologic malignancies, wherein autologous T lymphocytes are harvested, genetically reprogrammed in vitro, and then reinfused into the patient. However, some patients develop CRS. Emerging clinical evidence demonstrates multisystem toxicity profiles, notably transient elevations in hepatobiliary enzymes (aspartate aminotransferase [AST] and alanine aminotransferase [ALT]) and hyperbilirubinemia, indicative of CAR-T cell-mediated hepatic injury [5, 8, 9].

HBV infection remains a significant global public health concern, with serological evidence of current or past infection detected in approximately 30% of the world’s population [10, 11]. Despite this widespread burden, epidemiological characterization HBV-related acute-on-chronic liver failure (HBV-ACLF) within HBV-infected populations remains unclear. Nevertheless, emerging clinical studies suggest that 35% of HBV-related cirrhosis patients experiencing acute decompensation progress to ACLF [12, 13]. The pathological changes resulting from various underlying liver diseases and acute precipitating factors differ among patient groups [14]. This pathophysiological complexity has hindered the development of an international consensus regarding standardized diagnostic criteria. China, which bears the highest regional prevalence of HBV, has HBV-ACLF as the predominant subtype among ACLF cases [15–17]. HBV-ACLF represents a common form of end-stage liver disease in patients with chronic HBV infection, characterized by the rapid deterioration of underlying chronic liver conditions, multi-organ failure, and high short-term mortality.

The pathogenesis of liver failure is highly complex, involving diverse etiological pathways and multi-stage pathophysiological mechanisms. Currently, extensive research is underway both in China and internationally to elucidate the mechanisms underlying progressive liver failure and to develop therapeutic strategies aimed at mitigating ongoing parenchymal injury and promoting hepatic regenerative repair. There is an ongoing debate regarding the causal relationship between sepsis and the ACLF. Emerging evidence implicates cytokine storm-mediated immunopathology as a critical accelerant in the progression of liver failure [5], while recent advances further identify systemic inflammation as a principal contributor to HBV-associated ACLF [18]. Notably, the 2019 Asian Pacific Association for the Study of Liver (APASL) guidelines redefined sepsis as a consequence rather than an initiating trigger of the ACLF [19]. According to the APASL definition, sepsis is not considered a primary cause of liver failure; however, in Western definition, sepsis is regarded as the most common precipitant, with systemic inflammation considered the driving mechanism of ACLF [20].

Materials and methods

Study design and population

The retrospective cohort study included consecutive patients treated at the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University between January 2017 and December 2020. The cohort comprised 68 patients with HBV-ACLF, 30 patients with pre-HBV-ACLF, and 372 patients with hematologic tumors treated with CAR-T therapy. A comprehensive assessment was performed for the pre-HBV-ACLF and HBV-ACLF patients, including evaluation temperature, abdominal signs, routine ascites analysis (e.g. appearance, transparency, specific gravity, coagulability, mucin characterization, and nucleated cell count), biochemical indices of ascites, procalcitonin (PCT), white blood cell (WBC) count, (1–3)-β-D glucan test (G test), galactomannan test (GM test), chest CT, and other pre-relevant examinations. Among the 68 HBV-ACLF patients, all had one or two organ failures, and 32 of these patients were co-infected with bacterial infections. Among patients without co-infection at enrollment, 10 cases of secondary pulmonary infections occurred within 2 to 4 weeks, all of which led to a deterioration in liver function.

Diagnoses and exclusion criteria

ACLF was diagnosed according to the 2018 Chinese Medical Association (CMA) consensus guidelines [21]. HBV-ACLF was defined based on the 2018 COSSH-ACLF criteria: regardless of cirrhosis status, patients with chronic hepatitis B were diagnosed with ACLF if their total bilirubin (TBIL) was ≥ 12 mg/dL (1 mg/dL = 17.1 µmol/L) and international normalized ratio (INR) was ≥ 1.5 [22]. Pre-liver failure (pre-LF) was identified using the 2018 CMA criteria, which include elevations in pre-ALT and/or AST, TBIL levels between 85.5 and 171 µmol/L (or a daily increase ≥ 17.1 µmol/L), and prothrombin activity (PTA) between 40% and 50% (equivalent to INR < 1.5) [10]. Pre-HBV-ACLF was defined as patients with pre-ACLF who did not meet the criteria for the ACLF diagnosis. Pre-HBV-ACLF consisted of two groups of patients: patients with severe liver injury (total bilirubin, TBIL ≥ 85.5 µmol/L) with previously diagnosed CHB, and patients with previously diagnosed HBV-related liver cirrhosis presenting with one of the following five precipitating events: ascites, upper gastrointestinal bleeding, HE, bacterial infection and a high level of jaundice (TBIL ≥ 85.5 µmol/L) [23]. Infection diagnosis required confirmation through three aspects: clinical manifestations (e.g., fever, cough, expectoration, abdominal pain, and frequency, urgency of and pain with urination), laboratory evidence (e.g., blood, sputum, ascites, and urine), and radiological confirmation (e.g., CT).

Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: non-HBV liver failure etiology (such as autoimmunity, alcohol, drugs, genetic/metabolic factors, or indeterminate causes); other liver failure subtypes (acute/subacute liver failure, chronic liver failure without ACLF features); malignancies involving the liver or other organs; immunocompromised status (pregnancy or breastfeeding); patients who had received immunosuppressive therapy within 6 months prior to admission; or HIV infection.

Clinical data collection

Demographic and clinical data were systematically collected within 24 h of admission, including white blood cell (WBC) count, procalcitonin (PCT), TBIL, ALT, AST, INR, and serum cytokines including IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-17 A. Baseline clinical parameters documented at enrollment included the number of organ failures, presence of microbiologically confirmed bacterial infections, the temporal profile of secondary infections, infection classification, and final clinical outcomes. Disease deterioration was defined by the occurrence of liver function decline, liver transplantation, failure of other organs, or death. For patients receiving CAR-T therapy, liver function indices (LFIs) (TBIL, ALT, AST, and INR) at the peak of IL-6 release were measured. If there was no significant increase in inflammatory cytokines levels, LFIs were reassessed one week after treatment.

Measurement of serum LFIs and cytokines

Liver function parameters, such as ALT, AST, TBIL and INR, were measured using standardized assays at the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University. Liver function was tested via continuous methods, and blood coagulation INR was assessed using coagulation assays. Serum cytokine concentrations were measured as follows: a total of 1 ml of peripheral blood was obtained using an EDTA-anticoagulated vacuum blood collection tube (BD, USA). Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 2500 rpm for 10 min. The concentrations of IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-17 A were measured using a FC500 flow cytometer (Coulter, USA) with cytokine assay kits (Biolegend, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Data analysis was performed using FCAP microarray software (Softflow, USA).

Statistical analysis

Variables exhibiting non-normal distributions in each group were analyzed using nonparametric methods. Quantitative data are expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), while categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Differences in characteristics among the three groups were compared by Kruskal–Wallis rank sum tests, with post hoc multiple comparisons performed using the Bonferroni correction. For comparisons between two independent samples, the Wilcoxon rank sum test was employed, while the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for paired samples. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to assess the association between individual inflammatory cytokines and LFIs (i.e., TBIL, ALT, AST, and INR). Scatter plot with a fitted regression line was generated using R to visualize the relationship between inflammatory cytokines levels and LFIs. To improve visualization, the concentrations of the measured indics were log-transformed prior to plotting. Values below the limit of detection (LOD; i.e., 0) were imputed with half of the minimum observed value for each index. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 23.0 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.) and R Version 3.4.2 (R Core Team, 2017, R: A language and environment for statistical computing. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

TBIL and INR elevation in HBV-ACLF patients compared to pre-HBV-ACLF patients

To elucidate the pathophysiological roles of LFIs and inflammatory cytokines, we performed comparisons between HBV-ACLF and pre-HBV-ACLF patients. The results showed that the HBV-ACLF patients had significantly higher TBIL levels [269.85 (175.65; 377.25) µmol/L vs. 158.60 (69.80; 241.55) µmol/L; P < 0.05] and longer INR [2.43 (1.94; 2.75) vs. 1.27 (1.20; 1.43), P < 0.05] compared to pre-HBV-ACLF patients. Additionally, pre-HBV-ACLF patients exhibited significantly elevated hepatic markers relative to healthy controls [ALT: 172.1 (40.9; 462.5) U/L, n = 30 vs. 11.95 (6.18; 15.80) U/L, n = 20; P < 0.05; AST: 108.50 (61.70; 217.20) U/L, n = 30 vs. 10.80 (5.60; 15.3028) U/L, n = 20; P < 0.05; TBIL: 158.60 (69.80; 241.55) µmol/L, n = 30 vs. 9.20 (6.90; 12.93) µmol/L, n = 20; P < 0.05; INR: 1.27 (1.20; 1.43), n = 30 vs. 1.02 (0.99; 1.06), n = 20; P < 0.05] (Fig. 1A).

IL-10 elevation and IL-17 A decrease in HBV-ACLF patients compared to pre-HBV-ACLF patients

Serum IL-10 levels demonstrated a progressive increase across groups [healthy controls: 0.15 (0.10; 2.18) pg/mL; pre-HBV-ACLF: 3.80 (2.38; 11.83) pg/mL; HBV-ACLF: 5.95 (3.90; 14.75) pg/mL; p < 0.001], which was significantly higher in HBV-ACLF patients than in pre-HBV-ACLF patients[5.95 (3.90; 14.75) pg/mL, n = 68 vs. 3.80 (2.38; 11.83) pg/mL, n = 30; P < 0.001]. In contrast, the serum level of IL-17 A was significantly lower in HBV-ACLF patients compared to pre-HBV-ACLF patients [1.35 (0.59; 11.81) pg/mL, n = 68 vs. 2.90 (2.80; 22.53) pg/mL, n = 30; P < 0.001]. No significant difference was observed in serum IL-6 levels between HBV-ACLF and pre-HBV-ACLF patients [12.95 (6.60; 41.93) pg/mL, n = 68 vs. 14.15 (3.68; 24.13) pg/mL, n = 30; P = 0.353] (Fig. 1A).

IL-10 elevation and IL-17 A decrease in HBV-ACLF patients with secondary infection compared to pre-HBV-ACLF patients

While infection-induced CRS is well-documented, its specific impact on inflammatory cytokines profiles in pre-HBV-related live failure remains poorly characterized. To address this issue, we compared serum concentrations of inflammatory cytokines in pre-HBV-ACLF and HBV-ACLF patients with or without secondary infection. Serum IL-10 levels were modestly higher in HBV-ACLF patients with secondary infection than in pre-HBV-ACLF patients[11.25 (4.30; 16.25) pg/mL, n = 32 vs. 3.80 (2.38; 11.83) pg/mL, n = 30; P < 0.001], while IL-17 A was significantly decreased [0.70 (0.55; 11.46) pg/mL, n = 32 vs. 2.90 (2.80; 22.53) pg/mL, n = 30; P < 0.001] (Fig. 1A).

IL-6 dynamics in HBV-ACLF patients: elevation in those with secondary infection and decrease in those without compared to pre-HBV-ACLF patients

The serum IL-6 level was significantly higher in HBV-ACLF patients with secondary infection compared to those without secondary infection or pre-HBV-ACLF (40.60 (20.18; 133.75) pg/mL vs. 7.40 (4.23; 11.15) pg/mL or 14.15 (3.68; 24.1) pg/mL; P < 0.05, P < 0.01). Conversely, the serum IL-6 level was lower in HBV-ACLF patients without secondary infection than in pre-HBV-ACLF group [7.40 (4.23; 11.15) pg/mL vs. 14.15 (3.68; 24.1) pg/mL; P = 0.043]. However, there was no statistically significant difference in serum IL-6 levels between pre-HBV-ACLF and HBV-ACLF patients [14.15 (3.68; 24.1) pg/mL vs. 7.40 (4.23; 11.15) pg/mL; P = 0.353] (Fig. 1A).

Elevated IL-2, IL-6, IL-10, IFN-γ, and IL-6/IL-10 in HBV-ACLF patients with deteriorated outcomes

To elucidate prognostic inflammatory cytokines, we compared the cytokine profiles in HBV-ACLF patients with improved or deteriorated outcomes. The results showed that the serum levels of IL-2 [7.60 (0.60; 10.45) pg/mL vs. 1.85 (0.60; 3.85) pg/mL; P = 0.012], IL-6 [26.20 (8.98; 126.53) pg/mL vs. 10.40 (5.28; 15.75) pg/mL; P = 0.002], IL-10 [12.30 (5.60; 17.20) pg/mL vs. 3.90 (2.38; 5.95) pg/mL; P < 0.001], and IFN-γ [7.50 (0.70; 8.38) pg/mL vs. 2.50 (0.70; 5.75) pg/mL; P < 0.05] at enrollment were significantly higher in patients with HBV-ACLF whose condition deteriorated compared to those who improved. Conversely, patients with improving conditions showed a decrease in IL-10 levels [3.90 (2.38; 5.95) pg/mL vs. 12.30 (5.60; 17.20) pg/mL; P < 0.001]. Additionally, the IL-6/IL-10 ratio [2.28(1.09; 4.68) vs. 2.97 (0.94; 5.22); P < 0.05] also decreased significantly in the improved group compared to the deteriorated group (Fig. 1B).

Dissociation between inflammatory cytokines and hepatic dysfunction in CAR-T recipients

IL-6 and IL-10 exhibited the strongest positive correlations with liver function indices, particularly INR (IL-6: ρ = 0.60, P < 0.001; IL-10: ρ = 0.51, P < 0.001), underscoring their roles in CAR-T–induced CRS and coagulopathy. IFN-γ was moderately correlated with AST (ρ = 0.38, P < 0.001) and TBIL (ρ = 0.29, P < 0.001). IL-2 and TNF-α showed weaker but statistically significant associations across multiple indices (P < 0.05). Discussion

In this study, we conducted a stratified analysis of three clinically distinct cohorts: healthy controls, pre-HBV-ACLF, and HBV-ACLF patients. Our findings revealed a stage-dependent elevation in IL-10, which was strongly correlated with disease progression. This progressive cytokine dysregulation suggests IL-10 may play a pivotal role in orchestrating immunopathogenic cascades in the development of HBV-ACLF. IL-10 is widely recognized as an immunosuppressive cytokine [24], and it has recently been reported that peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from patients with chronic HBV infection secrete IL-10 in response to hepatitis B core antigen (HBcAg) stimulation [25]. Notably, we observed that serum IL-10 levels were markedly elevated in patients who experienced clinical deterioration compared to those who showed clinical improvement. Concurrently, liver function—reflected by serum levels of ALT, AST, TBIL, and INR—was impaired in pre-HBV-ACLF patients and further deteriorated in HBV-ACLF patients. These findings are consistent with previous studies and support the hypothesis that IL-10 contributes functionally to the pathogenesis of HBV-ACLF, suggesting its potential utility as a diagnostic biomarker, prognostic indicator, and therapeutic target.

Moreover, we found that serum IL-6 levels were significantly higher in HBV-ACLF patients with secondary infection than in those without, underscoring the well-established role of IL-6 in the immune response to HBV infection, in line with earlier reports [26–28]. These data suggest that IL-6 may serve as a sensitive biomarker for secondary infection and as an early indicator of clinical deterioration in HBV-ACLF. Interestingly, IL-6 levels were higher in pre-HBV-ACLF patients than in HBV-ACLF patients overall, and were lowest in HBV-ACLF patients without secondary infection. Nevertheless, a significant difference was observed between pre-HBV-ACLF patients and HBV-ACLF patients with secondary infection, possibly reflecting immune exhaustion in patients with ACLF [29]. A recent study further supports this interpretation, showing that T cell-, B cell- and NK cell-related immune modules were significantly downregulated, whereas genes associated with bacterial infection responses were markedly upregulated in HBV-ACLF patients [23].

Additionally, compared to patients with clinical deterioration, the IL-6/IL-10 ratio was significantly lower in those who showed clinical improvement. Previous studies have consistently reported elevated circulating levels of both IL-6 and IL-10 in patients with HBV-ACLF, reflecting the profound immune dysregulation characteristic of this condition [30, 31]. However, in the context of HBV-ACLF, the immunomodulatory effect of IL-10 may be overshadowed by the dominant pro-inflammatory signaling driven by IL-6 [23]. Neither IL-6 nor IL-10 alone sufficiently captures the complexity of immune imbalance in HBV-ACLF. Furthermore, emerging evidence indicates that HBV-ACLF patients with spontaneous bacterial peritonitis exhibit higher IL-6 levels and lower IL-10 levels compared to those without this complication [32]. Thus, the IL-6/IL-10 ratio serves as a more robust biomarker for evaluating the balance between pro-inflammatory (IL-6) and anti-inflammatory (IL-10) responses in HBV-ACLF.

Although the pathogenesis of HBV-ACLF has not been fully elucidated, hepatocellular injury and immune dysregulation are believed to play central roles. However, the mechanisms underlying persistent hepatocyte damage and death remain incompletely understood [33, 34]. HBV reactivation can profoundly impact the host’s immune system [35, 36], triggering excessive innate immune responses [37]. Recent evidence suggests that such activation may represent a critical mechanism of ACLF progression, with IFN-related signaling pathway potentially serving as the initial triggers [23]. Organ dysfunction secondary to HBV reactivation may stimulate cytokine cascades that aggravate hepatic injury [38]. In response to hepatocyte death, damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) are released, activating and recruiting Kupffer cells (KCs) and neutrophils to the injury site. While these immune cells contribute to the clearance of necrotic debris, they may also amplify hepatic inflammation and promote additional cell death [39–42]. Furthermore, DAMPs and pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) activate distinct signaling pathways that lead to the excessive release of both proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines. This dysregulated immune activation—commonly referred to as a “cytokine storm”—can result in systemic inflammatory response syndromes, tissue injury, and further hepatocyte death, perpetuating a vicious cycle [43]– [44]. As ACLF advances, anti-inflammatory responses gradually become dominant, giving rise to compensatory anti-inflammatory response syndrome (CARS) which is associated with immune suppression, particularly during the middle and late stages of the disease, often culminating in immune exhaustion and increased susceptibility to secondary infection and sepsis [45–47]. The IL-17 A signaling pathway also contributes to ACLF pathogenesis by promoting the release of chemokines and proflammatory cytokines. Yao et al. demonstrated that IL-17 A transcripts are expressed by CD4+ T-cells and induce IL-6 production in fibroblasts. IL-17 A has been identified as independent risk factor for ACLF, and a decreased Treg/Th17 ratio has been found to negatively correlate with disease severity [48–50]. In line with these findings, Stoop et al. reported that a reduced number of circulating CD4+ CD25+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) may facilitate widespread hepatocyte necrosis and apoptosis [51–53]. In our study, IL-17 A levels were significantly lower in HBV-ACLF patients compared to pre-HBV-ACLF patients, potentially reflecting more advanced disease at the time of enrollment.

Immunotherapy can induce CRS and generate adverse effects in patients [54]. In the present study, serum levels of IL-2, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, and IFN-γ were markedly elevated following CAR-T therapy, with IL-6 showing the most pronounced increase—consistent with typical CRS profiles. Moreover, We observed that IL-6 levels and IL-6/IL-10 ratio were negatively associated with clinical outcomes in CAR-T-treated patients with hematologic malignancies. Preclinical evidence indicates that IL-6 elevation following CAR-T administration arises predominantly from recipient macrophages and monocytes, rather than the infused CAR-T cells themselves. Importantly, CRS can be effectively mitigated by blocking the IL-6 receptor with tocilizumab(an anti-IL-6 receptor antibody) [55, 56]. Beyond its role as an anti-inflammatory cytokine, IL-10 has recently been shown to enhance T cell effector functions and promote metabolic reprogramming in CAR-T cells, supporting mitochondrial fitness and reducing T cell exhaustion in the tumor microenvironment [57, 58]. Notably, engineered CAR-T cells capable of autocrine IL-10 secretion have demonstrated improved antitumor efficacy in various solid tumor mouse models [59].

Although the immunopathological mechanisms underlying HBV-ACLF and CAR-T-induced CRS differ, both conditions involve systemic inflammatory responses, characterized by variable degrees of IL-6 and IL-10 elevation. At present, tocilizumab is the standard of care for managing CAR-T-associated CRS. In contrast, anti-cytokine therapies have not been widely explored in the context of HBV-ACLF. Our findings, together with prior studies, suggest that IL-6-targeted strategies—including receptor antagonism, direct neutralization, or monitoring of the IL-6/IL-10 ratio—may hold promise for improving clinical outcomes in both HBV-ACLF and CAR-T-treated patients.

This study has several limitations. Our sample size was relatively small, and straightforward staging of HBV-ACLF patients was not performed, which may have limited our statistical power to detect a real correlations between inflammatory cytokines and liver function parameters. Additionally, the mechanisms link between inflammatory cytokines and liver injury in HBV-ACLF remain incompletely understood. Future research is needed to validate these observations, evaluate the diagnostic utility of serum IL-6 and IL-10, and determine whether IL-10 modulation could serve as a therapeutic strategy to improve prognosis.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings demonstrate that serum IL-6 and IL-10 levels are strongly correlated with clinical outcomes in HBV-ACLF patients and undergoing CAR-T therapy patients. Furthermore, a lower IL-6/IL-10 ratio was consistently linked to improved prognoses, suggesting its potential as a shared prognostic biomarker across these distinct clinical settings.
