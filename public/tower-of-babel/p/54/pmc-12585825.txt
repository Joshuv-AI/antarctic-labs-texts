Family caregiver facilitating access to healthcare and providing daily support for older patients: A descriptive study
Authors: Junki Mizumoto, Hirohisa Fujikawa, Masashi Izumiya, Masato Eto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs older adults lose physical function with age, family members often become primary, unpaid caregivers. In Japan, despite having a universal long‐term care (LTC) system, families often still bear the caregiving burden. Limited research exists on who supports older outpatients with daily tasks and healthcare access. This study aimed to identify caregivers' involvement in healthcare access and assistance with household tasks for patients aged 80 years or older.MethodsA descriptive study at a suburban hospital in Japan from April 2023 to March 2024. We developed a self‐administered questionnaire about LTC certification, transportation to the hospital, and daily activities, and distributed it to all outpatients aged 80 and over. Caregivers or staff assisted patients unable to complete it themselves.ResultsAmong 371 older patients approached, 359 participated (median age 84; 61.8% female). Among 334 respondents, 32.4% relied on family for visiting the hospital, while 29.3% drove themselves. Fewer used LTC (6.4%) or public transport (7.0%). Reliance on family increased with higher care need levels. Among 358 respondents, 53.4% relied on families for shopping and 48.3% for laundry. Approximately one‐third handled these tasks themselves. Use of LTC services was limited (11.7% for shopping, 8.7% for laundry), and reliance on family increased with care needs.ConclusionsIn primary care settings, older adults frequently depend on family caregivers for both access to healthcare and the management of daily household tasks. Primary care professionals are well‐positioned to recognize and address these prevalent challenges.Keywords: access to care, family caregiver, geriatrics, older people

INTRODUCTION

Physical functioning and the ability to perform activities of daily living (ADL) among older adults decline progressively with age. 1 Family members are frequently the first to recognize and respond to this functional deterioration, often assuming the role of primary caregivers. 2 As healthcare delivery increasingly transitions from inpatient to outpatient and home‐based settings, the responsibility for providing essential care and support increasingly falls on family caregivers. 3 These caregivers typically offer informal, unpaid care and devote substantial portions of their time to caregiving activities. 2 The growing burden associated with caregiving responsibilities can negatively affect the caregivers' physical and psychological well‐being, potentially compromising patient outcomes as well. 3 In fact, many family caregivers extend their support beyond basic daily assistance to include healthcare‐related tasks and facilitation of access to medical services, further compounding their caregiving burden. 4

Even when formal caregivers supported by long‐term care services are available, family members frequently remain the primary providers of in‐home care and support for older adults. 5 Community‐dwelling older individuals often depend heavily on family members and unpaid caregivers for assistance with activities of daily living, such as meal preparation and laundry, as well as self‐care tasks including bathing and dressing.6, 7 For example, in the United States, although the utilization of formal home care among older adults with disabilities increased in 2016 compared to 2004, reliance on informal care also rose, with nearly three‐quarters of this population receiving informal care as of 2016. 8 A critical shortage of formal caregiving services persists. One study reported that approximately 30% of caregivers experienced at least one unmet care support need, 3 while another found that older adults receiving community‐based assistance received an average of 164 h per month of informal care, predominantly provided by family members. 9

Japan is one of the most rapidly aging countries in the world. To support older adults living in the community, Japan has implemented a long‐term care (LTC) certification system. Individuals aged 65 and older who currently require, or are expected to require, LTC are eligible to apply for certification. Based on their physical and cognitive status, applicants are categorized as not applicable, in need of support, or in need of nursing care. The system comprises seven levels: Support Levels 1 and 2 (for those needing support with daily tasks) and Care Need Levels 1 through 5 (for individuals requiring substantial support with essential activities such as eating and bathing). Higher levels correspond to greater care needs. Once certified, individuals can access services appropriate to their designated level of care. 10 This classification system is used nationwide and has been validated. 11 Typically, individuals at Care Need Level 2 have severe limitations in instrumental activities of daily living. 12 However, the existence of a well‐developed LTC system does not inherently alleviate the burden on family caregivers. In fact, compared to another Asian country (Thailand) lacking formal LTC systems, family caregivers in Japan reported significantly higher levels of caregiving burden, loneliness, and psychological stress. 13 Other previous research in Japan suggests that the burden on family caregivers is pronounced in specific populations, such as those caring for cancer patients with unknown primary origins 14 and patients with osteoporotic fractures. 15 Nonetheless, the overall extent of family caregivers' involvement among older outpatients remains insufficiently understood.

Understanding how the fundamental needs of older patients are being addressed is essential for delivering high‐quality care. Frail older adults often experience high rates of hospitalization when their needs related to ADL are unmet; however, these outcomes tend to improve when such needs are adequately supported. 16 Healthcare professionals who provide regular care to older adults must be aware of how patients' needs—such as access to healthcare and assistance with household chores—are being fulfilled. This awareness enables professionals to detect unmet needs or excessive reliance on family members and to intervene appropriately. Nevertheless, in Japan, there remains limited clarity regarding how these needs are being met among older patients who routinely receive primary care. This study aimed to identify caregivers' involvement in healthcare access and assistance with household tasks for patients aged 80 years or older, regularly attending a small hospital in a rural city in Japan.

METHODS

Setting

This study was conducted concurrently with another study 17 at the outpatient department for general internal medicine and family medicine in a small hospital located in a suburban area of Japan. A city where the hospital is situated has a population of approximately 500,000 individuals, including around 48,000 individuals aged over 80. The hospital is situated within a residential neighborhood, with the nearest train station approximately a 20‐min walk away. While there is no nearby street bus stop, a complimentary shuttle bus operates between the train station, residential areas, and the hospital.

Questionnaire

The first author collaborated with the department's nursing staff to design a self‐completion questionnaire, based on previous research.18, 19 The questionnaire included questions about the classification of certification for LTC; means of hospital transportation; and instrumental activities of daily living, such as shopping and laundry. We selected these two items as representatives of household outside the house (shopping) and inside the house (laundry). See Table S1 for details.

Participants

From April 2023 to March 2024, the questionnaire was distributed to all patients aged 80 and older who were regular visitors to the department's outpatient department. Patients were asked to complete the questionnaire while waiting for their consultation. For those unable to complete the form because of physical or cognitive disabilities, their accompanying caregivers were requested to assist, or the nursing staff completed the questionnaire based on the patients' responses.

Analysis

We summarized the ratios of each answer via Microsoft Excel 2021. Missing data were handled using pairwise deletion.

Ethical considerations

Patient consent was obtained through an opt‐out process. A study notice was displayed in the hospital's waiting area and on its website, allowing patients the opportunity to decline participation if they did not wish their data to be included. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Tokyo Graduate School of Medicine and Faculty of Medicine (No. 2022315NI). This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement 20 and the Consensus Reporting Items for Studies in Primary Care (CRISP) Statement. 21

RESULTS

A total of 371 patients were invited to participate in the study, of which 12 declined, resulting in 359 participants. The median age of the participants was 84 years (interquartile range: 82–88). Female participants accounted for 222 individuals (61.8%). Among the participants, 192 (53.5%) had not applied for LTC certification. A total of 57 participants (15.9%) were at Support Levels, while 101 participants (28.1%) were at Care Need Levels. Only a small number of participants were classified as Care Need Level 3 or higher. DISCUSSION

This study revealed the proportion of family caregivers' involvement in healthcare access and daily household tasks among older patients regularly visiting a primary care department in a small hospital in Japan. Among participants requiring any vehicle or support to visit the hospital, approximately one‐third drove themselves, another one‐third were accompanied by a family member, one‐sixth used taxi services, and the remaining one‐sixth relied on public transportation or LTC services. These findings indicate a limited utilization of official support systems for hospital visits.

Barriers to accessing primary care exacerbate health inequities, disproportionately affecting socially marginalized populations.22, 23 To enhance healthcare accessibility, the older population should be recognized as a group with distinct needs. 24 However, support for hospital visits remains insufficient. A 2018 study in the US revealed that most older adults depend on private vehicles or assistance from family members, friends, or paid caregivers for transportation to medical facilities, thereby placing those of lower socioeconomic status at heightened risk of losing access to care. 25 In Japan, about 80% of older adults in a rural area depend on cars in their daily livings, 26 and access to hospital care is an important issue. In urban settings, economically disadvantaged patients frequently rely on public transportation, yet its use is hindered by frailty and poorly maintained sidewalks. 27 The findings of this study suggest the need for more accessible and practical transportation services for older adults. In Japan, official LTC services classify assistance with hospital visits under home‐care services; however, these services do not explicitly permit family members to accompany patients, leading to situations where family members may decline to provide transportation. To improve access to care, it may be necessary to revise service regulations to allow for more flexible and supportive use, including provisions for family accompaniment.

This study also revealed that participants were more likely to receive support from family caregivers than from LTC services for both outdoor tasks, such as shopping, and indoor tasks, such as laundry. These findings align with previous research indicating that most family caregivers provide support for activities of daily living. 28 In addition, even among participants with higher LTC certification levels—who presumably have access to a wider range of services—the reliance on family members for these tasks did not diminish. This suggests that the existing LTC services may be insufficient to meet the full scope of daily living support required by older adults. Previous studies suggest that the functional independence of older individuals does not necessarily reduce the burden on family caregivers, 29 and primary care professionals should recognize that LTC certification levels do not directly correspond to the caregiving burden experienced by families.

Primary care professionals can derive the following key insights from this study. First, it is essential to recognize that many older adults face significant barriers to accessing medical care. Professionals should strive to address these challenges across micro, meso, and macro levels. Given the multifaceted nature of healthcare access barriers,24, 30 no universally effective intervention has been established, and individualized approaches are needed. Gaining an understanding of how patients travel to clinics may inform strategies to alleviate the burden on both patients and their families—such as coordinating transportation services, adjusting appointment schedules, or modifying the frequency of visits. Second, primary care professionals can coordinate supplemental support more effectively by identifying which needs are not adequately addressed by LTC services. Considering that many older adults with multiple chronic conditions depend on informal caregivers for daily functioning, 31 such insights may enhance the integration and efficiency of care. These insights can enhance primary care professionals' role in delivering comprehensive care that includes attention to the well‐being of family caregivers.32, 33, 34 Family caregivers frequently experience significant burdens,35, 36 and without appropriate support, caregiving can negatively affect their financial, emotional, and psychological health. 37 Implementing strategies to reduce caregiver strain is beneficial not only for the caregivers themselves but also for the patients they support.

This study has some limitations. First, this study was conducted in a small, single hospital. The way of hospital visits is greatly influenced by the geographical context of the hospital. 30 The findings that many older patients drive themselves or have family members bring them to the hospital may suggest the need to improve access to care. Second, the data primarily relied on patient self‐reports and lacked verification by other stakeholders. This approach stemmed from the simultaneous collection of subjective information regarding patients' daily lives through the questionnaire. In cases where patients were unable to respond because of cognitive decline or other reasons, family members provided answers; when this was also difficult, outpatient staff assisted with the responses. Discrepancies in perceptions between patients and family members may emerge, particularly concerning the individual primarily responsible for household tasks. Nevertheless, the high proportion of patients reporting reliance on family members for such tasks underscores the substantial burden placed on family caregivers. Third, we did not collect data on the household composition of the participants, which may hinder the external validity of the findings. Fourth, we only asked about shopping and laundry as instrumental ADLs. We limited the items of the questionnaire for participants' convenience. However, a more comprehensive survey is warranted. Fifth, this study used the LTC certification only by assessing patients' physical and cognitive decline, rather than using specific and validated measures to evaluate ADLs or cognitive function. While this approach reduced respondent burden and enhanced feasibility, it also overlooked detailed patient characteristics. For example, chronic disease status and poverty exert disproportionate effects on access to outpatient care38, 39; however, this study did not examine associations with patients' socioeconomic status or chronic conditions. Further research using more comprehensive questionnaires is necessary to identify which patient profiles are associated with greater caregiving burdens.

CONCLUSION

Older patients who regularly visit primary care facilities often rely on family caregivers for both accessing healthcare and managing routine household tasks. Primary care professionals are well‐positioned to identify these common challenges and address both the healthcare and social support requirements of older patients and their families.
