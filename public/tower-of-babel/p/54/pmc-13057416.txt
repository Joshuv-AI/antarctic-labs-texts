Clozapine for treatment‐resistant schizophrenia with epilepsy: A case report
Authors: Toru Horinouchi, Mayusa Mito, Toshiyuki Ishikawa, Yuichi Nakamura, Shuhei Ishikawa, Naoki Hashimoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTreatment‐resistant schizophrenia (TRS) poses a substantial clinical challenge for which clozapine (CLOZ) is the only effective treatment. However, clinicians may hesitate to prescribe CLOZ for TRS patients with epilepsy due to its presence because CLOZ is contraindicated in patients with uncontrolled epilepsy in some countries including Japan.Case PresentationA 36‐year‐old male with TRS and focal epilepsy underwent CLOZ therapy. CLOZ was initiated at 6.25 mg and gradually titrated to 250 mg over 3 months, achieving a plasma concentration of 449 ng/mL. Psychotic symptoms improved without worsening of seizure frequency or severity. Mild side effects such as drowsiness and drooling were manageable. Long‐term video electroencephalography monitoring detected spike‐and‐wave activity centered in the frontal region, but did not detect any overt epileptic seizures.ConclusionWe report a case of TRS with comorbid epilepsy in which CLOZ therapy was both effective and safe.

BACKGROUND

Treatment‐resistant schizophrenia (TRS) is a substantial clinical challenge for which clozapine (CLOZ) is the only effective treatment. 1 However, in Japan and the European Union (EU), the package insert for CLOZ lists uncontrolled epilepsy as a contraindication. Therefore, clinicians may hesitate to prescribe CLOZ for TRS patients with epilepsy due to its presence. Although our literature review identified several case reports and series documenting the use of CLOZ in patients with psychosis and pre‐existing epilepsy, 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 clinical evidence regarding management protocols remains insufficient. In this report, we present a case of successful CLOZ administration in a patient with TRS and epilepsy, managed through a cautious titration strategy guided by regular C‐reactive protein (CRP) monitoring and longitudinal clinical evaluations using standardized scales.

CASE PRESENTATION

Informed consent for publication was obtained from the patient, with minor modifications made to protect his identity.

The patient was a 36‐year‐old man with no significant medical or family history of mental illness or epilepsy. At the age of 7, he began experiencing sudden episodes of loss of consciousness. Despite treatment at a local clinic, these episodes persisted, and focal to bilateral tonic‐clonic seizures (FBTCS) later developed. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed no significant lesions; however, electroencephalography (EEG) and cerebral blood flow single‐photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) indicated focal epilepsy originating in the frontal lobe (no information on laterality).

After failing several entrance examinations, he entered a university in the Kanto region of Japan. While initially stable, he began experiencing monthly FBTCS episodes around the age of 24. Additionally, he began experiencing auditory hallucinations and a delusional mood, which he did not share with others for several years. Despite hospitalization and treatment for epilepsy, including lamotrigine (LTG), valproic acid (VPA), carbamazepine (CBZ), and acetazolamide (AZM), the seizure frequency decreased but did not resolve completely.

At age 28, a psychiatrist in the private clinic prescribed antipsychotics such as olanzapine and quetiapine, but their effects were limited. At the age of 29, he was referred to our department while taking olanzapine 20 mg, quetiapine 50 mg, zolpidem 10 mg, flunitrazepam 2 mg, LTG 300 mg, VPA 1200 mg, CBZ 400 mg, and AZM 500 mg.

His symptoms included active auditory hallucinations causing monologs, a delusional mood leading to social withdrawal, and persistent insomnia. Initially, interictal psychosis was suspected, and treatment with antipsychotic drugs was intensified. However, the sequential use of aripiprazole, risperidone, brexpiprazole, lurasidone, and paliperidone was unsuccessful. He experienced focal awareness seizures (FAS), characterized by dizziness and a loss of depth perception, focal impaired awareness seizures (FIAS), during which he repeatedly looked to the right, and FIAS with sleep‐related convulsions. However, these seizures were rare and occurred only several times a year. At that time, epilepsy was deemed relatively well‐controlled, and no temporal association was identified between seizures and psychotic symptoms. Consequently, his condition was re‐evaluated, and diagnosed as schizophrenia according to the DSM‐5‐TR criteria. 9 After confirming that the patient met the TRS criteria, the patient was admitted for CLOZ initiation.

At admission, his psychotropic medications included paliperidone 12 mg, LTG 300 mg (plasma concentration [PC] 13.56 μg/mL), VPA 1200 mg (PC 76.4 μg/mL), gabapentin 1000 mg (PC 12.0 μg/mL), lemborexant 10 mg, and trazodone 200 mg. Based on the low seizure frequency (the most recent seizure had occurred more than 2 years ago), excellent drug adherence, and PC of ASMs within the therapeutic range, the patient's condition was not categorized as “uncontrolled epilepsy.”

After confirming normal blood tests, electrocardiogram (ECG), and echocardiogram results, and after a thorough discussion of potential risks, including seizure exacerbation, the patient and his family consented to initiate CLOZ therapy. Treatment with CLOZ was initiated at a lower dose (6.25 mg) and titrated slower than recommended by the package insert. The blood test showed that the CRP level reached its highest value (4.19 mg/dL) 2 weeks after starting CLOZ, and then remained at approximately 0–1.0 mg/dL during his 5‐month hospitalization. It took more than 3 months to increase the daily dose of CLOZ to 250 mg (PC 449 ng/mL). Psychotic symptoms improved, delusional mood disappeared, and auditory hallucinations were subjectively reduced by 30%, as reflected by an improvement in the Brief Evaluation of Psychosis Symptom Domains – Japanese version (BE‐PSD‐J) score from 12 to 6. The side effects, including drowsiness, drooling, and constipation, were mild and did not necessitate discontinuation of CLOZ.

During his 5‐month hospitalization, he experienced three episodes of FAS characterized by dizziness, but no overt FIAS or FBTCS. ASM doses remained unchanged throughout this period. VPA PC (measured 7 times) and LTG PC (measured 6 times) remained stable, with mean (SD) values of 70.5 (7.4) and 14.3 (1.3) μg/mL, respectively, indicating consistent ASM levels during the admission. Routine EEG prior to CLOZ initiation showed no interictal epileptiform discharge (IED) (Figure 1a). After CLOZ was increased to 250 mg, long‐term video EEG monitoring was conducted for 4 days to investigate the possibility of epileptic seizures that the patient was unaware of; however, no epileptic seizures were detected. Spike‐and‐waves (SPW) centered in the frontal regions (F3 and F7, or Fp2 and F8) were scarcely observed (only three times over 4 days) (Figure 1b).

Occupational therapy was encouraged as a daytime activity, and the casework enabled him to access continuous support for employment services (Type B) which is one of the welfare service in Japan that provides work or training for people with disabilities.

At discharge, his psychotropic medications included CLOZ (250 mg), LTG (300 mg), and VPA (1200 mg). He continued to attend a vocational program with no exacerbation of symptoms or seizures.

DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

We successfully initiated CLOZ in a patient with TRS and epilepsy. Although the seizures were not completely suppressed by anti‐seizure medications (ASMs), no FBTCS or FIAS occurred after CLOZ initiation, with only infrequent FAS episodes observed. Various evaluation scales are useful for accurately monitoring the course of psychiatric symptoms and the range of side effects influenced by CLOZ.

Our literature review further supports the feasibility of CLOZ treatment in patients with psychosis and comorbid epilepsy (Table 2). In all 16 cases identified, psychotic symptoms improved. Regarding seizure outcomes, among the 12 cases with available data, 5 reported “improved,” 6 reported “no remarkable change,” and only 1 reported “worsened.” In these successful cases, the prescribed ASMs varied widely, although the use of VPA and LTG was relatively frequent. Furthermore, the final CLOZ dosage ranged broadly, indicating that treatment can be tailored to individual needs. The relatively high proportion of “improved” cases may be attributed to the fact that the stabilization of psychotic symptoms by CLOZ can enhance medication adherence and reduce emotional disturbances. 3 Furthermore, the fact that 11 out of 12 cases showed “improved” or “no remarkable change” suggests that pre‐existing ASM regimens may effectively suppress CLOZ‐induced seizures. Notably, however, one case was reported as “worsened.” In the case of schizophrenia with epilepsy, tonic‐clonic seizures multiply occurred after the CLOZ administration, even though the patient had been taking VPA and her epilepsy had been under control for over 20 years. 4 This deterioration cannot be easily explained by the type of ASMs or the CLOZ dosage alone compared to other successful cases, highlighting the unpredictability of seizure occurrence after CLOZ initiation in this population.

Regarding the occurrence of the three FAS episodes during hospitalization, their relationship with CLOZ should be considered. Assuming that the patient's self‐reported seizure frequency prior to admission was accurate, the emergence of frontal SPWs following CLOZ initiation—where none were observed before initiation—suggests that CLOZ may have enhanced epileptic activity, potentially triggering these FAS episodes as a side effect. Another possibility is that CLOZ induced subclinical epileptogenic activation of the pre‐existing focal epilepsy. However, given that these episodes remained within the scope of FAS and had a small impact on the patient's daily functioning, we consider epilepsy to have remained within the range of “no remarkable change” relative to the pre‐CLOZ baseline.

Several reasons could explain why we were able to use CLOZ without exacerbating seizures. One probable reason for this is the co‐administration of ASMs. Previous reports indicated that CLOZ was safe and effective in a patient with TRS and epilepsy, and that ASM co‐administration prevented seizure recurrence. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 17

Another probable reason is the PC of the CLOZ. If the PC of CLOZ exceeds 500 ng/mL, the concomitant administration of ASMs is considered to mitigate the risk of seizure exacerbation. 18 Although extrapolation of the results of this study should be carefully considered because of the comorbid epilepsy in our case, the fact that the PC of CLOZ was less than 500 ng/mL in our case may have contributed to the lack of exacerbation of the seizures.

In this case, CLOZ was initiated at a slower titration pace than recommended in the package insert and at a low dose of 6.25 mg daily, with CRP monitored. This aligns with individualized titration guidelines recommending a 6.25 mg/day starting dose in Asian patients, who are often classified as poor metabolizers, especially when factors such as concomitant VPA use, oral contraceptive use, or obesity are present. 19 The safety of this low‐dose approach has been reported, including successful titration to therapeutic levels even in a high‐risk patient with a history of CLOZ‐induced myocarditis. 20 Furthermore, concurrent use of VPA during CLOZ initiation has been associated with an increased incidence of side effects, including myocarditis, and a higher rate of CLOZ discontinuation. 21 , 22 , 23 Inflammation may significantly increase CLOZ concentrations via CYP1A2 inhibition; therefore, it is recommended to temporarily suspend dose escalation when CRP levels are elevated. 19 , 24 While several reports have documented CLOZ use in patients with epilepsy, none have described a management strategy incorporating regular CRP monitoring (Table 2). In this case, although CRP reached 4 mg/dL once 3 weeks after initiation, the overall CRP levels remained stable, allowing safe continuation of the dose escalation. This cautious approach, combining individualized starting doses with monitoring of inflammatory markers, is considered a contributing factor to achieving good tolerability despite the high risk of seizures. It should be noted, however, that CRP primarily reflects inflammatory status and CLOZ metabolism, and its relationship to seizure control is therefore indirect.

The clinical significance of the therapeutic response was further validated using multiple standardized scales. In this case, the BE‐PSD‐J score decreased from 12 to 6, achieving a 50% reduction in symptom severity. This outcome is noteworthy when compared to a previous study of 108 Japanese TRS patients treated with CLOZ, which reported a mean BE‐PSD total score of 12.0 (SD 3.9). 25 Our patient's baseline score of 12 was consistent with this average, and the subsequent reduction to 6 indicates a robust clinical improvement. Furthermore, the stability of the DIEPSS (score of 0) and JESS scores (score of 3) throughout the titration was instrumental in our safety monitoring. Given that CLOZ can cause various side effects, some of which may be severe, the use of multiple standardized scales ensures that even subtle adverse events are not overlooked, thereby significantly enhancing patient safety during the complex titration process.

These measures may have contributed to the favorable tolerability observed with CLOZ treatment in this case.

In patients with TRS who also have epilepsy, CLOZ could be introduced without exacerbating seizures by using a sufficient dose of ASM and by adjusting the CLOZ dose over time while monitoring the inflammatory response and PC.
