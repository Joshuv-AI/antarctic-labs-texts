Giving Meaning to Technological Artifacts in One’s Life: An Aspect of Personal Autonomy in a Technological Society
Authors: Shinya Oie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In today’s world, where advanced technology such as artificial intelligence (AI) is reshaping human society, how can individuals’ autonomy in the use of such technology be understood? This paper focuses on the meanings of technological artifacts in individuals’ lives—that is, the subjective interpretation of their function within one’s plan for use—and attempts to formalize a set of human capacities to actively give meaning to technological artifacts as a form of personal autonomy. This study shows that this set of capacities includes the capacity to recognize the functions of a given technology, the capacity to plan a practical activity using the technology to achieve a desirable existence, and the capacity to actually conduct the activity. These findings are applied and examined in an example involving the improvement of call center operators’ work using AI.

Introduction

Throughout history, humanity has always existed as beings who use tools, and tools have consistently been important objects that complement humans and make them human (Stiegler, 1998). However, especially in modern societies, people are surrounded by technological artifacts and environments on an unprecedented scale, shaping their lives while being influenced by them. Technological nudges (Thaler & Sunstein, 2021), persuasive technology (Fogg, 2003), and affordances (Norman, 2013) define, direct, and enable our daily lives in unprecedentedly sophisticated ways, and artificial intelligence (AI), when combined with these, further amplifies their influence.

One concept that holds promise for appropriately analyzing this situation and providing normative guidance is personal autonomy, which refers to an individual’s self-governance (Christman, 2009). Through this concept, it is possible to understand both human affectedness—the extent to which people’s actions and decisions are guided by technological artifacts and environments—and human agency—the extent to which people actively shape their own lives using these artifacts and environments. Exploring human autonomy in a technological society is critically important both from an ethical perspective—considering good actions and the good life for humans within such a society—and from a political philosophical perspective—examining the relationship between modern technologies such as information technology and democracy. This situation is readily apparent even from a cursory glance at various challenges such as technology addiction (Alter, 2018); misinformation, disinformation, and fake news (Broda & Strömbäck, 2024); and propaganda (Morgan, 2018).

This paper focuses on the capacity to give meaning to technology—that is, the capacity of human users to relate to the technological artifacts they utilize as having specific significance at key junctures in their own lives. While this capacity has not been sufficiently explored in previous research, it can be regarded as a crucial element of personal autonomy in a technological society. The purpose of this paper is to gain as detailed a descriptive understanding as possible of what this set of capacities involves. Importantly, this paper discusses the meaning of artifacts in one’s life from the perspective of the subjective interpretations of their functions.

For this purpose, this paper refines the concept of “relational freedom” proposed by the philosopher of technology Peter-Paul Verbeek (Verbeek, 2011, 2014). This concept describes how humans actively reflect on the influence of technology on their lives and incorporate it appropriately into their lives to accomplish self-formation. In the process of exercising this freedom, the user’s own activity of giving technology meaning—that is, a subjective interpretation of its functions—clearly plays a central role. For this reason, the concept of relational freedom serves as the primary frame of reference for this study. However, the above author merely notes the fact that users can employ a variety of uses for a given technology, without delving deeply into how these diverse uses relate to meaning in the user’s life. This situation renders his discussion fatally flawed as a description of the freedom of technology users, thus necessitating further explanation. Therefore, the author develops his discussion, addressing the above issue from the perspective of the mechanism through which artifacts are attributed to unique meanings in users’ lives and users’ capacity to actively engage with that mechanism.

The concept of multistability in postphenomenology plays a crucial role in developing this mechanism. Multistability is a concept that indicates the breadth and limits of the meanings attributed to an artifact. While this concept excels at analyzing the social meaning of artifacts, whether it explicitly includes individual life as a point of contention remains ambiguous. This study expands the concept to explicitly include this point—in other words, it presents a framework that clearly enables subjective analysis. The foundation for this extension lies in early Martin Heidegger’s analysis of the utility of tools. By adopting Heidegger’s approach, the subjective perspective of individual users is theoretically organized from the standpoint of purpose-oriented practical activity. By linking this practical activity to the self-formation perspective within Verbeek’s concept of relational freedom, the mechanism explored here is conceived.

This paper consists of three parts. The first part highlights the approach taken in this paper and distinguishes it from previous research in moral psychology regarding individual autonomy. In the second part, a model of the mechanism through which technological artifacts are attributed meanings in one’s life is discussed. At this point, Verbeek’s concept of relational freedom is introduced, along with an outline of the prospects and methods for refining it, followed by the actual endeavor to undertake this work. Finally, a model of the mechanism through which technological artifacts are given meanings in an individual’s life is presented. The set of capacities to actively engage with this mechanism is precisely the autonomy capacity explored in this paper—the capacity to give meaning to artifacts in one’s life. This insight is supported by a case study analysis of an AI tool designed for customer service operators.

The Capacity to Give Meaning to Technology as Personal Autonomy

Problem Setting

This paper focuses on the meaning of technological artifacts in the lives of individual users. It proposes the individual’s capacity to actively engage with the meaning of these artifacts as a key aspect of autonomy. In this section, preparatory work is undertaken. Specifically, this paper demonstrates how the approach taken relates to existing research in the theory of autonomy, how it differs from those studies, and how it opens up new horizons.

A car is obviously a means of transporting people and goods. However, for certain people, it is also a source of personal enjoyment, one that provides them with an opportunity, for example, to decorate the car’s interior and exterior or fill the interior with lovely scents. A kitchen knife may be an important tool for a sushi chef, but for someone else, it may be a source of fear originating from a childhood memory of cutting one’s finger. The world humans inhabit is a world of meaning, and the majority of its components in modern times are technological artifacts. Our lives, in turn, are fundamentally shaped by our engagement with the meaning of these artifacts. Viewed in this way, giving meaning to the technological artifacts surrounding us can be seen as the capacity to actively construct a meaningful world and life.

Exploring this capacity is highly significant in terms of both theory and practice. This is because the way in which technological artifacts are meaningful in individuals’ lives has become an extremely important ethical and political theme. These artifacts are gaining importance in the lives of individuals because technology plays an increasingly important role in stimulating human decisions and enabling or disabling human actions. Furthermore, with the increasing sophistication and advancement of technology, the scope of what can be achieved through technology, such as the effective use of personal information and other data, has expanded significantly. As a result, technological artifacts are now affecting individuals’ social lives considerably. This is clearly demonstrated by examples such as social media influencing voters’ decision-making (the most obvious and shocking case being the Cambridge Analytica scandal1) and suicide cases linked to AI addiction (for example, the case of a teenager who committed suicide after chatting with an AI chatbot2). Undoubtedly, in such cases, a key point of discussion is what meaning a particular technological artifact or environment (such as social media or AI) is assigned in the individual user’s life and whether people have been able to make appropriate decisions and shape their own lives in light of those meanings.

In response to this situation, while legal regulations and related discussions are essential, it is crucial for society to emphasize and nurture individuals’ active rethinking and conscious reconstruction of the meaning of technological artifacts and environments in their lives. It is unlikely that an approach biased toward either side alone can be sufficiently effective. Rather, a composite approach combining external constraints or incentives with individuals’ intrinsic efforts is desirable to resolve the situation. The capacity for people to engage with the meaning of technological artifacts in their own lives is therefore of urgent importance.

Approaches in Moral Psychology and the Meaning of Technology

The most influential concept for describing and normalizing human activity—particularly the process by which individuals shape their own lives through decision-making—is that of personal autonomy. Then, how can existing research on personal autonomy address the abovementioned issues? To consider this, let us take a look at how autonomy has been studied. Research findings on personal autonomy have primarily accumulated within the field of moral psychology. They can be broadly classified into four types of explanations: (1) explanations focusing on coherence (between the agent’s motives and the mental states expressing her or his perspective), (2) explanations relying on reason-responsiveness, (3) explanations giving attention to one’s reasoning process, and (4) incompatibilist explanations, which are different from the other three explanation types (Buss & Westlund, 2018).

Although the four explanation types differ, they share the common feature of addressing human mental phenomena (such as “desires”) and mental capacities (such as “reflection”) as their subject matter. For example, in a coherentist explanation, i.e., the first of the four types mentioned above, one of the main autonomy capacities is considered a sort of higher-order control over lower-order desires—which includes, for example, the greater capacity to reflect, identify, accept, and change lower-order desires (Frankfurt, 1971; Dworkin, 1988). By exercising such capacities, a person is supposed to evaluate whether one’s lower-order desires are meaningful in her or his life and incorporate them into one’s life or change them to fit one’s life.

To summarize moral psychology-based theories of autonomy, particularly coherentist accounts, would be to regard autonomy as a capacity for mental control over one’s mental phenomena. Now, is such an explanation sufficient when addressing the meaning of artifacts?

While the approach is reasonable, it requires significant development. The reason for this is that although the “meaning” of technological artifacts in one’s personal life is undoubtedly a type of mental phenomenon or thought, no research explicitly investigates this as a target of control by autonomy capacity. Hence, the following research is at least necessary: clarifying what the meaning of artifacts in one’s life is and what it means to control it.

Referring to research in the philosophy of engineering, the meaning of an artifact can be proposed to be understood as the subjective interpretation of its function within the use plan set by the user. The use plan here is defined as follows: a series of actions in which manipulations of an artifact are included as contributions to realize the given goal (cf., Vermaas & Houkes, 2006). Suppose that there is a woman who wants to make her time traveling by car and taking short breaks during the trip more relaxing. This woman’s use plan for her private car would be to enjoy a pleasant travel time and relaxing break. The given goal is to make the travel time and break pleasant, the object she manipulates to achieve this is her private car (specifically, its interior and devices), and the manipulation consists of decorative acts (such as placing a bottle of perfume or arranging small accessories). The series of actions involves active engagement with the car, including these decorative acts, as well as the actual driving and resting. Viewed in this way, the meaning attributed to the artifact—namely, the private car—is “a vehicle or space that provides pleasant travel and rest times.” Thus, this artifact is interpreted subjectively.

Considering this, the capacity to give meaning to artifacts as a form of autonomy capacity can be tentatively defined as follows: the capacity to realize desirable situations by formulating plans for using artifacts, employing them in the desirable manner within those plans, and thereby performing their functions as expected.

This capacity manifests in various aspects of human life, such as when one cherishes something, such as a car or a kitchen knife, but something that one can, nevertheless, discard when it is no longer needed or give a new or changed value, all the while experiencing emotions, such as joy, anger, sadness, and delight, in connection with the object and taking actions or making decisions concerning it. In the preceding example of the knife, the person who cut her or his finger may be able to overcome childhood trauma by incorporating a kitchen knife into her or his life in a different way (e.g., by learning to cook simple meals) and thereby creating a new life for her or himself. This can clearly be considered one of the capacities of utilizing technology, one that is separate from the capacity of knowing how—that is, the (often tacit) capability concerning specific methods of use, such as knowledge and techniques regarding how to perform tasks or follow procedures.

However, how can this capacity for autonomy—as a control over the meaning of artifacts in one’s life—be explored? This paper focuses on the perspective of the philosophy of technology. This research focuses on technological artifacts, particularly their meaning, and therefore has the potential to provide a direct solution to the issue. Specifically, this study builds on insights from prior research to explore the mechanisms through which technological artifacts are attributed specific meanings within individuals’ lives. The mechanism described here reveals how the technological world manifests as meaningful to individuals. If individuals can actively participate in this mechanism by exercising specific capacities, then it can be identified as the capacity to give meaning to technology.

Exploring the Meaning of Technology in One’s Everyday Life

Self-Practice of Subject Constitution and the Capacity for Relational Freedom

The discussion proceeds as follows. First, it provides a detailed examination of the concept of “relational freedom” proposed by Peter-Paul Verbeek, a philosopher of technology from the postphenomenological tradition (Verbeek, 2011, 2014). The exercise of this freedom clearly involves the unique activity of users giving meaning to technology; while this serves as the primary framework for this paper, his framework requires expansion based on other relevant research. By referencing the concept of “multistability” in postphenomenology and Martin Heidegger’s early analysis of the utility of tools, the mechanism by which technology is ascribed some meanings within the individual’s subjective world is elucidated. Verbeek’s concept of freedom is revised and expanded to encompass the individual’s capacity to actively engage in this mechanism.

Verbeek has conducted a well-known analysis of expecting parents who have access to obstetric ultrasound, exploring the theme of the “self-practice of subject constitution.” On the one hand, ultrasound technology, which makes the fetus visible, has the potential to embed specific perspectives on the fetus (e.g., a set of risks for disease) in expecting parents. On the other hand, expecting parents can deliberately use this technology in their own way to pursue their ideal existence (Verbeek, 2011, 2014). In short, technology has an impact on users when it is used, but users can thoughtfully incorporate that impact into their lives in their own way. Verbeek does not explicitly endorse research on individual autonomy. However, his exploration of self-direction exercised by individuals amidst external influences is likely to greatly inspire research on individual autonomy.

Verbeek positions this self-practice of subject constitution as the exercise of parents’ “relational freedom” and attempts to construct an ethics of the good life for technology users. This freedom can be called relational not because it signifies the absence of external influences but rather because it values actively engaging with such influences. This ethics is not about regulating individual behavior but rather about enabling individuals to explore their own good lives independently using technology. Subsequent researchers have attempted to develop this theory of ethics. For example, by carefully analyzing Verbeek’s description, Shinya Oie clearly identifies the components of what Verbeek refers to as the capacities of relational freedom. These are capacities for “realization” and “moral reflection” regarding the influence of technology and the “appropriation” and “modification” of technological influence in one’s own way to incorporate it into one’s life (Oie, 2023).

Verbeek’s self-practice of subject constitution clearly focuses on the exercise of capacities in the utilization of technology by individual users (namely, relational freedom). In this context, the question of what meaning technology (e.g., ultrasound diagnostic technology) has in the lives of users (e.g., expecting parents) is undoubtedly an important issue. However, his discussion cannot be said to be sufficient because he does not develop his research on the meaning of technology in one’s life. This problem is clearly evident in the fact that despite the assertion by Verbeek that various variants of relational freedom do indeed appear in the use of (ultrasound diagnostic) technology, he remains unable to adequately explain the reasons for such differences.

In other words, in Verbeek’s explanation, the more subtle question of what meaning ultrasound diagnostic technology comes to have in a person’s life is obscured, or black-boxed, by a broader framework in which parents, whose views of the fetus are influenced by technology, can still exercise relational freedom. The mechanism through which ultrasound diagnostic technology is given unique meaning in parents’ lives and the capacity of parents to actively engage in that mechanism need to be clarified.

The mechanism through which technological artifacts are given certain meanings in human life and outline the capacity of human users to give meaning to these artifacts on the basis of their interactions with the mechanism will be modeled in a way that integrates and deepens Verbeek’s ethical framework of the self-practice of subject constitution. This integration and deepening will result in a clearer understanding of the capacity of relational freedom than has been explicitly stated thus far, i.e., the capacity for realization and moral reflection on the impact of technology and the capacity for appropriation and modification to incorporate the impact of technology into one’s life.

Social Meanings of Technology and Multistability

What previous research must be developed if we are to construct a model of the mechanism through which technological artifacts are attributed certain meanings in individuals’ lives, thereby integrating Verbeek’s framework of self-practice? In fact, research on the concept of multistability in postphenomenology provides important insights into this topic.

To understand the concept of multistability, it is necessary to first clarify the theoretical context in which it is situated. Since the so-called “empirical turn” (Achterhuis, 2001), some of the most energetic research in the philosophy of technology (here referring particularly to the tradition of continental philosophy) has focused on analyzing the social meanings of everyday, concrete technological artifacts. In particular, such research has provided key insights into how technological artifacts come to occupy a place (or become available) within social activities and acquire distinctive social functions. One of the authors who has made major contributions to this field is Andrew Feenberg. His example of communication on the internet by patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) serves as an ideal type of communicative rationality brought about by the availability of technology (Feenberg, 1999). Furthermore, the example of Minitel he presented provided an indication of how the use or occupation of technological artifacts that had not been anticipated achieved a certain degree of standardization (Feenberg, 1995). The former example can be understood as an instance of technology acquiring a particular social meaning, whereas the latter example can be understood as an instance of the social meaning of technology undergoing a transformation. On the other hand, research has also presented the inflexibility of the social meaning or function of technology. For example, Langdon Winner’s famous case of discriminatory overpasses strongly argued that social problems, such as racial discrimination, can be concretized in the physical environment and become fixed (Winner, 1986). In this way, the fact that specific technological artifacts can have (multiple) social meanings has become an important element for consideration in the philosophy of technology. Note that the cited researchers are not so-called postphenomenological researchers but rather researchers in the field of the political philosophy of technology.

Multistability is a concept proposed by Don Ihde, a founding pioneer of postphenomenology, within the context of the social nature of the technologies described above. This concept indicates that a technological artifact can have multiple identities in a society (Ihde, 1990, p.144).Further, I have argued that at the complex level of a cultural hermeneutics, technologies may be variantly embedded; the “same” technology in another cultural context becomes quite a “different” technology.It is now time to place a name upon that ambiguous structure and describe what I take to be its shape or shapes. The name, following the perceptualist strategy I have chosen for that technology–culture structure, is multistability. (Ibid., emphasis in original)

Ihde’s research has been further advanced by subsequent researchers, resulting in important theoretical developments, such as the theoretical connection with Feenberg’s “critical constructivism” (Rosenberger, 2020) and the connection with affordance theory (de Boer, 2023). The former will be discussed in detail later. A postphenomenological researcher, Robert Rosenberger, provides a concise explanation of the concept of multistability.That is, a technology can always be put to multiple purposes, can always fit in to multiple contexts, can always be meaningful in different ways to different people, can always evolve differently within different cultures, and never reduces to only that meaning and usage for which its designer had in mind.At the same time, the notion of multistability also includes the idea that any technology is always limited with regard to what it can mean and how it may be used. In this terminology, any technology is restricted to particular, though possibly extensively multiple, “stabilities” (or “variations”). (Rosenberger, 2020, p.86)

In short, multistability is a concept that indicates the breadth and depth of the meaning attributed to technological artifacts.

Multistability and Giving Meaning to Technological Artifacts in One’s Own Life

It is precisely in demonstrating the breadth and limits of the meaning of technology that the concept of multistability can contribute to the goal of this paper. This is because exploring this concept can enable the modeling of the mechanism by which technological artifacts come to have unique meanings in a person’s life. This mechanism explains the process by which a technological artifact comes to occupy a specific position in an individual’s life, not as a given fact but as one of several possibilities. For example, a smartphone can have a wide range of meanings. It is, of course, a telephone and a computer equipped with various apps. However, it can also be a means of managing fear and anxiety about darkness or the city at night (Hardly 2021), a means of constructing identity, by taking selfies at museums, for example (Lewis, 2017), or a means of self-management through smartphone-based exercise (Zheng, 2023). The possibility of installing diverse apps enables such diverse meanings. On the other hand, smartphones can have a limited number of meanings. They cannot be a means of transportation that moves around or a place where one can swim or run. Their nature as palm-sized electronic devices (they do not have engines, cannot hold water like a swimming pool and are not wide and sturdy surfaces) limits the number of their meanings.

However, the above discussion focuses primarily on pointing out the fact that technological artifacts are multistable (i.e., capable of being given various different meanings) and does not sufficiently explore the mechanisms by which these variations in meaning arise. Thus, it is necessary to refine the concept of multistability by referring to prior research on how variations in meaning emerge.

Axis of Difference and Axis of Usage

Robert Rosenberger’s (2020) refinement of the concept of multistability is a good point of reference here. Rosenberger uses multistability to analyze discriminatory architecture and artifacts that prevent certain people from behaving in certain ways. Although his analysis focuses on the limited technological artifacts of discriminatory architecture, it is useful as a reference for developing the concept of multistability, as he provides an accurate explanation of the concept and adds important insights. His main contribution is the introduction of two new axes: the axis of difference and the axis of usage.

First, the axis of difference comes into play when the same artifact is experienced differently by different users. “This form of discrimination occurs as some users are put at a disadvantage as the device relates differently to the individual differences between people and groups of people” (Ibid., p.97). He cites the example of a hammer designed for men. It might be difficult for a woman to use a hammer designed for men given its weight and size. Such a dominant standard of hammer design highlights the fact that a certain dominant stability excludes other forms of stability.

The second axis is that of usage, which becomes an issue when users are excluded from specific uses. “An exploration of the multistability of technology upon this investigative pivot would consider the different purposes to which the object of study may be put […]. But we can additionally explore the particular ways that the target technology has been designed to ‘close off’ specific uses. Such moves to shut down particular stabilities can be analyzed in terms of their role in discrimination” (Ibid., p.99).

He refers to various public bench designs introduced as measures against certain forms of bench use by homeless people. For example, benches with partitions or armrests are designed to prevent a particular use, namely, sleeping.

To summarize, the axis of difference causes certain users to benefit from using an artifact, whereas the axis of usage forces all users to use an artifact in a specific way, with each axis producing a certain degree of exclusionary stability in terms of utilization.

Objective Meaning and Subjective Meaning

Using these two axes to analyze architectural discrimination appears to have achieved a certain degree of success. However, what is more important for this paper is the way in which Rosenberger introduces the two axes into the concept of multistability. According to Rosenberger’s analysis, both the first axis (the axis of difference) and the second (the axis of usage) share the following two characteristics. First, the person who analyzes the possible meanings of a technology does not have to be the same person who uses the technology. Second, the description of meaning is oriented toward what is true for the general users of that technology. In these two respects, Rosenberger’s analysis is appropriately characterized as an analysis of the “objective meaning” of artifacts.

The reason that Rosenberger followed the path of objective meaning is clear: it was necessary to describe discrimination on the basis of architecture not in the limited context of the person concerned but in terms of the objective generality of the individuals who use the technology. The significance of this description is beyond doubt.

However, another path difference seems open. This path involves analyzing both the first axis (the axis of difference) and the second axis (the axis of usage) in a different way from Rosenberger’s approach does. First, the person who analyzes the possible meanings of technology is the same person who uses the technology. Second, the description of meaning aims to be relevant within the context of the life of the person who describes it. In these two senses, this path is characterized as a path for analyzing the “subjective meaning” of artifacts. To give a concrete example, in this second approach, a character (Ann or Betty, or perhaps someone else) using a technological artifact (such as a heavy hammer) explores the meaning that artifact holds within her or his own life (it might be too heavy for hammering nails, but could it be used as a weight for something…?).

Please note that the terms “subjective” and “objective” are used here with specific connotations. Of course, the dichotomy between subjectivity and objectivity remains an important theme in philosophy. In particular, there is a vast body of previous research, mainly in the philosophy of science, on scientific objectivity. In this context, scientific claims and methods should not be influenced by the value judgments, biases, or interests of individuals or communities (cf. Reiss & Sprenger, 2020). This paper does not seek to contribute to this debate. Instead, the terms will be used in a limited sense, namely, in terms of who is speaking, for whom, and in what context.

Given the perspective adopted in this study—that technological artifacts can have various meanings in the life of an individual—the path to be pursued is the latter, i.e., the analysis of the subjective meaning of artifacts. From this perspective of analyzing subjective meaning, by interpreting Rosenberger’s two axes, it is possible to describe the mechanism by which a technological artifact comes to have meaning in a certain way—that is, how it becomes “stable”—for a particular user of that technology.

Expanded Inquiries: Heidegger, Verbeek, and AI-Based Voice Conversion Technology

Utility in Early Heidegger

Following the course of argument thus far, the path forward is that of considering how the axis of difference and the axis of usage work from the perspective of the subjective view of the users of technological artifacts. Note that the term “subjective” here means that the person analyzing the possible meanings of technology is the same person who uses the technology and that the description of meaning is relevant in the context of the life of the person describing it. How, however, is such consideration possible?

An important analysis of the subjective meaning of technology in the above sense has been conducted previously, namely, with respect to the concept of “utility” in Martin Heidegger’s early work. In Being and Time, Heidegger presents valuable insights into various matters, such as human existence, the environment, time, and daily life (Heidegger, 2010). However, what is particularly relevant to this paper is Heidegger’s analysis of the utility of tools. By integrating his analysis of utility with the idea of multistability (especially the axes of difference and usage), the features of the two axes from the subjective perspective of technology users can be examined.

The following is a brief summary of Heidegger’s analysis of utility. He called the totality of meaning that a person encounters the “world.” He also defined the entities that people engage with in their practical activities in the world as “useful things.” A useful thing has several properties. First, it does not exist for its own sake but always for some other purpose: the so-called “in-order-to” structure. For example, a hammer exists in order to drive nails. Second, a useful thing does not exist alone but is always placed in a network of interrelated purposes created together with other useful things, i.e., the “totality of useful things.” The hammer, in this case, is part of a network of purposes that includes nails, saws, files, etc. This network is created for the purpose of making something. Furthermore, a useful thing refers to its own use. In particular, it refers to the “work” that it creates together with other useful things and the purpose of that work, which is called the “totality of references.” For example, useful things, such as a hammer and a nail, are ultimately directed toward their final use as a chair, a product for humans to sit on. Useful things with the above characteristics are called “things at hand,” and a thing that comes to exist as something independent of its use rather than as a useful thing is called “the objective present.”

In terms of the focus of this paper, the above analysis of utility can be summarized simply as follows. A technological artifact is never meaningful in and of itself in a person’s life. Rather, it is meaningful only in terms of the purpose it serves (“in-order-to”), its functional conjunction with other artifacts (“totality of useful things”), and, ultimately, its purposefulness in relation to a practical activity (“totality of references”). More specifically, a technological artifact is meaningful when it is a component of a series of tools organized in order to contribute to a person’s practical activity. When separated from this series of components, the technological artifact loses its meaning within one’s life.

Reinterpreting the Axis of Difference and the Axis of Usage through Heidegger’s Subjective Approach in his Early Period

Starting from Heidegger’s perspective, let us consider how the axis of difference and the axis of usage are related to technological artifacts. These axes are defined as follows: with regard to a given artifact, the axis of difference serves as a yardstick for describing features that are advantageous to certain users but disadvantageous to others, whereas the axis of usage represents something that provides stability against exclusive use by imposing a specific mode of use on all users. How do these axes work from a subjective perspective?

The axis of difference, interpreted subjectively, works as the axis of “difficulty in becoming” the components of a series of tools organized in order to contribute to a certain practical activity of a person. On the other hand, the axis of usage, interpreted subjectively, is an axis that “limits” a person’s practical activities in a certain way, and it contributes precisely in order to contribute to those practical activities in which a series of tools is organized. Through those features, certain artifacts become stable—meaningful—in a certain way in a person’s life.

Let us interpret an example of the axis of difference presented by Rosenberger from the perspective we have just sketched: the example of a hammer for men (might be too large to be used by a woman, whom we might call Ann). For her, it is certainly difficult for this hammer to become one of the tools organized in order to perform practical activities, such as making chairs (by hammering nails). In this way, the hammer has no meaning—no stability—in her life, especially from the perspective of carpentry. However, the hammer can have a different meaning for her—i.e., it can be stable in a different way. For example, precisely because of its weight and size, the hammer can become a tool organized in order to perform the practical activity of, e.g., making pickles (by using it as a weight for making pickles).

Let us then interpret another example related to the axis of usage presented by Rosenberger from this perspective: the bench with partitions or armrests (which prevent the specific use of sleeping). For a woman (let us call her Betty), the practical activities that can be performed on this bench are certainly limited (to sitting on it). Therefore, it only holds a meaning—a stability—in her life in the form of something to sit on (along with other tools). However, the way this bench holds meaning in her life can vary as long as it is related to the practical activity of sitting, for example, sitting there on Sundays with her favorite coffee and sandwiches to pass the time.

As demonstrated thus far, the axes of difference and usage can be extended from a novel and significant angle: subjective meaning—that is, the meaning each person ascribes to artifacts in their own life. This allows the concept of multistability in postphenomenology to expand from the limited domain of the multiple meanings an artifact can hold socially into the new domain of meaning-making by each individual user.

The above discussion also serves to expand on the concept of a “use plan,” which was referenced in the first section when examining the meaning of artifacts in one’s life. A use plan was defined as “a series of actions in which manipulations of an artifact are included as contributions to realizing a given goal.” The axes of subjectively interpreted differences and usage represent the footholds or constraints that a person relies on when forming this use plan. In other words, they constitute the axes of difficulty and limitation in the formation of a use plan. The practical activity referred to in this paper, then, involves creating and executing a use plan.

Elaboration of Insight through Integration with Verbeek’s Theory of Self-practice and Relational Freedom

On the basis of the discussion thus far, the following subsections outline the mechanism by which technology has subjective meaning and how humans are capable of giving meaning to technology from the perspective of how they engage with that mechanism. To this end, the discussion returns to Verbeek’s theory of self-practice of subject constitution and relational freedom.

Verbeek argued that individuals can realize and reflect on the influence that a technology (such as ultrasound diagnosis) has on them (i.e., changing their view of the fetus) and incorporate the technology into their lives in a way that is desirable to them (e.g., using it only to confirm the date of birth without linking it to the selection of children) (Verbeek, 2011; Oie, 2023). Maintaining this framework of discussion concerning the self-practice of subject constitution, this paper attempts to develop a mechanism through which technology acquires subjective meaning in individuals’ lives. Through what mechanisms does ultrasound diagnostic technology come to hold subjective meaning for expecting parents? In other words, in what ways does it become stable in the lives of parents?

From the perspective of the subjectively interpreted axes of differences and usage, it is important to consider what types of practical activities are involved. In this case, “assessment of disease risk and checking for disabilities (leading to child selection)” and “confirming the expected date of birth” can both be considered highly practical activities. Verbeek would say that choosing which practical activities to engage in creates the desirable mode of existence. In fact, relational freedom is precisely the capacity to become the person one wishes to be through such a practical activity. In this case, each practical activity has a set of tools organized to contribute to them. Ultrasound diagnostic technology is one of these tools. However, the meaning of the technology changes depending on the practical activity. When expecting parents carry out the practical activity of assessing disease risks and screening for disabilities, the subjective meaning of ultrasound diagnostic technology is “a tool for confirming that there are no problems with the child.” On the other hand, when the practical activity confirms the expected delivery date, the subjective meaning of this technology is “a tool for thinking about the day when the child will be welcomed into the family.” In these cases, the axis of difference does not seem to work particularly significantly with respect to ultrasound diagnostic technology. This is because although assistance from doctors and technicians is a prerequisite, it is unlikely that a difference in the difficulty or ease of becoming one of the tools organized in order to carry out a given practical activity by individuals or groups will arise. Anyone who uses this technology can employ it (with appropriate support) in a given practical activity. On the other hand, this technology may have limited uses. In this sense, the axis of usage can work considerably. This is particularly noticeable when the check for disease risk or disability is mandatorily incorporated into the usage procedure. In such cases, a certain practical activity, i.e., fetal disease and disability screening, becomes unavoidable. Therefore, the subjective meaning of ultrasound diagnostic technology can be limited.

The mechanism thus conceived is that a technology becomes a component of a set of tools organized in order to contribute to the self-practical activities that a person performs to achieve the maintenance or change of their existence that they consider desirable in their life. The two axes work in becoming such a component: one represents the degree of difficulty or ease of becoming a component, and the other indicates the degree to which the practical activity itself is restricted in a specific manner.

Therefore, what capacity does a person have to engage in the mechanisms that make a technology (e.g., ultrasound diagnostic technology) have meaning in her or his life? This capacity can be expressed as the integration and development of Verbeek’s relational freedom. To reiterate, in Verbeek’s framework, relational freedom includes the capacity to “realize” and “morally reflect” on the impact of technology (in this case, changing views on fetuses) and the capacity to “appropriate” and “modify” the impact of technology to incorporate it into one’s life (Oie, 2023). Let us develop these capacities from the perspective of the mechanism considered above. Please note that this is a tentative list and not meant to be exhaustive.

First, the importance of the capacity to appropriately recognize the functions of ultrasound diagnostic technology should be noted. This technology has multiple functions, such as assessing the risk of disease, estimating the date of birth (as well as determining the sex of the fetus, confirming the movements of the fetus, and investigating the health of the mother, such as the condition of the placenta). Knowing about these functions is an important step in planning practical activities for individuals to shape their desirable existence. It is also important to observe that the functions of the technology identified here are precisely associated with the impact of technology that Verbeek primarily noted (e.g., changes in the view of the fetus). It is precisely such impacts that individuals can appropriate or modify. Therefore, it is essential to clearly visualize and list what the technology can do, i.e., to “recognize” the functions that the technology can perform.

Next, Verbeek’s ideas of appropriation and modification of the impact of technology can be incorporated into a broader framework for planning and conducting practical activities. For example, the capacity to “plan” a practical activity using ultrasound diagnostic technology is essential for expecting parents. Such planning naturally includes decisions on whether and how to use the technology and how to appropriate and modify its impact. Furthermore, the capacity to actually “conduct” the practice using ultrasound diagnostic technology is also necessary. This is, of course, related to the capacity of “knowing-how” described earlier, but it also includes the capacity to propose and negotiate how to use the technology from the user’s perspective. For example, when procedures for confirming the risk of disease or disability are built into standard operating procedures, it is possible for users to negotiate changes in how the technology is used, i.e., to negotiate the possibility of omitting the risk confirmation. This is what gives concrete substance to the capacity for appropriation and modification.

The capacities thus conceived for individuals to actively engage in the mechanism by which technology has subjective meaning include the capacity to recognize the functions of the technology, the capacity to plan practical activities, and the capacity to actually conduct the activity.

This discussion significantly refines Verbeek’s theory of self-practice of subject constitution and relational freedom. Specifically, it makes explicit—from the perspective of the subjective meaning of artifacts—the concrete process by which human individuals self-realize as ethical subjects, a process that was black-boxed within his original ideas.

Application: AI-based “Emotional Cancelling” Technology

For a more concrete understanding of this capacity, let us consider a specific example: the “Emotional Cancelling” technology developed by the University of Tokyo and SoftBank Corporation. Please note that this technology was still under development at the time of this paper’s writing, and its specifications and other details are subject to change.

This technology employs AI to convert the tone of a customer’s voice during a phone call to a gentler one before transmitting it to the operator.3 The development of this technology stems from a social issue known as “customer harassment.” This refers to behavior such as customers making intimidating remarks or unreasonable demands to employees who respond to them on the phone. This technology was developed in response to this issue and is said to be able to convert voices that operators find frightening, such as shouting voices, into calmer voices that are less likely to cause fear. Of course, care is taken to ensure that the content of the speech remains unchanged.

Let us consider a person, a telephone operator, using this technology. In this person’s life, in what mechanism does “emotion cancellation” technology come to have meaning? What is the person’s capacity within that mechanism to give meaning to the technology in her or his own way?

In this case, the subjective meaning of this technology in a person’s life manifests itself as the meaning in a series of tools organized to contribute to the work of telephone operators, which are the practical activities that the user may perform for the purpose of subject constitution. In other words, “emotion cancellation” technology becomes part of the telephone operator’s work system, which consists of telephones, PCs, monitor screens, documents, etc., and it acquires practical meaning through work performed by her or him to become a desirable operator within that system. From the perspective of the difficulty of becoming a system component, if this technology is designed to be user friendly through “human-centered design” (Norman, 2013) or “universal design” (Mace, 1985), the difficulty of becoming a system component may be significantly reduced. Conversely, depending on the design, it may hinder the subject constitution of specific users. Moreover, “emotion cancelling” technology may be exclusive to other practical activities; i.e., this technology is limited to a very narrow range of applications (i.e., operator tasks) and cannot be used to perform other practical activities. However, this in itself is not a problem at this point. At present, the social context in which this technology is used is quite limited: customer service. However, if the technology becomes widespread in various aspects of social life in the future, the situation will change significantly, and the axis of usage of this technology, as interpreted from a subjective point of view, i.e., the axis that limits its activities, will become a major issue.

Therefore, what is the capacity for a person to actively engage in the mechanisms by which this technology has meaning in her or his life? First, it is important for a person to recognize what “emotion cancellation” technology can do. Knowing in what ways and to what extent this technology changes the voice of the person on the other end of the phone line is essential for planning its use and actually using it. Next, it is necessary to have the capacity to plan the work of the telephone operator to which “emotion cancellation” technology contributes. Of course, this work is assigned as a business task within a company, but it must be planned as part of the operator’s daily activities performed to create her or his desirable existence. Furthermore, it is necessary to have the capacity to actually conduct such activity using this technology. This requires not only the capacity to become accustomed to using the technology (i.e., “knowing-how”) but also the capacity to adjust its use from the perspective of the telephone operator as the user and, in some cases, to negotiate with one’s supervisor or others to change its use.

How, then, can operators achieve self-realization by exercising these capacities? How does this serve as a form of personal autonomy? Let us revisit Verbeek’s theory of the self-practice of subject constitution, expanded upon earlier in this paper. By exercising the capacities outlined above—the capacity to recognize the functions of the technology, the capacity to plan practical activities, and the capacity to actually conduct the activity—operators can construct themselves in desirable ways. For example, they construct themselves as excellent operators who, without being intimidated by a customer’s strong tone, discerns their true intentions and genuine needs and conveys the appropriate message to the customer in a suitable tone. The exercise of such capacities can be appropriately described as a form of personal autonomy—the capacity to give meaning to technology in one’s life.

Conclusion

This paper explores a set of human capacities that constitute an important part of personal autonomy in a technological world, namely, the capacity of human users to position the technological artifacts they use as meaningful elements in their own lives. The purpose of this paper was to gain as detailed a descriptive understanding as possible of what this set of capacities involves. To this end, a model of the mechanism through which technological artifacts are attributed certain meanings in a person’s life was presented. It was revealed that this mechanism enables a technology to become a component of a set of tools organized to contribute to a certain practical activity that the person conducts to realize the existence that the person wishes to have. The set of concrete capacities that enable people to give meaning to technological artifacts in their lives was presented precisely as the capacities necessary to actively engage with this mechanism. These findings significantly advance studies of personal autonomy from a technological perspective. In particular, these findings are expected to help conceptualize the active construction of one’s life through the utilization of technology as a crucial aspect of autonomy. Notably, the above discussion was achieved by substantially expanding the concepts of multistability and relational freedom in postphenomenology, particularly from the perspective of individuals’ efforts to situate the artifacts they use within their own lives. This research thus demonstrates interdisciplinary achievements between the theories of personal autonomy and postphenomenology. Furthermore, the analysis of a case study involving the utilization of AI to assist call center operators applies the above discussion to the real world, providing a detailed account of the potential of AI and the active engagement of humans who utilize it.
