Quantitative assessment of graft maturation after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction using quadriceps tendon: Insights from ultra short echo time T2∗ mapping
Authors: Naoki Takemoto, Yasushi Takata, Miho Okuda, Yoshihiro Ishida, Manase Nishimura, Kentaro Fujita, Mikino Saito, Satoru Demura, Junsuke Nakase
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecently, the quadriceps tendon (QT) has gained attention as a graft material for anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction. However, no study has yet evaluated the maturation process of the reconstructed ACL using QT grafts. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-Ultra Short Echo time (UTE) T2∗ mapping is a precise technique for quantifying tissue states in tendons and ligaments. This study aimed to prospectively observe trends in UTE-T2∗ values for the intra-articular and intra-osseous regions of the reconstructed ACL from 6 to 12 months following ACL reconstruction using MRI-UTE-T2∗ mapping and to assess graft maturation of the reconstructed ACL with QT grafts.MethodsThis study enrolled 10 patients (five males and five females). For each patient, UTE-T2∗ values were measured at 6, 9, and 12 months postoperatively. A two-way mixed ANOVA was performed with time (6, 9, and 12 months) as a within-subjects factor and location (intra-articular, tibial intra-osseous, and femoral intra-osseous) as a between-subjects factor.ResultsA significant main effect of time was observed (F (2,54) = 38.162, p < 0.001), with UTE-T2∗ values decreasing as postoperative time progressed. Additionally, a significant main effect of measurement location was identified (F (2,27) = 95.853, p < 0.001), with intra-articular UTE-T2∗ values being significantly higher than those in the tibial and femoral intra-osseous regions (p < 0.001).ConclusionQuantitative assessment using MRI-UTE-T2∗ mapping following ACL reconstruction with QT grafts demonstrated that graft maturation progressed more rapidly in the intra-osseous region compared to the intra-articular region.Keywords: Anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, Magnetic resonance imaging, Quadriceps tendon, Return to sports, Ultra short echo time T2∗ mapping

Introduction

Accurately assessing graft maturation remains a key challenge in anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction. In this procedure, grafts undergo cellular reorganisation as they adapt to the biological and mechanical changes following surgery.1 Graft maturation is generally considered to occur in three phases: initial healing, remodelling, and maturation.2,3 As such, the accurate assessment of graft maturation is a critical factor in determining the appropriate timing for return to sports (RTS) post ACL reconstruction. Early RTS poses a high risk of graft re-rupture, emphasising the importance of this evaluation.4, 5, 6 However, ethically, it is impractical to assess graft maturation directly using tissue biopsies. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has previously been employed to non-invasively evaluate ACL graft maturation,7 using signal-to-noise ratio and central signal intensity as indicators. However, these metrics can vary due to factors, such as scanner and coil specifications, coil positioning, acquisition parameters, reconstruction algorithms, and signal normalisation.8,9 Moreover, Li et al. evaluated the MRI signal/noise quotient of ACL grafts at 3, 6, and 12 months after ACL reconstruction and did not find a correlation between MRI signal intensity and clinical or functional outcomes during the first year after ACL reconstruction when clinical decisions concerning advancement of physical activity are being made.10 Furthermore, the inherently short T2 relaxation times of tendons and ligaments challenge conventional MRI, often resulting in low signal intensities, complicating the assessment of graft maturation.5,6,11

MRI-Ultra Short Echo time (UTE) T2∗ mapping has recently emerged as a more precise technique for quantifying tissue states in tendons and ligaments. Special sequences capable of obtaining ultrashort (<1 ms) T2∗ signals readily acquire short and ultrashort signals.12 T2∗ values are indicative of collagen density and alignment, with high values reflecting low collagen density and disorganisation and lower values corresponding to high collagen content and organised alignment.13 This technique holds promise in the accurate assessment of the maturation of grafts following ACL reconstruction.5,11,14 In addition, some reports have shown a significant association between ACL mechanical strength and UTE-T2∗ values.15 Chu et al. previously investigated signal changes in ACL grafts over a 2-year period following ACL reconstruction using UTE-T2∗ and T2∗ mapping, finding that values on both modalities increased during the first 6 months postoperatively, followed by a subsequent decline.11 Furthermore, previous studies have suggested that UTE-T2∗ values peak around 6 months postoperatively, indicating that the remodelling process of ACL grafts likely begins approximately 6 months post-surgery.3,5,11

Traditionally, hamstring tendon (HT) and bone-patellar tendon-bone (BPTB) have been the primary autografts for ACL reconstruction, both demonstrating favourable outcomes.16 However, HT grafts can present with complications, such as unpredictable size and perioperative failure, in skeletally immature patients.17,18 BPTB grafts, while effective, are associated with donor-site morbidity, including anterior knee pain, patellar tendon rupture, and tibial tubercle apophyseal injuries, making them less suitable for younger patients.19,20 Recently, the quadriceps tendon (QT) has gained attention as a viable alternative, offering superior structural and mechanical properties compared to HT and BPTB, with fewer donor-site complications.21, 22, 23, 24 Clinical outcomes of ACL reconstruction using QT are comparable to those of HT and BPTB, but no studies have thoroughly examined the maturation process of QT grafts.25, 26, 27 As such, there is currently no data to indicate whether QT is superior, inferior, or equivalent to other grafts in terms of graft maturation. As mentioned previously, the accurate assessment of graft maturation is crucial for ensuring a safe RTS post-ACL reconstruction. Evaluating the maturation of QT grafts also holds significant clinical value, as it can aid in determining the appropriate timing for RTS and establishing rehabilitation protocols.

In this context, the present study aimed to address this gap by investigating the maturation process of QT grafts in ACL reconstruction. Using MRI-UTE-T2∗ mapping, we prospectively observed changes in T2∗ values in the intra-articular and intra-osseous regions of the reconstructed ACL from 6 to 12 months postoperatively. We hypothesised that UTE-T2∗ values would differ between these regions, with the intra-osseous regions maturing more rapidly than the intra-articular regions. We also hypothesised that UTE-T2∗ values would decrease after 6 months postoperatively.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study was conducted with the approval of the Ethics Committee of our hospital and adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The purpose of the study was explained to all participants, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant or their legal guardians in the case of minors. Between June 2022 and June 2023, 39 primary ACL reconstruction surgeries were performed in our institute. Among these, 20 utilised HT grafts, while 19 used QT grafts. In our department, HT grafts are generally the first choice for primary ACL reconstruction. However, QT grafts are selected in specific cases, such as in (1) young patients with open epiphyseal plates, (2) athletes participating in contact sports, such as martial arts or rugby, and (3) patients with chronic ACL injuries. Notably, BPTB grafts have not been used since January 2021.

Nineteen patients who underwent primary ACL reconstruction with QT grafts were initially considered for inclusion. The exclusion criteria included a history of injury or surgery on either the ipsilateral or contralateral knee and an inability to undergo MRI at the designated time points. Additionally, a 49-year-old man with a chronic ACL tear was initially included but later excluded because his age deviated significantly from the mean age of the study population. Following the application of these criteria, 10 patients were included in the study (five males and five females), with a mean age of 15.3 (8−22) years and a mean body mass index of 21.5 ± 3.5 kg/m2. All participants were followed up for at least 12 months postoperatively and underwent MRI examinations at 6-, 9-, and 12-month post-surgery.

Among the 10 enrolled cases, five patients had concomitant meniscal injuries requiring intraoperative repair. The mean Tegner activity scale was 6.9, with the following score distribution: 9 in one case, 7 in six cases and 6 in three cases.

Surgical procedure

All ACL reconstruction surgeries were performed by the same orthopaedic surgeon to maintain consistency. The QT graft was harvested without bone, measuring approximately 6 mm in thickness, 10 mm in width, and 50–55 mm in length. A single-bundle graft was prepared. The femoral tunnel was created using an inside-out technique centred on the anatomical ACL footprint with a rounded rectangular dilator.28 Similarly, the tibial tunnel was created using the same dilator, ensuring anatomical placement. The femoral side was secured using a cortical device (FiberTag® TightRope®, Arthrex, USA), and the tibial side was fixed in extension at 20 N using the TensionLoc™ Tibial Fixation Implant (Arthrex, USA) after pre-tensioning the graft. Postoperatively, all patients followed a standardised rehabilitation protocol.29

Postoperative rehabilitation began the day after surgery with joint contraction of the quadriceps and hamstring muscles, range of motion (ROM) exercises, and full weight bearing in the knee joint extension position. Several goals were set for rehabilitation progression and implemented in stages. First, the goal was to achieve an ROM of 0–90° by 2 weeks and 0–120° by 4 weeks postoperatively. At 4 weeks postoperatively, if the target ROM was achieved and swelling and pain were not noticeable, squats and front lunges were introduced to strengthen the lower limb muscles. Second, at 3 months postoperatively, isokinetic muscle strength measurements indicated that patients were allowed to begin running if their knee extensor and flexor strengths were at least 60 % of the limb symmetry index (LSI).30 Third, a similar muscle strength test performed 6 months postoperatively allowed participation in sports activities if the LSI was ≥90 %. Thereafter, rehabilitation shifted to a program that incorporated more sports-specific movements.

Imaging procedure

MRI scans were performed using a 1.5 T scanner (Ingenia 1.5 T CX, Philips Healthcare, Best, Netherlands) with an 8-channel receive knee coil. The UTE-T2∗ maps were generated using monoexponential fitting of a series of T2∗-weighted MR images acquired using the three-dimensional fast-field echo technique. The typical acquisition parameters were as follows: slice thickness = 3 mm, number of slices = 45, field of view = 16 cm, echo time (TE)/repetition time = 0.14, 4.74, 9.34, and 13.94 ms/29 ms, flip angle = 25°, acquisition matrix = 272 × 272, bandwidth = 522 Hz, and scan time = 9 min 31 s. Four sets of images were acquired using a single four-echo UTE sequence. Images were retrieved from a picture archiving and communication system, and T2∗ maps were computed on a pixel-by-pixel basis using a monoexponential fitting algorithm available on the scanner. The equation is expressed as follows: SI(TE) = S0∗exp (-TE/T2∗), where SI(TE) is the signal intensity at each TE, and S0 is the equilibrium magnetisation. For each patient, the UTE-T2∗ values were measured at 6, 9, and 12 months postoperatively. The slice used for T2∗ value measurement was selected based on the image in which the reconstructed ACL was most clearly visualised on oblique sagittal T2-weighted sequences. Following the established protocol, UTE-T2∗ values were measured at three distinct regions (proximal, middle, and distal) of the intra-articular portion of the ACL.31,32 For the intra-osseous regions, values were recorded at one location each on the tibial and femoral sides. The regions of interest were manually placed by an orthopaedic surgeon within artefact-free areas, using circles measuring 5–10 mm2 (Fig. 1). Since we typically create a bone tunnel of approximately 15 mm, the intra-osseous regions were selected at a position 5–7 mm from the intra-articular region. Each measurement was performed three times, and the average value for each region was used as the UTE-T2∗ value. The inter-observer (intra-articular: intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC], 0.84; 95 % confidence interval [CI], 0.57–0.93; tibial site: ICC, 0.94; 95 % CI, 0.88–0.97; femoral site: ICC, 0.76; 95 % CI, 0.50–0.89) and intra-observer (intra-articular: ICC, 0.97; 95 % CI, 0.93–0.99; tibial site: ICC, 0.85; 95 % CI, 0.65–0.96; femoral site: ICC, 0.93; 95 % CI, 0.83–0.98) reliability of this method have been reported to be good to excellent for both intra-articular and intra-osseous measurements.32

As a preliminary investigation in this study, UTE-T2∗ values were evaluated similarly but using a different patient cohort (six cases of primary ACL reconstruction with QT grafts: five males and one female, with a mean age of 23.0 ± 13.5 years) at 3, 6, and 9 months postoperatively. This investigation aimed to confirm the UTE-T2∗ values at 3 months and determine whether they increased between 3 and 6 months. At 3 months, the UTE-T2∗ values (ms) were 11.6 ± 3.4, 7.7 ± 3.4, and 9.2 ± 1.1 in the intra-articular, tibial intra-osseous, and femoral intra-osseous regions, respectively (Table 1). At 6 and 12 months, the corresponding values were 12.4 ± 0.9, 7.9 ± 0.7, and 7.0 ± 0.7, and 10.6 ± 0.7, 7.8 ± 0.6, and 6.9 ± 0.8, respectively. The peak UTE-T2∗ value (ms) in the intra-articular region was observed approximately 6 months postoperatively, supporting the findings of previous studies.5 Based on these results, measuring UTE-T2∗ values after 6 months postoperatively appears to be appropriate for the evaluation of graft maturation following ACL reconstruction.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated using G∗Power 3.1 based on an effect size of 1.3, an alpha error of 0.05, and a target power of 0.8. Previous studies indicated that a minimum of nine participants would be necessary to achieve sufficient power [5, 32]. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. A two-way mixed ANOVA was performed with time (6, 9, and 12 months) as a within-subjects factor and location (intra-articular, tibial intra-osseous, and femoral intra-osseous) as a between-subjects factor. Mauchly's test of sphericity was conducted, and if the assumption of sphericity was violated, the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied. Main and interaction effects were tested for significance, with a significance level set at p < 0.05. If a significant main effect was found, post-hoc tests with Bonferroni correction were performed for pairwise comparisons. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

At 6 months postoperatively, the UTE-T2∗ values (ms) were 12.7 ± 1.3, 7.8 ± 0.6, and 7.3 ± 0.8 in the intra-articular, tibial intra-osseous, and femoral intra-osseous regions, respectively (Table 2). At 9 months, these values were 10.9 ± 0.8, 7.8 ± 0.8, and 7.3 ± 0.8, respectively. By 12 months, the values had decreased to 10.2 ± 1.1, 6.9 ± 0.7, and 6.9 ± 0.8, respectively.

Mauchly's test of sphericity indicated that the assumption of sphericity was met for the main effect of time (W = 0.907, χ2 (2) = 2.525, p = 0.283). Therefore, the within-subjects tests were interpreted under the sphericity-assumed model (no correction). Similarly, the assumption of sphericity was considered valid for the time × measurement location interaction.

Main effect of time (postoperative 6, 9, and 12 months)

The two-way mixed ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of time (F (2,54) = 38.162, p < 0.001). UTE-T2∗ values (ms) were highest at 6 months postoperatively (9.279 ± 0.173) and significantly decreased at 9 months (8.673 ± 0.151) and 12 months (7.923 ± 0.162). Post hoc Bonferroni-corrected comparisons showed significant differences between: 6 vs 9 months: 0.606 ± 0.135, p < 0.001, 6 vs 12 months: 1.356 ± 0.152, p < 0.001, 9 vs 12 months: 0.751 ± 0.176, p < 0.001. These results suggest that UTE-T2∗ values significantly decrease over time postoperatively (Fig. 2).

Main effect of measurement location (Intra-articular and Intra-osseous regions)

The main effect of measurement location was also significant (F (2,27) = 95.853, p < 0.001). UTE-T2∗ values were significantly higher in the intra-articular region (11.264 ± 0.234) than in the tibia (7.429 ± 0.234) and femur (7.182 ± 0.234) (p < 0.001). Post hoc Bonferroni-corrected comparisons showed significant differences between: intra-articular vs tibial intra-osseous: 3.835 ± 0.331, p < 0.001; intra-articular vs femoral intra-osseous: 4.081 ± 0.331, p < 0.001; tibial intra-osseous vs femoral intra-osseous: 0.247 ± 0.331, p = 1.000 (not significant). The UTE-T2∗ values were highest in the intra-articular region, while there was no significant difference between the tibia and femur.

Interaction between time and measurement location

A significant interaction effect was found between time and measurement location (F (4,54) = 10.295, p < 0.001). UTE-T2∗ values in the intra-articular region significantly decreased over time, showing the highest values at 6 months and a significant decline at 9 and 12 months. In contrast, UTE-T2∗ values in the tibia and femur showed relatively little change over time.

Furthermore, no cases of graft re-rupture were observed during the follow-up period. At 12 months postoperatively, the side-to-side difference measured using the KT-1000 arthrometer was 0 mm in seven cases, 1 mm in two cases, and 2 mm in one case, with no evidence of instability in the reconstructed ACL in any patient. Additionally, the Lachman and Pivot shift tests were negative in all cases. In this cohort, RTS occurred at an average of 8.5 months postoperatively, with all participants successfully resuming their original sports activities.

Discussion

The most significant finding of this study was that UTE-T2∗ values in the intra-articular region were significantly higher than those in the intra-osseous regions of both the femoral and tibial tunnels at all time points. Furthermore, the significant decrease in UTE-T2∗ values in the intra-articular region identified between 6 and 9 months postoperatively indicated rapid graft maturation during this period.

This finding is consistent with previous work assessing ACL graft maturation using HT grafts.32 Both studies indicated that maturation of the reconstructed ACLs occurred more rapidly in the intra-osseous regions compared to the intra-articular region. Notably, significant maturation of the intra-articular graft occurred between 6 and 9 months postoperatively. Warth et al. previously observed a significant reduction in UTE-T2∗ values between 6 and 9 months in patients who underwent ACL reconstruction with either hamstring or patellar tendon grafts.5 Our current findings further corroborate this trend.

Interestingly, the UTE-T2∗ values of normal ACLs in 12 healthy knees, measured under the same conditions as in this study, have been reported to be 11.9 ± 2.4 (ms).31 At 6 months postoperatively, the reconstructed ACL's UTE-T2∗ values were still higher than those of normal ACLs, indicating a less organised tissue structure at this stage. However, by 9 months, the intra-articular UTE-T2∗ values dropped below those of normal ACLs, indicating that the reconstructed ACL had achieved a tissue structure more akin to that of a native ACL.

The finding that intra-osseous maturation is faster than intra-articular maturation suggests that intra-osseous fixation of the graft may provide sufficient stability in the early postoperative phase. This insight supports the introduction of more aggressive weight-bearing and range-of-motion exercises, considering the stability of the intra-osseous graft, during the period before 6 months postoperatively.

RTS following ACL reconstruction remains complex. As Webster et al. highlighted in their systematic review, various methods, including knee extensor strength, patient-reported outcomes, and balance tests, are commonly used for RTS assessment.33 However, Gill et al. found that many clinicians base RTS decisions on subjective judgment rather than objective measures.34 Psychological factors also influence RTS decisions and postoperative performance.35 Given the importance of objective data, we suggest that MRI should be performed at 6–9 months postoperatively to assess graft maturation. The UTE-T2∗ values from this study provide a valuable, objective criterion for clinicians. However, the decision to RTS should not be based solely on MRI findings. Instead, it should be a comprehensive evaluation that combines multiple assessments, including knee extensor strength and balance tests, of which MRI is a crucial component. The findings suggest that the intra-articular graft approaches the structural characteristics of a normal ACL only after 9 months. Therefore, while our results indicate that using QT grafts does not delay RTS, they also suggest that a premature RTS at 6 months postoperatively could increase the risk of graft re-rupture. Clinicians should thus exercise caution and base RTS decisions on a careful evaluation, possibly supported by objective imaging, between 6 and 9 months postoperatively.

The pattern of maturation observed in this study differed across the three regions, with the femoral tunnel first, followed by the tibial tunnel, and finally, the intra-articular region. This contrasts with our earlier findings on HT grafts.32 Specifically, the QT graft matured faster in the femoral intra-osseous region than the HT graft. We attribute this discrepancy to differences in suturing techniques. The QT graft was secured using a single-fold, single-bundle technique with a cortical fixation device (FiberTag® TightRope®, Arthrex, USA), leading to greater friction and less graft movement. Conversely, the HT graft used a four-fold technique with no sutures in combination with the cortical device (TightRope®; Arthrex, USA), which may account for the delayed maturation observed. While our study did not aim to directly compare the optimal MRI timing for HT and QT grafts, these findings highlight the potential for different maturation patterns and underscore the need for future comparative studies to fully understand these differences and their clinical implications.

This study had some limitations. First, the relatively small sample size and the age range of the participants, though improved after cohort refinement, may limit the generalizability of our findings. We acknowledge that the results may not be applicable to all age groups, particularly older patients, and that a larger sample size is necessary for a more robust analysis. Second, we did not evaluate factors that may affect UTE-T2∗ values, such as correlations with functional outcomes. This is a significant limitation, as a correlation with clinical outcomes is essential for validation. However, the findings of our study provide a critical foundation for future research to investigate these associations and to better understand the implications of graft maturation on patient recovery. Third, there were variations in patients' pre- and postoperative activity levels, which could have influenced the UTE-T2∗ values. Matching patients by activity level will be critical in future studies to ensure a more robust analysis. However, the minimal variability in UTE-T2∗ values observed in this study suggests that the findings are reliable even with a small sample size. Fourth, this study evaluated outcomes only up to 12 months postoperatively. However, as reports have indicated that graft ligamentisation may continue to progress beyond 1 year postoperatively, we plan to extend this research and report on long-term outcomes in the future.

The results of this study have several implications. First, the findings provide objective imaging evidence that the intra-osseous maturation process is faster than intra-articular maturation, which can inform rehabilitation protocols and RTS decisions. Additionally, the observed maturation timeline indicates that MRI-UTE-T2∗ mapping could serve as a valuable tool for tracking graft maturation and determining the appropriate timing for clinical interventions. Future research should explore the correlation between UTE-T2∗ values and clinical outcomes, such as knee function and patient recovery, to further validate these findings. Larger studies involving a broader range of patient activity levels are also required to confirm these results and establish more definitive guidelines for postoperative care.

Conclusions

Quantitative assessment using MRI-UTE-T2∗ mapping following ACL reconstruction with a QT graft demonstrated that graft maturation progressed more rapidly in the intra-osseous region than in the intra-articular region. At 6 months postoperatively, the graft had not yet fully matured, but by 9 months, it approached the structural characteristics of a normal ACL. While this study provides a foundation for the use of UTE-T2∗ mapping as an objective tool, further research is needed to correlate these findings with clinical and functional outcomes.
