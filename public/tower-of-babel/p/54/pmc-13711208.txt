Prognostic and clinicopathologic divergence in pulmonary mucinous adenocarcinoma versus nonmucinous adenocarcinoma with a mucin-laden feature
Authors: Fujun Yang, Ke Sun, Zhaoxun Li, Aijun You, Zhen Yang, Yimu Wu, Mengxing Li, Yu Liu, Dongliang Bian, Junhong Guo, Gening Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe discrepancies in clinicopathologic characteristics and prognosis between invasive mucinous adenocarcinoma (IMA) and nonmucinous lung adenocarcinoma with a mucin-laden feature (MNLA) remain unclear.MethodsData were collected retospectively for 978 consecutive patients with IMA/MNLA who underwent surgical resection between October 2017 and December 2019. Survival outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. The adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) of the prognosticator was calculated using a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model.ResultsPure IMA was associated with a higher prevalence of KRAS mutation (51.5%), while MNLA was associated with higher prevalences of EGFR mutation (40.4%) and EML4-ALK rearrangement (19.1%). Regarding prognosis, compared to patients with pure IMA, patients with MNLA or mixed IMA had worse disease-free survival (MNLA: aHR, 2.620, 95% CI, 1.690-4.061; mixed IMA: aHR, 3.458, 95% CI, 1.704-7.016) and overall survival (MNLA: aHR, 2.229, 95% CI, 1.185-4.193; mixed IMA: aHR, 1.723, 95% CI, 0.632-4.698). Among patients who experienced recurrence, those with pure IMA had a higher incidence of intrathoracic-only recurrence compared to those with MNLA (80.8% vs 39.6%), demonstrating that extrathoracic-only recurrence was more prevalent in MNLA (35.1% vs 7.3%). The recurrence pattern of mixed IMA was different from that of pure IMA (pure IMA vs mixed IMA: intrathoracic-only, 80.8% vs 50.0%; both intrathoracic and extrathoracic, 11.5% vs 40.0%).ConclusionsAlthough MNLA, pure IMA, and mixed IMA have consistent mucin-laden features, they appear to exhibit differences in prognosis, clinicopathologic characteristics, and molecular profiles.Key Words: lung adenocarcinoma, mucin-laden, clinicopathologic features, molecular parameter, driver mutations, prognosis, recurrence patterns

Central Message

Despite their similar mucin-laden features, pure invasive mucinous adenocarcinoma (IMA), mixed IMA, and nonmucinous lung adenocarcinoma with a mucin-laden feature (MNLA) exhibit distinct prognoses and clinicopathologic characteristics.

Perspective

The presence of shared mucin-laden features has led to uncertainty about whether invasive mucinous adenocarcinoma (IMA) and nonmucinous lung adenocarcinoma with a mucin-laden feature (MNLA) exhibit similar features. Compared to pure IMA, MNLA and mixed IMA were associated with worse prognosis. Pure IMA and MNLA exhibited different recurrence patterns and genetic mutation profiles. These findings suggest that they represent distinct groups with divergent biological behaviors.

Within the non–small cell lung cancer spectrum, lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) now accounts for the majority of cases worldwide.1,2 The substantial burden of lung cancer has spurred advances in LUAD histologic classification, underscoring the critical role of histologic subtypes in refining prognostic stratification and guiding personalized management strategies.2,3

Invasive LUAD is histologically classified into invasive mucinous adenocarcinoma (IMA) and nonmucinous LUAD based on unique goblet or columnar cell morphology.2 Emerging evidence has established divergent prognosis and clinicopathologic characteristics between IMA and nonmucinous LUAD.4,5 IMA exhibits abundant intracytoplasmic or extracellular mucin, but a small fraction of LUADs contain intracytoplasmic or extracellular mucin despite the absence of a mucinous histologic component.6,7 The lack of a standard nomenclature has resulted in variable terms for these tumors, including mucin-laden nonmucinous LUAD (MNLA), nonmucinous LUAD with mucin production, and nonmucinous LUAD with a mucinous feature.4,6,7

Based on current LUAD classification, a mucin-laden feature is considered a cytologic change or intratumoral environmental alteration rather than a characteristic of a specific histologic subtype. Importantly, there is a paucity of studies comparing MNLAs and IMAs. The overlap in mucin-laden features has created uncertainty in clinical practice as to whether MNLAs and IMAs have analogous biological behavior, complicating postoperative management strategies. Limited studies comparing the molecular features between IMAs and MNLAs have presented contradictory results. Kadota and colleagues8 reported that MNLA with extracellular mucin was associated with KRAS mutation, similar to IMA. Conversely, other studies have failed to replicate this association, instead reporting divergent molecular signatures between MNLA and IMA.7,9 Therefore, we sought to comprehensively investigate the disparity of prognosis and clinicopathologic characteristics between MNLA and IMA.

Methods

Study Population

Between October 2017 and December 2019, data on 1011 consecutive patients with an IMA or MNLA who underwent surgery in our institution were collected retrospectively. The exclusion criteria were (1) patients who underwent surgery performed solely for biopsy purposes, who were diagnosed with stage IV preoperatively, or underwent nonradical resection (R1/2) and (2) patients diagnosed with adenocarcinoma in situ (mucinous) or minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (mucinous). After exclusions, 978 patients were eligible for further analysis (Figure E1).

Patient flowchart.

This retrospective research study was approved by the Ethical Review Board of Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital (approval K23-290; approved August 2023) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013). Informed consent was waived owing to the study’s retrospective nature.

Pathologic Evaluation and Detection of Oncogenic Driver Mutations

All tumor specimens were fixed in 10% formalin and embedded in paraffin, then sectioned into ∼4-μm sections. Serial 4-μm sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin, then reviewed by 2 board-certified pathologists independently. For discordant cases, a third senior pathologist (with >15 years of pulmonary neoplasm subspecialty experience) conducted an independent evaluation without knowledge of the initial evaluation results to provide the final diagnosis. Only cases with a conclusive diagnosis after this adjudication process were eligible for inclusion in the study cohort. IMA (Figure 1, A and B) was defined according to World Health Organization classification of LUAD.2 Additionally, IMA cases with nonmucinous components accounting for at least 10% of the entire tumor based on semiquantitative evaluation were defined as mixed IMA (Figure 1, B),6 and the remaining cases were categorized as pure IMA (Figure 1, A) to ensure a clear distinction. MNLA was defined as a tumor with intracytoplasmic (signet ring cells) or extracellular (abundant mucin pools in tumoral alveolar or intraglandular spaces) mucin and positive histochemical mucin stains,8,10 as well as tumor without histologic mucinous, colloid, or enteric component (Figure 1, C and D). MNLA was classified according to the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) grading system11 as grade 1 (lepidic-predominant with <20% of high-grade patterns [solid, micropapillary, and complex glands]), grade 2 (papillary/acinar-predominant with <20% of high-grade patterns), or grade 3 (high-grade patterns ≥20%). Because there were only 3 cases of MNLA IASLC grade 1, these cases were combined with MNLA grade 2 cases for analysis.

Representative images of pure invasive mucinous adenocarcinoma (IMA) (A), mixed IMA (B), mucin-laden nonmucinous lung adenocarcinoma (MNLA) with intracytoplasmic mucin (C), and MNLA with extracellular mucin (D).

Van Gieson staining was used for visualizing vessel elastic fibers to determine vascular invasion, and hematoxylin and eosin staining was used for assessing lymphatic invasion. Perineural invasion was defined as a tumor involving epineurium and/or neural fascicles. For tumors close to the pleura, elastic staining was used to identify visceral pleural invasion (VPI). Tumor spread through air spaces (STAS) was identified according to a previous study.12 Pathologic stage was defined based on the ninth edition TNM classification of lung cancer.13

The examination for oncogenic driver mutations was performed as described in our previous study.10,14 In our entire cohort, 780, 222, 768, 766, and 222 patients were tested for EGFR mutation, KRAS mutation, EML4-ALK rearrangement, ROS1 fusion, and BRAF mutation, respectively. Therefore, the analysis of each specific mutation type included only the subset of patients who underwent that particular test.

Follow-Up and Recurrence Pattern

Postoperative follow-up was conducted primarily by outpatient clinics, supplemented by telephone interviews conducted by trained staff. The surveillance protocol comprised chest CT and biochemical blood tests at 3, 6, and 12 months postoperation during the first year, biannually for the subsequent 4 years, and annually thereafter. Brain magnetic resonance imaging, abdominal and cervical/supraclavicular ultrasound or computed tomography, and bone scans were performed every 6 months for the first 5 years and annually thereafter. Additional physical examinations and imaging were performed promptly if patients reported any new symptoms suspicious of recurrence.

Recurrence patterns were documented as 2 classification schemes based on the first recurrence sites: locoregional, distant, and both (as described in previous studies14,15), and intrathoracic, extrathoracic, or both. Intrathoracic recurrence was defined as recurrence lesions located in bilateral pulmonary, pleural cavity, hilar, or mediastinal lymph nodes. Because of rapid disease progression, recurrence patterns were missing for 7 patients in our cohort.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test or Mann-Whitney U test, and categorical variables were summarized as proportions and compared with the Pearson χ2 test. Disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS) were the primary and secondary outcomes of interest, respectively. Survival outcomes were estimated by the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using the Schoenfeld residuals test, and no significant violation was observed regarding our primary variable of interest (pathologic subtypes, P = .2083). Cox proportional hazards regression models were performed to investigate significant prognosticators and calculate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

The Boruta algorithm, which iteratively compares feature importance against shadow features generated by random forest and retains those features that are consistently superior to their random counterparts, was used to select predictive variables for inclusion in a multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model, thereby reducing the risk of overfitting or underfitting16 (Figure E2). Additionally, sequential Cox models with progressive adjustment were performed as a sensitivity analysis to evaluate prognostic stability.

Identification of key predictors using the Boruta algorithm for disease-free survival (left) and overall survival (right). STAS, Spread through air spaces; LVPI, lymphatic/vascular/perineural invasion; VPI, visceral pleural invasion.

A 2-sided P value <.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed with SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM) or R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Patient Characteristics

The study cohort comprised 549 (56.1%) females and 429 (43.9%) males, with a median age of 60 years (interquartile range, 53-66 years). Demographic and clinicopathologic characteristics of the entire cohort are presented in Table E1. The case mix was 418 pure IMA (42.7%), 529 MNLA (54.1%), and 31 mixed IMA (3.2%). Among these, 75.6% (n = 739) of all patients were classified as pathologic stage I. Regarding oncogenic driver mutation, KRAS mutation (31.1%), EGFR mutation (26.8%), and EML4-ALK rearrangement (12.9%) were the most prevalent genotypes; ROS1 fusion (1.7%) and BRAF mutation (1.8%) were relatively sporadic.

Comparisons of Histologic Subtypes

The demographic and clinicopathologic characteristics of the 3 pathologic subtypes are compared in Table 1. Differences among the 3 subtypes were observed in terms of age, sex, smoking history, surgical procedure, intraoperative lymph node assessment, tumor size, pathologic T/N stage, VPI, lymphatic/vascular/perineural invasion (LVPI), STAS, adjuvant therapy, KRAS/EGFR mutation, and EML4-ALK rearrangement (all P < .05) (Table 1). Specifically, MNLA was characterized by higher rates of lymph node metastasis, VPI, LVPI, STAS, EGFR mutation, and EML4-ALK rearrangement, whereas pure IMA was characterized by a higher incidence of KRAS mutation. Mixed IMA portended older age, a larger tumor size, a higher T/N stage, and a higher positive rate of STAS compared to pure IMA. Genetically, the profiling of KRAS mutation sites revealed that pure IMA was enriched with higher prevalences of KRASG12D (50.0%) and KRASG12V (28.8%) (Table E2).

When MNLA was divided into grade 1/2 and grade 3 groups according to the IASLC grading system,11 the grade 3 group was significantly associated with younger age, larger tumor size, higher T/N stage, higher positive rates of STAS/LVPI, greater receipt of adjuvant therapy, lower EGFR mutation rate, and higher EML4-ALK rearrangement rate (Table E3).

Survival Outcomes

During the median follow-up period of 63 months (interquartile range, 56-68 months), 190 patients experienced tumor recurrence and 95 patients died. Causes of death were tumor recurrence in 86 patients, other malignancy in 2 patients, and respiratory/cerebrovascular/cardiovascular disease in 7 patients.

Significant differences in DFS and OS were observed among the 3 histologic subtypes. Three-year DFS rates (95% CI) were 94.0% (91.6%-96.4%) for pure IMA versus 71.0% (56.7%-88.9%) for mixed IMA versus 75.1% (71.4%-79.0%) for MLNA; respective 5-year DFS rates were 92.7% (90.1%-95.4%) versus 67.7% (53.1%-86.4%) versus 68.0% (63.9%-72.2%) (P < .001 for all). Three-year OS rates were 98.0% (96.6%-99.5%) versus 83.9% (71.9%-97.9%) versus 91.7% (89.2%-94.1%), and 5-year OS rates were 96.7% (94.8%-98.7%) versus 80.6% (67.9%-95.8%) versus 83.9% (80.6%-87.3%) (P < .001 for all) (Figure 2, A and B, Table 2).

Univariable and Multivariable Analyses

Univariable analyses were performed to investigate the associations between clinicopathologic features and survival outcomes (Table 2). The Boruta algorithm identified 7 factors for inclusion in the multivariable analysis. After adjusting for multiple variables, pathologic subtype remained an independent predictor for DFS (with pure IMA as the reference; MNLA: adjusted HR (aHR), 2.620; 95% CI, 1.690-4.061; mixed IMA: aHR, 3.458; 95% CI, 1.704-7.016) and OS (with pure IMA as the reference: MNLA: aHR, 2.229; 95% CI, 1.185-4.193; mixed IMA: aHR, 1.723; 95% CI, 0.632-4.698) (Table 3). Calibration and discrimination curves were also generated. The bootstrap-corrected C-index was 0.813 for DFS and 0.851 for OS. The calibration curves demonstrated good agreement between the predictive and actual survival outcomes (Figure E3).

Sensitivity Analysis

To evaluate the stability of the prognostic impact of pathologic subtypes, subgroup analyses were performed according to multiple clinicopathologic characteristics (Figure E4). The results reveal that both mixed IMA and MNLA consistently showed a trend toward poorer DFS and OS compared with pure IMA across nearly all subgroups examined. In sequential Cox models progressively adjusting for demographic and tumor-related characteristics, pathologic subtype was independently associated with DFS (Table E4); however, the OS risk for mixed IMA was attenuated and lost significance after adjusting for tumor-related characteristics, while MNLA remained significant across all models. Furthermore, the analysis of interaction effects showed that the interaction term (pathologic subtypes × time of surgery) was not statistically significant (Table E5), suggesting the temporal stability of our findings.

Recurrence Pattern

The recurrence rate was significantly lower in the pure IMA cohort compared to the mixed IMA and MNLA cohorts (MNLA: 6.2% vs 32.3% vs 29.1%; P < .001) (Figure 3, Table E6). Recurrence sites are listed in Table E7. In patients with recurrence, the recurrence pattern did not differ significantly among the 3 groups when classified as locoregional, distant, or both (P = .196) but was significantly different when classified as intrathoracic, extrathoracic, or both (P = .002). Specifically, pure IMA had the highest incidence of intrathoracic-only recurrence (80.8%), while in contrast, among MNLA cases, 35.1% of all recurrence events occurred in extrathoracic organs without intrathoracic organ involvement.

B, Intrathoracic and extrathoracic patterns. “Unknown” was defined as a missing recurrence pattern owing to rapid disease progression.

Exploratory Analysis

When MNLA was divided into grade 1/2 and grade 3 subgroups, survival analyses revealed a clear trend of worsening DFS and OS across the following sequence: pure IMA, MNLA with grade 1/2, MNLA with grade 3, and mixed IMA (Figure E5, A and B, Table E8). Subsequently, when MNLA was divided into intracytoplasmic, extracellular, and both according to the mucin distribution pattern,8,10 a progressive worsening of survival was observed from pure IMA to MNLA with intracytoplasmic mucin, then to MNLA with both patterns, and finally to MNLA with extracellular mucin or mixed IMA (Figure E5, C and D, Table E9).

Discussion

Our study has revealed heterogeneity among pure IMA, MNLA, and mixed IMA. Histologic subtype appears to be closely associated with distinguishable clinicopathologic features and biologic behaviors. Notably, KRAS mutation was enriched in the pure IMA cohort, while MNLA was associated with EGFR mutation and EML4-ALK rearrangement. MNLA and mixed IMA were associated with a poorer prognosis compared to pure IMA. Regarding recurrence pattern, the pure IMA cohort had a higher rate of intrathoracic recurrence compared to the MNLA and mixed IMA cohorts.

IMAs have been considered a distinct category owing to their unique histology.2 The presence of mucin is an important accompanying feature in these tumors. For this reason, the presence of mucin in nonmucinous LUAD fuels clinical uncertainty about whether these tumors share similar clinicopathologic features and prognosis with IMA. The reasons for this uncertainty are multifactorial, including a lack of comprehensive documentation of their differences, absence of a standard nomenclature for MNLA, and conflation of some MNLA cases with IMA cases in practice.4,17

In our cohort, the prevalence of KRAS mutation (especially KRASG12D) was higher in IMA (51.5%), corroborating previous studies.4,5 However, MNLA cases had higher rates of EGFR mutation (40.4%) and EML4-ALK rearrangement (19.1%) compared to IMA. This genotypic difference is consistent with the report by Sonzogni and colleagues7 to varying degrees, but conflicts with the findings of Kadota and colleagues.8 The conflicting results might be attributed to differing origins of the study populations. Hence, it can be reasonably speculated that the origin of MNLA differs from the histologic mucinous origin and is more in line with the histologic nonmucinous origin.9,18

Considering the heterogeneous genetic features of the 3 cohorts, we also investigated clinicopathologic features and prognosis. Similar to previous studies,19,20 pure IMA exhibited lower rates of lymph node metastasis, VPI, LVPI, and STAS, while mixed IMA had a higher incidence of lymph node metastasis, poorer prognosis, and higher incidence of extrathoracic recurrence compared to pure IMA. These differences provide the rationale for evaluating mixed IMA as a separate group in the current analysis. These findings further corroborate a recent study21 suggesting a possible hypothesis that mixed IMA might not merely represent a combination of nonmucinous and mucinous components but may point toward a more aggressive biological phenotype. However, constrained by the small sample size and limited mortality events, the independent impact of mixed IMA on OS did not reach statistical significance. Thus, its aggressive behavior and long-term prognosis require validation in larger cohorts. Regarding MNLA, we also found that the clinicopathologic characteristics and prognosis of MNLA differed from those of pure IMA owing to the higher rates of lymph node metastasis, VPI, LVPI, STAS, and extrathoracic recurrence. Furthermore, MNLA had a more unfavorable prognosis compared to pure IMA. Recently, Di Federico and colleagues4 reported that LUAD with mucinous features (including MNLA with intracytoplasmic mucin and mixed IMA) has a different molecular profile and prognosis than pure IMA, and our results support their conclusion. Overall, our results suggest that these 3 subtypes may represent distinct groups that could help inform individualized postoperative management strategies (Figure E6), pending further validation in multi-institutional cohorts.

Nonetheless, our study has several limitations that merit mention. First, our findings are inevitably influenced by inherent shortcomings such as selection bias, loss of follow-up (<5 year: n = 182; of those 149 cases lost to follow-up), and generalizability owing to the study’s retrospective design and single-center database. Second, although a consensus diagnostic process involving 2 independent pathologists and a third senior arbiter was used, the interobserver agreement was not systematically recorded because of the retrospective design. The lack of concordance data may limit the assessment of diagnostic reproducibility across different institutions. Future prospective studies are needed to systematically evaluate interobserver agreement for these subtypes. Third, during the study period, molecular testing was not performed uniformly across the entire cohort owing to the variations in therapeutic accessibility of specific gene mutations and health insurance reimbursement. Consequently, mutation prevalence analyses were restricted to the subpopulations who underwent corresponding gene tests. Although this still might have introduced bias into estimates of mutation prevalence and limit the generalizability of our findings, it nonetheless provides a meaningful reflection of the mutational landscape in a real-world clinical setting. Finally, our data cannot support an investigation into the differences in the mechanism of mucin production between MNLA and IMA. Based on available evidence, this process appears to occur during the initial stages of IMA, whereas in MNLA it may be attributed to the launch of certain pathway at the postinvasive stage.4 Future studies to explore this topic are warranted.22

Conclusions

In summary, although mucin-laden features are shared, the differences in clinicopathologic characteristics, prognosis, recurrence patterns, and driver mutations indicate that MNLA, pure IMA, and mixed IMA appear to represent distinct groups with divergent biological behaviors. Prospective or multi-institutional studies are needed to validate the reproducibility, prognostic impact, and molecular profiles of this proposed 3-way classification in clinical practice.
