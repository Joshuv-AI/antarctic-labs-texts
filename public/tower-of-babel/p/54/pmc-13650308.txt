Redefining Worker Protection in the Algorithmic Era: From Algorithmic Justice to Insurance Systems for the Gig Economy
Authors: Jian Lee, Jinha Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid expansion of the digital platform economy has introduced novel occupational hazards driven by algorithmic management. This short communication examines the occupational health challenges faced by gig workers in the context of algorithmic control, focusing on four key mechanisms: trade-offs between economic success and worker health, uncertainty in terms of effort and reward, unreliable assessment of job performance, and incorrect attribution of responsibility. These algorithmic hazards differ substantially from conventional occupational risks and cannot be adequately addressed by existing occupational health and safety frameworks. To address these inequities, we propose redefining occupational health and safety standards under an algorithmic justice framework. Furthermore, we suggest implementing an algorithm tax and an algorithm-dependent insurance system to establish sustainable social safety nets. These measures, along with mandatory disclosure of algorithmic management systems as 'High-Impact AI,' offer a practical regulatory pathway to enforce worker protection and fairness within the evolving legal landscape in the algorithmic era.Keywords: Algorithm tax, Algorithmic justice, Algorithmic management, Gig economy, Social protection

According to the Boston Consulting Group's “The Future of Work” (2018) [1], it is estimated that between 4% and 45% of workers will become platform workers in the future, and 11–50% of platform workers will consider platform work as their main source of income. The International Labour Organization reviewed the status of digital platforms in G20 countries, highlighting that digital labor platforms have increased fivefold in the last 10 years across various geographical diversities, and that urgent social interests are needed for new policy actions [2].

A robot tax is a proposed tax on companies using automation, including algorithms, instead of human workers [3]. The purpose of the tax is to generate revenue for programs to mitigate the social and economic impacts of automation such as job losses and income inequality. In the same context, an algorithm tax can be discussed. Platform companies are using algorithms to allocate work, determine pay, and optimize efficiency and productivity. However, algorithms in gig economy systems are designed to optimize efficiency and productivity for economic success, not for occupational health and safety (OHS). Consequently, while this automation reduces administrative costs regarding human resources, it inadvertently strips away the protective layer of human oversight needed to identify and manage health risks. Unlike algorithmic systems in manufacturing or healthcare, where automation operates within established organizational structures with human managerial oversight and existing labor protections, gig economy algorithmic management acts as the sole interface between platform and worker, exercising comprehensive control over task allocation, performance evaluation, and compensation without human mediation. This unmediated control, particularly over workers who lack formal employment status, necessitates a purpose-built protection framework.

In reported literature concerning the concept of a digital platform economy and artificial intelligence (AI) technology, algorithmic management and worker health have frequently been discussed, involving the following topics: trade-offs between economic success and worker health, uncertainty in terms of effort and reward, unreliable assessment of job performance, and incorrect attribution of responsibility that cannot be monitored owing to the opacity of algorithms [4,5].

Digital labor platforms rapidly expanded between the 2010s and 2020s, growing fivefold in the last decade [2], and existing issues in OHS concepts in the gig economy started to evolve to adapt to algorithmic control. While algorithmic justice is an important aspect that should be considered as being tightly interconnected with all dimensions of platform labor for improvements, it has only recently become a research topic in occupational health literature. Algorithmic justice refers to a framework that aims to reduce bias and unfairness in algorithmic systems by ensuring fairness, transparency, accountability, safety, and respect for human rights, particularly in protecting worker health and rights in the gig economy.

Health equity and social protection of gig workers under the effects of algorithm-driven needs and new emerging issues regarding work–life, such as the projected 4–45% increase in the platform workforce [1], need to be prioritized to prevent an uncontrollable negative transformation without timely and effective long-term planning during the transition period to the algorithmic era. Interactions among algorithms, platforms, and workers during the evolution of AI-related changes can trigger the creation of common standards for all before new policy actions related to the necessity of an algorithm tax and insurance are addressed.

This perspective aims to examine the occupational health challenges faced by gig workers in the context of algorithmic management and to propose policy solutions based on algorithmic justice principles, specifically an algorithm tax or algorithm-dependent insurance system to protect gig workers' health and well-being.

This perspective presents a theoretical framework based on a literature review examining the relationship between algorithmic management and worker health in the gig economy. Four key algorithmic hazards are identified and analyzed: trade-offs between economic success and worker health, uncertainty of effort-reward, unreliable job performance assessment, and incorrect attribution of responsibility. Policy proposals, specifically algorithm tax and algorithm-dependent insurance, are presented to reframe OHS in the gig economy.

First, the best algorithms for economic success can aggravate physical and psychological demands or other hazardous working conditions, such as delivery workers' accident risk and software engineers' overnight work [4]. These phenomena can be defined as the trade-off between economic success and worker health in automatic algorithms. Similar concepts of trade-off were seriously discussed between public health and the macroeconomic shocks in the early stage of the COVID-19 pandemic [4].

Second, the use of algorithms to allocate work can also lead to a lack of transparency and accountability, as gig workers may not be able to understand why they are receiving certain assignments or how their pay is being determined. The lack of transparency and accountability in the allocation of work and pay through algorithms can create uncertainty for gig workers, which can result in stress and other negative impacts on their health [5]. This phenomenon can be defined as uncertainty of effort-reward in gig economy algorithms.

Third, there are limited validated methods for assessing the performance of gig workers. Traditional job performance assessment cannot be easily applicable in the gig economy workers, because gig workers often perform various tasks and work in informal work environments. Customer feedback is one of the most common methods used to assess the performance of gig workers, as it provides a direct measure of customer satisfaction with the service provided. However, customer feedback is not a reliable or objective measure of job performance, because it can be influenced by the customer's general mood, unmatched expectations, and the overall customer experience beyond human service as well as systemic satisfaction, which is not related to gig economy workers. Those are very similar to an artist's job identity and assessment of job performance, and these unreliable job performance assessments can be seriously related to job stress and mental health [6]. These phenomena can be defined as unreliable job performance assessment.

Fourth, in the similar context of unreliable job performance assessment, in algorithms, gig workers can be held accountable for negative feedback or complaints even when they are not at fault for a lack of performance. This can happen when customers are dissatisfied with the services provided by gig workers, but the cause of the dissatisfaction is actually a problem with the platform company's system or process. However, customers may not be able to differentiate between these factors, and job performance evaluation algorithms could not differentiate it either. In this scenario, the gig workers are unfairly held accountable for issues beyond their control, and negative feedback is passed on to the gig workers despite not being responsible. These phenomena can be defined as incorrect attribution of responsibility [7].

These four algorithmic hazards highlight the unequal distribution of benefits and risks associated with algorithms in the gig economy. While platform companies may benefit from the increased efficiency and profitability enabled by algorithms, gig workers may face negative impacts on their health and well-being. The mechanisms of algorithmic harm differ substantially from those of conventional occupational hazards, and existing OHS regulations do not effectively engage with these algorithmic hazards.

Importantly, the underlying causal mechanism of these hazards stems from the structural constraints of platform labor. Although gig workers formally retain the autonomy to refuse assignments, this freedom is substantively limited by 'de facto' control mechanisms. These include indirect penalties for refusal (e.g., algorithmic demotion or fewer future task allocations), economic dependency on the platform, and inherent information asymmetries between the platform and the worker. Such structural constraints transform algorithmic management from a mere task-dispatch tool into a pervasive control mechanism—one that generates occupational risks without assuming the corresponding employer responsibilities. This asymmetry provides the normative foundation for the proposed algorithm tax.

Arguably, occupational health research should have received more attention during the period when algorithms became the basis of the relationship between platform companies and labor. With the increase in digital innovation as the gig economy progresses, there is an even greater need for redefining OHS standards in terms of algorithmic justice as a result of the confirmed health risks that might cause irreversible harm to those in the workforce.

Algorithmic fairness and transparency have become more important owing to the 'black box' nature of management, specifically regarding uncertainty in terms of effort-reward and unreliable performance assessment. Thus, OHS frameworks need to evolve to incorporate the five core principles of fairness, transparency, accountability, safety, and human rights. However, unless this evolution occurs while the workforce's financial and health safety nets remain guaranteed, its development is questionable.

The trade-off between benefits and health risks associated with algorithms in the gig economy can be uncertain and unpredictable, making it difficult for gig workers to protect themselves against the negative impacts of algorithms on their health. An insurance system could provide a safety net [8] for gig workers, helping to mitigate the financial and health impacts of algorithm-related health risks. By providing financial support and access to healthcare services, an insurance system could help to ensure that gig workers are protected against the negative impacts of algorithms on their health, and that they are able to maintain their physical and mental well-being in the gig economy.

To facilitate its development, an algorithm tax, levied on companies optimizing efficiency via automation, and an algorithm-dependent insurance system should be promoted in the policy landscape for the gig workforce. This insurance system for gig workers could have a similar working structure to existing occupational health insurance systems, but with some differences in the method of funding the insurance. Unlike traditional occupational health insurance systems, which are often funded by employer contributions, an insurance system for gig workers could be funded through a different mechanism that does not rely on a specific company. This could include a tax on companies that use algorithm concepts or automation concepts in the gig economy, a social insurance program funded by the government, or a combination of various funding sources. The tax would be levied on platform companies in proportion to their algorithmic management intensity, for example, based on the volume of algorithmically dispatched tasks or as a percentage of algorithmically generated revenue. Precedents for such platform-level reporting obligations are reflected in several emerging regulatory frameworks; the ILO has highlighted the lack of transparency in algorithmic decision-making processes on digital labor platforms, and called for policy action to ensure workers' rights concerning algorithmic transparency [2]. Algorithmic management intensity could be assessed using platform-reported indicators such as the proportion of tasks dispatched without human intervention or the share of algorithmically determined pay adjustments, subject to mandatory disclosure obligations. Independent algorithmic audits—analogous in principle to financial audits—could provide a verification mechanism. However, the precise metrics and audit standards would require further development through regulatory and academic deliberation. This can be defined as an algorithm-dependent insurance system.

The concept of an algorithm-dependent insurance system is aimed at addressing the health inequity faced by gig economy workers. The system would provide coverage similar to existing occupational health insurance, but with a different funding mechanism to make it more accessible and sustainable for gig workers. This could include a tax on companies using algorithms or automation, a social insurance program, or a combination of funding sources, and would provide coverage regardless of the specific company a gig worker is working for. The goal is to provide a safety net for gig workers and mitigate the financial and health impacts of algorithm-related health risks in the gig economy. In practice, the system would function as a gap-filling mechanism, analogous to a risk-sharing model: algorithm tax revenues would be channeled into existing occupational accident insurance frameworks to extend coverage to risks not addressed under current schemes—primarily psychosocial health damage and income instability—without requiring the establishment of an entirely new institutional infrastructure. Claims could be administered through existing insurance institutions to minimize administrative duplication, with eligibility based on algorithmic audit records rather than formal employment status. The specific interface with national social security systems would inevitably vary by jurisdiction and warrants further policy development.

In the multidimensional gig economy relationship, the definitions and practices of social insurance are being transformed into a model that does not rely on a single employer, but is funded by the algorithmic value chain. Social security systems vary across countries and regions, and some countries may have better coverage for gig economy workers than others. However, the issue of adapting social protection systems to changing forms of work remains relevant globally. As the nature of work evolves and new forms of employment emerge, it is important to ensure that all workers have access to full and effective social protection, regardless of their employment status [9].

Moreover, legal frameworks, such as the Republic of Korea's AI Basic Act (scheduled for enforcement in 2026) [10] and the European Union AI Act [11], should be reconsidered under this tax-insurance model. Specifically, current policy discussions focus on mandatory labeling for AI-generated content (e.g., watermarking) to ensure transparency. This principle of 'mandatory disclosure' must be extended to algorithmic management systems in the workplace. Just as consumers have the right to know if content is AI-generated, workers have the right to know if their performance and retention are being evaluated by AI.

Designating algorithmic management systems in the gig economy as 'High-Impact AI' is a prerequisite for any downstream safety intervention. This designation aligns with the 'High-Risk AI' criteria in the EU AI Act, which categorizes AI systems that significantly affect workers' health, safety, and fundamental rights—including those determining access to employment and evaluating performance—as high-risk, subject to mandatory transparency obligations. This transparency allows stakeholders to identify potential psychosocial risks—such as anxiety from opaque monitoring—and discuss preventive measures before the system is fully deployed. Only through such 'procedural labeling' can the framework of algorithmic justice effectively mitigate OHS risks. These legal frameworks should ensure a better and well-defined reshaping period for worker protection in both the short and long terms. Meanwhile, holistic collaboration among the government, platforms, and researchers is likely to be a key aspect of the algorithmic justice concept in relation to interactions within the world of gig work. The main stakeholders in this system are gig workers, platform companies, government, healthcare providers, insurance providers, and researchers. Researchers can play an important role in establishing the goal of health equity for gig economy workers through the use of algorithm-dependent insurance systems by conducting studies and gathering data on the health impacts of algorithms on gig workers and the effectiveness of various funding mechanisms for insurance systems.

In summary, the use of algorithms and automation in the gig economy can lead to negative impacts on gig workers' health, including trade-offs between economic success and worker health, uncertainty of effort-reward, unreliable assessment of job performance, and incorrect attribution of responsibility. An algorithm-dependent insurance system can help mitigate these impacts by providing financial support and access to healthcare services for gig workers. Additionally, policymakers and government officials can take steps to implement the algorithm-dependent insurance system and enforce regulations to ensure the health and safety of gig workers.
