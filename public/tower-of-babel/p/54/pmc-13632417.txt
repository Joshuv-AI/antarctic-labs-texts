DNA damage at an early developmental stage affects neurodevelopment in sterlet (Acipenser ruthenus)
Authors: Abhipsha Dey, Małgorzata Rzepkowska, Magdalena Fajkowska, Roman Franěk, Christoph Steinbach, Martin Pšenička, Radek Šindelka, Martin Flajšhans, Ievgeniia Gazo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExposure to DNA damaging agents during fish embryogenesis can induce malformations and developmental disorders. However, there is scarcity of knowledge on dynamics of embryonic DNA repair and its effect on organogenesis. To address this knowledge gap, we exposed sterlet (Acipenser ruthenus) embryos to DNA-alkylating agent, methyl methanesulfonate (MMS), during blastulation (from 2 to 24 h post-fertilization (hpf)) and observed the effects at hatching (8 days post-fertilization (dpf)) and post-hatching stage (10 dpf).ResultsIn contrast to control, DNA alkylation damage in treated embryos significantly persisted up to 3 dpf but was resolved by 8 dpf. These observations coincided with a significant increase in expression of key base excision repair (BER) genes (apex1, ogg1, polβ, xrcc1, lig3) and 1.5-fold increase in APE1 protein levels by hatching. These findings suggest that sterlet embryos activate functional BER pathway in response to DNA alkylation. Despite successful DNA repair, embryos exhibited disrupted retinal layers differentiation and reduced expression of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) at 10 dpf.ConclusionsCollectively, our results suggest that DNA alkylation can interfere with organogenesis, potentially through misregulation of DNA repair proteins during critical developmental windows. We speculate that DNA alkylation can affect expression of genes critical for early brain and eye development indicating an organ-specific DNA damage response. Notably, this study emphasizes that future studies should address the link between activation of BER pathway and developmental abnormalities when sterlet embryos are genotoxically challenged.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12915-026-02667-8.

Background

DNA damage occurring during organogenesis can disrupt cell division, migration, and interaction, leading to congenital abnormalities [1]. In response to genotoxic stress, cells activate a range of DNA repair pathways that act as a first line of defense [2]. This response is particularly critical in early development, when embryonic cells undergo rapid proliferation—highlighting the pivotal role of DNA repair in regulating cell division and preventing accumulation of mutations [3, 4].

DNA alkylation is caused by methylating agents and it represents one of the most common genotoxic lesions [5, 6]. MMS is a monofunctional alkylating agent that induces the formation of N-methyl and O-methyl DNA adducts, such as N7-methylguanine (7meG), N3-methyladenine (3meA), and O6-methylguanine (O6meG) [7, 8]. While MMS itself is not typically present in aquatic environments, it serves as a valuable model compound, which resembles several environmental pollutants, such as mucohalic acids [9] and allylic reagents [5, 10]. In this study, we intend to understand the mechanistic insights on how DNA alkylation during early developmental stage can impair organogenesis. Direct ecological relevance of MMS toxicity during sterlet embryo development is not claimed through this study.

DNA alkylation can lead to cytotoxicity and mutagenesis and/or interfere with essential biological processes such as DNA replication and transcription [11, 12]. In eukaryotes, base excision repair (BER) serves as the primary DNA repair pathway for removing alkylation-induced damage [6]. This pathway is active throughout all stages of embryonic development in fish [13]. BER proceeds through four sequential steps, each mediated by a specific group of enzymes [14]. The process is initiated by DNA glycosylases, which recognize and bind to damaged bases, catalyzing the cleavage of the N-glycosidic bond and generating an apurinic/apyrimidinic (AP) site. Next, AP endonuclease incises the DNA backbone 5ʹ to the AP site [15, 16]. DNA polymerases then fill in the resulting gap through either a short-patch or long-patch repair mechanism, using the undamaged complementary strand as a template [17]. Finally, DNA ligases seal the remaining nicks in the strand, completing the repair process [18, 19].

Genotoxicants have been reported to alter expression of DNA repair genes and induce developmental malformations [20–29]. However, it remains unclear how activation of DNA repair pathways during embryogenesis may interfere with their developmental functions and contribute to malformation. Several DNA repair factors play dual roles in fish organogenesis beyond their canonical functions in DNA repair [30]. For example, Wang et al. [31] demonstrated that AP endonuclease (Ape1) is essential for cardiovascular, hematopoietic, neural, and notochord development in zebrafish. Similarly, loss-of-function studies have shown that AP endonucleases are indispensable for proper brain and neuron formation [32]. Ape1 regulates balance between oxidative stress and oxidative response in brain by regulating expression of Creb1 (cAMP response element-binding 1). Precisely, the study elaborates that loss of Ape1 results in increased reactive oxygen species generation and decreased Creb1 protein expression, leading to aberrant brain development. Furthermore, Ape1 participates in regulating transcription factors, such as AP-1 [33], Egr-1 [34], Mdm2 [35], and p53 [36], among others. Importantly, while these factors are crucial for normal development, their overexpression in response to genotoxicant exposure may also contribute to abnormal phenotypes.

Sturgeons are ancient fish species, having evolved approximately 200 million years ago [37]. Today, their populations are in decline due to multiple factors, with water pollution being one of the most critical [38]. The sterlet (Acipenser ruthenus), one of the smallest sturgeon species, is currently listed as endangered and exhibits high sensitivity to waterborne pollutants [39]. Owing to their external fertilization and development, fish embryos are particularly vulnerable to the constant presence of xenobiotics in aquatic environments. Notably, early-stage sterlet embryos are more susceptible to genotoxicants compared to embryos of some other fish species [40, 41]. Therefore, there is a need to provide in-depth analyzing of known genotoxicants as currently known threshold limits from model teleost species can be challenged by more susceptible representatives such as sturgeons.

The implementation of conservation efforts aimed at restoring sturgeon populations requires a deeper understanding of the factors and molecular mechanisms that contribute to embryonic malformations. Based on the above considerations, our study was designed to investigate how aberrant expression of BER pathway components—particularly APE1 following DNA alkylation affects their spatiotemporal expression and localization, and whether these changes correlate with the occurrence of malformations. In our study, first we exposed sterlet embryos to MMS and observed the malformations. Subsequently, we analyzed the DNA damage and the instigated repair pathway. Finally, we analyzed aberrant expression and localization of APE1 in malformed organs.

MMS exposure had no significant effect on survivability at 3 and 10 dpf (Fig. 1A), and hatching rates at 7 and 8 dpf (Fig. 1B). Hatching rate was evaluated at two time points because control embryos started hatching by 7 dpf, and all embryos were hatched by 8 dpf. At 10 dpf, 71.1% ± 7.8% of embryos exhibited eyes with less pigmentation compared to 5.8% ± 2.9% in the control group (Fig. 1C,D). Discussion

Efficient coordination between rapid cell division of developing embryonic cells and DNA repair is essential to ensure genomic integrity during organogenesis [4]. Early-life exposure to genotoxic agents is a known risk factor for both developmental and neurodegenerative disorders [42]. In the present study, exposure to MMS altered the expression and localization patterns of key components of BER pathway during sterlet embryogenesis. These findings provide new insight into the developmental response to exogenous DNA damage. From the results of this study, we hypothesize that such stress-induced changes interfere with normal, non-canonical roles of DNA repair proteins in organ formation, thereby contributing to the emergence of morphological abnormalities.

MMS exposure during very early stage has long-term consequences on development

Previous studies have shown that MMS exposure induces malformations in medaka embryos [43]. Embryos exposed to 10 mM MMS for 2 h at 20-somite stage developed bent tails, abnormal eyes, and microcephaly at hatching. Similarly, 1–2 hpf zebrafish embryos continuously exposed to 8 mM MMS survived for 50 days [10]. In the present study, sterlet embryos exposed to 200 μM MMS between 2 and 24 hpf developed phenotypic abnormalities observed at 10 dpf (Fig. 1D). While differences in experimental design prevent direct comparison between species, these results support the use of sterlet embryos as a sensitive model for studying developmental effects of DNA alkylation. Further studies are required to evaluate species-specific sensitivity to DNA alkylation under standardized exposure conditions. Notably, we observed a non-linear effect on MMS dose which can be attributed to a cytoprotective role of alkyltransferase as described by [44]. Guérard et al. [44] reviewed a handful of literatures which stated non- linear dose response relationship for small alkylating agents like MMS. DNA repair machinery has been described as one of the pivotal mechanisms for preventing genotoxicity at low doses. Additionally, mismatch repair mediated cell death also plays a role in getting rid of damaged cells by apoptosis [44].

Eyes are the primary photoreceptive organs in fish [45], and proper eye development is essential for larval orientation, recruitment, and survival. In the present study, reduced eye pigmentation was observed in 200 µM MMS-treated sterlet larvae at 10 dpf compared to controls (Fig. 1D). Two hundred micromolar MMS larvae resembled ocular albinism phenotype, which is associated with low acuity and photophobia (Fig. 1D) [46, 47]. Eye albinism affects the formation of retinal pigment epithelium [48].

Furthermore, our preliminary observations suggested impaired photoreactivity in the treatment group (data not shown). Light plays a critical role during early development, influencing larval swimming activity, orientation to water currents, and schooling behavior, all of which are key to recruitment success [49].

Rods and cones are highly specialized photoreceptor cells that function as light detectors and represent the first neurons in the visual pathway [50]. In this study, histological analysis revealed disrupted retinal organization in MMS-treated embryos, suggesting impaired differentiation of photoreceptor layers (Fig. 2). Although detailed identification of rods and cones would require electron microscopy analysis, the observed alterations in retinal architecture provide a plausible explanation for the reduced photoreactivity in MMS-exposed embryos.

A significant decrease in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) was also detected following 200 μM MMS treatment (Fig. 2C), corroborating previous observations of reduced eye pigmentation (Fig. 1D). The RPE appears dark brown due to its melanin content, which protects the retina and internal nerves from ultraviolet light and minimizes oxidative damage by reducing reactive oxygen species accumulation [51]. As the RPE is important for both photoreceptor maintenance and visual cycle function, its impairment may contribute to observed light insensitivity at 10 dpf.

Given that retina originates from a lateral evagination of forebrain [52], alterations in the gene regulatory network responsible for forebrain development may also contribute to impaired retinogenesis. To investigate this possibility, expression of genes involved in early eye and brain development was assessed by qPCR. The retinal neuroepithelium is derived from embryonic neural tube, which expresses a set of transcription factors, including pax6a, pax2a, shha, sox2, otx2, msx2, and rax, all of each play pivotal roles in retinal patterning and neurogenesis [53, 54]. Among these, pax6 is considered the master regulator of eye development [55], with dual functions: (1) promoting transcription of numerous genes involved in lens formation and retinal specification and (2) maintaining balance between neural progenitor proliferation and differentiation during brain development [56, 57]. The sonic hedgehog (shh) signaling pathway also contributes to neural patterning and specification of retinal cell types, including photoreceptors and glia, and is known to regulate pax6 expression in the optic region [58–60].

At 8 dpf, a significant upregulation of shha and pax6a expression was detected in MMS-treated embryos compared to control embryos (Fig. 2D). This response may reflect a compensatory mechanism aimed at promoting neuronal survival following MMS-induced genotoxic stress. Overexpression of shha has previously been linked to neuroprotection, while increased pax6 levels may sustain RPC proliferation. However, dysregulated pax6 expression has also been associated with the small-eye phenotype, as reported by Manuel et al. [61], and may contribute to reduced eye size observed in MMS-treated embryos.

Rax promotes evaginations of eye field neuroepithelium to form optic vesicle [62] and also induces upregulation of eye field transcription factor, pax6 [63]. The pax2 gene family is critical for development of optic stalk and cup [64]. In this study, a significant downregulation of rx and pax2a expression was observed in MMS-treated embryos, which may indicate impaired formation of optic vesicle and its progression into the optic cup. Further, histology of 10-dpf sterlet embryos demonstrated improper differentiation of optic cup in response to MMS exposure (Fig. 2). Improper development of optic cup can interfere with organization of neuroblastic layer, which gives rise to RPCs and retinal pigment epithelium.

The developing brain is particularly vulnerable to genotoxic agents, and the extent of resulting injury depends on the type of DNA damage and the efficiency of DNA repair mechanisms [65]. otx2 and pax2a are also commonly used as early brain development markers [32]. In the present study, a decrease in both otx2 (although insignificant) and pax2a expression was observed at 8 dpf treated embryos with 200 μM MMS. By 10 dpf, however, the expression of these genes was comparable to that of control embryos, suggesting a transient developmental delay in the treated group. These findings align with the observed changes in expression of multiple eye and brain development genes at 8 dpf, which was largely normalized by 10 dpf. This temporal expression pattern may indicate that neurodevelopment was initially impaired, but partially recovered over time, potentially through compensatory upregulation of developmental regulators. These results support the hypothesis that MMS exposure affects retinogenesis and neurogenesis by transiently altering the expression of genes critical for early brain and eye development.

TH expression is commonly used as a biomarker of dopaminergic activity, and its deficiency can affect dopamine synthesis [66]. Dopamine is a key neurotransmitter that mediates diverse functions, including retinal development, visual signaling, and refractive development [67]. In our study, a reduction in TH protein was observed in MMS-treated embryos compared to control, suggesting that MMS exposure affected neurological processes, which might have impaired eye development. These results suggest that MMS exposure may interfere with dopaminergic signaling pathways. Given the role of dopamine in eye and neural development, reduced TH expression may reflect disruptions in neurodevelopmental processes potentially contributing to the abnormal eye phenotype. These findings support the hypothesis that MMS exposure during early embryogenesis can have delayed effects on both retinal and neurological development (Fig. 3).

MMS induces methyl DNA adducts in sterlet embryos, which are repaired by BER pathway

MMS is a well-established model agent to study DNA alkylation damage [7]. In this study, the extent of DNA alkylation, strand breaks, and activation of repair pathways in MMS-exposed sterlet embryos was assessed. The comet assay is widely used for phenotyping DNA damage and repair dynamics [68]. According to Singh et al. [69], a reduction in DNA breaks over time is indicative of active DNA repair.

In enzyme-modified comet assay, we detected a significant increase in tail DNA% at 1 and 3 dpf in MMS 200 treated group, relative to control embryos. These findings indicate that MMS induces persistent additive DNA base modifications that are still detectable at least until 3 dpf.

An increase in DNA alkylation was observed in control embryos at 3 dpf compared to 1 dpf (Fig. 4B) followed by a gradual decrease by 8 dpf. This pattern likely reflects the high frequency of cell specification events and rapid proliferation characteristics of early embryogenesis. Cellular differentiation during organogenesis elevates the risk of replication stress, often manifested as stalled replication forks and associated DNA fragmentation [70]. By 3 dpf, the rate of cell proliferation slows, replication-associated stress is reduced, and DNA repair can proceed more efficiently before hatching at 8 dpf. By this stage, tail DNA % did not significantly differ between MMS-treated and control embryos, suggesting initiation of DNA repair pathway between 1 and 8 dpf.

Since DNA alkylation is repaired by BER pathway, the expression of key BER-related genes (apex1, ogg1, polβ, xrcc1, and lig3) was analyzed during different developmental stages. Expression of these genes followed a stage-dependent pattern. No statistically significant differences were observed between control and treated groups in 1-dpf and 3-dpf embryos. However, at 8 dpf, the treated group showed a significant upregulation of BER genes, followed by a decrease at 10 dpf. A similar pattern of increased BER gene expression during hatching was reported in sea urchin embryos [4], it was suggested that adjustments in DNA repair capacity may be necessary during this critical developmental transition. Given the higher level of DNA alkylation observed in treated embryos, it is plausible that enhanced BER activity at 8 dpf contributed to damage resolution and facilitated normal hatching by 8 dpf.

Ape1 overexpression is due to its DNA repair activity, and this overexpression may impair organogenesis

The teratogenic potency of MMS has been shown to correlate with the extent of DNA alkylation in rodent embryos [71]. Likewise, in sterlet embryos, we speculate that MMS-induced phenotypic malformations are linked to DNA alkylation. It is further hypothesized that DNA damage during development may impair organogenesis by interfering with the non-canonical, developmental roles of certain DNA repair genes.

Apex1 is considered the master regulator of major proteins involved in BER pathway in vertebrates, as it is imperative for abasic site cleavage [32]. An increase in Ape1 protein expression indicates a response to DNA damage. MMS exposure has been reported to cause accumulation of abasic sites in Ciona intestinalis embryos, and such accumulation was identified as a primary cause of malformations [72]. Inhibition of Ape1 activity in that model abrogated the repair of abasic sites, emphasizing its essential role in repairing alkylation-induced damage during embryogenesis.

Beyond DNA repair, Ape1 is also known to modulate the balance between oxidative stress and cellular response mechanisms, particularly in the developing brain of zebrafish [32]. Our immunohistochemistry results revealed increased Ape1 protein levels in the eyes of 10-dpf MMS-treated sterlet embryos. This increase may represent an adaptive mechanism to mitigate DNA damage in sensory organs. We speculate that Ape1 might have a role in the eye development of sterlet, which is yet to be characterized. Notably, Ape1 expression in control embryos was primarily localized in the eyes, suggesting inherent vulnerability of ocular tissues to genotoxic stress and suggesting an organ-specific DNA damage response.

At 10 dpf, IHC analysis revealed increased expression of Ape1 protein in the eyes and brain of treated embryos. However, western blot analysis did not detect a statistically significant difference in total Ape1 protein levels between control and treated groups at this stage. Two possible explanations may account for this discrepancy: (1) Ape1 protein may have been translocated specifically to the eyes and brain at 10 dpf without an overall increase in total protein levels; or (2) Ape1 may have persisted in the head region while being degraded in other organs following its overexpression at 8 dpf. These speculations require further investigation in studies focused specifically on the role of Ape1 in sterlet neurodevelopment and DNA repair.

Increased apex1 gene expression during embryogenesis may have distinct consequences beyond its canonical DNA repair function, especially under stress conditions. To date, the impact of increased apex1 expression on its developmental functions—and potential consequences for embryonic patterning—has not been well characterized. Moreover, Ape1 contributes to various intricate processes, including inflammation, oxidative stress, apoptosis, redox reaction, and immunity. Coordination of these pathways may play a crucial role in embryo development. We speculate that increase in Ape1 expression for repairing MMS-induced alkylation during development may disturb these functions of Ape1, which may result in malformations.

To investigate the role of Ape1 overexpression behind eye malformations observed in response to MMS treatment, we checked the expression of Creb1 and some of the transcription factors regulated by Ape1. Creb1 plays a crucial role in neurogenesis, and genetic disruption of Creb1 leads to neurodegeneration in brain [73]. Pei et al. [32] reported that Apex1 mediates zebrafish brain development via regulating Creb1. Our results indicated a reduction in Creb1 protein abundance, although statistically insignificant, in MMS 200 treated embryos. Since we did not detect a significant change in expression of Creb1 protein, we speculate that exposure to alkylating agents during development may have affected function of Ape1 proteins. This might have disrupted various repair pathways independently of the expression levels of the repair proteins. However, our speculations need further investigation. Furthermore, the mechanistic basis behind etiology of malformation during embryogenesis needs further research.

Redox domain of Ape1 is responsible for regulating transcription factors, such as AP-1 [33], Egr-1 [34], Mdm2 [35], and p53 [36], among others. In this study, MMS exposure significantly increased the expression of p53 and mdm2 in 8 dpf embryos. This indicates that upregulation of Ape1 at 8 dpf corresponds to upregulation of its downstream transcription factors at hatching. However, by 10 dpf, the mRNA levels of all these factors were similar to control. This may suggest that Ape1 overexpression involves transcriptional upregulation via its redox function at the hatching stage. Although a statistically significant increase in p53 expression was detected in MMS 200 embryos at 8 dpf, followed by a slight, non-significant increase at 10 dpf, no direct link to apoptosis or cell cycle arrest could be established through this study.

Our results suggest a link between increased Ape1 expression and the altered hatching process in treated embryos. We propose that modulation of proteins involved in the BER pathway—particularly Ape1—is critical during key developmental stages to ensure successful hatching in embryos exposed to MMS. These findings highlight the need for further investigation into the non-canonical roles of DNA repair proteins during embryonic development.

Conclusions

This study shows that exposure to MMS during early embryogenesis induces DNA alkylation damage in sterlet embryos, which persists up to 3 dpf. By the hatching stage at 8 dpf, DNA damage appears to be repaired, coinciding with increased expression of BER pathway genes and Ape1 protein.

Although the repair process is largely successful at the molecular level, MMS-exposed embryos still exhibit phenotypic abnormalities, particularly in the eyes and brain. These findings suggest that early genotoxic stress can interfere with normal development, even when DNA repair mechanisms are activated. The spatiotemporal expression of Ape1 points to a potentially organ-specific response to damage, but further studies are needed to clarify its developmental role and long-term consequences of early-life DNA lesions.

Additionally, it has been hypothesized that environmental stressors encountered early in life can contribute to development of certain diseases later in life [74, 75]. The results obtained suggest that early-life DNA damage could have sensitized sturgeon embryos to stress at later stages. Thus, embryo readjustments at hatching stage led to overexpression of BER pathway genes and Ape1 protein in the absence of DNA fragmentation in MMS-treated embryos. The present study indicates that further research is required to better understand the long-term consequences of DNA alkylation.

This study exclusively describes the mechanism behind DNA alkylation and malformation. It limits direct ecological relevance of MMS toxicity on sterlet embryo development. Our study delves into the hypothesis that overexpression of DNA repair genes and proteins during early embryo development may have long-term consequences on organogenesis. This aspect of genotoxicity needs critical attention while reporting studies on teratogenic potential of xenobiotics.

Methods

Animals and ethics

Gametes were collected from sterlet (Acipenser ruthenus) from February to April, 2022–2024 from broodstock and fertilized in vitro. All broodstock manipulations were conducted in accordance with the law on protection of animals against cruelty (Act No. 246/1992 Coll.) as part of a sturgeon breeding program implemented by Faculty of Fisheries and Protection of Waters in a certified facility (Ref. No. 6OZ3066/2020–18134, Czech Republic). Broodstock (32 females and 29 males; body weight (BW): 0.5–2 kg) were reared in the aquaculture facility of the Research Institute of Fish Culture and Hydrobiology, University of South Bohemia, Vodňany, Czech Republic. The induced spawning method was performed as described by [76]. Females received carp pituitary (CP) extract powder dissolved in 0.9% (w/v) NaCl solution: an initial dose of 0.5 mg/kg BW, followed by a second injection of 4.5 mg/kg BW after 12 h. Oocytes were collected 18–20 h after second injection. Males received an injection of CP (4.0 mg/kg BW) dissolved in 0.9% (w/v) NaCl solution; sperm was collected 36–38 h later and stored at 4 °C until use. Oocytes were fertilized with sperm in dechlorinated tap water at 16 °C for 2 min, then washed three times with 0.1% tannic acid solution to remove adhesiveness. Embryos were incubated in Petri dishes with dechlorinated tap water at 16 °C. Early developmental stages were identified based on available staging table [77].

Experimental design

To investigate the detrimental effects of DNA damage on organogenesis, sterlet embryos were exposed to genotoxicant MMS (CAS No.: 66–27-3; Sigma-Aldrich Co.) during blastula stage, from 2 hpf (6–8 cell stage) to 24 hpf (early gastrula). A simplified version of an experimental design is included as Additional file: 1 Fig. S2. A stock solution of 10 mM MMS was freshly prepared in distilled water on the day of exposure. Working concentrations of 150 μM and 200 μM MMS were diluted in dechlorinated tap water and used as embryo culture medium. MMS concentrations were selected based on preliminary trials (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). We selected a set of concentrations which induced malformed eyes in more than 10% embryos without affecting survivability and hatching. In each set of experiments, dechlorinated tap water was used as a solvent control. For each treatment, 50 fertilized sterlet embryos at 6–8 cell stage were transferred into Petri dishes containing either control solution or MMS in 35 mL of dechlorinated tap water. Each experiment was repeated 12 times over 2 years using embryos from different parent combinations. Within each experiment, three replicates were used for each treatment group (control and MMS). After 22 h of exposure, MMS solution was replaced with dechlorinated tap water, and embryos were incubated until 10 dpf. Dead embryos were removed daily throughout the experiment. Embryos were routinely monitored and counted daily until 10 dpf. The water was changed daily till 10 dpf. Embryos were observed and photographed using Leica M205 FA fluorescence stereomicroscope. A minimum of 100 embryos per treatment were visually analyzed across different experimental replicates.

Further, to validate the effects of MMS, we conducted an additional experiment to validate our findings. We exposed sterlet embryos to different doses of Diethylnitrosoamine (DEN) from 2 to 24 hpf (during blastulation) and let them grow till hatching (8 dpf). DEN is an alkylating agent with similar mechanism of action as that of MMS [78] and in an ideal situation, effect of MMS may mimic the effect of DEN. DEN is present in food, water, and daily supplies and is regarded as a toxicant of carcinogenicity [79]. Precisely, we exposed sterlet embryos to 5, 10, 20, 50 and 200 µg/mL and observed long-term consequences of DEN exposure during early developmental stages of sterlet. Doses were selected from literature studying effect of DEN on zebrafish embryo development [79].

Histology of sterlet embryos

Sterlet embryos at 10 dpf were fixed in modified Davidson’s fixative (10% formaldehyde, 15% ethanol, 5% acetic acid, diluted in distilled water, pH 2.4) for 24 h. Freshly collected embryos were transferred to 2 mL of fixative and maintained on a 3D shaker during fixation. After 24 h, the embryos were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 10 min each and processed using a newly designed histological protocol specifically for processing sterlet embryos to prepare paraffin-embedded samples. Sections were cut at a thickness of 0.45 µm using a rotary microtome (Diaptah Galileo, Italy) and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Histological sections were examined under an Olympus BX51 microscope equipped with a digital camera. Images were captured using QuickPhoto MICRO 3.2 software (PROMICRA, Prague, Czech Republic) to detect potential tissue alterations. Sections from 30 biological replicates derived from three independent experiments were evaluated, and representative images were selected to illustrate the differences between control and treatment groups. Additionally, pigmentation of retinal pigment epithelium was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). The ratio of pigmented region to total eye area was calculated, with total eye area considered as 100%.

Comet assay

To detect double-strand breaks in DNA, we performed a neutral comet assay according to previously described protocols [80] with minor modifications [41]. We also employed an enzyme-modified alkaline comet assay to detect alkylated DNA bases, following previously described protocols [6, 81, 82]. This approach enabled us to assess the presence of alkylated bases at different developmental stages of sterlet embryos. For this assay, a cocktail of monofunctional 3-methyladenine DNA glycosylase (AAG,New England BioLabs, cat# M0313S) and AP1-endonuclease (APE1,New England BioLabs, cat# M0282) was used to convert MMS-induced 7meG and 3meA lesions into single-strand breaks (SSBs). This enzyme cocktail has been shown to selectively recognize alkylated bases, without detecting oxidized base lesions [6]. For validating specificity of the cocktail, we treated sterlet embryos (2 hpf old) with 0.1% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min and used them as a positive control. The results from positive control are included in Additional file 1: Fig. S3. To further validate our approach, we exposed sterlet embryos to 200 µg/mL DEN and performed alkaline and enzymatic comet assay (Additional file 1: Fig. S5).

Briefly, sterlet embryos at 1, 3, and 8 dpf from both control and treatment groups were transferred to 1.5-mL tubes (two embryos per tube) containing 1 mL of PBS and placed on ice. These developmental time points were selected based on preliminary experiments and a previous study by Gazo et al. [41]. The assay was repeated thrice using embryos from different batches. The embryos were mechanically disintegrated using a handheld manual homogenizer and resuspended in 800 μL of PBS. From each sample, suspension of 100 µL containing cells was mixed with 800 µL of 0.6% NuSieve GTG low melting point agarose (OxiSelectST,Cell Biloabs, INC., San Diego, CA, USA). Then, 40 µL of this mixture was added onto the slide, to solidify at 4 °C. After solidification, slides were then immersed in lysis buffer (2.5 M NaCl, 100 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris, 1% Triton X-100, 10% DMSO, pH 10) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. All samples were lysed under same conditions, regardless of whether they were used for neutral or enzyme-modified comet assay. Following lysis, slides intended for neutral comet assay were washed with Tris-based buffer (TBE: 90 mM Tris, 90 mM boric acid, 2.5 mM EDTA) and placed in a horizontal electrophoresis tank filled with freshly prepared TBE buffer. Electrophoresis was performed for 25 min at 50 V.

Conversely, slides for enzyme-modified alkaline comet assay were washed three times in PBS and incubated with AAG and APE1 (30 units/ml) working solutions for 1 h at 37 °C. The incubation time was selected based on protocols described by Collins et al. [81] and Muruzabal et al. [82]. Following enzyme treatment, slides were chilled at 4 °C for 10 min to halt the reaction and then washed with an alkaline electrophoresis solution (0.3 M NaOH, 1 mM EDTA, pH = 13). Subsequently, the slides were transferred to a horizontal electrophoresis tank filled with freshly prepared alkaline electrophoresis solution, and electrophoresis was performed for 9 min at 47 V.

Slides from neutral and enzyme-modified alkaline comet assays were washed three times with pre-chilled distilled water for 5 min each, dehydrated with 70% ethanol and air-dried for storage at 4 °C as recommended by [25]. Before analysis, 20 μL of PBS containing SYBR Green solution (Sigma-Aldrich) was added for agarose rehydration and DNA staining. Slides were analyzed under Olympus BX50 fluorescence microscope. The results of comet assay were assessed by counting 90–100 cells for each treatment and control group. The slides were randomized while assessing and scorer blinded. Comet images were performed using Casplab image analysis software (Comet Assay Software Project Lab). Percentage of tail DNA (the content of DNA in the comet tail) was measured using the formula:

RT-qPCR

At 1, 3, and 8 dpf embryos were collected and stored at − 80 °C until total RNA extraction. Six biological replicates (embryo batches) were analyzed. For each replicate, two embryos were pooled and homogenized in 1000 μL of Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher), and total RNA was extracted according to manufacturer’s protocol, including precipitation with 0.5 M LiCl. Samples were treated with DNase I while extracting the RNA. Total RNA concentration and RNA quality were evaluated using NanoDrop 2000 (ThermoScientific, Wilmington, DE, USA). Next, 1 µg of isolated total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using Maxima Reverse transcriptase kit (Invitrogen, Life Technologies). Total RNA concentration of the of sample having highest RNA quantity was used for normalization of each sample while loading it for cDNA synthesis. The reaction mix was prepared using 1 µL Oligo(dT)18, 1 µL dNTPs (10 mM each), and nuclease-free water (NFW) to a final volume of 14.75 µL. The master mix included 4 µL of 5 × reverse transcriptase (RT) buffer, 0.25 µL of Riboblock RNAase inhibitor, and 1 µL of Maxima RT enzyme. Both mixtures were combined to obtain a final reaction volume of 20 µL, incubated at 50 °C for 30 min and 85 °C for 5 min. The resulting cDNA was diluted with 180 µL of NFW and stored at − 80 °C. Primers were designed using Primer3 software (https://primer3.org/) based on nucleotide sequences from sterlet transcriptomes (NCBI) (Table 1). Since sterlet is a tetraploid species known to possess 2n = 120 chromosomes, we made sure that target genes do not have paralogous copies. For this, we performed BLAST analysis to check the paralogous copies of the target gene. While designing primers, we chose the highly conserved regions unique to the target transcript. Moreover, while choosing a transcript variant, we picked the variant which was biologically most relevant and had protein coding splice variant. Primers were designed within highly conserved regions unique to the target transcript, and their specificity was verified by melting-curve analysis during qPCR. For qPCR, 10 μL of master mix were prepared using 5 µL of Powerup SYBR Green Master Mix (Invitrogen), 0.5 µL of each primer (10 µM each), 2 µL of cDNA, and NFW. Amplification was performed using a Quantstudio 5 real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher, Grand Island, New York, USA) under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 60 °C for 20 s, and elongation at 72 °C for 20 s. Negative controls including no-template control and reactions lacking reverse transcriptase (-RT) were included to assess reaction purity and the efficiency of genomic DNA digestion Amplicon homogenicity was confirmed using melting curve analysis. PCR amplification efficiency was calculated using serial dilution of primers used for one of the target gene. The efficiency detected was 99% (data not shown). We did not use control genes for assessing relative mRNA expression. This was done because it has been previously described that expression of commonly used reference genes vary greatly during Xenopus laevis development [83]. The authors described that while comparing different developmental stages, developmental changes of mRNA expression can significantly compromise relative mRNA quantification based on reference genes. Additionally, previous studies from our research group observed similar observation during sterlet embryo development. Keeping this in view, in our study, gene expression in each treatment group was normalized to corresponding gene expression level in the control group. Data were analyzed using ΔΔCq method. Melt curve analysis for all the genes of qPCR showed a single peak confirming the single copy number of the gene.

Western blot analysis

Embryos at different developmental stages were mechanically dissociated in protein extraction buffer (8 M Urea, 2 M Thiourea, 4% CHAPS, 0.1% w/v Triton X-100, 100 mM dithiothreitol) supplemented with phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher, Catalog number 78420) and protease inhibitors (100 mM PMSF, 1 mg/mL pepstatin A, 5 mg/mL leupeptin). Proteins were extracted from four pooled embryos per condition. Three biological replicates from three different experiments were used for western blot analysis. Protein concentrations were estimated using Bradford assay, with absorbance measured at 595 nm on a Biobase EL10A microplate reader (Biobase).

For SDS-PAGE, protein samples were mixed with Laemmli buffer (Bio-Rad, Catalog number: 161–0737) and heated for 3 min at 95 °C. Proteins (50 µg per lane) were separated on a 10% acrylamide gel using a Mini-PROTEAN vertical electrophoresis system (Bio-Rad). Following electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. Transfer efficiency was controlled before immunodetection by staining the membrane with 0.1% (w/v) Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 in isopropanol and scanned using an imaging system (Fusion Solo 7S Edge; Vilber Lourmat, Collégien, France). After imaging, membranes were destained in acetic acid/ethanol/water (1:7:2). Destained membranes were washed with PBST (0.1% v/v Tween-20 in PBS) three times at room temperature. Membranes were blocked by incubation with 5% (w/v) bovine serum albumin in PBST for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies to Creb1 (Thermo Fisher, Catalog number 12208–1-AP), TH antibody (Merck, Catalog number T8700-1VL), and β-actin (Proteintech, Catalog number 20536–1-AP) diluted 1:1000 in 5% w/v bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBST. Membranes treated with APE1 antibody (Abcam, ab194) with 1:500 dilution were incubated at 4 °C for 48 h. After primary incubation, membranes were washed with PBST and incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse IgG (1:5000 in 5% w/v BSA-PBST) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using ECL-plus chemiluminescent substrate (GE Healthcare, Danderyd, Sweden) and visualized with the Fusion Solo 7S Edge imaging system. Intensity of each band was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH) and normalized against β-actin protein levels for each sample. Original uncropped images of blots are included in Additional file 1: Fig. S6.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Sterlet larva at 10 dpf was processed as described in the “Histology of sterlet embryos” section. Sections were collected on adhesive slides and dried at room temperature for 24 h. Prior to antibody incubation, slides were deparaffinized and rehydrated. To unmask the tissue epitopes, the sections were boiled in Tris–EDTA (pH 9) antigen retrieval buffer for 3 min, followed by cooling at room temperature. To reduce the endogenous peroxidase activity, sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. Non-specific binding was blocked with 1% BSA for 30 min at room temperature. Sections were then incubated with primary antibodies diluted in 1% BSA: APE1 at a 1:50 dilution (36 h at 4 °C), and TH at a 1:100 dilution (24 h at 4 °C). The slides were incubated for 36 h at 4 °C for APE1 and 24 h at 4 °C for TH. Following primary incubation, slides were washed thoroughly with Tris-based buffer and incubated with a secondary antibody (diluted in 1% BSA) for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the sections were washed with Tris buffer and incubated with DAB chromogen (Abcam, Catalog no. ab64238) for 40 s. Afterward, slides were rinsed under running water and counterstained with Harris hematoxylin. Finally, slides were dehydrated in graded ethanol series and cleared in xylene, mounted and stored at room temperature till analysis. Sections were examined using an Olympus BX51 camera, and photographs were taken using QuickPhoto MICRO 3.2 software (PROMICRA, Prague, Czech Republic). Sections from six biological replicates belonging to three different experiments were evaluated. The percentage of positive cells in the area of interest was evaluated manually by counting the total number of cells and number of positively stained cells within the same region.

Statistical analysis

Normality and homogeneity of the variance of all data were evaluated using Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Values of survivability (%), hatching rate (%), abnormal eye %, eye pigmentation, comet assay, RT-qPCR, western blot, and IHC are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of mean. The data did not follow normal distribution, so they were analyzed using Kruskal–Wallis nonparametric test for these parameters. Dunn’s test was used for multiple comparison. Analyses of survivability, hatching rate, comet assay, RT-qPCR, histology, western blot, and IHC were performed at a significance threshold of 0.05 using Graphpad Prism 10.0. software. Individual data points are represented as dots in graphs.
