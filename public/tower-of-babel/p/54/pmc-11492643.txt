Case reports of immune-related cystitis and the antibody combination hypothesis
Authors: Peng Zhang, Chunyan Yin, Ming Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune-related cystitis is a rare condition, and its diagnostic criteria and pathogenesis are not yet fully understood. Here, we report two cases of immune-related cystitis. Both patients were previously diagnosed with lung squamous cell carcinoma and received combined treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors and chemotherapy, leading to hemorrhagic cystitis. We reviewed the cystoscopic images and pathological features of previous cases and found that autoantibodies against hemidesmosomes may be the cause of immune-related cystitis, proposing the “antibody combination” hypothesis to explain the tissue specificity of the condition.Keywords: : autoantibodies, hemorrhagic cystitis, immune-related adverse events, immune checkpoint inhibitors, lung neoplasm

Introduction

Despite the significant improvements in cancer prognosis achieved by immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), these treatments can also induce immune-related adverse events (irAEs), among which immune-related cystitis is exceptionally rare. To date, nine publications have reported on this condition, each providing biopsy pathology data of the bladder mucosa at the onset of the disease [1–9]. The characteristics of the cases from these nine publications are listed in Supplementary Table S1. The pathogenesis of this disease remains unclear. We aimed to explore the potential mechanisms by analyzing the following two cases and reviewing the perspectives of previous literature.

Case one

Case presentation

A 59-year-old Chinese man presented with a one-month history of cough and sputum. He had no significant medical history or allergies. Physical examination revealed enlarged lymph nodes in the left supraclavicular fossa. Contrast-enhanced chest computed tomography (CT) scan showed enlarged lymph nodes in the mediastinum and a 29 × 21 mm mass in the left upper lobe adjacent to the left hilus. Subsequent bronchoscopic biopsy confirmed squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) pathologically. No brain metastasis was detected by MRI scan. The patient was diagnosed with SCC of the left lung, staged as cT3N2M0 and planned to undergo systemic chemotherapy instead of left pneumonectomy.

In the first 4 months following diagnosis, the patient underwent four cycles of initial chemotherapy, which consisted of carboplatin 400 mg on day one and gemcitabine 1.6 g on days one and eight every 21 days.

In the 6th month after diagnosis, the chest CT scan indicated progression of the primary lesion, resulting in atelectasis of the left upper lobe. Consequently, the patient underwent three cycles of second-line chemotherapy, consisting of nedaplatin 120 mg on day one and albumin-bound paclitaxel 300 mg on day one every 21 days.

In the 10th month after diagnosis, a subcutaneous mass measuring 10 × 5 mm appeared in the right hypochondriac region and was confirmed as metastatic SCC through pathological examination following mass resection. Simultaneously, a chest CT scan revealed stability in the primary lesion. Consequently, the patient began combined therapy, consisting of sintilimab 200 mg on day one and albumin-bound paclitaxel 300 mg on day one every 21 days, with carboplatin excluded due to poor performance status. After six cycles, a chest CT scan showed significant remission in the primary lesion. Additionally, the urinalysis was negative at that time.

In the 16th month after diagnosis, it was 16 days after the last cycle of combined therapy that the patient presented with urinary symptoms, including urgency, frequency, nocturia, painful urination and gross hematuria, which had persisted for more than 10 days. Physical examination revealed suprapubic tenderness. Blood analysis indicated normal platelet count and coagulation function. Urinalysis showed elevated counts of white blood cells and red blood cells: 1543.2/μl (normal range: 0.0–9.0/μl) and 13888.2/μl (normal range: 0.0–13.0/μl), respectively. Ultrasonography showed no significant lesions in the prostate, and the post-void residual urine volume was 11 ml. CT urography and pelvic MRI revealed no notable abnormalities in the urinary tract, except for a bladder wall thickness of approximately 5 mm. Urine cultures for potential pathogens, including bacteria, fungus, mycobacteria and mycoplasma, were negative, as was urinary cytology for malignancy. Subsequently, a cystoscopy was performed, revealing diffuse erythema and floating mucosa off the wall (Supplementary Figure S1). Biopsy results confirmed pathologically urothelium desquamation and granulation tissue, along with infiltration of neutrophils, lymphocytes and plasmocytes (Figure 1).

The pathological examination of the bladder mucosa biopsy in case one indicated urothelium desquamation, significant infiltration of acute and chronic inflammatory cells, and the formation of granulation tissue.

Initial diagnosis

Immune-related cystitis was considered.

Treatment

Chemotherapy and immunotherapy were discontinued and not resumed. Methylprednisolone 40 mg was administered once daily for 3 days, and the steroid dose was gradually reduced over the next 8 weeks.

Outcome & implication

The patient’s symptoms improved within 4 days after steroid treatment. Urinary symptoms did not recur after discontinuing the steroid. In the 19th month following diagnosis, a follow-up chest CT revealed osteolytic lesions in the right third and fourth ribs, suggestive of metastasis.

Case two

Case presentation

A 72-year-old Chinese male presented with a three-month history of hemoptysis and 1 month of chest tightness. He had no significant medical history or allergies. Physical examination revealed no significant abnormalities. A CT scan revealed a thick-walled cystic lesion in the right middle lobe of the lung (measuring 49 × 41 mm) with internal cavitation, accompanied by multiple enlarged lymph nodes in the mediastinum and right hilum, suggestive of lung cancer. Upon admission, liver and kidney function tests, as well as urinalysis, were normal. Acid-fast bacilli staining and tuberculosis culture of sputum and bronchial brushing were negative. A CT-guided percutaneous lung biopsy of the right lung lesion was performed, yielding a pathology report consistent with SCC. The clinical stage was determined as cT2N2M0.The treatment course was illustrated in Figure 2.

The treatment course of case two. The horizontal axis represented the follow-up time after the diagnosis of lung cancer, measured in months.

Three months after diagnosis, the patient developed abnormal liver function, with alanine aminotransferase levels of 298 U/l (normal range: 9–50 U/l) and aspartate aminotransferase levels of 107 U/l (normal range: 15–40 U/l), leading to the temporary cessation of chemotherapy. By the 4th month after diagnosis, the patient’s liver function had returned to normal, and chemotherapy resumed.

In the 8th month after diagnosis, the patient began experiencing urinary frequency, and nocturia, and gradually developed gross hematuria, incomplete emptying, and bilateral flank pain, along with left pelvic discomfort. Blood analysis indicated normal platelet count and coagulation function, but the creatinine level was elevated at 169.7 μmol/l (normal range: 57.0–111.0 μmol/l). Urinalysis revealed elevated red blood cell and white blood cell counts: 5488 and 75/μl, respectively. Urine cultures for potential pathogens, including bacteria, fungus, mycobacteria and mycoplasma, were negative, as was urinary cytology for malignancy. CT imaging indicated mild bilateral hydroureterosis, more prominent on the left side, and ultrasonography showed a postvoid residual urine volume of 13 ml. Lower urinary tract symptoms persisted despite treatment with tamsulosin.

In the 11th month after diagnosis, the serum creatinine level was 140.5 μmol/l, and CT still showed bilateral hydroureterosis. The serum level of anti-BP180 antibody was 749.9 pg/ml (normal range: 19.6–286.8 pg/ml), the serum level of anti-integrin α6 antibody was 463.7 pg/ml (normal range: 18.2–305.5 pg/ml) and the serum level of anti-integrin β4 antibody was 450.2 pg/ml (normal range: 15.6–268.4 pg/ml). Subsequently, cystoscopy revealed diffuse erythema, significant local edema and follicular hyperplasia, with floating mucosa in the bladder (Supplementary Figure S2). Biopsy results indicated pathologically urothelium desquamation and granulation tissue, along with chronic mucosal inflammation (Figure 3).

The pathological examination of the bladder mucosa biopsy in case two indicated urothelium desquamation, granulation tissue and chronic mucosal inflammation.

Initial diagnosis

Immune-related cystitis was considered.

Treatment

Methylprednisolone was administered at 40 mg once daily for 2 days, gradually tapered over 8 weeks.

Outcome & implication

After 4 days of steroid treatment, the serum creatinine levels returned to normal, urinary symptoms resolved within a week and urinalysis normalized after 4 weeks.

In the 14th month after diagnosis, a follow-up CT scan revealed improvement in bilateral hydroureterosis and an increase in the size of the right middle lobe mass. Consequently, the patient commenced definitive radiotherapy to the lesion, with a planned dose of 6000 cGy in 30 fractions to 95% of the planning target volume, administered five-times per week. During radiotherapy, the patient experienced urinary frequency once again. Urinalysis showed elevated levels of red blood cells at 532.5/μl and white blood cells at 831.7/μl. Urine culture was negative, and renal ultrasound revealed left-sided hydronephrosis. A recurrence of immune-related cystitis was considered, and the patient was treated again with methylprednisolone at 40 mg once daily, tapering over 8 weeks. Urinary symptoms improved once more. In the 16th-month follow-up, CT imaging showed a reduction in the size of the right middle lobe lesion and immunotherapy continued. During this period, the patient did not experience recurrent urinary symptoms. In the 22nd month, the serum levels of the three autoantibodies were re-assessed as recovery phase levels. The qualitative test for serum anti-BP180 antibody was positive. The anti-BP180 antibody level was 294.6 pg/ml, the anti-integrin α6 antibody level was 320.3 pg/ml, and the anti-integrin β4 antibody level was 272.8 pg/ml. However, in the 24th month, the patient presented with left hip pain, and a follow-up CT scan revealed pelvic bone metastases.

Thought experiment

Background assumption

Suppose there is Structure X, its extracellular architecture consists of two subunits, α and β, which exhibit significant antigenic differences while being otherwise identical in all other aspects. The functionality of Structure X within a unit volume is equal to the sum of the quantities of the two subunits, α and β. Structure X is distributed across nine tissues, ranging from Tissue (T) 1 to T9. While the content of subunits α and β within a unit volume varies among these nine tissues, the functionality of Structure X remains consistent across them all. Methods

Suppose there are autoantibodies against subunit α and β, known as anti-α and anti-β, respectively. An equal amount of these two antibodies is combined and added to the same volume of the aforementioned nine tissues. Plan One: The antibody combination can block the function of four subunit α and three subunit β per unit volume. Plan Two: The antibody combination can block the function of two subunit α and five subunit β per unit volume.

Conclusion

Discussion

Some prior literatures suggested that the pathogenesis of immune-related cystitis may be associated with abnormal upregulation of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression [2,6]. However, other studies found negative immunohistochemical staining for PD-L1 in the tissue [3,7]. In cases where there was an upregulated expression of PD-L1 prior to ICI treatment, there was a potential for persistent or recurrent urinary tract infection (UTI) due to the negative influence of PD-L1 on lymphocyte activation [10]. Interestingly, contrary to expectations, patients with high PD-L1 expression in the urothelium did not have a history of UTI; in fact, UTI was ruled out before the diagnosis of immune-related cystitis [2,6]. As PD-L1 expression was regulated by interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) [11], it was proposed that the increased expression of PD-L1 was induced by IFN-γ secreted by infiltrating lymphocytes, however, the cognate antigen of these lymphocytes in the urothelium remained unknown [2].

Urothelium desquamation was noted as a pathological feature in five cases of immune-related cystitis [1,5,7–9]. To investigate its pathogenesis and the role played by ICI, we chose the first case report for discussion. Two pathological features were observed in this case: minimal lymphocytic infiltration and complete sloughing off of the mucosal epithelium, including basal, intermediate and umbrella cells [1].

The potential etiologies for urothelium desquamation encompassed UTI [12], eosinophilic cystitis [13] and bladder cancer [14,15], all of which were ruled out in both the first case and our cases. Another possible factor was the complication of paclitaxel protein-bound particles for injectable suspension. According to the US FDA, less than 1% of the total administered dose was excreted into urine as the metabolites 6α-hydroxypaclitaxel and 3`-p-hydroxypaclitaxel. Additionally, its terminal half-life was approximately 27 h [16], much shorter than the onset time of immune-related cystitis in all the mentioned case reports. Albumin-bound paclitaxel rarely resulted in hemorrhagic cystitis. It only occurred to one female patient with breast cancer, who presented with urinary incontinence, urination ache and gross hematuria on the 2nd day of the first cycle of albumin-bound paclitaxel administration [17]. Therefore, other pathogenic factors should be considered, which may not be evident in hematoxylin and eosin-stained tissue sections, such as autoantibodies.

Given the extensive shedding of bladder mucosa observed during cystoscopy and the urothelium desquamation in pathological sections, we suspected that the tight junction structure attaching urothelial basal cells to the basement membrane may be compromised. This tight junction structure is known as type I hemidesmosome, with extracellular components including BP180, integrin α6 and β4 [18,19]. Therefore, we tested for the corresponding antibodies. In case two, the serum levels of these three autoantibodies were abnormally elevated. Although the changes in serum levels of anti-BP180 autoantibodies were more pronounced during the onset and recovery phases, can it be inferred that this single autoantibody alone can cause urothelium desquamation? Abnormal levels of anti-BP180 autoantibodies often led to bullous pemphigoid (BP) [20], and the expression levels of BP180 in skin tissues were much higher than in bladder (see Supplementary Figure S3) [21]. If abnormal anti-BP180 autoantibodies alone could cause urothelium desquamation, then patients with BP should often be accompanied by urothelium desquamation. However, in reality, cystoscopy findings in patients with BP typically demonstrated hyperemia/edema of the base of the bladder and the trigone area, with or without concomitant squamous metaplasia [22]. Urothelium desquamation was observed in only one case of BP [23]. Research had also shown that the binding of BP180 to laminin-332 alone was insufficiently strong to promote the adhesion of basal cells to the basement membrane in the absence of integrin α6β4 [24]. Hence, we speculated that additional antibodies could be involved in the urothelium desquamation observed in immune-related cystitis.

In a prior experiment led by Jonathan C.R. Jones, antibodies targeting integrin α6β4 were observed to disrupt the formation of hemidesmosomes and separate the bundles of intermediate filaments from the basal plasma membrane. Notably, this process did not involve either innate or adaptive immune cells, but solely antibodies [25]. Additionally, mutations in the ITGB4 gene had been implicated in urothelium desquamation as well [26,27]. These findings collectively confirmed that abnormalities in integrin α6 and β4 can lead to urothelium desquamation.

Moreover, using GEPIA (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn), we found that the expression levels of BP180, integrin α6 and β4 were significantly higher in lung SCC compared with normal lung tissue (refer to Supplementary Figure S6 for details) [28]. These overexpressed antigens may be recognized by MHC and presented to T lymphocytes during ICI therapy, leading to the production of corresponding autoantibodies by B lymphocytes, ultimately resulting in immune-related cystitis [29].

Therefore, we proposed that the development of immune-related cystitis may result from the combined action of autoantibodies targeting BP180, integrin α6 and β4. Due to limitations in research funding and technical capabilities, we were unable to perform additional analyses on the pathological slides, such as assessing the levels of BP180, integrin α6 and β4. However, the most significant information we observed from the pathological slides was the detachment of the urothelial cells, a finding also noted in previous case reports, which prompted us to investigate antibodies against hemidesmosomes. Since BP180, integrin α6 and β4 are all expressed by basal cells [19], their disappearance is likely if the basal cells themselves are lost.

Type I hemidesmosomes were not only found in the urothelium but also in basal keratinocytes of multilayered squamous epithelium [30,31]. On the other hand, type II hemidesmosomes, which consisted of only integrin α6β4 and plectin, were predominantly present in cylindrical epithelia [32]. We obtained the distribution of BP180, integrin α6 and β4 in normal tissues from the Human Protein Atlas dataset (https://www.proteinatlas.org) [21], as detailed in Supplementary Figures S3–S5. So why do elevated antibodies against hemidesmosomes in the serum only lead to urothelium abnormalities? To address this question, we proposed the “antibody combination” hypothesis in the thought experiment, aiming to elucidate the tissue specificity of immune-related cystitis. Based on this hypothesis, we still had three inquiries: Are other immune-related adverse events caused by specific antibody combinations as well? Are there specific antibody combinations that can selectively target cancerous tissues? Is it feasible to develop bispecific antibodies from two antibodies within these combinations? These hypotheses, potential pathogenic mechanisms and inquiries all require further validation through additional clinical cases and experiments.

In both the previously reported cases and those we have presented, UTI was ruled out before diagnosing immune-related cystitis [1–9]. However, can these two conditions coexist? ICI can lead to various adverse events, including UTI [33]. In our cases, the likelihood of UTI due to sintilimab injection ranged from 1 to 10% [34], while the probability of pembrolizumab causing UTI of all grades was approximately 11–31% [35]. On the other hand, urothelium desquamation increased the likelihood of UTI [36]. Additionally, UTIs can also result in urothelium desquamation [12]. Therefore, for persistent or recurrent UTIs, should we consider immune-related cystitis? How can we definitively diagnose this potential condition? Should we rely on cystoscopy, lesion biopsy and pathology indicating urothelium desquamation to determine if it is caused by UTI or ICI? Perhaps specific combinations of antibodies in the serum could help determine whether UTI is concurrently associated with immune-related cystitis.

Conclusion

In both cases of immune-related cystitis, we observed pathological features of urothelium desquamation. Elevated serum levels of anti-BP180 antibodies, as well as antibodies against integrin α6 and β4, may be implicated in its pathogenesis. We also proposed the “antibody combination” hypothesis to explain the tissue specificity of this condition. However, further clinical cases and research are needed to validate these ideas.
