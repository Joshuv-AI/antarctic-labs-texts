Development and Validation of a Monte Carlo Beam Model for 6, 8, and 15 MV Clinical Photon Beams on the Elekta™ Precise Linac
Authors: JJL Smit, Déte Van Eeden, F. C. P. Du Plessis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:This study investigates a single-source photon beam model and a single source for contamination electrons for Monte Carlo (MC) simulations. Our approach uses a beam characterization model-to-model clinical photon beams for 6, 8, and 15 MV on a linear accelerator. The flattening filter attenuates the photon fluence in the beam model before reaching the secondary collimation accessories. An error function was used to model scatter from these devices. This study aimed to develop a model for clinical X-ray source beams for MC dose calculations for an ElektaTM Precise Linac.Materials and Methods:The photon energy was modeled with the Schiff formula and off-axis beam softening. DOSXYZnrc was used to calculate X-ray dose distributions in water and RW3 solid water phantoms. These distributions were compared to measured EBT3 film or water tank dose data. A gamma (γ) index was calculated to compare the MC and the measured dose. The criteria for the γ-index were 2% dose/2 mm distance-to-agreement. Dose distribution data for square, rectangular, offset, and irregular fields were compared with measurements.Results:A range of fields for 6, 8, and 15 MV beams was modeled, simulated, and compared to corresponding measured water tank data. Rectangular, small, and medium-sized offset fields met the γ-index criteria of 2%/2 mm.Conclusion:The beam characterization model performed well against measured data and can be employed for dose verification ranging from regular fields to conformal treatments, as demonstrated by various multi-leaf collimator shapes.

INTRODUCTION

Monte Carlo (MC) simulation methods are regarded as the golden standard for dose calculations.[1] The simulations replicate individual particle tracks based on random numbers and interaction cross-sections to sample dynamic variables of particles from probability density functions.[2] The physics required for these simulations is well known, leading to the accuracy of these simulations. Several MC codes exist for dose calculations, for example, Geant4 Application for Tomographic Emission (GATE), FLUKA, IDEAL-DOSE, penetration and energy loss of positrons and electrons (PENELOPE), BrachyDose, BEAMnrc, and DOSXYZnrc.

The Geant4 Application for Tomographic Emission code is based on the general-purpose GEANT4 toolkit for applications in external beam nuclear medicine, radiation therapy (RT), brachytherapy, intraoperative RT, hadrontherapy, and in vivo dose monitoring.[3] FLUKA is a general-purpose code for particle interaction and transport for radiation protection studies applicable to high-energy accelerators.[4] The IDEAL-DOSE code is used for microdosimetry.[5] PENELOPE is a general-purpose code for coupled electron-photon transport simulation in arbitrary materials and complex quadric geometries.[6] The BrachyDose, BEAMnrc, and DOSXYZnrc codes are based on the Electron Gamma Shower (EGSnrc) code system. BrachyDose is used for brachytherapy sources dosimetry.[7] BEAMnrc is used for modeling ionizing radiation-producing devices, i.e., linacs and kilovoltage units.[89] DOSXYZnrc is used for three-dimensional (3D) dose calculation.[10]

The EGSnrc code system simulates coupled electron–photon transport and incorporates the implementation of the condensed history technique for charged particle transport simulation and accurate low-energy cross-sections. EGSnrc is an improved and extended version of the older EGS4 and original EGS packages, which were developed at Stanford Linear Accelerator Center.[11]

Tyagi et al. used MC simulations to model the geometry of 120 multileaf collimators (MLCs) for Intensity Modulated RT (IMRT) purposes for a VarianTM 21 EX (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA) linear accelerator (linac).[12] Dosimetric comparisons were made to homogeneous phantom geometry measurements under different IMRT delivery circumstances. Sikora et al. also used MC simulations to create a virtual source model of a mini MLC of an Elekta Beam ModulatorTM (Elekta Oncology Systems, Crawley, UK) for intensity-modulated radiosurgery treatments.[13] Both achieved overall agreement results within 2%/2 mm gamma index evaluation.

To implement MC dose calculation in the clinic, it is necessary to model the therapeutic beam with high accuracy. It can be done in more than one way. The most robust way is to model the entire linac and do a complete simulation to score beam parameters into a phase-space file (PSF). Subsequent simulation into a 3D dose-scoring geometry is then performed based on the simulated field shape for the treatment. Another approach is the substitution of the linac simulation with a photon source and beam shaping devices, and to construct a beam model instead. The idea is that the beam model should be able to conform the fluence to take the beam shape into account. We use the term beam model as opposed to a multisource model since we restrict ourselves to a single point source of photons and a contamination electron source. Error functions are then used to model the influence of these beam-shaping devices that, in reality, cause scattering from their edges.

Virtual source models consist of analytical equations to replicate the photons’ energy spectrum and fluence generated from the linac treatment head.[1415] An advantage of virtual source models is that they reduce simulation time to achieve faster results. The disadvantage of achieving faster results is that important scatter is missing for accurate dose distributions. Multiple sources are mostly used in source models, as the components in the treatment head increase the complexity of the models. Single target source models usually fail to compensate for scattered photons that are generated by the various components in the head, as the findings by Yan et al.[16] show. They compared three different source models with diode array measurements to find an appropriate head scatter source model for fast and accurate planar dose calculations for IMRT. They found the best agreement using the two-source model (primary target point source and flattening filter extra-focal source) in comparison to the three-source model (primary target point source, primary collimator, and flattening filter extrafocal sources) and single-source model (target point source). The three-source and single-source models underestimated head scatter for various symmetric rectangular and asymmetric fields. Measurement-driven models, where the development of models is derived solely from measurements, will be used in this study.[17] In our work, we revert to a single photon source but with an additional fluence modulation using error functions as described in the “Methods” section in an attempt to model beam scatter due to collimation devices in the linac head.

Fluence source models are also found in treatment planning systems, such as Varian’s EclipseTM, Elekta’s XiO® and Monaco®, in order for the algorithms to calculate the absorbed dose as accurately as possible from a linac’s commissioned data.[35]

This study aims to present a beam model based on mathematical equations that can replicate clinical X-ray beams as applied to an ElektaTM Precise linac and to validate the model to film and water tank measurements for a range of field shapes.

Theory

Figure 1 shows the configuration of components inside the treatment head of an ElektaTM Precise linac, according to the IEC 60601 standards.[18] The photon fluence from the target is initially shaped by the primary collimator into a circular field shape. It is then attenuated by a flattening filter, which is designed to create a uniform photon fluence distribution at about 10 cm depth in water. The circular field of the photon fluence is further defined by secondary collimators, for example, the jaws and MLCs. They are both manufactured from a tungsten, nickel, and cobalt alloy. The photon beam also interacts with the air along its path to the patient’s surface, which also generates contamination electrons that contribute to the surface dose.

Head configuration for an ElektaTM precise linear accelerator (linac) according to IEC 60601

Photons, electrons, and positrons are scored in predefined PSFs during the simulation process of the linac head using the BEAMnrc MC code. This, however, takes additional time to obtain PSF data for further simulation in the DOSXYZnrc code. In a PSF, every parameter or variable of the system is represented as an axis of a multidimensional space.[19] The variables of the particles simulated include the charge, energy, direction of the path, and position of the particles. This is where the power of a representative beam model lies.

The beam model of the ElektaTM Precise linac head consists of mathematical equations to replicate the photons’ energy spectrum and fluence field for various photon energy beams produced by the machine. The different parameters of the source model will be discussed in the following sections.

An Interactive Data Language (IDL) graphical user interface (GUI) was developed to generate source files for the source model that can be used as input for the MC simulations.

Photon energy spectra and fluence modeling

The beam model of the linac head must replicate the photon energy spectrum and fluence for various beam energies produced by the machine. We assume a single photon point source, as well as an additional point source for the energy spectrum and fluence to model contamination electrons.

The photon energy spectrum, Φp (E, r), is based on the modified Schiff formula[2021] and can vary in the off-axis distance (r) due to flattening filter-induced beam softening. In Figure 2, the thickness profiles are shown for the flattening filter for the three beam energies used in this study.

Figure 3 shows the calculated photon energy spectra for 6 MV at various radial distances (r) as obtained from the IDL program. The radial distance is the distance from the central axis (CAX) in cm. At a 4 cm radial distance, there is a higher number of photons of lower energy components than the profile at the CAX (r = 0 cm) due to the beam hardening effect from the flattening filter.

Photon energy spectrum profiles for 6 MV beams at various radial distances (r). As r increases, so do the low energy components increase to model off-axis beam softening

The fluence generated by the target is approximated by a Gaussian function in a plane below the flattening filter shown in Figure 4.

Contamination electron energy spectrum

The contamination electron energy spectrum was derived based on the difference between a clinical depth dose (measured in water) and a pure photon beam (simulated in water). Figure 8 illustrates this. The pure X-ray beam is represented by a depth dose curve for a 20 cm × 20 cm FS, while the clinical beam is the measured depth dose profile of the same FS for a 15 MV beam. Both were normalised at a depth of 10 cm. From Figure 8, it is clear that a dose is required in the build-up region; thus, contamination electrons for the source model are necessary. Electrons lose approximately 2 MeV/cm in water and can be used as a scaling factor to convert depth into energy values. The dose difference curve from Figure 8 is displayed in Figure 9 as an energy spectrum.

Percentage depth dose comparison between measured a clinical beam and a pure photon beam for a 15 MV 20 cm × 20 cm square field aperture. The green dash curve is the difference in dose between the data sets

Comparison of dose difference from build-up electron depth dose with the percentage depth dose of 15 MV 20 cm × 20 cm square aperture consisting solely out of electrons. PDD: Percentage depth dose

As a test, a 15 MV 20 cm × 20 cm source file, consisting entirely of electrons, was simulated, and its percentage depth dose (PDD) curve was compared to the dose difference curve [green curve in Figure 8], and the comparison is displayed in Figure 9 below. The comparison in Figure 9 indicates that the electron spectrum approximation is suitable for additional dose in the build-up region of a depth dose curve.

In Figure 8, the normalised depth dose curves converge to the same values at a depth of 3.5 cm, which translates into a 7 MeV electron range. Thus, the dose depths are converted into equivalent electron energies, meaning that they would have the range at the depth of interest. The next step is to set up a discrete electron energy spectrum with bin widths of 1 MeV. The relative amount of electrons in a bin corresponds to the relative value of the dose difference taken at the middle value of the energy bin. It was found that this approximation yielded good correspondence between clinical and simulated beam data presented in the results section.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Beam model parameters of the source model

Photon fluence and energy spectra for the MC beam models for each beam energy were set up for a series of square fields ranging from 1 cm × 1 cm to 40 cm × 40 cm. Dose distributions were simulated in water and compared with the corresponding measured data. PDD and dose profiles were compared. The gamma (γ) index was used as the metric to determine the correspondence between the MC and water tank data. A series of rectangular aperture configurations used to test the beam models included a 5 cm × 20 cm, 20 cm × 5 cm, 10 cm × 30 cm, and 30 cm × 10 cm in the X and Y directions. As a further test, irregular field apertures were created solely by MLCs with the X and Y jaws backing up at the outermost MLC positions of these apertures. Three aperture geometries were generated. The geometries used are based on the ones used by Georg et al.[23] A Triangle, Arrow and Mirror E field shape was set up for the 6, 8, and 15 MV beams.

Benchmarking X-ray field data measurements

Water tank measurements

To validate the beam model, benchmarking dose profile data in water were obtained in a Scanditronics Wellhöfer Blue Phantom using OmniProTM Accept 6.4a (IBA Dosimetry GmbH, Schwarzenbrück, Germany) scanning software. A CC01 ionization chamber was used for signal detection of the 1 cm × 1 cm–5 cm × 5 cm square FS apertures, with the CC13 chamber used for larger square, rectangular and offset FS apertures.

Gafchromic film measurements

For irregular FS apertures, Gafchromic® EBT3 film was used to obtain 2D dose distributions in an RW3 solid phantom. An HPTM Photosmart C4683 document scanner was used with a colour depth of 48-bit. The exposed films were scanned and converted into JPEG images. They were each inverted, and the red channel was analysed using ImageJ software. The Color Profiler tool in ImageJ was used to extract the red colour channel from each RGB colour channel, as it produces the maximum response to radiation.[24] The film was calibrated as follows: RW3 solid water slabs were used: 5 cm × 1 cm slabs for backscatter and 1.5, 2.0, and 2.5 cm slabs as build-up for the 6, 8, and 15 MV energy beams, respectively. Film pieces were placed at each energy’s depth of maximum dose, dmax, for 100 cm SSD and a 10 cm × 10 cm FS. For each energy, ten pieces of film were irradiated between 0 and 300 cGy at regular dose intervals to determine the pixel-to-dose curves. Film pieces were scanned 24 h after irradiation with a scanning resolution of 75 dpi. After the films were scanned, a region-of-interest of 72 × 72 pixels was drawn in for each film piece during analyses using ImageJ to obtain the average red pixel value to set up the pixel-to-dose relationships.

For irregular field dose measurement, the films were sandwiched between RW3 solid water slabs; 5 cm was used for backscatter, and 5 cm on top of the film for build-up.

Benchmarking the source model

Monte Carlo simulations

The beam models were tested using the DOSXYZnrc MC code for dose simulations. A water phantom was modeled, and the global electron and photon cut-off energy values were set at 0.7 and 0.01 MeV, respectively. The resolution of the water phantom was set up so that profile and depth dose data had a resolution of 0.2 cm. The water phantom was constructed for dose simulations for square, rectangular, and offset FS apertures. For the irregular FS apertures above, RW3 solid water slabs with a physical density of 1.045 g/cm were also modeled with a resolution of 0.2 cm. Gamma analysis

The gamma index, as denoted by Low et al.[26] is a combination of dose difference and distance-to-agreement (DTA) that is used to calculate a dimensionless metric for each dose point. The DTA measures the distance between a point on the reference distribution and a point on the evaluated distribution with equal dose.[2728] The reference distribution usually refers to the “gold standard”, which in this case will be the water tank measurements on the linac. The evaluated distribution will be the dose distribution obtained through the MC simulations with the beam model. From the results for the irregular geometries, one can conclude that the model performs satisfactorily up to the conditions over which it was tested. The high γ-index values can be attributed to noise in the measured or simulation data. Higher γ-index values are also seen in the umbra region outside of the field. These results show that the beam model can accurately model the MLCs.

A closer look into the source parameters for the photon beam model in Table 1 shows that the X and Y jaws have different FWHM (sigma_jaws) values and can be separated according to FS. Notably small fields up to 4 cm × 4 cm, and then large fields. The larger fields usually have a larger source of scattered photons, and this increases the photon source. This leads to an increase in beam penumbra, which is represented by the FWHM value. In order to stay within the gamma index limit of 2%/2 mm, it is necessary to adjust the FWHM value as the FS increases. Therefore, leading to two regions of FSs. The X-jaws are closer to the measurement plane and therefore their FWHM (0.042) is always less than that for the Y-jaws (0.197) for small fields. The effective transmission increases as the FS increases, as shown in the last two columns in Table 1. The 40 cm × 40 cm field shows an exception, but it is due to the measurement dimensions of the water tank. The effective transmission can have different dose components, such as transmitted photons through the collimators as well as in-phantom scatter, that can lead to dose addition beneath the collimators, which should increase with FS. For larger fields, a wider penumbra implies more scatter out of the field, which is defined by the position of collimation devices.

In Table 5, the contamination electrons have an increasing contribution to the surface dose up to build-up as the FS increases. This is expected since a larger volume of air is exposed to the X-ray beam, leading to higher contributions of electron contamination. Its relative contribution was modeled between 0.05 and one percent for large fields.

The single photon source model has implications requiring more elaborate fluence modeling to account for scatter effects. This would entail more commissioning of the model against water tank data for various FSs. It is, however, a task that has to be performed once and then used in other dosimetry applications for the linear accelerator under question. Since the beam model can model the photon beam accurately in relative dose data, it must also be adjusted with measured output factor data to convert to absolute dose. This again can be measured and calculated with standard methods.

CONCLUSION

In this study, a beam model was developed for MC simulation of an ElektaTM Precise linac. The model has been benchmarked against measurements in water and with Gafchromic EBT3 film for regular, rectangular, off-set fields and irregular field apertures. For the regular fields, the largest γ-index was seen for the 40 cm × 40 cm field, but the criteria were still met. Good results were obtained in the penumbra area, which shows the effectiveness of the error function used in the beam model. The effect of the flattening filter can also be seen in the larger fields like the 40 cm × 40 cm.

The source model parameter values established from square field PDD and profiles could reproduce similar data for rectangular fields. A γ-index evaluation criterion of 2%/2 mm was used for all comparisons. For the offset fields, good results were obtained for the crossline as well as the inline profiles and show the model’s robustness.

As an extension to the beam model capability, dose distributions were measured and compared with MC simulations for irregular MLC-shaped fields. The three cases discussed in the study, namely the triangle, arrow, and mirror, showed that a fixed set of penumbra modeling parameters and transmission levels can yield accurate results compared with film dosimetry. The goal was to have a few variable source parameters for a range of fields for a specific energy, to keep the source model simple, and to restrict it to a single point source for photons and contamination electrons.

This study showed that a linac can be accurately modeled by using a beam model derived from mathematical equations. The study shows that a beam characterization model can be used as an alternative to the extensive simulations needed to score beam parameters into a PSF for PSFs.[29] Since good agreement was found for all field types and MLC-shaped fields, the model should be able to accurately model dose distributions in a clinical set.
