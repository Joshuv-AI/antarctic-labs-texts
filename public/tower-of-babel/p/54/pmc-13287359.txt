Climate Change and the Switch from Pressurised Metered-Dose Inhalers to Dry Powder Inhalers: A Proportionate Balancing of Risk and Reward?
Authors: Sanjeeva Dissanayake, Jason Suggett, Alan Kaplan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urgent action is required to limit the increase in global temperatures. While mitigation efforts are primarily directed at transitioning away from fossil fuels, given their overwhelming contribution to greenhouse gas emissions, infrastructural change is slow to effect. Thus, behavioural changes, which can be effected rapidly, are also critical. To limit healthcare-related emissions, a widespread transition away from pressurised metered-dose inhalers (pMDIs), containing hydrofluorocarbons with high global warming potential (GWP), to dry powder inhalers (DPIs) with minimal GWP, has been proposed. This paper discusses whether the stated grounds for this transition are robust and are proportionate to the potential environmental gain and, while not to be ignored, whether a greater emphasis should be placed upon other measures that may have a greater impact.

Key Summary Points

To reduce healthcare-related emissions, switching from pressurised metered-dose inhalers (pMDIs) to dry powder inhalers (DPIs) has been advocated in the UK. However, DPI switching is a “low benefit” intervention, its environmental benefits are often overstated, and the requirements to safely transition a large population to new devices are frequently a neglected consideration in the literature.More fruitful targets, via policy enactment and public education, would be the acceleration of current energy decarbonisation efforts and modification of our travel behaviour and dietary habits.This paper thus questions the utility of large-scale DPI switching given the potential environmental gain, the prevailing primary care environment in the UK, and the advent of low global warming potential (LGWP) pMDIs, It suggests that appropriately funded efforts to improve clinical practice and establish a comprehensive inhaler disposal system would both be a more proportionate response and provide more durable and predictable benefit.

Introduction

Anthropogenic global warming, principally via the production of greenhouse gases (GHGs), has caused global surface temperatures to rise by over 1 °C since 1850. While the United Nations’ (UN) stated goal is to limit the temperature increase to between 1.5 and 2 °C by 2100, national policies actually implemented by 2020 are projected to result in warming by 3.2 °C in that timeframe [1]. In view of the increasing frequency of extreme weather events and ecosystem breakdown that is already apparent, the urgency of the situation now upon us is clear.

As with other industrial sectors, the healthcare sector, accounting for approximately 4–5% of global GHG emissions [2], must address the challenge of global warming. In some countries, such as the UK and USA, this has resulted in a keen focus inter alia upon emissions from the use of pressurised metered-dose inhalers (pMDIs) and proposals to transition patients with asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) to dry powder inhalers (DPIs) [3–5]. While it is irrefutable that pMDI use incurs a significant emissions cost, given the implications of switching of large patient populations from one inhaler type to another, it is important that the evidence to support such proposals withstand robust scrutiny.

To facilitate a rigorous critique of this issue, we examined the UK as a case study with high pMDI usage for which substantial emissions data are available, and on which a number of key publications have focused in recent years. We hypothesised that the strongest case for switching should arise from countries such as the UK where pMDI use is most prevalent.

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

pMDI Emissions and the Overall GHG Burden in the UK

In the UK, almost 10 million individuals are diagnosed with asthma or COPD [6]. Over 54 million pMDIs are prescribed annually, representing over three-quarters of all inhalers used [7] and accounting for approximately 3–4% of National Health Service (NHS) emissions [3, 7]. In view of the latter, a transition to DPIs has been recommended at both the UK parliamentary and advisory institutional levels [4, 8], with the core rationale for this proposal being the emissions reduction that might be achieved, although other assertions to support the transition, such as the greater efficacy of DPIs, have been advanced [4].

To better contextualise the emissions burden arising from pMDI use, it is useful to understand the wider context as a starting point. In the UK, annual GHG emissions equate to 406 million tonnes of carbon dioxide-equivalents (MtCO2-e). This emissions burden is derived from CO2 (79%), methane (13%), nitrous oxide (5%), and fluorinated gases (F-gases, 3%) which include hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs) [9]. The great majority of F-gases are derived from refrigeration and air-conditioning, with HFCs from pMDIs in the UK contributing approximately 0.2% (assuming 700 kilotonnes CO2-e) [9–12] of the UK’s annual GHG emissions.

Given that three-quarters of GHGs arise from fossil fuel use [13], the UN’s current and proposed mitigation pathways for limiting global warming primarily prioritise a shift away from fossil fuels to renewable energy [1]. Nonetheless, while continued decarbonisation of the energy grid will deliver further substantial reductions in emissions, including for the NHS, the timescale for infrastructural change is decadal and may be subject to political upheaval. As such, behavioural change at an individual level which can be effected rapidly, such as the proposed device transition, is important. To that end, the four highest-impact lifestyle changes identified for developed nations (each reducing an individual’s annual GHG emissions by ≥ 0.8 tonnes CO2-equivalents [tCO2-e]) are as follows: having one fewer child, living car-free (2.4 tCO2-e saved per year—domestic transport produces 29% of the UK’s total emissions), avoiding air travel (1.6 tCO2-e saved per return transatlantic flight), and eating a plant-based diet (0.8 tCO2-e per year [14]—without dietary modification, the UN’s 2 °C target is considered unattainable [15]).

The Reduction in Emissions Burden by Switching from pMDIs to DPIs

It has been suggested that switching from pMDIs to DPIs could effect similar emissions savings to a plant-based diet [10]. In a widely cited paper using GSK inhaler data, the potential emissions reduction from switching a patient from a (Seretide) pMDI maintenance inhaler and separate (Ventolin) pMDI reliever to two corresponding DPIs was estimated at 422 kg CO2-e (kgCO2-e) [10]. This estimate assumes that a patient uses 12 maintenance and 12 reliever inhalers per annum—implying 100% maintenance adherence and extreme reliever overuse, a pattern rarely, if ever, seen in clinical practice. Although salbutamol overuse is common, the average salbutamol intake is typically 3–4 inhalers per year [16–18], with maintenance usage typically averaging 3–6 inhalers per annum [19–21]. As such, the average annual emissions reduction per patient resulting from the above DPI switch would more realistically be between 141 and 226 kgCO2-e (0.14–0.23 tCO2-e)—a fraction of the savings achievable by transitioning to a plant-based diet from a high (1.23 tCO2-e) or medium meat diet (0.66 tCO2-e) [14, 22].

From a patient perspective, it is useful to contextualise the emissions associated with inhalers in simple, meaningfully illustrative terms to inform decision-making. It is arguable whether the Decision Aid on “Asthma inhalers and climate change”, published by the UK’s National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), which broadly advocates preferential DPI use based on their low GHG emissions, achieves this end. It includes a prominent infographic in which the emissions of both pMDIs and DPIs are contrasted with car journeys of varying lengths [8]. Notably, Flutiform and Symbicort pMDIs (both containing hydrofluoroalkane 227ea [HFA227ea], which has approximately 2.5 times the global warming potential [GWP] of HFA134a) are associated with GHG emissions equivalent to a 115-mile car journey (35 kgCO2-e)—while for DPIs or soft-mist inhalers (SMIs), GHG emissions are minimal (< 1 kg CO2-e). Frames of reference that are lacking are that Flutiform and Symbicort pMDIs are used for 30 days (assuming 100% adherence), while the other (HFA134a) salbutamol or preventer pMDIs also referenced can last for up to 3 months or more depending upon the regularity of their use or the prescribed dose; and that HFA227ea-containing inhalers such as Flutiform and Symbicort represent only 2% of pMDIs used in the UK [7]. Given that many patients may only superficially peruse the Decision Aid, and lack the knowledge base of the present readership, the headline comparison with a single car journey may therefore be less than ideally informative. More instructive are the comparisons within the 2018 report from the UN’s Medical and Chemical Technical Options Committee (MCTOC), which compare the emissions from a two-puff inhaler dose with everyday items such as bread, a fizzy drink or orange juice, a burger, or an average UK car commute (Fig. 1) [23]. Environmental Implications of Suboptimal Disease Control

Asthma and COPD management has long been directed at reducing symptoms and exacerbation risk [24, 25], which in turn reduces associated healthcare costs. More recently, there has been a focus on the increased emissions burden arising from poorly controlled disease [26–29]. Emissions are approximately two- to three-fold higher in uncontrolled versus controlled asthma [12, 28], with over 60% of asthma-related emissions in the UK stemming from short-acting β2-agonist (SABA) use. [28]. The continuing widespread overuse of SABAs in asthma [18] is particularly troubling, as the clinical risks associated with unopposed SABA overuse, including an increased risk of death, are well described [30–32]. In COPD, GHG emissions are seven-fold higher in frequent exacerbators versus patients without exacerbations, with the majority of GHGs arising from in-patient hospitalisation [26].

Given the link between disease control and emissions, it is relevant that the putative GHG savings from DPI switching detailed in the Janson et al. analysis [10] and elsewhere [11, 33] are predicated on the emissions life cycle of the inhaler alone. The corollary to a predominant focus on the device is that the requirements for safely transitioning patients to new devices are, in many instances in the literature, a neglected or secondary consideration. This has filtered through to national media [34, 35] and contrasts with the more balanced approaches evident in treatment guidelines [36–38]. Key to any successful device transition is patient engagement, adequate training on any new device, and careful follow-up. Regrettably, healthcare professionals’ knowledge of correct inhaler use is frequently poor [39, 40], while patients commonly receive little or no initial instruction or follow-up on correct inhaler technique [41]. This is problematic where, for example, the transition from a salbutamol pMDI to a DPI would require the use of a salbutamol Easyhaler, Novolizer, or Accuhaler, or terbutaline Turbuhaler—all reliever DPIs with distinct operational characteristics. Further, the greater the number of devices patients use, the greater the preponderance of handling errors [42], which may be reflected in poorer disease control and increased exacerbation risk [43]. Involuntary or “forced” switches may further exacerbate the situation, as they are commonly associated with worsening of asthma control and reduced medication adherence/persistence and can erode the patient-clinician relationship [44]. Although patients could theoretically use identical maintenance and reliever DPI devices, such as the Accuhaler, or a single maintenance and reliever Turbuhaler or NEXThaler device to ease a DPI transition, in reality device selection will be based on variables including the desired drug(s) and device combination, treatment response, patient acceptance, and local prescribing policy coupled with cost. A successful, widespread transition from pMDIs to DPIs would thus require a significant improvement in healthcare professionals’ competence as to the operation of a variety of maintenance and reliever DPI devices and a change in their behaviour in terms of educating and following up patients switched to a new device. In the absence of such, it is reasonable to surmise that the prevalence of poor disease control may increase during any overly rapid DPI transition. The latter may be problematic since the majority of asthma- and COPD-related emissions arise from SABA use and healthcare resource utilisation [26–28], thus potentially diminishing putative emissions savings from DPI switching. A potential increase in the prevalence of uncontrolled disease is also of concern from a health economic perspective given that health-related impacts and productivity losses account for three-quarters of the total overall cost attributable to asthma in the UK (estimated at £5.2B in 2022) [12].

Other Environmental and Clinical Considerations

It is also relevant that while the environmental debate around inhalers has centred on their emissions potential, reasonably so in view of global warming projections, multiple other environmental effects may ensue from their use. A comprehensive analysis of the environmental sequelae of inhalers revealed that, although the life-cycle GWP of HFA134a- and HFA227ea-based pMDIs greatly exceeds that of DPIs, for 8 of 13 other environmental effect categories, DPIs incur the greatest burden—in particular for marine eutrophication (blooms of algae leading ultimately to oxygen depletion and harm to underwater life), fossil resource depletion, and photooxidant formation with human and ecosystem toxicity [7]. Importantly, this analysis also assessed HFA152a, one of two new propellants with low GWP (LGWP) which are now being incorporated in multiple pMDI reformulations. (Note that the entirety of the preceding emissions discussion is focused upon the long-established propellants HFA134a and HFA227ea). The GWP of an HFA152a-based pMDI is approximately 92% and 97% lower than those of HFA134a and HFA227ea inhalers, respectively, and thus comparable to that for a DPI [7]. Further, DPIs have greater effects on 12 of 13 other environmental indices compared to HFA152a [7]. The study did not include an analysis of the other LGWP propellant, HFO1234ze. However, the GWP of HFO1234ze is approximately one-thousandth that of HFA134a [45], and as such, HFO1234ze formulations also have similar GWP to DPIs. Note that the first approvals for an LGWP pMDI (HFO1234ze-based budesonide/glycopyrronium/formoterol) were recently granted by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) and the UK’s Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) [46, 47]. It will be of interest to observe how the authorisation of the first LGWP pMDI is addressed in the NICE Decision Aid on “Asthma inhalers and climate change”, although budesonide/glycopyrronium/formoterol is currently only approved for use in COPD, since with the establishment of a development and regulatory pathway it is anticipated that other pMDI reformulation approvals will follow. The first LGWP pMDI approval beyond Europe is still awaited.

Aside from environmental concerns pertaining to a pMDI-to-DPI switch, the relative efficacy of these device types has also been invoked to support a DPI transition. The 2018 report from the UK parliament which recommended an NHS prescription target of at least 50% LGWP inhalers (then essentially DPIs) by 2022 inter alia included a statement that “in clinical practice you get… better symptom control, less exacerbations of disease [with DPIs than pMDIs]”, attributing this to ease of DPI handling [4]. However, not only are there patient subgroups such as young children [48] and the elderly [49] for whom DPIs may be suboptimal, particularly DPIs with high resistance and/or flow-dependent performance [50], the parliamentary position does not accurately reflect the broader patient literature or global treatment guidelines [24, 25]. Although there are a multitude of device handling trials attesting to different error rates with different device types (including differences between various DPIs), all device types require proper patient training and follow-up evaluation [24, 25]. Randomised controlled trials show no differences in DPI versus pMDI efficacy [51–53], while observational/real-world studies also show no consistent evidence in favour of one device type or the other [54–57]. Further, while it may be tempting to attribute poor respiratory outcomes in the UK to a comparatively higher rate of pMDI use than elsewhere, there is no correlation between the rate of pMDI use and asthma mortality across multiple European countries [58, 59]. Regrettably, poor asthma and COPD outcomes in the UK [60, 61] are consistent with the UK’s poor performance on healthcare outcomes across several major disease areas (such as cancer and cardiovascular disease) and may instead reflect poor clinical practices [61, 62] as well as lower than average per capita healthcare spending, capital investment, and a strikingly lower number of doctors and nurses versus its economic peers [60].

An Alternative Emphasis to Reduce Inhaler-Related Emissions?

Switching patients to DPIs can reduce GHGs but cannot be an isolated strategy, as it should consider the context of a health service in which asthma and COPD management is frequently suboptimal [61–63]. Hence, a rapid, large-scale device switch may increase emissions in patients whose disease becomes uncontrolled as a result of switching [12] and poses ethical questions in patients currently well controlled on pMDIs. Stigmatisation of pMDI use (“green guilt”) [24, 34, 35] may worsen adherence and treatment outcomes. Thus, although a rapid, large-scale device transition may appear an ostensibly attractive solution, it will not address underlying failings in patient care, and hence over-reliance on SABAs, which will continue to undermine clinical and environmental outcomes. Significant funding and time will likely be required to achieve the above since deficiencies in patient care and high levels of uncontrolled disease are long-standing [65, 66] and have proved largely refractory to guideline recommendations and attempted interventions over many years [19, 67]. However, in the absence of such measures, SABA- and healthcare-utilisation-related emissions will remain high, whereas a systemic strategy to limit SABA use and increase maintenance use (i.e., even before appropriate disposal considerations) could reduce inhaler-related GHG emissions by approximately 40% in the UK and Europe [68]. Such interventions not only represent good clinical and environmental practice but also avoid the potential for negative outcomes where excessive target-driven device switching is not appropriately managed and are more likely to provide durable, predictable outcomes.

Conclusion

Alongside industrial and infrastructural change, behavioural modification to mitigate the effects of climate change is critical, as it can be enacted rapidly. However, proposed interventions should elicit meaningful impacts, be proportionate to benefit, and avoid unintended sequelae, while public guidance should advocate consequential behavioural change. It is debatable whether UK parliamentary recommendations to transition patients from pMDIs to DPIs and the NICE Decision Aid achieve these ends. The proposed transition targets a low-impact intervention in a vulnerable subgroup and addresses only about 0.2% of the UK’s total GHG emissions. More fruitful targets, via policy enactment and public education, would be acceleration of current energy decarbonisation efforts and modification of our travel behaviour and dietary habits.

Further, the DPI switch literature frequently overstates potential GHG savings and overlooks the requisite training to transition large patient numbers to new devices and the increased SABA and resource utilisation that could occur in its absence in the context of current healthcare provision. Therefore, although device switching is appropriate for some patients, we consider it reasonable to question the short-term benefit that can be derived from large-scale DPI switching given the advent of LGWP pMDIs, the prevailing primary care environment, and the potential environmental gain, and suggest that long-overdue efforts to improve clinical practice and establish a comprehensive inhaler disposal system would be a more proportionate response. Although our analysis focused on the UK for reasons of data availability, it is likely applicable to other countries where pMDI use is prominent, such as the USA, and in which similar healthcare environment considerations apply.
