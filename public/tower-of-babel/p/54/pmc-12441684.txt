Pulmonary hyalinizing granuloma presenting with mixed imaging features in CT and FDG PET: A case report
Authors: Yoichiro Hijikata, Tomomi W Nobashi, Ryo Sakamoto, Kohei Ikezoe, Tomohiro Handa, Toshi Menju, Shinya Otsuki, Hiroyuki Katsuragawa, Kentaro Tsuji, Yuji Nakamoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 52-year-old man was referred to our hospital for further evaluation after suspicious pulmonary shadows were detected on a routine health checkup. Chest CT revealed multiple nodular and mass-like lesions with mixed characteristics in both lungs, along with interlobular septal thickening and lymphadenopathy. FDG-PET demonstrated variable degrees of uptake across the lesions. A surgical biopsy of a mass in the left lower lobe yielded a pathological diagnosis of pulmonary hyalinizing granuloma (PHG). PHG is an extremely rare benign pulmonary condition of unknown etiology, that can be associated with various diseases. Histologically, the nodules are characterized by dense, eosinophilic hyalinized collagen arranged in concentric lamellae, frequently exhibiting a whorled or layered pattern. Radiologic findings typically include multiple bilateral pulmonary lesions; however, cavitation and calcification are rarely observed and are considered non-specific. Definitive diagnosis requires histological confirmation. In this case, no underlying or associated systemic diseases were identified histologically; however, the presence of interlobular septal thickening was atypical for previously reported PHG findings, raising the suspicion of a coexisting disease with lymphatic involvement. Close clinical and radiological follow-up is warranted to monitor for potential comorbid conditions.Keywords: Pulmonary hyalinizing granuloma, FDG PET, CT, Rare lung disease

Introduction

Pulmonary hyalinizing granuloma (PHG) is an exceptionally rare benign pulmonary disorder that may present with nonspecific clinical symptoms such as cough and fever, or remain entirely asymptomatic [1,2]. Although an autoimmune etiology has been proposed, the underlying pathogenesis remains poorly understood [2].

PHG has been reported in association with a wide spectrum of conditions, including autoimmune, infectious, and neoplastic diseases, highlighting the need for thorough clinical evaluation when PHG is suspected [[3], [4], [5]]. Radiologically, PHG typically manifests as solitary or multiple pulmonary nodules; however, these imaging features can mimic malignant lesions, thereby requiring histopathological analysis for definitive diagnosis [1]. Herein, we present a case of PHG demonstrating diverse findings on both chest CT and FDG PET/CT.

Case presentation

Multiple pulmonary nodules were incidentally detected on a chest X-ray during a routine health check-up of a 52-year-old man, prompting further evaluation by a local physician. As bronchoscopy failed to yield a definitive diagnosis, the patient was referred to our hospital for further assessment. His medical history was unremarkable, although he was an active smoker (22.5 pack-years) at the time of presentation. Routine blood tests showed no significant abnormalities. Among autoantibodies, anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibody was weakly positive, and serum IgG4 was mildly elevated at 166 mg/dL (normal:11-121 mg/dL). Other antibodies, including those associated with vasculitis and connective tissue diseases, were negative. Additionally, infectious disease screening, including tests for tuberculosis and Aspergillus, was negative. Chest imaging revealed multiple nodules distributed in both lungs (Fig. 1). Contrast-enhanced CT exhibited multiple round masses, reticular ground-glass nodules, and irregular dense consolidations with interlobular septal thickening in both lungs. The masses showed relatively homogeneous enhancement without evidence of calcification or cavitation (Fig. 2). FDG-PET/CT demonstrated variable degrees of FDG uptake across the lesions, with relatively higher uptake in the mass-like lesions. Owing to the diversity in imaging findings, none of the lesions demonstrated features strongly suggestive of a primary malignancy.

While differential diagnoses such as infectious diseases (eg, tuberculosis), autoimmune conditions (eg, granulomatosis with polyangiitis) and IgG4-related disease were considered, they were regarded as less likely given the absence of clinical features indicative of such disorders. On the other hand, a neoplastic process, particularly malignant lymphoma, remained a clinical concern. Accordingly, an ultrasound-guided biopsy of a mass in the left lower lobe mass was performed to aid in diagnosis. Histology revealed prominent lymphoplasmacytic infiltration without malignant features, along with fibrotic stromal tissue containing occluded vascular-like structures. Although PHG was included in the differential diagnosis, a definitive conclusion could not be drawn based on the needle biopsy alone; thus, surgical resection of the mass in left lower lobe was undertaken.

Pathological examination revealed thick bundles of hyalinized and degenerated collagen fibers deposited in a nodular, keloid-like pattern, arranged in a characteristic concentric lamellar structure (Fig. 3). Multinucleated giant cells were interspersed throughout the lesion. Lymphoplasmacytic infiltration with lymphoid follicle formation was observed around the nodules, as well as peri-bronchial lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates within the nodular tissue. Scattered blood vessels were occluded by fibrosis and lymphoplasmacytic infiltration. Nodular amyloidosis was ruled out based on negative direct fast scarlet stain. No microorganisms were identified on special stains (periodic acid–Schiff, Gram, Grocott methenamine silver, and Ziehl-Neelsen). Based on these findings, a diagnosis of PHG was established.

No underlying systemic disease was identified from the resected specimen. For the remaining pulmonary lesions, a strategy of regular imaging follow-up was adopted. One year postoperatively, the lesions have remained stable, showing no evidence of either progression or reduction.

Discussion

Pulmonary hyalinizing granuloma (PHG) is an extremely rare condition. Clinical symptoms are typically non-specific, including cough, fever, and shortness of breath; asymptomatic individuals are also frequently observed at the time of diagnosis [1,2].

Radiologically, PHG typically presents as a well-defined mass ranging from 1 to 15 cm in size, often located peripherally or subpleurally. Calcification and cavitation are uncommon. FDG-PET/CT demonstrates increased uptake in approximately 60% of cases, although instances without significant FDG accumulation have also been reported [4,6,7]. Due to the absence of definitive radiological and metabolic features, histopathological confirmation is essential for diagnosis. However, CT-guided needle biopsies often have limited diagnostic accuracy, frequently necessitating surgical biopsy for definitive diagnosis [8]. On histopathological examination, lesions are characterized by a dense network of hyalinized collagen in the center of perivascular lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate, wherein the collagen is deposited in ropy, whorled bundles separated by clear spaces and surrounded by giant cells [9].

In the present case, consistent with previous reports, bilateral multifocal pulmonary lesions were observed, accompanied by FDG uptake and absence of cavitation or calcification. Notably, the surgically resected mass in the left lower lobe demonstrated strong FDG uptake. Histologically, the surgically resected mass in the left lower lobe exhibited prominent lymphoplasmacytic infiltration, indicating high metabolic activity, and providing a plausible explanation for the increased FDG accumulation. Although such prominent lymphoplasmacytic infiltration is not typical of PHG, similar findings have been described in a previously reported case [10]. In that case, malignant lymphoma was considered in the differential diagnosis; however, no monoclonality was detected, as was also true in our case. Given the difficulty in completely excluding lymphoma, they elected to continue careful follow-up to monitor for any lesion growth or emergence of features suggestive of lymphoma.

The etiology of PHG remains uncertain. Yousem et al. [11] proposed several possible mechanisms, including an autoimmune process; persistent antigen-antibody reactions akin to those seen in histoplasmosis; collagen deposition at small infectious foci caused by Histoplasma; and the possibility that PHG represents the end stage of infectious granulomas, autoimmune responses, or "burnt-out" inflammatory granulomas. Liu et al. also reported a case of PHG that developed after initiation of HIV therapy, suggesting a possible association with immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) [12]. Collectively, the most plausible hypothesis at present is that PHG may arise from an exaggerated immune response, although the exact pathogenesis remains unclear. Indeed, PHG has been reported in association with a wide spectrum of conditions, including IgG4-related disease and sarcoidosis, lymphoproliferative disorders like malignant lymphoma, neoplastic diseases such as breast cancer, lung cancer, and Paget's disease, as well as infectious diseases including tuberculosis and fungal infections [4].

Although no underlying disease was identified in our case, the presence of infiltrates and interstitial thickening–which are less common in PHG [6,7]–warrants continued vigilance. Regular follow-up is essential to exclude the possibility of co-existing disorders.

PHG itself typically carries a favorable prognosis and often requires no specific treatment [4,6]. While some reports describe lesion regression with corticosteroid therapy, its efficacy remains unproven. Given the patient’s asymptomatic status and the uncertain benefit of steroid use, we elected to manage the case conservatively with observation alone.

Conclusion

We encountered a case of PHG with prominent lymphoplasmacytic infiltration within the mass, which may have contributed to the observed FDG uptake. In this case of PHG, diverse imaging findings were observed on both CT and FDG PET. Once PHG is diagnosed, careful follow-up is thought to be appropriate to exclude the presence of underlying or coexisting diseases.
