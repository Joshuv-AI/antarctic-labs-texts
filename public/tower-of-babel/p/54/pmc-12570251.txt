Association between sensitivity to thyroid hormone indices and chronic kidney disease in US adults from NHANES 2007–2012
Authors: Yuanyuan Ye, Shiqun Liu, Shuang Shao, Yian Gu, Xuan Liu, Anting Yu, Ming Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThyroid function disorders are prevalent among individuals with chronic kidney disease (CKD). However, the relationship between thyroid hormone sensitivity and CKD remains poorly understood. Our study aims to explore the association between the thyroid hormone sensitivity index and CKD in the U.S. adults.MethodsA total of 5,454 adults (aged ≥20 years) from NHANES (2007–2012) were included. We extracted thyroid hormones and other indicators, and calculated the thyroid-stimulating hormone index (TSHI), thyroid feedback quantile-based index (TFQI), thyrotropin-thyroxine resistance index (TT4RI), and the FT3/FT4 ratio for peripheral sensitivity. Linear and logistic regression models were employed to evaluate the association between thyroid hormone sensitivity indices and CKD.ResultsParticipants with CKD exhibited elevated levels of TSHI, TFQI, and TT4RI, along with lower FT3/FT4 ratios, compared to those without CKD. After adjusting for multiple covariates, FT3/FT4 was negatively associated with the risk of CKD (OR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.72–0.96, p = .02). Both TSHI and TT4RI demonstrated U-shaped associations with CKD, indicating a significant nonlinear correlation. Stratified analysis revealed a decreasing trend in CKD risk with increasing FT3/FT4 levels among female participants and those with diabetes. For TSHI, a nonlinear U-shaped association with CKD was identified in participants with diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia.ConclusionFT3/FT4 levels were strongly associated with CKD risk, highlighting the complex interplay between the thyroid system and CKD. The observed U-shaped associations of TSHI and TT4RI with CKD offer novel insights for the evaluation and potential management of chronic kidney disease.Keywords: CKD, thyroid, sensitivity to thyroid hormone, NHANES

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is defined as kidney damage or dysfunction persisting for more than three months, resulting from various factors that induce structural or functional abnormalities in the kidneys [1]. Approximately 13% of the general population in the United States is affected by CKD, with prevalence rates increasing globally. Some of this rise can be attributed to the hormonal and metabolic effects on kidney function. Thyroid hormones play a crucial role in regulating glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and renal tubular function, while kidney disease, in turn, induces significant alterations in thyroid function [2]. It has been hypothesized that CKD predisposes individuals to thyroid hormone disorders due to non-thyroidal illnesses, malnutrition, inflammation, iodine retention, metabolic acidosis, medications, mineral deficiencies (e.g., selenium), and exposure to dialysis procedures (such as loss of peritoneal effluents) [3]. Although numerous studies have explored the relationship between thyroid dysfunction and declining kidney function, the findings remain inconsistent. Some studies suggest that the prevalence of thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) and/or free thyroxine (FT4) abnormalities increases from 5% to 14% as renal function deteriorates [4], while others report a significant positive association only between free triiodothyronine (FT3) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) [5].

Standard clinical evaluation of thyroid function involves serum measurements of FT3, FT4, and TSH levels. However, due to the complex interactions between FT3, FT4, and TSH, evaluating FT3 or TSH levels alone may be insufficient to comprehensively reflect the regulation of thyroid hormone homeostasis. To address this limitation, we propose using thyroid hormone sensitivity indicators such as Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone Index (TSHI), Thyrotroph Thyroxine Resistance Index (TT4RI), Thyroid Feedback Quantile-based Index (TFQI), and the FT3/FT4 ratio, which offer a more nuanced interpretation of thyroid status [6]. TSHI and TT4RI are calculated based on serum FT4 and TSH concentrations as indicators of central (pituitary) sensitivity to thyroid hormone. Higher values indicate reduced feedback sensitivity in the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis, which may be related to metabolic or inflammatory alterations. TFQI is a recently developed index that integrates population-based FT4 and TSH quantiles to reflect individual bias in feedback regulation. In contrast, the FT3/FT4 ratio reflects the peripheral conversion of FT4 to bioactive FT3 and is considered as an indirect measure of deiodinase activity and peripheral thyroid hormone sensitivity [7]. These indices provide a more comprehensive assessment of thyroid function than isolated measurements of TSH or FT4, as they integrate both central (pituitary) and peripheral (tissue) thyroid sensitivity. TSHI and TT4RI reflect the efficiency of the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid feedback loop, which cannot be captured by measuring TSH alone. TFQI reveals individual variability in thyroid regulation, offering a more precise measure of thyroid feedback sensitivity. The FT3/FT4 ratio reflects peripheral thyroid hormone metabolism, indirectly measuring deiodinase activity, which cannot be directly assessed by FT3 or FT4 levels.

Previous studies have demonstrated that thyroid hormone sensitivity is a reliable predictor of insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, cardiometabolic risk, and glucose-lipid metabolism disorders [8–10]. However, its relationship with CKD remains unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between thyroid hormone sensitivity parameters and the risk of CKD using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) collected between 2007 and 2012.

Materials and methods

Study population

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) adopts a complex, multistage probability sampling design to obtain nationally representative data on the health and nutritional status of the non-institutionalized U.S. civilian population. Participants undergo standardized interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory assessments to capture a broad range of health indicators. The survey protocol was approved by the Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. For the present analysis, we used NHANES data collected between 2007 and 2012. The flowchart detailing the inclusion and exclusion criteria for participant selection is presented in Figure 1. From the total of 30,442 participants in NHANES 2007–2012, exclusions were applied to individuals who met the following criteria: (1) they were under the age of 20 (n = 12,729), (2) they lost data for thyroid parameters (n = 9100), urinary albumin to creatinine ratio results or data to estimate GFR (n = 135) and (3) Patients with a history of cancer disease (n = 810), patients with a history of thyroid disorder (n = 652), patients with a history of taking steroid hormones or currently pregnant (n = 275), patients with a history of taking thyroid hormone level-altering medications (amiodarone, and lithium) (n = 27), missing data for poverty income ratio (n = 605), marital status or education level (n = 5) and one of the following: BMI, HbA1c%, UIC, TP, UA, HDL-c, TBil, potassium, phosphorus, smoking or alcohol drinking status, diagnosis of hypertension or diabetes (n = 650). In the final analysis, 5454 subjects were included in the study.

Assessment of thyroid function

The FT3 was measured with a competitive binding immunoenzymatic assay. The TSH (mIU/L) level was detected by a two-site immunoenzymatic (‘sandwich’) assay, and the FT4 was quantified with the Access Free T4 assay, a two-step enzyme immunoassay. The Tg (ng/mL) levels were assessed by a simultaneous one-step ‘sandwich’ assay, while TgAb (IU/mL) and TPOAb (IU/mL) were assessed by a sequential two-step immunoenzymatic ‘sandwich’ assay [8]. In addition to these conventional thyroid function tests, we calculated several indices of thyroid hormone sensitivity to provide a more comprehensive assessment of thyroid homeostasis. The FT3/FT4 ratio was calculated as FT3 (pg/mL)/FT4 (ng/dL), reflecting the peripheral sensitivity to thyroid hormones. A higher value reflects higher sensitivity. TT4RI was calculated as FT4 (pmol/L)*TSH (mIU/L). TSHI was calculated as ln TSH (mIU/L) + 0.1345*FT4 (pmol/L). For TT4RI and TSHI, the higher the values, the lower the central sensitivity to thyroid hormones. TFQI was calculated as cdf FT4 - (1-cdf TSH), the negative and positive values were regarded as increased and decreased sensitivity to FT4, respectively [7]. These indices were selected because they can identify subtle dysregulations in thyroid hormone action—often undetectable by conventional hormone measurements—particularly in individuals with chronic metabolic or inflammatory conditions. Their application enhances the precision of thyroid function assessment and improves our understanding of its systemic implications.

Definitions of CKD

CKD was characterized by an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 and/or a urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) of 30 mg/g or higher [11,12]. Based on eGFR (stages G1–G5) and proteinuria levels (categories A1–A3), CKD prognosis was classified into four risk tiers: low, moderate, high, and very high [13,14]. The eGFR was derived using the CKD-Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation, incorporating variables such as serum creatinine, age, sex, and race [15,16]. The formula for eGFR calculation was: 141 × min (SCr/κ, 1) α × max (SCr/κ, 1)−1.209 × 0.993Age × 1.018 if female × 1.159 if black. SCr indicates serum creatinine, with κ values of 0.7 (female) and 0.9 (male), and α coefficients of −0.329 (female) and −0.411 (male). The functions min and max represent the smaller or larger value between SCr/κ and 1, respectively [17].

Covariates

The potential covariates included age, sex, Race/ethnicity, education level, the ratio of family income to poverty, marital status, smoking behavior, alcohol consumption, body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), triglycerides, uric acid, HbA1c and hypertension. We categorized education level as ‘College or above’, ‘High School or equivalent’ and ‘Less than high school’. Race/ethnicity classifications comprised four groups: Mexican American, Non-Hispanic Black, Non-Hispanic White, and Other. Marital status was dichotomized into ‘Married or Living with partner’ and ‘Other Status’. BMI was calculated as weight divided by height squared (kg/m2). Poverty status was operationalized through the family income-to-poverty ratio. The smoking behavior was dichotomized into ‘Yes’ and ‘No’ based on the ‘Smoking—Cigarette Use’ section in the NHANES Questionnaire data. Alcohol consumption was classified into ‘Yes’ and ‘No’. We used Urine iodine concentration (UIC, μg/L) to determine iodine conditions in participants and divided it into the following three categories: iodine deficient (UIC <100), normal (100–299) and excessive iodine intake (≥300) [18]. In addition, we also included participants’ medical history, including: hypertension, glucose tolerance categories (diabetes, IFG or IGT and NGT), and hyperlipidemia. Diabetes was identified based on any of the following criteria: self-reported physician diagnosis, current use of antidiabetic medications, hemoglobin A1c ≥ 6.5%, or fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL. IFG/IGT was defined as meeting the following criteria: (i) fasting glucose plasma concentration from 100 mg/dL to 125 mg/dL; (ii) glucose tolerance test with 2-h plasma glucose concentration in a 75 g oral glucose test from 140 mg/dL to 199 mg/dL; or (iii) HbA1c from 5.7 to 6.4%. Hypertension was defined as having a self-reported history of diagnosis, current use of antihypertensive medications, or a systolic and/or diastolic blood pressure measurement of ≥140/90 mmHg. Hyperlipidemia was defined as a self-reported diagnosis, triglyceride levels ≥200 mg/dL, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels <40 mg/dL, or low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels ≥130 mg/dL17.

Statistical analysis

We downloaded the 2007–2008, 2009–2010 and 2011–2012 NHANES data that were pertinent to our current work. In accordance with the analytical guidelines prescribed by the NCHS, we took into account the complex survey design factors involved in NHANES, including weights, clustering, and stratification. The continuous variables were expressed as means (standard errors [SE]). Categorical variables were represented by the unweighted frequencies (weighted percentages). To compare characteristic differences between CKD and non-CKD groups, the weighted chi-squared and a weighted linear regression model were used for categorical and continuous variables, respectively. To examine the multivariable association between the sensitivity to thyroid hormone indices and CKD, three different multiple logistic regression models were built and calculated adjusted odds ratios (ORs), 95% CIs, and P values. No covariates were adjusted for as indicated in Model 1; Sex, age and race were adjusted in Model 2; Sex, age, race, education, ratio of family income to poverty, smoking behavior, alcohol consumption, BMI, triglycerides, uric acid, HbA1c and hypertension were adjusted in Model 3. Furthermore, restricted cubic spline (RCS) models with 3 knots were employed to examine potential non-linear associations between thyroid hormone sensitivity indices and CKD in the overall study population. Furthermore, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by sex, glucose tolerance categories, hypertension and hyperlipidemia. Correlations between FT3/FT4 quartile and CKD were examined in different subgroups and adjusted odds ratios (ORs), 95% CIs, and P values were calculated; In different subgroups, we carried out RCS to explore the non-linear relationship between TSHI and CKD. The p < .05 with effective confidence interval (CI) was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed by the software package R 4.3.1.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the participants with or without chronic kidney disease

This study analyzed data from 5454 participants who were selected through a complex multistage sampling design and recruited by the NHANES program team. The mean age of the participants was 44.18 years old, and 2941 were men. The demographic and clinical characteristics of all these participants were presented in Table 1. Among the 869 participants identified with CKD, 639 (73.5%) had albuminuria, defined as a urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) ≥ 30 mg/g. Compared to the non-CKD group, participants in the CKD group were more likely to be older, female and less educated. The CKD group also had higher rates of hypertension, abnormal glucose metabolism or hyperlipidemia than non-CKD group. Significant differences were observed between the two groups for smoking behavior, alcohol consumption, ratio of family income to poverty, BMI, HbA1c (%), albumin, blood urea nitrogen, triglycerides, uric acid, fasting glucose and eGFR (p < .05). In patients with CKD, FT4, Tg, TSHI, TFQI and TT4RI levels were significantly increased, while FT3 and FT3/FT4 levels were significantly decreased (p < .05). Comparative analysis revealed no statistically significant intergroup differences in UIC levels and marital status.

Associations between sensitivity to thyroid hormone indices and CKD

Logistic regression analysis was used to examine the relationship between sensitivity to thyroid hormone indices and CKD (Table 2). In non-adjusted crude model, our results showed that TSHI, TFQI and TT4RI were positively correlated with the risk of CKD, while FT3/FT4 was negatively correlated with the risk of CKD (p < .05). After controlling for multiple covariates, no significant associations were observed between TSHI, TFQI, or TT4RI and CKD. In contrast, the inverse relationship between FT3/FT4 and CKD incidence remained statistically significant in both models 2 and 3. In addition, every 1 increase in FT3/FT4 was associated with a 17% decrease in the risk of CKD in the multivariable model 3 (OR = 0.83, 95% CI 0.72–0.96, p = .02). The dose-response relationship between the sensitivity to thyroid hormone indices index and CKD was further explored using restricted cubic spline plots, and results were displayed in Figure 2. All analyses were adjusted for sex, age, race, education, ratio of family income to poverty, smoking behavior, alcohol consumption, BMI, triglycerides, uric acid, HbA1c and hypertension. Our results showed that there were nonlinear dose-response relationships between TSHI, TT4RI, FT3/FT4 with CKD, and the curves of TSHI and TT4RI were U-shaped. A significant inverse correlation was observed between FT3/FT4 ratio and CKD risk, where higher ratios correlated with reduced disease probability.

Subgroup analysis

Figure 3 and Figure 4 report the subgroup analyses stratified by sex, glucose tolerance categories, hypertension and hyperlipidemia. To further investigate the correlation between FT3/FT4 and CKD, Figure 3 presents the association of patients divided into quartiles based on FT3/FT4 with CKD risk in different subgroups. FT3/FT4 was divided into four groups, namely, ≤3.625, 3.626–4.111, 4.112–4.602, and >4.602 in Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4, respectively. All analyses were adjusted for multiple covariates. Our results showed that among male participants, CKD risk did not change significantly with the change in FT3/FT4 quartile grouping, while among female participants, Q2 and Q4 groups had significantly decreased risk compared to group Q1, and the OR (95% CI) values were 0.54 (0.35, 0.83) and 0.42 (0.27, 0.67), respectively. In the subgroup analysis stratified by glucose tolerance categories, compared with group Q1, diabetic patients in group Q3 and Q4 had a significant reduction in the risk of CKD, and the OR (95% CI) values were 0.59 (0.36, 0.98) and 0.51 (0.30, 0.87), respectively. No significant association was observed in patients with IFG or IGT. A significant reduction in the risk of CKD was observed in patients with hypertension in Q2 and Q4 compared to Q1, and no significant association was observed in the non-hypertensive group. For patients with hyperlipidemia, the risk of CKD was reduced by 32% in the Q4 group compared with the Q1 group, and the OR (95% CI) value was 0.68 (0.51, 0.90). The interaction term test found significant difference between CKD and sensitivity to thyroid hormone indices among different sex groups (p for interaction <.05), while there were no significant difference among different glucose tolerance categories, hypertension and hyperlipidemia groups (p for interaction >.05).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the relationship between sensitivity to thyroid hormone and the risk of CKD based on the NHANES 2007–2012 data. Our findings indicate that both peripheral and central thyroid hormone resistance indices are closely associated with CKD risk. Elevated FT3/FT4 levels were associated with a reduced risk of CKD. Furthermore, TSHI and TT4RI exhibited a nonlinear relationship with CKD risk, characterized by a U-shaped curve in the restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis. In the subgroup analyze between FT3/FT4 and CKD stratified by sex, glucose tolerance categories, hypertension and hyperlipidemia, we observed the strongest relationship between FT3/FT4 and CKD risk in female participants, participants with diabetes, hypertension, or hyperlipidemia. Additionally, subgroup analysis using interaction terms indicated that sex and thyroid hormone sensitivity interact to influence CKD risk. In contrast, glucose tolerance categories, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia did not significantly modify these associations.

We employed thyroid hormone sensitivity indices as predictive factors for CKD risk in U.S. adults, illustrating the varying strengths of association between central and peripheral sensitivity indices and CKD. By considering resistance to thyroid hormone as a potential contributing factor to kidney disease, our research introduces a novel hypothesis for early screening and treatment of CKD, while also enhancing our understanding of the physiological roles of thyroid hormones. The synthesis and secretion of thyroid hormones are controlled by the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis, where thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) stimulates the pituitary to produce and release thyrotropin (TSH), which in turn forms a negative feedback loop with thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3) [19]. Additionally, hormonal fluctuations can influence the extrathyroidal conversion of T4 to T3, allowing adaptation to various nutritional or disease conditions. In chronic renal insufficiency, iodine accumulation and inflammation may disrupt thyroid hormone synthesis and conversion. Thyroid hormones regulate renal blood flow and the metabolic environment by modulating the renin-angiotensin system, blood volume, and capillary permeability. They also maintain sodium-water balance, fluid homeostasis, and normal blood pressure through the regulation of renal tubular function [20]. Furthermore, TSH receptors are expressed in various tissues, including the kidneys, suggesting that TSH may influence kidney function either directly or through the modulation of other physiological systems [21].

Numerous studies have examined the effects of thyroid hormones on kidney function, but the findings have been inconsistent. A Dutch study found that FT3 levels were positively correlated with eGFR in individuals with normal thyroid function [22]. Similarly, a study based on a U.S. population suggested that FT3 and TT3 levels were negatively correlated with the stage of chronic kidney disease (CKD) [23]. Additionally, a study of elderly individuals in China demonstrated a significant association between renal function and FT3, while FT4 levels within the reference range showed no significant correlation with the prevalence of renal dysfunction in the elderly [24]. However, another Chinese study found no significant link between FT3 and kidney function, but observed that elevated FT4 levels significantly increased the risk of CKD [25]. Furthermore, a Korean study also indicated that higher FT4 levels were associated with a decline in eGFR [26]. Overall, these varied results highlight the need for further investigation into the complex relationship between thyroid hormones and renal dysfunction.

Given the intricate interplay within the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis, combined indicators provide a more comprehensive depiction of the relationship between thyroid hormones and CKD risk compared to individual indices. Previous studies have suggested that the Thyroid Feedback Quantile-based Index (TFQI) better reflects associations with obesity, metabolic syndrome, diabetes, and diabetes-related mortality [27]. Similarly, the FT3/FT4 ratio has been proposed as a reliable marker for detecting subtle changes in thyroid hormone metabolism and has been linked to poor prognosis in certain clinical settings [28]. Consequently, we employed thyroid hormone sensitivity indicators to quantify both central and peripheral thyroid hormone resistance and to explore their association with CKD risk.

Previous studies [29] have indicated that among patients with type 2 diabetes, higher TSHI and TT4RI levels are correlate with greater incidence of renal dysfunction, partially supporting our findings. In NHANES-based research, one study [30] investigating thyroid hormone sensitivity and all-cause mortality in patients with CKD found that higher TSHI and TT4RI levels were positively associated with mortality risk. Another recent study [31] focused specifically on cardiovascular-specific mortality and identified the Parametric Thyroid Feedback Quantile-based Index (PTFQI) as an independent risk factor for both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in the CKD population. In comparison, our study expands the scope by including both individuals with and without CKD, and shifts the focus toward assessing the potential relationships between thyroid hormone sensitivity parameters and the risk of CKD onset, rather than mortality outcomes. A hospital-based study [32] from China involving individuals undergoing routine health checkups reported a negative correlation between TSHI and TT4RI and kidney function among euthyroid individuals, and identified a positive association between the FT3/FT4 ratio and eGFR calculated using the CKD-EPI equation. In our study, we similarly found that FT3/FT4 was significantly inversely associated with CKD risk, consistent with previous research. However, our study further revealed a U-shaped relationship between TSHI and TT4RI and CKD risk, emphasizing the critical importance of maintaining balanced thyroid hormone sensitivity levels. The variation in research findings may be partly attributed to differences in the study populations. In addition, the observed positive associations between TSHI and TT4RI and all-cause mortality in CKD patients from the NHANES-based prognostic study may reflect the fact that all participants in that study were already within the higher-risk segment of the U-shaped curve we identified—beyond the inflection point—where elevated thyroid hormone resistance is more strongly linked to adverse outcomes.

The U-shaped association suggests that both diminished and excessive sensitivity to thyroid hormones may contribute to adverse renal outcomes. Reduced sensitivity may impair the physiological actions of thyroid hormones on renal hemodynamics, glomerular filtration, and tubular function. Conversely, heightened sensitivity may result in overstimulation of renal metabolic pathways, increased oxidative stress, or maladaptive activation of the renin - angiotensin - aldosterone system. These findings underscore the importance of maintaining thyroid hormone sensitivity within an optimal range to preserve renal function.

These findings carry potential implications for clinical practice. First, thyroid hormone sensitivity indices, such as FT3/FT4, TSHI, and TT4RI, may serve as valuable supplementary markers for stratifying CKD risk, particularly in individuals with euthyroid status but underlying metabolic disturbances. Second, the observed nonlinear associations suggest that both reduced and excessive thyroid hormone action may be detrimental to renal function, underscoring the importance of individualized assessment and dynamic management of thyroid status in patients at risk of CKD. Finally, these indices may aid in guiding more personalized screening and preventive strategies for CKD, potentially enabling earlier intervention even among high-risk individuals without overt thyroid dysfunction.

However, our research has several limitations worthy of consideration. Firstly, as with all cross-sectional analyses, temporal relationships cannot be established, and causality between thyroid hormone sensitivity and CKD risk cannot be inferred. Although we identified a significant association between thyroid hormone sensitivity indices—particularly the FT3/FT4 ratio—and CKD, it remains unclear whether altered thyroid hormone sensitivity contributes to the progression of CKD. Longitudinal studies or randomized controlled trials are necessary to better clarify causal associations. Future studies should investigate the longitudinal relationship between thyroid hormone sensitivity and renal outcomes, exploring specific mechanisms such as inflammation or endothelial dysfunction to better understand the underlying biological processes. Secondly, the reliance on a single thyroid hormone measurement limits our understanding of the impact of thyroid hormone fluctuations over time, as these may not accurately reflect longitudinal changes in thyroid function. Additionally, despite adjusting for multiple covariates, potential residual biases or confounding factors, such as medication effects or dietary factors not assessed in this study, may still persist and influence the results. In particular, proteinuria is common in CKD and may lead to urinary loss of thyroid hormone-binding proteins (e.g., thyroxine-binding globulin, albumin, prealbumin, and transthyretin), especially in nephrotic syndrome. This could lower both total and free thyroid hormone levels, potentially interfering with the accuracy of thyroid hormone sensitivity indices and affecting their association with CKD risk [33]. Thirdly, since our study sample was drawn from an American population, it remains unclear whether these findings can be generalized to other countries or ethnic groups. Due to the complexities of thyroid development in children, we classified participants aged 20 and older and excluded pediatric samples. As household income and marital status data were obtained through questionnaires, recall bias may have affected the accuracy of these self-reported variables.

Finally, while our findings suggest that altered thyroid hormone sensitivity may be associated with kidney health, it is premature to recommend thyroid hormone supplementation as a treatment strategy. Further research is warranted to determine which subgroups may benefit, explore underlying mechanisms, and evaluate the safety and efficacy of targeted hormonal interventions—such as thyroid hormone replacement—in slowing CKD progression, particularly among individuals with subclinical thyroid dysfunction.

Conclusion

FT3/FT4 levels were strongly associated with CKD risk, highlighting the complex interplay between the thyroid system and chronic kidney disease. The observed U-shaped associations of TSHI and TT4RI with CKD offer novel insights for the evaluation and potential management of chronic kidney disease. To validate and deepen the understanding of causality in these relationships, future studies should utilize longitudinal or experimental designs, with larger populations and longer follow-up periods.
