Design of orthogonal and portable RNA devices for post-transcriptional regulation by reverse engineering type I toxin-antitoxin systems
Authors: Guobin Yin, Luyao Zhang, Di Zhou, Siyan Zhou, Ruirui Xu, Guocheng Du, Jian Chen, Zhen Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractType I toxin-antitoxin (TA) systems are widely distributed in the prokaryotic world, known for their antitoxin RNA interfering with the cognate toxin mRNA translation. Beyond their canonical role in plasmid maintenance, these systems represent evolutionarily optimized regulatory modules with rapid kinetics and modular architectures. Here, we repurposed them as post-transcriptional regulatory RNA devices via reverse engineering. After isolating the core of type I TA pairs and demonstrating the independence between their structure and repression function, we reconstructed artificial TA pairs termed SRTS-OPRTS. A platform for generating orthogonal SRTS-OPRTS pairs with cross-species application (Bacillus subtilis, Escherichia coli, and Corynebacterium glutamicum) was developed by introducing structure and energy constraints. As an individual expressing element or a co-expressing 3′ UTR tag within specific mRNA, SRTS achieved quantitative regulation of the gene with 3′ UTR cognate OPRTS. Such portability enabled convenient construction of dynamic mutually inhibitory switches, where genes tagged by SRTS and OPRTS could regulate each other. Leveraging this approach, a selective lethal system was further constructed to enrich high-fluorescent mutants, resulting in up to 11.32-fold enhancement in mean fluorescence intensity. Overall, these synthetic RNA devices provide portable tools for gene regulation and offer a robust foundation for constructing dynamic genetic circuits.

Introduction

The toxin-antitoxin (TA) systems are widespread genetic elements found in bacteria and archaea, playing crucial roles in various cellular processes. Since their first discovery in 1983, TA systems have been reported to be involved in the ‘postsegregational killing’ (PSK) effect in plasmid addiction [1], programmed cell death such as abortive infection systems [2], and stress resistance [3]. These systems are typically genetic loci consisting of a stable toxin and its cognate antitoxin, generally categorized into eight types (Types I-VIII) according to their distinct neutralization mechanisms [4]. Among all TA systems, most toxins are proteins, except type VIII, which is RNA, while antitoxins can be either RNA molecules (as in types I, III, and VIII) or proteins (as in other types). To date, 536 TA loci have been experimentally characterized, among which approximately 20% are classified as type I TA systems [5]. Primarily, these antitoxins function as antisense RNAs that suppress lethal toxin expression through a complementary base-pairing mechanism, either by sequestering the toxin’s ribosome binding site (RBS) [6] or by forming RNA duplexes that are subsequently degraded by RNase III [7, 8]. Once the target toxin mRNA is degraded or its RBS is blocked, the toxin translation process will be prevented. The presence of these toxins renders RNase III essential in Bacillus subtilis, highlighting the critical role of RNase III-mediated decay in specifically degrading toxin mRNAs [9, 10].

Initially recognized as plasmid maintenance modules, TA system toxins have also been repurposed as positive selection markers for DNA insertion. Recombinants will be eliminated whose plasmids fail to inactivate the toxin through insertion of a DNA fragment into the toxin’s open reading frame (ORF) [11], or fail to activate the antitoxin by integrating the antitoxin ORF [12]. Moreover, the biological significance of TA systems extends beyond their role in maintaining mobile genetic elements through the PSK mechanism [13]. Due to their distinct regulation modes, a post-transcriptional regulation tool has been engineered to perform significant gene silencing based on type I TA systems [14], in which the 3′ UTR (untranslated region) of toxin encoding gene bsrG is grafted onto the target mRNA (termed operating region, OPR) and the cognate antitoxin SR4 is controllably overexpressed. As an RNA interference (RNAi) tool, the reversible and quantitative knockdown ability makes it suitable for investigating phenotypic effects of essential genes, providing an alternative to CRISPRi approaches that require additional dCas9 expression. [15–17]. However, repurposing more type I TA systems as RNAi toolboxes remains challenging due to the scarcity of well-studied type I TA systems and potential crosstalk arising from their high sequence homology [14]. Especially, the RNase-dependent degradation mechanism requires the conserved sequences for RNase recognition and cleavage, leading to high sequence similarity and severe crosstalk among these riboregulators.

Over the past decades, the extensive flexibility in RNA design architecture has facilitated the exponential expansion of RNA-based regulatory elements in synthetic biology. These advances encompass diverse functional modules, including trans-acting non-coding small RNAs for direct gene regulation [18, 19, 20], stimulus-responsive RNA switches capable of detecting specific RNA sequences or small molecular ligands [21–23], and elementary RNA building blocks for integrating complex biological circuits [24–26]. The functionality of these RNA entities relies on their specific sequence and precise conformation. For instance, the classic small regulatory RNA (sRNA) is constructed by adding a complementary sequence of the first 24 nt of the target gene coding sequence to an sRNA scaffold which recruits the RNA chaperone Hfq, finally achieving downregulation of the target gene through ribosome blocking [27, 28]. Beyond simple sRNA-mediated repression, RNA regulatory switches exhibit sophisticated sensing capabilities for specific ligands or sequences. These riboregulators can either recognize specific ligands via a dedicated binding domain or detect target sequences through precise complementary base pairing, both of which trigger conformational transitions that modulate gene expression states (ON/OFF). In the case of riboswitches designed for ligand-induced regulation, these typical architectures consist of an aptamer domain containing a specific ligand-binding pocket coupled to an expression platform. Upon ligand binding, the resulting conformational rearrangement in the aptamer domain directly influences regulatory activity within the expression platform [29, 30]. Building on these ligand-induced regulation strategies, some aptamers are integrated into sgRNA to modulate its functional conformation, enabling precise control over CRISPRi activity or gene editing processes [31, 32]. On the other hand, the sequence-induced switches consist of similar expression platforms and corresponding trigger RNAs that modulate the ON/OFF states of these platforms. For example, a well-designed trigger RNA that base-pairs with the aptamer sequence of natural riboswitches can also counteract the premature transcription termination without ligand binding via forming an anti-terminator stem loop [33]. Similarly, the small transcription activating RNAs (STARs) are designed to invade the stem of the pre-set terminator ahead target gene, in order to achieve anti-termination and gene activation [34].

Although conventional RNA-based regulatory tools predominantly rely on naturally occurring RNA scaffolds, the rational design principle exemplified by toehold switches represents a paradigm shift towards de novo designed RNA devices [26]. Similar to aforementioned switches, these toehold switches typically consist of a trigger RNA and related cis-repressed expression platform, where the ribosome binding site (RBS) of the target gene is sequestered within a hairpin with a single-stranded toehold domain. Upon the trigger RNA binding to the toehold domain, strand displacement occurs and disrupts the hairpin and liberates the RBS to allow translation initiation. In particular, this de novo design strategy of toehold switches harnesses fundamental thermodynamic principles and strand displacement reactions, not only offering remarkable sequence flexibility but also maintaining superior performance with enhanced orthogonality and broadened dynamic regulation range. Unlike wild-type RNA elements with context-dependent behavior and sequence constraints, toehold switches with extensive sequence space enable diversified engineering for gene regulation, molecular detection, and RNA computing [35]. These riboregulator engineering strategies emphasize the optimization of sequence specificity, cross-species functionality, and standardized modularity.

Here, our aim is to design cross-species and portable RNA devices to orthogonally modulate gene expression. Inspired by the critical role of RNase III in cleaving the RNA duplex formed between antitoxin RNA and toxin mRNA [8, 36, 37], we initially extracted the conserved RNase III recognition motif and demonstrated the structure independence for its recognition and cleavage. Then, the natural antitoxin RNA and cognate toxin mRNA were redesigned in pairs, termed SRTS and OPRTS, respectively, where SRTS could be individually expressed or co-transcribed with other genes as a single transcript to downregulate the OPRTS-tagged genes in a dynamic range. To broaden its potential applications, we further developed an automatic online platform for generating orthogonal SRTS-OPRTS pairs with structural and thermodynamic constraints. Meanwhile, these artificial RNA devices achieved gene downregulation not only in Bacillus subtilis but also in Escherichia coli and Corynebacterium glutamicum with co-expressed B. subtilis RNase III. Furthermore, these plug-and-play RNA devices showed robust performance in the construction of dynamic mutually inhibitory switches, and a selective lethal system was further constructed to enrich cells with high fluorescence intensity from a mutant library. In summary, we anticipate these standardized artificial RNA devices will be portable tools for post-transcriptional gene regulation and the construction of dynamic genetic circuits.

Material and methods

Materials

All analytical grade chemicals and reagents were obtained from commercial sources. Standard laboratory reagents were supplied by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Molecular biology grade reagents, including plasmid mini-prep kits and DNA gel extraction/purification systems, were acquired from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. (MA, USA). DNA modification enzymes comprising high-fidelity PrimeSTAR MAX DNA polymerase and conventional Taq polymerase were sourced from Takara Bio (Beijing, China). T5 exonuclease was procured from New England Biolabs (MA, USA). Custom-designed oligonucleotide primers were commercially synthesized by Genewiz (Suzhou, China).

Strains and plasmids

All the plasmid construction, amplification, and stock were conducted in E. coli JM109. The strain and plasmids used in this study are provided in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3 in detail. To avoid the effects of endogenous SR4/BsrG and SR5/BsrE, these cassettes in B. subtilis 168 were initially knocked out via the CRISPR/Cpf1 system [15].

The plasmids containing SR or OPR modules in this study originated from pTargetF, pACYCDuet-1, pECX99E, pXMJ19, pHT01-Pspove-gfp, and pCOLADuet-T7-gfp [27, 38]. Using such templates, general strategies of the insertion of SR and OPR were performed as follows. Briefly, linearized template plasmids were amplified via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with a pair of primers automatically designed by the PrimerDesign webapp (https://www.kangzlab.cn/PrimerDesign), whose overlap contained the short SR or OPR sequences as a homologous sequence in 3′ ends, and the PCR products were self-cyclized by endogenous DNA recombinase after being transformed into E. coli JM109.

The fragments of bsrG and promoter series of PbltD, Pxpac, PlytE, PyabR, and PysdB were amplified by PCR using B. subtilis genomic DNA as templates. The resulting promoter segments were cloned into pHT01-gfp-OPRTS6 vectors for later transcription strength evaluation. Based on these promoter-switched plasmids, the bsrG fragment was further cloned to replace the gfp sequence by the T5-exonuclease DNA assembly (TEDA) method to create the toxin expression plasmids [39]. The B. subtilis rnc gene was cloned to pTargetF and pXMJ19 in the same way to construct cross-species regulatory plasmids. The plasmids constructed by the TEDA method were assessed by colony PCR and further confirmed by Sanger sequencing in Genewiz (Suzhou, China). The promoter library of mCherry-SRTS6 variants of different transcription rates was constructed by mutagenic PCR in its promoter region, which was derived from a broad-spectrum promoter [40]. Briefly, random mutations were introduced into this promoter by PCR amplification using mutagenic primers Pmutant-F/R containing degenerate nucleotides at specific positions. The Pmutant-F (5′-ttgagcaactggatccNNNNNNNNNNNNNTTGACANNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNTATAATNNNNNNNAAGGAGGAAAAAATGGTGAGCAAGG-3′) sequence spanned the promoter region and its flanking sequence, with degenerate nucleotides strategically placed at designated mutation sites to generate sequence diversity. And the Pmutant-R (5′-ggatccagttgctcaaAAAAATCTCGGTCAGATGTTACTA-3′) was designed to anneal to the upstream flanking region of the promoter, generating PCR products with terminal homologous sequences (Underline sequence) that facilitated self-ligation upon transformation into competent E. coli cells. Instead of spreading on agar plates, competent E. coli cells were transferred to Luria–Bertani (LB) medium containing 100 mg/L ampicillin after transformation with the PCR products processed via the TEDA method. These cells were harvested after 16 h of cultivation for plasmid extraction to obtain the mutant promoter library, which was then transformed into B. subtilis Δsr4-bsrG Δsr5-bsrE to generate the final mCherry-SRTS6 library for subsequent experiments. The diversity of bases at different mutant positions was investigated via next-generation sequencing. And the recombinant plasmid sequences were confirmed in Genewiz Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China) by Sanger sequencing. These plasmids were respectively transformed to E. coli, B. subtilis Δsr4-bsrG Δsr5-bsrE, and C. glutamicum following previous reported methods [41, 42].

Analysis of type I TA systems

The mentioned type I TA systems were collected from TADB 3.0, and only experimentally validated TA loci were analyzed [5]. The toxin mRNA and antitoxin RNA sequences were gathered from T1TAdb [43] and listed in Supplementary Table S1. The interaction of toxin mRNA and antitoxin RNA was predicted via Python package ViennaRNA 2.6.4 [44].

Knockout of endogenous SR4/BsrG and SR5/BsrE cassettes

Gene editing of SR4/BsrG and SR5/BsrE in B. subtilis was performed using the CRISPR/Cpf1 method [15]. Briefly, the pHT-XCR6 plasmid harbouring Francisella novicida U112 Cpf1 was initially transformed into B. subtilis. The target sequences for crRNA design were selected by CHOPCHOP [45]. The crRNAs and corresponding homologous arms, which were the flank sequences of SR4/BsrG or SR5/BsrE cassettes in the B. subtilis genome, were inserted into the pcrF11 plasmid to create pcrF11-sr4-bsrG and pcrF11-sr5-bsrE, respectively. Subsequently, SR4/BsrG knockout plasmid pcrF11-sr4-bsrG was transformed into B. subtilis carrying pHT-XCR6 and inoculated into LB medium containing 5 mg/L chloramphenicol, 50 mg/L kanamycin and 3% (w/v) xylose. Following 12 h of xylose-induced Cpf1 expression, the culture was spread on agar plates with the same antibiotics, and the positive colonies were later selected by colony PCR and validated by Sanger sequencing. The SR4/BsrG knockout strain was further inoculated in LB medium with 0.005% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) for plasmid curing. Similarly, the SR5/BsrE cassette was then knocked out with plasmid pcrF11-sr5-bsrE, yielding the B. subtilis Δsr4-bsrG Δsr5-bsrE strain after completed plasmid curing.

Fluorescence intensity assay

Toxin-mediated survival assay

For toxicity analysis, 1 μg of plasmids harbouring toxin-OPRTS under the control of different promoters were individually transformed into 300 μl competent cells of B. subtilis 168 Δsr4-bsrG Δsr5-bsrE. In parallel, 1 μg of plasmids containing the same promoter series with gfp-OPRTS served as the control group. The transformed cells were spread onto LB agar plates supplemented with chloramphenicol (5 μg/mL) and incubated at 37°C for 18 h. To test the ability of SRTS neutralization of bsrG-OPRTS, we transformed these bsrG-OPRTS plasmids series into competent cells already harbouring pP43NMK-P43-SRTS or pP43NMK-PxylA-SRTS under the same conditions. In case of the inducible PxylA, 2% w/v xylose was added to the LB agar plates to induce SRTS6 expression. The plates were photographed by the GelDoC Go imaging System (BIO-RAD).

Flow cytometry analysis of mCherry-SRTS libraries

Flow cytometry was conducted on BD FACSAria III with BD FACSDiva software (BD Biosciences) to analyze the distribution of fluorescence intensity in the mCherry-SRTS libraries under different toxin pressure. Having transformed the plasmid library of mCherry-SRTS6 variant into B. subtilis 168 Δsr4-bsrG Δsr5-bsrE, the obtained variant library was subsequently prepared as competent cells and the pHT01-bsrG-OPRTS6 plasmid series for transformation. The mixtures were processed and incubated as a routine transformation before plating. Instead of plating onto the LB agar plates, the mixtures were transferred to 5 mL LB medium with chloramphenicol and kanamycin in 50 mL tubes and cultivated for 12 h. After being washed 3 times with PBS (pH = 6.5), cells were resuspended to be concentrated or diluted to OD600 ∼0.1 and then used for subsequent analysis. At a rate of 0.5 μL/s, ∼10 000 events were collected in every sample and processed using FlowJo_V10.9.0.

Cell passages under toxin pressure

To investigate the reduction in inactive subpopulations under bsrG toxin stress during successive passages, we employed recombinant strains harbouring pP43NMK-Pspove-gfp-P43-SRTS6 and pHT01-PyabR-bsrG-OPRTS6. Strains containing pP43NMK-Pspove-gfp-P43-SRTS6 and pHT01-PbltD-bsrG-OPRTS6 (non-lethal pressure) were used as controls. Single colonies were initially isolated from selective plates and cultured in LB medium supplemented with chloramphenicol and kanamycin. Prior to the passage experiments, seed cultures were harvested and washed three times with fresh LB medium containing only chloramphenicol. The washed cells were then inoculated at a 1:30 dilution ratio into 1.5 mL fresh LB medium (with chloramphenicol) in 24-well plates. Two subsequent passages were performed under identical conditions. The relative fluorescence intensity was monitored after each passage and at the end the experiment.

Microscopy and analysis

An Eclipse Ni-E microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) was used to observe the cell shape and fluorescence under a phase-contrast lens (Plan Fluor 100×/1.30 oil, WD = 0.2). GFP fluorescence was imaged using the green fluorescein set [excitation filter, 465–495 nm; dichroic mirror, 505 nm; emission filter, long pass (LP) 512 nm] while mCherry fluorescence was imaged using the red fluorescein set [excitation filter, 527.5–552.5 nm; dichroic mirror, 565 nm; emission filter, (LP) 577.5–632.5 nm]. For the merged fluorescence image, image processing was performed using ImageJ software (version 1.53, National Institutes of Health, USA) [48]. Single channels were extracted (green channel for GFP with brightness/contrast thresholds of 54–257, and red channel for mCherry with thresholds of 131–190) and merged with corresponding phase-contrast images (displayed in gray) using the “Merge Channels” function. All other images are shown as raw data without adjustment.

Results

Engineering type I antitoxin RNA core for gene regulation

Recent advances in bioinformatics and big data analytics have significantly accelerated the identification and characterization of TA systems. Although assigning biological functions to these systems remains challenging, numerous TA systems have been well characterized at the molecular level [13, 49]. Additionally, studies have demonstrated that RNase III is required for toxin mRNA-antitoxin RNA complex cleavage in certain cases [50]. Although diverse regulatory mechanisms are adopted, the type I antitoxin RNA-mediated toxin neutralization consistently begins with base pairing. Here, the TA mRNA interaction of 72 type I TA systems with experimental evaluation recorded in TADB 3.0 was analyzed and summarized (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Table S1). We found that the complementary pairing length in type I TA systems is distinct, ranging from 19 to 203 bp, which suggests a high tolerance in antitoxin RNA-toxin mRNA binding. Meanwhile, the MFE (Minimum Free Energy) difference between their complex and free states can be as low as -6.79 kcal/mol, indicating a potential chaperone dependence [51]. Hence, we focused on the type I TA systems in B. subtilis, whose mechanisms have been comprehensively studied, to avoid unknown function-dependent entities [8, 36, 37, 52] (Supplementary Fig. S1). The reported antitoxins of these TA systems showed high sequence similarity and retained an RNase III interaction motif in the 3′ end of their terminator hairpin (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. S2), confirming that the dominating part of antitoxin RNA interference was in the 3′ end. As described in previous studies, the truncated antitoxin with latter hairpins is sufficient for binding to the target full-length toxin mRNA [8, 14]. To investigate the minimal functional elements, we truncated two well-characterized type I antitoxins in SR4/BsrG and SR5/BsrE TA systems to generate SR4mini and SR5mini, respectively. Specifically, only the terminal hairpin structure of SR4 or SR5 containing the RNase III recognition motif was retained (Fig. 1C). Correspondingly, the complementary sequences, termed OPR4mini and OPR5mini, were inserted downstream of the stop codon of a green fluorescent protein (GFP) encoding gene to serve as reporters (Fig. 1D).

To avoid the interference from endogenous background expression, the engineered B. subtilis 168 Δsr4-bsrG Δsr5-bsrE was used as a host for studying the truncated pairs. As shown in Fig. 1E, both SR4mini-OPR4mini and SR5mini-OPR5mini regulatory systems performed robust repression efficiency (approximately 75%). Nevertheless, substantial crosstalk was also observed between SR4mini-OPR5mini and SR5mini-OPR4mini pairs, suggesting a non-specific interaction caused by sequence homology between these minimal modules. Thus, we further disrupted the hairpins of SR4mini and SR5mini, extracted the last 25 nt sequence containing the RNase III interaction motif in their 3′ terminal respectively and fused them with a Rho-independent terminator respectively, termed SR4core and SR5core (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Fig. S3). Consistently, both SR4core and SR5core maintained over 70% repression efficiency (Fig. 1G and Supplementary Fig. S4), which suggests the natural hairpin structure is dispensable for RNase III motif recognition and gene repression.

De novo design of the SRTS-OPRTS regulation system

The advances in computer-aided RNA design and fundamental understanding of RNA interaction empower us to rationally design synthetic RNA devices. Since the repression activity of the streamlined antitoxin RNA for the target gene had been evaluated and confirmed above, we proposed a general principle to design sets of SR-OPR regulation systems in silico. Different from the conventional RBS lock-up toehold switches that rely on a stem-loop structure to block the RBS [23, 25, 26], the double strands of RNase III cleavage sites were separated and respectively locked in the hairpin of SRTS and OPRTS. In addition, a toehold sequence, acting as the initial binding site, was fused to the 5′ end of SRTS hairpin while its complementary toehold was placed at the 3′ end of OPRTS hairpin. In this case, only a complete hybridization between SRTS and OPRTS could trigger the cleavage (Fig. 2A). In detail, for SRTS, part of the toehold-mediated strand displacement module (5′-GACCU-3′) was employed to lock the RNase III recognition sequence in the top interior loop of an artificial terminator, which contained a 6 bp variable bottom stem region (5′-R19RNNNN24-3′) to reduce sequence similarity while maintaining structural stability. Specifically, the two weak R-U base pairs were set in the bottom stem for promoting strand displacement. Additionally, an 18 nt free single-stranded sequence was placed at the 5′ end of the stem for triggering OPRTS binding. A ployU7 tail [47] was placed downstream of the stem to enable SRTS as a strong transcription terminator (Termination efficiency over 95%, Supplementary Fig. S5). For the cognate OPRTS, the RNase III cleavage sequence derived from toxin mRNA was also embedded in a 9 bp-stem-7 nt-loop hairpin to prevent potential structural interference from flanking sequences (Fig. 2A).

Unlike the “loop-kissing” model of natural type I toxin mRNA interacting with cognate antitoxin RNA [10, 37, 56], the disruption of the hairpin structures in the SRTS-OPRTS system depended on their accessible single strands annealing and subsequent strand displacements in both SRTS and OPRTS. The final conformation exposed the entire RNase III cleavage site and thereby promoted cleavage of OPRTS-tagged target mRNA (Fig. 2B). As a proof of concept, we randomly assigned nucleotides into the designated SRTS-OPRTS structure to generate four SRTS-OPRTS pairs (from SRTS0-OPRTS0 to SRTS3-OPRTS3). Accordingly, each SRTS was expressed under the constitutive strong promoter P43 to repress gfp-OPRTS driven by the constitutive strong promoter Pspove, in which the cognate OPRTS was inserted downstream of gfp stop codon. Clearly, all four SRTS-OPRTS systems showed high repression efficiency (over 75%, Fig. 2C), demonstrating the capacity of these artificial SRTS-OPRTS systems. Meanwhile, we also investigated the orthogonality of these pairs and noticed that the matched SRTS-OPRTS pairs achieved the highest repression rates (74.1% to 86.1%) compared to those of the non-cognate pairs (0% to 49.8%), indicating moderate crosstalk effects among these randomly generated SRTS-OPRTS pairs (Fig. 2D). The results indicate that more constraints should be integrated to output orthogonal SRTS-OPRTS systems.

Automatic design of orthogonal SRTS-OPRTS regulatory systems

To conveniently and efficiently generate different SRTS-OPRTS regulatory systems with high orthogonality, we established an automated computational pipeline for the de novo design of such RNA devices (Fig. 3A). The pipeline implemented a systematic design strategy, in which sequences required for structural stability were algorithmically assigned under hairpin folding constraints while maintaining the predefined RNase III interaction motif. All SRTS-OPRTS candidates underwent rigorous thermodynamic validation using the ViennaRNA package [44]. Through binding energy constraint-dependent screening (Fig. 3A), only the SRTS-OPRTS pairs that exhibited strong exclusive interaction specificity were selected and applied for further evaluation. As examples, 20 SRTS-OPRTS pairs (from SRTS4-OPRTS4 to SRTS23-OPRTS23) were generated by this pipeline, achieving thermodynamically favorable interactions (Minimal Free Energy, MFE, values below −80 kcal/mol, diagonal), with an energy gap exceeding 30 kcal/mol compared with non-cognate pairs (off-diagonal, Supplementary Fig. S6).

After characterization of the robust gene repression capability (Fig. 3B), we also conducted experiments and validated the strong orthogonality of these SRTS-OPRTS pairs (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. S7). Then, we further performed the thermodynamic analysis of the relationship between binding free energies and repression efficiencies across all 24 SRTS-OPRTS pairs (from SRTS0-OPRTS0 to SRTS23-OPRTS23) and their non-cognate combinations. The results demonstrate that the developed automated pipeline here effectively shields potential crosstalk by imposing thermodynamic constraints, as evidenced by the distinctly different regulatory activities between cognate SRTS-OPRTS pairs (ΔG < -50 kcal/mol) and non-cognate pairs (ΔG > -20 kcal/mol) (Fig. 3D). To exclude the potential that conserved RNase III interaction sequence or toehold itself could cause gene repression, SRTS6-Cut and SRTS6-Seed, respectively targeting RNase III interaction sequence and toehold sequence of OPRTS6, were designed and investigated, and the results demonstrate that individual expressions of SRTS6-Cut or SRTS6-Seed exhibit minimal regulatory effects. These results confirm that only the intact SRTS that completely base-pairs with OPRTS was competent to mediate strong regulation (Fig. 3E). For ease of use, we have implemented this automated SRTS-OPRTS design pipeline as a web-based tool on our RNADesigner platform (www.kangzlab.cn/SR-OPR), which will enable users to generate customized orthogonal SRTS-OPRTS pairs (Supplementary Fig. S8).

Cross-species implementation of the SRTS-OPRTS system

Given the ubiquitous distribution of type I TA systems across bacteria, we aimed to evaluate the broader applicability of this SRTS-OPRTS regulatory system in other model microorganisms. Initially, the wild-type SR4 and its cognate OPR4 from B. subtilis bsrG mRNA were employed to regulate gfp expression in E. coli, while a modest repression efficiency of 19.5% was observed. Upon co-expression of the B. subtilis rnc gene encoding RNase III (BsRNase III), the repression efficiency was increased to 69.6% (Supplementary Fig. S9), highlighting the crucial role of RNase III in SR-OPR regulatory systems. As a consequence, we investigated the artificial SRTS6-OPRTS6 regulatory system in E. coli and C. glutamicum with co-expression of B. subtilis rnc. As a result, about 90% repression efficiency was detected in both E. coli and C. glutamicum (Fig. 4A), demonstrating the effectiveness of SRTS-OPRTS systems. In some cases, simultaneous repression of more than one gene is required for optimizing the final output. To this end, we assessed the performance of the SRTS-OPRTS system in multiplexed gene regulation in E. coli. Notably, both the expression of gfp (∼79.6%) and mCherry (∼92.3%) was significantly repressed upon inducible expression of SRTS6 (Fig. 4B). These findings demonstrate the potential broad-host-range applicability of this BsRNase III-SRTS-OPRTS post-transcriptional regulatory system.

Development of dynamic mutually inhibitory switches based on SRTS-OPRTS systems

Genetic switches are reversible regulatory elements that alter the ON/OFF states of target gene expression. Functioning as sophisticated regulatory circuits, these switches usually integrate promoters, transcription factors, and other interconnected genetic elements, which dynamically determine the expression levels of specific genes in response to environmental stimuli or developmental cues. Therefore, genetic switches play a central role in cellular fate control and have become a focal point of growing scientific interest [57–59]. Here, we developed a dynamic mutually inhibitory (DMI) switch based on the SRTS-OPRTS system. We firstly investigated the controllable gene repression of the SRTS-OPRTS system, in which SRTS6 was individually controlled by a well-characterized xylose-induced promoter PxylA [59, 60], while OPRTS6 tagged gfp was driven by a strong constitutive promoter Pspove (Fig. 5A). Quantitative analysis results showed that a dynamic regulatory range was achieved by supplying different xylose concentrations ranging from 0–4.5% w/v (Fig. 5A). On this basis, we further recruited the other fluorescent protein coding gene mCherry to take this switch to the next level in mutual inhibition (Fig. 5B). In this case, SRTS6 was co-transcribed with mCherry as a 3′-UTR tag under the induction of xylose. Due to the RNase III-mediated SRTS-OPRTS regulatory mechanism, the mCherry-SRTS6 transcripts will hybridize gfp-OPRTS6 mRNA in their 3′ UTRs and trigger co-degradation of the duplex at the post-transcriptional level, leaving the exceeding or unpaired reporter transcripts available for translation, and the resulting fluorescence will be correlated with the xylose concentration. Excitingly, we noticed mCherry transcripts containing SRTS6 in its 3′ UTR were also capable of repressing the expression of OPRTS6 tagged gfp (Fig. 5C and Fig. 5E). By increasing the concentration of xylose, we step by step achieved state transitions from GFP to mCherry. On the contrary, when replacing the position of mCherry-SRTS6 with gfp-OPRTS6, it could be found that with the increased induction of gfp-OPRTS6, the mCherry-SRTS6 relative fluorescence intensity was also gradually decreased, suggesting a dose-dependent and mutual regulation on SRTS and OPRTS tagged genes (Fig. 5D).

Besides, we also investigated the dynamic response of this DMI switch during the cultivation of a recombinant strain harbouring PxylA-mCherry-SRTS6-Pspove-gfp-OPRTS6 switch. On the one hand, we observed that the fluorescence intensity of GFP decreased while that of mCherry gradually increased due to xylose addition after 12 h cultivation, suggesting an effective repression of gfp-OPRTS6 by the transcript mCherry-SRTS6 during the following cultivation (81.7% repression to GFP at 30 h, Fig. 5F). On the other hand, the GFP fluorescence intensity recovered upon the removal of the initially supplemented xylose at 12 h, while the mCherry fluorescence intensity faded away (GFP fluorescence intensity recovered to 70.4% at 30 h, Fig. 5G). The moderate efficiency of the transition could be attributed to the accumulated GFP or mCherry protein before 12 h and the accelerated consumption of xylose with high cell density. Additionally, the gfp-OPRTS6 and mCherry-SRTS6 mRNAs generated during the first 12 hours also slowed down their respective fluorescence transition processes, because these transcripts would pair with the newly transcribed mCherry-SRTS6 or gfp-OPRTS6 mRNAs and prevent them from being translated until these new transcripts became the majority. Therefore, only a slight fluorescent change on initially repressed mCherry-SRTS6 and gfp-OPRTS6 was observed in the 12–15 h. Taken together, these dominant fluorescence state transitions caused by xylose indicate the dynamic and mutual regulation of this DMI switch, and the results also demonstrate that SRTS-OPRTS can act as 3′ UTR tags to construct DMI switches at the post-transcriptional level.

Construction of a selective lethal system based on the SRTS-OPRTS system

The SRTS-OPRTS tags enabled us to conveniently create DMI switches by adding them to the 3′ UTR of genes of interest, respectively. We exploited this feature and created a selective lethal system by integrating SRTS6-OPRTS6 tags with the toxin gene to create a conditional lethal genetic switch, where toxin-OPRTS6 would trigger cell growth inhibition in the absence of sufficient SRTS-tagged gene or SRTS transcripts (Fig. 6A). In the toxin-mediated survival assay, we first investigated the lethal effect of B. subtilis endogenous Type I toxin bsrG, broad-spectrum type II toxin ccdB [61], and deoxyribonuclease DNase I [62] tagged with OPRTS6 under gradient expression level with five different B. subtilis endogenous promoters (PbltD, Pxpac, PlytE, PyabR, and PysdB) (Fig. 6A). As a result, the introduction of the constitutively expressed bsrG-OPRTS6 gene, which disturbed cell envelope biosynthesis, resulted in complete inhibition of colony formation (Using the pHT01 empty vector as a control), whose toxic effects were partly alleviated by SRTS6 transcription (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. S10). Similarly, ccdB-OPRTS6, which disrupts replication and transcription machinery, also showed remarkable growth inhibition and could only be recovered by SRTS6 when its expression was controlled by promoters of low transcription levels due to its strong toxicity. In contrast, the dnase-I-OPRTS6, which was supposed to affect the stability of the chromosome, only reduced the number of colonies on increasingly transcriptional levels. With favorable lethal effect and reversibility, the bsrG-OPRTS6 was competent for selective pressure to inhibit the growth of cells without sufficient SRTS or SRTS-tagged gene transcripts, namely, enriching a specific cell population with relatively high SRTS transcription.

Given this conceptual foundation of this selective lethal system, a mCherry-SRTS6 library with varying transcription strength was constructed via degenerate PCR, and was respectively co-transformed with the above five bsrG-OPRTS6 expressing plasmids along with the empty plasmid as a control to generate six distinct libraries with corresponding selective pressures (Fig. 6C). Theoretically, the survival of cells requires sufficient expression levels of mCherry-SRTS6 for repressing the expression of bsrG-OPRTS6. In this case, the selected and enriched cells should exhibit higher relative fluorescence intensity of mCherry. As shown in Fig. 6D, the flow cytometric analysis results showed that when increasing bsrG-OPRTS6 expression, the mean fluorescence intensity of each library was significantly enhanced compared to the empty plasmid control (Increased by 202%, 395%, 497%, 1132%, and 596%, respectively), with progressive right-shifts of the major population peaks in fluorescence distribution histograms. However, no further increase of the mCherry fluorescence intensity was observed with the PysdB-bsrG-OPRTS6 cassette, which suggested that the constitutive expression of bsrG-OPRTS6 driven by PysdB exceeded the mCherry-SRTS6 transcription capacity of all mutants in this library, being confirmed by the fewer and smaller colonies in the toxin-mediated survival assay and reduced cell growth upon cultivation (Supplementary Fig. S11). Besides, we performed the high-throughput sequencing on the mutant library under PyabR-bsrG-OPRTS6 pressure and found an enrichment of certain sequence motifs in the designated mutation regions, while these mutation sites were in a relatively balanced base composition in the initial mutant library or mutant library with empty vector (Supplementary Fig. S12).

Active population enrichment with the SRTS-OPRTS system

Cell population heterogeneity will lower the overall fluorescence intensity due to the presence of subpopulations with reduced fluorescence. Based on our SRTS-OPRTS-derived selective lethal system, we aimed to eliminate these underperforming subpopulations through successive cell passages. Utilizing bsrG-OPRTS6 as the selective pressure, subpopulations with insufficient SRTS6 will experience growth inhibition and be diluted out after several rounds of cell passages. As a proof of concept, we created a cell population heterogeneity situation by allowing plasmid loss in the absence of appropriate antibiotics. In order to associate fluorescence with cell growth, we constructed a model strain containing two compatible plasmids, a pHT01-derived plasmid containing bsrG-OPRTS6 cassette with chloramphenicol resistance and a pP43NMK-derived plasmid with kanamycin resistance containing both P43-SRTS6 and Pspove-driven gfp reporter gene (Fig. 7A). With only a chloramphenicol supplement, the inactive subpopulations were attributed to the loss of the SRTS expression plasmid, which also carried a constitutively expressed gfp reporter gene, thereby resulting in reduced overall fluorescence levels. Together with the PbltD-bsrG-OPRTS6 expression strain as control, the PyabR-bsrG-OPRTS6 expression strain was subcultured at a 1:30 dilution ratio in fresh medium supplemented with chloramphenicol for three successive passages. After three passages, the GFP fluorescence intensity in PyabR-bsrG-OPRTS6 expression strain gradually increased to 135% of its initial level, whereas the PbltD-bsrG-OPRTS6 expression strain showed a 45% decrease in relative fluorescence intensity. Notably, the relative fluorescence intensity difference in the last passage between these two strains exhibited a significant 6.7-fold increase compared to the beginning (Fig. 7B). This divergent pattern reflected that non-lethal toxin expression in the PbltD-bsrG-OPRTS6 strain allowed cells to survive without maintaining the SRTS plasmid carrying the GFP expression cassette, resulting in decreased fluorescence intensity in the group. In contrast, the high toxin expression in the PyabR-bsrG-OPRTS6 strain created a strong selective pressure for plasmid retention, as cell survival strictly depended on SRTS expression, so during successive passages, cells with compromised SRTS plasmid maintenance were progressively eliminated due to growth inhibition under toxin stress, leading to enrichment of the highly fluorescent population.

Discussion

Bacterial TA systems represent an untapped reservoir of molecular switches for synthetic biology applications. These naturally evolved regulatory modules, comprising a toxin and its corresponding antitoxin, offer unique advantages as genetic control elements due to their tight regulation, rapid response dynamics, and modular architecture [63]. Our engineering efforts to repurpose the type I TA system have revealed several key insights into both the intrinsic core of TA system function and its potential as a synthetic regulatory device. In this study, we extracted the unstructured key sequence of antitoxin RNA of type I TA system in B. subtilis and characterized its regulation activity (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Fig. S4), which contained RNase III cleavage site [8, 55], and subsequently scaffolded it with strand displacement modules to generate artificial post-transcriptional regulation RNA devices, the SRTS-OPRTS pairs (Fig. 2A). As previously characterized, RBS lock-up toehold switches reported [64–66], the branch-migration reaction can be driven by a minimal negative free energy difference of −1 to −2 kcal/mol, while a positive energy barrier of + 3 kcal/mol introduced by trigger RNA mismatches is sufficient to maintain the switch in its OFF state [67]. Considering these established thermodynamic parameters and the natural type I system binding energetics, we concluded that the -50 kcal/mol energy threshold implemented in our SRTS-OPRTS design provided adequate thermodynamic stringency for switch functionality (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, the incorporation of the stem region enhanced the structural stability of the RNase III recognition site within the SRTS and OPRTS hairpin loop, thereby significantly impeding potential loop-kissing interactions initiated from the conserved sequence in the loop (Fig. 2A). As a result, neither the isolated toehold module (SRTS-Seed) nor the locked RNase III interaction module (SRTS-Cut) of SRTS exhibited substantial repression against gfp-OPRTS alone (Fig. 3E). Therefore, we hypothesize that the incompetent repression ability of SRTS6-Cut results from its inability to fully release the hairpin of gfp-OPRTS6, as it lacks the seed region in intact SRTS6 and makes the RNase III cleavage site remain inaccessible.

With favorable thermodynamics minimizing the potential crosstalk among distinct SRTS-OPRTS pairs, the non-conserved nucleotides flanking the RNase III recognition motif were systematically assigned to SRTS-OPRTS scaffolds under defined structural and energy constraints, facilitating the automated construction of the orthogonal SRTS-OPRTS pair libraries (Fig. 3A). Utilizing this SRTS-OPRTS design online platform, these orthogonal regulatory RNA devices derived from type I TA systems can be systematically designed and efficiently constructed (Supplementary Fig. S8). Meanwhile, the de novo designed SRTS-OPRTS systems not only provide numerous and orthogonal alternatives for SR4-based regulation systems [14], but also maintain repression activities comparable to those of reported riboregulators derived from natural sRNAs, including RoxS (80.8%) and SR6mini (84.3%), under our experimental conditions [18, 38] (Supplementary Fig. S13).

As SR-OPR systems originated from B. subtilis type I TA systems, the B. subtilis endogenous RNase III is required to degrade SR-OPR RNA duplex [68–70]. In accordance with the distinct substrate specificity of endogenous RNase III of B. subtilis and E. coli [71], we initially investigated the full length of SR4-OPR4 only achieved a less than 20% repression rate without co-expressed B. subtilis RNase III (Supplementary Fig. S9). Therefore, we evaluated SRTS-OPRTS regulation systems with B. subtilis RNase III co-expression in other model microorganisms, E. coli, and C. glutamicum, and achieved over 90% repression efficiency, which conferred broad-host-range regulation ability of the BsRNase III-SRTS-OPRTS system (Fig. 4A). Moreover, we noticed that the SRTS6-OPRTS6 pair could also achieve a moderate repression rate (66.9%) in E. coli without BsRNase III, suggesting that endogenous RNases might also play a role in SRTS6-OPRTS6 duplex degradation. Meanwhile, the quantitative regulation could be achieved by fine-tuning the OPRTS:SRTS ratio at the transcription level. The xylose-induced SRTS transcription demonstrated a dose-dependent increase in repression efficiency to a constitutively expressed gfp-OPRTS, correlating with xylose concentrations up to 3% (w/v) (Fig. 5A), and the observed plateau in repression efficiency beyond this threshold is attributed to the inherent transcriptional capacity limitations of the PxylA promoter [60]. Moreover, according to the unwinding of RNA secondary structure by translating polyribosome [72], we hypothesize that the upstream coding sequence will not perturb the designated structure of 3′ UTR OPRTS or SRTS. This premise led us to investigate the potential regulatory ability of 3′ UTR SRTS-tagged protein-coding sequences. Interestingly, when SRTS6 was incorporated downstream of the mCherry stop codon as a terminator, termed mCherry-SRTS6, it exhibited mutual regulation with gfp-OPRTS6 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5C and 5D). This bifunctional behavior validates the successful integration of both regulatory and translational capabilities within a single genetic construct.

The naturally occurring protein-coding regulatory RNAs represent elegant examples of nature’s efficiency, serving dual roles as both protein-coding messenger RNAs and functional non-coding RNAs [73–75]. Through strategic placement of SRTS and OPRTS in the 3′ UTR, these tags enable rapid engineering of mRNAs into programmable trans-regulatory elements and create DMI switches at the post-transcriptional level beyond their natural purpose. Compared to previously reported toehold switch sensors triggered by the coding sequence of mRNA [26], the SRTS tag in 3′ UTR avoids the complicated binding caused by the complex secondary structures abound within mRNAs. Instead of targeting coding sequence in endogenous mRNA with the potential to form inhibitory secondary structures [26], our well-designed OPRTS sequences can provide more accessible binding sites for SRTS interaction and avoid translation machinery interference due to their strategic placement in the 3′ UTR region. Besides the mCherry-gfp DMI switch constructed by SRTS-OPRTS tag, a selective lethal switch where the reporter gene mCherry-SRTS6 regulated the toxin gene bsrG-OPRTS6 was utilized as a specific cell enrichment system. This design enabled the enrichment of subpopulations exhibiting elevated mCherry fluorescence, with bsrG-OPRTS6 transcription levels serving as the selection threshold, achieving a maximum 11.32-fold increase in population-averaged fluorescence intensity (Fig. 6D). The overall fluorescence intensity increase of the mutant library results from the growth inhibition of mutants without enough mCherry-SRTS transcripts, whose proportion decreases under such selective pressure. Similarly, the cell heterogeneity will also pull down the average performance in a specific cell group even without mutagenesis [76]. Therefore, we created a cell population heterogeneity model by allowing plasmid loss in the absence of appropriate antibiotics. Installed with SRTS6-bsrG-OPRTS6 lethal switch, we found that the bsrG-OPRTS6-based selective pressure coupled with successive passaging efficiently eliminated these growth-compromised subpopulations lacking SRTS expression, as evidenced by a 35% enhancement in fluorescence under pressure versus a 45% decrease without selection. (Fig. 7B).

Overall, our work establishes synthetic RNA devices derived from bacterial type I TA systems for post-transcriptional regulation. By reverse engineering of type I antitoxins in B. subtilis, a structure-independent core sequence containing RNase III cleavage sites was obtained and subsequently integrated into pairs of well-designed toehold switches together with their complementary pairing sequence. The high sequence flexibility within artificial SRTS-OPRTS pairs allows us to generate multiple variants of them, while the preset thermodynamic constraints maintain their orthogonality compared to our previously reported SR4-based regulatory system. Through systematic characterization, we demonstrate that the SRTS-OPRTS systems enable orthogonal gene regulation and exhibit broad host compatibility when co-expressed with B. subtilis RNase III. The integration of SRTS-OPRTS modules as tags into encoding gene’s 3′ UTRs reveals an efficient strategy for creating a post-transcriptional DMI switch, which has proved useful in enrichment of specific cell subpopulations and elimination of under-performance cells under selective pressure. Collectively, these findings not only demonstrate that the SRTS-OPRTS systems are portable and versatile regulatory tools but also highlight the potential of naturally evolved TA systems as a source of synthetic biological parts.
