Prevalence and risk of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease in patients with sarcopenic obesity: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Peng Wang, Xiaohui Liu, Xiaofei Du, Lixia Qiu, Yali Liu, Shanshan Xu, Yang Zhang, Jing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe coexistence of sarcopenia and obesity has been established as a pivotal factor driving the pathological progression of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). This study systematically evaluates the prevalence and risk of MASLD in patients with sarcopenic obesity (SO).MethodA comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Cochrane Library, EMBASE, Web of Science and SCOPUS up to March 2025. All studies investigating the association between SO and MASLD were included in this meta-analysis. Two independent reviewers performed screening and data extraction. ORs and 95% CIs were calculated using random effect models. Subgroup analysis was used to identify the sources of heterogeneity. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and quantified via the I² statistic. Quality assessment and publication bias (by Funnel plots and Egger’s test) evaluation were also performed.ResultsThirteen studies involving 35,373 SO patients (from six countries) were included after screening. Odds ratios (ORs) of the included studies were combined by random effect model. The pooled results revealed that 63.4% of SO patients had MASLD. Compared to non-SO individuals, SO was significantly associated with an increased risk of MASLD (OR = 4.45, 95% confidence interval (CI): 2.57–7.72, P < 0.001). Females exhibited a higher MASLD risk than males (OR = 4.22, 95% CI: 2.10–8.50 vs. OR = 7.56, 95% CI: 2.39–23.92). Substantial heterogeneity was observed across pooled results and subgroups. Additionally, SO patients had a 2.34-fold higher risk of MASLD-related fibrosis than non-SO individuals (OR = 2.34, 95% CI: 1.78–3.08, P < 0.001).ConclusionSO may be closely associated with a high prevalence of MASLD and accelerated fibrosis progression. These findings highlight SO as a potential high-risk population for MASLD, underscoring the need for targeted screening and intervention strategies. However, more high-quality research with unified definitions and different races is needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-025-01000-4.Keywords: Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, Sarcopenic obesity, Meta-analysis, MASLD, Systematic review

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly termed non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), has emerged as the most prevalent chronic liver disorder worldwide. The global prevalence of MASLD in adults has exceeded 30% and is increasing. Its progression is linked to adverse hepatic outcomes, including liver fibrosis, cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [1]. MASLD not only adversely affects liver health, but also is associated with cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and a variety of cancers. In the next ten years, the economic burden of MASLD will increase dramatically, beyond the scope of liver-related complications [2]. This will bring tremendous pressure to the medical system. The rising prevalence of obesity and metabolic syndrome has positioned MASLD as a major public health challenge. The pathogenesis of MASLD involves multiple factors such as dysregulated lipid metabolism, insulin resistance (IR) and chronic inflammation [3–5]. Despite lifestyle interventions and metabolic management are currently the main therapeutic approaches, the heterogeneity and complex pathophysiologic mechanisms of MASLD lead to significant limitations in its clinical management [1]. More precise risk stratification is urgently needed to better identify high-risk population and develop prevention and treatment strategies.

Sarcopenic obesity (SO), characterized by reduced skeletal muscle mass and excessive adiposity, is increasingly prevalent in aging populations and individuals with metabolic abnormalities [6, 7]. The muscle-fat imbalance exacerbates IR, oxidative stress and chronic inflammation, potentially promoting multi-organ metabolic disorders through lipotoxicity and dysregulated adipokine secretion [8]. IR is recognized as the central hub linking skeletal muscle loss and adipose tissue accumulation(particularly visceral fat)in SO, thereby driving a vicious cycle between these components. IR contributes to the decline in muscle mass and strength by impairing muscle protein metabolism. Furthermore, IR promotes ectopic lipid deposition and drives chronic inflammation, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction in skeletal muscle. This subsequently compromises muscle contractile function and promotes protein oxidative damage and breakdown [9]. While SO has been widely associated with metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular diseases [7], its impact on hepatic metabolism remains understudied. There is a lack of systematic summarization of the relationship between SO and MASLD.

Emerging evidence suggests that the coexistence of sarcopenia and obesity may synergistically elevate MASLD risk and accelerate fibrosis progression [10]. However, existing epidemiological studies on SO and MASLD report inconsistent findings, with limited quantitative assessments of disease severity and progression. This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to synthesize current evidence on the prevalence, risk and clinical implications of MASLD in SO populations. The findings provide a reference for the early identification of MASLD high-risk individuals and guide precision interventions for MASLD management.

Materials and methods

Literature search strategy

This meta-analysis adhered to the latest Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [11]. The protocol has been registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251040460). A comprehensive search was conducted across electronic databases, including PubMed, Cochrane, EMBASE, Web of Science, SCOPUS and references of the included studies, with the search period extending up to March 2025. No language restrictions were applied. Keywords included combinations of “MASLD” OR “NAFLD” OR “non-alcoholic fatty liver disease” OR “metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease” OR " metabolic associated fatty liver disease” OR “MAFLD” AND “Sarcopenic Obesity” OR “SO”. Additionally, reference lists of retrieved articles were reviewed to ensure inclusion of all relevant studies.

Eligibility criteria for study inclusion

The inclusion criteria for this meta-analysis were as following points: (a) patients diagnosed with MASLD via liver biopsy or non-invasive diagnostic and imaging methods (participants); (b) patients with confirmed SO based on diagnostic criteria (exposure); (c) all observational studies, including cohort, case-control and cross-sectional designs; (d) comparison of SO and non-SO (comparators)on occurrence and prognosis (outcome)of MASLD; and (e) no restrictions on sample size; (f) reported the prevalence of MASLD in SO patients or (g) examined the association between SO and MASLD. Exclusion criteria comprised: (a) case reports, conference abstracts, animal studies, or letters; (b) studies with a high risk of bias (based on the Cochrane risk bias assessment tool); and (c) studies with overlapping data.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two independent reviewers rigorously extracted relevant data according to the eligibility criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third expert. Extracted data included article title, publication year, first author, country, study design, sample size, gender distribution, outcomes, and diagnostic methods of SO. Adjusted OR values were derived from multivariable regression analyses after controlling for confounding factors. Any adjusted estimate reported by the original studies was accepted regardless of the covariates used.

The risk of bias in a study was assessed by one author, and another author was responsible for verifying the accuracy of the assessment. Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [12], applied independently by two reviewers. The NOS evaluates three domains: participant selection, comparability, and outcome assessment, with maximum scores of 4, 2, and 3, respectively (total score range: 0–9). Studies achieving an NOS score ≥ 6 were classified as high quality. GRADE assessment to evaluate the certainty of the evidence, along with a corresponding summary of findings table (Table S2-3).

Statistical analysis

All pooled data in the meta-analysis were analyzed using Stata 17 and GraphPad Prism 8. Heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic. I2 > 50% and P < 0.1 were considered criteria for high heterogeneity. Natural log-transformed ORs and 95% CIs were calculated using fixed effect models or random-effects models. Due to methodological heterogeneity among the included studies, we employed random-effects models. Subgroups (diagnosis of MASLD and SO, region and complication) were performed to explore sources of heterogeneity. Sensitivity analyses based on leave-one-out were performed to assess the robustness of the meta-analysis findings. Publication bias was assessed via funnel plots and Egger’s test. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P-value < 0.05.

Results

Study characteristics

As shown in the flowchart (Fig. 1), 1493 studies were initially identified through screening. After removing duplicates, 1291 articles were retained. Further, we excluded 1175 studies based on titles and abstracts. 116 studies with potentially relevant content were left. Among these, 51 studies were excluded due to unclear diagnoses, 43 for lacking valid outcomes and 9 for incomplete data. Ultimately, 13 studies [13–25] involving 35,373 patients with SO from diverse countries were included. Among studies reporting gender data, 28% of participants were male and 72% female. Detailed characteristics of the included studies are presented in Table 1. The meta-analysis included five case-control, three cohort and five cross-sectional studies. Data sources primarily included medical institutions and public databases. Geographically, four studies were from China, four from South Korea, two from the United States, and one each from Japan, Greece, and Turkey. All studies were rated as moderate quality, with scores ranging from 5 to 6.

Next, we conducted a risk of bias (ROB) assessment. According to the 13 studies included in this meta-analysis, all exhibited a high risk of bias concerning both random and blinding (random sequence generation, blinding of participants and individuals). Uncertainty regarding other bias was present in all studies. However, outcome data were complete without selective reporting for all studies except Lee’s. As per the methodological quality assessment presented in Figure S1A-B, the included literature was predominantly of moderate to high quality.

Prevalence of MASLD in patients with SO

Nine studies provided data on MASLD prevalence [13– [16, 18, 21, 22, 24, 25]. The pooled results showed that the prevalence of MASLD was 61.0% in 4629 patients with SO (Fig. 2). Notably, higher MASLD rates (63.0%) were observed in Asian SO populations (Fig. 3A), while other ethnic groups (including two from the United States and one from Greece) exhibited a lower prevalence of 58.0%. We then observed the prevalence of MASLD in different groups. The results showed that South Korea had the highest MASLD prevalence (73.0%) in SO patients, followed by China (59.0%) and Greece (60.5%), with the lowest rate in America (57.0%) (Fig. 3B).

Effect of SO on the occurrence of MASLD

Twelve studies evaluated the association between SO and MASLD using logistic regression (OR and 95% CI) [13–15, 17–25]. Three of these studies analyzed male and female populations separately. Due to significant heterogeneity (I² = 97.8%, P < 0.001), a random-effects model was applied to pooled log-transformed data. Meta-analysis demonstrated that SO patients had a 4.45-fold higher risk of MASLD compared to non-SO individuals (OR = 4.45, 95% CI: 2.57–7.72, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4). Next, we investigated the effect of SO on the risk of MASLD in different gender populations. Three studies analyzed male populations and four studies analyzed female populations. Gender-specific analysis revealed elevated MASLD risks in both males (OR = 4.22, 95% CI: 2.10–8.50, P < 0.001) (Fig. 5A) and females (OR = 7.56, 95% CI: 2.39–23.92, P < 0.001) (Fig. 5B), with random-effects models applied due to high heterogeneity.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore heterogeneity sources (Table S1 and Table 2). In terms of prevalence, we found high heterogeneity in Asia (I² = 95.79%, P < 0.001) and other groups (I² = 95.23%, P < 0.001). When we grouped by different countries, there was no heterogeneity among the Chinese population (I² = 0.00%, P = 0.47), while there was still considerable heterogeneity among the Korean (I² = 96.78%, P < 0.001) and American (I² = 94.80%, P < 0.001) populations. For MASLD diagnostic methods, no heterogeneity was observed in studies using controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) (I² = 0.0%, P = 0.696), whereas high heterogeneity persisted in abdominal ultrasound (I² = 91.2%, P < 0.001) and other diagnostic groups (I² = 98.0%, P < 0.001). Despite heterogeneity, SO remained significantly associated with MASLD in all subgroups. When stratified by SO diagnostic criteria, studies using skeletal muscle mass to visceral fat ratio (SVR) showed low heterogeneity (I² = 25.2%, P = 0.237), while others exhibited high heterogeneity (I² = 98.6%, P < 0.001). Both subgroups confirmed SO as a MASLD risk factor (OR = 3.21, 95% CI: 2.57–4.00; OR = 5.49, 95% CI: 2.29–13.15; P < 0.001). Geographic analysis revealed no heterogeneity in North American studies (I² = 0.0%, P = 0.502), but significant heterogeneity persisted in Asian and European studies (P < 0.001). Notably, SO was not significantly linked to MASLD in European populations (P = 0.097). Regardless of diabetes status, SO remained a significant MASLD risk factor (P < 0.001).

Association between SO and MASLD-associated fibrosis

Four studies assessed the relationship between SO and liver fibrosis. Pooled results (Fig. 6) indicated a 2.34-fold increased risk of fibrosis in SO patients (OR = 2.34, 95% CI: 1.78–3.08, P < 0.001). Subgroup analyses were not performed because of the minimal heterogeneity of the included studies (I2 = 1.1%, P = 0.400).

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess whether the results were affected by a single study (Figure S2A-C). Based on the leave-one-out method, we observed the effects of individual studies on prevalence, correlation and fibrosis outcomes, respectively. The results showed that no matter which study is excluded, the remaining combined results will not be changed. Funnel plots and Egger’s test indicated no publication bias among the 12 studies evaluating MASLD risk (Fig. 7A). Then, we used the trim and fill method to analyze fibrosis outcome. The results confirmed that there was no bias and the results were robust (p > 0.05) (Figure S3).

Discussion

As a prevalent metabolic disorder increasingly threatening global public health, MASLD currently lacks effective therapeutic interventions. Early identification of high-risk populations is crucial for precise disease prevention and management. With the rising prevalence of SO, its interaction with MASLD has garnered significant attention. The dual burden of reduced muscle mass or function and excessive fat accumulation positions SO as a key risk factor for MASLD [10]. However, systematic summaries of the clinical correlation between SO and MASLD remain scarce. As far as we know, there is currently no summary of the prevalence of MASLD among the SO population. At the same time, very few clinical studies have systematically summarized the effects of SO on the occurrence and progression of MASLD. Although several reviews have predicted the mechanism of the correlation between SO and MASLD [10, 26, 27], there is a lack of relevant clinical data. This meta-analysis comprehensively evaluates the relationship between SO and MASLD occurrence and fibrosis progression. The results demonstrate a notably higher prevalence of MASLD among individuals with SO (63.4%) compared to previously reported rates in general and obese populations. Furthermore, SO significantly elevates MASLD incidence and fibrosis progression, underscoring the critical interplay between skeletal muscle and adipose tissue in MASLD pathogenesis.

The precise mechanisms linking SO to MASLD remain unclear. Potential pathways include: (1) Skeletal muscle, as an insulin-sensitive and energy-regulating organ, may exacerbate hepatic lipid deposition and glucose dysregulation when functionally impaired; (2) Myokines such as myonectin and irisin, secreted by skeletal muscle, modulate oxidative stress and inflammatory responses [13, 28]; (3) Visceral fat accumulation releases free fatty acids and pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-6 and TNF-α), which impair hepatic function and promote muscle atrophy [29, 30]. Additionally, reduced physical activity due to muscle loss may create a vicious cycle of metabolic deterioration, accelerating MASLD onset and fibrosis.

We further explored sex-specific differences in SO-associated MASLD risk. Although SO consistently predicts MASLD across sexes, females and males showed different risk ratios, potentially attributable to hormonal changes. Accelerated estrogen decline in postmenopausal women may reduce hepatic metabolic protection, promote visceral adiposity and exacerbate muscle loss, thereby impairing glucose uptake and driving hepatic lipogenesis. Gender disparities in inflammatory responses, cytokine profiles, and mitochondrial function also contribute to the difference [31]. Subgroup analyses revealed lower heterogeneity in cohorts of CAP, SVR, North American and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) patients. Conversely, high heterogeneity persisted in other subgroups, necessitating further high-quality studies. Notably, European cohorts showed no significant SO-MASLD association, likely due to limited sample size (two studies include 340 SO patients). Intriguingly, SO defined by central obesity conferred greater MASLD risk than visceral fat-based definitions, suggesting systemic lipotoxicity as a key driver. Paradoxically, SO patients with T2DM exhibited lower MASLD risk, conflicting with prior reports [32]. This discrepancy may stem from gender bias (only female cohort in Su at al. study) and imbalanced sample sizes (only2 studies). GRADE assessment revealed very low certainty for MASLD risk associations due to heterogeneity, diagnostic variability, and regional bias. In future research, we should focus on studies that distinguish SO populations of different races and genders to verify this conclusion. Meanwhile, we should further explore the impact of SO on the long-term outcomes of MASLD (such as liver cirrhosis, liver cancer and death) through more prospective cohorts. In addition, we should strive to eliminate the influence of different definitions of SO, such as establishing a multidisciplinary consensus and standardizing the definition of SO.

This study suggested that there is a higher prevalence of MASLD in the SO population. The coexistence of sarcopenia and obesity appears to be a significant risk factor for both the development of MASLD and the progression of liver fibrosis. These findings suggest that crosstalk among muscle, adipose tissue, and the liver may play a key role in the pathological mechanisms of MASLD, offering new insights for exploring its etiology. In addition, SO may represent a high-risk population for MASLD. This provides crucial information for future precise risk stratification of MASLD and informs the development of clinical intervention strategies.

Several limitations warrant consideration: First, most of the studies included in this meta-analysis were Asian populations (9 studies). The small sample in Europe and the Americas may have contributed to the bias of this result. Second, there is high heterogeneity in the pooled results due to the quality of a few studies. More high-quality and large-sample studies are still needed to confirm these findings in the future. Third, the current lack of uniform diagnostic criteria for SO and population heterogeneity may lead to unrepresentative results. The definition and stratification of SO should be standardized in the future. Nevertheless, these findings have important clinical reference value.

Conclusion

SO is associated with higher MASLD prevalence and fibrosis progression. The results support SO as a potential risk population for MASLD. This study provides critical insights for MASLD risk stratification and targeted interventions. More high-quality studies are needed in the future to evaluate the prevalence of MASLD and the impact of SO on the outcomes of MASLD.
