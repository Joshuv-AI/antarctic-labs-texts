A multiomic investigation of human milk-derived extracellular vesicles in perinatal HIV and antiretroviral therapy exposure
Authors: Allison Kupsco, Jennifer Jao, Landon Myer, Elaine J Abrams, Fang Wang, Jalean Dominguez, Allison Zerbe, Elton Mukonda, Hlengiwe P Madlala, Sandisiwe Matyesini, Daniel T Robinson, Yunping Qiu, Irwin J Kurland, Haotian Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman milk (HM) is beneficial for early infant development, and breastfeeding is recommended globally for women with HIV (WHIV) on antiretroviral therapy (ART). HM has a high extracellular vesicle (EV) content, and distinct HM fractions may be enriched for unique small metabolites, lipids, oxylipins, and microRNAs, which may differ by HIV/ART exposure. Our objective was to characterize the metabolomic and miRNA differences between milk fractions and in relation to HIV/ART.MethodsHM was collected at 6 weeks postpartum from WHIV receiving ART and HIV-seronegative women in South Africa (N = 20/group). Milk was fractionated into whole milk, skim milk, and EVs, which we isolated from skim milk using size-exclusion chromatography. Widely targeted small metabolite (~ 550 metabolites) and lipid profiling (~ 1500 lipid species in 24 + lipid classes) were measured with Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography-Triple quadrapole Mass Spectrometry on whole milk, skim milk, and EVs. EV-miRNAs were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq. We used linear models to compare levels of lipids, metabolites, and miRNAs in HM EVs of WHIV versus HIV-seronegative women adjusting for covariates.ResultsThere were 658 lipids, 130 oxylipins, and 274 small metabolites detected across all HM fractions, with fewer compounds detected in HM EVs compared to whole and skim milk. HM EVs were enriched for fatty acid metabolism and metabolic products of linoleic acid (false discovery rate [FDR] p < 0.05). Triacylglycerols, TAG47:0 and TAG49:0, were lower in whole and skim milk of WHIV participants. Conversely, 18 small metabolites in HM EVs were associated with HIV status, but not in whole or skim milk. Of 187 detected EV-miRNAs, miR-148b-5p was significantly lower in WHIV, which was also validated by qPCR.ConclusionsThese preliminary results suggest that EVs may better represent differences in milk metabolites from HIV/ART exposure than whole milk or skim milk. EV contents altered in WHIV may be relevant for lipid, vitamin and energy metabolism, and metabolic health. Future research is warranted to assess the relationship between HM EV contents and child health.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov-ID: NCT 04991402.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s10020-026-01567-5.Keywords: Human milk, Extracellular vesicles, HIV, Metabolomics, Lipidomics, MiRNA

Background

In South Africa, women of reproductive age comprise over half of all persons with HIV (PWH) and almost 20% of all pregnant women are living with HIV (WHIV) (UNAIDS South Africa 2024; Dugdale et al. 2019). Although the risk of vertical transmission for WHIV on antiretroviral treatment (ART) is extremely low, there remain concerns regarding other potential health impacts for HIV-exposed uninfected children (CHEU). For instance, research suggests that CHEU have reduced early life growth compared to their HIV-unexposed uninfected (CHUU) counterparts in South Africa (Pillay et al. 2021; Roux et al. 2017, 2019; Tshiambara et al. n.d.; Nyemba et al. 2022), Nigeria, Uganda, Kenya, and Zambia (Jumare et al. 2019; Neary et al. 2022; Ndiaye et al. 2021; Lane et al. 2020; Rosala-Hallas et al. 2017). These differences may be programmed in infancy during a period where nutrition is paramount for long-term growth. During this period, this nutrition is primarily provided via human milk.

Breastfeeding is critical for child development (Meek et al. 2022; Horta and Lima 2019; Yan et al. 2014). The WHO recommends 6 months of exclusive breastfeeding (Meek et al. 2022), including for infants of WHIV in South Africa. It is estimated that 70–90% of women initially breastfeed, and 20–40% of women in South Africa exclusively breastfeed their infants for 6 months (Nieuwoudt et al. 1980).

Human milk (HM) is tailored to the nutritional requirements for infants. In addition to macronutrients, HM contains numerous bioactive compounds with essential roles in infant health, including oxylipins, hormones, oligosaccharides, poly-unsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), and other small metabolites (Baumgartel et al. 2023; Poulsen et al. 2022). These molecules may be packaged within HM extracellular vesicles (EVs), small lipid-bilayer bound vesicles produced primarily by mammary epithelial cells that transport bioactive cargoes (Alsaweed et al. 2016). Research suggests that HM EVs may survive the child’s acidic digestive and gastric environment to be taken up by intestinal cells (Ngu et al. 2023; Aguilar-Lozano et al. 2018; Khanam et al. 2023; Wolf et al. 2015; Luo et al. 2025), highlighting the potential for HM EV contents to confer cell-to-cell signaling between the mother and child.

HM EVs also include a wide variety of nucleic acids, including microRNAs (miRNAs), which are small non-coding RNAs that repress messenger RNA (mRNA) transcripts to alter transcriptional programming (Buratta et al. 2023). Studies show that HM EV-miRNAs regulate growth and metabolism (Ngu et al. 2023; Aguilar-Lozano et al. 2018; Khanam et al. 2023; Wolf et al. 2015). For instance, in rodents, miRNA-depleted milk EVs induce metabolic dysfunction to weaning pups, resulting in lower weight gain and fat accretion (Baier et al. 2014; Zhou and Zempleni 2019). In human studies, our previous work identified associations of HM EV-miRNAs with environmental contaminants, maternal BMI, and breastfeeding patterns in populations in Europe and the US (Kupsco et al. 2021, 2022; Holzhausen et al. 2023a, b; Cho et al. 2022).

Preliminary studies suggest that HM content, both contained within EVs and without, is altered in WHIV. For example, HM from WHIV on ART contained higher fat and lower carbohydrate levels compared with HIV-seronegative women (Fouché et al. 2016). Further, HM oligosaccharide 3’-sialyllactose was greater in WHIV (Niekerk et al. 2014). In a small study of 27 WHIV and 9 HIV-seronegative women, expression of 19 miRNAs was altered in HM EVs from WHIV (Zahoor et al. 2020). At 6 weeks postpartum, HM docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and eicosapentaenoic acid levels were significantly lower in WHIV in comparison to HIV uninfected mothers (Mabaya et al. 2022). Given the importance of these fatty acids for child growth and neurodevelopment (Mitguard et al. 2023), this suggests that HIV/ART exposure may adversely impact milk contents.

However, past studies of WHIV have only examined whole human milk, rather than focusing on the EV fractions, or only a single type of molecule (e.g. miRNAs or lipids). More data is needed on the bioactive milk components altered in WHIV and their relative milk fractions to protect developing children. Thus, we assessed the association of HIV/ART with lipids, metabolites, and miRNAs in HM EVs in a population of lactating women in South Africa. As free miRNAs are primarily degraded in the infant gut, we chose to focus our analysis on HM miRNAs encapsulated in EVs, rather than examine miRNAs from different fractions. We first compared the levels of these molecules between fractions, then examined the associations of these components with HIV-status.

Materials and methods

Study population

The Obesogenic oRigins of maternal and Child metabolic health Involving Dolutegravir (ORCHID) Study recruited pregnant WHIV and HIV-seronegative pregnant women from Cape Town, South Africa, as previously described (NCT 04991402) (Abrams et al. 2024). In brief, participants > 16yrs of age and ≤ 18 weeks gestational age, without self-reported diabetes or hypertensive disorders were enrolled between 2021–2023 from Midwife-Obstetric Units at the Gugulethu Community Health Centre. All WHIV received tenofovir + lamivudine + dolutegravir during pregnancy and postpartum. For this analysis, we selected at random 20 WHIV and 20 HIV-seronegative participants with milk available at 6 weeks postpartum who were nonsmokers, and matched for maternal BMI, parity, gestational diabetes status, and week postpartum. All women provided written consent at enrollment. All protocols were approved by the Faculty of Health Sciences Human Research Ethics Committee of the University of Cape Town (UCT-HREC 709/2020), and Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) of Columbia University Irving Medical Center (AAAT6112) and the Ann & Robert H. Lurie Children’s Hospital of Chicago (2021–4725).

Human milk collection

Participants and their infants returned to the study sites for a follow-up visit at 6 weeks postpartum to determine maternal and child health and to collect biosamples. Up to 20 mL of whole expression HM was collected with a manual breast milk pump into 50 mL sterilized tubes and divided into 4 mL aliquots, which were stored and shipped at −80 °C. Data on time of day, number of feeds per day, predominantly breastfed, and postpartum day were recorded. In our previous studies of HM EVs, even samples frozen at −80 °C prior to removal of cellular material have experienced very little cellular contamination (Kupsco et al. 2021; Holzhausen et al. 2023b).

HM processing and EV isolation

Three HM fractions were prepared for analyses: whole milk, skim milk, and skim milk EVs. HM was randomized across HIV status to reduce batch effects. To obtain skim milk, whole milk (1 mL) was thawed on ice and centrifuged at 200 g for 10 min to separate the lipid layer. A portion of skim milk was retained for metabolomics/lipidomics analyses. To isolate EVs, the remaining skim milk was centrifuged a further 3000 g for 15 min to pellet cells, cellular debris, and apoptotic bodies (Kupsco et al. 2021). EVs were isolated from the supernatant using size exclusion chromatography (SEC) (Izon Science, Medford, MA).

SEC was performed using an Automatic Fraction Collector (AFC) (Izon Science) to ensure precise and reproducible EV preparation. The internal scale and valve of the AFC was calibrated before each use. Izon qEVoriginal/70 SEC columns were loaded on to the AFC and flushed with 15 mL PBS that had been filtered through a 0.22 µm Amicon Ultra-2 filter (Sigma Aldrich) prior to use and after each sample. Columns were used a maximum of five times as recommended by the manufacturer. Skim milk (200–500 µL per sample) was loaded on to the column followed by 7 mL of filtered PBS. A void volume of 2.5 mL was discarded, followed by collection of five, 500 µL fractions for a total of 2.5 mL per sample. Samples were then concentrated to 150 µL using with the 0.22 µm Amicon Ultra-2 filter and stored at –20 °C for future assays.

HM EV quantification and validation

Validation of successful EV isolation was performed in accordance with Minimal Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles guidelines on a subset of samples from WHIV and seronegative women (Welsh et al. 2024). First we performed nanoparticle tracking analysis with the ViewSizer 3000 (Horiba Scientific) to estimate particle size range and concentration. Samples were diluted from 1:3000 in sterile PBS that had been filtered with a 0.22 µm filter to achieve an optimal number of particles per video. Specification included 30 frames per second for a 15 ms exposure period and at a gain of 30. We recorded 60, 30 s videos with 300 frames per video. The blue, green and red lasers were set at 210 mW, 12 mW and 8 mW, respectively. Samples were normalized to a blank PBS sample.

To assess the purity and morphology of EVs, two random study samples were sent for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) at the Weill Cornell Medical College Electron Microscopy & Histology Core as previously described (Comfort et al. 2021). Using at 100,000 × lens magnification, approximately 10 images were captured from 3 randomly selected grid areas per sample. We confirmed enrichment of EVs in four samples (two WHIV and two HIV-seronegative women) with the semi-quantitative Exo-Check Exosome Array (System Biosciences, Palo Alto, CA). The principle behind the Exo-Check Array is that each provided membrane is pre-printed with 12 spots that contain antibodies validated by SBI for eight known EV protein surface marker expression (e.g., CD81, CD63, EpCAM, ANXA5, FLOT1, ALIX, TSG101, and ICAM1) and one potential intracellular contaminant (GM130). Secondary detection is determined with HRP-conjugated antibodies provided with the kit. We have previously successfully used this array on similar milk samples (Kupsco et al. 2021). The array was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, protein levels were first quantified with a Qubit Flex using the protein BR assay (ThermoFisher Scientific). Next, 50 µg of protein was incubated with labeling reagent followed by blocking buffer. The sample was then included on the array overnight to ensure hybridization. The next day, the membrane was washed 3 times with wash buffer then developed with detection buffer for 5 min. Blots were imaged with the Azure 400 Visible Fluorescent Western Blot Imaging System (Azure Biosystems, Dublin, CA).

HM EV-miRNA isolation, library preparation, and sequencing

Small RNA was isolated from purified EVs using the plasma/serum circulating and exosomal RNA purification kit (Norgen Biotek, Ontario, Canada) and RNA yield and purity were assessed using the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 small RNA chip (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA). Small RNA libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Small RNA Library Prep kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA) following established protocols (Srinivasan et al. 2019). Libraries were cleaned and size selected on agarose gels before being pooled for sequencing. Final library quality and quantity was verified using the Bioanalyzer 2100 high-sensitivity DNA chip (Agilent). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq at the Yale University Genomics Core.

Bioinformatics analysis was conducted using the ExceRpt small RNA sequencing pipeline on the Genboree Workbench (http://genboree.org/site/exrna_toolset/) (Srinivasan et al. 2019; Giraldez et al. 2018). In brief, sequencing quality was assessed using FASTQC with only reads with phred scores > 30 retained. The ExceRpt pipeline was specified to perform adapter trimming and a minimum read length of 15 nucleotides with 0 mismatches allowed. The quality of our cell-free RNA was assessed according to External RNA Controls Consortium guidelines, including the proportion of rRNA reads, the proportion of reads mapping to the genome, and samples with < 100,000 transcriptome reads (Giraldez et al. 2018). Based on these criteria, two samples had < 100,000 transcriptome reads and a low transcriptome to genome ratio and were not included in the analysis. We used the trimmed mean of M (TMM) method from the EdgeR package to normalize miRNA counts data (Chen et al. 2014), which we converted into counts per million (CPM) for statistical analysis. Only miRNAs detectable in > 70% of samples were retained for analysis. Sequencing depth was sufficient for analysis (Supplemental Table S1).

qPCR validation

To provide orthogonal validation of sequencing-derived EV-miRNA findings, we measured expression of four candidate miRNAs (miR-148b-5p, miR-200a-3p, miR-30a-5p, and miR-30e-5p) qPCR) using miRCURY LNA miRNA PCR Assays (Qiagen). Assays were performed according to the manufacturers instructions in triplicate or duplicate. Replicates with coefficients of variation > 1% were excluded. Hsa-miR-16-5p was used for normalization. Relative expression was quantified using ΔCt values calculated as the difference between the target miRNA Ct and miR-16-5p Ct. Due to limited remaining samples, qPCR validation was restricted to four miRNAs selected from the sequencing results.

Whole milk, skim milk and EV metabolite and lipid profiling

We performed wide-targeted small metabolite (WTSM) screening (500 + small polar metabolites), widely targeted lipidomic profiling (WTLP ~ 1500 lipid species in 24 + lipid classes), and widely targeted free eicosanoid screening (WTE ~ 100 + oxylipins) as previously published with some modifications (Jao et al. 2022). In brief, 60 μL of sample was added with 240 μL of water containing stable isotope labeled small metabolites internal standards, 400 μL of methanol containing stable isotope labeled lipid internal standards, and 800 μL of chloroform. After vortexing for one minute, the samples were centrifuged. The top aqueous layer and bottom organic layer were pipetted into different vials and dried under gentle nitrogen flow. The top layer was redissolved in 100 μL of 20% methanol for WTSM analysis, and the bottom layer was redissolved in 100 μL of ethanol for WTLP analysis. Extraction of free oxylipins was performed via preconcentrating 60 μL sample in 940 μL of PBS then loading into a solid-phase extraction (SPE) column, eluted in 100% methanol, and dried before dissolving in 100 μL of methanol, pending for the WTE analysis. The assays were performed with Waters Aquity® Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography coupled with ABSciex® 6500+ QTrap Mass Spectrometer (UPLC-MS) in a Multiple Reaction Monitoring (MRM) mode with a gradient appropriate for each column. The ACE® pentafluorophenyl column was used for the WTSM assay, a Waters Charged Surface Hybrid Fluorophenyl column for the WTLP assay, and a Waters BEH Shield RP18 column for the WTE assays. MultiQuant® (ABSciex) software was used for data processing, including metabolite identification and quantification (relative to internal standards). A quality control (QC) sample containing a small portion of each individual sample was prepared for each assay and run multiple times. The relative standard derivations (RSD) were calculated and only analytes with a coefficient of variation (CV) less than 30% were included in the further data analysis.

Demographic and health assessments

Demographics and health status (e.g. gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and pre-pregnancy BMI), family and household composition, economic activity, HIV disease including history of HIV-related conditions and ART use including previous ART use, duration of current ART, and ART adherence were recorded using a combination of medical record review and locally established questionnaires (Abrams et al. 2024).

Statistical analysis

Whole milk, skim milk and EV metabolite, lipid, and oxylipin analysis

Metabolites with a CV greater than 30% were removed. Analytes which were below the limit of detection were replaced with a value equaling to the lowest detectable value in the dataset divided by square root of 2 and analytes detectable in > 70% of samples were retained for analysis. Most detected compounds were present in more than 75% of samples (Supplemental Table S2).

We used linear regression models to assess associations of HIV status with metabolites and lipids, with separate analyses for each fraction. Models were adjusted for maternal age (years), body mass index (BMI; kg/m2), and milk collection day postpartum. All metabolites and lipids were log2 transformed.

HM EV-miRNA analysis

QC indicated that two samples failed sequencing and an additional two samples were missing critical information on RNA input, resulting in a final sample size of 36 (N = 17 WHIV and 17 seronegative). Associations of EV-miRNAs with HIV status were assessed using the EdgeR package (Chen et al. 2014). First we computed a robust estimate of the negative binomial dispersion parameter, to account for outliers, then performed differential expression analysis with a generalized linear model followed by a likelihood ratio test for hypothesis testing. Models were adjusted for milk collection day postpartum and RNA extraction batch as precision variables. Additionally, we conducted a sensitivity analysis adjusting for other key variables known to influence HM-EV miRNA expression, including maternal BMI (kg/m2) and age (years) as above (Kupsco et al. 2021; Cho et al. 2022). We further conducted a sensitivity analysis removing participants with GDM. Pathway analysis was not conducted given only one miRNA passed FDR-significance. We assessed concordance between sequencing and qPCR measurements by correlating sequencing CPM and qPCR Ct values for each miRNA. Associations between HIV status and qPCR-derived miRNA expression were evaluated using linear regression models adjusted for batch, maternal age, maternal BMI, and milk collection day postpartum, consistent with the primary sequencing analyses. Non-amplifying miR-148b-5p samples were assigned the maximum cycle threshold (Ct) value.

All P-values were adjusted for FDR using the Benjamini–Hochberg method with significance determined at q < 0.05.

Results

Mean age was 25.1 ± 2 years for seronegative women and 26.4 ± 3.6 years for WHIV (Table 1). Most women were overweight with an average BMI of 29.4 and 29.9 kg/m2 for seronegative women and WHIV, respectively. There were two women per group with recorded GDM. HM was collected at 5.7 weeks postpartum. All infants were born full term. Among WHIV, 35% had a CD4 cell count > 500 cells/mm3 and 60% an HIV viral load < 50 copies/mL.

Associations of the milk metabolome and lipidome with maternal HIV status

In the EV fraction, 18 small metabolites were associated with HIV status (Fig. 3, Supplemental Table S3), including several carnitine esters and unsaturated fatty acids such as linoleic acid and γ-linolenic acid. Furthermore, ergocalciferol, retinol, and 6-phosphogluconate were greater in HM EVs from WHIV. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of fatty acid metabolism pathway (p = 0.03). These results appeared to be limited to EVs, suggesting potential differences in mechanisms regulating EV lipid cargo. Of the 18 metabolites, 17 were also detected in both whole and skim milk, yet did not differ by HIV status (Supplemental Figure S1).

In the skim fraction two triacylglycerols, TAG47:0 and TAG49:0, were lower in WHIV compared to HIV-seronegative participants (Fig. 3, Supplemental Table S4). The triple quadrupole-based method can aid in the identification of side chains of these TAGs. For TAG47:0, two product ions met the FDR threshold – TAG47:0–FA16:0 (fold change [FC] = 0.34, FDR = 0.01) and TAG47:0–FA17:0 (FC = 0.40, FDR = 0.04). A third fragment, TAG47:0–FA14:0, showed similar direction and magnitude of association (FC = 0.46, nominal p = 0.003), but did not pass FDR correction. Given that TAG47:0 necessarily comprises FA16:0, FA17:0, and FA14:0 side chains, the concordant decrease observed across all three fragments supports interpretation of the parent TAG47:0 species. As part of the same set of results, TAG49:0 showed evidence of multiple altered side-chain configurations. Product ions of TAG 49:0 lacking FA18:0 (TAG 49:0-FA18:0), lacking FA 16:0 (TAG 49:0-FA16:0) and lacking FA17:0 (TAG 49:0-FA17:0) were significantly lower in WHIV compared to HIV-seronegative participants (FC: 0.26–0.28, FDR: 0.01–0.04). Because several fatty acid combinations can sum to TAG49:0, these findings suggest that the lower abundance of TAG49:0 in WHIV is unlikely to reflect depletion of a single fatty acid side chain alone. For example, if FA 16:0 and FA 17:0 were part of the same TAG 49:0, then the remaining FA would have to be another FA 16:0 (not 18:0). Rather, the concordant reduction across multiple FA-loss product ions supports a broader difference in TAG49:0 composition.

These two triacylglycerols (TAG47:0 and TAG49:0) were not significantly different between WHIV and HIV-seronegative participants when measured in the whole milk or within the EV fraction after multiple testing corrections (Supplemental Figure S1).

No oxylipins were associated with maternal HIV status after multiple testing correction in any of the fractions (Supplemental Figure S1). However, among the top hits for whole milk oxylipins were products of different PUFAs, including linoleic acid (9(10)-EpOME and 12(13)-EpOME) and arachidonic acid (14(15)-EET, 8,9-DiHETE, 15-HETE,15(R)-HETE, and 11-HETE) (Supplemental Table S5). Overall, perinatal HIV/ART exposure was significantly associated with 18 small metabolites in EVs and two triacylglycerols in skim milk.

Associations of HM EV-miRNAs with maternal HIV Status

We identified 187 unique miRNAs detected in 70% of all EV samples, in WHIV and seronegative participants combined. miR-148a-3p had the greatest median expression in both WHIV and seronegative women (Supplemental Table S6). Of the 187 miRNAs, 13 were nominally associated with HIV-status (let-7i-5p, miR-10a-5p, miR-1260b, miR-147b, miR-148b-5p, miR-200a-3p, miR-26b-5p, miR-30a-5p, miR-30e-5p, miR-342-3p, miR-378e, miR-425-5p, miR-652-3p), of which miR-148b-5p reached FDR significance (Fig. 4, Supplemental Table S7). miR-148b-5p was moderately correlated with miR652-3p a, miR-26b-5p, and miR200a-3p (Supplemental Figure S2). From the miRNA with nominally significant association, the CPM varied from highly expressed (let-7i-5p, miR-200a-3p, miR-30a5p) to low expressed (miR-148b-5p, miR-378e, miR-1260b, miR147b) (Supplemental Figure S3).

Additional model adjustment for maternal age and BMI at milk collection did not meaningfully change associations with miRNAs that achieved significance in our main models (Supplemental Figure S4). Exclusion of four participants with GDM slightly attenuated our effect estimates, though the direction of the effect remained consistent for all associations (Supplemental Figure S4). Ten miRNAs were significant in sensitivity analyses but not in our main analyses (Supplemental Figure S5). Most of these were revealed by the exclusion of participants with GDM. Overall, though many miRNAs had high expression in our samples, one miRNA, miR-148b-5p was significantly associated with HIV-status. This did not change in our sensitivity analyses.

qPCR measurements demonstrated strong concordance with sequencing data for all four tested miRNAs. Expected inverse correlations were observed between qPCR Ct values and sequencing CPM values for all four evaluated miRNAs, including miR-148b-5p, miR-30a-5p, miR-30e-5p, and miR-200a-3p (Supplemental Figure S6). In linear models adjusted for batch, maternal age, maternal BMI, and milk collection day postpartum, HIV status was associated with higher ΔCt values for miR-148b-5p (β = 2.82, 95% CI: 0.75, 4.89; p = 0.009) and miR-30e-5p (β = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.10, 1.50; p = 0.027) (Supplemental Table S8), indicating lower expression among women with HIV. The direction of these associations was consistent with the sequencing findings.

Discussion

Human milk contains a plethora of bioactive compounds that shape long-term child health. Any and longer duration of breastfeeding are associated with lower infant mortality, lower risk of obesity, and lower diabetes risk through adolescence and adulthood (Meek et al. 2022; Horta and Lima 2019; Yan et al. 2014). However, maternal HIV/ART has the potential to alter milk components that may influence later growth and development. The goal of this study was to explore differences in small metabolites, lipids, and oxylipins between milk fractions and to further compare differences in these components and EV-miRNAs between WHIV and seronegative women. Overall, we observed that the molecular cargo of HM differed between milk fractions and that the lipid and metabolite landscape differed in HM from WHIV from South Africa in a compartment specific manner. We further identified miR-148b-5p in HM EV-miRNAs that was altered in WHIV.

The importance of the HM EV metabolome to early life growth and development has been demonstrated in several prior studies (Jiang et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2023; Meng et al. 2023). For example, long chain PUFAs, including linoleic acid, and amino acids have been identified in HM EVs and are known to play important roles in growth and development (Jiang et al. 2024; Meng et al. 2023). This is consistent with our observations that the HM EVs were enriched in metabolic products of linoleic acid compared to whole milk and skim milk. Notably, we also found that several unsaturated fatty acids, including linoleic acid and γ-linolenic acid, differed in HM EVs of WHIV. These findings suggest that maternal HIV/ART may be associated with differences in HM EV lipid composition, although the implications for infant growth, inflammation, immune development, or HIV transmission remain unknown.

In addition to unsaturated fatty acids, we also observed that two triacylglycerols, TAG47:0 and TAG49:0, were altered in the non-EV components of HM. Several other metabolites, including those involved in energy production and micronutrient pathways, were altered in the HM EVs of WHIV and methylmalonate, ergocalciferol, retinol, and 6-phosphogluconate were greater in HM EVs from WHIV. Prior studies have implicated these metabolites in diverse metabolic and immune-related processes in other biological contexts (Daneshmandi et al. 2026; Long et al. 2025; Li et al. 2022; Prairie et al. 2021). However, this is among the first comprehensive investigation of metabolomic variation in the context of WHIV and the functional and biological implications of these differences remain unclear. Further work is needed to establish whether the observed lipid alterations have clinically meaningful downstream effects. Future studies that integrate HM-EV metabolomics with infant growth and immune outcomes will be essential to clarify whether these metabolomic differences contribute to altered infant health trajectories.

Given the vulnerability of miRNAs to degradation when not encapsulated within EVs and the importance of HM EV-miRNAs for infant health (Jiang et al. 2021), we focused our analysis on HM EV-miRNAs. Notably, the association for miR-148b-5p, the primary finding identified in the sequencing analysis, was independently replicated by qPCR. Comparable to previous studies of HM EVs, miR-148a-3p was the dominant miRNA expressed in our samples (Herwijnen et al. 2018; Melnik et al. 2021). Previous work suggests that lower HM EV miR-148a-3p was associated with GDM, greater maternal overweight/obesity, and infant adiposity (Shah et al. 2021, 2022). Less is known about miR-148b-5p, which was significantly associated with HIV status in the present analysis. Studies have suggested that miR-148a-5p may be involved in metabolic and cellular regulatory pathways (Klerk et al. 2024; Kawagoe et al. 2020; Shu et al. 2019), but further work is needed to define the roles of miR-148a-3p and miR-148a-5p in the context of infant growth, HIV, and potential functional effects on infant health.

Little is known about miRNAs in lactating women with HIV. To our knowledge, only one other study to date has profiled HM miRNA levels in WHIV. Zahoor et al. conducted a study of 17 WHIV on ART and 9 seronegative controls from Nigeria, and found 15 HM EV-miRNAs significantly lower in WHIV and 18 miRNAs significantly greater (Zahoor et al. 2020). Of these, miR-30a-5p was identified as nominally significant in our analysis, albeit associated with HIV status in the opposite direction. The discrepancies in our findings may be explained by several factors. For instance, HM samples were collected at one week postpartum in comparison to six weeks postpartum in the present analysis. The composition of HM changes throughout lactation, which may partly explain these findings. Furthermore, Zahoor et al., did not disclose the ART regimen in their analysis, so differences in ART exposure may be driving observed differences. Finally, this study used a different EV isolation method and method of miRNA quantification. Previous work also identified differential expression of serum EV-miRNAs in people with HIV in comparison to seronegative controls (Biswas et al. 2019); however, none of these miRNAs were significantly altered in our analyses. Furthermore, several miRNAs are thought to play a role in HIV-infection (Morando et al. 2024), yet these did not differ between WHIV and seronegative women in the present study. Hence, additional studies on HM EV miRNAs in WHIV are warranted to confirm our results.

Our study has several strengths. We used a well-designed cohort with women following the same ART from South Africa. Notably, we measured small metabolites, lipids, and oxylipins from different milk fractions in WHIV and seronegative controls. Finally, this is one of the first multi-omic investigations of HM in WHIV. As such we were able to identify bioactive components that may be most relevant for breastfeeding WHIV.

This study was limited by a relatively small sample size, which may have increased the potential for false discoveries despite FDR correction. However, we were able to match participants on key characteristics, reducing variability between groups, and identified several biologically coherent patterns across multiple analytes and omics layers despite the limited sample size. Future studies will expand to greater sample sizes and include independent validation cohorts where possible. Additionally, we did not have HIV-1 mRNA levels in our milk samples. However, our previous research suggests that WHIV on ART in this study population have primarily nondetectable levels of HIV-1 in their breast milk (Abrams et al. 2025). Our EV protocol may have resulted in some contamination of cellular material as we were unable to remove cells prior to freezing. These findings may not be generalizable to other populations, given that the ORCHID cohort is entirely of black African or Mixed-race women in Cape Town, South Africa. Given our study design, we were unable to include an HIV-only exposure group that would have allowed us to disentangle the impacts of HIV infection from maternal ART regimen on HM contents. However, given the ubiquity of ART, this is not likely under a real-world exposure scenario today. Given the exploratory nature of the study, we were not able to perform functional validation of our findings. Future studies would benefit from in vitro work to determine the downstream target of these compounds in intestinal cells or in HIV-infected T-cells. In vivo rodent feeding models supplemented or depleted of these would also confirm the functional relevance of our results. However, we were able to validate our sequencing results with qPCR. Furthermore, integrated analyses of metabolomic and miRNA results could provide greater insight with larger sample sizes. Nonetheless, the goal of this pilot study was to identify potential molecular signatures and candidate biomarkers that could be indicative of the metabolic impacts of HIV/ART on HM.

Conclusions

In summary, we identified distinct landscapes of metabolites and lipids between whole milk, skim milk, and milk EV fractions. Interestingly differences in milk contents between WHIV and seronegative women were most pronounced in EVs, suggesting that EV biomarkers may better capture physiological differences in milk with HIV/ART exposure. We further observed several miRNAs were lower in the EV fraction of milk from WHIV. Taken together, our findings highlight the potential for HM EVs to serve as biomarkers reflecting HIV/ART. Future work will determine if these findings lead to alterations in child growth and metabolic health.
