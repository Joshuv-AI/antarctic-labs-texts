Rituximab-first versus eltrombopag monotherapy in pediatric persistent/chronic immune refractory thrombocytopenia after first-line therapy: a prospective multicenter cohort study
Authors: Jingyao Ma, Zhifa Wang, Juntao Ouyang, Zheyan Lin, Lingling Fu, Zhenping Chen, Yan Liu, Lijuan Wang, Xi Lin, Xiaoling Cheng, Yu Hu, Shuyue Dong, Jinxi Meng, Jie Ma, Runhui Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImmune thrombocytopenia (ITP) in children is an autoimmune disorder in which a significant subset develops persistent or chronic disease requiring second-line therapies, yet the optimal sequencing of agents such as rituximab (RTX) and eltrombopag (ELT) to achieve sustained remission off-treatment remains (SRoT) undefined.ObjectivesThis study assessed whether RTX-first sequential therapy outperforms upfront ELT monotherapy in achieving durable remission for pediatric persistent/chronic ITP (p/cITP) after first-line failure.MethodsThis nonrandomized multicenter prospective cohort study enrolled 55 children (April 2020 to July 2023) with refractory p/cITP until first-line therapy. They were assigned to RTX-first (RfG, n = 35: single RTX dose, then ELT for nonresponders) or ELT monotherapy (ELT-first group [EfG], n = 20) groups, with 12-month follow-up. Primary endpoints were SRoT and treatment duration (TD), and secondary endpoints were response rate and safety. The study is registered at ChiCTR2100048162.ResultsAmong the 55 enrolled children (RfG = 35, EfG = 20), RfG had superior SRoT (30%; 95% CI, 15.6%-48.7%) than ELT-monotherapy group (0%; 95% CI, 0%-18.7%; P = .016), although 12-month remission rates were comparable (84.8% vs 87.5%; P = .804). RfG had a shorter TD (median 18 vs 34 weeks; P = .009), even in severe cases (baseline platelets 20 ×109/L vs 29 ×109/L; P = .034). Early ELT (≤96 days) in RfG nonresponders improved outcomes (P = .024). Both treatments had favorable safety (grade 1-2 adverse events, <15%).ConclusionsRTX-first treatment yields a better SRoT and shorter TD than ELT monotherapy for pediatric p/cITP, with comparable remission and safety.

Introduction

Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) is an autoimmune disorder characterized by isolated thrombocytopenia (platelet count < 100 × 109/L) and an increased bleeding risk [1,2]. Although 70% to 80% of pediatric patients with ITP achieve remission with first-line therapies (corticosteroids or intravenous immunoglobulin [IVIg]), approximately 20% to 30% of them progress to persistent or chronic ITP (p/cITP), defined as thrombocytopenia lasting ≥3 months (persistent) or ≥12 months (chronic) [2]. These patients need second-line therapies that balance sustained efficacy, safety, and treatment burden.

Current second-line options include rituximab (RTX), a CD20-targeting monoclonal antibody that depletes autoreactive B cells and can induce durable remission in 20% to 40% of pediatric patients [3], and eltrombopag (ELT), a thrombopoietin (TPO) receptor agonist (RA) that rapidly boosts platelet production but requires prolonged administration to maintain a response [3]. RTX offers potential long-term remission; however, its delayed onset of action (4-8 weeks) contrasts with the rapid platelet elevation capacity of ELT (1-2 weeks). Nonetheless, ELT discontinuation frequently leads to platelet decline, necessitating extended treatment durations that increase the associated costs and toxicity risks [4,5]. The optimal sequence of these therapies remains undefined. We hypothesized that an RTX-first strategy, with ELT reserved for nonresponders, would yield higher rates of sustained remission off-treatment (SRoT) than initial ELT monotherapy in pediatric p/cITP. To test this hypothesis, we conducted a prospective multicenter cohort study comparing SRoT (platelet count, ≥30 × 109/L for ≥3 months posttreatment cessation), treatment duration (TD), and early response rate (ERR) and durable response rate (DRR). This study supports the need for the refinement of second-line ITP management in children, particularly in the context of prioritizing treatment discontinuation goals.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective multicenter cohort study compared 2 second-line strategies for pediatric p/cITP refractory to corticosteroids and/or IVIg—(1) the RTX-first group (RfG): escalating strategy with RTX induction followed by ELT for nonresponders; (2) the ELT-monotherapy group (EmG): ELT monotherapy. This study was conducted at 3 tertiary hospitals in China from April 2020 to June 2023 and included a 12-month follow-up period concluding in June 2024. The protocol was approved by the institutional review board of Beijing Children’s Hospital (Ethics Approval No. [2021]-E-066-Y) as the lead center, with reciprocal ethical clearance obtained from participating institutions. In full compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki principles, written informed consent was secured from all legal guardians before enrollment, with age-appropriate assent procedures implemented for participants of ≥8 years. This trial was prospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (identifier: ChiCTR2100048162).

Adverse events (AEs) were systematically recorded, including infusion reactions (fever and rash), infection, hematologic toxicity (neutropenia, anemia, and thrombocytosis), and hepatic dysfunction (alanine aminotransferase and/or bilirubin elevation). AEs were graded per the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0 [6].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) male and female patients aged 1 to 14 years diagnosed with primary ITP according to the 2021 Adapted Guideline for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Primary Immune Thrombocytopenia for Chinese Children [2]; (2) patients without a sustained response to first-line treatment (glucocorticoids and/or IVIGs); (3) patients with a continuous platelet count of <30 × 109/L with bleeding requiring second-line treatment; (4) patients who underwent at least 2 independent tests (interval, >7 days) confirming platelet counts of <30 × 109/L, with peripheral blood smears excluding interfering factors, such as microangiopathic hemolysis and platelet aggregation (suspected cases required confirmation through multidisciplinary consultation); and (5) patients who adhered to the treatment and follow-up periods.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with evidence of secondary thrombocytopenia with underlying causes, including systemic lupus erythematosus or other systemic autoimmune disease, congenital or inherited platelet disorders (eg., Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome or Bernard-Soulier syndrome), and bone marrow failure syndromes such as aplastic anemia; (2) previous administration of any second-line therapy for ITP, including RTX, TPO-RAs (eg, ELT and romiplostim), immunosuppressants (eg, cyclosporine or mycophenolate mofetil), or splenectomy; and (3) refusal or inability of the patient or family to provide informed consent or assent.

Grouping and interventions

Patients were stratified into 2 cohorts according to joint clinician-patient/guardian treatment decisions. Clinicians comprehensively explained the efficacy, safety, and monitoring requirements for both options. Final treatment allocations emerged from collaborative clinician-family deliberations.1)The RfG: Patients in this group received treatment following an escalating strategy from single-dose RTX (375 mg/m2, maximum dose 600 mg, given once). If no response or a loss of response was observed after RTX initiation, ELT was initiated, with dosage adjustments performed per the ELT manufacturer’s directions, previous studies, and the state of the disease. Outcomes

The primary endpoint of this study was the comparison of SRoT and TD between the RfG and EmG groups. The secondary endpoints encompassed the comparison of initial response rate (IRR), ERR, DRR, and remission rates (RRs) between the RfG and EmG groups. Additionally, we examined the differences in demographic and laboratory indicators between patients who responded to RTX at 3 months and those who did not within the RfG group. Furthermore, we analyzed the interval from RTX to ELT administration among patients in the RfG group who experienced RTX failure within 6 to 12 weeks, stratified by their response rates at 6 and 12 months.

Data collection

Data extracted from electronic medical records included demographics, disease duration, prior therapies, baseline platelet counts, bleeding scores, and immunological profiles (eg, antinuclear antibodies). Platelet monitoring was conducted as follows: weekly (month 1), biweekly (months 1 to 2), and monthly (months 3 to 12).

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed using SPSS, version 26.0 (IBM). Continuous variables are expressed as the mean baseline platelet counts, bleeding scores, and immunological profiles (eg, median [IQR]) for nonnormally distributed data, compared via a t-test or Mann-Whitney U-test analyses, performed using SPSS, version 26.0. Continuous variables are expressed as the mean baseline platelet counts, bleeding scores, and immunological values, analyzed using Fisher exact test. Survival curves for SRoT were generated via the Kaplan-Meier analysis using SPSS v26.0. A continuous P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Between April 2020 and July 2023, 55 pediatric patients with corticosteroid/IVIg-refractory p/cITP were enrolled for second-line therapy (Figure 1, Table 1). Among these patients, 35 in the RfG group initially received RTX, while 20 in the EmG group received ELT. Six patients (RfG, 2; EmG, 4) were excluded as they were lost to follow-up. In the RfG cohort, 23 of 33 patients (69.7%) required subsequent ELT therapy following RTX nonresponse or relapse. Among these 23 patients, 18 (78.3%) achieved a response, while 5 (21.7%) remained nonresponsive. By the end of the follow-up period, 28 of the 33 RfG patients (84.8%) achieved remission, with 10 patients (30.3%; 95% CI, 15.6%-48.7%) maintaining protocol-defined SRoT. The baseline characteristics were comparable between the groups in terms of age, sex, disease duration, and prior therapies (all P > .05). However, the RfG group exhibited significantly lower pretreatment platelet counts (median, 20 × 109/L; IQR, 12-27 × 109/L) than the EmG group (median, 29 × 109/L; IQR, 18-33 × 109/L; P = .034). Bleeding severity scores also differed significantly at baseline (RfG: median, 3; IQR, 2-4; EmG: median, 2; IQR, 1-3; P = .005) (Table 1).

Treatment response

At the 1-month follow-up, the IRR was significantly higher in the EmG than that in the RfG (85.0% vs 48.6%; P = .007), with patients in the EmG demonstrating faster platelet count elevation (median time to response [TTR], 14 days [IQR, 7-21 days] vs 28 days [IQR, 14-90 days]; P = .005).

By month 3, a trend toward higher early response rates persisted in the EmG (88.9% vs 58.8%; P = .058), although significance was not reached. This attenuation may reflect therapeutic crossover effects, as 23 RfG nonresponders received salvage ELT therapy at a median interval of 96 days (range, 42–208) post-RTX initiation.

At 6 months, DR rates (DRRs) showed no significant intergroup difference (RfG: 78.8% vs EmG: 88.2%; P = .410), likely attributable to ELT supplementation in 19 of the 23 (82.6%) RTX-resistant RfG patients. Discontinuation

Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed a significantly higher 12-month cumulative SRoT rate in the RfG vs the EmG (30.3% [10/33; 95% CI, 15.6%-48.7%] vs 0% [0/16; 95% CI, 0%-18.7%]; P = .016) (Figure 3), attributable to a shorter TD in the RfG. Among 23 RTX-refractory patients who crossed over to ELT, the median time to ELT initiation post-RTX failure was 96 days (range, 42-208 days), with 19 of 23 patients (82.6%) achieving a subsequent platelet response. Per protocol–defined SRoT criteria (platelet ≥ 30 × 109/L for ≥ 3 months postcessation), the 12-month treatment discontinuation rate precisely matched the final SRoT rate (30% in RfG; 0% in EmG), confirming that all patients who discontinued treatment maintained durable remission.Figure 3Propotion of patients remaing on eltrombopag treatment. Predictors of ER and SRoT

Our analysis revealed that RTX as a second-line strategy reduced the continuous TD in pediatric ITP, achieving 30.3% (95% CI, 15.6%-48.7%) SRoT vs 0% (95% CI, 0%-18.7%) in EmG (P = .016). The ERR significantly predicted the need for subsequent ELT escalation (P < .001). To identify actionable biomarkers for timely ELT initiation, we stratified patients by the 3-month ERR status and evaluated their clinical/immunological correlates. Elevated baseline levels of the following immunological markers were associated with higher 3-month ERR in RTX-treated patients. (1) humoral immunity: elevated serum IgA; (2) lymphocyte subsets: total lymphocytes, T lymphocytes (CD3+), helper T cells (CD3+CD4+), cytotoxic T cells (CD3+CD8+), B lymphocytes (CD19+), and TCR-αβ+ T cells; (3) cellular immunity imbalance: reduced natural killer (NK) cell counts (CD16+CD56+). Safety analysis

Both therapeutic regimens demonstrated favorable safety profiles throughout the 12-month study, with all AEs categorized as grade 1 to 2 per CTCAE, version 5.0. In the RfG (n = 35), infusion-related reactions were the most common AE (14.3%, 5/35), predominantly mild (grade 1, 3 cases; grade 2, 2 cases), managed symptomatically without treatment interruption. Hematologic toxicity included transient neutropenia (5.7%, 2/35) and anemia (2.9%, 1/35), all grade 1 and self-resolving. Proactively, all RfG patients received low-dose IVIg (0.2-0.3 g/kg monthly, 6× doses) post-RTX to mitigate hypogammaglobulinemia risk. No cases of persistent hypogammaglobulinemia (IgG < 4 g/L) or related infections occurred during the follow-up. The EmG (n = 20) exhibited a higher incidence of thrombocytosis (platelet > 450 × 109/L: 10.0% vs 2.9%, P = 0.27), with two grade 2 cases requiring dose reduction, and mild bilirubin elevation (≥1.5× upper limit of normal level: 10.0% vs 2.9%, = 0.27). Crossgroup analyses revealed comparable anemia (5.0% vs 2.9%; P = .67) and infection rates (15.0% vs 5.7%; P = .24), with no grade of ≥3 AEs or TDs in either group. Overall, the AE incidence remained statistically equivalent (RfG: 22.9% vs EmG: 25.0%; P = 0.85), confirming comparable tolerability between strategies.

Discussion

This prospective multicenter study provides critical insights into optimizing second-line therapy for pediatric p/cITP, demonstrating that an RTX escalation strategy achieves comparable long-term RRs with ELT monotherapy (84.8% vs 87.5%), while enabling 30% of patients to attain SRoT, a milestone unmet by upfront TPO-RA monotherapy. These findings challenge the current preference for TPO-RAs as second-line therapy and redefine success in ITP management by prioritizing durable immune modulation over transient platelet elevation. Although the ASH 2019 guidelines [1] favor TPO-RAs over rituximab per adult-centric evidence, our findings highlight a critical pediatric-specific consideration. The 30% SRoT rate with RTX-first strategy addresses the unique priority in pediatric ITP management—minimizing long-term drug exposure during critical developmental stages. This finding contrasts with TPO-RA’s universal dependency pattern observed in our cohort, despite comparable 12-month response rates. Thus, the current guideline recommendations should be interpreted in the context of population-specific goals. For pediatric patients in whom treatment discontinuation is prioritized, our data suggest that the RTX-first approach may provide superior long-term outcomes despite an initial slower platelet response.

Our findings align with prior studies demonstrating a delayed onset of action with RTX vs TPO-RAs. The median TTR for RTX was 28 days (IQR, 14-90 days) in our cohort, consistent with the 5.5-week TTR reported in pediatric ITP studies [[7], [8], [9]], whereas ELT achieved rapid platelet elevation within 14 days (IQR, 7-21 days), mirroring its known thrombopoietic mechanism via direct megakaryocyte stimulation [10]. Notably, despite ELT’s early advantage in IRR (85.0% vs 48.6%; P = .007), no significant differences emerged in ERR at 3 months (88.9% vs 58.8%; P = .058), DRR at 6 months (88.2% vs 78.8%; P = .410), or RRs at 12 months (87.5% vs 84.8%; P = .804) between the groups. This finding contrasts with those of comparable initial efficacy between TPO-RAs and RTX by Stolz et al. [11], potentially reflecting differences in crossover protocols; our RTX nonresponders received timely ELT rescue (median transition, 96 days post-RTX; range, 42-208 days), which attenuated between-group divergence at later endpoints.

The RTX-first strategy demonstrated superior therapeutic sustainability in pediatric p/cITP, with 30% achieving SRoT vs 0% in the ELT group (P = .016), alongside a significantly shorter TD. This advantage arises from RTX’s dual-phase immunomodulation: initial CD20+ B cell depletion disrupts antiplatelet antibody production, and subsequent immune reconstitution restores regulatory T cell (Treg) populations, fostering immune tolerance [12,13]. Although a single dose of RTX effectively depletes CD20+ B cells, approximately 70% of patients require sequential ELT therapy, suggesting that p/cITP may involve multiple pathological mechanisms. No early responders to RTX exhibited increased baseline NK cell counts (P = .031), indicating that pretreatment immune status may influence the efficacy of B cell–targeted therapy. Early combination with TPO-RAs such as ELT, when initiated selectively in RTX nonresponders, may achieve synergistic effects by promoting platelet production and suppressing autoimmunity via the JAK2/STAT5 pathway [14,15], unimpeded by autoantibody interference, a mechanism enabled by prior B cell clearance. If the early response to RTX is insufficient, the initiation of ELT is anticipated to build upon the immunomodulatory effects of RTX. With the autoimmune attack mitigated by RTX, ELT can then effectively stimulate platelet production through the TPO pathway without the interference of antiplatelet antibodies. This sequential approach enables a more controlled and targeted therapy, optimizing the benefits of both agents while minimizing potential adverse effects. This strategy aligns with the observed trend of enhanced efficacy in adult studies administered combination regimens (eg, RTX + TPO-RA) [16]. While international guidelines advocate observation for mild bleeding, Chinese clinical practice emphasizes early intervention, reflecting (1) disproportionate parental concern regarding potential bleeding risks and (2) institutional restrictions on group activities for thrombocytopenic children. This disparity underscores the need for culturally adapted treatment protocols.

Both agents exhibited favorable safety profiles: RTX-associated neutropenia (5.7%) and infusion reactions (14.3%) were transient (grades 1 to 2), whereas ELT-induced thrombocytosis (10.0%) and bilirubin elevation (10.0%) resolved with dose adjustment, aligning with pediatric safety benchmarks [17,18]. All patients in the RTX group received scheduled low-dose IVIg as immunoglobulin replacement prophylaxis following RTX. No persistent hypogammaglobulinemia or related infections were observed over the 12 months of follow-up. The calibrated sequencing of immunomodulation followed by thrombopoietic support exemplifies precision medicine, optimizing efficacy without compromising safety in pediatric ITP management. Notably, RTX’s shorter TD and reduced cumulative exposure align with cost-effective care principles, potentially alleviating familial anxiety, a critical psychosocial consideration in pediatric management.

Despite significantly lower baseline platelet counts in the RTX group (median, 20 × 109/L vs 29 × 109/L; P = .034), a marker of disease severity, comparable 12-month RRs underscore RTX’s efficacy in high-risk phenotypes. This resilience, coupled with optimized timing thresholds (eg, ELT initiation within 13.9 weeks improved 12-month complete RRs vs 19.6 weeks; P < .05) [5,17], positions RTX-first sequencing as a versatile strategy for heterogeneous ITP populations.

Building on our prior stepwise strategy (HDD → RTX → ELT) [19], this study positions RTX-first sequencing as a cost-effective approach for pediatric ITP. By integrating the economic validations by Zhang et al. [20] and safety data by Fu et al. [19], we propose reserving ELT for RTX nonresponders—a paradigm that minimizes lifelong therapy burdens while aligning with resource-conscious healthcare systems.

Expanding on RTX’s immunomodulatory mechanism, we identified baseline immunological profiles predictive of ERR. Patients with elevated IgA, total/CD4+/CD8+ T lymphocytes, and reduced NK cell counts exhibited superior ERRs (P < .05), suggesting a B cell–dominant pathophysiology amenable to RTX. This finding aligns with the demonstration of Treg restoration post-RTX by Stasi et al. [13], reinforcing that pretreatment immune signatures can stratify candidates for RTX vs upfront TPO-RA therapy. These biomarkers enable timely escalation to ELT in predicted RTX nonresponders, optimizing resource allocation, a critical advantage in resource-constrained settings.

In line with our phased strategy, 7 patients (RfG, 5; EmG, 2) transitioned to avatrombopag after TPO-RA failure, achieving platelet stabilization without splenectomy. This result mirrors real-world evidence reported by Wang et al. [18], in which avatrombopag salvaged 68% of pediatric ELT nonresponders, underscoring the viability of sequential TPO-RA use. Although not a primary endpoint, this observation highlights the necessity of maintaining therapeutic agility in chronic ITP management.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

This study advances pediatric ITP research through its prospective multicenter design, enabling external validity by capturing diverse treatment responses across geographical and institutional settings. The 12-month longitudinal follow-up period enables the robust evaluation of immediate outcomes (eg, IRR) and sustained endpoints, such as SRoT and TD. We provide clinically actionable evidence to inform guideline updates by directly comparing RTX-first escalation with ELT-monotherapy strategies.

However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, as a nonrandomized cohort study, there is potential for selection bias despite careful adjustment and stratification. The nonrandomized assignment may have permitted unmeasured confounding factors to influence patient selection and treatment outcomes; therefore, our findings should be interpreted with caution and confirmed in future randomized controlled trials. In addition, the modest sample size (35 patients in the RTX group; 20 in the ELT group) may have limited statistical power for detecting differences in secondary endpoints, such as infection rates. Furthermore, the exclusive focus on Chinese populations raises questions regarding generalizability to other ethnic groups with distinct genetic or immunological profiles. Finally, the absence of cost-effectiveness analyses and patient-reported quality-of-life metrics precludes a comprehensive assessment of societal and individual burdens.

Future research should prioritize large-scale multinational trials validating RTX-first efficacy in multiethnic cohorts to address these gaps. Health economic analyses are urgently needed to quantify the cost-to-benefit ratio of achieving SRoT vs chronic TPO-RA dependency. Integrating our identified biomarkers (eg, IgA and NK cell levels) with multiomics profiling could enable personalized sequencing algorithms, further optimizing treatment precision.

Conclusion

In this study, the RTX-first strategy achieved superior SRoT than the ELT monotherapy, although the 12-month RRs were comparable. Furthermore, RTX-first strategy resulted in shorter TDs, even in more severe cases. Supported by predictive biomarkers (elevated IgA and reduced NK cells), this approach warrants prioritization in guidelines despite the need for validation in diverse populations.
