Clinical and functional connectivity changes following the stanford accelerated intelligent neuromodulation therapy (SAINT) in bipolar I depression: A pilot study
Authors: Amy Bichlmeier, Jennifer E Siegel-Ramsay, Kevin Li, Caitlin DuPont, Alexa Comfort, Irving Reti, Brandon Bentzley, Nolan Williams, Andrew D Geoly, Peter P Zandi, Jorge RC Almeida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndividuals with bipolar disorder spend significantly more time depressed than manic, and treatment alternatives for treatment-resistant patients are greatly needed. Although the Stanford Accelerated Intelligent Neuromodulation Therapy (SAINT) intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS) protocol has shown efficacy in unipolar depression, its utility in bipolar depression remains largely unexplored. This study aims to assess the feasibility and safety of SAINT in patients with bipolar I depression that have failed to respond to a first-line treatment and examine underlying neural mechanisms of treatment response.MethodsTen participants with bipolar I disorder underwent a modified SAINT protocol in this open-label study. Depressive and manic symptoms were assessed throughout. Personalized left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) stimulation targets exhibiting negative resting-state connectivity with the subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sgACC) were generated using resting-state functional MRI. We then investigated changes in resting-state (DLPFC and sgACC) seed-to-whole brain connectivity, and its relationship to treatment response.ResultsImmediately post-treatment, depressive symptoms decreased by an average of 56.7 (SD=19.7%)%. Following treatment, participants exhibited increased connectivity between sgACC and two bilateral frontal pole clusters. Greater reductions in depressive severity were significantly associated with increased negative connectivity between left DLPFC target seed and four clusters in prefrontal and temporal cortices.ConclusionSAINT iTBS was well-tolerated in a bipolar I depression sample and associated with sgACC functional connectivity changes. Increased negative connectivity between the DLPFC target and prefrontal/temporal cortex was associated with treatment response. Larger, sham-controlled studies are needed to confirm these findings and explore predictors of treatment response in bipolar depression.Keywords: Bipolar disorder, Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), Intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS), Functional connectivity, Treatment-resistant depression

Introduction

Bipolar disorder is a severe mental illness with a lifetime prevalence of 4.4% in the United States [1]. Although the condition is differentiated from unipolar depression by the presence of mania, individuals with bipolar disorder spend significantly more time in depressive compared to manic/hypomanic mood states [2]. Furthermore, the severity of these depressive symptoms has a strong association with multiple domains of functional impairment (e.g., loss of employment, strained interpersonal relationships [3], [4]). First-line bipolar depression medications often cause severe side effects such as emotional blunting and weight gain [5], [6], and over 40% of patients require three or more concurrent medications [7]. Even with pharmacological options, a quarter of patients still meet criteria for treatment-resistant depression [8], [9]. This underscores the critical need for alternative therapeutic approaches, such as neuromodulation, to address the significant gaps in current standard care.

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation in depression

For several decades, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), a non-invasive neuromodulation technique that activates or inhibits brain activity depending on the frequency, has had approval from the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of unipolar depression. Although research supports the efficacy of rTMS when targeting dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) in individuals with unipolar depression, our understanding of its clinical application in bipolar depression is complicated by significant methodological and treatment heterogeneity (e.g., differing stimulation sites, target localization methods, and stimulation frequencies). Furthermore, studies of the underlying neurophysiological mechanisms following TMS treatment for bipolar depression are scant. A recent systematic review attempting to review neuroimaging correlates of treatment response following TMS in bipolar depression only found four studies that utilized functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) to analyze changes, all of which had mixed unipolar and bipolar cohorts [10]. Two of these mixed-sample studies found treatment response was correlated to functional connectivity changes. First, in a 4-week rTMS protocol stimulating the left DLPFC (bipolar n = 2, unipolar n = 24), reduction in depression symptoms was significantly correlated with changes in salience network connectivity, particularly in the prefrontal cortex [11]. Second, a protocol stimulating dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (DMPFC) found significant associations between treatment response and post-stimulation subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sgACC) connectivity to ventral striatum and regions of DMPFC (bipolar n = 4, unipolar n = 21) [12]. This latter study reported no difference in these results when excluding the four participants diagnosed with bipolar disorder. In depressed cohorts, changes in sgACC functional connectivity have been reported following rTMS treatment, with regions in DLPFC, ventromedial prefrontal cortex, and the default mode network [13], [14], [15]. Some of these studies have reported that these connectivity changes are associated with clinical improvement, but not always.

Intermittent theta-burst stimulation

Intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS) is a form of patterned rTMS that delivers stimulation in rapid succession as opposed to rhythmically with equal inter-pulse intervals and is thought to mimic the brain's endogenous theta waves. Compared to standard rTMS brain targeting, iTBS is shown to be similarly effective in unipolar depression [16], [17]. Previous studies in individuals with bipolar disorder have reported that over half the participants in the active iTBS groups met response criteria of ≥ 50% decrease in depressive symptoms [18], [19], [20]. A recent randomized clinical trial of accelerated iTBS in bipolar depression found that 50% of participants met remission criteria after five days of treatment, compared to none in the sham group [21]. In contrast, a sham-controlled study of iTBS in individuals with acute bipolar depression was halted due to low remission and response rates, however, the investigators noted that improved targeting methods might improve treatment outcomes [22].

SAINT Trials

The Stanford Accelerated Intelligent Neuromodulation Therapy (SAINT) protocol is an FDA-cleared accelerated, five-day treatment. The SAINT treatment method utilizes personalized functional connectivity-guided iTBS to alleviate treatment-resistant unipolar depressive symptoms in a shorter period, compared to standard rTMS and pharmacological interventions, which typically require several weeks of treatment to be effective. SAINT was shown to reduce depressive symptoms in both the original open-label study and the follow-up double-blind randomized control trial: immediate post-treatment response rates of 90.5% and 84.6%, and remission rates of 90.5% and 57.1%, respectively [23], [24]. A follow-up study focusing on only the participants within these aforementioned studies with bipolar disorder (bipolar I disorder = 2, bipolar II disorder = 5) found that, similar to those with unipolar depression, there was a significant decrease in depressive severity both immediately after treatment and in the weeks following. Five participants met remission criteria at immediate follow-up and two weeks post-treatment. No serious adverse events or treatment-emergent mania were reported [25].

The neurophysiological mechanisms underpinning SAINT in unipolar depression have been explored in only a handful of studies. Batail and colleagues reported that, compared to sham participants, individuals with treatment-resistant depression exhibited increased resting-state functional connectivity between left amygdala and left medial prefrontal cortex, as well as between right sensorimotor cortex and right frontal-operculum-insula. The active treatment group also exhibited a decreased cross-hemisphere post-treatment functional connectivity between the right amygdala and the left DMPFC. The connectivity changes that included regions in the amygdala were significantly correlated with changes in depression scores from baseline to post-treatment [26]. Another study reported significantly decreased functional connectivity between the left DLPFC stimulation target and a cluster in medial prefrontal cortex immediately following SAINT treatment in the active-stimulation group only [27].

Hypothesis

This research was conducted to assess the feasibility, safety and preliminary efficacy of the SAINT treatment in bipolar I depression. In line with previous findings of iTBS [15], [16], [17], we reported significant decreases in depressive symptoms post-treatment in roughly half of our cohort [28]. Within this follow-on study, we seek to build on these findings by investigating the neurobiological mechanisms underlying TMS treatment. In light of the limited neuroimaging literature examining SAINT iTBS in bipolar depression, the present study was conceived as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating investigation. Seed-to-whole-brain functional connectivity analyses were performed to characterize the broader network-level effects of SAINT treatment in individuals with bipolar disorder who have failed at least one first-line treatment. We were first interested in understanding how the SAINT iTBS intervention was associated with changes in resting-state functional connectivity. Specifically, we investigated the intervention-specific effects of SAINT iTBS by assessing changes in resting-state functional connectivity from subject-specific DLPFC seeds, defined as individualized stimulation targets that are maximally anticorrelated with the sgACC. We additionally examined changes in sgACC connectivity, given its established functional anticorrelation with the DLPFC, its putative modulation by SAINT iTBS, and its well-documented role in mood dysregulation [23], [24]. We were also interested in investigating whether changes in connectivity might be associated with changes in treatment effects. We hypothesized that SAINT treatment and alleviation of depressive symptoms would be associated with changes in functional connectivity in left DLPFC (iTBS target) or sgACC seeds. This study aims to characterize the neurophysiological response to SAINT in this cohort, to inform future large-scale sham-controlled follow-up research that ultimately improves treatment strategies and contributes to our understanding of longitudinal changes in depressive severity.

Methods

This open-label study was conducted at two sites, Johns Hopkins University and The University of Texas at Austin Dell Medical School, with approval from the Johns Hopkins University Institutional Review Board. All participants voluntarily consented prior to beginning study procedures.

Participants

Ten adult participants (mean age = 44 years; 60% women) completed the study, all with a primary diagnosis of bipolar I disorder, as confirmed by the clinician-administered Structured Clinical Interview for DSM−5 (SCID−5 RV). All enrolled participants self-identified as white, with three identifying as Hispanic/Latinx (30%). All participants had failed a minimum of one first-line bipolar depression therapeutic medication trial in the current episode. Of our ten included participants, six had failed one adequate treatment trial in the current depressive episode, and four had failed ≥ 2 treatment trials in the current depressive episode. At baseline, participants must have an active moderate-severe depressive episode as defined by the Montgomery-Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) (score ≥ 20). All participants were required to be on a stable dose of an anti-manic agent (e.g., lithium) for at least six weeks prior to treatment and could not deviate from their treatment regimen throughout study enrollment. Exclusion criteria included a history of brain injury, seizures or contraindications to MRI or TMS, diagnosis of obsessive-compulsive disorder, or active suicide risk. Participants could not have had a (hypo)manic episode in the past six months or electroconvulsive therapy in the past three months.

Clinical Assessments

Detailed information regarding all clinical and cognitive assessments can be found in a previous publication [28]. Following the completion of iTBS treatment, participants completed two follow-up visits: 1) within 5 days of the final iTBS session (in conjunction with the follow-up MRI scan), and 2) one-month post-treatment (no MRI). Depressive symptoms were measured at baseline, on each treatment day, and at the two post-treatment timepoints using the clinician-administered MADRS. Clinical response was defined as a ≥ 50% decrease in MADRS score, and clinical remission was defined as a MADRS score of ≤ 10. The Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) was administered at baseline and at the two post-treatment timepoints, and on each treatment day to monitor any shifts toward treatment-emergent mania. Additionally, after each treatment session, a modified Positive and Negative Affect Schedule most closely associated with manic symptoms (PANAS; items 3, 9, 11, 17, 18, and 19) was also administered to assess for any shift toward mania. If an increase of more than two points on more than two items occurred on the modified PANAS compared to baseline, a full YMRS would immediately be administered.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging Acquisition

Structural and functional imaging was done both before and after treatment. The post-treatment scan occurred within five days of the last treatment session. Whole-brain structural imaging was acquired with a T1-weighted magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo sequence with the following parameters: TR = 2300 ms, TE = 2.36 ms, FOV= 256 × 256 mm2, 1 slab of 192 slices, slice thickness = 0.90 mm, flip angle = 8°. Resting-state functional MRIs consisted of at least four 6-minute runs in which participants were asked to keep their eyes open while looking at a fixation cross. Brain images were acquired with an echo-planar imaging sequence with the following parameters: TR = 1300 ms, TE = 30 ms, FOV = 128 × 128 mm2, flip angle = 64°, 84 slices, slice thickness = 1.8 mm. Pre-treatment resting-state scans were reacquired if participants’ movement was outside the allowed parameters (> 20% of volumes with mean framewise displacement ≤ 0.4 mm or movement> 0.5 mm). The Johns Hopkins site used a 3-Tesla Siemens Prisma and the UT Austin site used a 3-Tesla Siemens Vida.

Personalized target, neuronavigation and treatment

Personalized left DLPFC target regions most anti-correlated with the sgACC were localized with structural and functional MRI scans as outlined in previous SAINT publications [23], [24] (Fig. 1A). The selection of the DLPFC target was guided by evidence that pre-treatment functional connectivity-based target selection may optimize clinical response in treatment-resistant unipolar depression [29], [30]. After locating the left DLPFC target region as per the SAINT protocol [23], [24] the participant’s structural MRI was utilized during the in-person iTBS treatment to relocalize the left DLPFC. Using each participant’s left DLPFC target depth and motor threshold, the optimal stimulation level was calculated, up to 120% motor threshold. We used an iTBS protocol of triple-pulse 50 Hz bursts given every 200 ms (at 5 Hz); 1800 pulses for each session (1800 pulses x 10 sessions per day = 18,000 pulses each treatment day), as has been validated for treatment-resistant unipolar depression [24]. This targeted, accelerated treatment method is hypothesized to have a cumulative stimulation effect that once-daily rTMS lacks [23]. While all treatment methodology was the same as previous SAINT publications, this protocol was modified for our bipolar sample, in that if at any MADRS administration during the five treatment days a participant met the remission criteria (MADRS score of ≤10), stimulation was discontinued. Additionally, if there was an increase of 2 or more points in at least 2 YMRS items from baseline, stimulation was discontinued.

Clinical outcome analyses

A Wilcoxon signed-rank test with an alpha level of 0.05 was used to compare pre- and post-treatment depression scores due to the small sample size. Baseline MADRS scores were compared to immediate follow-up scores, as well as the percentage of participants who met remission and response criteria (defined above).

Magnetic resonance imaging preprocessing and denoising

Functional and structural data were preprocessed with fMRIPrep 23.1.3 [31], [32], which is based on Nipype 1.8.6 [33], [34]. The preprocessed functional and structural data were imported into CONN [35] release 22.a [36] and SPM [37] release 12.7771 for smoothing (8 mm FWHM) and denoising. Full details of these preprocessing steps are included in the supplement.

Pre and Post Functional Connectivity Analyses

To characterize the neurophysiological response to SAINT treatment, we examined pre- and post-treatment changes in seed-to-whole-brain resting-state functional connectivity. Two seed regions were analyzed. Firstly, the sgACC, defined as the subcallosal cortex within the FSL Harvard-Oxford cortical atlas [38], was anticorrelated with the target DLPFC region as per the SAINT protocol [23], [24]. Second, personalized DLPFC targets were defined as 10-mm radius spherical seeds centered on each participant’s SAINT-derived MNI coordinate [23], [24], resulting in seed locations that varied across individuals according to their stimulation target. For each seed region, individual first-level seed-to-voxel connectivity maps were computed in CONN by correlating the BOLD time series in the seed with every voxel in the brain. Connectivity strength was represented by Fisher-transformed bivariate correlation coefficients. At the second level, individual Fisher-z maps from pre- and post-treatment sessions were entered into a two-tailed general linear model to assess within-subject changes in functional connectivity in CONN. Site was included as a between-subjects nuisance covariate to account for possible scanner-related variance (University of Texas or Johns Hopkins University). Group-level inferences were performed using random-effects models. Statistical significance was determined at a voxel-wise cluster-forming threshold of p < 0.01 (uncorrected) and a cluster-level false discovery rate (FDR) correction of p < 0.05.

Relationship between functional connectivity change and treatment response

We hypothesized that treatment-related changes in connectivity would be associated with the degree of mood improvement, reflected in decreases in MADRS scores from baseline to follow-up (ΔMADRS). Therefore, for each significant seed-to-voxel cluster identified in the pre- versus post-TMS analysis (2.7), individual Fisher-transformed bivariate correlation coefficients were extracted to quantify seed-to-cluster connectivity. Subsequently, Pearson correlation analyses assessed the association between changes in connectivity and corresponding ΔMADRS across participants (thresholded at p < .05).

To complement this targeted correlation analysis, we also conducted a hypothesis-driven bivariate regression analysis within the CONN toolbox to identify brain regions where functional connectivity changes (from baseline to follow-up) were associated with ΔMADRS. This approach enabled us to more broadly identify brain regions in which connectivity changes were associated with clinical improvement. We used a general linear model (GLM) framework where the dependent variable was the individual subject’s change in seed-based connectivity (sgACC and left DLPFC) and the primary independent variable was depressive symptom changes (MADRS scores). Again, we included site as a covariate and statistical significance was determined at a voxel-wise cluster-forming threshold of p < 0.01 (uncorrected) and a cluster-level false discovery rate (FDR) correction of p < 0.05. All significant cortical and subcortical clusters in seed-to-voxel analyses were labeled according to the Harvard-Oxford cortical and subcortical atlas. Peak BA Coordinates were defined with BioImage Suite Web version 1.4 [39], [40].

Results

Clinical results

All ten participants completed the study per protocol. Across all participants, there was a significant decrease in clinician-administered MADRS scores between baseline and immediate follow-up (p = 0.006; Cohen’s d = 2.96). On average, a 56.7% (SD = 19.7%) decrease in MADRS scores occurred between baseline and immediate follow-up. Five participants met response criteria by immediate follow-up (≥50% decrease in MADRS score), four of which met remission criteria (MADRS score ≤10). 60% of participants met remission criteria at some time point throughout the trial (Table 1). We reported only two adverse events of mild headaches during treatment. More detailed results about clinical outcomes can be found in our previous publication [28].

Functional connectivity changes linked to treatment response

For the above functional connectivity findings in 3.2, no significant relationships were observed between ΔMADRS and sgACC connectivity with either the right frontal pole cluster (r [8] = −0.13, p = 0.72) or the left frontal pole cluster (r [8] = 0.34, p = 0.34).

However, in the seed-to-whole brain analysis, treatment response was associated with decrease functional connectivity between the subject-specific left DLPFC target seed and four clusters (Fig. 3): Cluster 1) right postcentral and precentral gyrus, MNI peak coordinates [ + 48–16 + 44], BA peak = 4, cluster size = 942 voxels, cluster level p-FDR < .001, ΔZ = .020) Cluster 2) left postcentral, precentral and middle frontal gyrus (MNI peak coordinates [−38–28 +66], BA peak = 4, cluster size = 701 voxels, cluster level p-FDR <.001, ΔZ = .022) Cluster 3) right middle, superior and planum temporal gyrus (MNI peak coordinates [+60–26 +10], BA peak = 41, cluster size = 333 voxels, cluster level p-FDR =.024, ΔZ = .020) Cluster 4) right frontal pole, superior and middle frontal gyrus (MNI peak coordinates [+32 +32 +42], BA peak = 9, cluster size = 286 voxels, cluster level p-FDR =.038, ΔZ = .023). However, as aforementioned, the difference in connectivity between these regions was not statistically significant when considering only pre- to post-treatment brain response. Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate neurophysiological changes associated with an accelerated iTBS treatment exclusively in individuals with bipolar I depression. We observed increased functional connectivity from the sgACC to bilateral frontal pole regions following the intervention. This finding was not related to changes in depressive symptoms. Furthermore, increased clinical improvement was associated with more negative connectivity between left DLPFC seed and prefrontal/temporal cortices after treatment. Thus, connectivity changes associated with the intervention were observed within sgACC–frontal pole networks, whereas clinical improvement (i.e., improvement in depressive scores) was associated with altered connectivity of the individualized DLPFC stimulation target. Although preliminary, these findings provide a mechanistic framework that should be tested and refined in larger, prospective studies.

We reported increased functional connectivity between sgACC and right and left frontal pole regions following iTBS treatment. Middle prefrontal brain regions have been implicated in the recognition, processing and regulation of affective response and are closely interconnected with brain regions involved in emotional processing such as amygdala and sgACC [41], [42], [43]. Altered functional connectivity and reactivity in medial prefrontal brain regions have been implicated in the pathophysiology of bipolar disorder [44], [45], [46], [47], [48]. Liston et al. reported DLPFC-targeting TMS was associated with reduced sgACC and medial prefrontal cortex connectivity. These brain regions overlap with this study’s significant connectivity findings, albeit with treatment effects in the opposite direction [13]. However, the clusters identified in our study included more lateral prefrontal brain regions. Future research will need to clarify whether the observed differences between this study and ours are attributable to disorder-specific characteristics inherent to the cohorts, simply variations in treatment methods (SAINT vs. standard TMS), or analytical methodologies (e.g., study differences in sgACC seed). We postulate that hyper-reactivity of sgACC may potentially be a cross-diagnostic marker of depressive symptoms in individuals with both unipolar and bipolar depression [49], [50], [51], but there may be some disorder-specific differences in prefrontal treatment response, and our results, in addition to previous literature, is not enough to validate this speculation. Our result, although low in power, adds to the growing literature in transdiagnostic populations that indicates that sgACC and its connectivity with prefrontal brain regions may be important mechanisms of action for iTBS when targeting the left DLPFC. While our study may have lacked sufficient power to identify a correlation between sgACC-medial prefrontal connectivity and shifts in depressive symptoms, previous studies have reported increased activity in medial prefrontal brain regions during cognitive tasks as individuals progress toward recovery from bipolar depression [52], [53]. Therefore, subsequent, more strongly powered, follow-up iTBS studies in individuals with bipolar depression may wish to focus specifically on sgACC to medial prefrontal connectivity to better understand the relationship to treatment response, as well as the potential to identify treatment response predictors.

Additionally, we reported a significant improvement in depressive symptoms following stimulation, with 50% of participants meeting response criteria at immediate follow-up (≥50% decrease in MADRS score), which was within five days of completing treatment, and 60% of participants meeting remission criteria (MADRS score ≤10) at some point throughout their participation. While our clinical outcomes were more modest than the previous SAINT trials in depressed individuals (immediate post-treatment response rate of 90.5%, 84.6%, and 71.4%, and remission rate of 90.5%, 57.1%, and 71.4%; [23,24,25, respectively]), the treatment was generally well-tolerated with few adverse effects and no reports of treatment-induced mania [28]. Our clinical findings echoed previous iTBS work [15], [16], [17] and were comparable to the randomized clinical trial of accelerated iTBS in bipolar depression [21]. This latter trial, as well as ours, used an accelerated treatment paradigm and saw substantial clinical outcome differences in comparison to McGirr et al., whose sham-controlled iTBS trial in bipolar depression was halted due to low response/remission rates [22]. The variability in outcomes may be due to differences in treatment protocol. Namely, participants in McGirr et al. received 600 pulses per day, while those in the SAINT studies received 18,000 pulses per day [22].

A primary objective of the study was to investigate the relationship between treatment response findings and functional connectivity changes. Our regression analysis showed that a more pronounced decrease in MADRS symptoms post-iTBS treatment corresponded with reduced connectivity between left DLPFC and four clusters, three in the right hemisphere and one in the left hemisphere (Fig. 3). To our knowledge, this finding has not been reported previously in individuals with unipolar depression who have undergone iTBS. In contrast, Liston et al. found increased anticorrelations between DLPFC and ventromedial prefrontal cortex following TMS, but this result was unrelated to treatment response [13]. Some longitudinal evidence suggests that progression towards a euthymic state in individuals with bipolar disorder may be associated with changes in dorsal prefrontal brain function [52], [53], [54]. Nevertheless, it is also conceivable that these findings might mirror the diverse individualized response to the iTBS intervention within the target brain region. It is possible that this change in left DLPFC connectivity may serve as a neurophysiological marker of the intervention’s individualized impact, but our sample size limits the conclusions we can propose.

There are several limitations of this research. The present analyses were conducted as a secondary investigation of a study originally designed to evaluate the feasibility, safety, and preliminary clinical effects of SAINT in bipolar I depression [28]. Accordingly, the sample size was limited (n = 10). Therefore, this study serves as an exploratory foundation for future large-scale, sham-controlled investigations and contributes initial evidence regarding neural mechanisms underlying treatment response. In light of the modest sample size, we adopted a relatively liberal voxel-forming threshold with cluster-level FDR correction to balance sensitivity and specificity. Although this approach is consistent with exploratory neuroimaging practice, the possibility of inflated Type I error cannot be excluded and warrants cautious interpretation. Additionally, although we used conventional parametric statistical approaches commonly used in the neuroimaging literature; the modest sample size limits statistical power. Future large-scale studies will be essential to validate these results, identify predictors of treatment response, and clarify potential differences between bipolar and unipolar depression populations. On a similar note, our small sample is racially and ethnically homogeneous, so larger, more diverse samples in future studies would allow for greater generalizability. Furthermore, the absence of a sham treatment group prevents us from fully attributing the findings to the intervention. Finally, the long-term impact of iTBS in this sample is unclear, and our findings require follow-up with a longer-term longitudinal MRI protocol to determine whether the changes we observed immediately post-treatment are sustained over the long term.

Overall, this study demonstrates that accelerated iTBS protocols can be safe for bipolar depressed patients, but as a precaution, accelerated rTMS studies in bipolar depression should incorporate consistent mania scale administrations in their design, such as this one, to track potential mood swings during treatment. Future studies may consider the importance of using a single-diagnosis sample (e.g. bipolar disorder only), as opposed to a transdiagnostic design (e.g. mixed sample of unipolar and bipolar depression) when attempting to assess the neurophysiological impact of TMS, or gathering a large enough mixed sample to directly compare these groups. Neuroimaging evidence does suggest neurophysiological differences between individuals with unipolar compared to those with bipolar depression and future studies in bipolar cohorts may wish to target other brain regions (e.g., ventral prefrontal cortex) [55], [56].

Interestingly, the intervention was associated with changes in bilateral prefrontal connectivity, which appears to be indicated in unipolar depression as well, although the brain structures showing connectivity changes with this region differs per study. The effects of transcranial magnetic stimulation on neural connectivity in unipolar depressed populations have been vastly more explored than in bipolar depressed populations. These preliminary neurofunctional changes within prefrontal brain regions are an exciting first glimpse into the neurophysiological impact of this treatment in individuals with bipolar I depression, but require replication in a larger randomized, sham-controlled trial.
