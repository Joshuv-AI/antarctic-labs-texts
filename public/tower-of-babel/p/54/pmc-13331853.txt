Efficacy and safety of direct oral anticoagulants and low molecular weight heparin for cancer-associated venous thromboembolism in patients with extremes in bodyweight
Authors: Katy M. Toale, Claire A. Marten, Ashley Crouch, Clark R. Andersen, Fauzia Ullah, Cristhiam Mauricio Rojas Hernandez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The purpose of this study is to describe the safety and efficacy of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) and low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) for the treatment of cancer-associated thrombosis in patients with extreme bodyweights. This was a single center, retrospective cohort study of adult patients on therapy for at least 6 months. The primary efficacy outcome of this study was to describe the time to first recurrent venous thromboembolism (VTE) within 6 months, compared between treatment and across weight cohorts. The primary safety objective was to describe the time to first major bleeding (MB) event within 6 months of starting therapy, between treatment types and among the weight groups. Secondary outcomes included incidence of recurrent VTE, MB, clinically relevant non-major bleeding (CRNMB) and the rate of VTE- or bleeding-related mortality. The time to recurrent VTE was comparable between all groups. In the BMI cohort, the time to first MB was 19.5 times longer for DOACs compared to LMWH in the overweight population (p = 0.007) and to first MB or CRNMB was 11.98 times longer (p = < 0.0001). In the weight cohort, the time to first MB or CRNMB was 14.63 times longer for DOACs compared to LMWH in the overweight population (p = 0.011) and 6.41 times longer in the normal weight population (p = 0.0009). There was an insufficient number of patients in the underweight group to perform statistical analysis. The results suggest that in overweight patients, DOACs have similar efficacy and fewer bleeding complications than LMWHs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11239-026-03239-x.

Introduction

Patients with cancer are at increased risk for venous thromboembolism (VTE), including deep venous thromboembolism (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), even in the absence of traditional VTE risk factors [1]. These patients have an elevated risk of bleeding while on anticoagulant treatment and are more likely to develop recurrent thromboembolic complications [1–5]. Historically, low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) was the gold standard for the treatment of cancer-associated thrombosis (CAT) [6]; however, direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), such as apixaban, edoxaban, and rivaroxaban, were approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of VTE in 2012 to 2015 and have since been recommended for the treatment of CAT by the American Society of Clinical Oncology and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network® [7, 8].

Although the convenience of DOACs has led to increased use in recent years, there is a lack of clinical evidence supporting their use for the treatment of CAT in patients at extremes of bodyweight, such as in the overweight or underweight populations. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines underweight as a body mass index (BMI) < 18.5 kg/m2 and overweight as a BMI 25–29.9 kg/m2, class I obesity as a BMI of 30–34.9 kg/m2, class II obesity as a BMI of 35–39.9 kg/m2 and class III obesity, or morbid obesity, as a BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2 [9]. In a cancer population at already heightened risk of bleeding or developing recurrent thrombosis, it is possible that overdosing of an anticoagulant due to low body weight could result in a life-threatening bleeding while underdosing due to high body weight could result in VTE progression or recurrence [10].

In 2016, the International Society of Thrombosis and Haemostasis Scientific and Standardization Committee (ISTH SSC) recommended against the use of DOACs in patients with a BMI > 40 kg/m2 or body weight ≥ 120 kg due to lack of clinical evidence regarding the efficacy and safety in the morbidly obese population and concerns that the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) data suggested alterations in obese patients [11]. However, in response to ongoing research in this patient population, the ISTH SSC guidelines were updated in 2021 and recommended standard doses of rivaroxaban or apixaban for the treatment of VTE regardless of body weight or BMI. The updated guidelines additionally suggest avoiding dabigatran and edoxaban in patients with BMI > 40 kg/m2 or weight ≥ 120 kg due to lack of clinical and PK/PD data [12]. While providing clear guidance for management of patients with obesity, these guidelines do not address underweight patients or the management of patients with obesity and cancer.

The use of DOACs in patients with CAT is increasing in clinical practice due to convenience of oral administration compared to subcutaneous injections of LMWH. However, the use of DOACs in patients with extremes of bodyweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2 or weight < 50 kg and BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 or weight ≥ 120 kg) and CAT is limited due to a paucity of studies or guidelines addressing this unique patient population. The MD Anderson Cancer Center (MDACC) Anticoagulation Stewardship Program developed this protocol with a goal to compare and characterize the incidence of VTE recurrence and bleeding events in overweight and underweight patients treated with DOACs and LMWH for CAT. The results of this study will provide valuable information on the safety and efficacy of these agents in this patient population and guide clinical decision making in the treatment of CAT. Based on previous data in non-cancer associated VTE, we hypothesize that both LMWH and DOAC will be safe and effective for the management of CAT in extreme weights.

Methods

A single-center, retrospective cohort study was conducted to determine the safety and efficacy of DOACs and LMWH in patients with extreme weights. Data was collected at a single comprehensive cancer center from January 1, 2018, to May 31, 2023. The study was approved by the MDACC investigational review board and the need for informed consent was waived. This work was supported in part by the National Institutes of Health/National Cancer Institute (NIH/NCI) Cancer Center Support Grant award number P30CA016672 and utilized the Biostatistics Resource Group.

The study population was identified via a data query of the electronic health records of outpatient prescriptions for LMWH and DOACs. Adult patients, 18 years of age or older with a diagnosis of CAT who received either a DOAC or LMWH for at least 6 months were included. Sufficient evidence, including prescription refills or provider notes that the patient was actively taking the DOAC or LMWH for at least 6 months was required for inclusion. Patients were excluded if pregnant, if therapy was switched after the first month of treatment, death from causes other than thrombosis or bleeding within 6 months, or major bleeding within 30 days prior to VTE diagnosis. Previous data on overweight and underweight patients compared outcomes based on patient’s actual weight and/or BMI; therefore, data was analyzed for both categories in this study. The most recent recorded height and weight prior to or on the same day as the diagnosis date of index VTE was used to categorize patients as underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2 or weight < 50 kg), normal weight (BMI 18.5–29.9 kg/m2 or weight 50–119.9 kg) or overweight (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 or weight ≥ 120 kg). Patient creatinine clearance was calculated using the Cockcroft Gault equation with the most recent serum creatinine prior to the index VTE date. Actual body weight was used in the calculation, except for patients whose actual body weight was ≥ 120% of their ideal body weight in which adjusted body weight was used. For patients on DOACs, drug interactions were recorded based on the concurrent use of cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4) and P-glycoprotein (P-gp) inhibitors and inducers. CYP3A4 and P-gp inhibitors were defined as: cobicistat, conivaptan, indinavir, itraconazole, ketoconazole (systemic), posaconazole, saquinavir, telithromycin, ombitasvir, paritaprevir, ritonavir, tucatinib, tamoxifen, and sunitinib. CYP3A4 and P-gp inducers were defined as: apalutamide, carbamazepine, fosphenytoin, phenytoin, phenobarbital, rifampin, St. John’s Wort, dexamethasone, vinblastine, bosentan, efavirenz, etravirine, nafcillin, nevirapine, primidone, rifabutin, and rifapentine.

Bleeding events were categorized as described by the ISTH SSC [13]. Major bleeding (MB) was defined as acute or overt bleeding associated with one or more of the following occurrences: decrease in hemoglobin of at least 2 g/dL within 24 h, transfusion of 2 or more units of red blood cells within 24 h, bleeding occurrence at a critical site (intraspinal, intraocular, pericardial, intraarticular, or retroperitoneal), intracranial bleeding, or fatal bleeding. Clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding (CRNMB) was defined as acute, clinically overt episodes that did not meet the criteria for MB but were associated with medical interventions, unscheduled contact with a medical physician, pain, or interruption of medical therapy.

The primary efficacy outcome of this study was to describe time to first recurrent VTE within 6 months of starting DOAC/LMWH, between treatments and among the weight groups. The primary safety objective was to describe the time to first MB episode within 6 months of starting DOAC/LMWH, between treatments and among the weight groups. Secondary outcomes included incidence of recurrent VTE, incidence of MB, incidence of and time to first CRNMB episode, incidence of and time to first episode of bleeding (MB or CRNMB), and rate of VTE or bleeding associated mortality within 6 months of starting DOAC/LMWH, between treatments and among the weight groups.

Demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized using mean, median, standard deviation, quartiles, and range for continuous variables, and frequency and percentage for categorical variables. These variables were presented separately by treatment group (LMWH vs. DOAC) and by combinations of treatment and weight group (underweight, normal weight, overweight). Differences were assessed by t-test and Mann–Whitney test, analysis of variance and Kruskal Wallis test, or Chi-square test. Time-to-event outcomes including time from index to recurrent VTE, MB, CRNMB, any bleeding event (MB or CRNMB), and VTE- or bleeding-related death were censored at the earlier of either the last follow-up date or 180 days. They were summarized using Kaplan–Meier methods by treatment group and by combinations of treatment group and weight group and combinations of treatment group and BMI group. Based upon Kaplan–Meier data from figures in prior published studies comparing DOAC to LMWH, simulation-based power analyses for recurrent VTE and major bleeding within 6 months was performed and it was determined we likely lack sufficient data to demonstrate significant differences; however due to lack of data in cancer patients with extreme weights the information on VTE and bleeding with DOAC and LMWH in extremes of BMI will be an important contribution to the literature regardless of significance. Accelerated failure time models utilizing the log-normal distribution were used to model each time-to-event outcome with relation to treatment group and weight group or BMI group, including interaction; in these models, the underweight category of the weight and BMI groups were excluded due to small numbers and events, and likewise only for the normal BMI group for the model of time from index to bleeding-related death. Contrasts were used to assess differences between treatment groups separately by weight group or BMI group; note that the exponential of contrasts for accelerated failure time models are interpreted as survival time ratios, which may be loosely interpreted as the inverse of hazard ratios. Hommel-adjusted p-values were used to compensate for multiple comparisons. The log-normal distribution was selected for all models following comparison with Weibull, exponential, gaussian, logistic, log-normal, and log-logistic distributions, based upon evaluation of Akaike Information Criteria (AIC) and residuals. Additional models which incorporated all combinations of age at index, sex, and race/ethnicity as covariates were explored but rejected due to poor model fits per the AIC, as well as models which added Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) score as a covariate. Due to testing of both weight groups and BMI groups, the criterion for statistical significance was set at alpha = 0.05/2 = 0.025 to account for assessing outcomes with relation to both weight groups and BMI groups. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1.

Study data was collected and managed using research electronic data capture (REDCap) tools available at MDACC [14]. REDCap is a secure, web-based application with restricted access designed to support data capture for research studies, providing: (1) an intuitive interface for validated data entry; (2) audit trials for tracking data manipulation and export procedures; (3) automated export procedures for seamless downloads to common statistical packages; and (4) procedures for importing data from external sources. REDCap (https://redcap.mdanderson.org) is hosted on a secure server by MDACC’s Department of Research Information Systems and Technology Services.

Results

A total of 1,380 charts were reviewed for inclusion during the time period of January 1, 2018, to May 31, 2023. Of these patients, 986 were excluded with the most common reasons for exclusion being: lost to follow-up (n = 268), treatment for less than 6 months (n = 217) and indication other than acute CAT (n = 205) (Fig. 1). Primary outcome

In the BMI analysis, the primary outcome of time to recurrent VTE or MB was only able to be analyzed in the normal weight vs. the overweight group due to the small number of patients in the underweight group. When comparing the time to recurrent VTE in DOAC vs LMWH both normal weight and overweight patients, no difference was found (p = 0.91). When comparing time to recurrent VTE in overweight vs normal weight patients, no difference was found in patients on LMWH (p = 0.91) or DOACs (p = 0.91). When comparing time to MB in DOAC vs LMWH, there was no difference in the normal weight population (p = 0.26), however time to MB in the DOAC group was 19.5 times longer than in the LMWH group in the overweight population (p = 0.007). Secondary outcomes

In the BMI analysis, time to MB or CRNMB in the DOAC group was 11.98 times longer than the LMWH group in the overweight population (p < 0.0001). There was no difference in the normal weight population (p = 0.11). There were no statistically significant differences noted in the comparison of DOAC vs LMWH in normal weight patients and overweight patients or in normal weight vs overweight in the LMWH and DOAC groups (Tables 2 and 3).

The incidence of events in all patients as well as the incidence specifically in apixaban and rivaroxaban patients can be seen in supplemental Table 4. Overall, in the LMWH and DOAC arms 4.5% vs. 4.6% of patients experienced a recurrent VTE, and MB was noted in 12.6% vs 2.8% of patients, respectively. CRNMB was also more common in the LMWH group than DOAC group (11.7% vs 4.3%) and combined MB and CRNMB was 24.3 vs 7.1%. Supplemental Table 5 shows the absolute incidence of recurrent VTE, bleeding, and death for the underweight, normal weight and overweight groups. Recurrent VTE and bleeding was not common in underweight patients; however, it is difficult to draw conclusions due to the small number of patients in this category. In the overweight population it appears there is a trend toward more recurrent VTE in the LMWH group as well as more bleeding events.

There were 5 patient deaths that were attributed to thrombotic or bleeding events. Anticoagulation dosing was deemed appropriate in all patients. One thrombotic related death occurred in the DOAC arm in a patient who was 58.5 kg and BMI 18.5 kg/m2. Two bleeding related deaths occurred in the DOAC arm with one patient being 109.9 kg and BMI 35.5 kg/m2 and the other being 147 kg and 45.4 kg/m2. There were also two bleeding related deaths in the LMWH arm with one patient being 84.1 kg and BMI 31.8 kg/m2 and the other 142 kg and BMI 41.5 kg/m2.

Discussion

To date, this is the first evaluation of the efficacy and safety of DOACs and LMWH in patients with CAT and extreme weights. The results of this study show that DOACs in usual doses appear to be safe and effective in overweight patients. Despite reviewing a large number of underweight patients, only a small number met inclusion criteria; therefore, it is difficult to draw conclusions in this population.

Overweight population

Time to recurrent VTE was not statistically different when comparing overweight to normal weight patients in each treatment group. However, when comparing time to bleeding events in patients receiving LMWH vs DOACs in the overweight population it appears DOACs may offer a benefit when it comes to bleeding complications as both the time to MB and MB/CRNMB was less in the DOAC arm compared to the LMWH arm. In the major trials evaluating DOAC vs LMWH in mostly normal weight patients, DOACs had better outcomes with regards to recurrent VTE, worse outcomes with regards to CRNMB, and for MB it was dependent on the treatment used with apixaban showing favorable results compared with rivaroxaban [15–17]. In our data, 7 of the 8 MBs occurred in patients on apixaban; however, this may be attributed to apixaban being more commonly used at our institution (81% vs 19%) as seen in supplemental Table 3a and 3b. Of the DOAC patients with a BMI ≥ 40 mg/kg2 who experienced a bleeding/thrombotic complication, 4 had a MB, 1 had a CRNMB and 1 had a recurrent VTE. The increased time to MB and MB/CRNMB in the DOAC arm compared to the LMWH arm in overweight patients seen in this trial could be related to dosing strategies used in the overweight LMWH population at our institution. Historical data has suggested that the optimal dosing of LMWH in patients with obesity or morbid obesity should utilize total body weight without capping the dose for the treatment of VTE [18]. However, more recent recommendations for patients with a BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2 suggest using 0.7–0.8 mg/kg enoxaparin twice daily, to consider capping at 150 mg per dose, and to avoid once daily dosing, but do not specifically address patients with cancer [19, 20]. In this study, 30 patients in the LMWH arm had a BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2 and only 8 of those had their initial dose of enoxaparin reduced to 0.7–0.8 mg/kg. Nine of these 30 patients had dose adjustments due to anti-Xa level monitoring. One patient received once daily enoxaparin dosing, and no patient had a single dose which exceeded 150 mg. Six patients in the LMWH arm with a BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2 had a MB and of those, 3 were receiving enoxaparin 1 mg/kg twice daily. One patient in the LMWH arm with a BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2 experienced a fatal bleed and was receiving enoxaparin 1 mg/kg twice daily. Three patients in this group had recurrent VTE within 6 months, and of those 2 were receiving a dose of 0.7–0.8 mg/kg enoxaparin twice daily. While the numbers are small, the data suggested that adjusted dose LMWH of 0.7–0.8 mg/kg enoxaparin twice daily should be considered for patients with a BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2 and anti-Xa monitoring should be considered due to the high risk of recurrent VTE in CAT.

Previous data of DOAC use in patients with obesity and VTE showed either similar or superior outcomes of DOACs, mainly when compared to warfarin for treatment of obese patients; however, the number of patients with active cancer included in these trials was limited (Supplemental Table 6). Wysokonski et al. compared apixaban and rivaroxaban to LMWH or VKA for the treatment of VTE with 25% of the patients in the DOAC arm having a concomitant cancer diagnosis. The results found that in the rivaroxaban arm, obese patients, defined in the study as > 120 kg, with cancer had a higher recurrent VTE rate than non-obese patients with cancer. However, there were only 15 cancer patients in the > 120 kg rivaroxaban study group [21]. Similarly, three systematic reviews and meta-analysis by Karakasis et al. (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2), Wang et al. (weight ≥ 120 kg or BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2), and Park et al. (weight ≥ 120 kg or BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2), of warfarin vs DOACs in obese or morbidly obese patients showed DOACs to be favorable [22–24]. In a systematic review of anticoagulation dosing in obese patients, Abildgaard et al. recommend apixaban and rivaroxaban at standard doses for obese or morbidly obese patients with AF or VTE [20]. A review by Sebaaly et al., also supports the use of apixaban and rivaroxaban in patients with weight > 120 kg or BMI > 40 kg/m2 [25]. These systematic reviews and meta-analysis provide a clear consensus supporting the use of apixaban and rivaroxaban in patients with obesity but provide limited insight on patients with CAT. Potere et al. published a post hoc analysis of the AVERT trial comparing apixaban to placebo in ambulatory prophylaxis of obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) cancer patients and found no difference in outcomes between obese and non-obese groups on apixaban [26]. This post-hoc analysis provides data supporting the use of apixaban in patients with obesity and cancer but was limited to prophylactic dosing rather than treatment of CAT. Building upon this previous literature, the results of this study both align with the previous literature supporting the use of DOACs in patients with obesity or morbid obesity, while additionally supporting the safety and efficacy of the use of DOACs in obese or morbidly obese patients with cancer at standard doses.

Underweight

Unfortunately, due to the low number of underweight patients with CAT treated with either DOACs or LMWH in this trial, no definitive conclusions can be drawn. It is promising that none of the 32 patients in the underweight per BMI group or 31 patients in the underweight per weight group had MB or death related to bleeding. A few patients in the DOAC arm did experience recurrent VTE, which is consistent of other major trials of CAT. Overall, this finding suggests that both DOACs and LMWH are safe and effective in underweight patients with CAT. In the underweight by BMI group, 5 of the 23 patients included in the DOAC arm used a lower dose than recommended for the treatment of VTE. Two patients in the underweight by BMI group had recurrent VTE; however, both were on the recommended doses of DOAC. Two patients in the DOAC arm experienced a CRNMB episode and both were on lower doses of DOAC than recommended for treatment of VTE. The lower than recommended dose of DOAC may be related to a history of bleeding or provider preference. In the weight analysis, 5 of the 19 patients included in the DOAC arm used a lower than recommended dose. There was one incidence of recurrent VTE in a patient on the recommended dose of DOAC for treatment of VTE and the one incidence of CRNMB in a patient on a lower than recommended dose.

For LMWH, standard doses of 1 mg/kg twice daily for enoxaparin is recommended for the treatment of VTE in patients with low body weight; however the data to support this recommendation is scarce [19]. All of the underweight patients included in this analysis utilized this dosing strategy, so the results of our study support this recommendation in a low body weight population in addition to expanding the support for this recommendation to patients with low body weight and cancer.

There is considerably less data on DOAC use in underweight patients compared to patients with obesity (Supplemental Table 7). In healthy subjects weighing ≤ 50 kg both apixaban and rivaroxaban have shown to have an elevated Cmax in pharmacokinetic studies [27, 28]. Arachchillage et al. similarly showed that patients weighing < 50 kg had significantly higher rivaroxaban levels; however, found that this was not associated with an increased risk of bleeding [29]. Di Minno et al. conducted a meta-analysis of DOAC use for VTE in extreme body weights in 2015 and found that DOACs had comparable efficacy to warfarin in low weight patients, although the definition of low body weight utilized in the included studies varied from < 50 kg to < 70 kg [30]. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Boonyawat et al. in 2017, concluded that low weight patients with a weight ≤ 60 kg on either DOAC or warfarin had an increased risk of thromboembolism but no increased risk of bleeding compared to other weight groups; however this included studies of both AF and VTE [31]. In a review of the literature in 2020, Covert et al. summarized the results of DOACs for the treatment of VTE in underweight patients with a weight < 60 kg. Due to the lack of data they only recommended apixaban in patients unable to take warfarin [32]. The largest study of underweight patients was from the AMPLIFY trial which was not powered to specifically for underweight patients; however, the authors did present the results for patients ≤ 60 kg. Recurrent VTE was 2.7 vs. 4.3% p = 0.43 and bleeding was 0.4 vs. 2.9%, p = 0.32 in the apixaban and warfarin arms respectively [33]. Unfortunately, the low number of underweight patients in this trial led to no definitive conclusions being able to be drawn to contribute to the prior literature. The study of the use of DOACs in the treatment of underweight patients with CAT remains a needed area of further research.

There were several limitations of this study. First it was a single center study which could reduce generalizability. In addition, due to the retrospective nature, data was limited to what was available in the electronic health record. It is possible recurrent VTE, bleeding or even death may have resulted at an outside institution and this data was not available to the investigators. Another limitation was that only the initial dose of anticoagulant was recorded, and the study did not account for dose adjustments, anti-Xa monitoring, weight changes, organ function changes, ECOG changes, platelet count worsening or provider preference. Similarly, only the initial weight was used to categorize the patient by weight or BMI. It is common for patients with cancer to have significant weight changes during their treatment and that was not accounted for during this review. Any changes in organ function or thrombocytopenia which may have contributed to recurrent VTE or bleeding was not assessed; however anticoagulation management for these conditions is usually managed in a standardized way at the institution. In addition, a cutoff of BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 for the overweight cohort was used in accordance with the WHO definition of obesity [9]; however, the ISTH report of DOAC use in obesity they defines obesity as BMI ≥ 40 mg/kg2 [12]. Overall, there was 62 (22%) patients in the DOAC arm and 30 (57.7%) in the LMWH with a BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2. Finally, the authors attempted to review the chart thoroughly to ensure the patient was actively taking anticoagulation during the six-month period but some issues such as patient compliance, temporary holds for procedures or platelet counts may have been unaccounted for.

In conclusion, the results of our trial suggest that DOACs and LMWHs are as safe and effective in the overweight population compared to normal weight for the treatment of CAT. In addition, in the overweight population, DOACs have fewer bleeding complications while maintaining similar efficacy to LMWH. While both DOACs and LMWH appear to be safe and effective in the underweight population with CAT, more data is needed to validate this observation.
