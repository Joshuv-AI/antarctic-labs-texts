Association between serum vitamin D3 levels and left ventricular diastolic dysfunction in type 2 diabetes mellitus patients with controlled glycemia: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Debashis Priyadarshan Sahoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a prevalent metabolic disorder linked to cardiovascular complications, notably left ventricular diastolic dysfunction (LVDD). Emerging evidence suggests low serum vitamin D3 levels may contribute to LVDD, though this association is unclear in patients with well-controlled glycemia.Materials and Methods:A cross-sectional study involved 160 T2DM patients aged 40–75 years, with diabetes duration ≥5 years and HbA1c ≤7%. Serum 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3 levels were measured, and echocardiographic assessments evaluated LVDD, graded by E/A and E/e’ ratios. Vitamin D3 levels were classified as deficient (<20 ng/mL), insufficient (20–30 ng/mL), or sufficient (>30 ng/mL). Regression analysis adjusted for confounders like body mass index (BMI), diabetes duration, and lipid profile to explore the vitamin D3–LVDD relationship.Results:Participants had a mean age of 45.38 ± 9.91 years and a male-to-female ratio of 1.22:1. The mean vitamin D3 level was 20.43 ± 11.82 ng/mL, with 58% of LVDD patients showing deficiency. LVDD was observed in 61% of patients (42% Grade 1, 14% Grade 2, 5% Grade 3). A significant negative correlation existed between vitamin D3 levels and LVDD severity (P = 0.018, r = −0.9570). Vitamin D3 deficiency was linked to higher LVDD grades, independent of glycemic control. Diabetes duration and BMI also correlated with LVDD (P < 0.001).Conclusion:Vitamin D3 deficiency is significantly associated with LVDD severity in T2DM patients with controlled glycemia, suggesting it as an independent risk factor. Future research should investigate vitamin D3 supplementation for cardiovascular risk management in T2DM.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a common and growing metabolic disorder, which is primarily characterized by insulin resistance and hyperglycemia.[1] T2DM is a major health concern not only due to its impact on glucose metabolism but also because of its strong association with an increased risk of developing cardiovascular diseases, which are among the leading causes of morbidity and mortality. One of the most concerning cardiovascular complications of T2DM is left ventricular diastolic dysfunction (LVDD), a condition where the ability of the heart to relax and fill properly is impaired. LVDD can progress silently over time and eventually lead to heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), a form of heart failure that often goes unnoticed until it reaches an advanced stage, significantly affecting the quality of life and increasing the risk of hospitalization and death.[2]

In recent years, research has expanded beyond traditional risk factors for cardiovascular diseases in T2DM to explore other potential contributors. Among these, vitamin D3 has emerged as a molecule of interest, traditionally recognized for its role in regulating bone health and calcium metabolism. However, recent studies have highlighted the broader significance of vitamin D3, particularly its impact on cardiovascular health. Low serum levels of vitamin D3 have been linked to a variety of cardiovascular conditions, such as hypertension, atherosclerosis, and myocardial dysfunction, suggesting that vitamin D3 deficiency may play a role in the development and progression of these conditions.[34]

In the context of T2DM, patients often present with both glycemic control challenges and cardiovascular complications, making it crucial to investigate all potential factors that could influence heart health. One emerging area of interest is the relationship between vitamin D3 levels and LVDD in diabetic patients. This has led to increased attention on whether vitamin D3 deficiency could be an important and modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular complications, particularly LVDD, in T2DM patients.

The purpose of this study is to delve deeper into the potential link between serum vitamin D3 levels and LVDD in individuals with T2DM with controlled blood sugar. By exploring this connection, researchers aim to determine whether vitamin D3 deficiency could be a contributing factor to the onset or progression of diastolic dysfunction in this patient population. Uncovering such a relationship could have significant implications for the management of cardiovascular risks in diabetic patients. It may pave the way for innovative approaches in risk assessment, early detection, and intervention strategies, potentially involving vitamin D3 supplementation or other therapies aimed at mitigating the risk of developing heart failure and other cardiovascular complications in individuals with T2DM.

Materials and Methods

This cross-sectional observational study aimed to investigate the relationship between serum vitamin D3 levels and LVDD in patients with T2DM who had controlled blood sugar levels according to the American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria.[5] A total of 160 patients diagnosed with T2DM were included in the study. All participants had their blood sugar well controlled, with an HbA1c ≤7% for the past 6 months, and underwent detailed echocardiographic evaluation to assess LVDD.

Study population and setting

The study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital, and patients were recruited from outpatient clinics. Participants were aged 40–75 years with a confirmed diagnosis of T2DM for at least 5 years and controlled blood sugar as per ADA guidelines. Eligible participants had recent serum 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3 [1,25(OH) 2D3] levels measured within the past 3 months and underwent a comprehensive echocardiographic assessment to evaluate left ventricular diastolic function within the past 6 months. Patients on stable medication regimens for at least 3 months were included to minimize variability due to changes in therapy. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for the study consisted of adult patients aged 40 to 75 years who had been diagnosed with T2DM for at least 5 years, had controlled blood sugar (HbA1c ≤ 7%) as per ADA criteria, and had recent serum 1,25(OH) 2D3 levels measured.[56] All participants had undergone echocardiography to assess left ventricular diastolic function and were on stable medication regimens for antidiabetic.

Exclusion criteria included patients with uncontrolled blood sugar (HbA1c >7%), other forms of diabetes (Type 1, gestational, or secondary diabetes), or those taking high-dose vitamin D3 supplements (≥1000 IU/day) within the past 6 months. Patients with chronic kidney disease (epidermal growth factor receptor <60 mL/min/1.73 m²), hypertension, dyslipidemia, significant cardiovascular diseases such as heart failure, myocardial infarction, or coronary artery disease and those with other endocrine disorders such as hyperparathyroidism were excluded. Additional exclusion criteria included liver disease, bone diseases requiring vitamin D3 supplementation, recent cardiac surgery, and pregnancy or breastfeeding.

Clinical and laboratory data collection

After enrolment, each participant underwent a thorough clinical evaluation. Data on demographics (age, gender, body mass index [BMI], and duration of diabetes) were collected. A detailed medical history was obtained, with attention to comorbid conditions like hypertension, dyslipidemia, and smoking status. Clinical examination included measurement of blood pressure (BP) and BMI. BP was recorded after the participant had rested for at least 5 minutes, and BMI was calculated from measured height and weight.

Laboratory investigations involved blood samples collected after overnight fasting. HbA1c was measured to confirm adequate glycemic control. Serum 1,25(OH) 2D3 levels were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), with vitamin D status categorized as[6]:

Additional tests included lipid profiles (total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, and triglycerides), renal function tests (blood urea nitrogen and serum creatinine), and serum electrolytes (calcium, phosphorus, and magnesium). These parameters were recorded to rule out potential confounding factors related to cardiovascular health and vitamin D3 metabolism.

Cardiovascular evaluation

The cardiovascular evaluation was a crucial component of the study methodology and involved a series of noninvasive imaging and diagnostic tests. Each participant underwent a chest radiography in the posteroanterior view to evaluate the size and silhouette of the heart and to detect any pulmonary changes suggestive of heart failure or fluid overload. This was followed by a detailed electrocardiography to identify any electrical abnormalities, arrhythmias, or ischemic changes, which are often seen in diabetic patients with silent cardiac disease. The cornerstone of the cardiovascular assessment was the echocardiographic evaluation performed using the Philips X7-2 machine by a single experienced operator, ensuring consistency and minimizing inter-operator variability. Echocardiography is the gold standard for assessing diastolic function, and in this study, it was used to measure key parameters indicative of diastolic dysfunction. These parameters included the early-to-late diastolic trans mitral flow velocity (E/A) ratio and the early diastolic mitral annular tissue velocity (E/e’) ratio.[7]

E/A ratio

This ratio compares the early (E) to late (A) diastolic filling velocities. Doppler echocardiography was used to measure these velocities, which reflect the ability of left ventricle to fill during early diastole (E wave) and the atrial contraction phase (A wave). Additional echocardiographic measurements included left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), and left atrial diameter, which provided a comprehensive assessment of cardiac function.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using a Statistical Software Package (e.g. SPSS IBM (2009). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics, with continuous variables (such as serum vitamin D3 levels and echocardiographic parameters) presented as means ± standard deviations and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages.

For the primary analysis, the relationship between serum vitamin D3 levels and the presence and severity of LVDD was assessed using Pearson’s correlation and regression models. Participants were grouped by their vitamin D3 status (deficient, insufficient, or sufficient), and the E/A and E/e’ ratios were compared across these groups to determine the impact of vitamin D3 levels on diastolic function. Linear regression was used to calculate the correlation among the significant study findings.

Subgroup analyses were performed to examine the association between vitamin D3 levels and cardiovascular risk factors, such as BP and lipid levels. Differences in echocardiographic parameters (E/A and E/e’ ratios) between groups were evaluated using ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test. Multivariable regression models were used to identify independent predictors of LVDD in the study population. A P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Study timeline

The study was conducted over 24 months, with data collection spanning 20 months. The remaining time was dedicated to data analysis, interpretation, and manuscript preparation.

Ethical consideration

The study was carried out in accordance with a rigorous ethical framework, as required by the hospital’s Ethics Committee. Ethical approval was secured prior to the start of the research, and all participants were fully informed about the study’s goals, procedures, potential risks, and benefits. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant to ensure voluntary participation and adherence to ethical standards. Participant confidentiality and anonymity were safeguarded throughout the study, with all personal data securely stored and used solely for research purposes. The study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, ensuring respect, dignity, and protection of participants’ rights throughout the research process.

Results

Demographics

The present study involved a total of 160 participants with a mean age of 45.38 ± 9.91 years. The gender distribution showed a male-to-female ratio of 1.22:1. The largest proportion of participants (46.2%) fell within the 41–50 years age group, followed by 29.8% who were over 50 years old, 13.5% in the 31–40 years age group, and 10.6% in the 20–30 years age group. On average, participants had been living with diabetes for 9.83 ± 4.86 years with mean HbA1c of 5.7% ±1.1%. The mean BMI of the study population was 23.70 ± 2.82 kg/m².

Among the study participants, the distribution of LVDD grades was as follows: 39% of participants had LVDD Grade 0 (no dysfunction), 42% had Grade 1 (mild dysfunction), 14% had Grade 2 (moderate dysfunction), and 5% had Grade 3 (severe dysfunction). The mean E/A ratio, which is an indicator of diastolic function, was 1.03 ± 0.36, while the mean E/e’ ratio, another key marker for assessing diastolic function, was 7.80 ± 1.94.

Statistical analysis revealed no significant correlation between LVDD and either age or gender (P = 0.19 and P = 0.99, respectively). However, there was a significant correlation between LVDD and both the duration of T2DM and BMI (P = 0.00000016, r = 0.6915 and P = 0.00000225, r = 0.4365, respectively). Association of serum vitamin D3 with left ventricular diastolic dysfunction

The majority of patients with LVDD were found to have vitamin D3 deficiency, with 58% of LVDD patients exhibiting low levels of vitamin D3. The most common LVDD grade among participants was Grade 1, affecting 42% of patients. A significant correlation was identified between serum vitamin D3 levels and LVDD (P = 0.018, r = −0.3011), indicating that lower vitamin D3 levels are strongly associated with the presence and severity of LVDD. Discussion

Prevalence of left ventricular diastolic dysfunction in Type 2 diabetes mellitus patients with controlled blood sugar

In the current study, 61% of the T2DM patients exhibited some degree of LVDD, despite having controlled blood sugar (HbA1c ≤ 7%). This high prevalence of LVDD, even in those with well-controlled glycemia, reflects the multifactorial nature of cardiovascular complications in diabetes. LVDD in diabetic patients is a concern because it often progresses silently, eventually contributing to HFpEF. The fact that a large proportion of patients exhibited mild to moderate LVDD (42% with Grade 1 and 14% with Grade 2 LVDD) underscores the subtle progression of this dysfunction. It highlights the inadequacy of glycemic control alone in preventing cardiovascular complications.

Vitamin D3 Deficiency and left ventricular diastolic dysfunction: Correlating with previous studies

In the current study, 58% of the patients with LVDD had low vitamin D3 levels, with an average serum concentration of 20.43 ± 11.82 ng/mL. These findings are consistent with previously observed thresholds, where vitamin D3 deficiency or insufficiency has been associated with adverse cardiac outcomes. This strengthens the hypothesis that vitamin D3 deficiency might be an independent risk factor for cardiovascular dysfunction, especially LVDD, in T2DM patients.

The strong negative correlation between vitamin D3 levels and LVDD found in this study (P = 0.018, r = −0.9570) aligns with prior research suggesting that vitamin D3 deficiency may influence cardiovascular health. In recent years, several studies have explored the relationship between vitamin D3 deficiency, LVDD, and T2DM, shedding light on the potential mechanisms and therapeutic implications.

Recent research has highlighted the strong link between vitamin D3 deficiency and adverse cardiac outcomes, especially among diabetic individuals. For instance, Gotsman et al. (2020) identified low vitamin D3 levels as an independent predictor of LVDD in patients with diabetes. This deficiency appears to negatively affect myocardial calcium handling and promotes inflammation, both of which contribute to cardiac dysfunction in these individuals.[8]

Additionally, Pokhrel et al. observed that vitamin D3 deficiency correlates with poor glycemic control and dyslipidemia in patients with T2DM. These conditions significantly heighten the risk of cardiovascular disease, suggesting that addressing vitamin D3 deficiency could help improve cardiovascular health in these patients. They further noted that supplementation might help alleviate glycemic and lipid disturbances.[9]

Another study by Papandreu et al. (2015) emphasized the association between vitamin D3 deficiency and an increased risk of both type 1 and type 2 diabetes, as well as cardiovascular diseases. The researchers found that low vitamin D3 levels are linked to insulin resistance and impaired glucose metabolism, affecting individuals across different populations. Moreover, they pointed out that genetic factors, such as variations in the vitamin D3 receptor gene, may influence susceptibility to diabetes. Notably, they observed that vitamin D3 supplementation could improve glycemic control and reduce cardiovascular risk factors.[10]

In a recent meta-analysis, Iqhrammullah et al. (2024) concluded that serum vitamin D3 levels are a crucial factor in the development of cardiovascular diseases among diabetic patients. They advocate for the careful monitoring of vitamin D3 levels to mitigate the associated risks. However, the potential benefits of vitamin D3 supplementation have been debated.[11]

These findings collectively underscore the importance of understanding the complex role of vitamin D3 in managing diabetes and cardiovascular health, while also highlighting the need for individualized approaches to supplementation and treatment.

In contrast with our study and abovementioned studies, a study conducted by Miao et al. inferred that high-dose vitamin D3 supplementation did not improve biomarkers of glycemia, inflammation, neurohormonal activation, lipids, or cardiovascular outcomes.[12]

Possible mechanisms of the correlation between vitamin D3 and left ventricular diastolic dysfunction

Calcium homeostasis and myocardial function: One of the primary mechanisms through which vitamin D3 impacts cardiac function is by regulating calcium homeostasis. Vitamin D3 enhances calcium absorption in the intestines and influences calcium levels in the bloodstream, crucial for cardiac contractility. Myocardial relaxation and filling depend heavily on the proper regulation of intracellular calcium. In LVDD, impaired calcium handling leads to increased stiffness of the left ventricle. Vitamin D3 deficiency may exacerbate this dysfunction by altering calcium cycling within cardiomyocytes, contributing to impaired diastolic relaxation[13]Inflammation and oxidative stress: T2DM is characterized by chronic low-grade inflammation and increased oxidative stress, both of which are implicated in the pathogenesis of LVDD. Vitamin D3 has been shown to have anti-inflammatory properties, suppressing proinflammatory cytokines like tumour necrosis factor-α, interleukin (IL)-6, and IL-1β, which can damage cardiac myocytes and promote fibrosis. A deficiency in vitamin D3 could therefore worsen myocardial inflammation, accelerating the structural changes in the heart that lead to diastolic dysfunction[1415]Endothelial Dysfunction and Myocardial Ischemia: Vitamin D3 also plays a crucial role in maintaining endothelial function. Deficiency in vitamin D3 can lead to endothelial dysfunction, which impairs nitric oxide (NO) production and contributes to arterial stiffness and increased myocardial ischemia. Impaired endothelial function limits coronary blood flow, reducing oxygen delivery to the myocardium during diastole, which can further worsen LVDD[1617]Renin Angiotensin Aldosterone System (RAAS) Modulation: Another mechanism linking vitamin D3 deficiency to LVDD is its role in modulating the RAAS. Vitamin D3 suppresses renin expression, and its deficiency has been associated with activation of the RAAS. Overactivation of RAAS contributes to hypertension, myocardial hypertrophy, and fibrosis, all of which can exacerbate diastolic dysfunction. Elevated RAAS activity in diabetic patients with vitamin D3 deficiency could explain the higher prevalence and severity of LVDD observed in this study.[1819]

Correlation with demographic and clinical variables

While vitamin D3 deficiency appeared as a significant factor in LVDD, the study also highlighted the role of other clinical variables. There was a significant positive correlation between LVDD and both the duration of T2DM (P = 0.00000016, r = 0.068) and BMI (P = 0.00000225, r = 0.5937), suggesting that these factors also contribute to the development of diastolic dysfunction.

Limitations

Despite the significant findings, the present study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish a causal relationship between vitamin D3 deficiency and LVDD in T2DM patients. Longitudinal studies would be necessary to determine whether low vitamin D levels directly contribute to the progression of LVDD over time. Additionally, the sample size of 160 participants may limit the generalizability of the results to the broader diabetic population, particularly those with more diverse comorbidities or different ethnic backgrounds. The exclusion of patients with uncontrolled diabetes or advanced kidney disease or those on high-dose vitamin D3 supplementation might have also introduced selection bias, potentially affecting the external validity of the findings. Another limitation is the reliance on a single measurement of serum vitamin D3 levels and echocardiographic parameters, which may not fully capture the dynamic nature of these variables over time. Furthermore, potential confounders such as dietary intake, physical activity, and sun exposure, which could influence vitamin D3 status, were not accounted for in the analysis. Finally, while the study adjusted for certain cardiovascular risk factors, it did not account for others like inflammatory markers, which could have provided additional insights into the mechanisms linking vitamin D3 deficiency and diastolic dysfunction.

Conclusion

This study provides convincing evidence that low serum vitamin D3 levels are significantly associated with LVDD in T2DM patients with well-controlled blood sugar levels. The findings suggest that vitamin D3 deficiency is a modifiable risk factor for LVDD, which has important therapeutic implications. Routine screening for vitamin D3 levels and appropriate supplementation may help mitigate cardiovascular risks, particularly diastolic dysfunction, in diabetic patients. Moreover, comprehensive cardiovascular risk management, including lifestyle modifications and potential anti-inflammatory or RAAS-targeted therapies, should be considered to address the multifactorial nature of cardiac dysfunction in T2DM. Future research, especially in the form of randomized controlled trials, is needed to further elucidate the role of vitamin D3 in preventing or reversing LVDD in this population.
