A temporally controlled isothermal amplification-CRISPR/Cas12a platform for the rapid detection of monkeypox virus
Authors: Zhijie Wei, Xun Xu, Qiao Qiao, Jinlei Guo, Tao Wu, Heng Rong, Shuo Ning, Xiaojuan Zhu, Kangchen Zhao, Jincan Ke, Lele He, Ying Chi, Yiyue Ge, Lunbiao Cui, Xiaoyan Min
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGiven the continuous threat posed by emerging and re-emerging infectious diseases worldwide, a rapid, sensitive, and practical molecular detection technology is urgently required for timely point-of-care (POC) diagnosis. Although the quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) based technologies exhibit high sensitivity and specificity compared with traditional pathogen detection methods, they are not applicable for POC detection scenarios.MethodsBased on the photocontrolled principle, this study established a universal photoactivation strategy for LbCas12a by modifying four sites in the repeat region of LbCas12a crRNA with the photocleavable protecting group 6-nitropiperonyloxymethyl (NPOM). This strategy was integrated with multienzyme isothermal rapid amplification (MIRA) to develop a photocontrolled one-pot rapid detection method for monkeypox virus (MPXV), termed the temporally controlled MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a (TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a) assay.ResultsThe TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay exhibited a 100-fold improvement in detection sensitivity over the conventional one-step assay, achieving a limit of detection (LOD) of 3.9 copies per reaction, which was comparable to stepwise detection assay. The entire assay can be completed within 40 min, faster than the widely used qPCR. In clinical sample detection, this method showed good consistency with qPCR, with a Kappa coefficient of 0.980 (P < 0.001), a sensitivity of 98.4%, and a specificity of 100%.ConclusionsThis method effectively avoids amplicon contamination while maintaining high sensitivity, and exhibits excellent universality and expandability. It enables detection of other pathogens simply by modifying the spacer sequence of crRNA, providing a novel technical approach and research perspective for POC detection of MPXV and other pathogens.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12985-026-03210-5.

Introduction

In light of the global co-epidemic of emerging and re-emerging infectious diseases and traditional infectious diseases (e.g., monkeypox, COVID-19, influenza) in recent years, rapid and accurate detection of pathogenic microorganisms is crucial for infectious disease surveillance, public health security, and clinical diagnosis. In contrast to conventional pathogen detection approaches, including pathogen isolation and culture as well as serological detection, molecular detection technologies typified by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) possess prominent merits including rapid assay speed, high sensitivity and excellent specificity [1–3]. However, such methods rely on sophisticated professional instruments and equipment, and impose rigorous requirements on laboratory settings. Consequently, they can hardly meet the demands for point-of-care (POC) detection scenarios, namely instant results, portability and ease of operation [4].

The clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats and associated protein (CRISPR/Cas) system is an adaptive immune system in prokaryotes, which was initially applied in gene editing [5–7]. In 2016, this system was first applied to nucleic acid detection [8], pioneering the application of CRISPR technology in molecular diagnostics. The combination of CRISPR and isothermal amplification for nucleic acid detection can further enhance detection sensitivity. Moreover, the entire detection process is performed at a constant temperature, eliminating the reliance on sophisticated instruments and equipment and thus possessing point-of-care diagnostic applicability. A number of studies have reported performing isothermal amplification and CRISPR detection in a stepwise manner, which is not only cumbersome in operation but also introduces the risk of aerosol contamination [9–11]. However, when researchers attempt to integrate isothermal amplification and CRISPR/Cas detection into a single tube, a significant decrease in the overall detection sensitivity of the system is observed [12, 13]. This phenomenon is primarily attributed to the continuous degradation of amplicons and primers by activated Cas nucleases in the reaction system, which reduces the efficiency of isothermal amplification [14]. To improve detection sensitivity, the “spatial isolation” strategy, which achieves the separation of isothermal amplification and CRISPR/Cas detection in a single tube, has emerged as a prominent research focus [15–17]. Nevertheless, these approaches are associated with disadvantages including cumbersome manipulation, poor reaction reproducibility, and dependence on microfluidic devices. Some studies have attempted to inhibit this competitive cleavage by attenuating the activity of Cas enzymes—for instance, using suboptimal protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sites [18] or adding Cas enzyme activity inhibitors (e.g., heparin sodium) [19]. Yet, these methods cannot fully abrogate the cleavage of nucleic acid substrates by Cas enzymes, and the attenuated CRISPR system also compromises the final detection efficiency.

In recent years, photocontrolled isothermal amplification-CRISPR/Cas nucleic acid detection technologies have attracted considerable attention, as they enable precise and flexible on-off control over the activity of Cas enzymes. Typical examples include modifying the spacer region of CRISPR RNA (crRNA) with the photocleavable protecting group 6-nitropiperonyloxymethyl (NPOM) [20], as well as designing photocleavable DNA linkers that can complementarily pair with the crRNA spacer region [21, 22], The timing of CRISPR reaction initiation can be regulated through simple and rapid light irradiation. Although these approaches effectively resolve the compatibility issue between the CRISPR system and the amplification system in a single tube, they are limited by poor universality. That is, the modification sites and number of photocaged groups, as well as linker sequences, must be reoptimized for every new detection target. Alternatively, another study reported the full-base acylation of crRNA using photoprotective agents [23], but this approach is tedious to operate and costly. Given that the crRNA direct repeat region is highly conserved and responsible for Cas enzyme binding [24], this study focuses on the modification of photolabile protecting groups on the direct repeat region of LbCas12a crRNA to establish a universal Cas12a photoactivation strategy. Using monkeypox virus (MPXV) as a representative target, we integrated this strategy with the multienzyme isothermal rapid amplification (MIRA) technique to develop a photocontrolled one-pot rapid detection method for MPXV ( termed as temporally controlled MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a, TC-MIRA- CRISPR/Cas12a ). This method not only ensures high detection sensitivity but also effectively prevents amplicon contamination. The entire assay can be completed within 40 min. Most importantly, this strategy exhibits excellent universality and extensibility, enabling the detection of various pathogens merely by altering the spacer sequence of crRNA.

Materials and methods

Clinical samples and virus strains

A total of 103 clinical samples (including serum, throat swabs, and vesicle fluid) were obtained from individuals suspected to be infected with MPXV in Jiangsu Province, and all samples were confirmed by virus isolation and/or qPCR.The MPXV strains were isolated from vesicle fluid in Biosafety Level-3 laboratory of Jiangsu Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention.

Primer, probe, crRNA, and double-stranded DNA (dsDNA)target design

A total of 37 representative full-length genome sequences of MPXV were downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) GenBank database and subjected to sequence alignment. The conserved region of H4L gene was selected for the design of crRNA and dsDNA targets, which were applied to develop the universal Cas12a photoactivation strategy, as well as forward and reverse MIRA amplification primers, which were utilized to establish the TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay.The sequence of the fluorescent probe used to characterize the trans-cleavage activity of LbCas12a was obtained from a previous study [25]. All primers, probes, uncaged crRNA (wild-type crRNA without NPOM photocleavable group modifications), and dsDNA targets used in this study were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China, and the detailed sequences are listed in Table 1.

Nucleic acid extraction

DNA was extracted from 200 µL of each sample or viral strains (inactivated in the BSL-3 laboratory) using the automated nucleic acid extractor GeneRotex 96 (Tianlong, Xi’an, China) and a viral DNA extraction kit (Tianlong, Xi’an, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted nucleic acids were stored at − 80 °C until further use.

Double-stranded DNA target preparation

To generate the dsDNA targets, the target strand and non-target strand (10 µM each) were added into 1× PCR buffer (Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Japan). The mixture was annealed at 70 °C for 5 min, followed by gradual cooling to room temperature. The annealed products were stored at − 80 °C until further use.

Nucleic acid–protein interaction force prediction

The nucleic acid–protein interaction force was predicted using AlphaFold3 software, available at https://alphafoldserver.com. The crystal structure of LbCas12a (PDB ID: 6KL9-A) was retrieved from the NCBI database.

Conventional CRISPR-LbCas12a reaction

In a 20 µL reaction system, 250 nM LbCas12a (Cat. No. 32108-03, Tolobio, Shanghai, China), 250 nM uncaged crRNA, 800 nM FQTA probe and 1 nM dsDNA target were mixed in 1× HOLMES Buffer for Cas12a, the accompanying buffer of the LbCas12a kit. The mixture was transferred to a real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min, with fluorescence detection performed every minute.

Photoactivatable CRISPR-LbCas12a reaction

In a 20 µL reaction system, 250 nM LbCas12a, 250 nM caged crRNA (modified crRNA with NPOM photocleavable group; Hippobio Co., Ltd., Huzhou, Jiangsu, China; Table 2), 800 nM FQTA probe, and 1 nM dsDNA target were mixed in 1× HOLMES Buffer for Cas12a. The reaction mixture containing caged crRNA was then either subjected to 30 s of irradiation with a 35 W UV lamp (λ = 365 nm) or left untreated. Finally, the mixture was transferred to a real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min, with fluorescence detection performed every minute.

MIRA primer pair screening

MIRA kits (containing Buffer A, Buffer B and lyophilized enzyme mix) were purchased from Amp-Future Biotech Co., Ltd., Changzhou, China; Cat. No. WLB8201KIT. A two-step protocol was employed to screen MIRA primer pairs, and the experimental procedures are described as follows.

First, a 25 µL MIRA reaction system was prepared, consisting of 14.7 µL Buffer A reconstituted with lyophilized enzyme mix, 4.55 µL nuclease-free water, 1 µL forward primer (10 µM), 1 µL reverse primer (10 µM), 1.25 µL Buffer B and 2.5 µL DNA template. After amplification at 37 °C for 20 min, 5 µL of the amplification product was transferred into a 15 µL CRISPR/Cas12a detection system, which contained 2 µL 10× HOLMES Buffer for Cas12a, 1.6 µL FQTA probe (10 µM), 0.5 µL LbCas12a (10 µM), 0.5 µL crRNA (10 µM) and 10.4 µL nuclease-free water. Finally, the mixture was transferred to a real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument and incubated at 37 °C for 40 min, with fluorescence detection performed every minute.

Conventional one-step MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection

The procedures for conventional one-step MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection are described as follows. First, the MIRA amplification mixture was added to the bottom of an Eppendorf tube, which consisted of 17.64 µL Buffer A reconstituted with lyophilized enzyme mix, 0.15 µL forward primer (10 µM), 0.15 µL reverse primer (10 µM), 1.5 µL Buffer B and 1 µL DNA target. Subsequently, the CRISPR reaction mixture was added to the tube cap, containing 3 µL 10× HOLMES Buffer for Cas12a, 2.4 µL FQTA fluorescent probe (10 µM), 1.05 µL LbCas12a (10 µM), 1.05 µL crRNA (40 µM) and 2.06 µL nuclease-free water. Finally, these solutions were mixed by centrifugation. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 90 min, with fluorescence detection performed every minute.

TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection

The experimental procedures for TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection are described as follows.

First, the MIRA amplification mixture was added to the bottom of an Eppendorf tube, consisting of 17.64 µL Buffer A reconstituted with lyophilized enzyme mix, 0.15 µL forward primer (10 µM), 0.15 µL reverse primer (10 µM), 1.5 µL Buffer B and 1 µL DNA target. Subsequently, the CRISPR reaction mixture was added to the tube cap, containing 3 µL 10× HOLMES Buffer for Cas12a, 2.4 µL FQTA fluorescent probe (10 µM), 1.05 µL LbCas12a (10 µM), 1.05 µL caged crRNA (40 µM) and 2.06 µL nuclease-free water. After mixing by centrifugation, the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 10, 20, or 30 min to perform MIRA amplification. A 35 W UV lamp (λ = 365 nm) was used to irradiate the reaction system for 30 s to activate the LbCas12a system. Finally, the mixture was further incubated at 37 °C for an additional 80, 70, or 60 min to conduct CRISPR detection, with fluorescence signals measured every minute using the real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument. The whole process is presented in Fig. 1.

Analytical sensitivity of TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay

Digital PCR was performed using the QIAcuity One (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) and the QIAcuity Probe PCR Kit (Cat. No. 250101, QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) to quantify MPXV strains. Serially diluted MPXV templates at concentrations of 104, 103, 102, 101, and 100 copies/µL were prepared to evaluate the detection sensitivity. Each dilution contained eight technical replicates, and probit regression analysis was conducted on the detection results to determine the limit of detection (LOD).

Analytical specificity of TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay

Six types of viral nucleic acids, including herpes simplex virus (HSV), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), enterovirus A71 (EV-A71), dengue virus (DENV), coxsackievirus A16 (CVA16), and measles virus (MeV), were used as templates to evaluate the specificity of the established TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay for MPXV. All viral strains were collected from routine infectious disease surveillance program of the Jiangsu Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention, and each virus was verified by qPCR.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 23 software. The limit of detection (LOD) of the established assay was determined via probit regression analysis. The concordance between TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection and qPCR detection was evaluated using the Kappa statistic. The schematic diagrams in this study were created with BioGDP.com [26].

Results

Development of a universal photoactivatable strategy for cas12a

In this study, the AlphaFold3 software was employed to predict the nucleic acid-protein interaction forces. Uridine ribonucleotides within the repeat sequence of crRNA1 that may influence stem-loop formation were replaced with adenosine ribonucleotides (Fig. 2a) to simulate the disruption of hydrogen bonding interactions induced by 6-nitropiperonyloxymethyl-caged thymidine (NPOM-dt, Fig. 2b) incorporation at this locus, thus predicting alterations in the interaction forces between the crRNA stem-loop structure and Cas12a. We predicted a total of 26 mutant crRNA1 variants with mutations at 2 to 5 distinct quantities and sites (U-to-A substitution, Supplementary Table 1). The interaction capacity between these mutated crRNA1 variants and Cas12a was evaluated based on the predicted template modeling (pTM) and interface predicted template modeling (ipTM) scores, which respectively reflect the reliability of the overall structure and protein–nucleic acid interface binding (detailed evaluation criteria are listed in Supplementary Table 1).

(a) The stem-loop structural sequence of crRNA1, used for prediction of nucleic acid-protein interaction force, with uridine ribonucleotides mutated to adenosine ribonucleotides; (b) Structural schematic of 6-nitropiperonyloxymethyl-caged thymidine

Six mutant crRNA1 variants that were predicted to have a high likelihood of losing all binding to Cas12a (ipTM < 0.6, Supplementary Table 2) were selected, and six distinct NPOM-dT caged crRNA1 variants were synthesized accordingly (Fig. 3). These caged crRNA1 variants were employed to establish a universal photoactivatable strategy for Cas12a. The results demonstrated that both Caged crRNA1-e (Fig. 3e) and Caged crRNA1-f (Fig. 3f) exhibited significant fluorescence signals without 365 nm UV light irradiation, indicating that these two modification strategies failed to fully silence the activity of crRNA. In contrast, the other four caged crRNA variants exhibited no observable fluorescence signals in the non-irradiated group. Nevertheless, their activity recovery efficiencies after light irradiation varied considerably. Among them, Caged crRNA1-b achieved the most favorable activity recovery, with its activity being nearly consistent with uncaged crRNA1 when both reaction rate and endpoint fluorescence value were comprehensively evaluated (Fig. 3b). To verify the universality of this strategy for crRNAs with different spacer sequences, we adopted the identical modification strategy used for Caged crRNA1-b, and only replaced the spacer sequence to test two additional caged crRNAs (crRNA2 and crRNA3). The results demonstrated that this modification strategy yielded consistent activity silencing and recovery effects across all three crRNAs (Fig. 4), suggesting that the modification strategy possesses universality and scalability.

Optimization of the reaction conditions for TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection of MPXV

After identifying the optimal modification strategy for the repeat region of crRNA, we integrated the photoactivatable CRISPR detection assay with MIRA to establish a TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay for the detection of MPXV. Using MPXV genomic DNA as the template, three primer pairs were designed targeting the specific recognition region of crRNA1 (H4L gene), and the optimal primer pair was screened out via the two-step method (Fig. 5a). Among these pairs, the F3R3 combination exhibited the most favorable overall amplification performance; thus, F3R3 was selected as the primer pair for subsequent experiments to establish the single-tube TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection system, and then the key reaction parameters were optimized sequentially. First, the amplification temperature for MIRA was optimized (Fig. 5b). The results showed that the reaction efficiency was optimal at 37 °C, which was thus designated as the final reaction temperature. Next, for the concentration of the primers(Fig. 5c), the highest amplification efficiency was observed at a final primer concentration of 50 nM, which was therefore used for all subsequent experiments. When optimizing the dosage of B buffer (Mg²⁺ concentration, Fig. 5d), 1.5 µL was identified as the optimal dosage. Subsequently, during the optimization of Cas12a enzyme concentration, it was considered that the single-stranded DNA-binding protein present in the MIRA enzyme system would adsorb crRNA [27, 28], resulting in the effective concentration of crRNA in the system being lower than the actual added amount. Therefore, excess crRNA (2 µM) was added to the system to screen for a suitable Cas12a enzyme concentration(Fig. 5e). The results demonstrated a positive correlation between the reaction rate and enzyme concentration within a certain range; the reaction rate reached saturation at an enzyme concentration of 350 nM, and no further increase was observed with a continued rise in enzyme concentration. Hence, 350 nM was selected as the final working concentration. Finally, we optimized the concentration of caged crRNA(Fig. 5f). The results indicated that the reaction rate reached saturation at 1400 nM, which was confirmed as the final working concentration for caged crRNA.

Sensitivity evaluation of the TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay

Based on the optimized experimental conditions, we performed a sensitivity assay and determined the LOD of this assay. Using serially diluted MPXV DNA as the template with the amplification time set at 30 min (Fig. 6a, b), the results showed that the photocontrolled single-tube detection assay could detect template DNA at a concentration of 100 copies per reaction (at a total reaction time of 40 min). Subsequently, a parallel assay was conducted using the conventional one-step assay under the same experimental system (Fig. 6c). It was found that due to the competitive cleavage of templates by the CRISPR reaction, the conventional one-step assay could only detect template DNA at 10² copies per reaction (with the reaction time extended to 90 min). In comparison, the TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay exhibited a 100-fold higher sensitivity.

(a) Sensitivity assay of the TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay; (b) Fluorescence intensities in the sensitivity assay of the TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay after a 40-min reaction; (c) Comparison of fluorescence intensities between the TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay and the conventional one-step assay after a 90-min reaction

Furthermore, to shorten the detection time, we respectively set the MIRA amplification time to 10 min, 20 min and 30 min, to compare the effects of different amplification durations on the detection performance for low-concentration DNA templates (Fig. 7). The results indicated that when the amplification duration was 10–20 min, the template DNA at a concentration of 10¹ copies per reaction could be detected within 20 to 30 min; when the amplification duration was 30 min, the template DNA at 10⁰ copies per reaction could be detected within 40 min. Therefore, shortening the amplification duration contributes to a further improvement in detection speed when analyzing samples with relatively high viral loads. Meanwhile, to determine the LOD of the established assay, eight replicate experiments were performed for each serial dilution (10³, 10², 10¹, and 10⁰ copies/µL) of MPXV DNA template (Table 3). Probit regression analysis of the data yielded an LOD of 3.9 copies per reaction for this method, which is close to the LOD of 1 copy per reaction achieved by the two-step assay.

Specificity evaluation of the TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay

To verify the specificity of the TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay for MPXV, we used MPXV-specific primers and crRNA to detect six different viruses, including herpes simplex virus (HSV), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), enterovirus A71 (EV-A71), dengue virus (DENV), coxsackievirus A16 (CVA16), and measles virus (MeV). The results showed that this assay exhibited no cross-reactivity with any of these six viruses (Fig. 8a, b), indicating that the TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay possesses excellent specificity.

Evaluation of the detection performance in clinical samples

Nucleic acids were extracted from 103 clinical MPXV samples using an automated nucleic acid extractor and subjected to parallel detection by qPCR and the single-tube TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay (Table 4). With qPCR results as the reference standard, the accuracy of the established assay in clinical sample detection was evaluated. The results showed that the TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay exhibited a sensitivity of 98.4% (61/62), a specificity of 100% (41/41), a positive predictive value of 100% (61/61), and a negative predictive value of 97.6% (41/42). The Kappa test revealed excellent consistency between the two detection methods (Kappa = 0.980, P < 0.001, n = 103).

Discussion

The integration of isothermal amplification technology and CRISPR/Cas technology into a single tube can simplify experimental operations and effectively prevent amplicon contamination. However, addressing the system compatibility between the two reactions often involves a compromise in detection sensitivity, or the employment of custom-fabricated reaction vessels. In this study, we developed a universal photoactivatable strategy for Cas12a activity regulation by modifying the direct repeat region of crRNA with NPOM photolabile caging groups. By coupling this strategy with MIRA, an ultrasensitive photocontrolled single-tube nucleic acid detection platform was established. The CRISPR reaction can be precisely initiated with only 30 s of UV irradiation, which does not interfere with template amplification while enabling highly efficient recovery of Cas12a activity.

First, we employed AlphaFold3 software to preliminarily screen the key sites affecting the binding of crRNA to the Cas12a. Among 26 potential modification combinations, we selected and synthesized six types of NPOM-caged crRNAs bearing 2–5 NPOM moieties at varying quantities and positions. Subsequently, a fluorescence reporter assay was performed to evaluate the silencing effect of each modification strategy on crRNA activity, as well as the recovery efficiency upon photoactivation. The optimal modification strategy was ultimately determined: one NPOM-dT was individually introduced at the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, and 4th uracil ribonucleotides in the crRNA stem-loop structure (5′ to 3′). The incorporation of four NPOM-dT modifications disrupts the hydrogen bonding within the stem-loop structure, preventing its spatial folding into the functional secondary structure required for Cas12a binding, and consequently silencing both the cis- and trans-cleavage activities of Cas12a. Following exponential amplification of the target template, the NPOM modifications can be rapidly photolyzed by 30 s of UV irradiation, thereby relieving the inhibitory effect. The activity of the decaged crRNA (crRNA with NPOM photocleavable group removed by UV irradiation) is nearly identical to that of the unmodified counterpart, enabling efficient activation of Cas12a cleavage activity to accomplish signal amplification and output. During the course of this study, one report demonstrated that embedding a single NPOM-dT at one site within the repeat recognition sequence (RRS) of crRNA could silence crRNA activity [29]. The mechanism was the interference with Cas12a-mediated precursor crRNA processing, which prevented the conversion of precursor crRNA into mature crRNA and the subsequent formation of a functional ribonucleoprotein complex with Cas12a. As a result, the activation of both cis- and trans-cleavage activities of Cas12a was ultimately inhibited, and the LOD for Mycoplasma pneumoniae achieved by this method was 18 copies per reaction. In the present study, the established single-tube TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay achieved a LOD of 3.9 copies per reaction for MPXV in clinical samples, which is comparable to that of the two-step MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay (1 copy per reaction). Compared with the one-step MPXV detection assay developed by Feng et al. [12], which exhibited a LOD of 75 copies per reaction, the analytical sensitivity of our method was improved by approximately 19.2-fold, representing a significant advantage in the detection of samples from the early stage of infection or those with low viral loads.

Other studies have also achieved sequential reactions of isothermal amplification and CRISPR detection in a single tube via alternative physical approaches, with favorable detection sensitivity. For instance, Curtin et al. [30] devised a combinable 3D-printed microfluidic chip, while Lin et al. [31] proposed a phase separation strategy mediated by glycerol viscosity. However, in comparison with the photoactivatable strategy established in the present study, these approaches either involve cumbersome operations that compromise reaction reproducibility, or yield detection performance susceptible to ambient temperature, accompanied by uncertain timing of CRISPR reaction initiation.

Regarding the development of photocontrollable strategies, previous studies have designed photocleavable DNA linkers complementary to the crRNA spacer region [32] or modified the spacer sequence with NPOM photolabile groups [20] to prevent target recognition and binding, thereby inhibiting Cas12a activity. However, these approaches suffer from inadequate universality. The four-base photolabile group modification acting on the crRNA repeat region developed in the present study does not involve protection or modification of the crRNA spacer region—the domain responsible for target recognition and complementary pairing. This implies that the modification strategy remains applicable even when the detection target is altered, without the need for re-screening the modification quantities and positions, thus exhibiting remarkable advantages in universality and expandability. Given that the repeat sequence of crRNA is highly conserved across the Cas12a family, we speculate that this modification strategy can be extended to other Cas12a variants, such as FnCas12a and AsCas12a. Furthermore, the mechanism of the photoactivatable strategy established in this study is to modulate the initiation time of the CRISPR/Cas cleavage reaction. Both DNA and RNA pathogens are converted into double-stranded DNA products after MIRA/RT-MIRA amplification, which serve as the direct substrates for the CRISPR/Cas cleavage reaction. Therefore, this photoactivatable strategy is also theoretically applicable to RNA pathogens.

In addition, MIRA amplification technology enables exponential amplification of DNA templates at a constant temperature. The high-abundance amplification products can rapidly trigger the robust cis- and trans-cleavage activities of Cas12a, generating obvious fluorescent signals within dozens of minutes. The single-tube TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection platform established in the present study allows result determination within 40 min. In contrast, the qPCR method typically requires 1.5 h owing to its reliance on thermal cycling. Therefore, our method greatly shortens the detection time while maintaining analytical sensitivity, presenting outstanding performance advantages for POC detection. Finally, the timing of Cas12a activation is regulated by light illumination in this study, rendering the detection process flexible and controllable. For the detection of samples with high viral loads, the assay time can be further shortened by adjusting the timing of photoactivation. Furthermore, the entire reaction is performed without opening the tube, eliminating the risk of aerosol contamination.

Although our detection method exhibits favorable performance and application potential for POC detection, this study also has certain limitations. First, the TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay relies on a nucleic acid extractor for nucleic acid extraction, yet the impact of different nucleic acid extraction methods, as well as rapid nucleic acid extraction and nucleic acid extraction-free technologies, on the final detection results has not been verified. In addition, the evaluation of the detection performance in clinical samples was based on a relatively small sample size, which may compromise the representativeness and comprehensiveness of the results. Finally, the current study design targets only a single detection target. In future, priority can be given to developing multiplex detection capability to enable simultaneous diagnosis of multiple pathogens in a single test, thereby enhancing its high-throughput detection capacity in the field of rapid on-site diagnosis.

Conclusion

Collectively, this study establishes a universal photoactivation strategy for Cas12a, and using MPXV as a representative pathogen, develops a photocontrolled one-pot rapid detection assay (TC-MIRA-CRISPR/Cas12a) for MPXV. This assay exhibits high analytical sensitivity and specificity, effectively avoids amplicon contamination, and possesses excellent universality and expandability, providing a novel research perspective for POC pathogen detection.
