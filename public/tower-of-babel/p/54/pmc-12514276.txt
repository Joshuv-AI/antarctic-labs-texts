Chemically-defined medium formulation and adaptation method for supporting growth of endothelial cells
Authors: Laura A E Brunmaier, Travis W Walker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemically-defined (CD) media offer critical advantages for in vitro systems, requiring consistency, tunability, and component transparency, particularly in applications such as bioassays, drug testing, and translational research. While serum-containing media remain widely used, reliable protocols for adapting cells to CD medium, especially for sensitive adherent cell types, are underreported. This study presents a streamlined protocol for adapting human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) to a custom CD medium, with a focus on optimizing weaning strategies and identifying surface coatings that support robust attachment and viability. The protocol evaluates gradual and stepwise adaptation approaches to minimize cellular stress, while incorporating defined extracellular matrix proteins to promote adherence under serum-free conditions. Among tested coatings, fibronectin substantially improved cell attachment and viability during CD medium adaptation, outperforming laminin and collagen IV. To support quantifiable and reproducible tracking of cell growth, we applied a trainable AI-based image analysis method for confluence assessment throughout the adaptation process. This work provides a reproducible, modular framework for transitioning HUVECs, and potentially other human cell lines, to CD medium, while preserving cell health and experimental utility across multiple passages. The methodology may also facilitate the design of quantitative and ethically aligned bioassays, accelerating progress in standardized cell culture practices.Subject terms: Assay systems, Biologics, Biomimetics

Introduction

The development of cell culture media that supports consistent and physiologically relevant growth conditions is central to advancing in vitro research, particularly in applications requiring controlled environments, such as drug development, disease modeling, and tissue engineering. Xeno-free, chemically-defined (CD) media operate as a powerful tool to address the limitations of undefined supplements, offering advantages in reproducibility, mechanistic studies, and regulatory compliance. Removal of undefined or animal components from translational research closely aligns with federal initiatives to reduce the use of animals in research and increase safety, such as the FDA New Approach Methodologies (NAM), the FDA Modernization Act 2.0, and the NIH National Toxicology Program Tox21 (NTP), which is a collaborative effort between agencies. However, transitioning established cell lines from serum-containing (SC) media to CD medium remains challenging due to potential growth inhibition, altered adhesion dynamics, and loss of phenotype.

The transition to chemically-defined components in cell culture has become increasingly important across biomanufacturing and biomedical research. CD medium offer advantages in reproducibility, safety, and regulatory alignment. Currently, fetal bovine serum (FBS) remains widely used for its growth-promoting properties. However, the use of FBS carries significant drawbacks like batch variability, potential disease transmission, and ethical concerns, as well as international calls to halt its usage1–4. Serum is the fluid component of blood, and it contains growth hormones, electrolytes, amino acids, and metabolites that are not only variable between batches but difficult to quantify within those batches5. The unavoidable ambiguity of FBS creates a condition where quantifiable bioassays are unattainable with the current state of the art and contributes to the challenges surrounding reproducibility. Termed the “reproducibility crisis,” replication among pre-clinical studies has been a long-time problem, but work aimed at reproducing influential papers in recent years have made a significant impact6,7. These challenges underscore the need for reproducible protocols that guide the transition from SC medium to CD medium.

Human-derived alternatives to fetal bovine serum (FBS), such as umbilical cord serum, have shown promising results in supporting in vitro cell growth8. While sourcing serum from humans may reduce concerns related to zoonotic disease transmission and animal welfare, it does not eliminate the potential for human-to-human transmission of pathogens. Both contamination and pathogen, human or animal sources, pose significant risks to translational bioprocessing and bioengineering workflows8,9. In addition to biological contaminants, chemically active compounds that are present in human serum may also be transferred to cultured cells, introducing variability and compromising data reproducibility. Notable examples of such contaminants include halogenated organic compounds10, bisphenol A (BPA)11, and perfluoroalkyl and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS)12, which are often linked to environmental exposure and persist in biological fluids.

CD medium formulations are not atypical in cell culture and are common in stem cell culture. However, despite the increasing availability of CD media formulations, systematic methods for adapting cells that were previously cultured in SC medium are limited. Successful adaptation is not only contingent on the composition of the basal media but also heavily influenced by weaning strategies, timing, and the extracellular matrix (ECM) cues that are provided during attachment. Common adaptation techniques often rely on abrupt or empirical changes in media composition, which may fail for sensitive or anchorage-dependent lines.

Furthermore, attachment substrates play a critical role in modulating cellular responses to new environments. Synthetic attachment proteins, such as recombinant vitronectin or truncated fibronectin domains, offer chemically-defined alternatives to traditional matrix coatings, yet their optimal use in adaptation protocols remains underexplored. Standardizing weaning procedures in tandem with defined ECM cues can therefore support robust and scalable adaptation workflows across cell types.

Here, we present a practical and systematic protocol for adapting cells to a custom animal-free, chemically-defined media, with emphasis on weaning strategies and ECM conditions that are tailored to the needs of adherent cells. By focusing on the interplay between weaning kinetics and chemically-defined attachment factors, this work provides a framework to enhance the reliability and success of CD medium adoption in a range of experimental contexts.

Materials and methods

Cell maintenance

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), pooled in EGM-2, were purchased from Lonza (C2519A). Cells were cultured in T-75 flasks (Cell Treat, 229340) according to Lonza culturing methods with EGM-Plus Endothelial Cell Growth Medium Plus BulletKit, referred to as SC medium (Lonza, CC-5035). When the cells became roughly 80% confluent, such that , they were passaged again to fully recover from cryopreservation. Briefly, used medium was aspirated from the flask, and the cells were washed once with 1 PBS to remove any remaining medium. TrypLE (5mLThermoFisher, 12604013) was used to release the adherent cells from the flask, and it was subsequently deactivated with equal parts of growth medium. Cells were transferred from the flask into a conical tube for centrifugation at 200g for 5min. After the cells were pelleted, the supernatant was removed, and the cells were resuspended in growth medium. The cells were seeded in new flasks at a splitting ratio that was no larger than 1:3.

After the second recovery passage from cryopreservation, when the cells had reached roughly , the cells were harvested from the flask as previously explained. To mitigate stress or damage that can be caused by TrypLE, soybean trypsin inhibitor (ATCC, 30-2104) was used, as opposed to trypsin neutralizing solution (Lonza, CC-5002), as we transition to a method of culturing in the CD medium. After centrifugation, the cells were resuspended in growth medium that is conducive to the adaptation method that was being followed for the specific CD medium exposure. The CD medium formulation includes the addition of no antibiotics; therefore, during culturing, clean lab coats were mandatory, lab access was restricted, and the biosafety cabinet underwent ultraviolet sterilization for 15 min before and after use. The incubator and water bath were cleaned and inspected regularly for cleanliness during CD medium experiments.

Medium preparation

During medium preparation, any non-sterile ingredients [L-glutamine (Sigma, G8540) and ascorbic acid (Sigma, A4403)] were measured and added to the basal medium, DMEM/F12 no glutamine (ThermoFisher 21331020); then, the solution was filtered through sterile filter (0.22m). After sterile filtration, the remaining ingredients [heparin solution (Stemcell Technologies, 07980), hydrocortisone (Sigma, H0135), ITSE+A (InVitria, 777ITS092), recombinant human VEGF 165 (R&D systems, 293-VE-050/CF), recombinant human FGF basic (R&D systems, 233-FB), rh-EGF (Lifeline CellTech)] were measured and added as needed to formulate the specific medium type. CD medium was aliquoted and stored at . CD medium that was stored at was kept for no longer than 14 d and for no more than 2 re-heatings. The cold storage for CD medium had all lighting dismantled because of the light-sensitive nature of some ingredients in the medium formulation. An increase in exposure of CD medium was advanced while passaging, specifically during the resuspension of the cell pellet. Medium was exchanged every 48 h during experimentation. Adaptation methods

Two adaptation methods were identified in this procedure, and they are completed for the formulation of CD medium. The first method is direct adaptation (DA). In DA, we recovered the early passage HUVECs from cryopreservation in EGM-2. Experimentation began by culturing HUVECs in CD medium of 100% (). The second method is gradual adaptation (GA). In GA, after the HUVECs have recovered from cryopreservation for 2passages, the proportion of CD medium is incrementally increased while decreasing the proportion of SC medium. The starting GA proportions were 0.25; 0.33, and 0.50, as shown in Table 2. After the ECs reached a confluency that was suitable for passaging, the proportion of CD medium was increased. The goal in GA was to find the most efficient route to with minimal stress to the cells. HUVECs have a finite number of viable in vitro passages; thus, expending the fewest number of passages for CD medium adaptation will allow for more experimental passages with fully adapted cells.

Surface coating

Surface coatings were implemented whenever the concentration of CD medium met or exceeded . Manufacturer procedures for coating were followed with all materials. Concentrations of recombinant human fibronectin (R&D systems, ACFP4305B) at human collagen type IV (Sigma, CC076) at , and laminin from human fibroblasts (Sigma, L4544) at were used.

Imaging

All brightfield imaging was completed on a Nikon Eclipse Ti-S. Objectives that were used during imaging were 4 and 10. Image capturing was conducted via a Guppy Pro F-125 and processed using Vimba Viewer software (Allied Vision).

Image analysis

An ImageJ segmentation plugin, Trainable Weka Segmentation version 3.3.413,14, was used to determine cell confluence () of brightfield images. Two different classifiers were used to analyze cell confluence over time in a more objective manner when compared to only measuring confluence visually through the microscope. However, cell morphology, which included observance of typical cuboidal EC morphology versus the spindle-like morphology that is typical to mesenchymal cells, was evaluated visually. The first classifier training was intended for a high density of cells with normal morphology that was cultured in SC medium. The training consisted of 27cell traces and 23background traces. The second classifier training was intended for cells with a rounded morphology and was used in experiments where we approached failure. The two classifiers were necessary because of the differences that are produced in brightfield when cells are more flat and spread on the culturing surface versus when they are rounded and detaching. The binary classified image that was produced from the trainable WEKA was analyzed through ImageJ software, which provided a surface-area coverage that was used to provide a quantitative measure of confluency, defined further as . Successful adaptation was determined via cell attachment measured via WEKA, cell morphology identified via brightfield and time to confluency. Three biological replicates determined the quantification of confluency.

Results

Evaluation of cell response to chemically defined medium

After being harvested from umbilical cords, HUVECs have a finite number of passages that they can be used for experimentation, which motivated the development of the most efficient adaptation method with the fewest number of passages that were required to fully adapt the HUVECs. The first step was to determine if the medium was sufficient in sustaining cell growth over time without passaging. Initially, the cells were not passaged in order to reduce the number of variables that can cause stress on the cells and isolate the response to the medium. Cells that have previously been grown in vitro with serum become adapted to serum, possibly causing cell stress if serum is removed too quickly. Therefore, the slow removal of serum from the medium increases the chances that the cells will survive when transitioning to a new medium. A variety of adaptation rates were established to evaluate how the rate of introducing CD medium to the cells affected the morphology of the cells and the ability of cells to attach to the culturing surface Table 2. Adaptation rates are discussed in more detail in Section 3.3.

The following information provides our justification for use of medium constituents. Ascorbic acid, a common medium additive among commercial formulations of EC medium, helps to mitigate oxidative stress during in vitro culture15. L-glutamine has been found to be a potent energy substrate for ECs, and it is necessary for proliferation16,17. Epidermal growth factor (EGF) and hydrocortisone have been found to elicit a concerted proliferative response in ECs, which is critical when depleting and removing serum from medium18. Heparin interacts with a variety of different proteins, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and fibroblast growth factor (FGF), enhancing the proliferative and mitogenic response to these growth factors by the cells19,20. Insulin-transferrin-selenium (ITS) supplementation is essential when eliminating serum from the medium, and it has been shown to accelerate proliferation, viability, and expected protein expression in cells21,22. Recombinant human albumin that was used as a replacement for bovine serum albumin (BSA) in our formulation of CD medium aligns with our goal of achieving a fully defined methodology. The successful use of a recombinant human protein is advantageous from a translational perspective because of its control and sustainability. The FDA has approved clinical products that contain BSA, such as various adhesives23. However, transitioning to a human recombinant form of albumin offers a controllable protein output in a form with potential for industrial scalability.

Adaptation of HUVECs to chemically-defined medium

Use of serum, FBS or human derived, has long been standard practice in cell culture due to its broad range of synergistic components, including hormones, growth factors, carrier proteins, and nutrients that collectively support robust in vitro growth and survival across many cell types4,5,25. In EC culture, these undefined serum components not only sustain proliferation, but they also contribute to cellular attachment and phenotype maintenance.

However, serum also introduces significant variability and reproducibility challenges, especially in adherent cell types. In particular, it supplies key attachment factors, such as fibronectin, vitronectin, and globulins, which help mediate cell adhesion to the substrates that are used in in vitro culture25,26. These components are not only poorly defined but differ significantly between serum lots, undermining efforts to standardize bioassays and interpret results across experiments. Recognizing the reliance on serum-derived adhesion support, we focused on identifying discrete, defined components that could replicate these functions, thereby enabling a transition to conditions of fully CD medium without compromising endothelial health or morphology.

To address this issue, we incorporated the use of surface-coating proteins that are typical to the endothelial basal lamina, such as fibronectin, collagen IV, and laminin, to improve cell attachment during passaging26. Additionally, based on observations from earlier gradual adaptation trials (GA1 to GA3), we implemented a refined adaptation strategy, GA5, which introduced smaller increments at higher concentrations of CD medium. This method was designed to reduce cell stress near full adaptation and to improve stability during early attachment. While GA5 was not included in the initial confluency comparison under non-passaged conditions, it was intentionally applied in coating experiments for its improved control over cellular behavior at high values of .

Throughout the adaptation process, the days that the HUVECs took to reach the target confluence were recorded, and we related that data to HUVECs that were growing in SC medium using the same densities of cell seeding and coatings. We found that, even at high concentrations of CD medium but less than , the growth rates of HUVECs that were adapting to CD medium were comparable to our control HUVECs (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This work presents a streamlined and reproducible framework for transitioning ECs to xeno-free, CD culture conditions. By optimizing the interplay between gradual weaning and ECM coatings, we reduce cellular stress and support robust adaptation, addressing major barriers to reproducibility in serum-free workflows. These findings are especially relevant in applications such as drug development, tissue engineering, and high-fidelity in vitro disease modeling. Additionally, this study builds upon prior work that aimed toward serum-free, microphysiological systems23,27,28.

Using HUVECs as a model system, we evaluated both DA and GA strategies. As expected, based on prior work29, DA induced significant detachment and spindle-like morphology, indicating a hostile environment for adherent endothelial cells. In contrast, GA allowed for a smoother transition with better retention of morphology and viability. These results underscore the importance of incremental serum depletion and support previous findings that abrupt removal of serum compromises attachment and phenotype.

Once cells tolerated the CD medium under non-passaged conditions, we incorporated passaging, establishing objective criteria to define adaptation success: achieving within 24 hours of seeding, retaining typical endothelial morphology, and maintaining consistent confluence across passages. As CD medium levels increased, cell attachment declined, motivating the introduction of ECM protein coatings.

We observed that fibronectin, collagen IV, and laminin each improved initial attachment in CD medium at , with a across all coatings. However, under fully CD conditions, fibronectin uniquely preserved EC morphology, while laminin and collagen IV surfaces led to spindle-like projections and deteriorating morphology. Fibronectin supported the most favorable attachment and morphology over a period of under CD conditions, suggesting it may offer a more stable short-term environment for phenotype maintenance when compared to other coatings that were tested. The WEKA segmentation method that was used to calculate confluency does not inherently distinguish between attached and floating cells in brightfield images. As such, in conditions where detachment was observed, the presence of non-adherent cells may have skewed values upward. Refinements of this approach will incorporate classifier training or imaging methods that can better differentiate adherent versus suspended cells, improving segmentation accuracy under conditions of CD medium. Open-source tools such as CellProfiler or advanced ImageJ plugins may allow automated analysis to complement confluence and attachment data, particularly during adaptation processes where subtle morphological shifts are critical indicators of cell health.

Importantly, HUVECs cultured on fibronectin-coated surfaces retained proliferative capacity after transitioning to . Cell confluency increased over , unlike the preceding passage, suggesting a short latency period before full adaptation. The observed proliferation rebound highlights the importance of allowing time for cellular adjustment after serum withdrawal.

While detailed protocols for adapting cells to chemically defined conditions remain underreported, our method supports functional adaptation to CD medium within five passages, with retention of morphology and a promising expansion capacity when compared to serum controls. This strategy supports alignment with regulatory efforts such as the FDA Modernization Act 2.0 and global initiatives discouraging animal-derived materials in therapeutic production. Additionally, our use of recombinant human albumin over bovine serum albumin enhances translational relevance and minimizes contamination risk.

While promising, this protocol is specific for ECs and may require cytokine adjustments for different cell types. Media supplement lots should be validated to ensure consistency, and further studies are needed to confirm preservation of endothelial phenotype at the transcriptomic level. Moreover, while fibronectin proved effective for HUVECs, its suitability across other primary or induced cell types remains to be determined.

Overall, this work contributes a defined and scalable approach to serum-free endothelial culture. It establishes a practical foundation for building high-fidelity, chemically-defined in vitro models for use in toxicology, regenerative medicine, and preclinical assay development.

Conclusions and future work

This study presents a reproducible adaptation strategy that transitions HUVECs from SC to CD conditions in five passages. The protocol supports comparable confluency kinetics to serum controls during the early stages of the weaning process, although some reduction in proliferation and confluence was observed after full adaptation. While the protocol demonstrated success in adapting HUVECs, further work is needed to assess its applicability across the diverse range of endothelial cell subtypes, which may have varying attachment, growth factor, or matrix requirements.

These findings establish a foundation for building human in vitro systems and support reproducible EC. Downstream applications may include high-content bioassays or toxicity screening. The protocol addresses a key gap in the literature by providing a practical, adaptable approach to CD media transition for adherent human cells.

Future work will focus on refining the composition of the CD medium, particularly the concentrations and combinations of growth factors to tailor the formulation for specific assay types or functional endpoints. While this study did not include molecular or staining-based assays to quantify viability, the ability of HUVECs to survive enzymatic passaging, reattach, and reach defined confluency under conditions of CD medium serves as a strong functional indicator of viability. While confluency, morphology, and attachment were used as practical indicators of adaptation, this study did not directly assess endothelial-specific functionality under CD conditions. Future work will include assays such as tube formation, production of nitric oxide, and marker expression to confirm that HUVECs retain their specialized functions post-adaptation.
