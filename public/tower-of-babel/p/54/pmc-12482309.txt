Relapsing polychondritis with isolated tracheal involvement: Imaging findings on CT, MRI, and PET-CT: A case report and literature review
Authors: Yurong Jiang, Qian Su, Yu Zhao, Fengyu Sun, Piaoran Wang, Xiangzheng Wu, Jiongyue Yun, Huaigui Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRelapsing polychondritis (RP) is a rare autoimmune disorder characterized by recurrent inflammation and progressive destruction of cartilage-rich tissues. While auricular and nasal cartilage are commonly affected, isolated involvement of the trachea and main bronchi is exceedingly rare. We present the case of a 53-year-old female with low-grade fever, sore throat, and nonproductive cough. Laboratory results showed elevated inflammatory markers, including C-reactive protein (CRP), rheumatoid factor, and interleukin-6 (IL-6). Imaging studies CT, MRI, and PET-CT revealed irregular thickening and stenosis of the tracheal and bronchial cartilage rings. The patient responded favorably to glucocorticoids and immunosuppressive therapy, with significant improvement noted on follow-up imaging. This case emphasizes the importance of a thorough diagnostic approach in recognizing RP with atypical respiratory involvement.Keywords: Relapsing polychondritis, Autoimmune disease, Tracheal stenosis, Bronchial inflammation, Glucocorticoids, Immunosuppressive therapy

Introduction

Relapsing polychondritis (RP) is a rare systemic autoimmune disease that predominantly affects tissues rich in cartilage and proteoglycans. It is characterized by recurrent episodes of inflammation and progressive destruction of cartilage in the affected regions, commonly involving the ears, nose, trachea, and costal cartilage [[1], [2], [3]].

The estimated global prevalence of RP varies between 4.5 and 25 cases per million, with an annual incidence of 0.71 to 3.5 cases per million [2,4,5]. Although the exact etiology of RP remains unclear, it is believed to be associated with an abnormal autoimmune response targeting cartilage tissues. In this article, we present a rare case of RP with isolated involvement of the trachea and both main bronchi, an unusual manifestation of the disease. The diagnosis was confirmed through comprehensive imaging examinations, including CT, MRI, and PET. We provide a detailed discussion of the patient’s clinical presentations, imaging findings, and treatment approach, along with a literature review.

Case presentation

A 53-year-old female presented with unexplained low-grade fever persisting for over 2 weeks, with body temperature fluctuating between 37.1°C and 37.7°C. She also experienced a sore throat and intermittent cough without sputum production. There was no history of recent respiratory infections, trauma, or significant comorbidities. Laboratory tests revealed an elevated white blood cell (WBC) count of 10.02 × 109/L, increased C-reactive protein (CRP) at 10.60 mg/dL, elevated rheumatoid factor (RF) at 88.3 IU/mL, increased serum amyloid A (SSA) at 39.75 mg/L, and elevated interleukin-6 (IL-6) at 210.9 pg/mL. Additionally, the patient also tested positive for antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and anti-SSA antibodies, serological tests for Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia, respiratory syncytial virus, adenovirus, Coxsackievirus group B antibodies, and tuberculosis were all negative.

The unenhanced chest CT scan revealed irregular thickening of the tracheal and main bronchial cartilage rings, with sparing of the posterior membrane and accompanied by varying degrees of stenosis (Fig. 1A and D). The tracheal walls appeared roughened with peritracheal fat stranding (Fig. 1B). Additionally, multiple enlarged lymph nodes were observed in the mediastinum, particularly in the pretracheal region, pulmonary artery window, subcarinal space and both lung hila (Fig. 1C). T1-weighted imaging demonstrated prominent circumferential isointense signal within the perichondral and chondral tissues (Fig. 2A). T2-weighted imaging displayed a mild, diffuse hyperintense signal surrounding the trachea and main bronchi, particularly involving the anterior and lateral aspects of the airway wall, consistent with inflammatory changes (Fig. 2B and C). PET-CT scan revealed increased FDG uptake surrounding the trachea, enlarged lymph nodes, bilateral primary and lobar bronchi (Fig. 3), with an SUVmax of approximately 7.8, suggesting active inflammatory activity.

The patient was treated with glucocorticoids and immunosuppressive therapy as part of the standard regimen. Specifically, she received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 40 mg/day for 5 consecutive days, followed by oral prednisone 30 mg/day with a gradual taper. Methotrexate (10 mg/week) was also initiated as a steroid-sparing agent. Clinical improvement (reduction of cough, throat pain, and exertional dyspnea) was noted within 2 weeks. A follow-up unenhanced chest CT at 6 weeks marked improvement in the thickening of the tracheal and bronchial cartilage rings, along with a reduction in stenosis (Fig. 4A and D). The density of the surrounding adipose tissue appeared clearer (Fig. 4B) and the lymph nodes were smaller compared to pretreatment measurements (Fig. 4C). Methylprednisolone was tapered by 5 mg every 1–2 weeks to 10 mg/day, then by 2.5 mg every 2 weeks based on symptoms and inflammatory markers; MTX was maintained and escalated to 15 mg/week if tolerated. Laboratory monitoring (CBC, liver enzymes, creatinine) was performed every 2–4 weeks during the first 2–3 months, then spaced. The patient achieved complete remission by 6 months, with normalization of inflammatory markers, resolution of respiratory symptoms, and stable imaging on follow-up CT at 1 year later. Gastroprotection and calcium/vitamin D were provided during glucocorticoid therapy. Considering the patient’s clinical presentation, imaging findings, and laboratory results, a diagnosis of relapsing polychondritis was confirmed. The patient’s symptoms and imaging abnormalities improved significant with treatment, highlighting the critical importance of early diagnosis and appropriate management in this rare condition.

Discussion

Relapsing polychondritis (RP) is a rare, systemic autoimmune disorder characterized by recurrent episodes of inflammation and progressive destruction of cartilaginous tissues. While RP most commonly affects the ears, nose and joints, involvement of the respiratory tract, particularly isolated tracheobronchial inflammation, is far less common and is associated with poorer prognosis if not treated promptly [2,4]. The progression of RP is highly unpredictable, ranging from mild, intermittent episodes of auricular or nasal chondritis to severe, life-threatening involvement of the tracheobronchial tree or cardiovascular system. Symptoms can vary widely and may include painful swelling of the ears, sore throat, hoarseness, coughing, or stridor, which can often result in misdiagnosis as an acute infection or asthma. This case demonstrates illustrate the typical clinical presentation of RP with airway involvement, and shows positive response to glucocorticoids and immunosuppressive therapy. The findings underscore the critical importance of early recognition and treatment in improving patient outcomes.

Pathogenesis

RP is considered an immune-mediated disease involving both humoral and cell-mediated mechanisms. Genetic predisposition is supported by HLA class II associations, most consistently HLA-DR4 [6,7]. Autoantibodies to cartilage components, particularly type II collagen, as well as candidate antigens such as matrilin-1 and COMP, have been reported [8,9]. Experimental models demonstrate that matrilin-1–induced chondritis is dependent on B cells and complement activation, supporting a combined antibody- and T-cell–driven pathogenesis [10].

Recently, a large-scale autoantibody reactome analysis identified 14 novel autoantibodies in RP, which not only differentiated RP from healthy and disease controls but also predicted disease activity and recurrence [11]. Among these, anti-C4B correlated positively with disease activity, and complement C4B deposition in tracheal tissue alongside reduced plasma levels suggested abnormal complement activation as a key pathogenic mechanism. These findings further support the role of dysregulated humoral immunity and complement pathways in RP.

Clinical relevance of respiratory involvement

In RP, respiratory tract involvement occurs in approximately 30-60% of cases and is often associated with more severe disease progression [[12], [13], [14]]. Tracheobronchial chondritis can lead to airway stenosis, respiratory distress, and, in extreme cases, life-threatening respiratory failure. Notably, airway chondritis can occur independently, particularly when it involves the lower respiratory tract, such as the trachea and bronchi [15]. In this case, the patient presented with isolated tracheal and bronchial involvement without the typical features of auricular or nasal chondritis, complicated the early diagnosis. Studies suggest that such isolated airway involvement may result in diagnosis delays, increasing the risk of irreversible airway damage.

Imaging plays a crucial role in identifying airway involvement in RP. In this case, CT scans—acquired during the inspiratory phase [16]—revealed irregular thickening and stenosis of the tracheal and bronchial cartilage rings, which are hallmark features of RP affecting the respiratory tract [17]. MRI scans demonstrated prominent inflammatory changes in the tracheal and bronchial cartilage rings without radiation exposure [18], making it a valuable alternative for diagnosis of RP. PET-CT showed active metabolic uptake, indicating ongoing inflammation [19]. These findings align with other studies suggesting that PET-CT is effective in monitoring disease activity and guiding treatment decisions [20].

Diagnostic challenges and differential diagnosis

RP is often misdiagnosed as other inflammatory or infectious diseases due to overlapping clinical symptoms. In this case, the patient presented with nonspecific respiratory symptoms like sore throat, cough, and low-grade fever, which mimicked common respiratory infections. Negative test results for tuberculosis, Mycoplasma pneumonia, and other infectious agents further complicated the diagnostic process. Similar diagnostic challenges have been noted in pediatric cases with airway involvement, where initial symptoms are often mistaken for asthma or croup [21]. Given the tracheal involvement, granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) was an important differential diagnosis [22]. While both diseases can present with airway stenosis, GPA tends to cause circumferential subglottic stenosis, whereas RP-related stenosis tends to be anterior and localized to the trachea and bronchi [22]. Additionally, the absence of renal involvement and ANCA antibodies in this patient helped exclude GPA.

To aid radiologic differentiation, Table 1 summarizes key imaging features of RP and common mimickers including GPA, amyloidosis, sarcoidosis, and infectious tracheitis. RP typically shows smooth thickening of the anterior and lateral airway walls with posterior wall sparing, often with cartilage calcifications and airway malacia [16,17]. GPA presents with circumferential subglottic stenosis, pulmonary nodules, or cavitation [22]. Amyloidosis features nodular or irregular wall thickening with submucosal calcifications [23]. Sarcoidosis often shows airway wall thickening with bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy or small-airway disease [24]. Infectious tracheitis demonstrates acute mucosal edema and wall thickening. These radiologic patterns, together with clinical context, enhance diagnostic accuracy.

Although unenhanced CT was sufficient in this case to identify key features of RP, contrast-enhanced imaging may provide added diagnostic value [25]. Enhanced CT can better delineate peritracheal inflammation and lymphadenopathy, while contrast-enhanced MRI can reveal active chondritis through characteristic enhancement patterns [7]. These features may assist in distinguishing RP from mimickers [22].

Functional evaluation and limitations

Although pulmonary function tests (PFTs), including flow-volume loops, were not performed in this case due to the urgency of airway compromise and the patient’s intolerance during the acute phase, they are considered valuable tools for assessing the functional severity of airway involvement in RP [1,26]. Flow-volume loops, in particular, may demonstrate characteristic patterns of fixed or variable airway obstruction that correlate with imaging findings [27]. In future similar cases, the inclusion of PFTs could enhance clinical assessment, aid in early detection of airway dysfunction, and serve as a baseline for monitoring treatment response and disease progression.

Therapeutic approach and outcome

Treatment strategies for RP with airway involvement typically ranges from symptomatic treatment to a combination of glucocorticoids and immunosuppressants to control inflammation and prevent disease progression [28]. In this case, the patient responded well to glucocorticoids and immunosuppressants, with follow-up CT showing reduced thickening of the tracheal cartilage and improved airway patency. Timely initiation of immunosuppressants was essential for achieving remission and preventing complications such as tracheomalacia or dynamic airway collapse, which have been reported in similar cases [29]. Long-term management of RP requires careful monitoring for relapses, especially given the unpredictable disease course. Studies emphasize the importance of regular imaging and pulmonary function tests to detect early signs of airway deterioration [30]. Furthermore, in severe cases with extensive airway damage, interventions like stent placement or tracheostomy may be required [31].

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of comprehensive diagnostic evaluation, including advanced imaging techniques, in identifying rare manifestations of RP. Isolated tracheobronchial involvement, though uncommon, should be considered in patients with unexplained respiratory symptoms and inflammatory markers. Early diagnosis and timely immunosuppressive therapy can greatly improve outcomes by preventing irreversible airway damage. Future research should focus to explore targeted therapies, including biologics, which have shown promise in managing refractory cases of RP.
