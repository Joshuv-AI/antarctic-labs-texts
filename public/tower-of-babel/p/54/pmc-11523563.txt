Spatiotemporal relationships between neuronal, metabolic, and hemodynamic signals in the awake and anesthetized mouse brain
Authors: Xiaodan Wang, Jonah A. Padawer-Curry, Annie R. Bice, Byungchan Kim, Zachary P. Rosenthal, Jin-Moo Lee, Manu S. Goyal, Shannon L. Macauley, Adam Q. Bauer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYNeurovascular coupling (NVC) and neurometabolic coupling (NMC) provide the basis for functional magnetic resonance imaging and positron emission tomography to map brain neurophysiology. While increases in neuronal activity are often accompanied by increases in blood oxygen delivery and oxidative metabolism, these observations are not the rule. This decoupling is important when interpreting brain network organization (e.g., resting-state functional connectivity [RSFC]) because it is unclear whether changes in NMC/NVC affect RSFC measures. We leverage wide-field optical imaging in Thy1-jRGECO1a mice to map cortical calcium activity in pyramidal neurons, flavoprotein autofluorescence (representing oxidative metabolism), and hemodynamic activity during wake and ketamine/xylazine anesthesia. Spontaneous dynamics of all contrasts exhibit patterns consistent with RSFC. NMC/NVC relative to excitatory activity varies over the cortex. Ketamine/xylazine profoundly alters NVC but not NMC. Compared to awake RSFC, ketamine/xylazine affects metabolic-based connectomes moreso than hemodynamic-based measures of RSFC. Anesthesia-related differences in NMC/NVC timing do not appreciably alter RSFC structure.

INTRODUCTION

Functional neuroimaging techniques have revolutionized our understanding of healthy brain function and its disruption during disease.1 Data acquired with these methods are often interpreted as surrogates of underlying neuronal and/or metabolic activity. In the healthy brain, local changes in blood flow and energy utilization are coupled to changes in local neuronal activity, phenomena described as neurovascular coupling (NVC)2 or neurometabolic coupling (NMC)3–6 (Figure 1A). Through NVC, patterns of spontaneous or task-driven neural activity manifest as fluctuations in blood oxygenation, most often studied using blood-oxygenation level-dependent (BOLD) functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) or optical intrinsic signal imaging (OISI).7,8 NMC is defined as the metabolic rates associated with supporting spontaneous or stimulus-evoked activity, the bulk of which is due to homeostatic maintenance by neuronalglial networks within the gray matter.9–11 Oxygen consumption and glucose uptake measured by 15O2- and 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET), respectively, provide additional proxies for changes in cerebral metabolism due to neurotransmission.12,13

Despite these techniques being used for decades, regional differences in NVC and NMC and how these process covary over the brain are not firmly established. Recent work in humans and rodents has revealed that NVC varies over the cortex,14–16 while differing coupling relationships occur within the brainstem,14 thalamus,14 or hippocampus.17 Increases in cerebral blood flow are often accompanied by increases in oxidative metabolism,18–21 suggesting a positive correlation between regional NMC and NVC amplitude. However, this observation is not the rule22–27 as local metabolism and hemodynamics can be uncoupled from one another and from underlying neuronal activity.18,28–30

Understanding this decoupling is especially important when mapping underlying brain network organization. Throughout the brain, temporally correlated spontaneous activity can be observed in neuronal, metabolic, and hemodynamic signals.31,32 These patterns of activity are used to define widely distributed topographies called resting-state networks that correspond to sensory, motor, and other functional systems associated with cognition.33–35 However, mapping brain function in the context of altered NVC or NMC can be problematic. NVC and NMC evolve during development,36,37 NVC and glucose homeostasis are altered under anesthesia,38–42 and many conditions affecting the central nervous system disrupt individual or multiple pathways involved in NMC and NVC.2,8,36,43–46 Understanding how local neuronal activity manifests as changes in either metabolic or hemodynamic activity requires the ability to simultaneously measure these quantities.

The expense and relatively low spatiotemporal resolution of PET currently limit its widespread use for examining brain energetics and network organization in small animals. An optical alternative to PET capitalizes on endogenous flavoprotein autofluorescence (FAF)47,48 from two electron-carrying molecules (flavin adenine dinucleotide and flavin mononucleotide), associated with mitochondrial electron transport chain activity.49 When oxidized during increased metabolic activity, FAF can serve as an endogenous measure of oxidative metabolism.50,51 Whether FAF exhibits spontaneous fluctuations causally coupled to neuronal activity and whether these dynamics encode patterns of functional brain organization have not been demonstrated.

Genetic engineering techniques in mice have provided new opportunities for extending wide-field optical imaging (WFOI) methods to direct measures of neuronal activity using fluorescent, genetically encoded calcium indicators (GECIs).52 We and others38,53–56 have reported coupling between cell-specific calcium transients and blood oxygenation, each of which can inform underlying functional network architecture. How these signals link to changes in ongoing oxidative metabolism reflected by FAF and whether regional changes in NVC and NMC directly impact RSFC measures have not been demonstrated. In part, this knowledge gap exists because the excitation/emission spectra of commonly used GECIs such as GCaMP57 overlap with those of flavoproteins.47,48 To bridge this gap, we leveraged mice expressing the red-shifted GECI, jRGECO1a,58 under a Thy1 promoter and dual-fluorophore WFOI (2F-WFOI). These tools allowed for visualizing calcium fluctuations primarily from excitatory neurons of layers 2/3 and 5 in relation to cortical FAF and hemodynamic activity. Ketamine/xylazine is a commonly used anesthetic causing excitatory-inhibitory circuit disinhibition,59–61 reductions in glucose uptake and cerebral blood flow,62,63 and the instantiation of slow (0.4–4Hz) oscillations64,65 over the cortex. We hypothesized that K/X would alter band-specific changes in cortical activity, regional NVC/NMC relationships, and therefore estimates of RSFC.

Our results indicate that NVC and NMC relative to activity in cortical pyramidal cells vary over the cortex and that NVC is significantly more sensitive to K/X anesthesia compared to NMC. Spontaneous neuronal, metabolic, and hemodynamic activity each provide frequency-band-specific information regarding underlying brain network organization (i.e., RSFC) across conditions. However, functional connectomes based on hemodynamic activity were largely preserved across states compared to those based on FAF. Anesthesia-related differences in the timing (delay, width) of NVC/NMC relative to excitatory activity were not predictive of regional changes in RSFC measures. These observations inform fundamental relationships between how changes in excitatory activity can manifest in neuroimaging measures of metabolic and hemodynamic activity and, in turn, estimates of brain network organization.

RESULTS

Validation of 2F-WFOI in Thy1-jRGECO1a mice during resting-state and task-based paradigms

2F-WFOI allowed for simultaneous mapping of cortical calcium, FAF, and hemodynamic activity (Figure 1). However, Thy1-jRGECO1a mice have not had the same extensive validation as other GECI lines of mice. For example, recent studies demonstrated that, under resting-state conditions, some strains of GCaMP6f mice exhibit epileptiform-like activity,66,67 but not all strains.68,69 Prior to evaluating any measure, we surveyed our datasets for epileptiform-like dynamics in spontaneous jRGECO1a fluctuations using analyses outlined in Steinmetz et al.66 A 2-mm2 (5 × 5 pixel) region of interest was placed in the centroids of several brain regions (Figure S1) for evaluating calcium activity. Aberrant activity manifests as epochs of high fluorescence emission (prominence) over a short time span (peak width), which form a separate cluster distinct from normal intrinsic fluctuations (see Figure 3 in Steinmetz et al.66). No evidence of aberrant activity was observed in any Thy1-jRGECO1a mice used for this study (Figure S1).

Successful mapping of each contrast was then determined by evaluating evoked activity following whisker stimulation (Figure 2). Red-shifted GECIs offer the benefit of reduced absorption-related artifacts compared to GCaMP or FAF as shown by jRGECO1a fluorescence changes during stimulation. Raw (uncorrected) jRGECO1a fluorescence remains positive (Figures 2A and 2B), while raw FAF emission increases initially but then decreases to negative values (Figures 2C and 2D) as total hemoglobin (HbT) increases (Figures 2E and 2F). Corrected jRGECO1a and FAF reveal focal activity in left somatosensory barrel cortex, left motor barrel cortex, and right homotopic somatosensory barrel cortex. Activity within auditory cortex corresponds to perceptible acoustic stimuli from the Picospritzer during air puff delivery. Evoked time courses demonstrate clear temporal separation between calcium, FAF, and hemoglobin (Figures 2F and 2G; Table S2).

Spontaneous neuronal, metabolic, and hemodynamic activity during wakefulness and anesthesia

In awake mice (Figure 3A), cortical power spectral density in calcium signaling falls off approximately as 1/frequency, over about four orders of magnitude up to ~5 Hz similar to GCaMP6f.70 The spectral content of FAF and hemoglobin both have an approximate log-linear relationship but taper around 1 Hz, resulting in reduced dynamic range (FAF, 13 dB; HbT, 26 dB over 0.01–10 Hz) (Figure 3A). Over infraslow frequencies (infraslow activity [ISA], 0.009–0.08 Hz), the slope of the power spectra tended to flatten38,71 under K/X, and all contrasts exhibited significantly lower power (calcium, 0.38 ± 0.14, p = 0.0031; FAF, 0.55 ± 0.24, p = 0.019; HbT, 0.096 ± 0.040, p = 0.0022) (Figure 3C). In K/X anesthetized mice, fluctuations in calcium concentration exhibit a broad spectral peak centered near 1 Hz, characteristic of increased delta activity (Figure 3A), approximately 33 times larger (p = 3.6 × 10−5) than what was observed during wake (Figure 3C). FAF signals exhibit the same peak under K/X, approximately 3× larger than awake mice (p = 7.8 × 10−4), suggesting increased metabolic activity associated with coherent neuronal oscillations over this range. These oscillations were not observed in hemodynamics. Spectral peaks near 1 and 2.5 Hz (and their harmonics) are consistent with systemic physiology (e.g., respiration, pulse) under anesthesia and our prior reports.72,73

Topographical maps of absolute band-limited power determined whether arousal-related changes in global brain activity were specific to cortical region (Figure 3B). Awake ISA maps for all contrasts display similar spatial features. Higher activity occurred in somatosensory hindpaw, medial and lateral parietal (also called anterior visual area [VISa] and rostrolateral visual area [VISrl]), and visual and retrosplenial regions, with lower activity in sensorimotor cortex (Figure 3B). Over 0.4–4 Hz, calcium activity was localized to primary visual, retrosplenial, and lateral somatomotor regions, whereas FAF and hemodynamic activity were spatially less specific (Figure 3B) possibly due to a low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) above ~1 Hz). Anesthetized topographies of ISA fluctuations were relatively flat with slightly higher activity in somatomotor cortex (Figure 3B). Anesthetized delta calcium and FAF activity was higher in posterior secondary motor and retrosplenial cortex, while hemodynamic activity was relatively uniform (Figure 3B). Compared to anesthetized maps, awake maps exhibit higher ISA in regions including hindpaw, medial (VISa) and lateral parietal (VISrl), and visual and retrosplenial cortex across contrasts. Compared to anesthetized signaling, awake jRGECO1a and FAF show lower delta activity in medial and more lateral cortex. Power maps of HbT reveal comparable spatial patterns across states.

Spontaneous neuronal, metabolic, and hemodynamic activity exhibit state-dependent coupling

A representative set of ongoing spontaneous fluctuations (Figure 4A) shows how regional patterns of activity propagate within and across contrasts. Increases in calcium concentration within primary somatosensory and portions of motor, 14.44 s), are followed by increased FAF (~14.44–14.84 s) then HbT (15.64–16.04 s). This ongoing intrinsic activity (Figure 4B) was used to estimate NMC and NVC assuming a linear model of coupling in each pixel.53,74–76 Weighted least-squares deconvolution estimated hemodynamic response functions (HRFs) and metabolic response functions (MRFs) at each location (Figures 4C and 4D). Maps of time to peak (Figure 4C, top), full width at half maximum (FWHM) (Figure 4C, second row), and amplitude (Figure 4C, third row) summarize cortical NVC and NMC during wake and anesthesia (Table S3). In line with previous reports,53,75 globally over the cortex, HRFs in awake mice exhibit shorter time to peak (awake, 0.86 ± 0.082; anesthetized, 1.2 ± 0.12; p = 0.0018), narrower width (awake, 1.1 ± 0.14; anesthetized, 2.0 ± 0.44; p = 0.0071), and higher amplitude (awake, 3.0 × 10−3 ± 5.7×10−4; anesthetized, 7.7 × 10−4 ± 3.6 × 10−4; p = 2.8 × 10−4) (Figure 4D; Table S3) compared to anesthetized mice. Conversely, K/X did not appreciably affect average NMC (Figure 4D; Table S3). Failing to account for hemoglobin absorption artifacts resulted in erroneous coupling estimates (Figure S2).

Finer examination of cortical NVC reveals region- and state-dependent coupling. In awake mice, the somatosensory cortex exhibited faster NVC (i.e., shorter time to peak, p = 0.0087) while visual and retrosplenial cortices exhibited slower NVC (time to peak, p = 0.023, p = 0.022, respectively) compared to cortical averages (Figures 4C and S3A). K/X generally lengthened the time to peak between calcium and hemodynamic signaling within most cortical regions (Figure 4C). The HRF width is inversely proportional to the overall frequency content in hemodynamic signaling and was also region and state dependent. Awake mice exhibited narrower HRFs in motor (p = 0.016) but wider HRFs in somatosensory (p = 0.05) regions (Figures 4C and S3A). HRFs under K/X were wider in somatosensory (p = 0.037) and narrower in retrosplenial cortex (p = 0.027) compared to average HRF widths under anesthesia. Compared to awake mice, changes in HRF widths were most pronounced in motor and parts of somatosensory regions (Figure 4C; FWHM p values range from 8.2 × 10−4 to 0.047). Regional HRF amplitude was highest in somatosensory (p = 0.0024) and parietal cortex (VISa, VISrl, and anterolateral visual area [VISal], p = 0.0015) and lowest in motor regions (p = 0.0022) (Figures 4C and S3A). K/X globally reduced HRF amplitude in a region-specific manner such that coupling under anesthesia was relatively uniform over the cortex (Figure 4C; amplitude p values range from 1.8 × 10−5 to 0.015). The correspondence between measured and predicted hemodynamics was highest in somatosensory(p = 0.0031), parietal (also called VISa, VISrl, and VISal, p = 5.2 × 10−4), and auditory (p = 0. 0398) cortex (Figures 4C and S3A). Despite the global reduction in coupling, K/X anesthesia did not appreciably affect how well predicted hemodynamics matched measured activity (Figure 4C, correlation; Figure S3B,C). Across cortical regions, the correspondence (Pearson r correlation coefficient) between measured and predicted hemodynamic activity ranged between [0.38, 0.79] in awake mice and [0.26, 0.80] under anesthesia.

NMC in awake mice was also region dependent. Compared to cortical averages, NMC in motor cortex was faster (p = 0.015) but slower (p = 0.032) in somatosensory (Figures 4C and S3A, time to peak). K/X altered these relationships and was associated with slower NMC in motor (p = 0.037) and somatosensory (p = 0.0023) regions but faster NMC in visual (p = 0.0062) and retrosplenial (p = 3.3 × 10−5) cortex (Figure S3A). MRFs were wider in motor (p = 0.024) and somatosensory (p = 0.0017) regions and narrower in parietal regions (VISa, VISrl, and VISal, p = 0.034) and visual (p = 0.0080) and retrosplenial (p = 2.7 × 10−4) cortex (Figures 4C and S3A, width). In both awake and anesthetized mice, regional MRF amplitudes were highest in the somatosensory region and parietal cortices and lowest in the motor region and visual and retrosplenial cortex (Figures 4C and S3A, amplitude, awake, p = 0.0011, 7.5 × 10−4, 0.034, 0.015, and 0.033; anesthetized, p = 3.1 × 10−4, 9.3 × 10−4, 0.0012, 0.029, and 0.036, respectively). K/X did not appreciably affect regional MRF parameters (Figure 4C, p values range from 0.0584 to 1) except for MRF width in somatosensory nose (p = 0.045) and coupling amplitude in supplemental somatosensory (p = 0.0041), posterolateral visual (p = 0.0049), and primary auditory areas (p = 0.0077). Like NVC, the correspondence between measured and predicted metabolic activity was highest in somatosensory (p = 0.0015) and parietal (p = 0.0071) cortex (Figures 4C and S3A, correlation). However, the most salient effect that K/X had on cortical NMC was the correspondence between measured and predicted FAF activity in nearly every cortical region (Figure 4C, the p values range from 1.7 × 10−5 to 0.13). This observation is likely due to increases in shared frequency content (i.e., delta activity) across both calcium and FAF signals under K/X. Across cortical regions, the correspondence between measured and predicted metabolic activity ranged between [0.33,0.85] in awake mice and [0.30,0.96] under anesthesia.

Visual inspection of NVC and NMC maps (Figure 4C) reveal that some MRF/HRF parameters exhibit similar topographies (e.g., amplitude, correlation) suggesting an inherent link between NMC and NVC. To investigate this relationship, we examined whether aspects of NVC (NMC) were predicative of NMC (NVC) at the regional level (Figure 5). In awake animals, the time to peak of regional HRFs/MRFs was weakly anticorrelated (Figure 5A, first row, R2 = 0.16, p = 7.3 × 10−3), but became stronger (Figure 5B, first row, R2 = 0.39, p = 6.6 × 10−6) under anesthesia. HRF/MRF widths were generally not associated during wake (Figure 5A, second row, p = 0.88), but significantly correlated under K/X (Figure 5B, second row, R2 = 0.54, p = 1.3 × 10−8). Interestingly, coupling amplitudes were strongly predictive of one another in either state (Figure 5A, third row, awake, R2 = 0.69, p = 4.4 × 10−12; Figure 5B third row, anesthetized, R2 = 0.82, p = 2.6 × 10−17). Further, regions with the longest HRF time to peaks also tended to show the lowest MRF area under the curve, and vice versa (Figure 5A, fourth row, awake, R2 = 0.27, p = 4.4 × 10−4; Figure 5B, fourth row, anesthetized, R2 = 0.25, p = 5.7 × 10−4).

Convergence and divergence of neuronal, metabolic, and hemodynamic measures of brain organization

We and others have reported that ongoing neuronal and hemodynamic activity informs underlying functional network architecture demonstrated by patterns of RSFC.38,64,73,77 However, the presence of spontaneous FAF dynamics and whether these fluctuations encode patterns of functional brain organization are less studied. For all contrasts, maps of zero-lag correlation were created for regions of interest within cingulate, motor, somatosensory, retrosplenial, parietal (also called VISa, VISrl and VISal), and visual cortex (Figure S4). Awake ISA fluctuations for each contrast (Figure S4A) exhibited general features associated with patterns of RSFC73; for example, strong positive ipsilateral correlation around seed locations and with contralateral homotopic regions. Some regions exhibit reciprocal correlation structure, e.g., between motor and somatosensory or between retrosplenial and parietal regions (also called VISa, VISrl and VISal). RSFC maps based on infraslow FAF activity overlapped highly with those based on neuronal calcium dynamics (Dice coefficient across all map comparisons, 0.67 ± 0.11). Similar agreement was observed between neuronal calcium and hemodynamic maps (Dice coefficient across all comparisons, 0.62 ± 0.23), with the largest disparity observed between RSFC maps of cingulate cortex (Dice, 0.37 ± 0.088).

A different picture was observed over higher (delta, 0.4–4 Hz) frequency ranges in awake mice where FAF and HbT signaling is less frequency dependent (i.e., spectrally whitens) (Figure S4B). While neuronal delta activity exhibits patterns of correlation consistent with neuronal ISA topographies (Dice coefficient between neuronal ISA and delta maps, 0.75 ± 0.037), these features are largely absent in the FAF maps, which exhibited weak, positive correlation around each seed, consistent with low signal-to-noise ratio measurements (Dice coefficient, 0.096 ± 0.035) (Figure S4B). Zero-lag correlation maps of HbT signaling over the delta range exhibited some features observed in the ISA maps (e.g., positive zero-lag correlation among ipsilateral and homotopic regions) (Figure S4B, HbT) (Dice coefficient, 0.50 ± 0.10). However, correlation strengths were generally lower over the cortex (Figure S4E).

K/X anesthesia is associated with increased oscillatory delta-band neural activity that propagates along the anterior-posterior axis.38,64,70 In maps of zero-lag correlation, this activity manifests as strong apparent positive and negative correlation observed in anesthetized calcium maps (Figures S4C and S4D). While the power spectra of FAF activity (Figure 3A) also contain increased delta oscillations, zero-lag correlation maps of anesthetized FAF delta activity resemble more closely topographies reported for awake delta neuronal activity (Dice coefficient, 0.69 ± 0.094). Maps of zero-lag correlation based on anesthetized hemodynamic delta activity exhibit reduced spatial specificity and generally lower correlation strength (Figures S4D and S4E).

Seed-based maps of zero-lag correlation can depend highly on the seed region selected. To obtain a more holistic picture of the functional network organization (i.e., a functional connectome) reported by each contrast, zero-lag correlation was calculated over eight overlapping, two-octave frequency bands for all pairwise comparisons within our field of view (Figure 6A). Among the three contrasts, calcium activity shows the highest degree of consistency across all frequency ranges examined in both awake (averaged across all frequencies, Spearman’s ρ = 0.97 ± 0.023) and anesthetized states (averaged across all frequencies, Spearman’s ρ = 0.97 ± 0.025) (Figure 6B, top). Anesthetized FAF fluctuations (Figure 6B, middle), and to lesser extent those of HbT (Figure 6B bottom) also display similar patterns of correlation (averaged across frequencies, FAF, Spearman’s ρ = 0.95 ± 0.038; HbT, Spearman’s ρ = 0.86 ± 0.17). However, connectome mapping in awake mice becomes less reliable for FAF and HbT activity above 0.32 Hz (FAF) and 0.64 Hz (HbT) due to the marked reduction in high-frequency power. Comparing connectomes across states (Figure 6, right column), patterns of correlation are relatively similar between awake vs. anesthetized calcium dynamics (Spearman’s ρ = 0.89 ± 0.048) and all but the highest frequencies of hemodynamic fluctuations (across all frequencies, Spearman’s ρ = 0.79 ± 0.14). Connectomes based on awake FAF dynamics less resemble those under anesthesia (across all frequencies, Spearman’s ρ = 0.70 ± 0.076). Thus, despite the effects of K/X in modifying interregional correlation strengths, hemodynamic measures of network organization were more robust across states compared to FAF-based measures.

Do changes in neurometabolic/vascular coupling affect non-neuronal measures of RSFC?

K/X profoundly altered NVC compared to NMC, and HbT- and FAF-based measures of RSFC exhibited state dependences. We therefore hypothesized that regions exhibiting the largest changes in coupling would exhibit the largest alterations in FAF or HbT-based RSFC. Two strategies were employed to test this hypothesis. The first evaluated how regional differences in NMC/NVC parameters corresponded to regional changes in RSFC topographies (i.e., the spatial distribution of correlation coefficients with respect to a given seed) reported by FAF or HbT (Figure 7). Changes in MRF parameters were not associated with state-dependent changes in RSFC based on FAF (Figure 7, left column) (time to peak, R2 = 0.076, p = 0.11; width, R2 = 0.013, p = 0.84; amplitude, R2 = 0.032, p = 0.3). The HRF time to peak and width were not significantly associated with HbT-based RSFC differences (time to peak, R2 = 0.056, p = 0.17; width, R2 = 0.037, p = 0.27). Conversely, differences in HRF amplitude significantly correlated with RSFC differences (amplitude, p = 0.00061). However, the amount of variance explained by this relationship was relatively modest (R2 = 30%) and primarily driven by specific regions: primary somatosensory barrel cortex, hindpaw, and supplemental somatosensory regions.

Our second strategy examined estimates of RSFC based on neuronal calcium (ground truth) versus hemodynamic (indirect) signaling given the observed changes in NVC across conditions. Surrogate time series were constructed following prior reports78 to match the spectral content and correlation structure as our measured awake and anesthetized calcium data in order to create a stationary null model of regional neuronal activity (see STAR Methods) for both conditions (Figure S5A). HRFs measured in each region demonstrate profound amplitude attenuation and minimal phase differences above approximately 0.1 Hz (Figure S5B), giving rise to the “low-pass characteristics” that typify the transfer function of the hemodynamic response (Figure S5B). Regional simulated calcium data were convolved with the measured regional awake (or anesthetized) HRFs (Figure S5C) to synthesize hemodynamic signaling. Zero-lag correlation between all simulated HbT time series generated a model of RSFC for both conditions (Figure S5D). Lastly, to assess how changes in NVC from anesthesia would affect hemodynamic-based RSFC arising from awake neuronal activity, surrogate awake calcium data were convolved with HRFs measured under K/X. The simulated differences in RSFC due to changes in regional NVC (Figure S5G) are appreciably smaller in magnitude than those measured (Figure S5F, bottom). Instead, the distribution of RSFC values (Figure S5D) correspond more strongly to the underlying RSFC derived from underlying (simulated) neuronal activity (Figure S5A, left). From these observations, we conclude that changes in regional NVC due to K/X are not sufficient to explain anesthesia-related differences in RSFC based on hemodynamic activity.

DISCUSSION

2F-WFOI of Thy1-jRGECO1a mice allowed for mapping calcium, FAF, and hemodynamic activity to examine spatiotemporal relationships between changes in neuronal activity, oxidative metabolism, and hemodynamics. We demonstrate regional differences in NVC and NMC in the awake mouse brain, that K/X anesthesia profoundly altered NVC, whereas NMC was largely kept intact. Interestingly, regions exhibiting the largest NVC differences due to anesthesia did not necessarily exhibit the largest RSFC changes. We elaborate on these findings below.

Neuronal and metabolic signaling

Changes in calcium concentration observed in Thy1-jRGECO1a mice originate within subsets of cortical neurons in layers 2/3 and 5. FAF corresponds to redox-state alterations of flavoproteins79,80 and is predominantly found in mitochondrial enzyme complexes, with the largest concentration of mitochondria in neurons, followed by glial and endothelial cells, respectively.81 Within CNS cells, mitochondria differ in their spatial distribution, and exhibit greater abundance in presynaptic terminals81 and dendrites.82 In the mammal, mitochondria are distributed with varying densities across the CNS, where regions with larger vascular density contain relatively more mitochondria.81 Thus, some similarities between neuronal, metabolic, and subsequent hemodynamic signaling (as in Figure 3) might be anticipated. The tight coupling between evoked calcium and FAF activity is in line with prior work demonstrating that increases in oxidative metabolism resemble the temporal envelope of evoked multiunit activity,83 and the onset time ratios observed between evoked jRGECO1a and FAF are consistent with other work examining GCaMP and FAF.84 However, since most studies examining FAF have been performed in anesthetized animals,84–86 it is difficult to compare our awake evoked dynamics with other studies. In rats, Weber et al.85 reported that time to 75% of maximum for FAF is approximately 1.5 s and that of cerebral blood flow is 3.4 s under α-chloralose and isoflurane. Shibuki et al.86 reported a delay of ~1.2 s in the peak time between FAF and 530-nm reflectance in urethane-anesthetized rats. We did not collect evoked responses under anesthetized conditions, but our measured delays of 1.2 s between FAF and HbT under anesthetized resting-state conditions are in line with this prior work.

Under K/X anesthesia, pronounced oscillatory activity over the delta range was observed in jRGECO1a and FAF signals. We hypothesize that this is due to elevated oxidative metabolism during anesthesia to support delta-wave propagation.87 These state-dependent differences in FAF signaling are consistent with studies exploring cerebral metabolism across the sleep-wake cycle, where different brain regions and arousal states (e.g., wake, nonrapid eye movement [NREM], rapid eye movement) show differential rates of glucose metabolism and fuel utilization88 (e.g., aerobic glycolysis is higher during wake compared to sleep89). Thus, lower delta FAF activity during wakefulness could illustrate the brain’s reliance on aerobic glycolysis and lactate production for task-based behaviors, rather than FAF-dependent changes in oxidative metabolism. Conversely, during NREM sleep, higher delta power is a marker of slow-wave activity,64 necessary for restorative sleep,90 and has higher levels of FAF-dependent oxidative metabolism.91 Increases/decreases in oxidative metabolism could also be frequency dependent as other evidence suggests that cerebral metabolic rates of both glucose and oxygen decrease during sleep.91

While awake delta-band FAF activity was weak in healthy young mice, we expect this would change in disease models with sleep impairment, such as Alzheimer’s disease, stroke, type-2 diabetes, or aging. Using polysomnography coupled with quantitative electroencephalogram (EEG), NREM-slow-wave sleep is rich in delta activity but, when perturbed, there is increased delta activity during wake demonstrating increased sleep drive and propensity for sleep.90 These characteristic changes in delta activity during wakefulness are found in numerous diseases that impact sleep.92 Having established that mice exhibit spontaneous FAF dynamics, future studies can examine how FAF signaling changes during disease relative to sleep/wake states.

Regional and state-dependent differences in NMC and NVC

A growing number of studies have reported regional differences in NVC across species within cortex14–16,93 (rodents and humans), brainstem14 (rats), thalamus14 (rats), and hippocampus17 (mice). In line with previous reports in mice,53,93 we show delays between awake spontaneous calcium and hemodynamic signaling ~0.9 s, and that the HRF lag increased by ~40% under anesthesia. While our results are in general agreement (i.e., reduced, slower NVC under anesthesia) with other studies,63,75,94–98 different anesthetics might differentially alter local NVC.

Over the cortex, NVC/NMC amplitude was strongest within portions of somatosensory cortex (hindpaw, barrel) and parietal regions (also called VISa, VISrl, and VISal), findings that persisted independent of state. These observations cannot be explained alone by the magnitude of underlying cortical activity. The topographies of cortical power drastically reduce between awake and anesthetize mice for all contrasts, with the largest changes in these regions (Figure 3). In addition, while spontaneous behaviors99,100 could be contributing to these findings in awake animals, they are not present under K/X. Interestingly, lower coupling amplitude and correlation (i.e., model fit) are observed within motor cortex compared to other brain regions (Figure S3). These observations under awake conditions are consistent with prior work showing that increased neural activity is not always accompanied by changes in blood volume during locomotion in rodents.101,102 That stronger coupling in parts of somatosensory and parietal cortex and weaker coupling within motor regions persisted across states suggests an inherent physiological role for these regional differences (discussed below).

In part, local NVC relationships appear to be dictated by the resident vasculature composition and density,17,103 which might explain regional timing differences (i.e., MRF/HRF width, delay). Direct vasoactive messaging from different cell types can differentially alter vascular tone,104–108 and, depending on regional network architecture, pathway-specific variations in NVC/NMC could depend on the cell types engaged42,109,110 (excellent reviews of these topics are available elsewhere111–120). Additionally, regional differences in NVC/NMC and the correspondence between these phenomena could be due to local differences in energy utilization. For example, increases in cerebral blood flow (CBF) are often accompanied by increases in oxidative metabolism.18–21 In this scenario, one might anticipate positive correlation between NMC and NVC amplitude. However, other studies report nonexistent,102 or even opposite, relationships between neuronal activity, blood flow, and/or metabolism.22–27,121 These regional differences could highlight a disconnect between a region’s need for glucose versus the need for oxygen (both supplied by the vasculature). While glucose can fuel ATP generation via glycolysis or oxidative metabolism, glucose also serves as a biosynthetic substrate for amino acid and neurotransmitter synthesis.18,28 Therefore, the glycolytic needs of a brain region can be somewhat independent of oxygen consumption and mitochondrial respiration.28,122–124 Relative to ATP generation, brain regions with high levels of neuronal activity and RSFC in humans often rely heavily on glycolysis to rapidly generate ATP (e.g., the default mode network, which includes the murine cingulate and retrosplenial regions125,126). Glycolysis is not dependent on oxygen, which could be one explanation for a quick glucose demand matched with a slower oxygen utilization. In this event, one might expect weak positive (or no) correlation between NMC and NVC timing. Additionally, lactate production through glycolysis generates nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+), which is an essential cofactor for both glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle.127 If NAD+ is depleted, both glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation will stop until NAD+ is replenished. Therefore, our data could be highlighting a hierarchical order of glucose use in fast-acting brain regions: glycolysis provides immediate energy, while oxygen-dependent, ATP-generating mitochondrial metabolism comes secondarily. This is supported by multiple studies that demonstrate that fluctuations in interstitial fluid lactate levels (compared to glucose) change more rapidly with wake-associated behaviors and arousal transitions.123,128

While regional coupling differences could reflect the difference in oxidative versus nonoxidative metabolism, we found a somewhat counter-intuitive anti-correlation between local HRF and MRF delays. During wake, this relationship is weak (16% variance explained) but strengthens under K/X (39% variance explained). One intriguing possibility for these findings is that NVC supports both oxygen-dependent and glycolytic metabolism. Recent theoretical work suggests that, although rapid NVC might be more closely linked to oxygen-dependent and anticipatory metabolism, subsequent NVC might be required to maintain homeostasis due to the byproducts of glycolysis.112,129 Our observed anti-correlation between HRF time to peak and MRF time to peak could be related to timing and amplitude differences between oxygen-dependent and glycolytic metabolism differentially relayed by NVC and NMC (the MRF here largely reflects oxygen-dependent metabolism). In support of this speculation, regions exhibiting longer HRF time to peak also showed lower MRF area under the curve (Figure 5). Similarly, the reduced correlation between the HRF and MRF widths in the awake state relative to anesthesia might relate to a change in the balance between oxygen-dependent and glycolytic metabolism, which has been reported during sleep in humans130 and during anesthesia in rodents.131,132

Separately, while ATP production within mitochondria would clearly support processes involved in NVC/NMC, a more direct role of mitochondria in regulating these processes is increasingly recognized.133,134 Neuronal activity and neurometabolic regulation are hypothesized to be controlled through mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake. Recent fMRI studies have shown that increasing or decreasing mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake capacity impacts both intrinsic and evoked activity in the form of altered RSFC and NVC.133,135 Differences in regional mitochondrial density and mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake homeostasis, and how these changes feedforward or back on local excitability,136 could impact the degree to which neuronal, metabolic, and hemodynamic activity are coupled across the brain.

Anesthesia can profoundly alter neurovascular responses through a variety of mechanisms, including modulating excitability, neuronal processing, vascular reactivity, and systemic physiology.40,53,94,95,137–139 Interestingly, while K/X significantly affected NVC, K/X did not appreciably alter NMC. It would be advantageous from an evolutionary point of view to preserve tight coupling between neurons and their energy stores. Because the MRF was largely unchanged across states, the MRF between neuronal calcium and FAF could represent an optimal coupling relationship across conditions. Alternatively, unchanging NMC across conditions could rather reflect limits on NMC or on our ability to distinguish changes with this method. However, the strong correspondence between the FAF and calcium signals, and the degree to which calcium is predictive of subsequent FAF activity, even under anesthesia, suggests that the majority of the FAF signal comes from excitatory activity (the predicted FAF signal explained 56% of the measured awake FAF signal variance and 85% under K/X).

Mapping brain organization with neuronal, metabolic, and hemodynamic activity

Most awake RSFC maps based on jRGECO1a fluorescence resembled those previously reported using GFP-based GECIs (e.g., GCaMP38,70,140,141). However, due to the deeper tissue penetration of jRGECO1a excitation/emission, some topographies (e.g., cingulate, retrosplenial) appear to report broader connectivity than previously reported38,70,140 and instead mirror topographies of network structures observed using fMRI.142 For example, seeds placed in left cingulate cortex (Figure 6A) reveal long-range connectivity with posterior brain regions, including retrosplenial cortex and medial parietal regions. Similarly, seeds placed in left retrosplenial and medial parietal cortex reveal reciprocal connectivity with cingulate and more lateral cortex, regions consistent with the mouse default mode network.143

Taking advantage of the spectral separation between jRGECO1a and FAF, we were able to determine that spontaneous fluctuations in oxidative metabolism also exhibit patterns of activity associated with brain network organization. Like the calcium-based maps, FAF-derived RSFC maps were state and frequency dependent. Correlated FAF activity was largely not observed over delta frequencies in awake mice. Spatially limited, low-magnitude correlation coefficients are features indicative of low signal-to-noise ratio measurements, as reflected FAF activity above ~1 Hz. Under K/X anesthesia, pronounced oscillatory activity over the delta range resulted in strong positive and negative zero-lag correlation in the calcium-based maps over large portions of the cortical surface obscuring underlying RSFC structure.64 Conversely, delta FAF oscillations under anesthesia resulted in correlation structure more spatially similar to those observed in the awake, calcium-based maps.

Coherent patterns of spontaneous activity within and across hemispheres represent highly organized synaptic signaling within the brain.144 RSFC has been traditionally defined over infraslow frequencies because this range of frequencies contributed most to RSFC structure using fMRI in humans.145 However, we see in awake mice that hemodynamic activity appears to contain some information regarding network organization over higher frequencies (>1 Hz). These findings are in line with more recent fMRI studies taking advantage of technological advancements improving the frame rates of fMRI.146 Physiological signaling (e.g., pulse, respiration) can affect RSFC measures,147 and some degree of coherence in the zero-lag correlation maps for frequencies above ~1 Hz (Figure S4) could be due to systemic influences.

While K/X affected contrast-specific RSFC strength relative to wake, hemodynamic-based estimates of RSFC structure were kept relatively intact compared to those from FAF. Interestingly, anesthesia-related differences in NVC or NMC timing did not correspond to contrast-specific RSFC differences between conditions (Figures 7 and S5). That is not to say that regional differences in NVC (or NMC) cannot influence estimates of RSFC based on zero-lag correlation. The idea that regional coupling differences might influence RSFC estimates is especially important in the context of neurovascular diseases, for example, following stroke,148,149 during which timing differences can be appreciably larger than those observed in the present study. Further, Pearson correlations are non-directed measures of interaction and ignore the temporal structure within the data. Through evaluation of pairwise cross-correlation (for instance), one could account for regional coupling delays and potentially reveal underlying “true” connectivity structure38,70,150 or infer directed “information flow” between regions.70,151,152 Evaluating lagged relationships using more sophisticated strategies reveals that RSFC patterns emerge from underlying temporal processes.153 This lag structure in humans and mice appears to be significantly more sensitive to state changes than RSFC.70,154 Evaluating RSFC (lagged or otherwise) between regions exhibiting vastly different impulse response function (IRF) widths would be especially problematic as differences in spectral content might obscure correlation structure. As brain mapping methods advance, more encompassing descriptions of global functional brain organization have emerged,155,156 potentially mitigating shortcomings of any individual strategy.

Beyond addressing the challenges in interpreting and analyzing functional neuroimaging, the demonstration of spatially varying NVC and NMC—and their inconsistent relationship with one another particularly under anesthesia—raises important questions about how CBF and metabolism are managed. These differences may, for example, relate to differences in tissue cellular and subcellular composition,157 balance of excitation and inhibition,158 or the degree of mature versus immature cortex.159 Regulation of NVC and NMC could also differ between task and rest. The underlying assumption that task-evoked vascular and metabolic dynamics are the same as resting-state vascular and metabolic dynamics has not been firmly established.146,160–165 Although further experiments are needed, our results clearly show that measuring NVC and NMC in vivo, along with increasingly available techniques to dissect and influence brain tissue metabolism and composition,166 will help lead to a better understanding of how the brain organizes its blood flow and metabolic rates during different imaging paradigms.

Limitations of this study

It is important to note that GECI-related measures do not completely capture neuronal activity.167 Incorporating voltage-sensitive dyes/indicators or electrophysiological probes would allow for determining how subthreshold activity or multi- versus single-unit spiking events relate to local metabolic activity.5,6,167,168 Measurements of FAF intensity represent flavoprotein concentration and not absolute metabolism.169 For example, increases in flavoprotein production or decreased flavoprotein consumption could result in similar FAF intensity.170 Combining FAF measurements with those of phosphorylated forms of NAD (NAD/NADH) could disambiguate changes in local tissue energetics and identify shifts of metabolism toward or away from glycolysis.171

While our data were censored for gross translational movement due to motion, we did not collect independent measures of behavior time-locked with 2F-WFOI. Spontaneous facial and body motions (termed fidgeting) strongly correspond to ongoing changes in arousal, which itself is reflected in ongoing, spontaneous cortical activity.99,100,102,156,164,172 Whether epochs of arousal changes can or should be censored from neuroimaging data (beyond censoring gross movement) is an active area of investigation. Recent evidence suggests that fidgeting, arousal, and spontaneous activity all reflect a single latent process and are intimately interwoven.156,173 In humans and mice, restricting RSFC analyses to a small number of imaging frames during which the brain exhibits statistically larger bouts of activity (i.e., cofluctuations) are sufficient to recapitulate brain-wide connectivity information.174,175 Other studies show that estimates of RSFC structure converge to a common connectome with as little as 15 min of data regardless of whether fidgeting is removed.78 During periods of low arousal (e.g., when fidgeting subsides), mice can transition into sleep.176 This state change is associated with increased slow-wave activity, which might drive large changes in zero-lag correlation177 and thus inferences on underlying brain network organization. Spectral analysis of our awake data over time did not reveal the presence of any slow-wave activity. A secondary analysis using a sleep-staging algorithm178 revealed that only 0.46% of our awake neuroimaging was contaminated by epochs of sleep.

Due to wavelength-specific differences in tissue scattering and absorption, the reported neural, metabolic, and hemodynamic activity are sampled across different depths, which confounds direct comparisons across contrasts. NMC/NVC as defined here refers to the coupling between Thy1-expressing cells (primarily excitatory cells of layers 2/3 and 5) and changes in FAF and hemodynamics, neither of which are sourced to a particular cell population. These differences in cellular specificity could explain the differences observed in NVC but not NMC (as it was relatively unchanged) between conditions. Additionally, estimates of NVC and NMC were based on a causal, linear model for comparing against prior work.53,179–181 This model can account for a large amount of variance explained between (Thy1-based) neuronal calcium activity and FAF or hemodynamic signaling in many brain regions. However, the variability in model fit we observed over the cortex (R2, 10%–92% for FAF, and 7%–72% for HbT) clearly indicates other contributors to metabolic/hemodynamic activity (and thus FAF- or HbT-based estimates of RSFC) and that more complete descriptions of neurometabolic/vascular coupling are required. Further, the choice of regularization constant and/or other preprocessing steps (e.g., choice of band-pass filters) can alter the shape of the estimated IRFs. Future work could incorporate more elaborate modeling of nonlinearities,182–185 intermediate steps,5,6,109,186 or retrograde phenomena (e.g., vasculo-neural coupling).187–191 These strategies in combination with GECIs expressed in other cell types and/or electrophysiology could be used to establish (1) contributions from different excitatory and inhibitory neurons to NMC/NVC, and (2) how changes in cell-specific activity relate to RSFC measures. The fluorescence correction strategy employed specifically addresses hemodynamic-absorption-related effects, the accuracy of which is expected to be independent of the imaging paradigm (task versus rest). However, in some regions (e.g., large superficial vessels), this correction method can be inadequate and require more sophisticated mapping strategies to account for regional differences in optical properties.192

Animal reporting is according to ARRIVE guidelines.

Transgenic mice expressing jRGECO1a driven by the thymus cell antigen 1 (Thy1) promotor were acquired from Jackson Laboratories (JAX Strain: Tg(Thy1-jRGECO1a) GP8.20Dkim/J; stock: 030525) and maintained in the Bauer lab. These mice, originally from the Genetically-Encoded Neuronal Indicator and Effector Project, express an improved red fluorescent calcium indicator, jRGECO1a (derived from mApple), in subsets of cortical neurons in layers 2/3 and 5.58 Wild type mice (C57BL/6J) were generated from our own colony. Their ancestors were purchased from Jackson Laboratories (JAX Strain: C57BL/6J; stock: 000664).

Six homozygous Thy1-jRGECO1a mice (3 female, 3 male; 7–8 months of age) and six C57 mice (5 female and 1 male; 4–5 months of age) were used for experimentation. Mice were prepared for awake resting state imaging according to prior work.42,193,194 Prior to imaging, a cranial window was secured to the intact skull of each mouse with dental cement (Metabond) under 2% isoflurane anesthesia following scalp retraction according to our previously published protocols.195 For anesthetized imaging, mice were intraperitoneally injected with a Ketamine/Xylazine cocktail (86.9 mg/kg of ketamine, 13.4 mg/kg of xylazine) and were kept warm with a 37°C heating pad. Eye lubricant was applied to keep the eyes moisturized. The same mice were imaged under both awake and anesthetized conditions. For mice imaged on the same day (n = 3), awake imaging was performed first followed immediately by anesthetized imaging. The other 3 mice were imaged under anesthesia approximately 1 week after awake imaging. To ensure that there were no temperature-related changes in our imaging measures through the cranial window, the head temperature was measured to be 36.3°C and 36.6°C in a subset of awake and anesthetized animals, respectively, using an FLIR compact thermal camera. In awake mice, HbO and HbR signaling over the cortex revealed an average respiration rate of approximately 2–4 Hz and a heart rate of approximately 10 Hz (600 bpm), in line with prior work by us and others.72,73,196,197.

METHOD DETAILS

Dual-fluorophore WFOI system

A custom light engine consisting of 470nm (measured peak λ = 457 nm, LCS-0470-50-22, Mightex Systems), 530nm (measured peak λ = 525nm, LCS-0525-60-22, Mightex Systems), and 625nm (measured peak λ = 637 nm, M625L3, Thorlabs) Light-emitting diodes (LED) illuminated the skull (Figure 1B). Diffuse reflected light for optical intrinsic signal imaging and fluorescence emission were collected by a lens (focal length = 75mm, NMV-75M1, Navitar), split by a 580nm dichroic (FF580-FDi02-t3-25×36, Semrock) and sampled by two scientific complementary metal–oxide–semiconductor (CMOS) cameras with USB3 connectivity (Zyla 5.5, Andor). A 500nm long pass filter (FF01-500/LP-25, Semrock) in front of Camera 1 (CMOS1) blocked 470nm FAF excitation light and passed FAF emission and 530nm reflectance (Figure 1C). The 580nm dichroic and a 593nm long pass filter (FF01-593/LP-25, Semrock) in front of Camera 2 (CMOS2) blocked 530nm jRGECO1a excitation light and passed jRGECO1a emission and 625nm reflectance (Figure 1C). Each camera sensor was cropped to 1024×1024 pixels, and 2×2 binning was performed on camera to increase the acquisition rate. The frame rate of each camera was 100Hz, and all contrasts were imaged at 25 Hz. The LEDs and camera exposures were synchronized and triggered via a data acquisition card (PCI-6733, National Instruments) using MATLAB R2019a (MathWorks). For imaging Thy1-jRGECO1a and C57 mice, the pulse durations for the LEDs were as follows: 9ms (470nm excitation), 9ms (530nm excitation), 0.2ms (530nm reflectance) and 0.3ms (625nm reflectance). LED spectra were measured using an Ocean Optics USB 200+ spectrometer.

Initial pilot studies and results from our prior work were used to determine appropriate exposure times for each LED. The signal-to-noise ratio of each imaging channel was calculated as reported elsewhere198 and based on the following considerations. The sensors of our imaging cameras (Andor Zyla 5.5 USB 3.0) exhibit a readout noise of 2.6 e– RMS, a full-well capacity of 30,000 electrons (e–) and a sensitivity of 0.45 e–/AD count for 16-bit digitization. Over a 512 × 512-pixel area (i.e., prior to binning), pixel intensities (in e–) averaged over the cortex for each channel are as follows: 1.0 × 103 (jRGECO1a emission), 1.4 × 103 (FAF emission), 1.1 × 104 (532nm reflectance), 1.5 × 104 (625nm reflectance). We then spatially bin to 128 × 128 pixels so that each binned pixel contains 1.6 × 104 (jRGECO1a; Figure S6D left), 2.2 × 104 (FAF; Figure S6D right), 1.7 × 105 (532nm reflectance), 2.3 × 105 (625 nm reflectance) e–per channel. These values correspond to a shot noise level of 148, 126, 417, 482 e– RMS per channel and a collective readout noise of 20.8 e– RMS. From these values we determined our raw imaging data are dominated by shot noise at the following % per channel (Figure S6A): Awake: jRGECO1a emission: 0.76%; FAF emission: 0.67%; 532nm reflectance: 0.23%; 625nm reflectance: 0.20%. Anesthetized: jRGECO1a emission: 0.73%; FAF emission: 0.64%; 532nm reflectance: 0.22%; 625nm reflectance: 0.21%. Typical signal fluctuations of jRGECO1a, FAF and HbT averaged over the cortex (Figure S6B) are as follows: Awake: jRGECO1a emission: 4.1%; FAF emission: 2.7%; HbT: 7.2%. Anesthetized: jRGECO1a emission: 13%; FAF emission: 1.6%; HbT: 3.4%. Thus, the average SNR over the cortex (Figure S6C) of the acquired data at full frame rate is as follows: Awake: jRGECO1a: 5.5:1; FAF: 4.1:1; HbT: 32:1 Anesthetized: jRGECO1a: 18:1; FAF: 2.5:1; HbT: 15:1. Note that temporal smoothing (resampling) from 25Hz to 5Hz will further increase the SNR of each measurement channel by a factor of 2.2 (i.e., sqrt (25/5) as will spatial smoothing (e.g., via Gaussian kernel). While higher SNR/channel can also be achieved by increasing the exposure time (or LED brightness) for each LED, this approach can lead to reduction in the effective sampling rate and lead to increased photobleaching of flavoproteins and jRGECO1a.

Data acquisition

Mice were mounted to the imaging system as per our previous experiments using an aluminum bracket attached to a skull-mounted Plexiglas window.38 The body was supported by a felt pouch suspended by steel posts. Resting state imaging was performed for 30 min in each mouse for awake and anesthetized conditions. For task-based imaging, air puffs (40 PSI, 100 ms) were directed perpendicular to the mystacial whiskers (Picospritzer III, Parker Hannifin) at 3 Hz for 5 s, followed by 25 s of rest. This 30 s block design was repeated for 15 min for each mouse. Before any imaging run, dark counts due to background sensor noise were imaged for 1 s with all LEDs off.

Determining the crosstalk between FAF and jRGECO1a emission

The emission spectrum of FAF extends into the band of wavelengths passed by the dichroic mirror and long pass filter in front of CMOS2 and could therefore contaminate measurements of jRGECO1a fluorescence. Photons from the 530nm excitation LED above 593nm might also be captured by CMOS2. Two strategies were implemented to estimate these contributions in five C57BL/6J mice using the same imaging paradigm as performed in Thy1-jRGECO1a mice (i.e., same LED illumination power, LED duty cycles, sampling rates, etc.).

Our first strategy took advantage of the large increases in spontaneous delta band activity observed in both jRGECO1a and FAF under ketamine/xylazine anesthesia (see Figure 3; Figures S7A and S7B). In C57BL/6J mice, FAF captured by CMOS1 exhibited strong delta band activity (Figure S7B light green), like activity in Thy1-jRGECO1a mice captured by CMOS2 (Figure S7A dark purple). If FAF emission was detected by CMOS2, evidence of this high amplitude signal should be observed in C57BL/6J mice (Figure S7A, light purple). Instead, the raw signal captured by CMOS2 in C57BL/6J mice exhibits very low dynamic range (~10dB) and low SNR above ~0.2Hz. From this analysis, we conclude that the FAF contribution to resting jRGECO1a fluorescence is negligible. Similarities in low frequency signaling (below ~0.2Hz) during 530nm excitation (Figure S7A, light purple) and 625 nm reflectance in C57BL/6J (Figure S7A orange) and jRGECO1a mice (Figure S7A, yellow) mice suggest that the weak raw signal in CMOS2 detected from C57BL/6J mice is modulated by hemodynamic fluctuations. FAF power spectra in awake C57BL/6J and Thy1-jRGECO1a mice exhibit similar dynamic range (Figure S7C).

Our second strategy optimized our ability to detect weak FAF captured by CMOS2 by performing peripheral whisker stimulation in awake mice. In CMOS2, the block-averaged detected raw signal in C57BL/6J mice peaked around 0.32% while that in Thy1-jRGECO1a mice was 4.6% (ratio: 7.0%) (Figure S7D). The average ratio between each modulation peak of the raw jRGECO1a signal and the raw signal in C57BL/6J mice at the corresponding time points was 3.0%. Baseline raw light levels detected by CMOS2 in C57 mice were 3.9×104 ADU and 7.3×103 ADU in Thy1-jRGECO1a and C57BL/6J mice, respectively (raw jRGECO1a ratio = 19%). We therefore estimate the contribution of FAF (or the raw signal detected) in the jRGECO1a signal captured by CMOS2 to be 1.3% (i.e., 7.0 % × 19 %). Additionally, note that the block-averaged raw signal intensity from C57BL/6J mice in CMOS2 exhibits distinct dynamics from those observed in 625nm reflectance, raw and corrected FAF (in Figure 2) or jRGECO1a fluorescence (Figure S7D).

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Data analysis

A summary of the 2F-WFOI processing pipeline is outlined in (Figure 1E).

Spatial normalization

Images (512 × 512 pixels after on-camera binning) were binned 4×4 off camera (to a resolution of 128×128) to increase the signal-to-noise ratio of each image and to reduce data size. To coregister image sequences from each camera, four corresponding markers were chosen from images of 530nm reflectance (CMOS1) and 625nm reflectance (CMOS2) and spatially normalized using a projective transformation to account for slight differences in camera alignment and fields-of-view. Dark frames from each camera were averaged and subtracted from that camera’s image sequence. All image sequences were then affine transformed and aligned to the Allen mouse brain atlas as per our previous studies.73

Image processing

Slow trends in light level (e.g., due to fluctuations in LED illumination) were temporally detrended using a 5th order polynomial fit. The images were spatially smoothed with a Gaussian filter (5×5 pixel kernel with a standard deviation of 1.3 pixels). Changes in 530nm, and 625nm reflectance were interpreted using the modified Beer-Lambert law to calculate changes in hemoglobin concentration as per our previous studies42,195: (Equation 1) Φ(r,t)=Φ0e−Δμa(r,t)L

Here, Φ(r,t) is the measured light intensity, Φ0 is the baseline light intensity, Δμa(r,t) is the change in absorption coefficient due to changes in hemoglobin concentration, and L is the optical path length factor for photons traveling in tissue determined analytically assuming a semi-infinite geometry.199 Assumed optical properties are listed in Table S3. Changes in hemoglobin concentration were determined by inverting the system of equations: (Equation 2) Δμa,λ(r,t)=Eλ,iΔHbi(r,t) where E is the extinction coefficient for hemoglobin,200 and i is the index for different hemoglobin species.

Image sequences of fluorescence emission detected by CMOS1 (i.e., uncorrected FAF) and CMOS2 (i.e., uncorrected jRGECO1a fluorescence) were converted to percent change (Δ F/F × 100%) by dividing each pixel’s time trace by its average fluorescence over each imaging run. Absorption of excitation and emission light for each fluorophore due to hemoglobin was corrected as outlined.201 Image sequences of evoked responses were not temporally filtered. Unless otherwise stated, resting-state fluctuations of jRGECO1a, FAF, and hemodynamic activity were filtered using a fifth-order Butterworth filter to 0.009Hz–0.08Hz to examine infraslow activity (ISA) or 0.4Hz–4Hz to examine delta band activity and to align with prior language used in studies incorporating FMRI, WFOI and electrophysiological recordings. Gross translational motion was not detected in any of our imaging sessions using phase correlation applied to raw green reflectance image sequences of each run and mouse.

Stimulus evoked activity

Image sequences of stimulus evoked activity were averaged across blocks and then across mice. Images during the stimulation period were averaged to generate group-level maps of peak response for each contrast. Stimulus-evoked time courses were calculated by thresholding each contrast’s peak map at 75% of its maximum value and averaging all time courses in pixels surviving that threshold. Onset time was defined as the time when a given contrast reached 10% of its peak value. Rise time was defined as the difference between the onset time and the time when the contrast achieved 90% of the peak value. A decaying exponential function was fit to the end of the responses of jRGECO1a and FAF to calculate the decay rate. The mean difference between all peaks and troughs of stimulus evoked jRGECO1a responses were used to calculate the modulation depth.

Resting-state activity

Part of the goal of our study was to compare against prior work53,202 examining NVC between excitatory neuronal activity and changes in total hemoglobin (HbT). HbT also offers higher contrast-to-noise and can be more spatially specific than HbO or HbR.195,203 For these reasons most of the spontaneous hemodynamic activity evaluated in the manuscript is that of HbT (calculated as the sum of HbO and HbR).

Band-limited power

The power spectral density (PSD) of each time trace was calculated using the pwelch function in MATLAB. Each trace was divided into 8 segments with 50% overlap and windowed by a Hamming function. The periodogram of each segment was calculated via the discrete Fourier transform, squared, and all 8 periodograms were averaged. PSD curves for each mouse were calculated by averaging PSD curves across all brain pixels and runs. PSD maps for each mouse were calculated by integrating pixel-wise PSD curves over ISA and delta bands. PSD curves and maps were converted to decibels.

Estimating neurovascular and neurometabolic coupling using weighted deconvolution

Mouse level estimates of NVC and NMC (see below) were determined from spontaneous fluctuations of each contrast filtered between 0.02 Hz and 2Hz for comparison with previous studies.53 Filtered time courses were then averaged within regions defined by the Allen mouse brain atlas and split into 30-s epochs. Prior to calculating hemodynamic and metabolic response functions (HRFs, MRFs) used to estimate neurovascular and neurometabolic coupling, time courses for calcium, FAF, and HbT signaling were resampled to 250Hz to capture potentially crucial small-scale features following deconvolution. This strategy is operationally equivalent to interpolation and in line with our prior work evaluating lag structure of cortical calcium and hemodynamic activity.70 We chose to explicitly resample the input data (instead of interpolating the response functions) given the data-driven nature of the deconvolution method. NVC and NMC were modeled using discrete convolution, which can be written in the matrix form y=Xh and expressed as: (Equation 3) y1y2y3⋮yn=x100⋯0x2x10⋯0x3x2x1⋯0⋮⋮⋮⋱⋮xnxn−1xn−2⋯x1h1h2h3⋮hn where X is a delay matrix of the input signal (calcium fluctuations), n is the kernel size with length 7500 (i.e., 30 s*sampling rate for both NVC and NMC, with a sampling rate of 250 Hz), h is the impulse response function and y is the output estimated hemodynamic or metabolic activity. Impulse response functions were determined using a weighted least squares deconvolution approach53,202,204 with the solution205: (Equation 4) h=X⊤WX+λI−1X⊤Wy, where W is a weight matrix and was chosen to be the singular-value spectra of X. We note here that for a square matrix, the first singular value, W1,1, is an estimate of the spectral norm of X. In this context, the regularization constant, λ, can be expressed as a fraction of the norm of X⊤WX (i.e., λ=λ˜W1,13). The fractional regularization constant, λ˜, was chosen empirically and was set to 5 × 10−7 for both NVC and NMC estimates. We note that other preprocessing steps (choice of bandpass, etc.) might affect the choice of regularization constant and/or the shape of the estimated impulse response function. To minimize boundary effects and facilitate assessment of quasi-acausal relations, time traces were windowed using a Tukey window (cosine fraction of 0.3) and the output, y; was introduced with a delay of 3 s before solving the deconvolution. To quantify goodness-of-fit between estimated metabolic (hemodynamic) activity, response functions were convolved with measured calcium time courses and compared with measured metabolic (hemodynamic) in each region via the Pearson’s correlation coefficient. The residual error between the measured and predicted FAF/HbT signals was normalized by subtracting the corresponding mean and then dividing by the standard deviation of the measured FAF/HbT signal. Plots of residual power (Figure S3C) were calculated by the square modulus of the FFT of residual time courses for each mouse. Maps of normalized mean squared error were calculated by squaring the residual error at each time point and then averaging the values over time.

Importantly, estimates of NVC and especially NMC require accurately removing hemodynamic artifacts. As can be seen in Figure 2C and 2D, failure to account for changes in blood volume (in this case during task) result in apparent decreases in FAF despite increases in oxidative metabolism associated with local increases in neuronal activity during activation. Calculating NMC using uncorrected FAF results in an apparent “negative” coupling relationship that more closely resembles that of NVC (but flipped in sign) as reported in Figure S3. We note that given the computationally intensive nature of deconvolving calcium from hemodynamic time series to extract response functional for all pixels, runs and mice (2 weeks total), we opted to demonstrate this apparent “negative coupling” using cross-correlation.

Zero lag correlation

Measures of RSFC can be influenced by neural processes distinct from RSN communication (e.g., global slow delta activity under anesthesia64), yielding apparent “connectivity” in regions with shared activity. To avoid confusing these two concepts, we use the more general term of zero-lag correlation throughout the manuscript, and only refer to RSFC in specific instances where applicable. Canonical regions in the left and right hemispheres corresponding to cingulate, primary motor, primary somatosensory barrel, medial parietal (VISa), retrosplenial and primary visual cortices were chosen as representative RSFC mas for each contrast (Figure S4). Time traces for each region of interest (ROI) were calculated by averaging all time traces within a 0.25mm radius of a seed locus per our prior reports.38,73,148 Correlating each ROI’s time trace with all other pixel time traces produces a map of zero-lag correlation for a that seed. Calculation of zero-lag correlation between all pairwise comparisons within our field of view produces a whole cortex correlation matrix. Whole cortex correlation structure (Figure 6) was evaluated across frequencies and conditions over overlapping, dual-octave frequency bands spanning 0.01Hz–5.12Hz using Spearman correlation similar to our prior work.70 Each column (or row) of the whole cortex correlation matrix represents an RSFC map for each pixel.

For comparing changes in RSFC topographies to changes in NVC/NMC parameters (Figure 7) RSFC was calculated over infraslow frequencies (i.e., the frequency range most often used for mapping RSFC via BOLD-FMRI) for brain regions defined by the Allen Mouse Brain Atlas. Maps for left and right brain regions (e.g., left and right motor cortex) were averaged together producing 22 total maps for comparison. Spatial similarities between regional RSFC maps during wake and under K/X were determined by calculating the spatial correlation coefficient between each set of maps (e.g., the HbT-based RSFC maps for primary motor cortex during wake and anesthesia). Regional spatial correlation coefficients were then plotted against that region’s change in NMC or NVC delay, width, or amplitude.

In some instances, topographical differences in patterns of zero-lag correlation were evaluated using Dice overlap (see Results). For these comparisons, maps were binarized using a correlation threshold of z(r) > 0.5, and the Dice coefficient of overlap was calculated between a candidate map and the corresponding reference map.

Stationary null model for evaluating changes in coupling between direct and indirect measures of brain organization

In order to evaluate the effects of regional changes in NVC alone on estimates of hemodynamic-based measures of RSFC, stationary surrogate neuronal time series were constructed following prior reports78 to match the regional spectral content and correlation structure of the measured calcium data for awake and anesthetized conditions (see Figure S5A). Briefly, instances of a multivariate random normal process were constructed in MATLAB using normrand to initialize a stationary process with zero mean and unit variance. To match spectral content and preserve stationarity, the two-sided amplitude spectra of each instance was multiplied with the corresponding measured regional amplitude spectrum. Amplitude spectra were upsampled via Piecewise Cubic Hermite Interpolating Polynomial (MATLAB, ‘pchip’) to match signal lengths, and the inverse Fourier transform (MATLAB2022, ‘ifft’) was performed to generate a stationary time series for each region. Stationary data were then projected into the measured correlation space using eigen decomposition of the measured RSFC matrix to match the correlation structure of the measured awake or anesthetize calcium data.

Statistics

Pearson r values were transformed to Fisher Z scores prior to any statistical analysis. Pairwise comparisons were evaluated via Student’s t-test assuming unequal group variance. P-values <0.05 were considered significant.
