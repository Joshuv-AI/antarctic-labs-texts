Ion-disrupted hydrogen-bond networks enable fast water transport under two-dimensional confinement
Authors: Mingyang Xia, Xianglong Du, Canbin Wang, Jinguo Liu, Zhihua Zhou, Ying-Ru Qiu, Yao-Hui Wang, Fujie Tang, Sheng Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWater transport under nanoscale confinement often exhibits anomalously high flow rates that defy classical hydrodynamic descriptions. While such behavior is commonly analyzed assuming pure water, confined water under realistic conditions inevitably contains dissolved ions. How ion-water interactions reshape hydrogen-bond networks and regulate nanoscale water transport therefore remains poorly understood. Here we study ion-modulated water transport using vermiculite-based two-dimensional nanochannels with tunable, exchangeable interlayer cations. Despite larger interlayer spacings, membranes hosting strongly hydrated ions exhibit markedly slower water transport, whereas weakly hydrated ions enable ultrafast permeation. Infrared spectroscopy reveals ion-dependent restructuring of the hydrogen-bond network, with weakly hydrated cations disrupting hydrogen-bond connectivity and shifting the O-H stretching band toward higher frequencies. Molecular dynamics simulations further show that such disruption enhances the population and mobility of dynamically exchanging hydration water, which dominates two-dimensional water diffusion under confinement. These findings establish ionic effects as an essential component of confined water transport and provide a molecular framework for understanding anomalous water flow across natural and engineered nanochannels.Subject terms: Fluid dynamics, Two-dimensional materials, Two-dimensional materials

Introduction

Water transport under nanoconfinement exhibits a range of anomalous behaviors that challenge classical hydrodynamic descriptions developed for bulk water1–4. In both biological and artificial systems, confined water has been reported to flow at unexpectedly high rates, motivating extensive experimental and theoretical efforts to the understand the underlying mechanisms5–7. At the molecular level, water transport is intimately related to the structure and dynamics of the hydrogen-bond (H-bond) network, which governs key properties such as viscosity, diffusivity and dielectric response8–11. When confined to nanoscale dimensions, water molecules experience restricted coordination environments and perturbed H-bond connectivity, accompanied by pronounced interfacial ordering such as layering or orientational anisotropy near confining surfaces11,12. These structural changes can substantially modify water dynamics relative to the bulk.

Under realistic conditions, confined water rarely exists as a pure phase but instead contains dissolved ions. Even at low concentrations, ions can strongly perturb local water structure owing to the intense electric fields associated with their hydration shells13. In nanometer-scale confinements, where only a limited number of water molecules and ions can be accommodated, such perturbations can extend beyond the first hydration shell and influence the surrounding H-bond network. Additional complexity arises when confining walls selectively adsorb ions, further modifying local hydration environments13–15. Despite this, most studies of confined water transport continue to focus on ion-free systems, and how ion-water interactions modulate hydrogen-bond networks and influence water transport at the nanoscale remains incompletely understood.

Existing experimental and computational studies have reported seemingly conflicting effects of ions on the dynamics of confined water. In some cases, ions have been suggested to hinder transport by increasing effective viscosity through electrostatic friction or hydration-induced trapping16,17, whereas in others they appear to enhance water mobility by disrupting hydrogen-bond connectivity18,19. These observations point to a strong dependence on ion identity and hydration characteristics, yet a unified molecular-level picture linking ion-specific hydration, hydrogen-bond restructuring, and water transport under confinement is still lacking.

In this work, we study this challenge using vermiculite-based two-dimensional nanochannels with exchangeable interlayer cations (Fig. 1a). Vermiculite is a layered phyllosilicate mineral that hosts a high density of cation-exchange sites on its basal surfaces20, allowing its native ions to be readily substituted by a wide range of cations if exposed to suitable electrolytes. When assembled into laminar membranes, vermiculite forms two-dimensional interlayer nanochannels whose geometry remains largely unchanged upon cation exchange, while the local ionic environment experienced by confined water can be systematically tuned. Therefore, this platform enables a controlled, ion-specific investigation of how ion-water interactions modify H-bond networks and regulate water flow at the nanoscale.

Results and discussion

Stable aqueous dispersions of lithium-, sodium-, potassium-, cesium-, and ammonium- exchanged vermiculite nanosheets (Li-V, Na-V, K-V, Cs-V and NH4-V) were prepared via a two-step ion exchange process20. These monovalent cations share an identical charge but exhibit varied hydration diameters and hydration energies, enabling controlled modulation of ion-water interactions. The resulting nanosheets have a thickness of ~1.5 nm and lateral dimensions of several micrometers, as confirmed by atomic force microscopy and electron microscopy techniques (Supplementary Fig. S1), and were assembled into laminar membranes with thicknesses of several hundred nanometers via vacuum filtration (Supplementary Fig. S2). Details of membrane fabrication can be found in Supplementary Information. As shown in Fig. 1b, inductively coupled plasma mass spectroscopy confirms comparable cation exchange capacity across all membranes. The extent values are also close to the theoretical exchange capacity of vermiculite21 (Fig. 1b), indicating nearly complete ion exchange. X-ray photoelectron spectrum measurements also reveal cation-dependent shifts of the O 1 s spectra, supporting specific interactions between the exchanged cations and oxygen species in the vermiculite framework (Supplementary Fig. S3). Cation-dependent water contact angles (Fig. 1c) further reflect intrinsic surface properties governed by the exchanged ions20, further evidence of successful cation exchange.

Next, we examined water intercalation and the resulting interlayer spacing. The water content was quantified gravimetrically by comparing membrane weights in fully hydrated and dry states. The absorbed water amounts to ~30% of the dry membrane weight (Supplementary Table S1), corresponding to an effective cation concentration of approximately 4 mol L−1. X-ray diffraction measurements reveal a pronounced expansion of the interlayer spacing upon hydration (Supplementary Fig. S4), confirming water intercalation. Notably, the hydrated interlayer spacing d scales with the hydration diameter of the interlayer cations (Fig. 1d, e), indicating that hydrated cations act as spacers separating adjacent nanosheets.

Water transport was investigated through both evaporation and pressure-driven permeation experiments. In evaporation experiments, membranes were used to seal a container filled with deionized water, and the weight loss W was monitored over time. As shown in Fig. 2a, W increases linearly with time t, allowing the evaporation rate to be extracted as Ge = W/t. For K-V membranes, the observed Ge reaches more than 90% of the open-aperture evaporation rate. The latter was measured using an identical setup without membrane coverage. Moreover, Ge remains independent of membrane thickness over the range of 50–400 nm (Supplementary Fig. S5), indicating negligible transport resistance. While fast evaporation has previously been reported for weakly interacting or hydrophobic membranes5,18, the observation here is notable because vermiculite membranes are strongly hydrophilic.

The slight deviation of Cs-V from the monotonic hydration-energy trend may arise from specific Cs+ adsorption and reduced lamellar ordering22–24, as indicated by the weakened and broadened (001) XRD reflection (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Water intercalation is essential for evaporation to occur. In 2D laminar membranes, interlayer channels selectively open to water under humid conditions due to swelling18. Consistent with this behavior, gas permeation through dry vermiculite membranes is suppressed by 3-4 orders of magnitude compared to water transport, regardless of gas kinetic diameter or solubility (Supplementary Fig. S7, including the transport data of H2, He and CO2). Similarly, organic solvents such as ethanol and methanol exhibit transport rates one to two orders of magnitude lower than water (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. S5). These results indicate that water transport occurs predominantly through hydrated interlayer channels and is closely linked to the hydration properties of the interlayer cations.

Nevertheless, the thickness independence of evaporation rates suggests that evaporation is limited by water escape from the wetted membrane surface rather than bulk diffusion. To determine the intrinsic permeability, we performed pressure-driven permeation experiments, in which the membranes were supported by a rigid grid that allowed a water pressure difference of several bars across the membrane without damaging it. The permeated water volume Q scaled linearly with both the applied pressure P and time t (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. S8), yielding a permeation rate Gp = Q/P·t of up to 2000 L m-2 h-1 bar-1 for K-V membranes. This value is consistent with previous reports on vermiculite membranes20 and exceeds those of other 2D laminar and polymeric membranes by orders of magnitude25–34. In contrast to evaporation, Gp scales inversely with membrane thickness (Supplementary Fig. S8), indicating that bulk transport through the membrane is rate-limiting under pressure-driven conditions. Control measurements show no noticeable change in interlayer spacing and only minor exchanged-cation leaching during water permeation, confirming the aqueous stability of the vermiculite membranes under the measurement conditions (Supplementary Fig. S9).

Interestingly, water permeation does not increase with channel size. Instead, membranes with larger interlayer spacing exhibit slower water transport (Fig. 2c). For example, K-V membranes show approximately two-fold higher permeation rates than Li-V membranes, despite the latter’s considerably larger interlayer spacing. This counterintuitive behavior demonstrates that water transport is not governed solely by geometric confinement. The surface potential is also found nearly identical among the different cation-exchanged membranes, indicating that the cation-dependent water permeation rate is unlikely to originate primarily from different effective surface charges (Supplementary Fig. S10). On the other hand, permeation rates decrease systematically with increasing cation hydration energy, a trend also observed in evaporation experiments (Fig. 2c). These results indicate that cation-water interactions play a dominant role in regulating transport. While strong hydration could, in principle, immobilize water molecules, the coexistence of strong confinement and ultrafast permeation suggests a more complex mechanism.

To further reveal the cation-water interactions, we employed infrared (IR) spectroscopy to study the H-bond networks within the membranes. The normal-incidence IR geometry primarily probes the in-plane O-H response of confined water; comparison with bulk electrolyte solutions is provided in Supplementary Fig. S11 and Fig. S12 to separate the baseline confinement shift from ion-dependent spectral changes. Figure 3a shows the IR spectra of wet vermiculite membranes, after subtraction of the corresponding spectra of dry membranes. The dry-membrane spectra were obtained by storing the prepared membranes in a vacuum chamber during measurements, thereby mainly measuring contributions from immobile water molecules adsorbed within the membranes. In that way, the remaining signal predominately reflects the vibrational response of mobile, confined water. The OH stretching band of confined water exhibits a multipeak-like structure between ~3000 and 3800 cm-1, in stark contrast to that of bulk water (Supplementary Fig. S13), indicating a redistribution of H-bond strength under confinement. To quantify these changes, we analyzed the averaged wave number of the entire band, <v~>=∫v~Iv~dv~/∫Iv~dv~, which provides a measure of the averaged H-bond strength. I is the intensity. As shown in Fig. 3b, <ṽ> shifts gradually to higher wave numbers from Li-V to K-V membranes. It has been established that a higher OH stretching wavenumber corresponds to weakened H-bonding11. From this perspective, water confined in K-V membranes experiences weaker H-bonding than in Li-V membranes, implying a lower energetic cost for water molecules to escape from the H-bond network. This trend is also consistent with the observed decrease in water permeation as cation hydration energy increases, where the latter reflects the strength of cation-water interactions.

As a useful phenomenological analysis, the OH stretching band was decomposed into three constrained Voigt components, which capture the redistribution of spectral weight across different H-bonding regimes35. The high-wavenumber component can be associated with weakly H-bonded water molecules that interact only weakly with their surrounding H-bond network36. This component accounts for less than 20% of the total population and exhibits little variation in center frequency with cation type, indicating that weakly H-bonded water does not dominate the permeating water population. By contrast, the low-wavenumber and intermediate components correspond to strongly and intermediate H-bonded water, respectively, together accounting for more than 80% of the confined water population. As shown in Fig. 3b, both components exhibit systematic downshifts in wavenumber from K⁺ to Li⁺, indicating a progressive strengthening of H-bonding. A Li-V humidity-control experiment further shows that changing the interlayer spacing, and therefore the relative contribution of interfacial water, causes only a modest shift in the averaged O-H stretching band and leaves the strongly and intermediate H-bonded components nearly unchanged (Supplementary Fig. S14). Thus, the pronounced component shifts across the ion-exchanged series cannot be explained by interlayer-spacing effects alone37. Notably, for all membranes, the population ratio of intermediate to strongly H-bonded water exceeds 2 (Fig. 3c), considerably higher than that observed in bulk water (≈1; Supplementary Fig. S13), which suggests that confinement favors less developed H-bond network than in the bulk. This cation-dependent modulation of the H-bond network is consistent with the recent work on Hofmeister-series ions in bulk water, which shows that K+ ions tend to disrupt the H-bond network and reduce water viscosity, whereas Li+ ions enhance H-bond connectivity and increases viscosity38. In this context, our IR results provide direct spectroscopic evidence that a similar Hofmeister trend persists under nanoconfinement. Although the same qualitative ion-specific O-H stretching trend was also observed in bulk electrolyte controls, confinement produces a larger blue shift, a higher intermediate/strongly H-bonded water ratio, and a much stronger cation-dependent transport contrast (Supplementary Fig. S6 and Fig. S11). The preference for intermediate H-bonded configurations, particularly in the presence of structure-breaking cations such as K+ ions, implies weakened network connectivity, which likely facilitates rapid water transport through the membranes.

Machine learning-accelerated molecular dynamics simulations were performed to provide a molecular-level interpretation of the experimentally observed cation-dependent modulation of hydrogen bond networks and water permeation. The computational details can be found in Methods section “Molecular dynamics simulation” and Supplementary Fig. S15–17 for model validation. The interlayer spacing, cation number and water content in the simulation models were chosen based on experimental measurements (Supplementary Table S2). At equilibrium, K+ ions locate at ~2 Å above the vermiculite surface, whereas Li+ ions exhibit a broader vertical distribution, as shown by simulation snapshots (Fig. 4a) and ion density profiles along the vertical z direction (Supplementary Fig. S18). Under confinement, water form two hydration layers around both Li+ and K+ cations (Fig. 4b). Analysis of the mean square displacement reveals faster water diffusion in K-V than in Li-V. The resulting diffusion constant in K-V ( ~ 0.60 × 10-9 m2 s-1) is about twice that in Li-V ( ~ 0.28 × 10-9 m2 s-1), in qualitative agreement with experimental trends. By contrast, water motion along the z direction is strongly suppressed due to the 2D confinement (Supplementary Fig. S20). Other models with optimized Li-V channel sizes are also performed, while the results show qualitatively the same cation-dependent diffusion trend (Supplementary Fig. S21).

To understand the origin of this difference, we performed hydration-shell-resolved MSD analysis (Fig. 4c). Water molecules in the first hydration shell of interlayer cations are relatively immobilized and contribute only weakly to the overall in-plane diffusion, with Li⁺-coordinated water being substantially more immobilized than K⁺-coordinated water due to the higher hydration energy of Li⁺ ions. In contrast, nearly half of the mobile water population undergoes frequent exchange between hydration shells, and this transition water dominates planar diffusion. Because the interlayer cations are preferentially located near the charged vermiculite surfaces, the hydration-shell-resolved mobility is naturally coupled to the distance from the confining walls. We therefore performed a layer-resolved MSD analysis to clarify how water mobility varies across the channel (Supplementary Fig. S22). This analysis shows that interfacial water is strongly slowed, whereas water farther from the wall exhibits higher in-plane mobility. Thus, the hydration-shell-resolved and layer-resolved analyses provide complementary views of the same confined-water dynamics: Li⁺ stabilizes slowly diffusing hydration/interfacial water near the surface, whereas K⁺ promotes a larger dynamically exchanging water population with higher lateral mobility.

We emphasize that this hydration-shell exchange mechanism is specific to confinement. As demonstrated in our simulations of bulk electrolytes (Supplementary Fig. S23), transition water also diffuses faster than first- and second-shell water, but the overall diffusion is dominated by the large population of ion-unassociated water located outside the hydration shells. Under two-dimensional confinement, this ion-unassociated population is strongly reduced because most water molecules are close to either interlayer ions or charged vermiculite surfaces. For example, only ~4.6% of water in K-V falls outside the defined hydration-shell categories. As a result, confined-water diffusion becomes much more sensitive to hydration-shell exchange and ion/interface-associated water dynamics. This explains why the dynamically exchanging transition-water population plays a central role in fast in-plane diffusion under confinement.

Hydrogen-bond statistics further reveal a cation-dependent restructuring of the network (Fig. 4d). Around Li⁺ ions, hydrogen bonds display similar O-O distances and angular distributions across all water populations (Fig. 4d for the H-bonds between the first hydration shell water and the outer transition water, and Supplementary Fig. S24 for the H-bond statistics of all water), indicating a relatively uniform and well-connected network. In contrast, water molecules in the first hydration shell of K⁺ ions exhibit longer O-O distances and smaller O-H···O angles, characteristic of weakened hydrogen bonds (compared to that of Li+ as well as to other types of water in K-V, Supplementary Fig. S24). These disrupted local environments shorten hydrogen-bond lifetimes (Fig. 4e) and promote frequent shell-to-shell exchange, leading to enhanced water mobility. These findings are consistent with Hofmeister-type ion-specific effects38, in which K+ ions tend to weaken H-bond connectivity, whereas Li+ ions stabilize hydration structures.

To conclude, we reveal the underlying mechanism by which confined ions regulate water transport under two-dimensional confinement. Rather than simply slowing water through steric crowding or enhanced viscosity, adsorbed cations actively disrupt the collective H-bond network that normally constrains molecular rearrangements in confined water. This ion-induced disconnection of water reduces hydrogen-bond lifetimes and facilitates rapid exchange between the first and second hydration shells, providing a molecular pathway for fast permeation that is inaccessible in bulk water. Our results thus suggest the importance of ionic effects in explaining fast water permeation observed under realistic experimental conditions where ions are inevitably present in confined water channels. Moreover, the distinct abilities of monovalent interlayer cations to destabilize confined H-bond networks provide a molecular basis for ion-specific (Hofmeister-like) modulation of water transport under confinement.

Methods

Preparation and characterization of vermiculite laminar membranes

Monolayer vermiculite nanosheets were prepared following the reported two-step ion exchange method20,39,40. First, 1 g of commercial thermally expanded vermiculite was mixed with 500 mL saturated sodium NaCl solution. The mixture was then heated under reflux at 120 °C for 24 h with continuous stirring. After removing the residual NaCl solutions via thoroughly washing with deionized water, the collected vermiculite solid was further mixed with 250 mL of 2 mol L-1 LiCl solution, where the larger hydrated diameter of Li⁺ ion promotes the exchange of the interlayer Na⁺ and expands the interlayer spacing, facilitating the subsequent exfoliation of monolayer nanosheets. The mixture was again heated and washed following the above procedures. Finally, the resulting solids were dispersed in deionized water and subjected to ultrasonication for 30 min. The dispersion was then centrifuged at 3000 r min-1 to remove any bulk vermiculite particles. As shown in Fig. S1, the resulting nanosheets have lateral sizes of about 10 μm, and a thickness of about 1.5 nm, which is the typical thickness of monolayer vermiculite crystals41.

To prepare the vermiculite membranes exchanged with desired cations, a diluted aqueous suspension containing 0.1 mg of monolayer vermiculite nanosheets was mixed with 80 mL of 0.1 mol L-1 chloride salt solution MCl, where M = K, Na, Li, Cs, NH₄, etc. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 1 h until the nanosheets were uniformly dispersed. In this process, the cation adsorption sites on vermiculite surfaces were replaced by M⁺. The M-V dispersion was then vacuum-filtered through porous polyethersulfone substrates. The resulting membrane was immersed in deionized water for 10 min to remove residual salts, followed by drying in a vacuum at 50 °C for 12 h to eliminate excess moisture. As shown in Fig. S2, the resulting membranes have a layered structure and a typical thickness of a few hundred nanometers. Electron microscopy further reveals a smooth and continuous membrane surface without macroscopic defects. The exchanged cations were found uniformly distributed in the membranes.

Measurements

To estimate the amount of adsorbed water in the wet vermiculite, we prepared M-V (M = Li, Na and K) using the above methods. For dry mass (mdry) measurements, the vermiculites were vacuum-dried at 90 °C for 24 h and were weighed in a controlled environment with relative humidity (RH) below 1%. While for wet mass (mwet) measurements, the samples were then placed in 100% RH environment for 24 h prior to weighting. Results are summarized in Table S1.

Water evaporation measurements were performed using the gravimetric loss method. Specifically, the membrane was placed to seal a container filled with water (1 mL). The container was then placed in an ultramicrobalance located within a controlled temperature (25 °C) and humidity (RH < 1%) environment, and its mass change was recorded over time (Fig. S5). For methanol and ethanol evaporation, the water was replaced by the desired liquids.

To measure gas permeation through vermiculite membranes, the prepared M-V membranes were used to separate two custom-made vacuum chambers. One chamber was filled with gases under permeation (He, H2, CO2) with controlled pressure (as the driving force), while the other chamber was kept under vacuum and was connected to a mass spectrometer to evaluate the permeated gas. As demonstrated in Fig. S7, gas permeation rate is orders of magnitude smaller than the evaporation of water. This can be attributed to the closure of interlayer channels in a water-deficient environment. Note that the impermeability of gases also indicates our membranes are free of straight-through defects. Therefore, the path of water transport can be attributed to the swollen interlayer channels at high humidity.

Water permeation was measured by applying a pressure gradient across the membranes to obtain the permeation rate (Fig. S8). To that end, the sample was placed into a high-pressure permeation cell (HP4750 Cell Manual), where the membrane was sandwiched between two water reservoirs. Deionized water was added to the filling reservoir, the pressure of which can be tuned by the pressurized nitrogen. The transported water was then collected at the permeation reservoir.

Molecular dynamic simulations

To accelerate molecular dynamics simulations, we performed machine learning-accelerated molecular dynamics (MLMD) methods42,43. Specifically, we constructed various vermiculite channel models with different parameters listed in Table S2. To maintain a charge-neutral condition, the number of cations matches the number of silicon atoms substituted by aluminum in the inner silicate layer. Although computational limitations prevented modeling open channels with net water flow, our results confirm that the present models adequately capture ion-specific effects on water transport.

All DFT calculations were conducted using the mixed Gaussian and plane-wave basis set within the CP2K/QUICKSTEP package44. The exchange-correlation energies were treated with the revised Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (revPBE) functional45,46, while dispersion corrections were incorporated in all calculations using the Grimme D3 method47 with Becke-Johnson (BJ) damping48–51. The Gaussian basis set was configured as double-ζ with one set of polarization functions (DZVP-MOLOPT-SR-GTH)52,53, and the wavefunction energy cutoff was set to 800 Ry. Core electrons were represented by Goedecker-Teter-Hutter (GTH) pseudopotentials54. The orbital transformation (OT) algorithm was employed for SCF wavefunction optimization55 with the SCF convergence threshold set to 1×10-6 a.u.

We employed the Deep Potential (DP) model to predict potential energies and forces56–58. The training dataset for the DP model was generated using the ai2-kit workflow59, which operates similarly to the DP-GEN framework60. Our training methodology followed an approach similar to that described in Ref61. Below, we briefly describe the training procedure. We first randomly selected several structures from ab initio molecular dynamics simulations to train four initial models. Subsequently, three consecutive steps—exploration, labeling, and training—were iterated until the machine learning potential achieved sufficient accuracy. The exploration phase in ai2-kit was performed under NVT ensemble conditions at 300, 330, and 360 K. The upper and lower trust bounds for model deviation were set to 0.60 eV/Å and 0.20 eV/Å, respectively. Training started with small-cell vermiculite models, proceeding to supercells after initial convergence. The final dataset contained 3958 structures. Additionally, bulk LiCl and KCl aqueous solutions were included in the active learning. It should be noted that the ML models used to simulate the bulk and interface systems were trained separately. As the current ML model does not account for long-range interactions, training it on both bulk and interface datasets can yield unreasonable simulation structures. Training them separately avoids such problems and obtains reasonable results, see the next section for validations. The final dataset of bulk solutions contained 934 structures.

After obtaining the DP model, we conducted MLMD simulations to sample molecular configurations. All MLMD simulations were carried out in the NVT ensemble using the LAMMPS package62. Simulations were performed employing a Nosé-Hoover chain thermostat with three chains63,64. The temperature damping parameter was set to 1 ps, and the equations of motion were integrated using a 0.5 fs time step. To better sample the configurations, we initially performed a 1 ns simulation at 360 K, followed by a 5 ns simulation at 300 K, with the final 4 ns of the trajectory used for analysis. The trajectory frame interval used for analysis was 0.05 ps.

To evaluate the accuracy of the ML model, we randomly selected 20 structures from the final simulation trajectory and assessed the model’s performance in predicting energies and forces, as shown in Fig. S15. The root mean square error (RMSE) for force predictions is only slightly above 0.1 eV/Å, which generally meets the accuracy requirements for ML model predictions. However, a systematic deviation is observed in energy predictions, which may be attributed to the absence of structures with identical composition in the training dataset and the neglect of long-range interactions. Nevertheless, the linear correlation suggests that no significant impact on the MD simulations. Moreover, ref. 65. suggests that reasonable results can be achieved without long-range interactions for similar surface-adsorbed ion systems. Further validation comes from the reasonable results in the structural analysis in Supplementary section “Molecular dynamics simulation”.

The hydrogen bond was identified using the geometric criterion from ref. 66. In this criterion, a hydrogen bond is formed when the intermolecular distance R of two oxygen atoms is smaller than 3.5 Å and the OH⋅ ⋅ ⋅O angle θ is larger than 140°. A weaker bond corresponds to a longer distance and a smaller angle. Based on this criterion, we calculated the joint distributions of hydrogen bond angle and length for water (as donor).
