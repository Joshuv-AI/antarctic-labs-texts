Efficacy of a novel bacteriophage in controlling Escherichia coli associated with swine farm environments and its potential for biofilm disruption
Authors: Phitchayapak Wintachai, Renuka Thonguppatham, Duncan R. Smith, Supayang Piyawan Voravuthikunchai, Sutthirat Sitthisak, Ratchadaporn Boripun, Komwit Surachat, Martha R. J. Clokie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) Escherichia coli infections in swine farms, reported globally, including in Thailand, causes symptoms ranging from mild to severe and leads to economic losses. Numerous studies have demonstrated a correlation between antibiotic use and the development of antibiotic resistance, highlighting the risk associated with antibiotic misuse and overuse. However, no practical alternative for E. coli infection in swine and their environments has been established. In this study, an Escherichia phage specific to MDR E. coli, named phage vB_EcoM_PPW9 (phage vECPPW9), was isolated and characterized. The antibacterial and antibiofilm activities of the phage were evaluated. This phage formed small plaques with halos, and transmission electron microscopy revealed that it possessed an icosahedral head and a contractile tail. Phage vECPPW9 lysed 40% of the tested E. coli isolates and exhibited high adsorption efficacy, a short latent period, large burst size, and moderate stability across various temperatures and pH levels. Even at low multiplicity of infection, the phage demonstrated strong lytic activity, confirmed by observations of bacterial morphology under a scanning electron microscope. Genomic analysis revealed that phage vECPPW9 had a linear double-stranded DNA genome of 152,457 base pairs, comprising 284 predicted coding sequences and 13 tRNA genes, and lacked genes associated with virulence or antibiotic resistance. Phylogenetic analysis classified phage vECPPW9 as a novel member of the Phapecoctavirus genus within the Stephanstirmvirinae subfamily. Additionally, phage vECPPW9 exhibited strong antibiofilm activity by inhibiting biofilm formation and reducing established biofilms, as demonstrated by biofilm biomass measurements and bacterial cell viability counting. This antibiofilm effect was also observed on both rubber tube and stainless steel surfaces. Given the urgent need for antibiotic stewardship and alternative antimicrobial strategies, phage vECPPW9 represents a promising candidate for controlling MDR E. coli infections in swine farming.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42644-3.

Introduction

The emergence of bacterial resistance to antibiotics has become a major global concern, affecting humans, animals, and the environment. Among livestock, swine play a crucial economic role and contribute significantly to the national income of Thailand1. Escherichia coli, a member of the Enterobacteriaceae family, can cause infection in swine, particularly in neonatal and weaned pigs, leading to a condition known as colibacillosis2. This disease is characterized by symptoms such as diarrhea, rapid dehydration, acidosis, and death. However, the severity of the symptoms varies depending on the virulence factors present, the age of the swine, and their immunity status3. One of the primary causes of infections is bacterial contamination in farm environments, including wastewater and soil. When such contaminations and infections occur, biocidal agents and antibiotics are commonly used for treatment. However, the use of biocidal agents can promote antibiotic resistance in Gram-negative bacteria4. Furthermore, the overuse and misuse of antibiotics have contributed to the emergence of antibiotic-resistant E. coli strains in these environments. Notably, zoonotic transmission of pathogenic E. coli strains, including Shiga toxin-producing E. coli and extraintestinal pathogenic E. coli, has been reported3,5. These bacteria can be transmitted to humans through direct contact with infected animals, the consumption of undercooked pork products, or environmental routes, such as contaminated farm water runoff6. Given the rising threat of antibiotic-resistant E. coli, alternative treatment strategies should be prioritized to mitigate its impact.

Various studies have focused on discovering novel antimicrobial agents, including new antibiotics7, nanoparticles8, peptides, and bacteriophages9,10. Bacteriophages (phages), which are viruses that infect bacteria, replicate within bacterial cells and ultimately cause cell lysis. As a result, phages have emerged as a promising alternative strategy for controlling bacterial infections11. Research on phages targeting E. coli associated with infections in swine and swine farm environments has shown promising results. For instance, phages BF9, BF15, and BF17 exhibited high lytic activity against extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-and AmpC beta-lactamase-producing E. coli isolated from swine manure12. Moreover, 52 phages were isolated from swine, chicken, and other animals. Among these, phage vB_EcoStr-FJ63A was selected for further study and demonstrated the ability to reduce colistin-resistant E. coli13. In Thailand, several E. coli-specific phages, including vB_Eco-RPNE4i3, vB_Eco-RPNE6i4, vB_Eco-RPNE7i1, and vB_Eco-RPNE8i3, have been isolated and evaluated for their effectiveness in controlling swine enteric colibacillosis pathogens in vitro14. Interestingly, a phage cocktail consisting of vB_EcoM-RPN170, vB_EcoM-RPN171, vB_EcoM-RPN187, vB_EcoM-RPN226, vB_EcoM-RPN242, and vB_EcoP-RPN243 was found to restore gut bacterial composition and improve pig growth performance through phage treatment15. Recently, Escherichia phage, vB_EcoM-RPN242, was isolated and genetically characterized16. Despite the growing interest in phage therapy in Thailand, there are currently no commercially available phage-based treatments for E. coli infections. Based on this background, the present study aimed to isolate and characterize an Escherichia phage. Its antibacterial and antibiofilm activities were evaluated to assess its potential as an alternative treatment strategy.

Results

Phage isolation, morphology, and host range

A phage specific to E. coli, which was isolated from an environmental water sample, formed small plaques with halos measuring 0.1–0.2 mm in diameter on an E. coli bacterial lawn (Fig. 1a). The morphology of the phage under transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed a head with a diameter of 57.62–62.6 nm and a short contractile tail (94.44 nm) (Fig. 1b).

To assess the efficacy of phage vECPPW9 in bacterial lysis, the host range of phage vECPPW9 was determined using a spot test assay against MDR E. coli isolated from swine farms. Phage vECPPW9 successfully lysed 8 out of 20 MDR E. coli isolates. The bacterial isolates susceptible to lysis by phage vECPPW9 were selected for the efficacy of the plating assay (EOP). Phage vECPPW9 demonstrated high activity against 6 isolates and moderate activity against 2 isolates of MDR E. coli (Supplementary Table S1).

Adsorption of phage and phage life cycle

The adsorption rate of Escherichia phage vECPPW9 to bacterial cells increased over time at 37 °C. More than 90% of the phage particles adsorbed to the bacterial cell within 10 min (Fig. 2a). The adsorption rate constant (k) was calculated to be 2.6 × 10−10 mL/min. To investigate the phage life cycle, a one-step growth curve was performed. The results showed that the phage had a latent period of 20 min, a rise period from 20 to 100 min (80 min in duration), and a burst size of 317 plaque-forming units (PFU) per infected cell.

Evaluation of bacterial lytic activity of phage

Bacterial growth in broth culture during treatment with phage vECPPW9 was assessed by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD600). The results showed that E. coli growth in the control group (without phage treatment) increased continuously over a 24-h period (Fig. 2c). In contrast, bacterial growth was completely inhibited within 1 h after treatment with the phage at multiplicities of infection (MOIs) of 0.01, 0.1, 1, and 10. Regrowth of bacteria treated with phage vECPPW9 at MOIs of 0.01, 0.1, and 1 was observed at 8 h post-incubation, whereas those treated at an MOI of 10 showed regrowth at 9 h post-incubation. After 24 h, the bacterial density in all phage-treated cultures (MOIs 0.01 to 10) remained lower than that of the control. Moreover, bacterial cell viability assessed at 24 h revealed a significant reduction of approximately 1.91 to 3.10 log units in phage-treated cultures across all MOIs (Fig. 2d).

Morphology of bacterial cells after phage treatment

The morphology of E. coli cells following phage vECPPW9 treatment was examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) and compared to untreated control cells. The untreated E. coli cells exhibited a smooth cell membrane and a normal rod shape (Fig. 3a). In contrast, phage-treated cells showed clear signs of lysis, with numerous debris particles resulting from bacterial destruction observed (Fig. 3b).

Stability of phage under various environmental conditions

The stability of phage vECPPW9 was evaluated under different temperature and pH conditions to determine the optimal storage parameters for the phage. Regarding temperature stability, phage viability remained stable within the temperature range of 4 °C to 37 °C (Fig. 4a). However, a significant reduction in viability was observed at −20 °C, 50 °C, and 60 °C, and no phage viability was detected at 70 °C. In terms of pH, phage viability was maintained within a pH range of 4 to 11 (Fig. 4b). A significant decline in viability was detected at pH 3 and 12, while no viable phage was observed at pH 1, 2, 13, and 14.

Whole genome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

The genome of phage vECPPW9 was sequenced and subsequently annotated. It was a linear DNA genome with a total length of 152,457 base pairs (bp) and a GC content of 39%. A genome map and annotation were generated using PhageScope web server (Fig. 5; Supplementary Table S2). Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted, resulting in 284 coding sequences (CDSs). The predicted protein sequences were further analyzed by BLASTp against the NCBI non-redundant database to identify putative functions. Among these, 60 CDSs were assigned to 4 major functional categories: (i) Phage morphology, including membrane proteins, tail spike proteins, baseplate protein, baseplate wedge subunit, tail sheaths, tail fiber proteins, baseplate spike, baseplate hub, virion structural proteins, tail length tape measure protein, tail assembly chaperone, minor head protein, tail completion or Neck1 protein, and putative major head protein; (ii) DNA metabolism and replication, including HNH endonucleases, NrdA-like aerobic NDP reductase large subunit, ribonucleotide reductase of class Ia (beta subunit), dTDP-4-dehydrorhamnose 3,5-epimerase, nucleotide-sugar epimerase, putative glutaredoxin 1, PhoH-like phosphate starvation-inducible protein, NinI-like serine-threonine phosphatases, DNA methyltransferase, Sir2 (NAD-dependent deacetylase), DNA ligases and ATP-dependent DNA ligase, endonuclease, ribonucleoside-triphosphate reductases, phosphatase, DNA primase/helicase, putative DNA N6-adenine methyltransferase, DNA polymerase I, phosphoesterase, and putative exonucleases; (iii) DNA packaging, including the terminase large subunit, ATPase, and portal protein; (iv) host cell lysis, including endolysin, hydrolase, and Rz-like spanin. Additional genes encoding tellurite resistance, histidyl tRNA synthetases, and glycosyltransferases were also identified. The remaining 224 CDSs were classified as hypothetical phage proteins. Additionally, 13 tRNA were identified, but no CRISPR/Cas elements were detected. No antibiotic resistance genes were identified based on analysis against the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database. The PhageScope results indicated that the host of phage vECPPW9 belongs to the genus Escherichia within the family Enterobacteriaceae. The phage was classified as a member of Caudoviricetes and was determined to be a virulent phage.

Genomic comparison and phylogenetic analysis of phage

A genomic comparison of phage vECPPW9 with other phages in the database was conducted using the ViPTree web server to investigate its evolutionary relationships. The ViPTree analysis revealed that phage vECPPW9 was associated with the Pseudomonadota phylum as its host group (Fig. 6a). Additionally, the phylogenetic tree was constructed by the VICTOR web service. Phage vECPPW9 exhibited close similarity to Escherichia phage ECO340P1 (OP172791.1), a member of the Phapecoctavirus genus and the Stephanstirmvirinae subfamily (Fig. 6b). The sequence similarity between these two genomes was further assessed using EMBOSS Stretcher, which determined an overall similarity of 54.8%. The PhageClouds database was utilized to assess the novelty of the phage based on phage genomic sequences (Fig. 6c). The results suggest that phage vECPPW9 represents a novel member of the Phapecoctavirus genus, Stephanstirmvirinae subfamily, Caudoviricetes order, Uroviricota phylum, Heunggongvirae kingdom, and Duplodnaviria realm.

Efficacy of phage vECPPW9 in preventing biofilm formation

The ability of phage vECPPW9 to prevent biofilm formation was evaluated by measuring biofilm biomass and assessing bacterial viability within biofilms, expressed as colony-forming units (CFUs). At day 1 post treatment, biofilm biomass was reduced by approximately 15.82 to 77% in wells treated with phage vECPPW9 at concentrations ranging from 101 to 108 PFU/well (Fig. 7a). Bacterial viability was reduced by approximately 1.07 to 2.29 log units (Fig. 7b). Furthermore, the preventive effect of phage vECPPW9 on biofilm formation was assessed at day 3 post-treatment. Phage treatment at 101 to 108 PFU/well resulted in a biomass reduction of approximately 10.77% to 75.57% (Fig. 7c) and a decrease in bacterial cell viability by approximately 0.89 to 1.93 log units (Fig. 7d).

Efficacy of phage vECPPW9 in eradicating preformed biofilms

Biofilms of E. coli were cultivated in wells. On day 1 and day 3 post-incubation, the biofilms were treated with phage vECPPW9 at concentrations ranging from 101 to 108 PFU/well, followed by a 24 h incubation. Biofilm biomass and bacterial cell viability were subsequently assessed. For 1-day-old biofilms, treatment with phage vECPPW9 resulted in a reduction of biofilm biomass by approximately 13.05% to 76.08% (Fig. 8a) and a decrease in bacterial cell viability by 1.03 to 2.25 log units (Fig. 8b). Similarly, for 3-day-old biofilms, biofilm biomass was reduced by approximately 9.29% to 69.44% (Fig. 8c), while bacterial cell viability decreased by 0.74 to 1.88 log units following phage vECPPW9 treatment (Fig. 8d).

The morphologies of biofilms, both untreated and treated with phage vECPPW9, were examined using SEM. In the untreated control biofilms, numerous intact bacterial cells were observed forming dense aggregates (Fig. 9a, b). Intercellular connections and extracellular matrix-like material were observed, consistent with typical biofilm architecture. The bacterial cells exhibited a rod-shaped morphology with smooth and intact cell membranes. In contrast, biofilms treated with phage vECPPW9 displayed a higher proportion of disrupted bacterial cells (Fig. 9c, d). Bacterial membranes appeared damaged, and numerous cell fragments and debris were observed.

Antibiofilm activity of phage vECPPW9 on swine farm-related surfaces

To simulate swine-relevant surfaces, rubber tubes and stainless steel coupons were used as models to evaluate the ability of phage vECPPW9 to reduce biofilm formation and remove preformed biofilms at days 1, 2, and 3 post-incubation. In the biofilm prevention assay, biofilm biomass in the control groups (without phage treatment) increased significantly with prolonged incubation time on both rubber tube and stainless steel surfaces (Fig. 10a and b). In contrast, treatment with phage vECPPW9 resulted in significantly lower biofilm biomass on both surfaces at all time points (days 1, 2, and 3) compared with the controls. Among the phage-treated groups, biofilm biomass on day 3 was higher than that observed on days 1 and 2.

Discussion

E. coli infections in swine and their farm environment have been reported worldwide3. The evolutionary dynamics of bacterial genomes vary across geographical regions17. Notably, recent studies have identified antibiotic-resistant E. coli isolated from large-scale swine farms in Beijing, China, and other countries18. These isolates exhibit resistance to antibiotics commonly used in swine production, including tetracyclines, penicillins, and amphenicols. Moreover, resistance has also been detected against quinolones/fluoroquinolones and aminoglycosides, even in settings where these antibiotics are not routinely used in swine farms. In Thailand, multiple studies have likewise reported antibiotic-resistant E. coli, with resistance profiles extending to beta-lactams, tetracyclines, polymyxins, phenicols, and sulfonamides/dihydrofolate reductase inhibitors19,20. Alarmingly, resistance to colistin, a last-resort antibiotic for treating MDR bacterial infections, has also been documented, further complicating treatment options9,21. The increasing prevalence of antibiotic-resistant E. coli in swine farms poses a significant challenge, contributing to the rise of MDR infections with limited therapeutic alternatives22.

Phages, which are abundant in the environment, have emerged as a promising alternative strategy for controlling E. coli infections due to their bactericidal activity, high specificity, and ability to target biofilms. Unlike broad-spectrum antibiotics, phages selectively eliminate target bacteria without disrupting the surrounding microbiota, thereby minimizing the risk of dysbiosis23. In this study, we isolated and characterized the lytic phage vECPPW9, which targets E. coli. Its antibacterial and antibiofilm activities were evaluated to assess its potential as an alternative strategy for controlling MDR E. coli infections in swine-associated environments.

Phage vECPPW9, a novel member of the Phapecoctavirus genus within the Stephanstirmvirinae subfamily, was successfully isolated. The phage formed small plaques with halos on the bacterial lawn, suggesting the potential presence of a depolymerase enzyme that may facilitate the degradation of capsular polysaccharides, exopolysaccharides, and lipopolysaccharides, including biofilm structures24,25. The host range and effectiveness of phage vECPPW9 were evaluated using the EOP assay, and the phage exhibited lytic activity against E. coli isolates. Phage infection dynamics, from adsorption to the release of newly formed phages, are crucial for understanding the efficacy and speed of bacterial infection, replication, and lysis. A high adsorption rate, which is critical for phage effectiveness, indicates that phage vECPPW9 rapidly attaches to bacterial cells, enabling efficient infection and replication. The rapid host recognition likely contributed to the observed efficient bacterial clearance. However, high adsorption efficiency may also increase the probability of multiple phage particles infecting the same bacterial cell, potentially influencing phage yield dynamics during amplification, as previously reported for phage T4 infecting E. coli ATCC 11,30326. The duration of the latent period reflects the time required for intracellular replication and assembly of progeny virions, while the burst size represents the amplification capacity per infected host cell27. A short latent period combined with a moderate burst size in phage vECPPW9 is advantageous in environments where bacterial densities fluctuate, as it enables rapid infection cycles. These growth characteristics are important considerations for optimizing phage therapy and biocontrol applications. Moreover, the results revealed that the growth parameters of phage vECPPW9 were comparable to those of other Escherichia phages exhibiting similar morphology, characterized by an icosahedral head and a contractile tail, such as phage MJ128, phage P829, phage Sa45lw30. These phages have been reported to have latent periods of 20 to 30 min and burst sizes of 74 to 300 PFU per cell. These findings suggest that phage vECPPW9 shares conserved infection dynamics characteristic of contractile-tailed lytic phages, further supporting its potential reliability for therapeutic and biocontrol applications. The optimal MOI of phages is crucial for phage therapy, as it ensures treatment efficacy while minimizing unnecessary phage usage. The lytic activity of phage vECPPW9 was assessed at various MOIs. MOIs of 0.1, 1, and 10 produced similar rapid effects, while a lower MOI results in a slower but still effective bacterial clearance. However, bacterial regrowth was observed after 8 h post-infection, indicating the emergence of phage-resistant bacterial populations. Previous studies have shown that phage resistance can arise through multiple mechanisms, including spontaneous mutations, restriction-modification systems, and adaptive immunity mediated by the CRISPR-Cas system31. Among these, spontaneous mutations are considered a major driver of phage resistance mechanisms32. Such mutations often alter the structure or expression of phage receptors, thereby preventing phage attachment. In addition, bacteria may obstruct phage access to receptors by increasing production of extracellular matrix components, which limit phage diffusion, or by generating competitive inhibitors that interfere with receptor binding33. To address this, strategies such as phage cocktails or phage-derived proteins (e.g., depolymerase, endolysin, and tail fiber proteins) may offer enhanced therapeutic efficacy34,35. Environmental factors, particularly temperature and pH, play a crucial role in phage stability36. pH variations can limit phage application in animals, as different organs exhibit distinct pH conditions, such as the gastrointestinal tract (pH 6–7), and the stomach (pH 1.15–4)37,38. To evaluate the stability of phage vECPPW9, its viability under various temperature and pH conditions was evaluated. The phage exhibited moderate stability, supporting its potential for development as a therapeutic product.

Whole genome sequencing revealed that phage vECPPW9 possesses a genome size of 152.5 kb. Its genome size and G + C content were comparable to those of the previously reported Escherichia phages, such as Escherichia phage ECO340P1 (OP172791.1), Escherichia phage vB_EcoM_ESCO37 (OM386659.1), and Escherichia phage Mt1B1_P17 (NC_052662.1). The genome of phage vECPPW9 contained genes associated with phage morphology, DNA metabolism and replication, DNA packaging, and host cell lysis. Notably, proteins involved in bacterial lysis, including endolysin and spanin, were identified. Endolysin, a peptidoglycan hydrolase, degrades the bacterial cell walls, whereas spanin disrupts the outer membrane39,40. These proteins act synergistically to lyse the host cell and facilitate the release of progeny phages41. In addition, genome analysis confirmed the absence of genes associated with lysogeny, bacterial virulence, antibiotic resistance, and transposons, reinforcing the safety of phage vECPPW9 for therapeutic applications. PhageScope analysis also confirmed that phage vECPPW9 was a virulent (lytic) phage, meaning it did not integrate into the host genome or establish lysogeny.

Phylogenetic analysis showed that phage vECPPW9 was closely related to Escherichia phage ECO340P1, classifying it within the Phapecoctavirus genus, Stephanstirmvirinae subfamily, Caudoviricetes order, Uroviricota phylum, Heunggongvirae kingdom, and Duplodnaviria realm. This classification aligns with TEM observations, which revealed a head and contractile tail. TaxMyPhage analysis further confirmed its classification within the Phapecoctavirus genus. Comparative genomic analysis between phage vECPPW9 and phage ECO340P1 revealed that phage ECO340P1 contained 273 CDSs, slightly fewer than the 278 CDSs identified in phage vECPPW9. Both phages encode essential proteins for phage replication and host lysis. Pairwise alignment using Emboss Stretcher showed a sequence identity of 54.8% between phage vECPPW9 and phage ECO340P1, confirming that phage vECPPW9 is a novel species within the Phapecoctavirus genus, as a sequence identity below 95% indicates novelty based on phage taxonomy guidelines42. Phage novelty was also confirmed through analysis of the PhageClouds database. Interestingly, Escherichia phage ECO340P1, originally isolated in China, suggests that related phages may have a broader global distribution, potentially spanning geographically distant regions43.

Biofilms are a major virulence factor that contributes to the antimicrobial resistance mechanism44. Composed of an extracellular matrix, biofilms provide structural support and protect bacteria from environmental stressors, including antibiotics and host immune responses. This makes biofilm-associated bacteria difficult to eliminate. Previous studies have demonstrated biofilm formation by E. coli isolated from swine and swine farm environments45. The level of biofilm formation varies among E. coli isolates46, and biofilms are strongly linked to antibiotic-resistant development47. Consequently, the increasing prevalence of biofilm formation by E. coli in swine and farm environments underscores the need for alternative treatments targeting biofilms. Based on the previous reports, endolysin and depolymerase have been reported as effective agents in preventing biofilm formation. For instance, endolysin LysAB1245 removed biofilms formed by Pseudomonas aeruginosa K1255 and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus NPRC 001R48. Similarly, endolysin Ply113 reduced dual-species biofilms of Enterococcus faecium–Staphylococcus aureus and Enterococcus faecalis–S. aureus49, while LysEP114 and LysEP135 reduced bacterial viability in planktonic biofilms50. A novel phage depolymerase, Dpo10, effectively inhibited biofilm formation51, and Dpo42 prevented E. coli biofilm formation52. In this study, genomic analysis of phage vECPPW9 revealed the presence of an endolysin gene, a peptidoglycan hydrolase enzyme, in the genome of phage vECPPW9. Additionally, the plaque morphology of phage vECPPW9 exhibited a halo, suggesting the possible presence of a depolymerase encoded in its genome. Analysis using the DePolymerase Predictor, a machine learning tool, indicated a high probability (0.97) of a depolymerase associated with ORF21753. Subsequently, structural modeling with the Phyre2.2 web server also identified a depolymerase domain within ORF217. These results suggest that the depolymerase encoded by phage vECPPW9 may facilitate biofilm dispersion and reduction by degrading extracellular polymeric substances surrounding the biofilm matrix54. The combined presence of endolysin and depolymerase activity may cause a dual antibiofilm mechanism, enhancing phage penetration into structured biofilms and improving bacterial clearance activity, supported by the previous study55. Consequently, bacterial cells within biofilms become more susceptible to phage infection and other antimicrobial treatments. Moreover, depolymerases are known to degrade both bacterial capsular polysaccharides and lipopolysaccharides, thereby enhancing phage attachment, increasing bacterial susceptibility to host immune responses, and improving antibiotic efficacy56. The antibiofilm activity of phage vECPPW9 was experimentally confirmed through in vitro assays on plastic surfaces, demonstrating its ability to both prevent biofilm formation and eradicate preformed biofilms. Moreover, this activity was concentration-dependent, suggesting that phage dosage might play a critical role in achieving optimal biofilm control.

In veterinary medicine, previous studies have reported that animals can be treated with phage mixtures specific to Salmonella enterica and E. coli administered through drinking water or aerosolization57,58. Moreover, phages have been incorporated into feed for both pigs and poultry59. However, drinking water in livestock production systems has been reported to be highly contaminated with bacteria, including E. coli60,61. Similarly, feed has been identified as a potential vehicle for transmission of bacterial pathogens, including E. coli62. Stainless steel and plastic materials are commonly used in water and feed storage systems in livestock farms. In addition, rubber tubing and plastic pipes are frequently utilized in water distribution systems, where biofilm formation may occur. A previous study reported a correlation between microbial contamination of trough surfaces and livestock drinking water, highlighting the interaction between biofilm-associated and planktonic bacterial populations63. Therefore, preventing biofilm formation and reducing established biofilms may improve the microbiological quality of swine drinking water and feed. In addition to the antibiofilm activity of phage vECPPW9 described above, stainless steels and rubber tubes were incorporated in this study to better simulate swine farm-related surfaces. The observed increase in biofilm biomass from day 1 to day 3 indicates progressive biofilm development under static conditions. This finding is consistent with a previous report demonstrating time-dependent E. coli biofilm accumulation on abiotic surfaces64. Phage vECPPW9 significantly reduced biofilm biomass at all time points tested. These results are consistent with a previous study in which biofilm biomass of E. coli strain ZK126 increased from day 1 to day 7, while phage T4 significantly reduced biofilm biomass at all time points. Although biofilm biomass increased with longer incubation periods, phage-treated groups consistently showed lower biomass than untreated controls64. This suggests that phage vECPPW9 remained effective against both early-stage and more mature biofilms. The ability to reduce biofilm biomass across different maturation stages may be attributed to phage replication within the biofilm matrix and subsequent bacterial lysis. Moreover, phage-encoded depolymerase may facilitate the degradation of EPS, thereby enhancing biofilm disruption. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential application of phage vECPPW9 for controlling biofilm formation on swine farm-related surfaces, which may contribute to improved hygiene and water quality management in swine production systems.

In conclusion, phage vECPPW9, a novel member of the Phapecoctavirus genus within the Stephanstirmvirinae subfamily, demonstrated potent antibacterial and antibiofilm activities against E. coli isolated from wastewater associated with swine farm environments. These findings underscore its potential as a promising biocontrol agent for managing bacterial contamination and biofilm formation in agricultural settings. Phage vECPPW9 may be developed as a formulation for administration through drinking water or feed supplementation to reduce E. coli infections in swine. Furthermore, its antibiofilm activity suggests potential applications as a surface treatment, such as in sprays or washing agents, to control biofilm-associated contamination in water and feed storage systems.

Nevertheless, the experiments were conducted under static in vitro conditions, which may not fully represent dynamic flow systems or the complex microbial communities present in farm environments. Further studies are warranted to expand the host range by including a broader panel of bacterial isolates, to investigate antibiofilm activity in ex vivo models, and to evaluate the safety and therapeutic efficacy of this phage in animal models, as well as its effectiveness under practical farm conditions.

Methods and methods

Animal ethics and biosafety approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Biosafety Committee of Walailak University, Thailand (WU-IBC-67-019) and by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Walailak University (WU-ACUC-67090).

Bacterial strains

Twenty MDR E. coli isolates, previously isolated from wastewater samples, were kindly provided by Ratchadaporn Boripun from Akkhraratchakumari Veterinary College, Walailak University. Antibiotic resistance profiles of these MDR E. coli isolates were included in Supplementary Table S320. The bacteria were streaked onto tryptic soy agar (TSA; Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and incubated at 37 °C overnight. The grown colonies were then picked and cultured in tryptic soy broth (TSB; Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) for further experiments. The bacterial cultures in TSB were incubated at 150 rpm for either 6 h or overnight.

Isolation and purification of phage

The phage was isolated and purified following previously established methods65. MDR E. coli PPW007 was used as the host for phage isolation. Environmental water samples were collected from the Chao Phraya River, Bangkok, Thailand. The samples were centrifuged at 6000× g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove debris, and the resulting supernatant was filtered through a sterile 0.22 μm filter (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Ten milliliters of the water sample and 10 mL of TSB were mixed with 1 mL of a logarithmic-phase culture of the isolated E. coli strain (OD600 = 0.08). After overnight incubation with agitation at 150 rpm and 37 °C, the mixture was centrifuged at 6000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, followed by filtration of the supernatant through a sterile 0.22 μm filter. The double-agar overlay method was performed to detect phages. A melted semisolid medium (0.75% bacteriological agar; Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) was combined with a diluted bacterial host in 15 mL Falcon tubes, vortexed, and then immediately poured onto TSA plates. After the soft agar solidified, the filtrate was spotted onto the agar. The plates were air-dried and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Plaques were picked and subjected to three rounds of purification using the double-agar overlay method to obtain a purified phage.

Phage propagation

Phage propagation for phage stock preparation was carried out using the double-agar overlay method65. Melted semisolid medium was combined with 200 µL of diluted phage suspension and 200 µL of diluted bacterial host (OD600 = 0.08) in 15 mL Falcon tubes. The mixtures were vortexed and immediately poured onto TSA plates, allowing the soft agar to solidify. After overnight incubation at 37 °C, 5 mL of SM buffer was added to the semi-confluent plates. The plates were gently shaken at 150 rpm at 37 °C for 3 h. The supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 6000× g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a sterile 0.22 μm filter and stored at 4 °C as a phage stock. The phage titer was determined by standard plaque assay and reported as PFU/mL.

Electron microscope characterization of phage

The morphology of the phage was analyzed using transmission electron microscopy. A three-microliter aliquot of the phage suspension was applied to a carbon-coated copper grid and stained with 2% (v/v) uranyl acetate (pH 6.7). The grid was incubated at room temperature for 30 s, and excess liquid was blotted off. The morphological characteristics of the phage were examined using a JEM 2010 electron microscope (JEOL, Freising, Germany) operating at 160 kV.

Evaluation of phage host range using the standard spot test method and EOP assay

The standard spot test method was performed as previously described, using 20 E. coli isolates65. Two hundred microliters of exponential-phase bacteria (OD600 = 0.08) was mixed with melted semisolid medium and poured onto TSA plates. After the top layer solidified, 10 µL of phage suspension, at serial dilutions ranging from 101 to 108 PFU/mL, was spotted onto the plates. The plates were air-dried and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Lysis zone was recorded as (+) for complete lysis or (−) for no lysis.

Bacterial isolates susceptible to phage lysis were selected for the EOP assay, which was performed as previously described66. A mixture of 200 µL of each diluted phage at 101 to 108 PFU/mL, 200 µL of a log-phase bacterial isolate (OD600 = 0.08), and melted semisolid medium was vortexed and poured onto TSA plates. After solidification, the plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C. EOP was calculated as the ratio of the average PFU on the target bacteria to the average PFU on the host bacteria. EOP values were categorized as follows: high (EOP ≥ 0.5), moderate (0.1 ≤ EOP < 0.5), low (0.001 < EOP < 0.1), or inefficient (EOP ≤ 0.001). All experiments were independently conducted in triplicate.

Phage adsorption rate assay

Phage adsorption to the bacterial host was evaluated as previously described67,68. E. coli (OD600 = 0.08) was incubated with the phage at a MOI of 0.1, and the samples were collected every 1 min for 10 min. At each time point, the samples were immediately filtered using a 0.22 μm pore size filter, and the filtrate was diluted and subjected to the double-agar layer assay. The adsorption rate constant (k), measured in mL/min, was calculated using the following equation: k = (2.3/Bt) × log(P0/P). In this equation, B represents the bacterial cell concentration (CFU/mL), and t is the time interval during which the titer decreases from P0 to P. P0, and P correspond to the initial and remaining concentrations of free virus particles (PFU/mL), respectively. Each experiment was conducted independently in triplicate.

One-step growth curve

A one-step growth curve of the phage was performed following a previously described method65. Exponential-phase bacterial cells (MDR E. coli PPW007; OD600 = 0.08) were incubated with the phage at an MOI of 0.1 for 10 min at 37 °C, followed by centrifugation at 6000× g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in pre-warmed TSB. During incubation at 37 °C with shaking at 150 rpm, samples were collected every 10 min interval for 120 min. The collected samples were filtered through a 0.22 μm pore-size filter, diluted in SM buffer, and the double-agar overlay method was used to determine the phage titer. The latent period, defined as the time required for intracellular phage replication, as well as the rise period and burst size (the number of phage progeny released per infected bacterial cell), were determined. All experiments were conducted independently in triplicate.

Bacterial cell lysis assay

The bacteriolytic efficacy of the phage was assessed following a previously described method65. MDR E. coli PPW007, the host isolate of phage vECPPW9, was cultured overnight and subsequently adjusted in TSB to an OD600 of 0.08. The bacterial culture was then infected with the phage at MOIs of 0.01, 0.1, 1, and 10. A bacterial culture without phage treatment served as the control. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C with vigorous shaking at 150 rpm, and the samples were collected for OD600 measurement at hourly intervals from 0 to 10 h post-incubation. Additionally, the samples were continuously incubated at 37 °C with vigorous shaking at 150 rpm. At 24 h post-incubation, the samples were collected and measured by using a UV spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). To determine bacterial viability at 24 h post-incubation, the samples were collected and diluted in TSB, followed by plating for colony-forming unit counting. Each experiment was performed independently in triplicate.

Assessment of bacterial morphological changes induced by phage infection using SEM

Exponential-phase E. coli (MDR E. coli PPW007; OD600 = 0.08) was incubated with phage vECPPW9 at an MOI of 0.1 at 37 °C for 2 h. E. coli without phage treatment was used as a control. The samples were then centrifuged at 6000× g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellets were resuspended twice with PBS, and a 20 µL aliquot of bacterial suspension was deposited onto sterile glass slides. After air drying, the cells were fixed with 2% (v/v) glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M PBS for 1 h at 4 °C. The samples were subsequently washed twice with PBS and dehydrated using a graded ethanol series (20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, and 100%), with each ethanol concentration performed in duplicate for 10 min each. Following dehydration, the samples were dried using a critical-point dryer (Quorum K850, Quorum Technologies, UK) with CO2 as the transitional medium and subsequently sputter-coated with gold using a sputter coater (Cressington 108 Auto, Cressington Scientific Instrument, UK). The bacterial cells were visualized under a field-emission SEM (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Images were captured at a magnification of 15,000×.

Temperature and pH stability assays

Temperature and pH stability tests were evaluated as previously described65. For thermal stability testing, phage vECPPW9 suspensions were incubated at -20 °C, 4 °C, 25 °C, 37 °C, 50 °C, 60 °C, and 70 °C for 2 h without agitation. After incubation, samples were cooled on ice, and the phage titers were determined using the double-layer agar method with the host isolate MDR E. coli PPW007.

For pH stability testing, phage vECPPW9 was inoculated into SM buffer adjusted to pH values ranging from 1 to 14 using HCl or NaOH. The mixtures were incubated at room temperature for 2 h. Following incubation, samples were neutralized to pH 7 prior to titration, and phage titers were determined using the double-layer agar method. Each experiment was independently conducted in triplicate.

Phage sequencing and whole-genome analysis

Phage genomic DNA was extracted using the Maxwell RSC Tissue DNA kit (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA), followed by the measurement of DNA quality and quantity. DNA libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Nano DNA library preparation kit (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), and the quality was assessed using an Agilent Technologies 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). High-throughput sequencing was performed on the Illumina sequencing platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), with sequencing services provided by Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, Republic of Korea). FastQC (version 0.11.7) was used to assess data quality and SPAdes de novo (version 3.15.0) was employed to assemble the filtered reads into a contig. PhageScope was used to annotate genes and construct a genome map, including the prediction of tRNA (https://phagescope.deepomics.org/)69. BlastP was performed to compare the predicted amino acid of each CDS against the NCBI database for function prediction (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi)70. Antibiotic resistance genes were identified based on analysis against the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database71. The VipTree web server was utilized to generate a proteomic phylogeny (https://www.genome.jp/viptree//), and Victor was used to construct a phylogenetic tree (https://ggdc.dsmz.de/victor.php)72,73. Percent nucleotide homology was determined using the EMBOSS Stretcher web server (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/jdispatcher/psa/emboss_stretcher)74. The novelty of the phage was identified by the PhageClouds database (https://phageclouds.dk/)75.

Efficacy of phage in preventing biofilm formation

The capacity of phage vECPPW9 to prevent the formation of biofilms was evaluated through biomass measurement and cell viability counting65. MDR E. coli PPW007, a host isolate of phage vECPPW9, was used as the model strain in this study. Phage vECPPW9 was serially diluted in TSB, and 100 µL of the diluted phage was added to flat-bottomed 96-well polystyrene microtiter plates. The final concentrations of phage vECPPW9 in the wells ranged from 101 to 108 PFU/well. Subsequently, 100 µL of exponential-phase E. coli (OD600 = 0.08) was added into the wells and gently mixed. The plates were incubated without shaking at 37 °C. At 1- and 3-days post-incubation, the medium in the wells was removed, followed by two gentle washes with 200 µL of PBS. For biomass measurement, the biofilms were stained with 200 µL of 0.1% (w/v) crystal violet solution for 30 min. The wells were then washed twice with distilled water and air-dried. The stained biofilms were dissolved in 200 µL of absolute ethanol, and absorbance was measured at OD595 using a standard microplate reader (Multiskan SkyHigh, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Biofilm biomass was normalized to the untreated control, which was defined as 100%. The percentage of biofilm biomass in phage-treated samples was calculated using a direct proportional comparison relative to the control. For bacterial viability in biofilms, 200 µL of TSB was added to the wells, and the biofilms were resuspended. The bacterial suspension was serially diluted in TSB and subsequently plated by dropping onto TSA plates. The plates were incubated at 37 °C overnight, and bacterial colonies were counted and calculated in CFU/mL. Wells containing bacteria without phage treatment were used as the biofilm control. Each experiment was independently conducted in triplicate.

Ability of phage to remove biofilms

The ability of phage vECPPW9 to remove biofilms was assessed according to a previous report65. MDR E. coli PPW007, a host isolate of phage vECPPW9, was used as the model strain in this study. A hundred microliters of exponential-phase E. coli (OD600 = 0.08) was cultured in new flat-bottomed 96-well polystyrene microtiter plates containing 100 µL of TSB at 37 °C without shaking. The medium in the wells was removed and replaced with 200 µL of fresh medium daily. After either 1 or 3 days of incubation, the medium was removed, and the wells were washed with PBS. Phage vECPPW9 at concentrations ranging from 101 to 108 PFU/well, diluted in TSB, was added to the wells, followed by incubation at 37 °C without shaking. At 24 h post-incubation, the supernatant was removed, and the wells were washed twice with PBS. The biomass of biofilm and the bacterial cell viability within biofilms were measured as described above. All experiments were independently conducted in triplicate.

Biofilm morphology under SEM

The morphology of biofilms and biofilms after phage treatment was observed under SEM. MDR E. coli PPW007, a host isolate of phage vECPPW9, was grown in a 24-well plate containing sterile glass coverslips, and the culture medium was refreshed daily. On day 3 post-incubation, the medium was removed, and the coverslips were washed with PBS. Phage vECPPW9 (108 PFU/well) was then added to each well of the 24-well plate, followed by incubation for 24 h. After washing the coverslips with PBS, they were fixed with glutaraldehyde and dehydrated using a series of alcohol solutions before sample preparation for SEM, as described above.

Ability of phage to prevent biofilm formation on swine farm-related surfaces

Rubber tubes (1 cm in diameter and 1 cm in height) and stainless steel coupons (0.5 cm × 0.5 cm) were selected as representative surface materials related to swine farms for the biofilm assay, with slight modifications from previously described methods76,77. The MDR E. coli PPW007 isolate was used as the bacterial model in this study. Bacterial cultures were grown overnight and subsequently diluted with TSB to an OD600 of 0.08. Sterile rubber tubes and stainless steel coupons were individually placed into the wells of 24-well plates.

To evaluate the ability of phage vECPPW9 to prevent biofilm formation on both surfaces, 2 mL of the bacterial suspension was added to the wells, followed by the addition of phage vECPPW9 at a concentration of 108 PFU per well (10 µL). The surfaces without phage addition were used as controls. The plates were incubated at 37 °C under static conditions. At 1, 2, and 3 days post-incubation, the rubber tubes and stainless steel coupons were aseptically transferred to new 24-well plates and gently washed twice with 200 µL of PBS to remove non-adherent cells. The remaining biofilms were stained with 200 µL of 0.1% (w/v) crystal violet solution for 30 min, after which the crystal violet solution was removed. The samples were washed twice with distilled water and air-dried at room temperature. The stained biofilms were then solubilized with 2 mL of absolute ethanol for 30 min, and absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a UV spectrophotometer.

To assess the efficacy of phage vECPPW9 in removing preformed biofilms on both surfaces, 2 mL of bacterial suspension was added to each well containing sterile rubber tubes and stainless steel coupons, and the plates were incubated under static conditions. Fresh medium was replaced daily in each well. At 1, 2, and 3 days post-incubation, the culture medium was removed and replaced with fresh TSB. Phage vECPPW9 was then added at a concentration of 108 PFU per well (10 µL) to the treatment groups, while wells without phage served as controls. The plates were incubated under static conditions for an additional 24 h. Subsequently, the rubber tubes and stainless steel coupons were transferred to new 24-well plates, gently washed twice with PBS, stained with crystal violet solution, solubilized with ethanol, and quantified by measuring absorbance at 595 nm as described above. All experiments were independently conducted in triplicate.

Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism, version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test, with p-values below 0.05 considered statistically significant. PFU and CFU data were analyzed using a lognormal one-way ANOVA, which is equivalent to ANOVA performed on log-transformed values, whereas percentage data and absorbance were analyzed without transformation.
