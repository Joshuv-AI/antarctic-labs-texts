From intraoperative visual inspection to postoperative quantification: ICG fluorescence angiography for acute mesenteric ischemia validated by histopathology
Authors: Zhi Fan, Shuang Guo, Tianyi Ma, Ying Xiao, Rongrong Zhu, Peng Zhang, Weiwei Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAcute mesenteric ischemia (AMI) is a rare and life-threatening condition that leads to intestinal necrosis. Accurate assessment of intestinal viability and perfusion is crucial for reducing its high mortality rate.Case presentationA 65-year-old female patient diagnosed with acute mesenteric ischemia complicated by intestinal necrosis underwent exploratory laparotomy. Under indocyanine green (ICG) imaging guidance, the necrotic bowel segments were resected. Postoperative quantitative analysis and histopathology confirmed intestinal necrosis.DiscussionDue to the lack of specific appearance in the early stages of intestinal wall necrosis caused by acute mesenteric ischemia, relying solely on white light or subjective qualitative ICG fluorescence imaging makes it difficult to precisely define the resection margin. This case innovatively applied quantitative ICG fluorescence analysis, revealing a correspondence between specific perfusion parameters, including T0 and slope, and the histopathological grading of intestinal necrosis. These findings are consistent with trends reported in colorectal surgery, although differences in underlying pathophysiological mechanisms may limit comparability. Therefore, quantitative ICG may provide surgeons with an objective intraoperative decision-making tool.ConclusionThis case demonstrates that ICG fluorescence imaging combined with quantitative analysis may provide additional objective information for assessing intestinal viability.

Introduction

AMI is a condition characterized by reduced or interrupted blood flow to the intestines, leading to ischemic necrosis of the intestinal mucosa and potentially full-thickness necrosis of the intestinal wall [1]. From an epidemiological perspective, the incidence of AMI is relatively low, but it increases significantly with age. Among individuals over 75 years old presenting with abdominal pain, AMI has become one of the primary causes, surpassing appendicitis [2]. Etiologically, AMI is primarily categorized into arterial, non-occlusive, and venous types, with the arterial form being the most prevalent [3]. The prognosis is extremely poor, with a medium-to-long-term mortality rate approaching 70% [3]. Without timely intervention, prolonged ischemia leads to full-thickness necrosis of the bowel, resulting in suppurative infection and potentially fatal shock [4].

The current mainstream treatment strategy holds that early revascularization should be performed for most AMI patients. If preoperative assessment suggests intestinal necrosis, exploratory laparotomy is required to resect the necrotic bowel segments. However, during exploration, it is sometimes difficult to clearly distinguish between necrotic and non-necrotic tissue [5].

ICG imaging is currently a widely used method in clinical practice. Its safety and clinical utility in assessing bowel perfusion have been well established in colorectal surgery [6, 7]. However, its intraoperative application still primarily relies on subjective qualitative assessment [8]. Notably, qualitative ICG alone does not significantly improve outcomes. A clinical trial primarily involving AMI cases demonstrated that qualitative ICG did not alter rates of progressive intestinal necrosis, reoperation, or mortality among patients undergoing surgery [9]. Therefore, further development of quantitative ICG assessment may be clinically relevant. Its application may assist surgeons in accurately determining whether intestinal necrosis has occurred during surgery and in defining the resection margin. However, existing research lacks detailed reports on quantitative ICG during surgery for AMI patients.

In this case, we first employed ICG technology during surgery to qualitatively analyze intestinal necrosis. Postoperatively, we retrospectively conducted a quantitative analysis of ICG perfusion patterns. The results supported the initial assessment and were ultimately compared with pathological findings of necrotic bowel segments, revealing a relationship between quantitative ICG parameters and the extent of necrosis. This paper reports a case of AMI caused by SMA(Superior Mesenteric Artery) embolism in a 65-year-old female patient. The case report adheres to the SCARE 2020 Guidelines for Surgical Case Reports [10].

Case presentation

A 65-year-old female patient weighing 83 kg was admitted for “mid-upper abdominal pain for 4 days, worsening for 1 day. The patient initially presented with mid-upper abdominal pain, which subsequently intensified and spread diffusely throughout the abdomen. Abdominal computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed a filling defect in the superior mesenteric artery, distal to its origin and beyond the first main branch (Fig. 1), confirming a diagnosis of AMI. On admission physical examination, tenderness and rebound tenderness were noted in the right upper quadrant and bilateral lower abdomen, without muscle guarding. Laboratory tests showed marked inflammation: C-reactive protein (CRP) was elevated at 152.73 mg/L, white blood cell count was 24.24 × 10⁹/L, D-dimer 0.89 mg/L FEU. Emergency blood gas analysis showed pH 7.44 with mildly elevated lactate at 1.4 mmol/L. Comprehensive assessment of symptoms, signs, and imaging findings indicated risk of intestinal necrosis. Emergency interventional thrombectomy and exploratory laparotomy were performed.

Immediately following endovascular thrombectomy, laparoscopic exploration was performed. Under white light illumination, a segment of small intestine approximately 5 cm in length appeared grayish-white, raising suspicion of necrosis (Fig. 2). Then, in order to clearly observe the necrotic area of the intestine and precisely remove the necrotic intestine, we conducted an exploratory laparotomy. To assess intestinal perfusion and define resection margins, 25 mg of ICG was administered intravenously intraoperatively, followed by a 10 mL saline flush. Crucially, the fluorescence imaging camera (OPTO-CAM214K; Optomedic, China) was fixed at a distance of 20 cm from the intestine, focusing on the suspected necrotic area and its margins, capturing the entire perfusion process from ICG injection to tissue visualization. Based on macroscopic findings and qualitative fluorescence imaging, the necrotic intestinal segment was resected and anastomosed. After resection, the designated ROIs on the specimen were marked with sutures, and corresponding tissue samples were obtained for histological examination to ensure alignment between fluorescence ROIs and pathological findings. The quantitative fluorescence analysis was performed retrospectively after surgery.

Postoperatively, the intraoperative ICG imaging videos were imported into ImageJ software (version 1.54p, National Institutes of Health, USA) for retrospective fluorescence time–intensity quantitative analysis (Fig. 2). Three regions of interest (ROIs) were selected on the images: the necrotic core, the necrotic margin, and the relatively normally perfused area. Each ROI had an area of approximately 300 pixels. Then we evaluated the perfusion kinetics using the following parameters derived from the fluorescence-time curve (FTC). The time to first fluorescence signal (T0) was defined as the time interval from ICG administration to the first sustained upward trend in fluorescence intensity. Following T0, the intensity increased until reaching the maximum fluorescence intensity (Fmax); this duration is termed time-to-peak (TTP); Finally, the mean ascending slope (Slope) was calculated as the differential of the FTC during the TTP interval (Fig. 3). Histopathological grading was independently performed by two pathologists who were blinded to the intraoperative findings; in cases of disagreement, a third pathologist was consulted for final assessment. The grading was performed according to the Park/Chiu scoring system [11], pathological results showed Grade 8 necrosis in the central necrosis zone (Fig. 4a), Grade 7 necrosis in the marginal necrosis zone (Fig. 4b), and Grade 2–3 necrosis in the normal perfusion zone (Fig. 4c). Overall, with increasing necrosis severity, there was a tendency toward larger T0 values, smaller Slope values, and lower Fmax values (Table 1).

The patient recovered well after surgery and was transferred back to the general ward after two days of observation in the intensive care unit (ICU). The patient was subsequently discharged without complications. The patient has been discharged for four months and is currently tolerating a normal diet.

Discussion

Patients with AMI who are suspected of having intestinal necrosis should undergo exploratory laparotomy as soon as possible to resect necrotic bowel segments [4]. During operation, the most challenging aspect lies in distinguishing viable from necrotic bowel segments to ensure optimal surgical outcomes [12]. The degree of ischemia, its etiology, and duration vary among patients. Moreover, necrotic segments may not initially exhibit characteristic signs of necrosis [13]. Therefore, objective tools are essential during surgical exploration to assess intestinal perfusion and viability [5].

Regarding the application and current research status of quantitative ICG fluorescence in AMI, few studies have been reported to date. In colorectal surgery, however, quantitative ICG has been more extensively investigated, particularly for the prediction of anastomotic leakage (AL). In a study including 70 patients, the reported perfusion parameters at the anastomotic site in patients without AL were T0 of 19.8 ± 11.8 s, Fmax of 212.7 ± 12.0, and slope of 17.4 ± 7.4, whereas in patients with AL, T0 was 19.9 ± 14.3 s, Fmax was 207.9 ± 18.3, and slope was 10.8 ± 6.2.Furthermore, a case series demonstrated differences in fluorescence dynamic patterns between ischemic and normoperfused colonic segments in patients with mesenteric injury (Ref 13). In one representative case, the normoperfused colonic control segment exhibited Fmax of 169.8 and slope of 12.02, whereas the corresponding affected segment showed a markedly reduced Fmax of 8, slope of 0.5, and a prolonged T0 of 44 s. In the present study, a pathological necrosis grade of 8, representing irreversible necrosis, corresponded to T0 of 15.7 s, slope of 3.7, and Fmax of 29. In contrast, a region that appeared macroscopically well perfused (actual necrosis grade: 2–3) exhibited T0 of 11.6 s, slope of 26.2, and Fmax of 224. These findings suggest differences in T0, slope, and Fmax across bowel segments with varying degrees of necrosis, and the observed trends are consistent with those in AL and mesenteric injury. However, different etiologies are associated with distinct pathophysiological mechanisms, and the comparability of these parameters remains limited. In addition, quantitative ICG parameters may also be influenced by patient-specific systemic conditions that affect overall microcirculatory perfusion, which should be considered when interpreting the results.

In this case, 25 mg ICG was administered intravenously, corresponding to approximately 0.30 mg/kg body weight. A stable camera position was maintained over the suspected necrotic area. Following ICG injection, the endoscopic camera’s recording function was activated and maintained for a continuous recording. Postoperatively, we compared intestinal histopathological findings with quantitative ICG parameters and found a descriptive correspondence between them: higher Slope values indicated better blood perfusion and lower tissue necrosis severity; while a longer T0 indicated delayed blood flow arrival and more severe tissue necrosis. Although fluorescence intensity also showed a decreasing trend with increasing necrosis severity, its correlation was less pronounced than that of Slope and T0 in this case. These findings constitute the primary discoveries of this study.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study was a single-case study, and only three ROIs were analyzed, resulting in a limited sample size and reduced representativeness. Second, T0 was determined manually from the fluorescence time–intensity curve, and no predefined numerical threshold was applied, which may introduce some degree of subjectivity. Third, quantitative fluorescence parameters may vary among different imaging systems, which could affect the reproducibility and generalizability of the results. Fourth, comparison with colorectal anastomotic perfusion studies is limited because of differences in the underlying pathophysiology. Finally, the optimal dose of ICG for gastrointestinal perfusion assessment remains uncertain and requires further investigation.

This study first proposes the potential of using quantitative ICG in AMI surgery to aid in the diagnosis of intestinal necrosis. Moving forward, we need to conduct postoperative ICG imaging analysis on a larger number of cases to progressively establish a standardized mathematical model correlating ICG quantitative parameters with the extent of intestinal necrosis. This will provide surgeons with new objective indicators to guide precise resection during future surgeries, ensuring the removal of necrotic tissue while maximizing the preservation of functional bowel segments.

Conclusion

In this case, quantitative ICG fluorescence analysis parameters, particularly T0, Slope, and Fmax, were consistent with the histopathological grading of intestinal necrosis. These findings suggest that quantitative ICG fluorescence analysis may provide additional objective information to support intraoperative assessment and decision-making in acute mesenteric ischemia. Further studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate these preliminary observations.
