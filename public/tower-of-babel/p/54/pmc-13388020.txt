An Overview of the Rationale and Outcomes of Residential Camps for Children with Inflammatory Bowel Disease
Authors: Emile T Crouzen, Andrew S Day
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeInflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic immune-mediated condition often beginning in childhood, impacting both physical and mental health. Children with IBD may face psychological distress, stigma, developmental challenges and general impact on quality of life (QoL). Residential camps offer holistic support by providing safe, inclusive spaces where children can connect, share experiences, and build resilience.MethodsThe study aimed to review the impact and outcomes of residential camps for children with IBD, with regards to factors such as emotional well-being, social functioning and disease-specific knowledge. This review explored the evolution of these camps while considering the mechanisms of benefit, and implications for future practice and research. Comparisons were made to camps for children with other chronic diseases.ResultsStudies of IBD camps across multiple countries show consistent psychosocial benefits, including improved QoL, social functioning, confidence, disease knowledge, and reduced isolation. Similar outcomes have also been reported in camps for other chronic illnesses such as diabetes, asthma, and haemophilia, supporting the value of structured, peer-based interventions in enhancing self-management and emotional well-being more generally.ConclusionStructured routines, peer support and role modelling may be key contributors to the positive effects associated with residential camps for children with IBD. Though formal economic evaluations are limited, the multifaceted benefits of camps - psychological, educational, and potentially physiological - highlight their value in the comprehensive care of children with IBD.

INTRODUCTION

The term inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a collective of chronic, incurable, immune-mediated illnesses of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, with onset throughout life, but particularly in adolescence and early adult years [1]. The key subtypes of IBD are known as ulcerative colitis (UC), Crohn’s disease (CD) and IBD-unclassified (IBDU). Individuals with IBD typically present with GI symptoms such as abdominal pain, weight loss or bloody diarrhoea. In addition, IBD often impacts adversely upon quality of life (QoL), and is also associated with increased rates of anxiety and depression [2]. IBD in children is typically more aggressive and more extensive than in adults and may impact adversely upon growth or development [3].

Although IBD is incurable, there are various available interventions that focus upon either induction or maintenance of remission. In children, these include medical (e.g., corticosteroids or biologics), nutritional (e.g., exclusive enteral nutrition) or surgical (e.g., resectional surgery) interventions. Some of these interventions might lead to side effects (e.g., acne and fluid retention secondary to corticosteroids) or disruptions to usual daily activities (e.g., short admissions for drug infusions) that impact adversely on the QoL of children [45]. Furthermore, children may not respond as expected and require changes in therapies. Others may require multiple simultaneous interventions.

Consequent to their disease, or to the interventions required for management, children with IBD may suffer psychological distress, isolation, low self-esteem around body image, future uncertainty and have reduced capacity to undertake expected educational activities [67]. Furthermore, children with IBD will face these issues in the context of normal development challenges (such as fitting in with peers), especially during teenage years. Symptoms of IBD are often humiliating to discuss, have a high impact on social functioning and may prevent children from engaging in activities as they may need to be near to a bathroom or healthcare facilities. In addition, GI symptoms and diseases have social stigma, meaning that individuals with IBD may not be able to mention or discuss their condition [8].

As a result of these various factors, many children and adolescents require additional support. While psychological support or intervention may be appropriate, peer support is often most beneficial, especially when provided through activity-based programmes [9]. Activity-based peer support approaches, such as residential camps, may be one key avenue to holistically enhance the outcomes of these young people. This manuscript reviews the benefits associated with these interventions generally and with specific regard to children and adolescents with IBD and draws comparisons from camps that focus on children with other chronic illnesses.

THE EVOLUTION OF RESIDENTIAL CAMPS FOR CHILDREN WITH IBD

Camps – particularly summer camps - historically have had a positive impact on children’s well-being and are a very popular activity, particularly in the United States of America (USA). It is estimated more than 26 million American children and adults take advantage of a camp experience each summer [10].

In the 1800s the first rural and alpine sanatoria were established in Europe to cater to the needs of people with tuberculosis [1112]. The first camps for chronic illness and disability were held in the 20th century, with the first camp for people with a disability in the 1960s [13]. This and subsequent camps started a movement that led to increased acknowledgement of disability and enforcement of new legislation and rights on an occupational and societal level [14]. Camps for adults and children with various chronic illnesses followed in the subsequent decades.

Camp Magic, held in in 1992 in Texas, USA, appears to have been the first camp for youngsters with IBD. Following on from this, numerous other camps have been held in the USA, with support and sponsorship from various sources, particularly the Crohn’s and Colitis Foundation of America (CCFA) [15]. These include Camp Guts and Glory, Camp Oasis and Camp Gut Busters.

More recently, camps have been held in other countries, including New Zealand, Australia and Canada. Whilst the first camp for children with IBD in Australia (Camp GetAway) was held in 2008, Camp Fearless has been held as a weekend activity-based camp in 2024 and 2025 [16]. The first IBD Camp in NZ (Camp Purple Live) was held in 2015, with annual camps since (with the exception of one cancellation during the coronavrirus disease pandemic) [17]. Camps have also been held in various locations in Canada over the last 10 years [18].

THE BENEFITS OF CAMPS FOR CHILDREN WITH IBD

Residential camps for children with IBD have been shown to lead to various positive outcomes, across different spheres (Table 1) [15192021222324]. Firstly, camps are associated with various psychological benefits. For example, a study conducted at Camp Oasis (in the USA) demonstrated significant improvements in campers’ emotional well-being, self-esteem, and social confidence [15]. The camps were shown to provide a unique environment where children feel understood and supported, helping them build resilience and coping mechanisms. Furthermore, campers often report reduced feelings of isolation, as they connect with peers who face similar challenges. These psychological benefits can positively impact the children’s overall QoL, including emotional stability and school performance.

A further study evaluating the outcomes of camps conducted in Camp Oasis found that participants reported significant improvements in their health-related quality of life (HRQoL), including better disease-specific knowledge and emotional resilience [19]. This study collected pilot data following camp from a Facebook group of Camp Oasis campers. The positive outcomes were attributed to a supportive environment where children could freely share experiences with peers facing similar challenges, fostering a sense of normalcy and reducing feelings of isolation.

In addition, emerging evidence suggests camps may positively influence physical symptoms in children with IBD. A study focusing on cortisol levels, an indicator of stress and inflammation, found reductions in stress-related biomarkers among campers with IBD following their participation in a camp [25].

Moreover, camp activities typically provide structured routines and may include education about managing IBD, which not only builds confidence but also helps children adapt to their condition. These experiences also have been linked to reduced stress and improved emotional well-being, both of which are crucial for better management of IBD symptoms [26]. The impact extends beyond the camp experience, as children report improved coping skills and a greater sense of independence, enhancing their overall life satisfaction.

The positive influence of camps on children appears to also lead to positive outcomes for parents for similar reasons: recognition, education, adherence and reflection. Workshops for parents that enable educational activities and parent-to-parent supports can also improve disease-related stress and wellbeing: similar effects have been described in siblings [2728].

A survey conducted among participants of Camp Purple Live in New Zealand supported earlier findings related to IBD and other chronic illnesses, highlighting the camp’s positive impact on young people living with these conditions [20]. Respondents reported improved QoL, including enhanced emotional well-being, greater social connection, and increased confidence in managing their condition. The camp environment, centred around shared experiences, peer support, and tailored programming, was identified as a key factor contributing to the overall beneficial experience.

OBSERVATIONS ARISING FROM RESIDENTIAL CAMPS FOR CHILDREN WITH OTHER CHRONIC DISEASES

A number of reports have provided information on outcomes arising from camps for children with various other chronic diseases. Although there are inherent differences between the camps and between tools used to assess outcomes, there are several common themes arising. Furthermore, camp structures may have similarities (for instance, dietary patterns relevant to children with diabetes and IBD). The outcomes documented at and following these camps are relevant to and augment the overall findings reported with camps for children with IBD.

Camps for children with diabetes have documented improved adherence to blood glucose monitoring and insulin administration [29]. These benefits have been attributed to the application of structured routines and role-playing to reinforce habits that support adherence. Enhanced adherence to medical therapies was also illustrated in a camp for children with asthma [30]. Better management of triggers for airway symptoms was noted in the children attending that camp.

Several reports have shown improved disease-specific knowledge arising from various camp settings. In the setting of diabetes camp(s), focused education has been shown to result in improved carbohydrate counting and more effective management of insulin dosing [31]. Improved essential knowledge about recognising and preventing bleeding episodes was noted in a camp for children with haemophilia [32].

Several reports also note improved overall QoL. One report of children with cystic fibrosis attending a camp noted reduced anxiety, improved self-esteem, and a stronger sense of community [33]. The normalization of illness and shared experiences were felt to be key contributors to these observations. Camps for children with diabetes have also noted enhanced QoL [31]. These were attributed to helping children balance disease management with regular childhood activities, enhancing their overall life satisfaction.

Common to the positive outcomes of these camps for children with various chronic diseases is a structure that includes leaders or role models at camp. Interestingly, the Royal Children’s Hospital in Melbourne, Australia, has developed a process around camps for children with chronic disorders [34]. This process is based around ‘Chronic Illness Peer Support’ (CHIPS) workers whose defining role is to explore concepts of leadership, learning more about things such as communication skills, public speaking, group dynamics, team building and other important life skills for children with chronic illness.

DISCUSSION

Key aspects of camps are bonding and growth through shared experiences. Children with IBD may be exposed to episodes of pain, increased self-consciousness, isolation, and require hospitalisation. Camps are an ideal opportunity for children to develop a sense of identity and familiarity in the presence of other children in the same situation with supportive adults who may also have the same condition. The observations of outcomes from camps for children with other chronic conditions is that psychological and somatic enhancements are obtained, reflected in improved disease course.

The underlying goal of camps for children with IBD is to provide a safe space for vulnerable children and to enhance holistic aspects of their disease management. Children with IBD are met with their nutritional and medical needs during a camp and are provided with opportunities to normalise their IBD as all campers are in the same situation. Further, some children with IBD may meet other children with the same condition for the first time on a camp. Altogether, camps endeavour to provide a space where true empathy and understanding can be found and shared, thereby enhancing well-being and reducing isolation.

Volunteer leaders joining such camps take on a pivotal role within this safe space. Their focus should be upon the campers and not themselves. The volunteers often have the diagnosis themselves and might be viewed by campers as role-models. Furthermore, campers may return as volunteers when older [29].

Generally, camps might include children with varying disease severity and very different disease courses. Some parents and campers may worry about future complications when talking with other campers or camp leaders or may develop anxiety about the future course of their disease in general. This may be of greater risk in adolescents, who are more conscious of later life.

Some applicants may be concerned that the camp will be centred around bowel disease. However, nearly all camps will be activity-based, entailing various activities such as campfires, sports and games. Either automatically or opportunistically, the campers’ struggles with IBD may be shared. This form of talking therapy is an important contributor to the role of camps in improving overall well-being in children with IBD [35].

Complex interventions for child health should be justified in economic terms. However, the application of such analysis is contentious. Several reasons contribute to this: the unique status of children, the lack of strong clinical trial evidence for these interventions, and the absence of methods to measure health economic outcomes across various developmental stages. Additionally, there are the multiplicative effects of life experiences to consider.

Depending on the nature and duration of the activity, school camps in New Zealand typically incur costs ranging from NZD 15 to NZD 100 per child per day for camp participation [3637]. When contextualised against the substantial financial burden of IBD management – which often involves costly biologic therapies, immunosuppressive medications, advanced imaging (e.g. magnetic resonance imaging), multidisciplinary care, and inpatient hospitalisation – this expenditure represents a relatively modest and potentially cost-effective investment in promoting psychosocial and health-related outcomes.

CONCLUSION

Camps for children with IBD are now well-established in a number of countries. Evaluations arising from these camps demonstrate important positive outcomes arising from participation. Altogether, it is clear that these camps can provide a key role in the overall management of children with IBD.

Some of these benefits have also been demonstrated in evaluations of camps held for children with other chronic diseases. While some of these benefits are generic for children with any chronic conditions, others reflect specific features for children with that illness. Overall, these data provide further illustration of the importance and significance of camps focusing on children with IBD.

However, further research is required to delineate other outcomes of camp attendance. The persistence of improved QoL after camp attendance has not been evaluated, nor has the impact of repeated camp attendance been considered. Furthermore, it would be intriguing to evaluate and ascertain whether enhanced general well-being leads to improved adherence and enhanced disease outcomes. Finally, work should consider the economic costs and benefits of hosting camps for children with IBD.
