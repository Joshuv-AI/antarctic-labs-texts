Communication, family support, and the hidden burden of psychological intimate partner violence
Authors: Paulio P Reyes II, Pinky Larcelle L Gas-ib
Published: 2026

Dear Editor:

We read with great interest the article by Darwish-Mateos et al. on intimate partner violence (IPV) among Spanish adolescents and its relationship with family and social support.1 The authors reported a high prevalence of psychological violence, particularly controlling behaviors (55%), humiliation (40.1%), and social violence (37.9%), while 80% of participants perceived violent behaviors within relationships. Furthermore, family dysfunction and non-nuclear family structures were associated with a greater likelihood of perpetrating violence. These findings highlight the important role of family and social environments in shaping adolescents’ experiences of IPV.

We would like to expand this discussion by emphasizing a dimension that may help explain both the persistence and invisibility of psychological violence: communication. The predominance of control, humiliation, and social restriction observed by Darwish-Mateos et al. suggests that many manifestations of psychological IPV may be conveyed through everyday communicative practices. Verbal humiliation, coercive discourse, threats, monitoring, and controlling expressions are not merely indicators of conflict; they may constitute mechanisms through which psychological violence is exercised and maintained.

From this perspective, family and social support may influence not only the occurrence of IPV but also the way violent behaviors are interpreted and normalized. When controlling or humiliating forms of communication become embedded in family or peer interactions, adolescents may perceive them as ordinary components of intimate relationships rather than manifestations of abuse. This normalization may help explain why a large proportion of participants perceived violent behaviors while such practices continue to occur at high rates.1

The implications extend beyond victimization and perpetration. Psychological violence that is normalized may remain undisclosed, creating a hidden burden that is difficult to identify in clinical practice. Similar patterns have been documented in other cultural contexts, where psychological violence represented the most prevalent form of abuse and victims frequently delayed or avoided reporting because controlling and humiliating behaviors were perceived as normal within intimate relationships.2 These observations suggest that normalization may represent an important barrier between experiencing violence and seeking help. Given that psychological violence is among the most common forms of IPV worldwide, failure to recognize and disclose these behaviors may contribute substantially to the global burden of violence against women.3

For primary care professionals, this highlights the need to move beyond the detection of physical violence alone. Routine enquiry regarding relationship violence should also explore communicative manifestations of abuse, including humiliation, coercive control, threats, social isolation, and persistent monitoring. Particular attention may be warranted among adolescents from dysfunctional or non-nuclear family environments, where the normalization of these behaviors may reduce recognition and disclosure of abuse.

We congratulate Darwish-Mateos et al. for their valuable contribution and encourage future research examining how family and social support networks influence not only the occurrence of IPV but also the communicative processes through which violence becomes normalized, concealed, and underreported. Words may not only reflect violence; they may also normalize it, obscure its recognition, and hinder disclosure. Improving our understanding of these mechanisms may strengthen prevention efforts and facilitate earlier detection of hidden forms of violence in primary care settings.
