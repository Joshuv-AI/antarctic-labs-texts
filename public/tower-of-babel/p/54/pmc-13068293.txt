Real-world treatment patterns and medical burden in patients with NSCLC: a retrospective database study in Japan
Authors: Linghua Xu, Sona-Sanae Aoyagi, Yusuke Nukui, Tomoyo Oguri, Kanae Togo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimWe investigated treatment patterns and healthcare resource utilization (HRU) to clarify unmet medical needs in second and later lines among Japanese patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).MethodsThis retrospective study included patients with NSCLC having first-line treatment from a claims database (January 2019−December 2024). Treatment patterns, time to treatment discontinuation (TTD), and HRU were described for patients with/without actionable genomic alteration (AGA) therapy.ResultsOverall, 22,886 patients (with AGA, 7,831; without AGA, 15,055) were included. In patients with AGA, targeted therapy (48.6%), platinum doublet (12.1%), and chemo monotherapy (10.4%) were common second-line treatments, with median TTD 7.5, 4.3, and 3.0 months, respectively. In patients without AGA, chemo monotherapy (45.1%), ICI monotherapy (17.8%), and platinum doublet (13.9%) were the most prescribed second-line treatments with relatively short median TTD (2.7, 3.7, and 3.2 months, respectively). Hospitalization rates were higher in patients with second-line treatment duration <3 versus ≥3 months (incidence/100 person-years, 67–88 vs 35–38).ConclusionTreatment patterns were heterogeneous with short durations that were associated with higher rates of HRU. The study findings highlight the challenge of current treatments, and need for effective treatment options in clinical practice in Japan.KEYWORDS: Claims database, healthcare resource utilization, non-small cell lung cancer, medical burden, real-world data, treatment discontinuation, treatment patterns, unmet medical needs

Introduction

Globally, lung cancer remains the most common diagnosed cancer and cause of cancer-related mortality, with 2.48 million new cases and 1.8 million deaths in 2022 [1,2]. The burden of lung cancer is projected to increase to 4.62 million new cases and 3.55 million deaths by 2050, assuming stable incidence and mortality rates as in 2022 [2]. In Japan, lung cancer ranked as the third most common diagnosed cancer in both sexes and was the leading cause of cancer-related death in males (52,908 deaths in 2023) [3].

Lung cancer is categorized as non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small cell lung cancer, with NSCLC accounting for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases [4]. According to the Japanese and international guidelines for NSCLC, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) are standard of care (SoC) across first- and second-line therapy in patients with targetable mutations [5,6]. Currently approved TKIs are used for genomic alterations such as epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations, anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) fusions, proto-oncogene 1 receptor tyrosine kinase (ROS1) fusions, v-raf murine sarcoma viral oncogene homolog B (BRAF) mutations, mesenchymal-epithelial transition factor (MET) alterations, rearranged during transfection (RET) fusions, neurotrophic tyrosine receptor kinase (NTRK) fusions, kirsten rat sarcoma (KRAS) G12C mutations, and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) mutations [5]. For those without targetable mutations/driver alterations, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI), such as programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-(L)1) inhibitors prescribed based on PD-L1 expression levels, preferably pembrolizumab monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapy, have reshaped first-line SoC in Japan [5]. While first-line regimens are clearly defined, the therapeutic landscape for subsequent treatments remains heterogeneous [7,8].

For second- and later-line treatment options prescribed in patients with metastatic NSCLC who experience disease progression, clinical guidelines recommend several chemotherapy options, including docetaxel (DTX) monotherapy, DTX combined with ramucirumab, as well as other chemotherapies such as pemetrexed, S-1, and nab-paclitaxel [5,9]. In the phase III REVEL trial, median progression-free survival (PFS) was improved with DTX + ramucirumab (4.5 months) versus placebo + DTX (3.0 months), albeit with higher rates of adverse events [10]. Real-world treatment duration and tolerability in NSCLC may differ from trial outcomes, particularly for the aging population in Japan. Though there are multiple treatment options available after first-line therapy, only a limited number of patients receive subsequent therapy and achieve sufficient therapeutic benefit over a long period [11], and in practice, many do not experience prolonged treatment efficacy [12]. Additionally, short treatment duration due to early discontinuation may lead to the deterioration of the patient’s clinical condition, thereby increasing the burden for patients and healthcare resource utilization (HRU) [13]. However, the real-world evidence reporting treatment patterns in second- and later-lines, and the medical burden for patients with NSCLC are limited in Japan.

A few real-world studies in Japan have shown a trend for increasing use of targeted therapies and ICIs in first-line treatment. A claims analysis of 9,373 patients with NSCLC (2015–2023) showed that uptake of targeted and ICI therapies nearly doubled in first-line [14]. However, it did not report second- and later-line treatment, highlighting the need for further real-world evidence on treatment patterns across lines of therapy, as well as investigations into disease burden and unmet medical needs for patients with NSCLC in the Japanese context.

Thus, this study aimed to investigate the real-world treatment patterns and treatment duration, as well as HRU for second-line treatment, to identify the burden of and unmet medical needs for patients with advanced or recurrent NSCLC in Japan.

Methods

Study design and data source

This was a retrospective, database study of Japanese patients with NSCLC who initiated first-line SoC treatment, using anonymized claims data managed by Medical Data Vision, Co., Ltd. (MDV). The data were collected for the study duration from 1 January 2019 to 31 December 2024, with due permission from the MDV database. The index date was defined as the start date of the first-line treatment excluding neoadjuvant therapy, adjuvant therapy, or chemoradiotherapy for NSCLC during the study period. The baseline period was considered from 3 months prior to the index date. The follow-up period was from the index date until the date of the last record of the patient in the MDV database or the end of the study period, whichever occurred first.

The MDV database provides hospital-based claims data and diagnosis procedure combination data from ≥20-bed hospitals across Japan. It includes diagnosis, procedure, prescription, and laboratory test data collected after a hospital visit for both outpatient and inpatient, unless the patient has been transferred to another hospital. As of the end of September 2025, it comprised large-scale medical data of more than 53 million patients from 540 hospitals across Japan [15].

Study participants

Patients with confirmed diagnosis of NSCLC (ICD10 codes: C340, C341, C342, C343, C348, and C349) who initiated first-line SoC treatment for NSCLC (Supplementary Table S1), age ≥18 years at the index date, and with complete data for baseline period and at least 6 months data for follow-up period in MDV database were included in the study. Patients who received any anticancer treatments (excluding adjuvant, neoadjuvant therapy, or chemoradiotherapy) for NSCLC within 3 months prior to the index date, or received only adjuvant, neoadjuvant, or chemoradiotherapy were excluded from this study. The included patients were stratified into two groups with/without actionable driver mutations targeted therapy, based on whether they received at least one actionable genomic alteration (AGA) targeted therapy.

Study endpoints

Demographics and clinical characteristics included age, sex, smoking history, cancer stage, and follow-up period. Treatment patterns included treatment regimens, treatment lines, start of treatment line, and treatment discontinuation. Treatment regimens included the drugs in the following categories prescribed within the same month as the NSCLC diagnosis: target therapy, platinum doublet therapy, platinum doublet therapy + ICI, ICI monotherapy, cytotoxic anti-cancer chemotherapy (chemo monotherapy), and other (if used in combination). If any of these treatments were prescribed within 42 days based on the administration per the guidelines [5,16] from the start date of the first prescription in each line, they were considered as combination therapy for that treatment line. For treatment lines, the same regimen, including maintenance therapy, dose modifications, and planned treatment interruptions were considered part of the same line of treatment. The initial regimen was regarded as first-line treatment, and transition to a new regimen prescription, or transition to the same regimen after a treatment gap of ≥90 days, was considered as a subsequent treatment line. The 90‑day treatment gap was selected based on the clinical practice patterns for NSCLC in Japan [5]. The start date of a treatment line was defined as the first date of the prescribed regimen in that line. The previous treatment line was considered discontinued if a subsequent line was initiated, or if ≥90 days had elapsed between the end date of the current treatment line to the end of the study period. For patients without discontinuation, time to treatment discontinuation (TTD) was censored at the last date of dosing. HRU was assessed as hospitalization status, emergency room visits (≥1 record), and intensive care unit (ICU) use (≥1 record) during and after second-line treatment.

Statistical analysis

Sample size calculations were not conducted, as data for all eligible patients were included for analyses. Analyses were performed using statistical software SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). All analyses were stratified by two groups: patients with and patients without AGA. Categorical variables were presented using frequencies (n) and percentages (%). Continuous variables were presented using descriptive statistics including median, interquartile range (IQR), minimum (min), and maximum (max). No imputations were carried out for missing data. Kaplan–Meier methods were used to calculate TTD as median and 95% confidence intervals (CI) by each treatment regimen category. Transitions between treatment lines were illustrated with Sankey plots. Incidence rates (per person-years [PY]) of each HRU outcome were calculated for overall and by second-line treatment duration <3 months versus ≥3 months.

Ethical considerations

In this secondary analysis study, all data were anonymized prior to entry. Japanese Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects do not apply to studies that use anonymized data; hence, patient consent and review by an institutional review board/research ethics committee were not required.

Results

Demographic and baseline characteristics

Of the 473,312 patients in the MDV database during the study period, 149,169 had a confirmed diagnosis of NSCLC. Overall, 22,886 patients were included per the predefined eligibility criteria. Of 22,886 patients, 7,831 and 15,055 were with and without AGA target therapy, respectively (Figure 1).

Treatment patterns

Treatment regimens and discontinuations in patients with AGA

Of the 7,831 patients with AGA who had first-line treatment, 47.5% of patients (3,722/7,831) moved to second-line treatment, while 28.4% of patients (2,227/7,831) withdrew from systemic therapy at first-line, and 24.0% (1,882/7,831) of patients were censored. At second-line, 50.6% of patients (1,885/3,722) moved to third-line treatment, while 29.7% (1,107/3,722) withdrew from systemic therapy, and 19.6% of patients (730/3,722) were censored (Figure 2(a), Supplementary Table S2). A trend of repeated prescriptions of targeted therapy was seen across all lines of treatment in patients with AGA. Among the 6,100 patients who received targeted therapy in the first-line, 1,086 (17.8%) were prescribed with targeted therapy in the second-line. Of these, 18.1% (197/1,086) were further prescribed targeted therapy in the third-line. In addition, 15.6% of patients who were prescribed targeted therapy in the first-line, switched to another treatment category in the second-line, and switched back to targeted therapy in the third-line (Figure 2(a), Supplementary Table S3).

Upon further examination of treatment patterns by line of therapy, 77.9% (6,100/7,831) received targeted therapy as first-line treatment. For second-line treatment, targeted therapy (48.6%; 1808/3,722), followed by platinum doublet (12.1%; 450/3,722), and chemo monotherapy (10.4%; 386/3,722) were commonly used (Figure 3(a)). The distribution of patients across targeted therapies is shown in Supplementary Table S4. Among those with targeted therapy, EGFR-TKI were prescribed in 82.7% of patients in the first-line and 73.6% of patients in second-line. Additionally, ALK-TKI were prescribed in 10.6% and 12.7% of patients in first- and second-line, respectively (Supplementary Table S4). Among the chemo monotherapy regimens in the second-line, S-1 monotherapy (26.7%; 103/386) was the most prescribed regimen, followed by DTX + ramucirumab (23.8%; 92/386), while DTX monotherapy was prescribed to 17.6% (68/386) patients (Supplementary Table S5). In third-to-later line treatments, targeted therapy (>35%) and chemo monotherapy (28.5–34.7%) were the most commonly prescribed (Figure 3(a)). Among those treated with chemo monotherapy, common regimens were DTX monotherapy (14–20%), DTX + ramucirumab (14–31%), and S-1 monotherapy (>25%) in each line (Supplementary Table S5). Less than 10% of patients used other therapy regimens (monotherapy and combination therapy of platinum doublet + ICI) in third-to-later line treatments (Figure 3(a)).

In first-line treatment, the median TTD for targeted therapy was 15.5 months, whereas in second-line treatment, it was 7.5 months, followed by platinum doublet (4.3 months) and chemo monotherapy (3.0 months) (Figure 3(a)). In subsequent lines, although the median TTD for targeted therapy (third-line: 5.1 months, fourth-line: 4.7 months, and fifth-line: 3.7 months) was relatively longer than that of chemo monotherapy (<3 months), the proportion of patients receiving chemo monotherapy was similar (Figure 3(a)).

Treatment regimens and discontinuations in patients without AGA

Of the 15,055 patients without AGA who had first-line treatment, 50.4% (7,586/15,055) moved to second-line therapy, 42.5% (6,405/15,055) withdrew from systemic therapy at first-line, and 7.1% (1,064/15,055) were censored. At second-line, 45.2% of patients (3,429/7,586) moved to third-line treatment, while 44.5% (3,376/7,586) withdrew from systemic therapy, and 10.3% (781/7,586) were censored (Figure 2(b), Supplementary Table S2). There was a notable switch to other treatment regimen in patients without AGA. Of the patients who received platinum doublet (20.4%; 3,068/15,055), platinum doublet + ICI (21.4%; 3,216/15,055), or ICI monotherapy (18.9%; 2,847/15,055) in the first-line, 24.2% (743/3,068), 35.9% (1,154/3,216), and 18.1% (516/2,847), respectively, switched to chemo monotherapy in the second-line (Figure 2(b), Supplementary Table S6).

Among patients with second-line treatment, chemo monotherapy (45.1%; 3,420/7,586), followed by ICI monotherapy (17.8%; 1,349/7,586), and platinum doublet (13.9%; 1,051/7,586) were the most prescribed treatments (Figure 3(b)). In patients who were prescribed chemo monotherapy as second-line, DTX + ramucirumab (28.9%; 987/3,420), S-1 monotherapy (27.8%; 952/3,420), and DTX monotherapy (23.9%; 816/3,420) were the most prescribed regimens (Supplementary Table S5). In the third-to-later lines of treatment, chemo monotherapy (>55%), ICI monotherapy (>10%), and platinum doublet (6–12%) were most prescribed treatments (Figure 3(b)). Among chemo monotherapies, S-1 monotherapy (27.7–36.7%) was the most used therapy; however, as the treatment lines progressed, a wider variety of regimens were prescribed with comparable prescription rates (Supplementary Table S5).

In first-line treatment, the median TTD for platinum doublet + ICI, ICI monotherapy, and platinum doublet was 6.9 months, 6.3 months, and 3.4 months, respectively (Figure 3(b)). In second-line treatment, the median TTD was 3.7 months for ICI monotherapy, 3.2 months for platinum doublet, and 2.7 months for chemo monotherapy (Figure 3(b)). In third- to later-lines of treatment, the median TTD was close to 3 months for all regimens except platinum doublet + ICI in the third-line (4.9 months) (Figure 3(b)). Overall, the median TTD in patients without AGA was shorter than in those with AGA, especially for chemo monotherapy.

Furthermore, when stratifying the duration of second-line treatment (<3 months vs ≥3 months), the incidence/100 PY (95% CI) for hospitalization and ICU use was higher with treatment duration <3 months (67 [62–73] and 2 [1–3], respectively) as compared to duration ≥3 months (35 [33–37] and 1 [1–1], respectively) in patients with AGA. Discussion

In this study, we investigated the real-world treatment patterns among Japanese patients with NSCLC, using a claims database, over a five-year period. As the evidence for common second-line regimens in Japan remains fragmented, we focused on unmet medical needs by identifying the trends in treatment after first-line SoC therapy for NSCLC. A trend of repeating treatment with targeted therapy was observed in patients with AGA; among those without AGA, switching to a different class of treatment was more common. Targeted therapy (48.6%) in patients with AGA and chemo monotherapy (45.1%) in those without AGA were the common second-line treatments. Although many patients were prescribed chemo monotherapy in the second-line, the treatment regimens varied among these patients. Moreover, there was also no clear trend among treatments other than chemotherapy. These findings reflect the heterogeneity of treatment strategies in second-line therapy. In second- to later-line treatment, most treatments had a median TTD between 2 and 6 months in patients with or without AGA; only a few, such as targeted therapy in the group of patients with AGA (7.5 months), were continued for longer than 6 months in the second-line. Additionally, the median TTD in patients without AGA was relatively short compared to that in patients with AGA. Incidence rates for hospitalization and ICU use were higher in patients receiving second-line therapy and who had shorter treatment durations, irrespective of AGA status.

In this study, we observed a tendency for repeated use of targeted therapies among patients with AGA. Currently, five EGFR-TKI drugs (erlotinib, afatinib, osimertinib, dacomitinib, and gefitinib) are approved in Japan [17]. The repeated use of targeted therapies in our cohort likely reflects the wide variety of available regimens in targeted therapy, suggesting that clinicians are exploring multiple therapeutic options for patients. Although various targeted therapy regimens may be attempted and appear to have longer treatment duration compared to other therapies, their effectiveness in later treatment lines does not last as long as that in the first-line setting for patients with AGA, and eventual resistance to treatments, which may influence long-term efficacy, remains a core challenge for targeted therapies [18]. As the clinical guidelines recommend prescription of chemotherapy following targeted therapy [5], our findings show that, despite a relatively high proportion of patients receiving chemo monotherapy after targeted therapy, these therapies were rarely continued for longer periods in the clinical setting. These short treatment durations rather reflect a lack of more effective alternatives, compelling the continued use of targeted agents. Furthermore, although the efficacy of targeted therapy in first- and second-line is supposed to be high, our real-world data showed that 28.4% of patients withdrew systemic therapy after first-line and 29.7% after second-line therapy. These findings suggest that for patients with AGA, there remains an unmet medical need for more effective therapies that can provide sustained treatment benefit over longer durations.

Among patients without AGA, as the options for existing effective treatments are limited, there is a tendency for chemo monotherapy to be continued after first-line therapy, even when the TTD is relatively short. This is evident from the higher proportion of patients (>55%) prescribed chemo monotherapy in third- and later-lines. Furthermore, in patients without AGA, a wide variety of treatment regimens were prescribed, with frequent switching to other treatment regimens. A similar pattern was reported in a US-based real-world study, wherein continued use of nonplatinum chemotherapy despite poor outcomes and frequent retreatment with platinum + ICI after prior discontinuation was noted, indicating the limited later-line treatments [19]. These findings suggest there are limited treatment options in later lines, and there remains an unmet medical need for effective regimens that can be continued over longer periods, particularly in second- and later-line settings. Additionally in our study, most treatment durations for chemotherapy were shorter than those with AGA regardless of treatment regimen. This may reflect the tendency of patients with AGA to respond better to chemotherapy compared to those without AGA. Previous retrospective analyses showed similar trends: patients with EGFR mutations had better responses to platinum-based first-line chemotherapy [20], and ALK-positive patients experienced significantly longer PFS compared to triple-negative patients [21]. In our study, the proportion of patients who withdrew from systemic therapy after first- and second-line therapy was higher among those without AGA (42.5% and 44.5%, respectively) compared to those with AGA (28.4% and 29.7%, respectively). These findings suggest that patients without AGA face even greater challenges in treatment compared to those with AGA, and there is a more critical need for drugs that enable longer-term treatment.

Currently, new drugs with various mechanisms of action for NSCLC are under development and are expected to be effective against the unmet medical need in the present NSCLC treatment landscape [7,18,22]. The phase II trial (INSIGHT 2) reported the promising clinical activity of tepotinib, a highly selective MET inhibitor in combination with osimertinib (objective response rate [ORR, 95% CI]: 50% [39.7–60.3]) [23]. A phase I trial of sigvotatug vedotin, an investigational antibody-drug conjugate reported a confirmed ORR (95% CI) of 19.5% (12.6–28.0), supporting initiation of its phase III study in second- and third-line NSCLC (NCT06012435) [24]. Phase II studies of combination therapies of durvalumab + ceralasertib, and acasunlimab + pembrolizumab showed promising efficacy, resulting in initiation of their phase III trials [25,26]. Amivantamab, a fully humanized bi-specific antibody in combination with chemotherapy was approved by the FDA as a first-line treatment for EGFR exon 20 insertion mutation. Zenocutuzumab was recently granted accelerated FDA approval [27]. Newly developed antibody-drug conjugates targeting the human EGFRs 2 and 3, trophoblast cell surface antigen 2, and MET are other promising candidates [7]. These agents are expected to be effective against the unmet medical need in the present NSCLC treatment landscape. However, real-world evidence and trial findings in Japanese patients with NSCLC are needed for future prospects.

Our findings related to HRU showed higher incidence rates of hospitalization and ICU need in patients with second-line treatment duration of <3 months, regardless of their AGA status. Reasons for shorter treatment duration may include early disease progression, treatment-related adverse events, or deterioration of the patient’s general condition, all of which are associated with poorer prognosis. Consequently, these factors often lead to hospitalization [7,28,29]. Particularly, patients with a decline in the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status frequently experience difficulty in continuing treatment for even short periods and often require hospitalization [30]. Disease progression in subsequent treatments is associated with longer duration of hospitalization and increased healthcare costs, while implying a negative impact on patients’ quality of life [31]. Therefore, prevention of disease progression and maintaining treatment for long periods can substantially reduce the economic burden on both patients and healthcare systems, as summarized in a recent systematic literature review by Jovanovski et al. [32].

This study has, however, several limitations intrinsic to the database characteristics. Although the study design was appropriate to meet the scope of research, it was a retrospective, observational study by nature wherein anonymized data of Japanese patients with NSCLC were collected from the MDV database. The MDV database provides longitudinal, large-scale data on routine Japanese clinical practice enabling evaluation of treatment sequencing and duration, as well as data related to medical costs and insurance; however, it lacks traceability once patients are transferred to a different hospital [33]. Patients could not be followed up after discharge from hospital, or transfer to another hospital outside the MDV, which may have led to follow-up bias. Due to the absence of information and missing data, post-discharge outcome of patients could not be evaluated. In addition, some patients were censored in the study due to the cutoff date. Moreover, patients’ data of diagnosis or treatment at other medical facilities prior to visiting hospitals within the MDV network could not be accessed. The database lacks information such as reasons for switch/change and discontinuation of treatments and use of biomarkers, and those deemed unnecessary for reimbursement, hence limiting the in-depth findings.

The information on the disease stage was limited in our study because the MDV database captures the disease stage only for inpatients, and baseline disease stage was obtained within three months before the index. Although first-line SoC treatment was defined as treatment for Stage IV NSCLC based on the Japanese guideline, the study population included patients before Stage IV because physicians may have initiated treatment earlier depending on the patient conditions, and baseline disease stage may reflect an earlier point in the disease trajectory. Furthermore, the MDV includes data mainly from participating acute-care hospitals, thus generalizability may be limited. The scope of study did not include statistical analyses comparing the proportions of treatment modes or testing for the difference in the incidence of HRU. Further, a 90-day cutoff between treatment line was adopted in this study, which was defined based on the clinical practice patterns for NSCLC in Japan. Different operational definitions of treatment lines using alternative cutoff intervals to define a subsequent line of therapy could lead to different categorizations and potentially influence the study findings. However, the lack of sensitivity analyses employing alternative cutoff intervals is a significant limitation of this study. Other limitations such as coding variability and inaccuracies in administrative datasets may lead to misclassification of diagnoses, procedures, or treatments, resulting in under- or over-reporting of treatments. Lastly, contemporary precision oncology recognizes diverse AGAs with distinct targeted agents and substantial differences in treatment patterns and time-to-treatment outcomes. While targeted therapies were analyzed as a single category to facilitate a more interpretable understanding of the treatment patterns in this study, aggregating the diverse AGAs could obscure potentially critical differences in prognosis and treatment responsiveness.

Conclusion

This retrospective study revealed that treatments after first-line therapy among Japanese patients with NSCLC were heterogeneous and with short duration in a real-world scenario, highlighting a significant unmet medical need for treatment options that remain effective over longer durations. Notably, patients without AGA have greater needs than those with AGA. Furthermore, shorter treatment durations were associated with higher rates of HRU. The development of new drugs that can address these unmet medical needs in current NSCLC treatment is highly anticipated.
