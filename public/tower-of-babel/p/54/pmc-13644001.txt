Partially reversible bilateral non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy and branch retinal artery occlusion following semaglutide use. a case report
Authors: Wael A Alsakran, Hamdah M Alkhaldi, Moustafa S Magliyah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NA-AION) is one of the most common causes of optic neuropathy and vision loss in individuals over the age of 50, with an annual incidence of 10.2 cases per 100,000 [1]. It accounts for approximately 85% of ischemic optic neuropathy cases, compared to the less prevalent arteritic variant [2]. The optic disc configuration has a significant impact on the development of NA-AION, in which a small and crowded optic disc carries the highest risk [3]. Additionally, systemic vasculopathy pathologies interfering with disc perfusion, such as diabetes and dyslipidemia, have been linked to NA-AION development [3].

Semaglutide (Ozempic, Novo Nordisk), a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA), was recently approved to treat diabetes and obesity [4]. However, emerging reports have linked the probable association between semaglutide and increased risk of ischemic optic neuropathy in the form of NA-AION [5, 6]. Herein, we report a young patient with no medical comorbidities who developed bilateral NA-AION associated with unilateral branch retinal artery occlusion (BRAO) during semaglutide therapy.

Case report

A 37-year-old obese male, who was not diabetic, not hypertensive, or dyslipidemic, presented with a sudden drop in vision in the right eye and blurred vision in the left eye. He gave a history of using semaglutide for 11 months for the purpose of losing weight, starting with 0.5 mg weekly for two months, and was escalated to 1 mg weekly for nine months. He did not report significant or rapid weight loss while using semaglutide. The patient lost 7 kg during the 11 months of semaglutide use.

On examination, his best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 6/200 in the right eye and 20/25 in the left eye. A relative afferent pupillary defect (rAPD) of 0.9 log units was observed in the right eye. Ishihara color-vision testing yielded 0/15 and 10/15 results in the right and left eyes, respectively. Goldmann visual field testing demonstrated a horizontal band-like scotoma crossing fixation and affecting the superior field in the right eye, and was unremarkable in the left eye (Supplemental Fig. 1). Anterior segment examination, including intraocular pressure, was unremarkable bilaterally. Dilated fundus examination showed bilateral optic disc swelling, more significantly in the right eye (Fig. 1A and B), along with an inferior macular ischemia indicating branch retinal artery occlusion (BRAO). Optical coherence tomography (OCT) of the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) at the optic disc showed optic disc thickening in the right eye more than the left eye (Fig. 1C). Fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) showed delayed retinal arterial filling corresponding to the site of BRAO with late leakage from the optic disc (Fig. 2A and B).

Further work-up, including toxoplasmosis and bartonella serologies, serum angiotensin-converting enzyme, serum lysozyme, chest computed tomography, contrasted magnetic resonance imaging of the brain and orbits, magnetic resonance arteriography and venography was unremarkable. Lumbar puncture revealed an opening pressure of 17 cm H2O. Cardiovascular and hematological investigations, including echocardiography, carotid Doppler ultrasonography, and coagulation profile, were also performed to exclude other causes of BRAO, and were all unremarkable. The diagnosis of bilateral NA-AION and right BRAO, possibly associated with semaglutide use, was made, and the discontinuation of the semaglutide was suggested without initiation of any systemic treatments. One month later, he presented with BCVA of 20/30 in the right eye and 20/20 in the left eye. Color-vision results were 12/15 and 15/15 in the right and left eyes, respectively. Dilated fundus examination showed small optic nerve heads in both eyes with marked improvement of disc swelling in both eyes (Fig. 3A and B). The cup-to-disc ration was 0.98 in the right eye and 0.92 in the left eye. OCT of the RNFL at the optic disc showed marked reduction of swelling in both eyes (Fig. 3C). However, there were persistent visual field defects in both eyes (Supplemental Fig. 2A and B), and the patient reported prolonged defects in his visual field, mainly in the right eye, at 3 months, 6 months, and one year after presentation. The BCVA was stable in all his follow-up visits, while OCT showed retinal thinning corresponding to the area that had BRAO.

Discussion

NA-AION is a major cause of severe visual impairment in adults, representing the most common cause of acute optic neuropathy in patients over 50 years of age, and the second most frequent etiology of all optic neuropathies after glaucoma [1, 2]. Patients with systemic risk factors such as diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and sleep apnea are more likely to develop NA-AION, particularly when combined with local factors in the form of small and crowded optic nerve heads [3].

Semaglutide is a GLP-1 RA that has recently been approved and is widely used to treat type 2 diabetes and, more recently, obesity [4]. Semaglutide has gained popularity due to its remarkable glucose control and cardiovascular benefits demonstrated in major trials, such as STEP and SUSTAIN trials [4, 7]. However, semaglutide has recently raised concerns regarding potential side effects, including NA-AION, especially in individuals who are prone to ischemic episodes [5, 6, 8–11]. The pathogenic mechanism is not fully defined and is poorly understood. Three possible causes were hypothesized, including, firstly, medication-induced GLP-1 RA-enhanced autonomic tone on GLP-1 receptors in the optic nerve, which may interfere with optic disc perfusion [7, 12]. Another argument was related to the known lowering effect of blood pressure by semaglutide, which may induce postural hypotension, and hence compromise the perfusion of the optic nerve [8]. The final theory proposed that optic nerve enlargement, presumably produced by fast hyperglycemia correction in diabetic patients, might result in successive NA-AION secondary to the optic disc crowding [9]. Although some authors were unable to determine whether the development of NA-AION was caused by the medication or by the culprit of vascular risk factors, Grauslund et al. discovered a persistently higher risk of NA-AION in diabetic individuals even after glycemic control [5, 6]. This indeed can be substantiated, as noted in our patient, who had no known systemic illnesses apart from being overweight, and his unremarkable investigations, including the glycosylated hemoglobin and lipid profile. However, the degree of visual improvement reported in the affected eye is relatively marked for NA-AION, which usually results in more permanent and more severe visual loss [13].

Apart from the negative associations with systemic risk factors, the unique features of this case include the bilateral involvement and the development of BRAO. The usual presentation of NA-AION includes unilateral involvement with acute painless visual loss associated with an altitudinal field defect. However, the visual loss can range from mild to severe, and visual field defects may be variable and include altitudinal, arcuate, diffuse, or atypical patterns [13]. This might explain the good visual acuity with inferonasal depression in the visual field of the left eye. However, it is uncertain whether the altitudinal field defect in the right eye is attributed to the NA-AION, BRAO, or a combination of both diseases. Two studies reported bilateral NA-AION [8, 9]. One case of bilateral sequential NA-AION occurring during semaglutide therapy was described in a patient with diabetes and hyperlipidemia, with the second eye becoming affected three weeks after the first. Nocturnal hypotension was hypothesized to have a role in NA-AION formation [8]. Katz et al. documented two further cases in which the optic nerves were swollen at presentation, and both patients were diabetics. They hypothesized that fast glycemic management would cause optic nerve enlargement and crowding, predisposing the optic nerves to ischemia [9]. Aside from being overweight, the reported patient in this paper did not have any systemic comorbidities. In 979 subjects with overweight or obesity, semaglutide exposure was associated with a hazard ratio of 7.64 (95% confidence interval 2.21–26.36) of developing NA-AION within three years [5]. Nevertheless, in all of those cases, the manifestation was unilateral, as opposed to our patient’s involvement in both eyes [5]. Another differential diagnosis of bilateral optic disc swellings, which might be partially reversible, is the elevated intracranial pressure (ICP). The opening pressure on lumbar puncture was normal in our patient, ruling out increased ICP. In addition, the presentation of this case lacked the typical symptoms of increased ICP, including headaches, nausea, vomiting, and tinnitus. In addition, the rapid improvement of disc edema after semaglutide discontinuation is atypical of high ICP induced papilledema. Dehydration, nocturnal hypotension, and sleep apnea might be possible confounders that affect the causation with semaglutide. In addition, the patient did not report any snoring, arousals, abnormal sleep positions nor daytime somnolence. Because formal sleep studies were not performed, undiagnosed obstructive sleep apnea cannot be excluded and remains a potential confounder in the interpretation of the association between semaglutide exposure and NA-AION.

This case raises the hypothesis of a possible association between semaglutide exposure and the development of NA-AION with concomitant BRAO. The causality cannot be established based on a single case report. There have been multiple reports of BRAO in association with NA-AION. The primary relationship was hemodynamic dysfunction due to cavernous sinus thrombosis, hemodialysis, and blood loss following a surgical intervention [14, 15]. For such a relationship, two theories have been proposed. The first is that the retinal and ciliary arterial trees were simultaneously affected. The second hypothesis is that the enlarged ischemic optic nerve compresses the retinal arteries, leading to sequential rather than simultaneous BRAO. Since our patient had both findings at the first evaluation, we are unable to determine if this connection is sequential or simultaneous. However, the incidence of BRAO in the eye with a more severely swollen optic disc increases the likelihood of the compression effect on retinal arteries, resulting in a significant reduction in the diameters of retinal arteries [16]. Shabto et al. reported the incidence of central retinal artery occlusion after semaglutide injection [10]. The suggested hypotheses were either an inadvertent intravenous infusion of semaglutide or an increase in blood viscosity due to medication [10]. However, the patient described in their research did not suffer from an ischemia that impacted the optic nerve. On the other hand, one of the cases reported by Katz and colleagues indeed had a combined NA-AION with paracentral acute middle maculopathy, which was hypothesized to be related to GLP-1 receptor expression in the ganglion cell layer [9]. Other possible explanations for the BRAO include simultaneous retinal and posterior ciliary circulation hypoperfusion, microvascular endothelial dysfunction, and thrombotic or vasospastic mechanisms. In addition, the possibility of an embolic event can be considered despite negative investigations. To our knowledge, no study has reported the incidence of BRAO as an isolated finding or in association with NA-AION following semaglutide injection. In conclusion, semaglutide use may be associated with an increased risk of developing NA-AION, which can be partially reversible. Importantly, the causal relationship cannot be definitively established based on a single case. The absence of systemic risk factors, along with the presence of predisposing risk factors of the optic disc, indicates that optic nerve assessments and retinal examinations might be advised before the initiation of semaglutide treatment, even in the absence of systemic risk factors. Further epidemiological and mechanistic studies are required before routine ophthalmic screening prior to semaglutide initiation can be recommended. This paper suggests that patients with crowded optic discs need to be informed of the potential risk of developing NA-AION following semaglutide exposure.
