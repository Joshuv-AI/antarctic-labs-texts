Outcomes of Fluoroscopy‐Based Asymmetric Underexpansion With the Self‐Expanding ACURATE Neo and Neo2 Valve
Authors: Daisuke Sato, Noriaki Moriyama, Hirokazu Miyashita, Yoichi Sugiyama, Tommi Vähäsilta, Teemu Laakso, Sebastian Dahlbacka, Tiina Vainikka, Juho Viikilä, Mika Laine, Mikko Jalanko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAsymmetric underexpansion of transcatheter heart valves (THVs), as observed on fluoroscopy, may influence prosthesis function or long‐term durability of transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI).AimsThis study aimed to evaluate the effect of stent frame asymmetry on hemodynamic performance and clinical outcomes in ACURATE neo and neo2 THVs.MethodsIn a retrospective registry, the TAVI asymmetry index was defined as the ratio of the THV stent frame diameter. Patients were divided into three groups based on this ratio: a tubular shape, TAVI asymmetry index < 10% (group 1); a tapered shape, a bottom diameter at least 10% greater than the upper diameter (group 2); and a flared shape, an upper diameter at least 10% greater than the bottom diameter (group 3). Impaired THV hemodynamic performance was defined as a mean residual THV gradient ≥ 20 mmHg and/or at least moderate paravalvular regurgitation.ResultsFrom December 2015 to 2023, 585 patients were included (group 1, n = 447; group 2, n = 96; and group 3, n = 47). Compared to group 1, groups 2 and 3 exhibited a higher incidence of impaired THV hemodynamic performance (4.9% vs. 17.5% vs. 11.9%; p < 0.001). There was no significant difference in all‐cause mortality or heart failure hospitalization at 3 years among the three groups (p = 0.787). In multivariable analysis, stent frame asymmetry (groups 2 and 3) was an independent predictor of impaired THV hemodynamic performance.ConclusionsStent frame asymmetry of ACURATE neo and neo2 was associated with impaired THV hemodynamic performance but not with clinical outcomes.Keywords: ACURATE neo, ACURATE neo2, bioprosthetic valve dysfunction, fluoroscopic images, stent frame asymmetry, transcatheter aortic valve implantation

Introduction

Underexpansion of the transcatheter heart valve (THV) prosthesis following transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is associated with worse hemodynamics, which potentially affects THV durability and long‐term clinical outcomes [1, 10]. THV underexpansion can result from various factors, including anatomical characteristics such as bicuspid anatomy and heavy calcification of the aortic valve. Procedural issues can also lead to THV underexpansion, for example, improper valve sizing due to borderline anatomy or inadequate balloon dilatation before or after THV implantation. These factors can contribute to stent frame deformation and asymmetric THV expansion, leading to an increase in blood stasis, flow stagnation, or shear stress on the prosthesis leaflets, and promoting the occurrence of hypoattenuating leaflet thickening (HALT) [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Therefore, ensuring optimal THV expansion is essential for achieving long‐term favorable hemodynamic performance and durability after TAVI.

Compared with postprocedural CT analysis, stent frame asymmetry in THVs can be immediately detected on fluoroscopy after THV implantation by identifying an asymmetric expanded stent frame. In balloon‐expandable THVs used for native aortic stenosis, asymmetric expansion has been shown as an excellent predictor of impaired hemodynamic valve performance [6]. As with the balloon‐expandable THVs, asymmetric expansion after implantation of self‐expandable THVs can also be detected on fluoroscopy. However, the effect of stent frame asymmetry on hemodynamic and clinical outcomes remains uncertain.

The aim of this study was to evaluate and compare the hemodynamic and clinical outcomes associated with fluoroscopy‐based stent frame asymmetry in the self‐expandable ACURATE neo and neo2 THVs (Boston Scientific). Additionally, it examined predictors of THV geometry in groups with and without stent frame asymmetry.

Methods

Study Population

This is a single‐center retrospective study. The present study enrolled consecutive patients who underwent TAVI for native severe aortic stenosis with self‐expandable ACURATE neo and neo2 THVs in Helsinki University Central Hospital. We excluded patients with: (1) transcatheter aortic valve (TAV)‐in‐TAV or TAV‐in‐surgical aortic valve; (2) unachievable TAVI procedure (delivery failure, or no implantation); (3) poorly visualized TAVI stent frame on fluoroscopic images; (4) missing data at discharge (including echocardiographic data); or (5) conversion to emergency surgical aortic valve replacement. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of Helsinki University Central Hospital, and informed patient consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study (Central Illustration 1).

Definition of TAVI Asymmetry Index

All patients underwent preprocedural multislice cardiac computed tomography (CCT) to assess aortic valve anatomy and suitability for treatment. Based on the CCT assessment, the fluoroscopic three‐cusp coplanar view could be determined. For each case, the final fluoroscopic image (after post‐dilatation if performed) from the three cusp‐coplanar view with the THV stent frame aligned parallel was used to measure the upper frame diameter (Node 2, bottom of upper crown) and the bottom frame diameter (Node 0) (Figure 1). The TAVI asymmetry index was defined as the ratio of diameters of the THV: TAVI asymmetry index (%) = ([longer frame diameter/shorter frame diameter]−1) × 100. Patients were classified into three groups on the basis of the shape of the THV: a tubular shape (group 1), a tapered shape (group 2), and a flared shape (group 3). All patients in a tapered shape had a bottom length at least 10% greater than the upper diameter, with a TAVI asymmetry index ≥ 10%. Patients in a flared shape had an upper diameter at least 10% greater than the bottom diameter, with a TAVI asymmetry index ≥ 10%. Patients in a tubular shape had a TAVI asymmetry index < 10%.

Data Collection and Endpoints

Baseline clinical, procedural, and follow‐up data were prospectively recorded in a database maintained by Helsinki University Hospital. Follow‐up data were obtained from referring physicians and the hospital medical record system.

Impaired THV hemodynamic performance was defined using the Valve Academic Research Consortium 3 definitions, as moderate or greater paravalvular regurgitation (PVR) and/or a residual mean THV echocardiographic gradient ≥ 20 mmHg [7]. Hemodynamic valve deterioration was defined according to Valve Academic Research Consortium 3 definitions (Supporting Information Methods) [7].

Preprocedural computed tomography (CT) imaging was performed using a dual‐source 128‐row multislice CT system (Somaton Definition Flash, Siemens Healthcare). Acquired images were transferred to and evaluated by a dedicated workstation (3mensio Structural Heart, 3mensio Medical Imaging BV). Aortic valvular complex and left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) calcium volume were quantified in contrast‐enhanced images using a predefined Hounsfield unit threshold of 850 as previously described [8]. The Agatston methodology uses a computed tomographic attenuation threshold of 130 HU to define areas of calcium from noncontrast computed tomographic scans. Aortic annular ellipticity was assessed on CT in a transverse double oblique plane at its widest dimensions and was defined as: 100 × (minimum diameter/maximum diameter) [9].

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were described as absolute numbers with relative percentages and compared using the χ 2 or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were expressed as mean (±SD) or median (interquartile range) and compared using the Student t‐test or the Mann−Whitney U test depending on the distribution of the variables. In addition, one‐way ANOVA or the Kruskal−Wallis test was applied to test for three groups. The cumulative incidence of clinical events was estimated using the Kaplan−Meier method, and differences were assessed using the log‐rank test. Correlations were examined with a calculation of Pearson's coefficients. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. The association between clinical variables and impaired hemodynamic valve performance was assessed using univariable and multivariable logistic regression analysis. Logistic regression analyses were conducted to calculate odds ratios, along with their corresponding 95% CIs. Variables with p < 0.20 on univariate analysis and a type of stent frame shape were selected for the multivariate model. All analyses were carried out using SPSS version 29 (IBM, Armonk, NY).

Results

Baseline Patient Characteristics

Out of 603 consecutive patients who underwent TAVI with ACURATE neo or neo2 THVs between December 2015 and 2023, 585 were included in the present analysis. The mean age of the study population was 81.3 ± 6.1 years, the mean Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) Predicted Risk of Mortality (PROM) score was 4.0% ± 2.8%, and 430 patients (73.5%) were women. The aortic valve anatomy was bicuspid in 49 patients (8.4%). Figure 2 shows the study flowchart. There were 447 patients (76.4%) in group 1 (Tubular type), 96 patients (16.4%) in group 2 (Tapered shape), and 42 patients (7.2%) in group 3 (Flared shape). Baseline patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Compared with group 1 and group 3, group 2 had higher baseline transvalvular gradient (mean gradient 43.8 vs. 48.9 vs. 40.7 mmHg; p = 0.006), more aortic valve calcium on CT (306.2 ± 276.9 vs. 370.4 ± 403.6 vs. 247.5 ± 165.1 mm3; p = 0.010), and higher rates of bicuspid anatomy (7.4% vs. 14.6% vs. 4.9%; p = 0.049).

Procedural Results and the Hemodynamic Performance

All patients underwent TAVI via transfemoral access. Technical success was comparable in patients with stent frame shapes (Table 2). There was no significant difference in pre‐ and post‐dilatation rate (98.9% vs. 99.0% vs. 100%; p = 0.790; 8.9% vs. 9.4% vs. 16.0%; p = 0.242; respectively). The successful valve implantation rate was same between the three groups. Compared with group 1, group 2 had lower depth of implantation, and group 3 had higher depth of implantation (4.13 ± 1.58 vs. 4.45 ± 1.34 vs. 2.09 ± 2.53; p < 0.001). The median duration of hospital stay after procedure was 1 (1−3 days) day. Compared with group 1, impaired THV hemodynamic performance was more prevalent in group 2 and group 3 (4.9% vs. 17.5% vs. 11.9%, p < 0.001). There was no difference in all‐cause mortality or cardiovascular mortality at 30 days between groups. Clinical events such as stroke, acute kidney injury, permanent pacemaker implantation, vascular closure device success, and major vascular events were similar between the three groups. Compared with group 1 and group 3, group 2 had a higher rate of new‐onset left bundle branch block (5.1% vs. 16.7% vs. 2.4%; p < 0.001).

Clinical Outcomes and Follow‐Up Results

The 1‐year and long‐term follow‐up results are shown in Table 3 and Supporting Information S1: Table 2. The incidence of all‐cause death or heart failure hospitalization at 1 year were similar in the three groups (10.5% vs. 11.5% vs. 11.9%; p = 0.936). Group 2 and group 3 had significantly higher mean transvalvular gradient at 1 year than group 1 (6.7 ± 3.2 vs. 10.7 ± 4.5 vs. 8.2 ± 7.0 mmHg; p < 0.001). The prevalence of moderate or severe PVR and valve deterioration was similar in the three groups (2.4% vs. 3.7% vs. 0%; p = 0.527). The median long‐term follow‐up duration for surviving patients was 1255 (748−2061) days. There was no significant difference in the incidence of death and re‐intervention after TAVI in long‐term follow‐up between three groups. The incidence of clinical outcomes at 3 years of the three groups are demonstrated in Figure 3. There was no significant difference in all‐cause of mortality, heart failure hospitalization, and composite outcomes between three groups (p = 0.645; p = 0.064; p = 0.787; respectively). Compared with the tubular group, a combined asymmetric shape group (tapered and flared groups) had significantly higher mean transvalvular gradient at 1 year (7.3 ± 3.6 vs. 9.3 ± 4.6; p < 0.001) (Supporting Information S1: Table 2).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the outcomes associated with fluoroscopy‐based THV stent frame asymmetry and hemodynamic performance in patients with ACURATE neo and neo2 THVs. The major findings of this study are given as follows: (1) The rates of tubular, tapered, and flared shape implantation were identified as 76.4%, 16.4%, and 7.2%, respectively. (2) The asymmetric prosthesis frame deformation was associated with impaired hemodynamic valve performance, independent of bicuspid anatomy, aortic valve calcium, and size of THV. (3) There was no association between asymmetric prosthesis frame deformation and mortality up to 3 years of follow‐up.

Evaluation of Underexpansion Based on Fluoroscopy

THV underexpansion is increasingly recognized as important as it is associated with THV dysfunction and degeneration, with mechanisms like pinwheeling of the leaflets and increased incidence of HALT with possible long‐term durability [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 10]. Post‐TAVI CT imaging remains the gold standard for assessing THV expansion. However, it may involve additional costs and delays in detecting underexpansion, potentially postponing necessary corrective procedures. In contrast, fluoroscopy‐based assessment can be performed immediately after THV implantation, allowing for timely additional interventions, such as post‐dilatation, during the same session. Thus, being able to promptly recognize underexpansion at the time of the procedure is crucial for ensuring optimal long‐term durability without delay. In balloon‐expandable THVs, the commissural post height method detected on fluoroscopy showed an excellent correlation with CT for the assessment of THV expansion [11]. Mazynyczka et al. reported increased TAVI asymmetry index in 17% of the patients with balloon‐expandable THVs and was associated with impaired hemodynamic valve performance but not with clinical outcomes [6]. In our study, using a similar method to measure asymmetry index, approximately one‐quarter of patients exhibited asymmetric stent frame THV implantation, which was associated with impaired hemodynamic valve performance in patients with ACURATE neo and neo2 THVs.

ACURATE THVs Characteristics, Calcium, and Hemodynamic Performance

The ACURATE neo and neo2 THVs consist of a self‐expanding nitinol frame with porcine pericardium leaflets in the supra‐annular position and stabilization arches with an open‐cell geometry. Unlike surgical aortic valve replacement, where the calcified leaflets are removed, THVs are implanted within native calcified leaflets, which may affect THV configuration. The extent of THV landing zone calcification and extensive leaflet calcification is associated with prosthesis underexpansion, malposition of the frame, and higher rates of PVR [12, 13]. In bicuspid anatomy, THV deformation, as well as annular and LVOT calcification has been shown to correlate with a higher rate of all‐cause mortality at 3 years [14]. In our study, severe aortic valve calcification and bicuspid anatomy was more prevalent in patients with tapered shape. These findings suggest the importance of a thorough preprocedural evaluation of annular and aortic root calcification for predicting THV underexpansion and hemodynamic outcomes.

Implantation Depth and Hemodynamic Performance

In our study, a deeper implantation depth in patients with tapered‐shaped implantation was related to a higher asymmetry index. Furthermore, the incidence of the new‐onset left bundle branch block was higher in these patients. On the other hand, patients with flared‐shaped implantation demonstrated a higher implantation depth. With the ACURATE neo and neo2 THVs, deeper implantation depth of THV has been linked to the occurrence of HALT, increased risk of PVR, and new‐onset conduction disturbance [15, 16, 17]. Our findings underscore that optimizing implantation depth may help to improve hemodynamic outcomes.

Long Term Outcomes Associated With Asymmetric Deformation

We found that asymmetric expansion of the THV was associated with impaired hemodynamic valve performance. Patients with fluoroscopy‐based stent frame asymmetry had a higher risk for increased residual transvalvular gradients. There is a complex nonlinear relationship between transvalvular gradient after TAVI and mortality, which is dependent on left ventricular ejection fraction and different flow patterns [18]. Even mild PVR seems to be associated with an increased risk of mortality at 5 years after TAVI [19]. In our study, the incidence of death and heart failure hospitalization up to 3 years of follow‐up were no significantly different. There was a trend to a higher rate of aortic valve reintervention after TAVI in patients with asymmetric implantation during long‐term follow‐up, although the difference was not statistically significant.

Future Directions

Our findings may have clinical implications for THV deployment strategies to mitigate stent frame deformation. Indeed, self‐expandable prostheses are shown to exhibit more eccentric and underexpanded morphologies compared with balloon‐expandable THVs [20]. Post‐dilatation may serve as a crucial option to acutely optimize the prosthesis geometry, which can reduce relevant PVR [21]. In our study, post‐dilatation improved the stent frame asymmetry. However, the benefit of post‐dilatation should be carefully weighed against potential procedural risk such as prosthetic leaflet damage and annular rupture. The next generation ACURATE Prime valve, offering a higher radial force design, may facilitate more optimal deployment and reduce the risk of stent frame deformation.

Study Limitations

Our findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, this study was a nonrandomized single‐center retrospective and observational study. Second, the sample size was small, and larger cohorts are needed to confirm these findings. Third, our study lacked a core laboratory for imaging analysis. Fourth, stent frame asymmetry was not assessed in multiple different views. This may have led to an underestimation of THV underexpansion because THVs have three‐dimensional structures. Fifth, CT was performed only before TAVI. CT analysis after TAVI remains the gold standard method as it provides a thorough evaluation of the THV, including the hemodynamic consequences of stent frame deformation [22]. Sixth, our study lacked data of commissural alignment. The presence of commissural misalignment has been associated with a higher incidence of HALT and with a higher transvalvular gradient increase after TAVI. Seventh, there was an underrepresentation of men in our cohort, which might introduce a degree of selection bias. The individual valve choice in each patient was based on the operating heart team's discussion at the time of the procedure. Finally, the number of patients who had long‐term follow‐up was limited. Long‐term outcomes are still uncertain and should be the subject of future research.

Conclusions

This study highlights that fluoroscopy‐based stent frame asymmetry of ACURATE neo and neo2 THVs for native aortic valve stenosis was associated with impaired hemodynamic valve performance. Procedural strategies, such as optimal implantation technique and post‐dilatation, may help to minimize asymmetric underexpansion. Careful follow‐up may be warranted in patients exhibiting stent frame asymmetry after TAVI using ACURATE neo and neo2 THVs.
