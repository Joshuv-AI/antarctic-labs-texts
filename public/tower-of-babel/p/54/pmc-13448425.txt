Circulating micronutrients as protective factors for the retinal structure and vasculature: Mendelian randomization analyses
Authors: Linxin Wei, Meiqian He, Qing Zhao, Xinyu Zhao, Youxin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpidemiological data and clinical trials have suggested that micronutrients may preserve retinal integrity by mitigating neurodegenerative alterations and microvascular damage. To strengthen the genetic evidence, we investigated the causal effects of various circulating micronutrients (CMs) on retinal imaging parameters using Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses.MethodsTwo-sample MR analyses utilized genetic variants as instrumental variables to investigate the effects of 14 CMs on retinal structural parameters including ganglion cell inner plexiform layer (GCIPL) thickness, outer nuclear layer thickness, and retinal nerve fiber layer thickness, as well as retinal vascular parameters comprising vascular density (VD) and fractal dimension (FD). Inverse variance weighted model was employed as the primary analytical method, with 5 sensitivity methods supplemented to confirm the results' robustness.ResultsGenetic predisposition to higher circulating folate levels was robustly associated with increased macular GCIPL thickness (β=0.94, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.17–1.70, P=0.017), which remained significant (P=0.039) after MRlap correction. While nominal associations were observed between circulating vitamin B12 and vitamin E levels with both VD (vitamin B12: β=0.33, 95% CI: 0.09–0.56, P=0.007; vitamin E: β=0.19, 95% CI: 0.04–0.34, P=0.016) and FD (vitamin B12: β=0.24, 95% CI: 0.05−0.42, P=0.012; vitamin E: β=0.18, 95% CI: 0.04−0.33, P=0.016), these findings warranted a cautious interpretation due to inconsistencies across sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsOur MR analyses evaluate the complex relationship between CMs and retinal imaging features, suggesting the protective and thus potentially therapeutic effects of folate in maintaining GCIPL thickness. Conversely, the potential associations observed for other micronutrients remain tentative and warrant cautious interpretation and further empirical validation.

Introduction

Micronutrients, encompassing water- and fat-soluble vitamins and trace minerals, have been widely recognized of great significance for human health and survival.1 While vitamin and mineral supplements are commonly utilized to address micronutrient deficiencies and ensure adequate intakes, over-the-counter supplementation is also being investigated in randomized controlled trials for its potential therapeutic effects at pharmacological doses.2 In the field of ophthalmology, emerging clinical studies suggest that circulating micronutrients (CMs) may exert protective effects against retinal disorders and visual impairments. For instance, the Age-Related Eye Disease Study (AREDS) demonstrated that supplementation with 500 mg vitamin C, 400 IU vitamin E, and 15 mg β-carotene significantly slowed the progression of non-central geographic atrophy lesions towards the central macula.3 Additionally, oral nicotinamide (vitamin B3) supplementation has been observed to improve inner retinal function in glaucoma patients, as assessed by photopic negative response parameters.4

The antioxidant properties of CMs are considered a primary mechanism underlying their protective effects on the posterior segment of the eye. The retina and optic nerve, characterized by high metabolic rates and oxygen consumption, are susceptible to oxidative damage due to elevated production of reactive oxygen species.5 Coupled with low antioxidant enzyme levels and abundant oxidizable structures, these tissues are particularly vulnerable to oxidative damage. And this is implicated in the pathophysiology of various ocular diseases including diabetic retinopathy (DR), age-related macular degeneration (AMD), retinal vessel occlusion, and glaucoma.6 Preclinical studies, epidemiological data, and intervention have enriched the evidence of various micronutrients' potential in preserving the retinal integrity. This may function through maintaining vascular homeostasis, improving glucose and lipid metabolism, and bolstering antioxidant defenses.7, 8, 9 However, prior conventional studies are limited in establishing causality due to residual or unmeasured confounding factors.10 Furthermore, chronic degenerative disorders like AMD and glaucoma often exhibit a prolonged latency between exposure and clinical manifestation, complicating the analysis on the relationship between factors of interest and disease occurrence or progression.11 Therefore, the causal effect of CM levels in the setting of retinal pathologies remains insufficiently illustrated.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a technique exploiting the random allocation of genetic variants at conception to assess the causal effect of an exposure on an outcome, robust to the influence of environmental confounding.12 Previous MR studies utilizing multiple genetic variants have evaluated the causal effects of certain micronutrients on the risk of retinal diseases.13 In this study, we employed MR analyses along with publicly available data to investigate the potential causal roles of 14 micronutrients on retinal structural and microvascular parameters, as measured by fundus photography and optical coherence tomography (OCT). We aimed to provide comprehensive evidence on the associations between CMs and retinal imaging alterations beyond certain confirmed disease diagnoses.

Methods

Study design

We conducted two-sample MR and sensitivity analyses to evaluate the potential causal effects of various CMs on retinal imaging parameters. The MR design is based on three key assumptions that instrumental variants (IVs) (1) are robustly associated with the exposure, (2) influence the outcome exclusively through the exposure, and (3) are independent of potential confounders.14 A flowchart illustrating the study design is shown in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Schematic overview of the study design. Data sources and genetic instruments selection

Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) summary statistics for 14 CMs were obtained from the IEU Open GWAS Project (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). IV sets for each micronutrient were defined according to the criteria outlined by Cao et al.15 Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were selected as IVs if they met the following conditions: (1) a GWAS-correlated P value threshold of P<5 × 10−6; (2) a clumping window larger than 10000 kb and a r2 cutoff of 0.001, and (3) an F-statistic >10 to eliminate linkage disequilibrium and remove weak IVs.

Outcome GWAS data for retinal layer thickness, including outer nuclear layer (ONL) thickness, retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness, and ganglion cell inner plexiform layer (GCIPL) thickness (unit: μm) were sourced from previously published UK Biobank studies.16,17 OCT imaging was conducted under standardized conditions using a 3-dimensional macular volume scan protocol (6 × 6 mm2, 512 A-scans per B scan; 128 horizontal B-scans in a raster pattern), and automated segmentation of retinal layers was performed using the Topcon Advanced Boundary Segmentation algorithm. The associations between SNPs and retinal thickness metrics were assessed using an additive linear regression model, adjusting for eye-specific, demographic, and technical covariates.

GWAS data for retinal microvasculature were derived from Zekavat et al.'s GWAS study,18 which analyzed 97895 fundus images from 54813 UK Biobank participants. Researchers adjusted the age, age squared, sex, smoking status, and ancestry, and developed a deep learning model of vascular segmentation and feature extraction. The branching complexity of retinal microvasculature was quantitatively described by vascular density (VD) and fractal dimension (FD, unit: standard deviation, SD). VD referred to the total number of pixels in the segmented vessels given a fixed dimension of 320 × 320 pixels for each image, while FD was calculated using a box-counting method.

MR analyses

Two-sample MR

Five MR methods were used for the MR analysis, including inverse variance weighting (IVW), MR-Egger regression, weighted median methods, Simple Mode, and Weighted Model.19 The IVW method served as the primary analytical approach, since it allows for overdispersion, aggregates the Wald ratio for each SNP with the outcome, and yields an overall estimate of causality,20 while the supplementary methods verify the results' robustness. The random-effect IVW model was applied if heterogeneity was present, otherwise, the fixed-effect IVW model was used.21 This approach was supplemented by visual tools, including scatter plots, forest plots, and funnel plots.19,22 Effect estimates were reported as β-coefficients with corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI).

Horizontal pleiotropy and heterogeneity were assessed using MR-Egger intercepts23 and Cochran's Q statistics,24 respectively. Leave-one-out analysis was conducted to test the potential influence of each IV on the overall results by sequentially excluding each SNP at a time.25 The MR Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) method (NbDistribution=1000) was also used to detect and correct for outliers.26 A multiplicative random effects IVW model was adopted at the presence of heterogeneity, and if pleiotropy existed, the whole MR result was discarded. For all analyses, results were considered significant at P<0.05.

MRlap analysis

Given that both the exposure and outcome GWAS datasets were largely derived from the UK Biobank cohort, potential sample overlap cannot be ruled out. To mitigate potential bias arising from this overlap, particularly in the absence of direct quantification, we applied the MRlap correction method (threshold=5 × 10−6, pruning distribution=10000, pruning linkage disequilibrium=0.001) to refine the IVW estimates.20,27 If the MRlap-corrected effects align closely with the observed effects and exhibit no significant disparity, it is reasonable to place confidence in the inverse-variance weighted-MR estimates. Conversely, if a substantial discrepancy is evident, the corrected estimates should be prioritized, as they inherently control for bias introduced by sample overlap.

All statistical analyses were performed using "TwoSampleMR", "MR-PRESSO", and "MRlap" packages in R software (version 4.4.3).

Causal effects on GCIPL thickness

The IVW analysis revealed a positive correlation between genetically predicted circulating folate level and GCIPL thickness (β=0.936, 95% CI: 0.169−1.704, P=0.017; Fig. 4). This association remained consistent across multiple MR methods. No outliers were detected using MR-PRESSO (P=0.795), and sensitivity analyses indicated no evidence of heterogeneity (Q_pval=0.691) or horizontal pleiotropy (MR-Egger intercept=0.0172, P=0.649; Table 2). After correction for potential sample overlap using MRlap, the estimated effect of folate level on GCIPL thickness was attenuated (corrected β=0.524), yet it remained statistically significant (P=0.039; Table 3). Causal effects on RNFL thickness

MR analysis demonstrated a negative correlation between genetically predicted circulating vitamin D and RNFL thickness (β=−1.084, 95% CI: −2.093–−0.075, P=0.035; Supplementary Fig. 1). MR-PRESSO detected no outliers (P=0.590), and sensitivity analyses revealed no evidence of heterogeneity (Q_pval=0.621) or horizontal pleiotropy (MR-Egger intercept=0.0933, P=0.235; Table 2). However, the corrected MRlap results differed substantially from the initial IVW findings and were no longer statistically significant (Table 3), indicating that the primary IVW result may have been biased due to sample overlap. This finding should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Causal effects on ONL thickness

No significant causal associations were identified between any CMs level and ONL thickness in IVW analysis. MR-PRESSO analysis identified outlier SNPs for zinc concerning ONL thickness; however, the corrected results remained insignificant (P=0.084). MRlap-corrected results were consistent with those obtained from the primary IVW analyses, indicating minimal bias due to potential sample overlap and supporting robustness of the causal inference.

Causal effects on VD

Our primary MR analysis indicated that circulating vitamin B12 and vitamin E levels were positively associated with VD (vitamin B12: β=0.325, 95% CI: 0.088−0.562, P=0.007; vitamin E: β=0.186, 95% CI: 0.035−0.338, P=0.016; Supplementary Fig. 2, 3). No outliers were identified by MR-PRESSO (P=0.203 for vitamin B12; P=0.452 for vitamin E), and no significant pleiotropy or heterogeneity was detected (Table 2). However, the MR-Egger estimates indicated a negative direction of effect for vitamin E, contrasting with other analytical methods. Furthermore, MRlap correction suggested inconsistency in vitamin B12 findings compared to the primary MR analysis (Table 3), highlighting possible biases from sample overlap. Consequently, the positive associations observed for vitamin B12 and vitamin E with VD should be interpreted with caution due to potential biases.

Causal effects on FD

MR analyses indicated that higher circulating vitamin B12 and vitamin E levels were positively associated with FD (vitamin B12: β=0.238, 95% CI: 0.052−0.423, P=0.012; vitamin E: β=0.183, 95% CI: 0.035−0.332, P=0.016; Supplementary Fig. 4, 5). MR-PRESSO analysis revealed no outlier SNPs for vitamin B12 (P=0.893) or vitamin E (P=0.599), and sensitivity analyses demonstrated no heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy (Table 2). However, direction of the vitamin E effect was inconsistent across methods, with MR-Egger showing a negative effect contrary to other analyses. Moreover, MRlap analyses yielded inconsistent results for vitamin B12 compared to primary MR findings (Table 3), suggesting potential biases arising from sample overlap. Thus, the positive correlations of vitamin B12 and vitamin E with FD warrant cautious interpretation.

Discussion

Investigating the roles of micronutrients in retinal structure and microvasculature could contribute to developing more noninvasive strategies for preventing or managing retinal diseases through dietary interventions. In this study, MR analyses indicated that a 1-SD higher in circulating folate level was causally associated with a 0.936-μm increase in macular GCIPL thickness. In addition, we observed a potentially negative effect of circulating vitamin D on RNFL thickness, as well as positive effects of vitamin B12 and vitamin E levels on VD and FD, which however require more cautious interpretation. The present study provides the first genetic evidence supporting protective roles of several micronutrients in maintaining retinal structure and microvasculature, based on well-established imaging parameters.

The observed protective effect of B vitamins, especially folate, on the retinal integrity can be explained by their established biological functions. B vitamins are a group of essential water-soluble co-factors that regulate homocysteine conversion, mitochondrial free radical quenching, and myelin synthesis.46 Previous findings have revealed that their deficiency could significantly reduce DNA methylation, leading to epigenome-dependent alterations in the disease-related gene expression.47 Specifically, both in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that folate deficiency might induce chromosomal breaks, uracil misincorporation into DNA, micronucleus formation, and mitochondrial DNA deletions.48 These manifestations of genomic instability could contribute to the pathogenesis of retinal disorders such as DR.49 Secondly, folate also exerts protective effects by alleviating hyperhomocysteinemia. Accumulated homocysteine promotes retinal pathologies through increased oxidative stress, upregulation of inflammatory mediators, apoptosis of retinal ganglion cells, extracellular matrix remodeling, and impairment of the blood–retinal barrier (BRB).50,51 Clinical evidence has linked elevated circulating or ocular homocysteine levels to DR and primary open-angle glaucoma incidence.52,53 Moreover, an OCT-based study also reported that each unit increase in plasma homocysteine was associated with a 0.0884-μm decrease in the average GCIPL thickness.54 Thirdly, experimental findings suggested that folate could maintain microvascular homeostasis through homocysteine-independent pathways.55,56 Folic acid deficiency was observed to exacerbate aberrant retinal vessel growth, whereas supplementation suppressed it—potentially via downregulation of HIF-1α/EPO signaling primarily in Müller glial cells.55

Regarding the protective effects of vitamin D supported by prior human-level studies, in vitro studies also demonstrated that its supplementation could mitigate oxidative, inflammatory, and hyperoxic conditions of retinal cells.57, 58, 59 Prior cross-sectional imaging research also supported its beneficial effects, linking vitamin D deficiency to thinner nasal superior peripapillary RNFL thickness and vascular dysfunction signs in pediatric subjects.60 However, our MR findings did not establish a causal protective effect; instead, they suggested an association between elevated circulating vitamin D levels and RNFL thinning. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. First, systemic concentrations may not reliably reflect local vitamin D levels within the ocular microenvironment. Previous studies reported that eyes with diabetic macular edema exhibited elevated levels of vitamin D in the aqueous humor, despite no significant difference in serum vitamin D levels compared to healthy controls.61 Second, vitamin D might exert differential effects on retinal structure depending on the pathological context. For instance, higher serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels have been found to correlate with an increased risk of early AMD but a decreased risk of late AMD,62 suggesting that its role might vary across disease stages. Such complexity may contribute to the null or unexpected findings in large-scale MR studies. Therefore, given the established biological plausibility and observational evidence, it's still necessary to emphasize the role of maintaining adequate amounts of circulating vitamin D in promoting retinal health.

In the field of micronutrient research, MR analyses offers unique advantages by enabling the assessment of causal effects for individual micronutrients, rather than evaluating the combined effects of supplement mixtures as in clinical trials. Previous analyses using data from diverse GWAS sources have reported several noteworthy associations: (1) Elevated magnesium or calcium levels were associated with a reduced risk of AMD.13,15 (2) A circulating vitamin C metabolite may exhibit a substantial intraocular pressure-lowering effect.63 (3) Vitamins B6 and E were significantly correlated with a reduced risk of proliferative DR.39 These genetic findings shed light on the pathogenesis of retinal diseases and meanwhile guided the development of targeted nutritional interventions. However, replacing the disease status with imaging parameters as the outcome of CM exposure may supplement other valuable information. On one hand, retinal imaging measurements are increasingly recognized as valuable tools for risk stratification, treatment response assessment, and prognosis prediction beyond simple diagnosis of retinal diseases.64 On the other hand, pre-clinical retinal alterations occurring prior to clinical diagnosis also constitute a noteworthy aspect of retinal health potentially affected by CMs. And these early signs might be already visible under the progressively advanced imaging techniques. For example, individuals with early-stage AMD have demonstrated measurable structural alterations, such as thinned RNFL, GCIPL, and ONL, compared to healthy controls.65 Similarly, diabetic eyes without a DR diagnosis already had detectable morphological changes on fundus photography, including reduced vessel area rates and decreased vessel tortuosity.66

Considering the insignificant results observed in our studies, the following reasons may provide a partial explanation. Firstly, excessive levels of certain micronutrients may exert harmful effects that attenuate or even counteract their retinal protective properties. For instance, iron is among the most abundant metals in the retina, maintaining normal retinal function and morphology via participating in oxygen transport, energy production, and catalysis of metabolic events.67 However, based on the findings of localized iron overload in the retina of AMD, DR, RP patients, the damaging roles of iron and iron-related proteins in the incidence of retinal degenerative diseases have recently attracted much attention.68 Secondly, the limited scope of available GWAS data constrains our ability to investigate the effects of diverse forms of transport, distribution, and metabolism for each micronutrient in MR analyses. Serum concentrations of trace minerals reflecting the circulating levels of free cations can fluctuate physiologically. In contrast, simultaneous assessment of the storage or transport proteins would offer a comprehensive picture of the overall homeostasis. For vitamins, there might exist differential mechanisms and biological efficacy among various metabolite components, such as the differences of diverse types of vitamin E regarding their effects of antioxidant cytoprotection.69 Thirdly, due to the selective permeability of BRB, circulating levels of micronutrients may not accurately reflect intraocular concentrations. Consequently, future MR studies should move beyond systemic metrics by utilizing ocular tissue-specific variables, such as retinal expression quantitative trait loci or by employing interaction analyses to attain more precise and mechanistically grounded causal inferences.

Several limitations should be considered for the present study. Firstly, while the IVW method was employed as the primary analytical approach, it may be susceptible to bias in the presence of horizontal pleiotropy. To address this, we incorporated MR-Egger regression, which accounts for directional pleiotropy and provides complementary insights. Consistency in the direction of effect estimates across various MR methods was emphasized to enhance the robustness of our conclusions. Secondly, the population included in this study was predominantly of European ancestry. This relatively homogeneous background could minimize potential biases arising from population stratification and enhance the internal validity of our causal estimates; however, we acknowledge it is challenging to achieve perfectly matched populations across all databases, even within a single ancestry group. Besides, given that genetic polymorphisms influencing micronutrient metabolism can vary across different genetic backgrounds, additional studies in other racial groups are necessary to verify the applicability of the findings in this study. Thirdly, the relationship between CMs and retinal parameters may vary across different disease stages and distinct background diseases. Therefore, our results should be interpreted and applied with caution.

Conclusions

This Mendelian randomization study provides genetic evidence supporting a causal association between genetically predicted higher circulating folate levels and increased GCIPL thickness. These findings highlight a protective role and therapeutic potential of folate in maintaining retinal structural integrity. While our results also hint at potential retinal microvascular benefits from vitamin B12 and E, these associations were sensitive to methodological variations and therefore remained suggestive until confirmed by future research. Independent replication in ethnically diverse populations and across a spectrum of disease severity is essential to substantiate these observations and evaluate their translational relevance to clinical practice.
