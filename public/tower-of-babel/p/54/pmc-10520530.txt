Hepatic subcapsular hematoma following endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography. Report of two cases
Authors: Jorge Sotelo-Carbajal, René Edivaldo Hernández-Zamora, Neftali Elizabeth Montaño-García, Juan Antonio López-Prida, Eddy Fredy Acosta-Aguirre, Quitzia Libertad Torres-Salazar
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importanceHepatic subcapsular hematomas (HSH) are an extremely rare post-endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) complication. Mortality exhibits disparities depending on the integrity of the hepatic capsular envelope, with ruptured HSH being associated with higher case fatality rates (2.2 % compared to 21.4 %).Cases presentationTwo clinical cases are presented concerning a 20-year-old female patient and a 40-year-old male patient, who were diagnosed with choledocholithiasis and underwent ERCP procedures with the use of a wide-bore guidewire (WBG), with the undesired result of HSH as a complication. In both scenarios, a surgical approach strategy was chosen to manage this situation. The outcome turned out to be successful in the first case, in contrast to the unfortunate death of the patient in the second case.Clinical discussion and conclusionsConservative approaches prevail in the management of HSH, as they often present intact, resulting in a low mortality rate. However, surgical approaches are reserved for consideration in situations of hemodynamic instability that persists despite the conservative measures implemented.

Introduction

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is one of the most common diagnostic-therapeutic endoscopic procedures. Its overall complication rate stands at around 6.85 % with a morbidity of between 6.27 % and 7.51 % and a case-fatality rate of up to 21.4 % [1,2]. HSH is an uncommon but dreaded post-ERCP complication, until now, 67 cases have been reported worldwide and its mechanism is still unclear, so the identification of risk factors, patterns of presentation and successful treatment approaches may improve the safety and efficacy of medical procedures [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. In this case series, this study presents two cases of post-ERCP HSH, a procedure indicated by a diagnosis of choledocholithiasis, one ruptured and one non-ruptured, which occurred at the same center and in the same month. Patient characteristics, medical history, clinical presentation, diagnosis, treatment employed, and results will be discussed to increase the information available on HSH. The work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [10].

A bibliographic review of hepatic hematoma as a post-ERCP complication is also carried out with a search of cases reported in the last 20 years in PUBMED introducing the terms “hepatic haematoma after ERCP”, “hepatic haematoma and ERCP”, “ERCP complications” “case report”, “Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography and hepatic hematoma”.

Clinical case I

Female patient, 20 years old, who denies personal pathologic history. Her illness began 4 days ago, with abdominal pain in the right hypochondrium and jaundice, laboratory studies with total bilirubin 6.9 mg/dL, direct bilirubin 6.1 mg/dL, hemoglobin of 11. 3 g/dL, ultrasound of the liver and biliary tract reports numerous stones and a 2.5 mm gallbladder wall, common bile duct ectasia with a diameter of 7.3 mm secondary to a 4.5 × 4.3 mm lithium in the supraduodenal portion.

As a study protocol, ERCP was performed in a private environment with sphincterotomy and a hydrojag guide was inserted into the common bile duct to extract lithosclerosis, and an Amsterdam type plastic biliary prosthesis was applied. At 18 h after the procedure, she was hospitalized at a second level hospital and reported tachycardia of 140 beats per minute, blood pressure of 90/60 mmHg and lipotimia. Conservative management with intravenous crystalloid boluses based on Hartman's solution was started. An emergency laboratory was performed where metabolic acidosis was found with an elevated gap anion of 24 meq/L associated with lactate of 11.2. Hemoglobin of 8.2 g/dL, platelets of 299 thousand, serum creatinine of 2.12 and urea of 49. He has elevated transaminases at the rate of TGO 433 and TGP 861, amylase 310.

As the shock state did not remit, transfusion of blood product and vasoactive amines was started, maintaining mean arterial pressure (MAP) >65 mmHg. A thoracoabdominal computed axial tomography (CAT) scan was performed, showing the presence of scarce free perihepatic fluid and pelvic hollow, without the presence of free air (Fig. 1). Therefore, it was decided to continue expectant management with continuous monitoring, achieving perfusory MAP for 12 h.Fig. 1Simple CAT scan of the abdomen. Presence of perihepatic free fluid, segment VI and VII.Fig. 1

When hemodynamic status worsened, with BP 75/50 mmHg, it was decided to perform an emergency exploratory laparotomy (EX LAP). The finding was HSH with involvement of >50 % of the area with capsule rupture of approximately 2 cm in the right lobe (Fig. 2). There was 400 cc of free hematic fluid found in the right slider and pelvic hollow. Evacuation of hemoperitoneum and packing is performed.Fig. 2Subcapsular hematoma with a 2 cm opening in the hepatic border.Fig. 2

She was admitted to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) with a diagnosis of grade IV hemorrhagic shock associated with HSH grade III, acute liver failure and AKIN III acute kidney injury. In her first hours post-surgery, she presents bleeding through the surgical wound. She continues with vasopressors. 24 h later, a new EX LAP was performed due to refractory shock, where unpacking was performed, Pringle maneuver for 10 min, new packing was performed and placement of a bogotá bag, the findings were intraparenchymatous hepatic hematoma of 50 % of the right lobe with rupture of the hepatic capsule with active bleeding not localized the origin, hemoperitoneum of 1500 cc, edema of intestinal loops.

A third intervention was performed in the following 24 h due to hemodynamic instability, the patient was unpacked, bleeding was identified in the hepatic capsule with a 10 × 6 cm anterior hematoma (Fig. 3), 4 Lembert stitches were made in the liver with polyester suture 2. Post-surgery, the abdomen was closed and serohematic fluid was drained through the surgical wound. Cryoprecipitates, erythrocyte concentrates, platelets and fresh frozen plasma were transfused, and antifibrinolytic (tranexamic acid) and vitamin K were also administered during her stay in the ICU. Management with vasopressor amines, obtaining a MAP of 79 mmHg.Fig. 3Hepatic subcapsular hematoma with the presence of multiple clots on the capsular surface.Fig. 3

Hepatic subcapsular hematoma with the presence of multiple clots on the capsular surface.

At 21 days post-surgery, after remission of hemorrhage, acute liver failure, acute renal injury and hemodynamic stabilization, she was discharged from the hospital to her home.

Clinical case II

A 40-year-old male patient, with no relevant medical history, experienced symptoms one week before hospitalization. The symptoms consisted of pain in the upper central abdomen, with irradiation to the upper right flank region. The pain was related to the intake of medication to stimulate the gallbladder and was accompanied by a feeling of nausea. Blood test results showed an increased number of white blood cells, elevated blood glucose levels, as well as increased direct bilirubin levels (3.2 mg/dL). In addition, increased values of alkaline phosphatase, GGT (gamma-glutamyltransferase) and amylase (695 U/L) were observed. Abdominal ultrasound revealed a common bile duct with a diameter of 3.3 mm, portal vein with a caliber of 9 mm and a gallbladder of dimensions 73 × 49 × 30 mm with a wall thickness of 2 mm. A posterior sonic shadow measuring 6 mm to 10 mm was observed adjacent to a gallbladder lithiasis. The pancreas had a normal size and shape. An abdominal computed tomography (CAT) scan revealed a slightly enlarged pancreatic head with surrounding fat striae. The common bile duct showed hyperdense images in its intrapancreatic portion, with a diameter of 10 mm. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of acute pancreatitis of biliary origin was established. Following symptom improvement, an endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) was planned at a private medical center. The ERCP report showed the presence of an upper juxtaampullary diverticulum, a mild dilatation of the pancreatic duct at the head of the pancreas (4 mm) and difficulty in accessing the common bile duct with a hydrojag guide. A 5 Fr and 5 cm Amsterdam type plastic biliary prosthesis with airflow was inserted. A new procedure was recommended within 7 days (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Case 2, male, 40 years old. After the procedure was performed, the patient continued presenting jaundice and elevated bilirubin levels with a pattern suggestive of obstruction. In response to this, a cholangioresonance scan was performed, which revealed a distended gallbladder with homogeneous contents and a thin wall. Multiple defects were observed in the intrapancreatic portion of the common bile duct due to a well-defined, hypointense 6 mm round lesion. The pancreas maintained its thickness, morphology and signal intensity within normal limits, with no evidence of inflammatory changes. The suggested diagnosis was choledocholithiasis and vesicular lithiasis (Fig. 5).Fig. 5Clinical case 2. Subcapsular hematoma with capsule rupture at the inferior border of segments V and VI.Fig. 5

Subsequently, a second ERCP was performed 10 days after the previous one. The report of this second ERCP described the major papilla with an edematous and feathery appearance, with signs of changes due to the previous instrumentation and the presence of a functional pancreatic prosthesis. The selective insertion of a hydrojag guidewire was achieved, allowing the opacification of the discretely dilated common bile duct that harbored 3 calculi inside, with dimensions of 5–10 mm. A Soehendra type balloon passage was performed, with increased diameter from 12 to 15 mm, followed by dilatation of the bleb and extraction of a balloon containing 3 L, yellowish. Finally, an Amsterdam Wilson Cook type biliary prosthesis was applied to conclude the procedure.

24 h after the procedure, the patient presented paleness, excessive sweating and an elevated heart rate between 110 and 120 beats per minute, with a blood pressure of 90/60 mmHg. It was decided to perform an emergency computed tomography of the abdominal region, which revealed abdominal distension and stool retention in the colon (coprostasis). There was no evidence of air or free fluid in the abdominal cavity. Considering this situation, management with intravenous fluids, control of blood glucose levels and close monitoring of renal function was implemented to prevent acute kidney injury. Initially, the patient responded well to these measures, and an apparent improvement in his hemodynamic status was observed over the next 24 h.

48 h after the procedure, the patient began to show signs of low cardiac output, abdominal pain, muscle rigidity, irritation in the membrane lining the abdominal cavity, and the abdomen became very tense, acquiring a wood-like sensation to the touch. In addition, blood test results indicated a decrease in hemoglobin level from 12.0 g/dL to 8.2 g/dL. Considering this situation, it was decided to perform another CT scan, which revealed the presence of free fluid in the region around the liver (perihepatic), in the area around the spleen (perisplenic), in both abdominal corridors and in the pelvic cavity. The gallbladder was distended and contained stones in its interior. Given these findings, it was considered necessary to perform an emergency surgical intervention known as exploratory laparotomy.

During exploratory laparotomy, a hemorrhage in the coronary ligament of the liver and a 15 × 13 cm hepatic hematoma with active bleeding were identified. In addition, 1500 mL of blood was found in the abdominal cavity and 500 mL of clots. A series of measures were carried out to control the bleeding, including sutures and packing of the affected area with compresses. Despite these efforts, the patient continued presenting severe complications, including deterioration of the shock state, anuria (lack of urine production), hypothermia, metabolic acidosis and need for mechanical ventilation. Despite multiple blood transfusions and other treatments, the patient did not respond favorably and his condition gradually worsened. Inadvertently, he died within 24 h.

Discussion

ERCP stands out as one of the most prevalent diagnostic and therapeutic endoscopic procedures. The overall rate of complications associated with this procedure is around 6.85 %, with morbidity ranging between 6.27 % and 7.51 %, and a mortality rate of 0.33 % [1,2]. The most frequent complications, together with their respective incidences, include pancreatitis with 3.47 %, infections with 1.44 %, bleeding with 1.34 % and perforations with 0.6 %. In addition, other rare but relevant complications emerge, such as pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum and hepatic subcapsular hematoma. Within this spectrum, HSH emerges as a low frequency complication, but of consideration after ERCP [3].

Two possible mechanisms have been proposed to explain the appearance of hepatic subcapsular hematomas (HSH) as a result of endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP). One of them suggests that it could originate from the unintentional laceration or rupture of small intrahepatic blood vessels due to the contact of the tip of the guidewire used during ERCP [9]. The second proposed mechanism suggests that the traction generated by using a balloon in the bile duct to expel stones could cause rupture of the biliary ductules and blood vessels, thus triggering bleeding. This latter mechanism can explain the cases of HSH in which a guidewire was not used [1].

The categorization of MSM can be divided into non-ruptured and ruptured, with the latter having a higher mortality rate (21.4 %) compared to non-ruptured (2.2 %), although the incidence of non-ruptured is higher (76.3 % vs. 23.7 %) [1]. Among the most frequent signs and symptoms were abdominal pain (83.3 %), anemia (56.7 %), hypotension (28.3 %), fever (18.3 %) and right shoulder pain (13.3 %). Non-ruptured MSM tended to manifest later, with an average symptom onset time of 27 h (range 2 h to 15 days) after ERCP. Meanwhile, ruptured HSH show an average onset of clinical manifestations at 12 h (range 1 h to 96 h) after ERCP [1].

Regarding the treatments applied, it is observed that 39.3 % of the MSM were approached conservatively, involving continuous monitoring of hemodynamic status, administration of blood transfusions, hydration, follow-up of hemoglobin levels and liver function tests, as well as imaging studies to monitor the evolution of the hematoma and the use of antibiotics [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. Surgical intervention was chosen in 27.9 % of HSH cases, mainly due to failure to maintain hemodynamic stability or worsening clinical status despite conservative treatment. These surgeries usually comprised evacuation of the hematoma, hemostasis and packing of the affected area. In addition, in some cases, percutaneous drainage was applied (24.55 %) [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. In situations where the hematoma showed signs of septic necrosis, necrosectomy was performed [8].

In contrast to the surgical approach, a non-surgical method has been explored, specifically embolization of the bleeding site. Although this modality has been less common, its effectiveness has been evidenced in 9.8 % of HSH cases [20]. Even though HSH is an uncommon complication of ERCP, its high mortality rate relative to its frequency makes it a clinical issue of utmost importance. Given the limited number of reports globally, it is crucial to continue documenting cases of HSH through clinical case reports recording the clinical presentation, diagnosis employed, treatment strategies, and outcomes obtained.

Taking into account all the aforementioned considerations, we can conduct an analysis of the two presented cases, taking into consideration two pivotal factors that may have influenced the vital outcome of patients with hematoma. These factors encompass age as a variable, as the second case involves a 40-year-old male (representing a 20-year age difference compared to the first case, in which the patient survived). The second critical factor is the time interval between the performance of endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) and the subsequent laparotomy. It is worth noting that in the first case, the computed tomography (CT) scan and surgery were performed within approximately 30 h, while in the second case, the patient waited for >48 h after ERCP before undergoing laparotomy.

In this context, a review of the medical literature has been performed through the PubMed search engine, identifying 13 cases in the last 20 years, mostly approached with a conservative approach. The latter is referred to in this context as stabilization of the patient without resorting to surgical, endovascular or image-guided procedures, through the use of blood product transfusions, monitoring and medical management. Among the 23 reported cases, including the two cases presented here, four resulted in patient death.
