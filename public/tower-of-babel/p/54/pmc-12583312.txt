Congenital Hypothyroidism: Long‐Term Growth and Intellectual Outcomes With a Lower Initial Levothyroxine Dose
Authors: Jun‐Hong Park, Jae Hyuk Oh, Min Hyung Cho, Young Suk Shim, Hae Sang Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThis study aims to review the long‐term outcome of congenital hypothyroidism (CH) and whether patient‐ or treatment‐related factors impact the outcomes, especially focusing on the initial Levothyroxine dose.MethodsThis is a retrospective, cross‐sectional study of the children diagnosed with CH who received Levothyroxine at Ajou University Hospital between 2003 and 2024. Comparative analysis was performed between the low (<10 mcg/kg) and high (≥10 mcg/kg) initial dose groups. Repeated‐measures analysis of covariance was employed to evaluate longitudinal changes in growth outcomes, and multivariate linear regression was utilised to evaluate the effects of clinical factors on the intelligence quotient (IQ).ResultsAmong the study population, 84 of 144 children were prescribed an initial low dose of Levothyroxine. Most children in both initial dose groups showed appropriate growth within the normal range in the biennial growth evaluation from ages four to eight and in the Wechsler IQ exam. The initial dose seemed to not significantly affect the growth outcomes over time, as no significant differences between the low‐ and high‐dose groups were observed (p values: 0.545, 0.609, 0.532, and 0.501 for bone age—chronological age, height z‐score, weight z‐score, and BMI z‐score, respectively). The effect of the initial dose group on the full‐scale IQ was also not statistically significant (p = 0.362).ConclusionWe demonstrated the favourable long‐term outcomes in linear growth and neurodevelopment among children with CH, even in lower initial Levothyroxine doses.

Introduction

Congenital hypothyroidism (CH) is known as the most common cause of preventable neurodevelopmental dysfunction in neonates. If not treated, CH children experience severe growth retardation and developmental delay [1]. Since the 1950s, Levothyroxine, a synthetic form of thyroid hormone, has been established as the standard treatment to normalise thyroid hormone levels and prevent morbidities in children with CH [2].

Most centres follow the guidelines on CH suggested by the American Academy of Paediatrics (AAP), emphasising the importance of early screening and adequate treatment with Levothyroxine [3]. Constant updates in the guidelines, especially according to the recently updated version by the European Society for Paediatric Endocrinology [4], also recommend commencing the treatment as soon as the CH is diagnosed, with an initial daily dose of Levothyroxine from 10 to 15 mcg/kg. Earlier studies reported concerns regarding early overtreatment, suggesting potential long‐term neurocognitive adverse effects such as ADHD and metabolic consequences [5, 6]. There also is a trend toward reconsidering the Levothyroxine dose with some studies suggesting that lower doses may be sufficient in the treatment of CH [7]. Laura Niuro et al. even claimed that 2‐year growth outcomes among 6.8, 7.4, and 10.8 mcg/kg Levothyroxine dose groups all fall within normal ranges [8]. Similarly, Shan He et al. recommended tailoring the initial dose based on pretreatment TSH levels, suggesting that CH children with milder biochemical abnormalities may not require higher doses [9].

Despite these discussions, a long‐term study evaluating growth and neurodevelopmental outcomes of CH children initially treated with a lower Levothyroxine dose is scarce. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the long‐term growth and neurodevelopmental outcomes of children with CH, focusing on the influence of a lower initial dose of Levothyroxine compared to current recommendations.

Methods and Materials

Study Design

We retrospectively reviewed the electronic medical records of the CH children treated with Levothyroxine at the Department of Paediatric Endocrinology in Ajou University Hospital between 2003 and 2024. The institutional review board approved the study, and since it involved solely data from medical records and did not harm the subjects, informed consent was waived (IRB: AJOUIRB‐SM‐2024‐461).

Patients were included if they had undergone newborn screening and initiated Levothyroxine therapy based on either a markedly elevated initial thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) > 40 μIU/mL, confirmatory TSH > 20 μIU/mL or follow‐up TSH > 10 μIU/mL after 4 weeks, in line with current guidelines. Both in‐house and referred cases were eligible. Among the Levothyroxine‐treated CH children (n = 765), we excluded the ones with known congenital anomalies, extreme prematurity with gestational age (GA) below 32 weeks, and very low birth weight (BW) below 1500 g (n = 190), which are known to impair the growth and neurodevelopment in children [10]. Since recombinant growth hormone use might mask the growth impairment, the ones prescribed with recombinant growth hormone were also excluded.

Initial Levothyroxine doses were determined by paediatric endocrinologists, in reference to international guidelines (typically 10−15 mcg/kg/day). However, during the early study period, the available formulations were limited to 50, 100 and 150 mcg tablets, which were commonly divided into 25 or 33 mcg doses for neonates. This constrained dosing flexibility, especially in neonates with lower body weight. Furthermore, the widespread implementation of newborn screening led to increased identification of milder cases of CH, in which clinicians tended to opt for more conservative initial doses. As a result, a range of initial doses was observed across the cohort. For the purpose of analysis, we stratified patients into ‘Low’ (<10 mcg/kg) and ‘High’ (10−15 mcg/kg) dose groups, using the lower boundary of current guidelines as the cutoff.

Consistent with the AAP guidelines, we generally operate a trial of Levothyroxine discontinuation for every CH child whose serum TSH remains within the normal range at 3 years old in Ajou University Hospital. The ones with normal serum TSH and free thyroxine (fT4) levels after a month of discontinuation were considered to have had transient CH (TCH) and thus continued with only regular follow‐up without further treatment. Children whose serum TSH level increased above 10 μIU/mL at any point of follow‐up examination were categorised as permanent CH (PCH) and resumed with Levothyroxine therapy.

Most children underwent either ultrasonography or Technetium‐99m scintigraphy to evaluate the structure and position of the thyroid. The ones without either of these radiologic exams, incomplete medical record, or lost to follow‐up visits were considered incomplete data and, thus also excluded from the study (n = 431). In the Korean healthcare system, patients are not restricted to a single institution and can freely seek care at multiple hospitals [11]. Consequently, numbers of children who initially presented with CH at our centre later received follow‐up care elsewhere, particularly after the trial withdrawal of Levothyroxine, which is common among those with TCH. Additionally, a substantial portion of historical records before the adoption of electronic medical records system were paper‐based, and some were incompletely converted, which contributed to the exclusion of 431 patients.

As a result, 144 children were enroled in the study, as presented in Figure 1. Among the target population, subjects with at least three available growth data (height, weight and bone age [BA]) with approximately biennial intervals were further investigated for their growth outcome (n = 69). In parallel, subjects with full reports of either the Korean Wechsler Intellectual Scale for Children or the Korean Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence at the age of six were preceded by the evaluation of intellectual outcomes (n = 98).

Flowchart of the study. Among the target population, 69 subjects who underwent at least three growth evaluations with different age intervals, and 98 subjects with available full Wechsler intelligence quotient examination reports were further analysed.

Data Collection and Statistical Analysis

Birth data comprising GA and BW of the study population were collected. Age at treatment initiation in days, initial Levothyroxine dose in micrograms per kilogram, and serum levels of fT4 and TSH before the treatment were also collected.

Based on both thyroid ultrasonography and scintigraphy, we identified cases of thyroid dysgenesis, including ectopic, hemi‐agenesis, and hypoplasia. Normal radiological findings were defined as normally located and morphologically normal‐appearing thyroid glands, suggestive of dyshormonogenesis. Growth data consists of the chronological age in which measurements were taken, BA interpreted according to Greulich and Pyle's radiologic atlas [12], height, weight, and body mass index (BMI). The differences between BA and chronological age (BA−CA) were calculated and further utilised in the analyses. Z‐scores of height, weight, and BMI were computed based on the lambda‐mu‐sigma values from Korean children and adolescents' latest official growth chart [13].

Means and standard deviations were used to describe continuous variables. When the normality assumptions were met or sufficient numbers were supportive of the normality of the data, we performed independent t‐tests to compare continuous variables; alternatively, we used the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. The Student t‐test was utilised when the equality of variances could not be rejected by Levene's test; otherwise, Welch's t‐test was employed. For categorical variables, numbers and percentages were applied as descriptive measures.

We used repeated measures analysis of covariance (RM‐ANCOVA) to evaluate longitudinal trends in growth indicators (height Z‐score, weight Z‐score, BMI Z‐score, and BA−CA) across the low and high dose groups. The following covariates were included based on their known influence on growth and development: sex, BW Z‐score, treatment age, pretreatment fT4, pretreatment TSH, and the CH subtype (PCH or TCH). Mauchly's test of sphericity was performed, and if the assumption failed, the Greenhous‐Geisser correction was adjusted. Between‐group and within‐subject effects were analysed. Using multivariate linear regression analysis, a full‐scale intelligence quotient (FSIQ) was simultaneously assessed for the association with potential predictors. Statistical analysis was performed mainly by SPSS version 29.0.2 and visualisation of the data using R version 4.4.2.

Results

Characteristics of the Study Population

The baseline characteristics of 144 study subjects are presented in Table 1. The study population's mean GA was 38.88 ± 1.62 weeks, and their average BW was 3.20 ± 0.52 kg. Levothyroxine therapy was initiated when they were 27 ± 15 days old, reflecting variability in timing of referral and confirmatory diagnostic processes, with a dose of 9.35 ± 2.80 mcg/kg. Serum fT4 and TSH levels before the Levothyroxine therapy were 0.96 ± 0.37 ng/dL and 58.0 ± 129 μIU/mL, respectively. Thirty‐five (24%) of the study population failed the discontinuation trial and were treated with Levothyroxine to date. According to the thyroid ultrasonography and scintigraphy, the majority of subjects (78.5%) showed normally located and morphologically normal thyroid glands. One subject (0.7%) exhibited an intrathyroidal thymus, which was not considered a thyroid anomaly. These cases were interpreted as suggestive of dyshormonogenesis, although definitive diagnosis requires functional or genetic evaluation. In contrast, structural anomalies consistent with thyroid dysgenesis were identified in 30 subjects (20.8%), including ectopic thyroid (6.9%), hypoplasia (13.2%), and hemi‐agenesis (0.7%).

Comparison Between the Low and High Initial Levothyroxine Dose Groups

Table 2 summarises clinical characteristics and the results of comparative analyses between ‘Low’ and ‘High’ initial Levothyroxine dose groups. The birth history was relatively even in both groups with p values of 0.738 for the GA and 0.503 for the BW z‐score. The ‘Low’ group was initially prescribed Levothyroxine of 7.41 ± 1.47 mcg/kg, while the ‘High’ group used 12.07 ± 1.78 mcg/kg (p < 0.001). Children in the ‘Low’ group had more favourable pretreatment thyroid functions (fT4 1.08 ± 0.27 and TSH 26.95 ± 19.80) compared to that of the ‘High’ group (fT4 0.80 ± 0.42 and TSH 100.44 ± 191.29) with statistical significance (p < 0.001 and p = 0.004, respectively). Thus, the treatment initiation was comparably faster in the ‘High’ group (22.82 ± 7.16) than in the ‘Low’ group (30.8 ± 17.77, p < 0.001), which likely reflects clinicians' tendency to initiate therapy more promptly in infants presenting with more severe thyroid dysfunction at diagnosis.

These differences in pretreatment thyroid function and treatment initiation timing likely reflect underlying variations in not only clinical decision‐making, but also the disease severity among the groups. However, despite these disparities, the growth and intellectual outcomes were statistically similar between the initial dose groups. Most children showed growth trajectories including BA–CA, height z‐score, weight z‐score, and BMI z‐score within normal ranges (±2 SD) throughout the observed period, and the mean values depicted as line graphs of (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1) showed consistently overlapping patterns between dose groups, reinforcing the absence of clinically meaningful differences in growth across treatment doses. No extreme outliers significantly affected the group comparisons (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1). We implemented subjects with complete data on the growth outcomes (n = 69) to further comparative analyses (Table 2). Slight differences in growth parameters were noted at each time point. However, none of the differences were statistically significant (all p values above 0.05). As for the intellectual outcomes, most subjects showed normal FSIQ at around 100, in both groups see (Supporting Information S1: Figure 2). IQ scores were 99.00 ± 17.20 in the ‘Low’ group and 95.59 ± 14.26 in the ‘High’ group, but the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.301). Cohen's d values for key comparisons between the two dose groups were all small, supporting the interpretation that observed differences were minimal not only statistically but also in clinical magnitude.

The proportion of PCH was comparable between the two dose groups, accounting for 22.6% (19/84) in the ‘Low’ group and 26.7% (16/60) in the ‘High’ group, indicating that the CH subtype distribution was not substantially different across dose groups (not shown in Table). Nevertheless, we additionally compared CH types—TCH and PCH—to better characterise the cohort, and found no significant differences in any clinical variables or long‐term growth and intellectual outcomes (see Appendix 1 for detailed data).

The statistical interference was based on F‐statistics derived from model mean squares (between‐group and within‐group variance estimates). Specifically, Table 3A represents the within‐subject effects, which analyse whether growth parameters significantly changed over time (i.e., child's age at follow‐up) and whether these changes differed between the dose groups. Among the growth parameters, weight z‐score showed a statistically significant change over time (p = 0.028), consistent with the increase observed in Table 2 and Supporting Information S1: Figure 1. No statistically significant interactions between time and the initial dose group were found (p > 0.05 for all parameters), suggesting that the trajectory of growth outcomes over time was similar between across both groups.

Table 3B shows the between‐subject effects, which reflect average group differences across all time points. No significant differences were observed in any growth parameter between the ‘Low’ and ‘High’ dose groups after adjusting for covariates, which indicates that the initial Levothyroxine dose did not significantly affect overall growth outcomes.

Time and its interaction with other clinical variables also showed no statistically significant effect on growth outcomes, except for the time x sex effect on weight z‐score (p = 0.012, Appendix 2). Taken together, these results support the interpretation that initial dose variation did not substantially influence growth, either over time or in overall effect, after accounting for other clinical factors.

Discussion

Previous studies on children with Levothyroxine‐treated CH have already reported favourable growth outcomes. In 1997, Dickerman Z and De Vries, L. reported height z‐scores being −0.2 in males and 0.3 in females, and peak pubertal height velocity being 10.6 cm/year in males and 8.0 cm/year in females [14]. In our cohort, the prepubertal growth velocities were 6.82 cm/year from approximately 4 to 6 years and 6.42 cm/year from 6 to 8 years of age. Although these results are lower than previously reported pubertal rates, indicating that they reflect normal prepubertal growth rather than early pubertal acceleration, they are consistent with the Korean national growth curve for children and adolescents [13]. We also reported growth trajectories encompassing the BA–CA, height z‐score, weight z‐score, and BMI z‐score showing mostly favourable outcomes, which showed gradual increases over time (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1). At around 8 years old, the mean height z‐score increased to 0.24 ± 1.00, slightly above the population standard and previously reported final height z‐score (male: 0.10 ± 1.01 and female: −0.11 ± 1.09) in a Korean study [15]. Neurodevelopmental outcomes assessed by FSIQ, were also within the expected range. The mean FSIQ was 97.57, slightly below the general average of 100 but well within the normal distribution (80−120), and marginally lower than the 103.14 reported in the previous study [16]. This difference may be attributed to the inclusion of two outliers (presented in Supporting Information S1: Figure 2), identified to have comorbid autism spectrum disorder. We still included these outliers since they did not substantially change the regression coefficients or significance (Supporting Information S1: Table 1).

Despite the clinical heterogeneity inherent in CH cases detected by the newborn screening, our cohort exhibited consistently positive developmental outcomes. Most patients were referred from local birth centres or neonatal intensive care units following abnormal newborn screening results, and there was often a delay between the screening result and treatment initiation, due to logistical factors during the referral process or the time required for confirmatory TSH retesting. Additionally, as current guidelines do not require distinction between PCH and TCH, and our patients demonstrated a wide range of pretreatment thyroid function values reflecting differing severity [17]. To account for this heterogeneity, we included treatment age, pretreatment thyroid function tests, and CH subtype, factors known to negatively affect neurodevelopment and growth outcomes [18, 19], as covariates in our statistical models. None of these variables significantly influenced growth trajectories or IQ, based on covariate effects in the RM‐ANCOVA (Appendix 2) and multivariate regression analysis (Table 4). PCH and TCH also groups did not differ significantly in clinical variables or outcomes (Appendix 1), consistent with findings in a previous report [20].

Although higher initial doses of Levothyroxine have been recommended to for better outcomes [21, 22], the average initial dose in our cohort was slightly lower, at 9.35 mcg/kg. This was primarily attributable to limited tablet formulations available during the early study period and the increased detection of TCH following widespread newborn screening. Notably, the higher dose group had significantly lower pretreatment fT4, higher TSH, and earlier treatment initiation, suggesting that dose selection was guided by initial disease severity. However, after adjusting for these covariates, the initial dose was not independently associated with growth or IQ outcomes.

Recent discussions have questioned whether high initial doses are always necessary. We found that children who received lower initial dose still showed good long‐term outcomes, and the differences between two initial dose groups were consistently small (Cohen's d < 0.5 for all outcomes). These results aligned with the report by Andrea E. et al, who reported comparable outcomes with initial dose of 10−12.5 mcg/kg versus 12.5−15 mcg/kg [23]. Higher doses have been associated with an increased risk of overtreatment [24], which in some studies has been reported to be associated with attention‐deficit hypersensitivity disorder (ADHD) and other potential neurologic sequelae [25, 26]. In our cohort, three patients with confirmed ADHD were identified, though the variability in their initial doses (5.0, 13.16, and 15.96 mcg/kg) and the lack of structured ADHD screening prevent any conclusive association. Nevertheless, our results support a cautious, individualised approach to dose selection, balancing early efficacy with potential overtreatment concerns. While our retrospective findings do not establish non‐inferiority, it demonstrates no apparent disadvantage to lower initial dosing.

The majority of patients were ultimately classified as TCH, consistent with some previous reports in Korea, where TCH accounting for over 65%−75% of CH diagnoses [27, 28]. This likely reflects both the sensitivity of the Korean national newborn screening programme [29], and the traditional iodide‐rich diet (e.g., seaweed soup during pregnancy) [30, 31]. Because current guidelines recommend initiating treatment before TCH/PCH differentiation, dose decisions affect both groups. In this context, we conducted additional subgroup analysis in each CH subtype. In the TCH group, patients who received lower initial doses demonstrated comparable long‐term growth and neurodevelopmental outcomes (Appendix 3). Similarly, in the PCH group, lower dose recipients also showed comparable outcomes, although the sample size limited statistical power (Appendix 4). Taken together with our primary findings, these results provide real‐world support for individualised dosing strategies and suggest that lower‐dose regimens may be sufficient to support normal development in carefully monitored settings. Nonetheless, prospective studies, particularly in the PCH population, are warranted to validate these observations.

Our study benefits from the use of long‐term follow‐up data from a single centre. Strict exclusion criteria also enhanced the homogeneity of the study population, minimising the confounders. Furthermore, we comprehensively analysed growth and IQ long‐term outcomes until prepubertal childhood and validated the influences of multiple clinical parameters using statistical strategies such as RM‐ANOVA and multivariate regression analysis. Nonetheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. The single‐centre setting and relatively uniform cohort may limit the generalisability. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, we were unable to include potentially relevant factors such as socioeconomic background, genetic information, or the precise timing of TSH normalisation. Additionally, exclusions due to missing follow‐up data—while necessary for analytical rigour—may introduce selection bias. Despite these constraints, our findings provide valuable real‐world insight into long‐term outcomes in CH and raise important considerations for optimising Levothyroxine dosing. Further studies with larger, multicenter cohorts and more comprehensive parameters are necessary to validate and refine these observations.

Conclusions

This study supports the long‐term effectiveness of Levothyroxine treatment in CH, including in children who received lower initial doses. These findings suggest that individualised dosing may be sufficient to ensure favourable outcomes, supporting more flexible approaches in current CH management. Further larger, non‐inferiority trials are needed to optimise the dosing strategies.
