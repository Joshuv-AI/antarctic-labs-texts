Sarcopenia and fall-related outcomes in Chinese adults aged 45 years or older: a longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Xin Zhao, Chaoxuan Wang, Jian Wang, Xubo Ding, Yuchao Wang, Lan Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sarcopenia, the progressive loss of muscle mass and function, is a significant health concern in middle-aged and older adults, potentially linked to adverse outcomes like falls and fractures. A cohort study was conducted utilizing participants aged ≥ 45 years from CHARLS. Sarcopenia was defined using established criteria. Primary outcomes were isolated falls, isolated hip fractures, and composite outcomes. Associations were assessed using multivariable Cox regression (adjusted for confounders), propensity score matching (PSM), and inverse probability weighting (IPW), reporting hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).Conduct sensitivity analyses such as stratification to control for confounding. Among 6,939 participants (42.6% with sarcopenia), 10-year incident rates were 39.7% for isolated falls, 3.3% for isolated hip fractures, and 1.7% for composite outcomes. Sarcopenia was persistently associated with an increased risk of isolated falls across all analyses (adjusted HR = 1.20, 95% CI:1.10–1.31, p < 0.01; PSM HR = 1.19, p < 0.01; IPW HR = 1.26, p < 0.001). For isolated hip fractures, significant associations were found in adjusted (HR = 1.60, 95% CI:1.17–2.17, p = 0.003) and IPW analyses (HR = 1.78, p = 0.01), but not PSM (HR = 0.99, 95%CI:0.68–1.44, p = 0.958). No significant association was found between sarcopenia and composite outcomes.Sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the results. Sarcopenia is a significant risk factor for falls in middle-aged and older Chinese adults. Its association with hip fractures requires further investigation due to inconsistent findings across analytical methods.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43398-8.

Introduction

Sarcopenia is a progressive and generalized skeletal muscle disorder involving the accelerated loss of muscle strength, function, and mass1. Recent meta-analyses indicate a global overall prevalence of sarcopenia at 18.5%2, which not only increases with age but also exhibits significant geographical variation3. Muscle mass begins declining as early as age 30, with clinically significant reductions typically manifesting by age 504.This condition is intrinsically linked to frailty in older adults. Both sarcopenia and frailty manifest through overlapping clinical features including reduced appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM), diminished grip strength, impaired physical performance (evidenced by prolonged chair stand times)5, and overall functional decline. Sarcopenia constitutes a core physiological component of frailty and is associated with substantial adverse outcomes: increased fall and fracture risk6, elevated postoperative complications, extended hospitalizations, and heightened mortality with reduced overall and progression-free survival7. These associations underscore the critical importance of identifying and managing sarcopenia in both clinical and public health contexts.

Falls represent a common and serious issue for older adults, imposing substantial morbidity, mortality, and economic burdens on families and society. Falls have become the third leading cause of disability among older adults in mainland China8. A recent study estimated the lifetime risk of falls from age 50 to be 72.81% for men and 86.61% for women9.

Regarding hip fractures, global epidemiology and post-fracture outcomes show significant heterogeneity, with incidence rates ranging from 95.1 to 315.9 per 100,000 people10. In the elderly population, hip fractures constitute a severe public health problem characterized by high mortality, disability, complication rates, and economic burden. Incidence rises markedly with age. Studies by Ceolin, Nasser, and others11–13 report one-year mortality rates after hip fracture as high as 20%-30%. Complications such as re-fracture, delirium, and infection post-surgery14,15 lead to prolonged hospital stays, pain, and other adverse consequences16.

Multiple studies suggest that sarcopenia leads to declined physical function, such as impaired mobility and balance, thereby increasing fall risk in older adults17–19. However, its predictive value for hip fracture remains debated20–22, potentially due to methodological differences and population heterogeneity. Hip fractures often occur concomitantly with falls; research indicates that falls are a major risk factor for hip fractures and the primary cause of injury among those aged 65 and over23.Whether sarcopenia independently predicts isolated falls, isolated hip fractures, or the combined occurrence of falls with hip fracture warrants further confirmation.Therefore, this study aims to test the hypothesis that sarcopenia is independently associated with increased risks of incident falls, incident hip fractures, and their composite outcome in middle-aged and older adults.

Methods

Study Design

This longitudinal analysis utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS)24(https://charls.pku.edu.cn/), a publicly accessible database. CHARLS is a nationally representative, longitudinal survey of the middle-aged and older Chinese population (≥ 45 years), assessing social, economic, and health status among community-dwelling residents, reflecting the general profile of this demographic. The national baseline survey was conducted from June 2011 to March 2012, enrolling 17,708 respondents. Follow-up surveys were conducted every two years thereafter. To date, five waves of data have been released, with the first and third waves containing the physical measurements and functional performance assessments necessary for determining sarcopenia status. The survey covers a broad range of information, including demographics, family structure, health status, healthcare and insurance, employment and pensions, income and expenditure, housing conditions, and laboratory test results.

Participants underwent fasting venous blood collection after 12-hour fasting, followed by complete blood count (CBC) monitoring using automated analyzers at local CDCs or township health stations within 1–2 h. The median time from blood sample collection to CBC completion was 97 min.

The study received ethical approval from the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided signed informed consent24.

Study Population

The inclusion criteria for this study were: (1) age 45 or above at baseline; (2) no prior history of falls or hip fractures at the baseline survey; (3) complete baseline data for sarcopenia assessment; and (4) complete outcome data during follow-up. Ultimately, 6,939 participants were included (Fig. 1).

Outcomes and follow-up

Both falls and hip fractures were based on participant self-reported data, obtained by asking: “Since the last visit [last visit date loaded], have you had any falls/hip fractures?” (Answer: 1 yes, 2 no). If the answer was ‘yes’, follow-up ended for that event; otherwise, follow-up continued until participant death or the end of Wave 5 (2020).These datas on falls and hip fractures were obtained from participant self-report, which may be subject to recall bias.

Follow-up Time: Survey dates for 2011, 2013, 2015, and 2018 were provided in the 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020 data releases, respectively. Although the specific survey dates for 2020 were not published, all questionnaires for the 2020 wave were completed between July 2020 and February 2021. Therefore, the median timepoint of October 2020 was selected as the follow-up node for the 2020 wave.

Three follow-up outcomes were defined:

①Isolated falls: Events with fall but no hip fracture within the same biennial follow-up window. Follow-up time anchored to fall occurrence.

②Isolated hip fractures: Events with hip fracture but no falls in the identical follow-up cycle. Censored at fracture date.

③Composite outcomes: Restricted to events where fall and hip fracture occurred concurrently within one follow-up window. Censored at these events date.

Covariates

Potential confounders assessed at baseline included sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, comorbidities, and laboratory tests ( Table 1). Standardized questionnaires collected information on demographics, lifestyle factors, and comorbidities. Sociodemographic characteristics included sex, age, marital status (married, unmarried), education level (primary school and below, secondary school and above), and residence (urban, rural). Lifestyle factors included smoking (ever smoked/never smoked), alcohol consumption (ever drank/never drank), and sleep duration. Comorbidities included: hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, stroke, and arthritis. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg and/or self-reported history of hypertension diagnosis and/or current use of antihypertensive medication. Diabetes was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or self-reported history of diabetes diagnosis or use of any glucose-lowering medication. Dyslipidemia, stroke, and arthritis were defined based on self-reported physician diagnosis. Laboratory tests from the 2011 baseline included white blood cell count (WBC), platelet count (PLT), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), fasting glucose (Glu), serum creatinine (Cr), total cholesterol (TC), total triglycerides (TG), uric acid (UA), and hemoglobin (Hb).

Statistical analysis

Histograms, Q-Q plots, or the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test were used to assess normality for continuous variables. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while skewed continuous variables are described as median (interquartile range [IQR]). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (%). Comparisons of continuous variables between groups were performed using independent samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests based on distribution normality. Categorical data were compared using Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

The Cox proportional hazards model was employed to examine the relationship between sarcopenia and different outcomes. Four sequential models were constructed to systematically control for confounding factors: Model 1: adjusted for sociodemographic characteristics (sex, age, marital status, education). Model 2: Model1 + lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol consumption, sleep patterns). Model 3: Model 2 + comorbidities (hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, stroke arthritis). Model 4: Model 3 + laboratory parameters (WBC, PLT, BUN, Glu, Cr, TC, TG, UA, Hb).

Missing covariate data were addressed using multiple imputation implemented via the chained equations method with the ‘mice’ package in R, generating 5 imputed datasets. To evaluate the robustness of our findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted using 10 and 50 imputed datasets respectively.

To mitigate confounding and improve comparability between the sarcopenia and non-sarcopenia groups, we developed a unified propensity score model incorporating core covariates. Specifically, we estimated propensity scores for sarcopenia status using Cox proportional hazards regression, adjusting for all covariates in Table 1. Covariate balance was rigorously evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with |SMD| < 0.10 indicating adequate balance in both PSM-matched and IPW-weighted cohorts. Five analytical frameworks were implemented: (1) Unadjusted unmatched cohort (2) Multivariable analysis unmatched cohort (3) Propensity score-adjusted cohort (4) IPW-weighted cohort (5) PSM-adjusted cohort.It should be noted that although this method enhances comparability, it cannot fully account for unmeasured confounding factors.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the potential heterogeneity in the associations between sarcopenia and the three distinct clinical outcomes. Pre-specified subgroups were stratified by sex (male, fe), age (< 60 years, ≥ 60 years), sleep duration (< 6 h, 6–8 h, ≥ 8 h), and arthritis status (present, absent). For each outcome, Cox proportional hazards models were employed to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The presence of effect modification was formally evaluated through the inclusion of multiplicative interaction terms in the regression models. Statistical significance for interaction was determined using likelihood ratio tests, with a two-sided p-value < 0.05 considered indicative of significant heterogeneity.

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was conducted on both pre- and post-PSM data to assess survival time for isolated falls, isolated hip fractures, and the composite outcomes. The log-rank test was used to compare cumulative event probabilities between the sarcopenia and non-sarcopenia groups. Over the 10-year follow-up period, the number at risk was recorded at 2-year intervals to account for censoring.

To verify the robustness of our findings, we conducted a sensitivity analysis and implemented the following exclusion criteria: (1) Participants who experienced hip fractures and traffic accidents concurrently within one follow-up window(Table S2); (2) Exclud individuals under the age of 50( Table S5)( Adjusted HR changed from 1.60 (95% CI:1.17–2.17) to 1.64 (95% CI: 1.18–2.27)). The results of these analyses are consistent with those before exclusion.

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.2 (http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation) and Free Statistics software version 2.2.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The study cohort comprised 6,939 participants, with 2,957 (42.6%) being patients with sarcopenia. Sarcopenia patients exhibited significantly higher age (63.1 ± 9.4 years vs. 55.9 ± 7.6 years; p < 0.001), lower male prevalence (34.2%), and higher proportions of married individuals or those with primary education or below (95.1% vs. 4.9%; p < 0.001). Notably, urban village residency was significantly more prevalent among sarcopenia patients (71.6% vs. 28.4%; p = 0.004). Lifestyle factors showed distinct patterns: the sarcopenia group had lower smoking and alcohol consumption rates, along with shorter sleep duration. Sarcopenia was significantly associated with dyslipidemia and arthritis. Laboratory tests revealed lower levels of white blood cells, platelets, hemoglobin, triglycerides, and uric acid in the sarcopenia group, while no statistically significant differences were observed in cholesterol or blood glucose levels. (Table 1).

Cox proportional hazards model

Sarcopenia was associated with a significantly increased risk of isolated falls. After adjustment for all confounding factors, the risk of isolated falls in the sarcopenic group increased by 20% (95% CI: 1.10–1.31; p < 0.001). The stability of the hazard ratio after sequential adjustment indicated a robust independent association. Compared to falls, isolated hip fractures showed a stronger association, with a 60%-66% increased risk (Model 4: HR = 1.60,95% CI: 1.17–2.17; p = 0.003). Adjustment for comorbidities (Model 3) slightly amplified the effect (ΔHR = + 0.05), suggesting partial confounding by comorbidities. The point estimates for composite outcomes consistently showed a 28%-42% increased risk, but this was not statistically significant.

Propensity score matching

Following propensity score matching, 1,906 participants were included in each of the sarcopenia and non-sarcopenia groups using a 1:1 matching ratio. After matching, the two groups achieved sufficient balance with SMD values all < 0.10 (Table 2).

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis demonstrated consistent discriminative capacity across adjustment methods (AUC = 79.7%), with age and sex being the strongest predictors. At the optimal probability threshold of 0.4, the model achieved 71.2% sensitivity and 73.2% specificity. Discussion

This longitudinal study of a large, nationally representative cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese adults provides robust evidence regarding the associations between sarcopenia and the risks of isolated falls, isolated hip fractures, and their composite outcome. Although an International Classification of Diseases (ICD) code for sarcopenia now exists, diagnostic challenges persist due to the variety of operational definitions22. Adopted the unified diagnostic criterion proposed by Li et al.25 and Huang et al.20 to enhance the screening sensitivity for sarcopenia in community-dwelling older adults. After adjustment for confounding factors, sarcopenia was significantly associated with an increased risk of falls in non-hip fracture patients (HR = 1.20, P < 0.001), indicating a 20% higher risk compared to participants without sarcopenia. This finding is consistent with studies using different sarcopenia criteria17,26–28. Our survival curves indicate that the risk of isolated falls begins to diverge significantly between groups approximately two years after baseline, suggesting sarcopenia as a predictor for fall risk over a timeframe exceeding two years. This appears to differ from findings in populations over 80 years, where a markedly higher (over 3-fold) fall risk was observed within a 2-year follow-up26.

Previous longitudinal evidence has demonstrated an association between sarcopenia and elevated hip fracture risk in Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years, without distinguishing fall-related mechanisms20. This aligns with our pre-matching, multivariable-adjusted findings (Fig. 2, P < 0.05) However, propensity score-matched (PSM) analysis in our cohort revealed no significant differential risk for isolated hip fractures between sarcopenic and non-sarcopenic individuals (HR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.68–1.44; P = 0.958). This observation is reinforced by Harvey et al.‘s meta-analysis22, which indicated that sarcopenia’s fracture predictive capacity diminished after femoral neck bone mineral density (BMD) adjustment22,29,30. further supports the lack of independent association between sarcopenia and hip fracture in our study. Scott et al. corroborated this pattern, reporting elevated fracture rates in obese women with sarcopenia (HR = 2.8, 95% CI: 1.4–5.6) that lost statistical significance following total hip BMD adjustment31. Collectively, these findings suggest sarcopenia itself may not constitute a robust predictor of hip fracture. We propose that in middle-aged and older Chinese adults, the sarcopenia-hip fracture association exhibits instability and may be mediated by fall mechanisms or other confounders. The limited number of isolated hip fractures (n = 225) warrants validation in larger cohorts.

During the 10-year follow-up period, a total of 346 hip fractures occurred, while only 125 concurrent fall events were recorded. Even after excluding 30 participants who experienced car accidents during the same period, the incidence of non-fall-related hip fractures does not align with the findings (11%) of Jha et al. Possible reasons for this discrepancy include: (a) Limitations of the data source: This study relied on participants’ self-reported data. We recognize that self-reported data may have potential limitations, including recall bias (omission or misrecording of events), social desirability bias, and potential classification errors. For instance, patients may have experienced mild, atypical fall events (such as “slipping” without falling, or stumbling over furniture without complete loss of balance) that were not recorded, particularly among cognitively impaired participants or those with differing interpretations of the definition a “fall.” (b) Pathological fractures: Certain diseases can alter bone strength and structure, making the hip bones susceptible to pathological fractures during daily activities (e.g., turning, standing up from a seated position). Examples include advanced osteoporosis32, benign bone tumors33(e.g., simple bone cysts [43.9%], non-ossifying fibroma [27.3%]), and metastatic bone diseases (e.g., breast cancer34, hematologic malignancies, etc.). c). Stress fractures35: Chronic, repetitive minor injuries (e.g., overuse, prolonged weight-bearing) can lead to stress fractures. In endurance athletes or military recruits, cumulative microtrauma may result in stress fractures of the femoral neck. Although relatively rare in the elderly population, this should still be considered for active older adults. d) Other factors: Spontaneous non-traumatic hip dislocation caused by complications of screw fixation for femoral neck fractures is classified as iatrogenic non-fall fractures36. Congenital skeletal developmental abnormalities and drug side effects37,38 may also lead to non-fall-type fractures.

The observed association between sarcopenia and falls, may involve multiple interconnected mechanisms. First, physical inactivity accelerates muscle loss and impairs neuromuscular control. Resistance training improves muscle strength and postural stability, reducing fall risk by ~ 23% through enhanced balance and proprioception39,40. Second, nutritional deficiencies disrupt muscle protein synthesis. Individuals with malnutrition exhibit 45% higher fall risk41, likely due to impaired muscle maintenance—a process exacerbated in sarcopenia. Third, frailty syndrome shares pathophysiological pathways with sarcopenia42, and both conditions amplify fall susceptibility through compromised musculoskeletal resilience.

Notably, we found that sarcopenia was significantly associated with the risk of future falls in males, but this association was not significant in females. This finding aligns with evidence indicating accelerated muscle loss in aging men, potentially reflecting androgen-related metabolic shifts. Declining testosterone levels may elevate fall risk through mechanisms such as impaired neuromuscular function, accelerated muscle protein catabolism, and heightened inflammatory or infection susceptibility43.Second, while sarcopenia is more prevalent in women, severe sarcopenia exhibits a higher prevalence in men (8.4% vs. 75%)44, which may explain the stronger association with falls in males. Third, lifestyle factors likely contribute: Men are more prone to habits like smoking and excessive alcohol consumption that exacerbate muscle loss. Additionally, they demonstrate lower engagement and adherence to resistance training compared to women45.

This study has several limitations. First, data on falls and hip fractures were obtained from participant self-report, which may be subject to recall bias. Second, sarcopenia was assessed only at baseline using available data; information on bone mineral density was not obtained, and changes in sarcopenia status over time were not evaluated. Third, the CHARLS database lacks systematic documentation of fracture etiologies, necessitating reliance on proxy measures (e.g., concurrent traffic accident reports). This precludes definitive differentiation between pathological fractures (e.g., malignancy-related), syncope-induced fractures, and true spontaneous fractures—potentially diluting sarcopenia’s etiological contribution. Finally, although a range of covariates was considered, potential confounders (such as physical activity, frailty, etc.) may remain and could influence the conclusions.

Conclusion

In the Chinese population aged 45 and above, sarcopenia is significantly associated with the incidence of isolated falls and can serve as an independent risk factor for predicting falls.But exhibits inconsistent fracture associations across methodologies.
