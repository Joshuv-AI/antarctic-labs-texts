Comments on: 'End-of-Life Care Preferences of Terminally Ill Patients: A Cross-sectional Study'
Authors: Emilia Trofimena Saudella
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

I read with interest the article by Devakirubai and Gnanadurai exploring end-of-life (EOL) care preferences among terminally ill patients in a hospice. The findings offer an important insight into how patients receiving palliative care in India understand and express their wishes regarding EOL care.[1]

One finding that stood out was the high proportion of patients who had not communicated their EOL preferences. This highlights a challenge that is not unique to India; even when individuals have specific values or wishes, these are not always shared. The authors’ recommendation for improving awareness is important. Given that 71.66% of participants expressed a willingness to complete a living will or advance directive, there appears to be a gap between intention and action that could be addressed through targeted education and structured advance care planning (ACP) discussions.[2]

The study also discusses spirituality in EOL preferences. Most participants expressed specific needs, such as listening to religious songs or having a spiritual leader present. Incorporating such preferences into EOL planning may help patients feel more supported and comfortable.[3] The authors appropriately identify this as an area requiring further training, especially for nurses and healthcare support staff who will be present in the final days.

The authors note that most patients preferred a designated person to make decisions on their behalf when they lacked capacity. Clarification regarding whether these preferences had been formally documented or discussed in advance would have strengthened the interpretation, as formal documentation (e.g., a lasting power of attorney) may influence whether such preferences are upheld in practice.[2] While the study focuses appropriately on the hospice setting, expanding similar inquiries into community-based or outpatient palliative care populations in future work would strengthen these findings.

As a clinician with an interest in ACP, I appreciated the authors’ patient-centred approach. Their work underscores the ongoing need, across all settings, for sensitive conversations and systems that support patients in expressing and documenting their wishes.[2]
