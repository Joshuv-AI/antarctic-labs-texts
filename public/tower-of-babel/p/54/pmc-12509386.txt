Scenario-based simulation of carbon storage in Chengdu using MCCA–InVEST: land use change, spatial patterns, and driving mechanisms
Authors: Zhigang Li, Taolue Yan, Yulun Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo achieve Chengdu's dual-carbon goals and support high-quality development, it is essential to analyze the impact mechanisms of land use patterns on carbon storage in Chengdu. This study aims to provide evidence-based land use planning strategies that synergistically balance ecological security and economic growth for the Chengdu-Chongqing Economic Circle's dual-carbon objectives.MethodsThis study uses the MCCA - InVEST model to simulate the impact of land use changes on carbon storage in Chengdu City from 2025 to 2035, setting up three scenarios: natural development (ND), ecological conservation (EC), and sustainable development (SD). The MCCA model is used to simulate land use dynamics by integrating 11 driving factors. The InVEST model is then used to assess changes in carbon storage, with a focus on the area evolution and carbon storage response mechanisms of cropland , forests, and impervious land.ResultsLand use changes show that under the ND scenario, cropland decreases by an average of 0.82 million hectares per year, impervious land expand by 38.1%, and forest area increases by 20.8%. Under the EC scenario, the rate of cropland reduction slows, with forests growing at an average annual rate of 2.6%, and impervious land expanding by 28.1%. Under the SD scenario, which lies between the two, the reduction in cropland is 0.5 million hectares less than in the ND scenario, forests increase by 25.0%, and impervious land expand by 32.4%, achieving a balance between loss and gain. In terms of carbon storage, the ND scenario results in a net decrease of 9.6×10⁶ tons, the EC scenario results in a net increase of 13.2×10⁶ tons, and the SD scenario remains largely stable. In terms of spatial distribution, high carbon storage areas are concentrated in the Longquan Mountains and the western mountainous regions, with NDVI being the primary driving factor (q=0.7264). ConclusionThe SD scenario can balance ecological conservation and economic development. It is recommended to enhance forest construction in the Longquan Mountains and the western mountainous regions, and to promote 'compact' expansion in the urban core area. The MCCA-InVEST coupling model provides an effective tool for evaluating carbon storage across multiple scenarios. This study offers a scientific basis for Chengdu City to achieve its 'dual carbon' goals and serves as a reference for other cities conducting carbon storage assessments and land use planningSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13021-025-00328-x.

Introduction

The acceleration of urbanization and the intensification of human activities have made global warming one of the most critical climate issues [1]. In 2021, the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) in its Sixth Assessment Report (AR6) confirmed the direct Link between the extent of global warming and carbon dioxide emissions, stating that the global average surface temperature will rise by 0.27℃ to 0.63℃ [2] per trillion tons of CO2 emitted from human activities.By 2017, human activities had raised global temperatures by about 1 °C [3] compared to pre-industrial levels. Carbon dioxide, the most significant greenhouse gas, has seen its concentration increase from 300ppm in 1950 to 410ppm in 2019, a 48% increase [4]. Since the Industrial Revolution, land use changes have accounted for about one-third of the total carbon emissions from human activities, making it the second-largest source of carbon emissions. Improving the quality of the ecological environment and enhancing the carbon sink function of terrestrial ecosystems are crucial methods for achieving both economic and environmental benefits in reducing CO2 emissions.Sustainable ecological research is not only essential for climate change mitigation but also closely tied to improving human well-being. Recent studies highlight that climate and land-use policies should be evaluated in terms of both emission reduction effectiveness and their impacts on diverse dimensions of well-being, including economic income, health, safety, and social stability [5]. Equitable allocation of carbon emission responsibilities at the city level, by avoiding double counting and accounting for inter-city trade externalities, is crucial for fair and effective mitigation strategies [6]. Furthermore, the concept of spatial justice between ecosystem services (ES) and residents’ well-being (WB) emphasizes that ecological protection must be coordinated with improvements in social benefits to achieve long-term sustainability [7]. Land-use and land-cover change (LUCC) studies reveal that while economic benefits often increase with development, they may come at the cost of degraded ecosystem services and declining ecological well-being [8]. To address this, the integration of ecosystem service supply-demand balance with human needs assessment provides a valuable framework for constructing social-ecological security patterns that enhance both ecological resilience and human welfare [9]. Liu et al. [10] calculates the carbon storage in the western Sichuan plateau based on land use conditions. Vincent analyzed how increasing the proportion of forested land can help reduce carbon emissions in Africa [11]. Yu [12], on the other hand, analyzed and discussed the impact of land use on carbon storage and predicted future carbon storage changes under current scenarios. Meanwhile, cities are expected to become the primary areas of human concentration in the future, with China’s urbanization rate projected to reach 70% [13]. Research on urban carbon storage has emerged as a key area of interest. Kang [14] et al. quantified and differentiated the direct and indirect impacts of urbanization on the carbon sequestration capacity of vegetation in 508 major urban regions globally from 2000 to 2020. The study found that while urbanization typically has a negative impact, most cities have indirectly enhanced the carbon sequestration capacity of vegetation, resulting in an average global increase of 19.6%, which partially offsets the direct carbon loss (14.7%) caused by urbanization. Understanding the effects of urban environmental changes on the carbon sequestration capacity of vegetation and its driving mechanisms will enhance our understanding of how vegetation may respond to future global changes. Therefore, quantitatively assessing land use changes in urban areas and their impact on carbon storage, and exploring ways to optimize land use structures to boost regional ecosystem carbon storage, is crucial for understanding the characteristics of regional ecosystem carbon storage and achieving the’ dual carbon’ goals.

Scholars both domestically and internationally have conducted extensive research on the relationship between land use and carbon storage. In terms of the distribution of research areas, existing literature primarily focuses on three typical regions: (1) economically developed coastal areas such as the Yangtze River Delta, Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei, and Pearl River Delta urban agglomerations [12–15], where studies often focus on the loss of cropland and the compression of ecological space due to rapid urbanization; (2) watershed protection areas (such as the Sanjiangyuan region) [16]; (3) ecologically fragile areas for example the Yellow River Basin [17], which emphasize the carbon sink effects of ecological restoration projects. However, research on cities like Chengdu, an inland western economic core city, is notably insufficient. As the core engine of the Chengdu-Chongqing dual-city economic circle, Chengdu’s land use evolution differs from that of coastal cities and other western cities, presenting a unique development model constrained by the ‘mountain-plain’ dual spatial structure: under the ‘eastward advance and westward control’ strategy, the ecological barrier of the Longquan Mountains and the expansion of construction land in Tianfu New Area form a strong spatial competition, which is a typical representation of high-intensity conflicts between ecological conservation and economic growth in inland core cities. In terms of research content, previous studies have focused on carbon storage analysis based on past years ‘land use patterns [18], while future scenarios’ carbon storage analysis mostly focus on a single year [19], with few studies on multi-scenario future carbon storage simulation analysis and the impact of changes in land types on carbon storage under multiple scenarios. Accurately predicting future land use patterns is fundamental to assessing ecosystem carbon storage. From the perspective of research methods, previous approaches have primarily relied on the Invest model, using remote sensing satellite imagery or field sampling data to assess carbon storage. These analyses were typically conducted using a single method, with few instances of combining multiple models. Scholars both domestically and internationally have conducted extensive research on land use patterns and developed a series of models for future simulation. Currently, the most commonly used models are variations of traditional CA models, such as FLUS [20], PLUS [21], and CLUE-S [22]. Liang et al. [23] introduced the concept of mixed cells and developed the Mixed-Cell Cellular Automata (MCCA) model, which addresses the complexity of land use by overcoming the limitations of traditional single-land-use structures. The Invest model is a comprehensive system for assessing carbon storage, widely adopted by scholars worldwide due to its minimal data requirements, visualization capabilities, and spatialization features. This proposal combines MCCA and Invest models, offering a highly adaptable solution for carbon storage assessment.

Situated in the central Sichuan Basin, Chengdu is one of the most economically dynamic regions in western China and faces significant challenges in carbon reduction. As the core of the Western Development Strategy, Chengdu’s high-quality sustainable development is crucial for regional economic growth and achieving the carbon peak goal. To alleviate the pressure on Chengdu to achieve its dual-carbon goals, land and spatial planning must prioritize low-carbon optimization and enhance carbon sequestration. Research on the relationship between carbon storage and land use in Chengdu primarily focuses on the calculation of carbon storage, with little attention given to the spatial distribution of carbon storage. This paper uses the MCCA coupled with the Invest model to simulate land type scenarios and project future regional carbon storage, providing a basis for ecological economic development. Through this approach, we can better understand the direct impact of urbanization on carbon storage and uncover the indirect mechanisms by which urbanization enhances vegetation’s carbon sequestration capacity, which is crucial for formulating scientific and reasonable land use and carbon reduction strategies [24].

Data and methods

Overview of the study area

The capital of Sichuan Province, Chengdu (Fig. 1), is situated on the western edge of the Sichuan Basin, at coordinates 102°~104°E and 30°~31°N. The city covers an area of approximately 14,600 square kilometers, stretching about 200 km from east to west and about 180 km from north to south. The highest point in Chengdu is Mount Qingcheng, located in Dujiangyan City, with an elevation of about 1,260 m. The Longquan Mountains run from north to south, dividing Chengdu into two parts. The main urban area is located in the western part of the Longquan Mountains, while Jianyang City is situated in the eastern part. To the west of the main urban areas, the Longmen and Qionglai Mountains form the western geographical boundary of Chengdu. The western mountains (such as the Longmen and Qionglai Mountains) and the eastern mountains (the Longquan Mountains) contrast sharply with the central plains (the Chengdu Plain). Chengdu is located in a subtropical monsoon climate zone, characterized by distinct seasons and abundant rainfall. The average annual temperature is around 16 °C, and the average annual precipitation is about 1,000 millimeters. Chengdu’s summers are hot and humid, while winters are mild and dry. The main land types in Chengdu include agricultural land, urban construction land, and mountain forests. Agricultural land is mainly found in the surrounding areas of the city, used for growing grains and vegetables. Urban construction land is concentrated in the central urban area, including commercial, residential, and industrial zones. Additionally, Chengdu has mountain forests and grasslands designated for ecological conservation and leisure tourism.

Data sources

The land use data used in this study are sourced from the CLCD (China Land Cover Dataset) dataset. For Chengdu’s future development scenarios, this study integrates population density, GDP distribution, DEM, NDVI, road density, distance to expressways/main/secondary roads, river proximity, mean annual temperature, average annual rainfall, altitude, and slope gradient as driving factors to construct the MCCA model’s spatial adaptive map, ensuring reliable scenario simulations. All data are publicly available online. The land use type data (CLCD) is derived from the dataset created by Professors Yang Jie and Huang Xin at Wuhan University [24].

Carbon sink is calculated according to land use type

The InVEST model, or the Integrated Valuation of Ecosystem Services and Trade-offs, is a comprehensive evaluation model for ecosystem services and trade-offs. The carbon storage module of the InVEST model categorizes the carbon storage in ecosystems into four primary reservoirs: above-ground biological carbon (carbon from all living plant materials above the soil), below-ground biological carbon (carbon found in the living root systems of plants), soil carbon (organic carbon distributed in both organic and mineral soils), and dead organic carbon (carbon from litter, fallen, or standing dead trees). Introduction to MCCA model

The Mixed-cell Cellular Automata (MCCA) model, proposed by Liang Xun in the study [23], effectively overcomes the inherent limitations of traditional Cellular Automata (CA) models.Traditional CA models are only capable of handling qualitative and discrete changes in land use types. In contrast, the MCCA model simulates mixed land use structures, enabling the quantitative representation of multiple land use types and their coverage ratios within cells. This allows for the capture of quantitative and continuous land use change processes driven by socio-economic, ecological, and policy - political factors. Empirical studies demonstrate that, compared with traditional CA models (Overall Accuracy (OA) of 0.8967 and Figure of Merit (FoM) of 0.1530), the MCCA model exhibits remarkable superiority in simulating land use changes.It achieves an Overall Accuracy (OA) of 0.9303, a Mean Relative Entropy (meanRE) of 0.9768, and a mixed cell Figure of Merit (mcFoM) of 0.2959.

The development of the MCCA model holds significant theoretical and practical implications. Theoretically, it breaks away from the conventional focus on static land use patterns in previous research. By simulating the dynamic changes in mixed land use structures, it elucidates the interaction mechanisms of various driving factors in shaping the evolution of future land use patterns. Practically, it provides more accurate data support for environmental impact assessments, such as air quality, urban heat island effects, and ecosystem service functions. Additionally, it enables spatiotemporal continuous analysis and quantitative calculations of land use changes, thereby enhancing the scientific and forward - looking nature of urban planning and land resource management decisions.

Model result verification

According to the rules of the Markov Chain for predicting future land demand, the data for each period is spaced five years apart. To assess whether the model’s simulation accuracy meets the research requirements, the land use data from Chengdu in 2005 and 2020 were used as a baseline. The transition probabilities of various land use types in the study area from 2005 to 2020 were calculated using driving factor data. Using the 2005 land use data as a reference map, the calculated transition probabilities were applied to the model to generate the 2020 land use simulation data. The simulated data was then compared with the actual land use data from the same period in the study area. The overall accuracy (OA) was 0.8988, and the Kappa coefficient was 0.804, indicating that the model’s prediction of land use changes is highly reliable and meets the needs of subsequent research.

Forests, as a vital ecological barrier, have seen an increase in area across all three scenarios, but the intensity of restoration varies. In 2020, the base area was 321,800 hectares (22.4%). Notably, the SD scenario began to show policy effects by 2025, with an additional 356,800 hectares added, 14,000 hectares more than the ND scenario, indicating that ecological conservation policies significantly enhance ecological improvement. Implementing ecological conservation policies can greatly support Chengdu’s goal of becoming a park city. However, given the dual structure of farmland and forests, even if the urban expansion slows down, it is crucial to consider how to maximize forest growth while maintaining farmland.

The expansion of impervious land intuitively reflects urbanization. In 2020, the base area was 150,200 hectares (10.5%), and by 2035, under the ND/SD/EC scenarios, it is projected to reach 207,100,198,800, and 192,300 hectares, respectively, representing increases of 38.1%,32.4%, and 28.1%. Discussion

Features and advantages of MCCA-Invest model

The land use change prediction capability of the MCCA-Invest combination model can assess and predict carbon storage under various scenarios. The model’s cells contain the coverage ratios of different land uses, providing a new perspective for modeling the spatiotemporal dynamics of mixed land use structures. Compared to traditional CA models, the hybrid cell CA model differs significantly in four key aspects of cellular automata: cell state, cell space, cell neighborhood, and transformation rules. The MCCA model proposed in this paper uses regression algorithms to automatically construct the transformation rules of cellular automata based on changes in land use over two periods. It also proposes a mechanism to simulate the competition among various land use types at the sub-cell scale. This mechanism can be used to quantitatively simulate changes in land use structure within pixels and the detailed and continuous changes in land use ratios within land units. By introducing a hybrid pixel competition mechanism, the MCCA model significantly enhances the accuracy of dynamic simulation of mixed land use structures, providing a methodological innovation for the detailed assessment of the impact of land cover changes on carbon storage. The OA parameters are more accurate than those of the PLUS model [21], and it can simulate detailed land change processes, making it a hybrid, continuous, and multi-dimensional cellular automaton. The introduction of the MCCA model represents a new attempt in the application of geographic cellular automata and an important theoretical supplement, promoting the development of mixed land use research from static mapping to dynamic simulation. The INVEST model offers a convenient, fast, and accurate method for calculating carbon storage. Compared to other methods, it requires less data and operates more quickly. It can map the spatial distribution and dynamic changes of carbon storage, reflecting the relationship between land use changes and carbon storage, and quantifying the value of ecological services dynamically. This provides an operational approach for rapidly assessing and tracking the city’s carbon storage in real time. It also supports the calculation of carbon storage under different future scenarios. Coupling these two models can provide a combination of methods for rapid carbon storage calculations, offering a solution for the annual real-time tracking and calculation of carbon storage in the future. Compared to the previous PLUS-Invest model [15], this method has improved accuracy and better simulates changes in land types. However, compared to using the InVEST model alone [36], this method does not account for potential changes in land types and cannot accurately simulate future carbon storage changes.

Evolution of land use pattern in Chengdu and its implications under the interaction of policy intervention and natural conditions

Land use change is considered one of the most significant factors contributing to changes in carbon storage in global terrestrial ecosystems [37]. This study used the Mixed-cell Cellular Automata (MCCA) model to simulate land use changes under different scenarios in Chengdu City. The results indicate that policy interventions significantly influence the evolution of land use patterns. In the natural development (ND) scenario, land use changes exhibit a relatively free driving characteristic, with farmland continuously decreasing and impervious surfaces expanding without restraint, reflecting the pressure on ecological space from urbanization in the absence of policy regulation. In the ecological conservation (EC) scenario, although forest areas have increased significantly and the expansion of impervious surfaces has been effectively curbed, the substantial reduction in farmland highlights the conflict between ecological conservation and cropland security. This scanario simulate Chengdu’s strict policies [38, 39] which have strict protection on cropland and promote forest expansion aim at achieving ”Park City”. In the sustainable development (SD) scenario, there is a certain degree of balance achieved between ecological improvement and rational urban development, demonstrating the positive impact of policy regulation on land use.This is exploratory simulation that government may take potential actions when facing conflict between “Cropland Red Line”and “Park City”.

From the perspective of the impact of land use changes, the continuous reduction in cropland poses a potential challenge to Chengdu’s food supply and agricultural industry development. In the future, new models for the protection and efficient use of cropland need to be explored. The expansion of forest areas, particularly in Longquan Mountain and the western six cities, has significantly enhanced regional ecosystem services and carbon sequestration capabilities, providing strong support for achieving the’ dual carbon ‘goals. However, the rapid expansion of impervious land in the city’s core area has intensified the urban heat island effect [40], negatively impacting the urban living environment and ecological resilience.

The spatial differentiation in regional development highlights significant differences in land use changes between the core urban area and the suburbs. This difference provides a basis for optimizing Chengdu’s territorial space development pattern. In the future, efforts should focus on enhancing regional coordinated development by promoting intensive land use and ecological restoration in the core urban area, and fully tapping into the potential of forest expansion in the suburbs to achieve an organic integration of urban and ecological spaces. Additionally, the MCCA model used in this study has limitations in data accuracy and the consideration of complex socio-economic factors. Future research could incorporate more detailed land use data and dynamic policy simulations to provide a more scientific basis for urban sustainable development.

Carbon storage changes and attribution of changes in future scenarios

This study elucidates the dynamic mechanisms of carbon storage in Chengdu through multi-scenario simulations using the MCCA model, showing that in the ND scenario, a reduction of 26.5 million hectares in cultivated land resulted in a net carbon storage loss of 2.89 million tons, with a 20.8% increase in forest cover compensating only 83.7% of the carbon loss from cultivated land and validating the model’s efficacy in simulating carbon balance in mixed land use systems, where the imbalance was attributed to the lag in forest expansion relative to construction land development [30]. In the SD scenario, a forest carbon sink increment of 26.66 million tons nearly offset carbon losses from 27.60 million hectares of cultivated land, achieved by controlling impervious surface expansion to 32.4% and optimizing zonal afforestation in the Longquan Mountain region. The EC scenario yielded a net “excess carbon gain” of 3.0 million tons, with forest carbon sequestration efficiency increasing by 15.7% to 12.8 tons/ha as a result of modeling afforestation of low-efficiency sloping croplands in six western counties under high-cost thresholds, leading to a 22.5% higher carbon sink than the SD scenario and constraining impervious surface expansion to 28.1%, which highlights the maximum carbon sink potential through land use structure optimization. These results align with Zhao et al. [41], confirming forests as the largest terrestrial carbon sink [29].

However, the scenario simulations reveal a critical contradiction: although Chengdu’s urbanization rate has exceeded 80% [42] and the expansion rate of impervious surfaces tends to slow down, as a nationally important grain production base, it faces more intense competition for land resources between cropland protection and ecological construction compared to coastal cities (e.g., Guangzhou, Shanghai, and other regions with less stringent cropland protection policies).Under the current push for carbon peaking targets, how to achieve reforestation goals while strictly maintaining the cropland protection redline has become an urgent challenge for Chengdu’s municipal government. Notably, this challenge is not unique to Chengdu—several major cities in southwestern China (e.g., Kunming, Xi’an) face similar dilemmas. Therefore, if Chengdu can explore a sustainable development pathway that balances food security and ecological construction, it will not only support local green development but also provide replicable solutions for similar cities.

Through the Moran’s Index and hotspot analysis, it is found that the main high carbon density areas in the future will be located in mountainous regions such as Longquan Mountain and the six western counties, while low carbon density areas will be concentrated in the city’s core. This is closely tied to Chengdu’s actual economic development and urban expansion. While the plains are naturally conducive to urban expansion, the constraints of the cropland red line and sustainable development strategies, along with Chengdu’s ongoing urbanization process, will lead to a more cautious and steady approach to future urban expansion compared to the past. The expansion of low carbon density areas will be restricted, making it possible for carbon sinks to gradually increase, which will provide key priorities for Chengdu’s 2030 carbon neutrality strategy.

When attributing carbon storage, the findings indicate that the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), a key indicator of vegetation cover, has a significant explanatory power (q = 0.7264) that surpasses other factors. This suggests that vegetation restoration and protection are essential for enhancing regional carbon sequestration capacity. Notably, the interaction between human factors (such as population density and GDP) and natural factors (such as slope, elevation, and precipitation) generally explains more than 0.7 of carbon storage variability. This finding highlights the profound impact of urbanization and natural environmental elements on changes in carbon storage. Based on these findings, we propose a differentiated spatial optimization strategy: in the western mountainous areas with superior ecological conditions (such as Dujiangyan, Pengzhou, and five other counties and cities), implement strict ecological conservation policies, focusing on forest ecosystem conservation; in the transitional zone of Longquan Mountain, where farmland and forest land are intermingled, implement refined land use management and explore a ‘farmland protection-carbon sink enhancement’ coordinated development model; in urban built-up areas and their expansion boundaries, strictly control the disorderly expansion of construction land, and enhance urban carbon sequestration capacity through measures such as three-dimensional greening and ecological corridor construction.

Research deficiencies and future prospects

In this study, building on previous findings, we used meteorological data from the study area to refine the carbon density data. This localized approach proved more effective than using national-scale carbon density data in regional studies, thereby enhancing the accuracy of the assessment results. Although the revised carbon density data is closer to the actual conditions in the study area, environmental fluctuations and human activities can still cause changes. The study’s reliance on precipitation to adjust soil carbon density has certain limitations [43]. Additionally, small parks, road green spaces, and community green spaces in urban areas cannot be fully accounted for in the overall green space due to issues with CLCD data, which affects the calculation of carbon storage. Future research should delve deeper into these issues.

The LUCC (Land Use and Land Cover Change) process is often influenced by natural conditions, socio-economic factors, and human activities. Some of these factors are difficult to quantify and are subject to uncertainty [44]. The MCCA model incorporates data such as population, GDP, DEM, proximity to road networks, and vegetation cover (FVC), using these 11 datasets along with a set of water body constraints to predict future land use trends. However, the modeling process lacks a deep consideration of social and economic factors [45]. Additionally, the spatial resolution and interpretation accuracy of remote sensing images limit the precision of the model’s simulation results. Therefore, LUCC simulations should be integrated with the actual conditions and the constraints of the study area to further enhance the accuracy of the simulations and achieve more realistic outcomes.

However, under the sustainable development scenario, carbon storage is expected to remain largely stable by 2035, with a significantly smaller reduction compared to the natural development scenario. In the ecological conservation scenario, carbon storage is expected to increase by 13.2 million tons by 2035. The sustainable development and ecological harmony protection scenarios have successfully reversed the downward trend in carbon storage seen in the natural development scenario. Particularly, under the ecological harmony protection scenario, the vegetation cover area of Longquan Mountain has steadily increased for 15 consecutive years, transforming from a single point to extensive green coverage. Under strict policies for farmland and forest protection, the forest area in the five western counties has also gradually expanded.

The scenario prediction indicates that the distribution of carbon storage in Chengdu will continue to feature the city’s core area as a low-carbon storage zone, while the Longquan Mountains and western mountainous areas will remain high-carbon density zones. Therefore, priority should be given to implementing forest vegetation restoration and ecological corridor construction projects in these regions to enhance the area’s carbon sink capacity. Under a sustainable development scenario, optimizing land use, controlling the expansion of impervious land, and improving urban greening quality can help achieve a balance between economic growth and environmental protection. The city’s core area should adopt a’ compact’ spatial planning strategy to effectively mitigate the negative impact of inefficient land expansion on carbon storage.
