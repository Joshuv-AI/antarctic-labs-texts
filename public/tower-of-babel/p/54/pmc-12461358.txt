Velocity Map Imaging of NO (A) from Photodissociation of the Ar-NO Complex
Authors: Bradley F. Parsons, Emerson Pappas, Kylie Reardon, Kathleen Loder
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We have photodissociated the Ar-NO complex from 4 to 337 cm–1 above the energy required to form NO (A 2∑+). In the experiment, the NO (A) product was nonresonantly ionized, and velocity map ion images were recorded at a resolution sufficient to identify individual rotational states. From the data, we determined the minimum energy required to form the NO (A) product to be 44294.3 ± 2.2 cm–1. We also determined the ground state dissociation energy for Ar-NO to be 95.4 ± 2.2 cm–1 and the excited-state dissociation energy to be 52 ± 2 cm1. The ground-state dissociation energy agrees with experimental results and a recent three-dimensional ab initio potential energy surface; however, the excited-state dissociation energy is larger than predicted by theoretical surfaces. We also determined the NO (A) rotational state distribution, which was bimodal due to the rotational rainbow effect and showed significantly less contribution from hotbands compared with previous experiments. Our data also allowed for measurement of the angular anisotropy parameter, β, over the range of NO (A) rotational states. We observed that for the lowest rotational levels, β changes from ∼ −0.3 at low excitation energy to ∼+0.4 at high excitation energy, which is consistent with previous work. Furthermore, we report β values for the highest rotational states formed at each excitation energy. Our experiment demonstrated that for a given excitation energy, the high rotational states consistently exhibited a more negative β compared with the low rotational levels. Qualitatively, this behavior can be attributed to excitation to regions of the excited state resembling a skewed T-shape that then dissociate to high NO (A) rotational levels with β ≤ 0.

I. Introduction

The NO ground state is the double degenerate X 2Π state with a bond length of 1.1508 Å and the lowest excited state is the A 2∑+ state. Complexation with an argon atom splits the ground state degeneracy resulting in a pair of Renner-Teller states having A′ and A″ symmetry with the latter state lower in energy. − For the higher energy A′ state of Ar-NO, the lone electron of NO lies in the plane of the complex while for the A″ state the lone electron is perpendicular to the complex plane. Theory and experiment agree that the overall Ar-NO ground state is approximately T-shape with a center-of-mass separation of about 3.7 Å. − As discussed below, theory and experiment also agree on the ground state dissociation energy but the theoretical excited state dissociation energy is lower than that determined from experimental values. To investigate this disagreement, we have used velocity map ion imaging to study photodissociation of the Ar-NO complex and report values for both ground and excited state dissociation energies.

The Ar-NO ground state dissociation energy, D 0″, from previous experimental and theoretical studies, is summarized in Table . Two clusters of experimental energies have been reported with values near 88 cm–1, , or near 95 cm–1. ,− Theoretical methods have treated the Ar-NO complex in Jacobi coordinates (R, θ) where R is the separation between Ar and the NO center-of-mass and θ is the angle between R and the NO bond, r. Numerous ground state potentials have been calculated by fixing the NO bond length to that of the NO (X) ground state and varying R and θ. The resulting potential energy surfaces gave zero-point ground state dissociation energies consistent with the lower clustering of experimental measurements. − However, a recent three-dimensional ab initio ground state potential varied the NO bond length in addition to R and θ and found a zero-point dissociation energy of 97.2 cm1, which is more consistent with newer experimental measurements.

The experimental excited state dissociation energy, D 0′, can be calculated from the ground state dissociation energy and the energies for the Ar-NO (A) ← Ar-NO (X) and the NO (A) ← NO (X) origin transition, as summarized by the cartoon in Figure S1. In Table we give D 0′ values calculated from previously reported D 0″ experimental measurements and the energy ranges from 44 cm–1 to 52 cm–1, with the latter value using the most recent experimental D 0″. , RCCSD­(T) calculations from Cybulski and Fernandez found a linear excited state with a dissociation energy of 32.6 cm–1 consistent with the unscaled potential from Klos et al. , The latter study also presented an excited state potential energy surface scaled by a factor of 1.23 to give a dissociation energy of 44 cm–1. ,

Determination of D 0′ from experimental data requires the Ar-NO (A) ← Ar-NO (X) origin transition energy, which is known from resonance enhanced multiphoton ionization (REMPI) spectroscopy. ,− The reported spectra consist of an origin band at 44242.1 cm–1 with two additional strong bands at higher energy. ,,,, The electronic excitation spectrum has been simulated using ab initio potentials for the Ar-NO ground state and the scaled excited potential energy surface. The theoretical spectrum was broadly consistent with experiment and showed the two strong features above the origin result from states having strongly mixed stretch–bend character accessed by the floppy Ar-NO complex.

Photodissociation of Ar-NO through the lowest excited state gives a ground state Ar atom and the NO (A) product. The minimum energy required to form the NO (A) product, which is termed the appearance energy (E appearance) has been measured with reported values summarized in Table . Both Tsuji et al. and Holmes-Ross and Lawrance reported an appearance energy near 44287 cm–1 but the latter noted their value may include a contribution from a nonzero average internal energy of Ar-NO formed in their molecular beam. , More recent measurements gave a higher E appearance value ranging between 44291 and 44294 cm1. ,,

For photoexcitation above E appearance, the additional energy is partitioned into center-of-mass (CM) recoil and rotational excitation of NO (A). Sato et al. observed a bimodal NO (A) rotational state distribution following photodissociation with the distribution peaking at high rotational quantum numbers and the second peak near the energetic limit. The observed rotational state distribution resulted from the rotational rainbow effect and model calculations reproduced the bimodal distribution. Parsons et al. photodissociated Ar-NO and used velocity map imaging with nonstate selective ionization of the NO (A) products to observe the rotational state distribution. The reported distribution was also bimodal with the second peak near the energetic limit, but the experimental resolution was not sufficient to observe individual rotational states and no definitive angular distribution was reported. Furthermore, at low excitation energies the rotational state distribution showed clear contribution of energetically disallowed rotational states, which originated from Ar-NO complexes having significant internal energy before excitation, e.g., hotbands. Roeterdink et al. coupled state-selective ionization with velocity map imaging to investigate the near threshold formation of NO (A, n) products where n is the rotational quantum number. This experiment focused on previously reported above-threshold resonant bands, which were observed in the NO (A, n = 2) yield spectrum and the resonances were attributed to dissociation through a type of vibrational Feshbach resonance. These experiments reported the angular distribution for NO (A) rotational levels but only up to 17 cm–1 above the appearance energy. Their reported anisotropy parameter, β, was negative but reduced from the limiting value expected for a perpendicular angular distribution and the reduction was attributed to a 13 ps predissociation lifetime for the complex. Holmes-Ross and coworkers reported a series of experiments using both NO (A) REMPI spectroscopy and velocity map imaging. ,,, Their results provided the rotational state distribution and showed high energy tails in the kinetic energy spectrum attributed to hot band contributions. , They also reported angular distributions for the lowest rotational levels of the NO (A) product and observed the anisotropy parameter changed from negative to positive as the excitation energy increased; however, the anisotropy was not reported for higher rotational states.

Here we report experiments using velocity map imaging with nonstate selective ionization of NO (A) to investigate photodissociation of the Ar-NO complex. Our experiments, conducted by a team of undergraduate students working alongside the principal investigator, show higher energy resolution compared with previous nonstate selective experiments. The images typically resolved the highest allowed NO (A) rotational levels and analysis of the data allowed the appearance energy as well as the ground and excited state dissociation energies to be determined. We also determined the NO (A) rotational state distribution as a function of excitation energy, which was bimodal and showed no significant contribution from hotbands. The velocity map images had sufficient resolution to determine the anisotropy parameter for individual or sets of rotational levels at all excitation energies. Therefore, we report the change in anisotropy parameter as a function of rotational quantum number for each excitation energy. Our experiments demonstrate that at high excitation energy, the product angular anisotropy changes from parallel to perpendicular as the rotational quantum number increases.

II. Results

For our velocity map imaging (VMI) experiments, described in the Methods section, the NO flight time was 9.5 μs, which resulted in a minimum velocity resolution of 5 m s–1 per pixel and the NO ionization energy is 9.2 eV. During ionization, most energy in excess of the ionization energy is partitioned into electron kinetic energy; however, a small fraction will also be partitioned into NO fragment recoil and this fraction is given by f=meme+mNO+ . Previous experiments with nonstate selective ionization used two photons near 225 nm to form the NO cation, and for this ionization scheme the NO recoil velocity is about 14 m s–1. Thus, NO ionization using this previous scheme would result in a minimum experimental resolution of about 3 pixels. Alternatively, for excitation with one 225 nm photon and subsequent ionization at 299.1 nm, the NO recoil velocity would be about 6 m s–1, corresponding to a minimum experimental resolution of about 1 pixel. To maximize image resolution, we have used the latter ionization scheme for all experiments reported here.

In the left frames of Figure , we present ion images of the NO fragment following photodissociation at photon energies of 44316 and 44630 cm–1, frames A and B respectively. The NO ion images at all other photon energies are given in Figures S2–S15. The ion images typically consist of a series of concentric rings at well-defined NO velocities. In this energy range, photodissociation of a simple Ar-NO complex can only partition energy into CM recoil and the energetically available NO (A) n rotational levels. Alternatively, higher order Ar x NO y clusters with x or y > 1 may also partition energy into either rotation or secondary dissociation of fragments such as Ar x or NO y‑1. Dissociation of higher Ar x NO y clusters would provide additional energy partitioning modes that blur rings observed in the images. As such, observation of sharp rings suggests the majority of NO fragments arise from photodissociation of a simple Ar-NO complex. Furthermore, the resulting CM translational energy distribution can be well fit assuming photodissociation of Ar-NO resulting in only the energetically allowed NO (A) rotational levels. Therefore, we analyze the ion images by extracting the NO velocity distribution and then transforming into a CM translational energy distribution, P(E T), assuming photodissociation of an Ar-NO complex and accounting for the velocity to energy Jacobian. III. Discussion

Analysis of the ion images gave an NO (A) appearance energy of 44294.3 ± 2.2 cm–1. As summarized in Table , previous measurements of the appearance energy have given values clustered near 44287 cm–1 or between 44291 and 44294 cm1. For the reported values near 44287 cm–1, Holmes-Ross and Lawrance noted that significant Ar-NO internal energy could result in the lower measured appearance energy. Furthermore, the additional internal energy from hotbands can result in rotational states beyond the available energy and these contributions are typically observed at low E avail. ,,, Fits to our P(E T) do not show population in any states beyond the available energy, which suggests hotbands do not significantly contribute to our appearance energy and our value is consistent with the most recent measurements. ,

From the appearance energy, the ground state dissociation energy, D 0″, of Ar-NO (X) may be calculated using, see Figure S1: 6 D0˝=Eappearance−ν00,NO where ν 00,NO is the NO (A) ← NO (X) origin band at 44198.9 cm–1. , Using our appearance energy we determine the Ar-NO ground state dissociation energy to be 95.4 ± 2.2 cm–1, which is consistent with the most recent experimental values in Table . , Our ground state dissociation energy is also consistent with the recent three-dimensional ground state potential energy surface, which gave a zero-point dissociation energy of 97.2 cm–1.

The excited state dissociation energy for Ar-NO (A),D 0′, is related to the ground state energy, see Figure S1, according to 7 D0′=ν00,NO−ν00,Ar−NO+D0˝ where ν 00,Ar–NO is the Ar-NO (A) ← Ar-NO (X) origin band. ,,, Using our ground state dissociation energy, (eq ) gives an excited state dissociation energy of 52 ± 2 cm–1. As discussed in the Introduction, we also used (eq ) to calculate the value for D 0′ from previously reported experimental values of D 0″, which are given in Table and our results are consistent with the most recent experiments. Table also summarizes results from recent theoretical work that show the Ar-NO excited state to have the deepest well for a linear geometry with the Ar atom closest to the nitrogen atom, i.e., linear Ar–N–O. As discussed in the Introduction, Cybulski et al. and Klos et al. used RCCSD­(T) calculations to determine D 0′ to be 32.6 cm–1 and 32.0 cm–1 respectively. , The latter study also introduced a scaling factor to give an excited state potential with a D 0′ value of 44 cm–1, which was consistent with Tsuji et al.’s experimental value, although it should be noted this experimental value was derived from a lower appearance energy of 44286.7 ± 0.3 cm–1. , Castro-Palacio et al. reported a CASSCF-MRCI excited state potential with the deepest well for linear Ar–N–O but with a shallow well depth of 20 cm–1. Ershova and Besley found density functional theory to give an unsatisfactory description of the excited state and their CCSD­(T) calculation gave D e′ to be 63 cm–1 but D 0′ was not reported.

Compared with our experiment, the theoretical excited state potential energy surfaces give lower D 0′ values. Our determination of D 0′ using depends on the established origin band transitions for Ar-NO and NO as well as the depth of the ground state well. Thus, the recent agreement between theory and experiment for a ground state dissociation energy of 97 or 95 cm–1, respectively, suggests a deeper excited state well compared with the theoretical predictions.

Our calculation of the appearance energy and ground and excited state dissociation energies assume Ar-NO has zero internal energy prior to excitation. While we cannot directly measure the Ar-NO (X) internal energy, we note that the rotational state distributions in Figures and S2–S15 show no evidence of energetically disallowed transitions due to hotbands, even near the energetic threshold. Furthermore, Holmes-Ross and Lawrance observed high energy tails due to hotbands in their reported kinetic energy distribution, with these tails extending about 100 cm–1 beyond the nominal available energy. , We expect hotbands to contribute most strongly near threshold; but, the P(E T) at the lowest available energy, 4 cm–1 Figure S2, extends only about 6 cm–1 beyond the energetic limit and we do not observe any significant high energy tails in the other CM translational energy distributions. However, we did observe a broad background in some ion images at available energies at or below 36 cm–1, see for example Figures S2 and S4 through Figure S6. We note that the background in Figure S2 is slightly asymmetric, which likely originates from a small angular tilt (<0.2°) of the molecular beam axis relative to the surface normal for the detector. For near threshold photodissociation, the photon wavelength is nearly resonant with higher rotational levels of the NO (A) ← NO (X) band, and special attention was required to carefully detune the excitation laser to avoid ionizing the small population of rotationally excited thermal NO (X) in our vacuum system. The broad background observed in some of the ion images below 36 cm–1 available energy could be due to either 1) background thermal NO (X) that is nonresonantly ionized or 2) dissociation of clusters having a broad distribution of internal energies approaching the dissociation limit, hotbands. We believe the former explanation is more consistent with the data since 1) the broad NO background is observed in some but not all images below 36 cm–1 and 2) the asymmetry observed in Figure S2 indicates that the background does not originate from NO in the molecular beam.

From fits to the observe P(E T), we determined the NO (A) rotational state distributions in Figures and S2–S15. Consistent with previous reports, the distribution of rotational states extends to the maximum allowed rotational level and for higher excitation energies, the distribution is bimodal, due to the rotational rainbow phenomena. Briefly, for photodissociation of a triatomic system, the observed rotational state distribution may be modeled using a rotational excitation function J(γ0) and weighting function W(γ0), where γ0 is the initial Jacobi angle. For photodissociation yielding a homonuclear diatomic, J(γ0) would be zero at 0°, 90°, 180° since these geometries result in no torque on the diatom and J(γ0) would be a maximum near 45°. For photodissociation yielding a heteronuclear diatomic, the magnitude of J(γ0) would be zero at 0° and 180° with a minimum around 90° and maxima near 45° and 135°; however, the potential anisotropy would result in different magnitudes for the two maxima. The weighting function, W(γ0), is determined in part by the equilibrium ground state geometry and a rotational rainbow occurs when the weighting function maps the maxima of the excitation function onto the observed rotational state distribution. Thus, for photodissociation yielding a heteronuclear diatomic fragment, a bimodal rotational state distribution is expected as observed in the data.

Previous work observed a bimodal distribution at available energies above about 50–100 cm–1. , In our experiments, we observed bimodal rotational state distributions as low as E avail = 28 cm–1 and extending to the highest excitation energies, Figures S5–S15. Thus, the data gives a lower onset for the bimodal distribution than previous experiments, which is consistent with lower contribution from hotbands in our experiment.

Finally, we consider the anisotropy parameter, β, extracted from the ion images. As discussed above, Roeterdink et al. reported the anisotropy for near threshold dissociation and the observed β values were slightly negative. Holmes-Ross et al. also reported the anisotropy parameter for the lowest NO (A) rotational level (n = 0) and found β changes sign from negative to positive as the excitation energy increased. , Figure shows the measured anisotropy parameter for the lowest NO (A) rotational level, n = 0, versus the available energy from our data.

Anisotropy parameter, β, versus available energy for the n = 0 level of NO (A).

Our experiment finds β < 0 below 150 cm–1 E avail and β > 0 at higher energy; which is consistent with previous work although the change in sign for β was found closer to 110 cm–1 available energy in the previous state-selective experiments. ,

Holmes-Ross et al. presented a model for the change in sign for β as energy increased. In the model, the overall excitation is treated as essentially localized to the NO moiety since the Ar atom provides only a weak perturbation. For a localized excitation, the A 2∑+ ← X 2Π transition dipole is perpendicular to the NO bond axis and so for any linear Ar-NO complex the transition dipole is also perpendicular to the recoil axis between Ar and the NO center of mass. Thus for dissociation of a linear complex geometry the limiting value of β would be −1. For T-shape Ar-NO the ground state transition dipole is either parallel to the recoil axis for A′ ← A′ excitation or perpendicular for A′ ← A″ excitation. As such, dissociation from a T-shape geometry results in β as a weighted average of −1 and 2 giving a mean value of +0.5. Owing to the large geometry change between the T-shape ground state and the linear excited state, different regions are sampled as the excitation energy increases. Thus, at high excitation energy the Franck–Condon region is accessible and photodissociation takes place from nearly T-shape regions of the excited state resulting in β > 0. However, for low excitation energies, the Franck–Condon region is energetically inaccessible and the maximum amplitude for the excited state shifts toward skewed Ar-NO geometries, which results in a negative observed β value.

The model qualitatively reproduced the change in β for the lowest rotational states as the excitation energy increased; however, the anisotropy of higher rotational levels were not reported. Our experiments allowed for simultaneous measurement of β for all NO (A) rotational levels at a given excitation energy and Figure C presents β versus n for an E avail of 337 cm–1. In Figure C, for the highest rotational levels the observed β is either 0 (isotropic) or slightly negative and similar behavior is apparent for available energies as low as 164 cm–1, Figures S11–15. Thus, Figure C shows a trend of decreasing β value as n increases and this behavior can be explained qualitatively using the above model. At high excitation energy (∼337 cm–1), the Franck–Condon region is accessible and for photodissociation of nearly T-shape Ar-NO only a small torque would be applied to the diatom resulting in low rotational excitation and β approaching +0.5. However, the skewed regions of the excited state near θ ∼ 45° and 135° are also accessible, albeit with smaller Franck–Condon overlap owing to the T-shape ground state. Photodissociation from these skewed regions results in a large torque giving rotationally excited NO; but with isotropic or slightly negative β values as observed in our experiment.

IV. Conclusions

We have presented experiments for the photodissociation of Ar-NO using velocity map imaging and have determined the NO (A) appearance energy and ground state dissociation energy in good agreement with recent experiments and the most recent ground state potential energy surface. ,, We determined an excited state dissociation energy that differs from the theoretical value for zero-point dissociation energy by about 20 cm–1, which suggests that the excited state well may be deeper than predicted. We observed a bimodal NO (A) rotational state distribution consistent with the rotational rainbow phenomena and with an onset at lower energies than observed in previous experiments. The lower onset may result from reduced hotband contribution in the current work. Finally, we determined the anisotropy parameter for both low and high rotational levels as a function of excitation energy and we found that β decreases as the rotational state increases, which is consistent with a model linking the observed anisotropy to skewed and T-shape regions of the excited state.

V. Methods

The setup for our experiments has been described previously − and we discuss only the details here. For these experiments we expand a gas mixture consisting of 2% NO (Sigma-Aldrich) and 98% Ar (Linweld) using an Even-Lavie nozzle (Lamid, 150 mm orifice, ∼20 μs pulse) with a nozzle stagnation pressure of about 11 atm. , The pressure in the source region of our vacuum system is about 5 × 10–6 Torr during experiments and the expanding gas forms a supersonic jet, which passes through a 3 mm skimmer (Beam Dynamics) located 5 cm downstream from the nozzle. The molecular beam temperature for our apparatus, operating under similar conditions, has been measured to be around 5 K and other molecular beam experiments under a variety of conditions have observed the population in the upper NO 2Π3/2 spin–orbit state to be negligible. − The resulting molecular beam propagates along the centerline of our conventional velocity map imaging ion optics. Between the first and second plates of the ion optics, we cross the molecular beam with laser light near 225 nm generated by frequency doubling the output of a 355 nm pumped dye laser (Sirah Cobra Stretch) operating with Coumarin 450 dye (Exciton/Luxottica). We control the 225 nm laser power using a variable waveplate (Alphalas GmbH) and Glan laser polarizer (ThorLabs) set to pass vertically polarized light and typically we use less than 250 μJ/pulse for experiments. We also intersect the molecular beam with light at 299.1 nm, which is generated by pumping a H2 Raman shifter with the 266 nm output of a Nd:YAG laser (Amplitude SureLite). In our experiment, the 225 nm light photolyzes the Ar-NO complex and the 299.1 nm light then ionizes NO (A) fragments. The laser overlap is optimized using a three-axis translation stage and for all experiments the measured signal was confirmed to be zero with either photolysis or ionization laser blocked.

After photolysis and ionization, the VMI ion optics project NO+ onto a microchannel plate (MCP) detector (Sydor Technology). We obtain ion images by pulsing the MCP voltage at the m/z = 30 arrival time and a CMOS camera (ThorLabs CC126MU) records 10 ms images synchronized with the ionization laser. Individual images are recorded and then event counted with a Python script using the 3.10 SciKit Imaging libraries and the composite image is Abel inverted using the Basex software. Finally, we use three digital delay generators (SRS DG535 and Quantum Composers 9514+) to synchronize timing for the lasers, molecular beam, MCP pulser and CMOS camera.
