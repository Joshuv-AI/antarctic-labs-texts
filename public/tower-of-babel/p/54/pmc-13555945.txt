Determinants of surgical specialty interest among clinical-year medical students in Syria: a multicenter cross-sectional study
Authors: Mohammad Alaa Aldakak, Rahaf Massoud, Zeina Jamaleh, Mariam Mahfoud, Ibrahim Alkhatib, Hamza Hdeoa, Aghyad Akmik, Heba Attaf, Youssef Latifeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurgical workforce recruitment remains a global concern, particularly given persistent gender disparities in surgical specialties. In Syria, evidence on medical students’ surgical career intentions remains limited. This study assessed planned surgical specialty interest and associated demographic, educational, exposure-related, mentorship, and gender/social perception factors among fourth-, fifth-, and sixth-year medical students.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was conducted among medical students in Syria between January and May 2026. The Arabic questionnaire assessed sociodemographic characteristics, surgical exposure, mentorship, specialty preferences, perceived determinants of specialty choice, and surgery-related perceptions. The primary outcome was planned entry into a surgical specialty versus non-surgical or undecided preference. Data were analyzed using bivariate tests and multivariable binary logistic regression.ResultsA total of 680 students were included; 350 were female (51.5%), and the median age was 23 years (IQR, 22–24). Overall, 252 students (37.1%) planned to enter a surgical specialty. Males were more likely than females to plan a surgical specialty (47.0% vs. 27.7%, p < 0.001). In the adjusted model, females without hijab (AOR = 0.445, 95% CI: 0.290–0.684, p < 0.001) and females with hijab (AOR = 0.313, 95% CI: 0.198–0.494, p < 0.001) had lower odds of surgical specialty interest compared with males. Observing surgeries for more than 20 h (AOR = 3.054, 95% CI: 1.405–6.639, p = 0.005) and having a surgeon mentor (AOR = 2.342, 95% CI: 1.537–3.568, p < 0.001) were associated with higher odds of surgical interest.ConclusionSurgical specialty interest among Syrian medical students was associated with sex/hijab status, operative exposure, mentorship, and university. Structured surgical exposure, formal mentorship, and visible role models may support more informed and equitable surgical career choice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09795-3.

Introduction

Surgical specialties remain essential to healthcare systems because they provide definitive management for a wide range of emergency and elective conditions, yet many countries continue to report concerns regarding the future surgical workforce and the recruitment of medical students into surgical careers [1, 2]. Interest in surgery is shaped by a complex interaction of personal interest, clinical exposure, perceived lifestyle, mentorship, working conditions, financial expectations, and gender-related perceptions [1, 3, 4]. Although women now represent a substantial proportion, and in some countries the majority, of medical students, their representation remains disproportionately lower in several surgical specialties and at senior surgical career stages [5, 6]. These disparities are not limited to individual preference; they may also reflect unequal access to operative exposure, mentorship, role models, and supportive learning environments during medical training [2, 5, 6].

Gender differences in surgical career interest appear early during medical education and may be reinforced by limited mentorship, lack of same-gender role models, concerns about long working hours and family life, and perceptions of physical demands or gender-based stereotyping [2, 5, 6]. Recent studies have shown that hands-on surgical activities, active participation in the operating room, structured mentorship, and supportive supervision are associated with greater surgical career interest, particularly when students can meaningfully interact with surgical teams and develop a sense of belonging in the surgical environment [2, 7]. Conversely, poor work-life balance, high workload, hierarchical environments, lack of mentorship, and gender-based stereotypes have repeatedly been identified as deterrents to surgical careers [1, 8, 9].

Evidence from low- and middle-income countries suggests that the educational environment may be especially important in shaping surgical career intentions. In Kenya, Mwachiro et al. reported that surgical trainees’ perceptions of their learning environment differed by training paradigm and gender and were influenced by institutional setting and faculty availability [10]. Studies from sub-Saharan Africa also emphasize the role of social support, mentorship, and gender-equity structures in medical and surgical career development. For example, qualitative work from Rwanda identified teacher, family, financial or institutional support, and access to female mentors or role models as important support systems for female students pursuing medical education [11]. More broadly, recent African surgical training data highlight persistent gender-related barriers, including exclusion, harassment, and negative patient perceptions, underscoring the need to examine surgical career choice within its local educational and sociocultural context [12].

In Syria, limited available evidence suggests marked gender differences in specialty preference, with surgical specialties chosen by 40% of male medical students compared with 15% of female medical students at Aleppo University [13]. The same Syrian study showed that female students were more likely to prioritize work-life balance and to perceive reduced opportunities for professional advancement because of gender, suggesting that specialty choice may be shaped by sociocultural and gender-related factors in addition to academic or clinical interests [13]. However, available Syrian data remain limited and largely single-center, and there is a lack of multicenter evidence examining how surgical exposure, mentorship, perceived lifestyle, gender-related attitudes, and social factors are associated with surgical specialty interest among fourth-, fifth-, and sixth-year medical students. Therefore, this study aimed to assess planned surgical specialty interest among fourth-, fifth-, and sixth-year medical students in Syria and to identify the demographic, educational, exposure-related, and gender/social perception factors associated with planned surgical specialty interest.

Methods

Study design, setting, and participants

This was a cross-sectional online survey conducted among undergraduate medical students in Syria between January and May 2026. The target population included students enrolled in the fourth, fifth, and sixth years of medical school, which represent the clinical years in the Syrian undergraduate medical curriculum. Students in earlier medical school years and individuals who were not medical students were excluded. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and no financial or academic incentives were provided.

In the Syrian medical education system, surgical teaching is introduced progressively during the clinical years, although the structure and intensity of exposure may vary across universities and teaching hospitals. Based on the internal curriculum structure of Damascus University, which was the largest participating university, fourth-year students receive approximately 60 h of general surgery teaching and approximately 60 h of cardiothoracic and vascular surgery teaching. Fifth-year students receive approximately 40 h of neurosurgery and urology teaching and approximately 60 h of pediatric, plastic, and orthopedic surgery teaching. Sixth-year students complete an approximately 8-week surgery clerkship, corresponding to about 240 h. However, operative-room observation is not uniformly mandatory or structured, and students generally observe rather than assist in procedures. Formal surgical skills laboratory exposure is limited, with practical teaching focused mainly on history taking and physical examination. Therefore, actual surgical exposure may vary by university, teaching hospital, supervising team, and student initiative.

A convenience sampling strategy was used. The questionnaire was distributed electronically through student social media platforms, university-related groups, and student communication channels. The survey was administered using Google Forms. Because the questionnaire link was distributed through open electronic channels, the total number of students who received or viewed the survey invitation could not be determined; therefore, a true response rate could not be calculated. A total of 690 responses were submitted. Ten responses were excluded because of incomplete or internally inconsistent data, leaving 680 complete responses for the final analysis. The participant flow is shown in Supplementary Figure S1.

Sample size

The estimated population of fourth-, fifth-, and sixth-year medical students in Syria was approximately 20,000. Assuming a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and a conservative expected proportion of 50%, the minimum required sample size was approximately 377 participants after finite population correction. The final analytic sample of 680 participants exceeded this minimum requirement.

Questionnaire development and measures

The questionnaire was developed by the study team in Arabic. It was designed specifically for this study to assess medical students’ interest in surgery, prior surgical exposure, mentorship, perceived determinants of specialty choice, and surgery-related perceptions. The questionnaire was reviewed for content and clarity by a physician specialized in medical education and by a surgeon. Because the instrument was not intended to function as a validated psychometric scale, no composite score was calculated; all Likert-type items were analyzed individually.

The questionnaire collected data on sociodemographic characteristics, including age, gender, academic year, university type, university, academic performance, relationship status, caregiving responsibilities, place of upbringing, and governorate. Place of upbringing and governorate were collected as contextual geographic variables because students from different settings may have different educational backgrounds, access to teaching hospitals, exposure to surgical role models, and opportunities for informal clinical exposure. Surgery-related exposure variables included having a physician in the first-degree family, previous observation of surgery in the operating room, total time spent observing surgeries, completion of a surgery rotation, participation in surgical skills workshops, participation in surgery-related research, presence of a surgeon mentor, mentor specialty, whether the mentor was of the same gender, exposure to surgery-focused media, perceived adequacy of information to choose a future specialty, and perceived need for more structured exposure to surgical specialties.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome was planned entry into a surgical specialty. Participants were asked, “What specialty are you planning to enter?” with response options including general surgery, cardiothoracic surgery, neurosurgery, plastic and reconstructive surgery, orthopedic surgery, urology, other surgical specialties, undecided, and other non-surgical specialties. Responses were dichotomized into surgical specialty versus non-surgical or undecided. General surgery, cardiothoracic surgery, neurosurgery, plastic and reconstructive surgery, orthopedic surgery, urology, and other surgical specialties were classified as surgical specialties. Undecided responses and other non-surgical specialties were classified as non-surgical or undecided.

Likert-scale item coding

Importance factors related to specialty choice were assessed using a five-point Likert scale: 1 = not important at all, 2 = not important, 3 = moderately important, 4 = important, and 5 = very important. For comparative analyses, these items were recoded into three categories: not important, moderately important/neutral, and important. Specifically, responses 1–2 were categorized as not important, response 3 as moderately important/neutral, and responses 4–5 as important.

Surgery-related perception and social/gender-related items were assessed using a five-point agreement scale: 1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, and 5 = strongly agree. These items were recoded into three categories for analysis: disagree, neutral, and agree. Responses 1–2 were categorized as disagree, response 3 as neutral, and responses 4–5 as agree.

Data management

All submitted responses were reviewed and cleaned before analysis. Responses with incomplete or internally inconsistent data were excluded. Because only 10 of 690 submitted responses were excluded and the final analytic dataset contained complete data for all variables included in the final tables and models, a complete-case analysis was performed and no imputation was used. Small university categories were grouped into an “Other” category to reduce sparse cells, and governorates with small frequencies were similarly grouped.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26. Continuous and ordinal variables were summarized as median and interquartile range when appropriate, while categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Bivariate associations between categorical variables and planned surgical specialty interest were examined using Fisher’s exact test for 2 × 2 tables and Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact testing with Monte Carlo estimation for larger contingency tables, as appropriate. Effect sizes were reported using Phi for 2 × 2 comparisons and Cramer’s V for larger tables. For item-level analyses, percentages were calculated within each planned-specialty group. Place of upbringing, academic year, and governorate were examined in bivariate analyses because of their potential relevance to educational exposure and specialty choice.

A multivariable binary logistic regression model was used to identify factors independently associated with planning to enter a surgical specialty. Variables were selected based on statistical significance in bivariate analyses and theoretical relevance to surgical career interest. The final model included sex/hijab status, total time spent observing surgeries, participation in surgical skills workshops, participation in surgery-related research, having a surgeon mentor, and university. Adjusted odds ratios, 95% confidence intervals, regression coefficients, standard errors, Wald statistics, and p-values were reported. Model fit was assessed using the omnibus test of model coefficients, Nagelkerke R², the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and classification performance.

Because surgical exposure may differ across fourth-, fifth-, and sixth-year students, additional analyses were conducted to address potential heterogeneity by academic year. First, exposure-related characteristics and mentorship were summarized by academic year. Second, a sensitivity multivariable logistic regression model was performed by adding academic year to the primary model. Separate year-specific multivariable models were not fitted because some operative exposure categories, particularly more than 20 h of operative observation, contained small numbers after stratification.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Damascus University under approval number MD-230226-642. Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants before starting the questionnaire. Participation was voluntary, and the survey was anonymous, with no names, student identification numbers, or directly identifying information collected.

Results

After exclusions, a total of 680 medical students were included in the final analysis. The median age was 23 years (IQR, 22–24), and the sample was nearly balanced by gender, with 350 females (51.5%) and 330 males (48.5%). Most participants were enrolled in public universities (80.9%), and the largest university groups were Damascus University (30.6%), Tishreen University (25.4%), and Homs University (16.3%). Sixth-year students represented the largest academic-year group (43.1%), followed by fourth-year (29.3%) and fifth-year students (27.6%). Most participants were single or not in a relationship (92.4%), reported no children or caregiving responsibilities (96.0%), and had an urban upbringing (72.2%) (Table 1).

Regarding surgery-related exposure, 454 students (66.8%) had previously observed surgery in the operating room, although only 38 (5.6%) had spent more than 20 h observing surgeries. Most students had completed a surgery rotation (87.1%), while 237 (34.9%) had participated in surgical skills workshops and 87 (12.8%) had participated in surgery-related research. A surgeon mentor was reported by 156 students (22.9%), most commonly in general surgery, cardiothoracic surgery, or orthopedic surgery. Overall, 605 students (89.0%) reported that they would benefit from more structured exposure to surgical specialties (Table 2).

When exposure-related characteristics were summarized by academic year, completion of a surgery rotation increased from 69.8% among fourth-year students to 91.0% among fifth-year students and 96.2% among sixth-year students. Similarly, the proportion of students who had not attended surgery before decreased from 50.8% among fourth-year students to 30.9% among fifth-year students and 22.9% among sixth-year students. The proportion reporting more than 20 h of operative observation was low overall but increased across academic years, from 1.5% among fourth-year students to 5.3% among fifth-year students and 8.5% among sixth-year students. Having a surgeon mentor was reported by 18.6%, 24.5%, and 24.9% of fourth-, fifth-, and sixth-year students, respectively (Table S2).

Overall, 252 students (37.1%) planned to enter a surgical specialty, whereas 428 (62.9%) were either undecided or planned to enter a non-surgical specialty. Among planned specialties, undecided responses were most frequent (33.7%), followed by other non-surgical specialties (29.3%), general surgery (10.6%), orthopedic surgery (7.6%), and plastic and reconstructive surgery (6.0%). Median interest scores were highest for general surgery (median, 5; IQR, 3–8), followed by plastic and reconstructive surgery and cardiothoracic surgery (Table 3).

In bivariate analyses, planned surgical specialty interest was significantly associated with gender, sex/hijab status, university, total time spent observing surgeries, participation in surgical skills workshops, participation in surgery-related research, having a surgeon mentor, and having a surgeon mentor of the same gender. For example, males were more likely than females to plan a surgical specialty (47.0% vs. 27.7%, p < 0.001), and students with a surgeon mentor were more likely to plan a surgical specialty than those without one (54.5% vs. 31.9%, p < 0.001) (Table S1). In contrast, place of upbringing (rural vs. urban), academic year, and governorate were not significantly associated with planned surgical specialty interest in bivariate analyses. Planned surgical specialty interest was 40.2% among students with rural upbringing and 35.8% among those with urban upbringing (Fisher’s exact p = 0.330), and did not differ significantly across academic years (p = 0.126) or governorates (p = 0.161).

When importance factors were compared by planned specialty choice, several factors differed significantly between students planning surgical and non-surgical or undecided careers. Students planning surgery more frequently rated hands-on procedures as important (82.5% vs. 62.4%, p < 0.001), as well as teamwork and the operating room environment (80.2% vs. 61.0%, p < 0.001) and intellectual challenge/case complexity (61.1% vs. 48.8%, p = 0.008). In contrast, work-life balance and working hours/on-call frequency were more strongly weighted among non-surgical or undecided students. These patterns were also reflected in the signed Cramer’s V plot, where the strongest positive associations favored hands-on procedures and teamwork/OR environment, while the strongest negative associations favored working hours/on-call frequency and work-life balance (Table 4 and Fig. 1).

Discussion

Principal findings

In this multicenter cross-sectional study of fourth-, fifth-, and sixth-year medical students in Syria, 37.1% of participants planned to enter a surgical specialty, whereas the remainder were undecided or planned to pursue non-surgical specialties. In the adjusted model, planned surgical specialty interest was independently associated with sex/hijab status, operative observation exposure, having a surgeon mentor, and university. Compared with males, females without hijab had lower odds of planned surgical specialty interest (AOR = 0.445, 95% CI: 0.290–0.684, p < 0.001), as did females with hijab (AOR = 0.313, 95% CI: 0.198–0.494, p < 0.001). Students who had observed surgeries for more than 20 h had higher odds of planning a surgical specialty (AOR = 3.054, 95% CI: 1.405–6.639, p = 0.005), and having a surgeon mentor was also independently associated with surgical interest (AOR = 2.342, 95% CI: 1.537–3.568, p < 0.001). These findings suggest that surgical career interest in Syria is shaped by gender-related context, meaningful operative exposure, mentorship, and institutional environment, rather than by personal preference alone.

Gender differences and the interpretation of sex/hijab status

The gender gap observed in our study is consistent with the limited available Syrian evidence. Asaad et al. in Syria reported that surgical specialties were preferred by 40% of male medical students compared with 15% of female students at Aleppo University, and that female students were more likely to prioritize work-life balance and perceive fewer opportunities for professional advancement because of gender [13]. Our adjusted findings reinforce this local pattern, as both female groups had significantly lower odds of planned surgical specialty interest compared with males.

However, the sex/hijab variable should be interpreted cautiously. It should not be understood as showing a causal effect of hijab itself, but rather as an exploratory contextual marker that may capture overlapping gendered, cultural, social, and visibility-related dimensions influencing how female students perceive surgical careers in Syria. To our knowledge, direct evidence examining hijab or religious dress itself as an independent determinant of surgical specialty interest remains limited. Therefore, this finding warrants further qualitative exploration rather than causal interpretation. In the Syrian setting, this variable may reflect perceived social expectations, family or community norms, anticipated acceptability of surgical work, and perceived barriers within surgical training environments.

Broader regional and international evidence supports the importance of gendered context in surgical career choice. Dawood et al. in Pakistan reported greater preference for surgery among male students and identified lack of mentorship, gender inequity, and family obligations as deterrents [8]. Ser et al. in Malaysia found that male students were more likely to select purely surgical specialties, whereas female students gave greater weight to work-life balance and working conditions [14]. Gnanalingham et al. in the United Kingdom reported that men were nearly twice as likely as women to consider cardiothoracic surgery, while female students more often identified gender, lack of same-gender mentorship, and long working hours as barriers [6]. Similarly, Entezari et al. in Canada found that female students considering orthopedic surgery reported lower exposure, less mentorship, fewer same-gender mentors, and more discouragement than male students [15]. Evidence from African settings also supports this interpretation: Neil et al. in Rwanda identified family, teacher, financial or institutional support, and access to female mentors or role models as important support systems for female medical students [11], while Mitei et al. reported persistent gender-related barriers among surgical trainees in Africa, including exclusion, harassment, and negative patient perceptions [12].

Operative exposure and mentorship

Operative exposure was one of the strongest modifiable factors in our model. Students who had observed surgeries for more than 20 h had approximately threefold higher adjusted odds of planned surgical specialty interest compared with those who had not attended surgery before (AOR = 3.054, p = 0.005). This finding is especially relevant in Syria because operative-room observation is not uniformly mandatory or structured, and students generally observe rather than assist. Therefore, students with greater operative exposure may have had more opportunities to understand the surgical environment, interact with surgical teams, and imagine themselves in a surgical role.

This finding is consistent with several studies. Sunter et al. in Türkiye reported that students who had opportunities to observe surgeons’ working conditions were more likely to choose surgery [1]. Homberg et al. in Germany found that positive hands-on surgical experiences, including assisting and suturing, were associated with later surgical career aspirations in both sexes [2]. Chen et al. in Taiwan also showed that a laparoscopic training workshop improved technical performance and increased mean surgical career interest after the workshop [7]. Although our study measured observation rather than active operative participation, the direction of findings supports the importance of meaningful surgical exposure.

Because operative exposure may differ across academic years, we examined whether academic-year heterogeneity explained the main associations. In stratified descriptive analyses, completion of surgical rotations and time spent observing surgery increased across training years, but academic year itself was not significantly associated with planned surgical specialty interest. In the sensitivity regression model additionally adjusted for academic year, the main associations with sex/hijab status, more than 20 h of operative observation, and surgeon mentorship remained materially unchanged. This suggests that the observed associations were not explained solely by differences between fourth-, fifth-, and sixth-year students.

Having a surgeon mentor was also independently associated with planned surgical specialty interest (AOR = 2.342, p < 0.001), making mentorship one of the clearest actionable findings in our study. Dawood et al. in Pakistan identified lack of mentorship as a major deterrent to surgical interest [8], while Dawar et al. in Pakistan described how a surgery interest group in a low- and middle-income country provided exposure, skills training, and academic engagement for medical students [9]. Odonkor et al. in a multinational neurosurgery survey found that only 27.1% of trainees had ever had a neurosurgery mentor, and that 59.0% of female respondents reported that access to a female neurosurgeon mentor would increase their interest in neurosurgery [5]. In Syria, where formal career advising and structured surgical exposure may be limited, mentorship may help students clarify surgical pathways, understand training demands, and overcome uncertainty, particularly among students without family or social connections in surgical fields.

Participation in surgical skills workshops and surgery-related research were significant in bivariate analyses but did not remain independent predictors in the adjusted model. This should not be interpreted as evidence that these activities are unimportant. Rather, their effects may overlap with mentorship, previous motivation, and operative exposure. Workshops and research opportunities may be more impactful if integrated into longitudinal mentorship and structured exposure programs rather than offered as isolated activities.

Lifestyle, workload, and economic considerations

The item-level analyses showed that lifestyle concerns were more aligned with non-surgical or undecided specialty preference. Work-life balance and working hours/on-call frequency were among the strongest factors favoring non-surgical or undecided preference in the signed Cramer’s V analysis. This finding is consistent with Asaad et al. in Syria, who reported that female students at Aleppo University prioritized work-life balance more than male students [13]. It also aligns with Alhammad et al. in Saudi Arabia, Dawood et al. in Pakistan, and Sunter et al. in Türkiye, who reported that lifestyle, family responsibilities, training hours, workload, and working conditions influence specialty choice [1, 8, 16].

Similar concerns have also been reported outside the region. McLennan et al. in Canada found that pressure on personal relationships, resident working hours, surgeon working hours, and competitiveness were perceived barriers to surgical careers [17]. Ketzer et al. in Germany and Austria reported that work-life balance, family compatibility, part-time options, hierarchy, and physical workload were especially relevant for female students considering orthopedic and trauma surgery [18]. Gnanalingham et al. in the United Kingdom similarly found that female students more frequently identified long working hours and physical demands as barriers to cardiothoracic surgery [6].

In Syria, these concerns should be understood within a setting of prolonged training, economic strain, and uncertainty about future professional stability. Our data do not directly measure the national economic situation or the structure of surgical practice; however, the relevance of income, job opportunities, and lifestyle in bivariate analyses suggests that financial and professional security may influence how students evaluate surgical careers. Students may perceive surgical practice as requiring a longer pathway to independent practice, greater dependence on established senior surgeons or high-volume centers, and less immediate professional autonomy compared with some non-surgical specialties that may allow earlier clinic-based work. This interpretation remains exploratory, but it may help explain why lifestyle and career-security concerns were more aligned with non-surgical or undecided preferences in our cohort.

Perceived fit and gender-related barriers

Perceived fit with surgery was one of the most important themes in our findings. Students planning surgical careers were more likely to agree that they could imagine themselves working in a surgical specialty, that they would belong in a surgical team, and that they had enough information to decide. These items had some of the strongest signed effect sizes, suggesting that surgical interest is closely linked to professional identity, perceived belonging, and confidence in career decision-making.

This aligns with Nguyen et al. in Vietnam, who found that specialty choice is shaped by alignment between students’ personal attributes, professional identity, and perceived specialty demands [19]. Godbole et al. in India and Tobiaszewska et al. in Poland also support the concept of person–specialty fit in specialty preference [3, 4]. In our cohort, perceived belonging in a surgical team may be particularly important because formal operative participation is limited and students may rely heavily on observation, informal feedback, and role models to decide whether surgery feels accessible to them.

Gender-related perception items further help explain why some students may not see surgery as accessible or sustainable. Non-surgical or undecided students more often endorsed perceptions that surgical practice was too stressful, that surgical work required physical effort beyond their ability, and that gender stereotypes could negatively affect a surgical career. Kumar et al. in India reported similar barriers in orthopedic surgery, including lack of female mentors, perceived physical demands, gender bias, and social stereotypes [20]. Şimşek et al. in Türkiye found that female students were more likely to perceive inequality and a glass ceiling in neurosurgery, although most students rejected the idea that women lack the physical ability for neurosurgery [21]. These findings suggest that addressing misinformation, stereotypes, and hidden-curriculum messages may be as important as increasing exposure itself.

Institutional differences

University remained independently associated with surgical specialty interest. Compared with Damascus University, students from Homs University (AOR = 2.320, 95% CI: 1.391–3.871, p = 0.001) and Aleppo University (AOR = 2.136, 95% CI: 1.018–4.482, p = 0.045) had higher odds of planning a surgical specialty. This finding should be interpreted cautiously, as our cross-sectional design cannot determine whether these differences reflect student composition, clinical exposure, surgical teaching quality, mentorship availability, student-to-faculty ratio, or institutional culture.

The university-level association may be particularly relevant in Syria because medical schools may share broad similarities in overall structure but differ in implementation, teaching-hospital organization, department culture, and informal access to operative observation. Damascus University is the largest participating institution and has a substantially larger student body than many other medical schools, which may reduce individualized access to surgical teams, mentors, and operative environments. Mwachiro et al. in Kenya showed that perceptions of the surgical learning environment differed by training paradigm, institutional setting, faculty availability, and gender [10]. This supports interpreting the university variable in our study as a possible marker of learning-environment differences rather than as a direct causal effect of university itself. Further qualitative and longitudinal research is needed to determine which institutional factors most strongly shape surgical career interest in Syrian medical schools.

Educational implications

Our findings suggest several practical priorities. First, surgical exposure should be more structured and active. Students should not only observe operations but also receive supervised opportunities for operating-room orientation, basic skills practice, suturing, assisting where appropriate, and interaction with surgical teams. Homberg et al. in Germany and Chen et al. in Taiwan support the role of hands-on surgical activities and laparoscopic training in strengthening surgical interest [2, 7]. Second, mentorship should be formalized, especially for female students and students without existing access to surgeons. Evidence from Dawar et al. in Pakistan, Odonkor et al. in multinational neurosurgery settings, Gnanalingham et al. in the United Kingdom, and Entezari et al. in Canada supports the importance of mentorship and same-gender role models [5, 6, 9, 15]. Third, students need realistic information about surgical training, workload, career pathways, income expectations, and family-life compatibility. This is particularly important in Syria, where financial uncertainty and limited structured advising may make career decisions more dependent on informal impressions.

Strengths and limitations

This study included a large multicenter sample of fourth-, fifth-, and sixth-year medical students from Syria and examined surgical specialty interest using demographic, educational, exposure-related, mentorship, lifestyle, and gender/social perception variables. Unlike studies limited to simple preference reporting, we used bivariate analyses, effect sizes, multivariable logistic regression, and sensitivity analysis adjusted for academic year.

However, several limitations should be considered. The cross-sectional design prevents causal inference, and specialty plans may change over time. Convenience sampling through online distribution may have introduced selection bias, and because the survey link was distributed through open electronic channels, a true response rate could not be calculated. Only 10 submitted responses were excluded because of incomplete or internally inconsistent data, and no imputation was performed; although this represents a small proportion of submitted responses, non-random missingness cannot be completely excluded. The questionnaire was developed for this study and was not a validated psychometric scale; therefore, Likert-type items were analyzed individually rather than combined into composite scores. The study relied on self-reported exposure and perceptions rather than objective measures of operative participation, mentorship quality, or institutional training structure. University-level differences should also be interpreted cautiously because they may reflect unmeasured curricular, hospital-level, supervisory, or student-composition differences. Finally, sex/hijab status was an exploratory contextual variable and should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal effect of hijab or religious dress on surgical specialty interest.

Conclusion

Planned surgical specialty interest among Syrian fourth-, fifth-, and sixth-year medical students was independently associated with gender-related context, operative exposure, surgeon mentorship, and university. The strongest modifiable findings were operative exposure and mentorship, while lifestyle concerns, perceived fit, and gender-related barriers helped explain why many students remained non-surgical or undecided. In Syria, efforts to support informed surgical career choice should focus on structured operative exposure, formal mentorship, visible female surgical role models, and transparent counseling about surgical training and career realities. Future qualitative and longitudinal research is needed to clarify how cultural, gender-related, institutional, and educational factors interact to shape surgical career decisions.
