Documentation-based fall categories, baseline functional status, and diagnosed multiregional injury after falls in older adults: A single-center retrospective study
Authors: Takahito Hirai, Yoko Tarumi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTContext:Older adults presenting to the emergency department (ED) after a fall show substantial heterogeneity in injury burden. However, the relationship between routinely documented fall information at presentation and broader injury distribution remains unclear.Aims:To examine whether documentation-based fall categories and baseline functional status information are associated with multiregional injury in older adults after falls.Settings and Design:Single-center, retrospective observational study conducted in a tertiary care ED from October 2022 to March 2024.Materials and Methods:Consecutive patients aged ≥65 years presenting after a fall were included. Falls were classified as A, dizziness/presyncope-associated; B, tripping/slipping/environmental; C, loss of balance during movement/transfer; and D, indeterminate/unclear. Baseline activities of daily living (ADL) were categorized as independent, dependent, or undocumented. The primary outcome was multiregional injury, defined as injuries involving ≥2 of seven body regions.Statistical Analysis Used:Multivariable logistic regression and sensitivity analysis.Results:Among 1014 cases (median age, 82 years; 41.1% male), multiregional injury occurred in 38.6%. Category D was associated with lower odds of multiregional injury than category B (adjusted odds ratio [OR], 0.68; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.48–0.98). Undocumented baseline ADL (adjusted OR, 1.44; 95% CI, 1.01–2.05) and ambulance transport (adjusted OR, 1.64; 95% CI, 1.08–2.49) were associated with higher odds. Sensitivity analyses showed similar patterns.Conclusion:Documentation-based fall categories, ambulance transport, and undocumented baseline functional status were associated with multiregional injury after falls in older adults. Nevertheless, these findings are exploratory, as documentation quality and diagnostic intensity may have influenced the outcomes.

Introduction

Falls are a leading cause of emergency department (ED) visits among older adults, contributing to morbidity and functional decline.[123] Fall-related injury patterns vary widely, with some patients sustaining injuries in multiple body regions.[456] Information on fall circumstances and baseline function is readily available during ED assessment and may help clinicians identify patients who warrant broader trauma evaluation, thereby supporting earlier recognition of multiregional injury and potentially reducing missed injuries or delayed diagnosis. Baseline activities of daily living (ADL) status is frequently missing or undocumented in emergency settings, and this status may itself be clinically informative.[7] Accordingly, we aimed to examine the association between fall mechanism, ADL category, and multiregional injury in older adults presenting to the ED after a fall.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This single-center, retrospective observational study was conducted in the tertiary care ED of a hospital in Japan from October 1, 2022, to March 10, 2024. The procedures followed were in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible committee and with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for written informed consent was waived because this was a retrospective study using existing de-identified clinical records. An opt-out notice was provided via the institutional website in accordance with institutional policy.

Participants

Using the electronic medical record system, ED visits were screened by searching for the keyword “fall” in the history of present illness field. Eligibility was confirmed through manual chart review. Consecutive patients aged ≥65 years who presented to the ED after a fall were included. Falls were defined as unintentional events that cause the patient to come to rest on the ground or at a lower level.

Visits were excluded if (1) chart review excluded a fall event, (2) the fall was from a height, or (3) the fall was related to a traffic injury. Patient data were retained whenever the primary outcome was ascertained. To preserve real-world generalizability, additional clinical exclusion criteria were not applied.

Classification of fall mechanisms

Fall mechanisms were classified into four predefined categories based on the history documented in the medical records.

Category D was treated as a distinct category, rather than being excluded, because uncertainty in the mechanism may represent clinically relevant vulnerabilities (e.g., impaired recall and unreliable history) or limitations in documentation.

Baseline activity of daily living

Frailty was operationalized using baseline ADL status before injury, as documented in the medical records. ADL status was categorized as independent, defined as independent ambulation and self-care; dependent, defined as requiring any assistance, use of a mobility aid (e.g., cane, walker, or wheelchair), or total assistance; or unknown, when there was insufficient information to determine baseline ADL.

Missing ADL information was not imputed and was classified as the “unknown” category in all the analyses.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was multiregional injury, defined a priori as injury involving two or more of the following seven predefined body regions: posterior or lateral head, anterior head or face, spine or back, thorax or abdomen, pelvis or proximal femur, distal femur, and upper extremities.

Each region was coded as injured (=1) if any trauma diagnosis involving that region was documented during the index ED evaluation, based on clinician assessment and any imaging performed (radiography and/or computed tomography) at the treating physician’s discretion. Multiple injuries in the same region were recorded. The block_sum value was calculated by summing the seven binary region indicators (range, 0–7), and multiregional injury was defined a priori as block_sum ≥2.

Secondary outcomes included block_sum modeled as a count outcome.

Covariates

Potential confounders were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and data completeness. The following variables were included in the multivariable models: age (continuous); sex; fall mechanism category (A, B, C, or D); ambulance transport (yes/no); alcohol use before injury (yes/no); delayed presentation, defined as presentation at ≥2 days after injury (yes/no); and baseline ADL category (Independent/Dependent/Unknown).

In a fully adjusted exploratory model, comorbidities and medication burden (e.g., total number of regular medications) were additionally included to assess potential confounding factors according to baseline health status.

Variables reflecting in-hospital clinical decision-making (e.g., need for medical intervention and diagnostic protocols) were not included to avoid an overlap with other ongoing projects and maintain the focus on injury patterns at presentation.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics are summarized using medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables and counts with percentages for categorical variables. Between-group comparisons (multiregional injury vs. no multiregional injury) were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and the Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to examine the associations between fall mechanism categories, baseline ADL categories, and multiregional injuries. Category B was used as the reference mechanism, as it is common and clinically interpretable. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were determined.

Hierarchical models were prespecified to assess the robustness of the findings to covariate adjustment:

Prespecified sensitivity analyses included redefining multiregional injury as block_sum ≥3, modeling block_sum as a count outcome (Poisson regression with robust standard errors), and repeating the analyses after excluding patients in category A (restricted to categories B/C/D).

All analyses were performed using JMP® Pro version 15.0 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was set at a two-sided P value <0.05.

Results

Study population

A total of 1058 ED visits containing “fall” in the history of present illness column were screened. After a chart review, 31 visits were excluded because a fall did not actually occur, leaving 1027 confirmed fall cases. We further excluded 13 cases involving falls from heights or traffic accidents. The final sample comprised 1014 older adults [Figure 1].

Baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the included patients are summarized in Table 1. The median age of the cohort was 82 (IQR: 75–88) years, and 41.1% were male. Multiregional injury, defined as involvement of ≥2 body-region blocks (block_sum ≥2), occurred in 391 patients (38.6%). The distribution of the fall mechanism categories was as follows: A, 197 (19.4%); B, 310 (30.6%); C, 256 (25.2%); and D, 251 (24.8%). Baseline ADL was independent in 468 (46.2%), dependent in 293 (28.9%), and unknown in 253 (25.0%). Ambulance transport was used in 84.8% of visits, and alcohol use prior to the fall was reported in 5.7%.

When comparing patients with and without multiregional injuries, demographics and most fall-related factors, including age and sex, were similar. However, patients with multiregional injuries were less likely to have reported malaise/illness (3.1% vs. 6.4%, P = 0.019) and had a lower prevalence of hypertension (53.7% vs. 61.5%, P = 0.021) compared with those without multiregional injuries. The incidence of other comorbidities did not differ significantly between the groups. Prior stroke was less prevalent in patients with multiregional injury than in those without (5.9% vs. 10.6%, P = 0.012).

Mechanism-specific multiregional injury rates

The proportion of multiregional injuries (block_sum ≥2) varied across mechanism categories [Figure 2]. The multiregional injury rate was 36.0% for mechanism A (71/197; 95% CI: 29.7–43.0), 43.5% for mechanism B (highest proportion; 135/310; 95% CI: 38.1–49.1), 39.1% for mechanism C (100/256; 95% CI: 33.3–45.2), and 33.9% for mechanism D (lowest proportion; 85/251; 95% CI: 28.3–39.9).

Multivariable associations with multiregional injury

The multivariable logistic regression analysis (Model 2: fall mechanism + baseline ADL [3 categories] + age, sex, ambulance transport, alcohol use, and delayed presentation at ≥2 days) is presented in Table 2 and Figure 3. These adjusted associations are visually summarized in Figure 3. Mechanism D (vs. B) was associated with lower odds of multiregional injury occurrence (adjusted OR: 0.68, 95% CI: 0.48–0.98; P = 0.036). The “unknown” baseline ADL category (vs. independent) was associated with higher odds of multiregional injury occurrence (adjusted OR: 1.44, 95% CI: 1.01–2.05; P = 0.043). Ambulance transport was also associated with multiregional injury occurrence (adjusted OR: 1.64, 95% CI: 1.08–2.49; P = 0.020). Mechanism A (vs. B) showed a similar trend but did not reach statistical significance (adjusted OR: 0.69, 95% CI: 0.47–1.01; P = 0.056). In Model 2, age, sex, alcohol use, and delayed presentation were not significantly associated with multiregional injury occurrence.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses generally supported the primary findings [Supplementary Tables S1 and S2]. First, redefining multiregional injury as ≥3 body-region blocks (block_sum ≥3; 105/1,014, 10.4%) yielded directionally similar but less precise estimates, with no clear statistically significant association for mechanism categories in the Model 2 framework. Second, restricting the population to mechanism B/C/D (excluding A) produced consistent results for key covariates. Notably, mechanism D (vs. B) remained associated with lower odds of multiregional injury occurrence (adjusted OR: 0.66, 95% CI: 0.46–0.94), and ambulance transport remained positively associated (adjusted OR: 1.57, 95% CI: 1.01–2.44). Third, treating block_sum as a continuous count outcome in the Poisson regression analysis with robust standard errors (median 1 [IQR, 1–2]; range 0–5) showed similar patterns: ambulance transport was associated with a higher block_sum (incidence rate ratio [IRR]: 1.12, 95% CI: 1.02–1.24), whereas delayed presentation at ≥2 days was associated with a lower block_sum (IRR: 0.85, 95% CI: 0.74–0.97).

Discussion

In this single-center, retrospective study of 1014 older adults presenting to a tertiary care ED after a fall, we examined whether documentation-based fall categories and baseline functional status information available at presentation were associated with diagnosed multiregional injury. Three main findings emerged. First, multiregional injury diagnosis was common, occurring in 38.6% of cases. Second, compared with the reference category of tripping/slipping/environmental falls, indeterminate or unclear falls were associated with lower odds of diagnosed multiregional injury in the primary multivariable model. Third, undocumented baseline ADL and ambulance transport were associated with higher odds of diagnosed multiregional injury. Overall, these findings suggest that routinely available chart-based information may be associated with broader injury distribution. This broader perspective is clinically relevant because even apparently low-energy, ground-level falls in older adults can be associated with multisystem injury, substantial morbidity, and adverse longer-term outcomes.[8910]

Interpretation of documentation-based fall categories

A central motivation for this study was the pragmatic reality that fall-related information in emergency care is often incomplete or heterogeneous.[711] Similarly, recent emergency medicine reviews have emphasized that the evaluation of older adults after a fall should integrate injury detection with an assessment of precipitating factors, functional status, and geriatric risk, rather than focusing on a single lesion alone.[1213] Previous research in older adults presenting after falls has often focused on specific injuries such as traumatic intracranial bleeding rather than focusing on broader multiregional injury patterns.[4] Population-based emergency department (ED) data also show that fall-related injuries in older adults are common and heterogeneous, although prior work has generally described overall injury burden rather than diagnosed multiregional injury derived from routinely documented chart variables.[14] Therefore, we retained indeterminate or unclear falls as a separate category rather than excluding them. This approach reflects real-world ED practice, where uncertainty about the circumstances of a fall is itself part of the initial clinical information.[711]

Unexpectedly, the indeterminate category was associated with lower odds of diagnosed multiregional injury than tripping/slipping/environmental falls in the primary model. However, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence that unclear falls are intrinsically protective. Rather, it may indicate that documentation-based categories capture more than do biomechanics alone. The indeterminate category likely includes a mixture of lower-energy falls with limited documentation, falls in which the mechanism was not recorded in a busy clinical setting, and cases in which history reliability was reduced by acute illness, pain, delirium, or communication barriers.[711]

In contrast, tripping or slipping events often occur during ambulation and forward progression.[515] Accordingly, the present classification should be understood as a chart-based categorization of fall circumstances rather than a precise biomechanical classification of injury mechanism. This distinction is important because the observed associations may reflect a combination of actual fall circumstances, patient-level characteristics, and documentation context.[711]

Baseline activity of daily living as a frailty proxy and the meaning of “unknown”

Another important finding was the association between undocumented baseline ADL and higher odds of diagnosed multiregional injury. In the previous version of the manuscript, baseline ADL was positioned primarily as a proxy for frailty. However, the present results support a more cautious interpretation.[16171819] Although retrospectively assigned validated frailty instruments such as the Clinical Frailty Scale may show reasonable reproducibility, we did not use a validated frailty tool in this study; instead, we relied on a simpler documentation-based ADL variable.[1617] Recent ED studies suggest that the Clinical Frailty Scale can be applied with acceptable inter-rater reliability at presentation, and frailty itself has been associated with fall-related injury characteristics in older adults.[1819] Furthermore, when ADL was undocumented, this may have reflected not only patient vulnerability but also limitations in information availability at presentation.[711] Several explanations are plausible. Patients with greater injury burden may have had less reliable history records due to pain, acute distress, impaired communication, or altered mental status, making premorbid functional assessment more difficult. In other cases, collateral information may simply not have been available during the ED visit.[711] Undocumented ADL may therefore represent an informative documentation status rather than a direct measure of dependence or frailty.[71116] This interpretation is methodologically more defensible and better aligned with the nature of the available data.

Clinical implications

From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that routinely documented information at ED presentation may help identify patients who require careful evaluation for broader injury distribution. In particular, ambulance transport and undocumented baseline function may serve as practical indicators that the patient could have more widespread injuries than those initially apparent. Meanwhile, studies of geriatric ground-level falls suggest that imaging yield and diagnostic escalation depend strongly on bedside history, physical examination, and the clinical context, underscoring that these variables should complement, rather than replace, clinical judgment.[2021] This interpretation is broadly consistent with the findings in a previous study showing that initial clinical acuity in the ED, and injury severity are associated with downstream disposition in older patients who sustain low-energy falls.[22] However, the results do not support treating an indeterminate fall history as automatically indicative of high injury burden. Instead, chart-based fall categories are likely more useful when interpreted together with other readily available clinical information rather than in isolation. That said, this study should not be taken as establishing a diagnostic rule. The outcome in this study was diagnosed multiregional injury, which may not necessarily reflect the true underlying injury burden. Furthermore, the detection of injuries likely depended in part on clinician judgment and imaging intensity. Therefore, the clinical implication is limited to cautious risk awareness rather than prescriptive decision-making. Accordingly, these variables may be better viewed as prompts for heightened geriatric trauma awareness and broader assessment than as stand-alone decision rules.[23]

Robustness across sensitivity analyses

The prespecified sensitivity analyses generally supported the overall direction of the findings. Analyses using a stricter outcome threshold and alternative modeling strategies yielded similar patterns, although some estimates were attenuated or became less precise. These results suggest that the main findings were not solely an artifact of a single analytic choice. However, consistency across sensitivity analyses does not eliminate the possibility of residual confounding, misclassification, or ascertainment bias.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It included a relatively large consecutive cohort of older adults presenting after falls, used information that is routinely available in actual ED practice, and evaluated injury burden across multiple body regions rather than focusing on a single injury type. Additionally, we prespecified hierarchical models and conducted sensitivity analyses to test the stability of the observed associations.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the retrospective, single-center design limits generalizability and is inherently vulnerable to residual confounding and documentation bias. Second, the chart-based fall categories were not validated biomechanical categories and may have been misclassified. Third, the primary outcome was diagnosed multiregional injury, which depends not only on the underlying injuries but also on diagnostic intensity. Patients who underwent broader imaging may have had more injured regions identified, and imaging practices may have been associated with ambulance transport, acuity, history reliability, or other clinical factors. Therefore, ascertainment bias cannot be excluded. Fourth, baseline ADL is not a validated frailty measure, and undocumented ADL likely reflects both patient factors and information availability. Finally, because this study relied on routine clinical documentation, unmeasured factors related to cognition, supervision, living situation, and injury evaluation may also have influenced the results.

Conclusion

Documentation-based fall categories, ambulance transport, and undocumented baseline functional status were associated with diagnosed multiregional injury in older adults presenting to the ED after a fall. These associations should be interpreted as exploratory rather than causal. In particular, the finding for indeterminate falls likely reflects documentation context as well as fall circumstances, and undocumented ADL should not be considered equivalent to frailty. Future studies using standardized fall classification, validated functional or frailty measures, and more uniform injury assessment are needed to determine whether these readily available clinical indicators can support practical risk stratification after falls in older adults.
