A nomogram estimates postoperative urinary tract infection risk after upper urinary tract stone surgery with double-J stents
Authors: Yan Liu, Chao Zhou, Ji Ai, Zhenhao Li, Han Zhu, Shiyu Wang, Jian Gao, Huijiu Luo, Yu Ding, Zeju Zhao, Guobiao Liang, Shulian Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPostoperative urinary tract infection (UTI) is common after upper urinary tract stone surgery in patients with indwelling double-J stents, but dedicated risk-stratification tools for this peri-stent period remain limited. We aimed to develop and related-center validate a nomogram for individualized UTI risk estimation, while distinguishing earlier postoperative risk prediction from near-removal risk updating. In this retrospective two-center cohort study conducted from January 2023 to July 2025, 756 eligible patients were analyzed. Patients from the Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University (Hospital A) were randomly divided into training (n = 382) and internal validation (n = 164) cohorts, while patients from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University (Hospital B) formed a related-center validation cohort (n = 210). LASSO and multivariable logistic regression were used to construct the model. A pre-removal model included antecedent predictors available before the stent-removal encounter, whereas an updated at-removal model additionally incorporated urine nitrite positivity measured at stent removal. Postoperative UTI occurred in 17.5% of the training cohort and 18.6% of the related-center validation cohort. The pre-removal model included postoperative residual fragments (> 4 mm), longer DJ stent dwell time, prior UTI, moderate-to-severe hydronephrosis, and daily fluid intake > 3000 mL/day as a protective factor. The updated at-removal model additionally incorporated urine nitrite positivity at stent removal. The updated at-removal nomogram showed good discrimination, with AUCs of 0.873 in the training cohort, 0.835 in the internal validation cohort, and 0.932 in the related-center validation cohort. The pre-removal model also maintained good discrimination, with AUCs of 0.852, 0.807, and 0.926, respectively. This nomogram may serve as a potentially useful decision-support tool for structured postoperative surveillance in DJ-stented patients after upper urinary tract stone surgery. Introduction

Indwelling ureteral stents (double-J stents) are routinely placed after endoscopic or percutaneous interventions for upper urinary tract stones to ensure drainage and support of the ureter. However, their use is associated with various complications, among which urinary tract infection (UTI) is one of the most frequent and clinically significant1,2. Previous studies have reported that up to one-third of patients experience stent-related symptoms postoperatively, including UTIs, hematuria, irritative voiding symptoms, and fever3. UTIs not only cause patient morbidity but can progress to severe outcomes such as pyelonephritis, bacteremia, or urosepsis in susceptible individuals4–6. Indeed, UTI is the most common postoperative complication of stone removal procedures, and although progression to urosepsis is rare, it carries life-threatening risk7,8. Prompt identification and management of UTIs in this setting are therefore crucial for patient safety and recovery.

Various patient and perioperative factors are known to influence infection risk after urinary stone interventions. Factors such as female sex, advanced age, diabetes mellitus, positive preoperative urine culture, large stone burden, prolonged operation time, and preoperative stenting have all been implicated in increasing the likelihood of post-procedural infectious complications9,10. The presence of an indwelling stent itself facilitates bacterial colonization via biofilm formation, which can seed UTIs especially with longer stent dwell times11,12. Despite this knowledge, predicting which patients will develop a postoperative UTI remains challenging13,14. Clinicians often rely on general risk factors or subjective judgment, and current guidelines primarily emphasize prophylactic antibiotics and removal of uncontrolled infections prior to stone surgery rather than individualized risk prediction15,16.

Recently, there have been efforts to create prediction models or nomograms for infection-related outcomes after ureteroscopy or percutaneous nephrolithotomy, but these studies have focused on severe infections (e.g., urosepsis) or specific subpopulations and have rarely included external validation17,18. For instance, Liu et al. (2025) developed a nomogram for post-ureteroscopic sepsis based on factors like female sex, urine culture, stone size, albumin, and operative time10, and Qi et al. (2024) proposed a model for UTI risk in obstructive ureterolithiasis using indicators such as urine nitrite and leukocyte esterase17. However, no prior study to our knowledge has specifically addressed the risk of UTI in the subset of patients who have undergone stone removal and have a double-J stent in situ, a scenario that is very common in practice. Such patients may have unique risk dynamics due to the presence of the stent, which can remain for weeks and serve as a nidus for infection19,20. A tailored risk assessment tool for this context is lacking.

In this study, we aimed to develop a clinically interpretable nomogram for postoperative UTI risk stratification in patients undergoing upper urinary tract stone surgery with indwelling DJ stents. We further distinguished two clinically relevant stages of risk assessment: a pre-removal model based on antecedent predictors available before the stent-removal encounter, and an updated at-removal model incorporating urine nitrite positivity measured at stent removal. This distinction was intended to preserve temporal interpretability while supporting both postoperative surveillance during stent indwelling and dynamic risk reassessment around stent removal. In line with the TRIPOD reporting framework, we report the model-development strategy, related-center validation, discrimination, calibration, and decision-curve analysis to evaluate the potential clinical utility of this decision-support tool.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective, two-center cohort study at the Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University (Hospital A) and the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University (Hospital B), both tertiary referral hospitals in Guizhou, China. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University (Approval No. KLL-2023-643), and the collaborating center granted institutional permission for data use in accordance with local oversight requirements. Given the retrospective use of de-identified clinical data, the requirement for written informed consent was waived. Consecutive patients who underwent endourological surgery for upper urinary tract stones—retrograde ureteroscopic laser lithotripsy or percutaneous nephrolithotomy—with postoperative double-J (DJ) stent placement between January 2023 and July 2025 were eligible. A total of 1,003 patients were screened; 247 were excluded (144 incomplete data, 68 uncontrolled preoperative UTI, and 35 receiving systemic immunosuppression), leaving 756 patients for analysis. Of these, 546 patients from Hospital A formed the development dataset, which was randomly divided 7:3 into training (n = 382) and internal validation (n = 164) cohorts, while 210 patients from Hospital B constituted an independent related-center validation cohort (Fig. 1).

Data collection and definitions

Clinical data were abstracted from electronic medical records using a standardized case-report form and predefined coding rules. Data extraction at each center was performed by trained investigators and cross-checked by a second reviewer for completeness and consistency. Any discrepancies in variable coding or outcome classification were resolved by discussion and, when necessary, adjudicated by a senior investigator. Baseline characteristics for the development dataset (training vs. internal validation) and for the training versus related-center validation cohorts are summarized in Tables 1 and 2, respectively. Candidate predictors were defined before analysis, and key variables were categorized a priori for clinical interpretability, including stent dwell time (≤ 30, 31–60, > 60 days), hydronephrosis severity (none–mild vs. moderate–severe), and high fluid intake (> 3,000 mL/day vs. ≤ 3,000 mL/day).

Demographics/comorbidities. Age, sex, body mass index, hypertension, and diabetes.

Laboratory indices. Preoperative hemoglobin, serum albumin, serum creatinine, and urinalysis (including nitrite).

Stone features. Recorded stone-related variables included location (kidney/ureter/both), maximal diameter, number, and staghorn calculus (if present). Stone composition was not included because composition data were not available in a sufficiently complete and standardized manner across both centers.

Hydronephrosis. Classified on ultrasound/CT as none–mild vs. moderate–severe.

Operative variables. Surgical approach (retrograde ureteroscopy vs. percutaneous antegrade nephrolithotomy), operative time, and postoperative residual fragments on postoperative imaging were recorded. Postoperative residual fragments were defined a priori as the presence of any fragment > 4 mm on postoperative imaging, according to the study’s operational definition. This threshold was not derived from the present dataset and was applied consistently across the training, internal validation, and related-center validation cohorts.

Stent parameters. DJ caliber (5 Fr vs. 6 Fr), number (unilateral vs. bilateral), and indwelling duration. Stent dwell time was analyzed as a three-level categorical variable (≤ 30, 31–60, > 60 days) aligned with routine scheduling windows. Staged procedures and re-stenting were also recorded when applicable.

Postoperative management. Prophylactic antibiotic use and duration were abstracted from electronic medical records. Average daily fluid intake during stent indwelling was obtained from postoperative follow-up documentation and patient-reported adherence to standardized hydration counseling. At discharge and follow-up, patients were routinely advised to report their usual daily intake according to commonly used containers, such as cups, bottled water, or thermos bottles. The reported number and approximate volume of these containers were converted into milliliters by trained investigators using standardized assumptions, for example 250 mL for a standard cup and 500 mL for a regular bottled-water container, unless the patient reported a specific container volume. When intake varied across days, the average daily intake during the stent-indwelling period was recorded. “High fluid intake” was defined a priori as > 3000 mL/day. Because this variable was based on patient-reported intake rather than prospectively measured drinking volume or objectively measured urine output, it should be interpreted as a semi-quantitative reported intake variable rather than objectively confirmed diuresis.

Outcome. The primary outcome was symptomatic postoperative UTI occurring either during stent indwelling or within 7 days after stent removal. We grouped these events into a single postoperative outcome because both represent clinically relevant infectious complications arising within the same peri-stent postoperative episode after stone surgery, while recognizing that they may involve partially different mechanisms, such as stent-associated colonization during indwelling versus infection temporally related to removal. UTI was defined as significant bacteriuria (≥ 10^5 CFU/mL on midstream clean-catch urine culture) together with compatible symptoms, including fever (≥ 38 °C), new or worsening frequency or urgency, dysuria, or flank pain. Asymptomatic bacteriuria and likely contaminants (e.g., Lactobacillus spp.) were not classified as UTI. Patients underwent urinalysis and urine culture at the scheduled stent-removal visit (approximately 2–6 weeks postoperatively) or earlier if symptoms developed during indwelling. Accordingly, urine nitrite positivity at stent removal was not assumed to precede all postoperative UTI events and was interpreted in this study as part of an updated near-removal risk assessment. Because this was a retrospective analysis, exact encounter-level sequencing of symptom onset, nitrite testing, and stent removal could not be reconstructed with complete granularity for every case. Outcome adjudication was performed independently of model development using predefined clinical and microbiological criteria.

Missing data and sample-size considerations

Primary analyses used a complete-case approach restricted to individuals with all candidate predictors and outcome available (756/1,003; 75.4%). In the analysis dataset (n = 756), no predictors or outcomes had missing values. Because the incomplete charts lacked analyzable source data for one or more key variables, multiple imputation or reweighting was not feasible. We recognize that exclusion of 24.6% of screened patients may have introduced selection bias if missingness was related to illness severity, documentation quality, or care processes. We therefore report the screening flow and exclusion reasons transparently (Fig. 1), interpret model performance cautiously, and provide related-center validation and bootstrap optimism-correction as partial safeguards against overfitting (Supplementary Figure S1; Supplementary Table S2). A formal sensitivity analysis comparing included and excluded patients was not feasible because the excluded records lacked analyzable values for one or more key predictors and therefore did not permit reliable reconstruction of baseline comparability.

Model development (training) and internal validation

To develop a parsimonious and clinically interpretable prediction model, we combined penalized variable selection with multivariable logistic regression and then evaluated discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility in the training, internal validation, and related-center validation cohorts. All clinically plausible candidate predictors were prespecified and entered without p-value prefiltering. We first described associations using univariable logistic regression (Table 3, univariable column) to aid interpretability.

LASSO was used as a shrinkage-based selection step among prespecified clinically plausible variables, whereas the final multivariable logistic model was fitted to provide interpretable odds ratios, confidence intervals, and standard regression coefficients for nomogram construction. Predictors with non-zero LASSO coefficients were entered into the multivariable logistic model, and backward elimination (retention p < 0.05) was then applied for the final clinical model (Table 3, multivariable column; Fig. 3).

The pre-removal model included five antecedent predictors available before the stent-removal encounter: postoperative residual fragments (> 4 mm), DJ stent dwell time, prior UTI, hydronephrosis severity, and daily fluid intake > 3,000 mL/day, whereas the updated at-removal model incorporated urine nitrite positivity measured at stent removal as a sixth predictor. This distinction was introduced to separate earlier postoperative risk stratification from near-removal risk updating.

Performance metrics and clinical utility

Model discrimination was summarized by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC, with 95% CIs). Calibration was assessed visually (loess-smoothed calibration plots) and quantitatively via calibration slope and calibration-in-the-large (intercept). Overall accuracy was indexed by the Brier score. Clinical utility was examined with decision curve analysis (DCA), comparing net benefit of the model against “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies across plausible threshold probabilities. Discrimination (Fig. 4), calibration (Fig. 5), and DCA (Fig. 6) are reported for the training, internal validation, and related-center validation cohorts; bootstrap-based optimism-corrected estimates for the training cohort are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Risk stratification

To facilitate clinical interpretation, we prespecified clinically interpretable thresholds (e.g., 5%, 10%, 20%) to define low-, intermediate-, and high-risk strata. These thresholds were intended to support postoperative risk stratification and follow-up planning rather than to mandate specific management actions. Operating points illustrated on ROC curves reflect the nominal thresholds used for cohort-level summaries (Fig. 4).

Statistical software and reporting

All analyses were performed in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Penalized regression used glmnet; logistic modeling and calibration used stats/rms; ROC analysis used pROC; DCA used rmda; bootstrap resampling used boot. Two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables are described as median (IQR) and compared with the Mann–Whitney U test; categorical variables as counts (%) and compared with χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests (Tables 1, 2). Univariable and multivariable odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported in Table 3; the complete model specification is provided in Supplementary Table S1; bootstrap performance metrics are summarized in Supplementary Figure S1 and Supplementary Table S2 (Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6).

Results

Model performance and validation

Discrimination. To preserve temporal interpretability and reflect clinical use more explicitly, we evaluated two clinically distinct models. The pre-removal model excluded urine nitrite at stent removal and included only antecedent predictors available before the stent-removal visit. This pre-removal model achieved AUCs of 0.852 in the training cohort, 0.807 in the internal validation cohort, and 0.926 in the related-center validation cohort, indicating that discrimination remained good even before incorporation of nitrite at removal. For the updated at-removal model, as shown in Fig. 4, the AUC was 0.873 (95% CI 0.857–0.935) in the training set, 0.835 (95% CI 0.742–0.912) in the internal validation set, and 0.932 (95% CI 0.901–0.963) in the related-center validation cohort. Bootstrap internal validation (B = 1,000) yielded an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.860 (bootstrap 95% CI 0.836–0.918), closely matching the apparent AUC; the ensemble of bootstrap ROC curves is shown in Supplementary Figure S1, and numerical metrics (apparent and optimism-corrected) are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. These findings indicate good discrimination, although the high performance observed in the related-center validation cohort should be interpreted in the context of case-mix differences and validation within a related institutional network.

For illustration, a nominal probability threshold of 0.12 in the related-center validation cohort, annotated in Fig. 4, identified UTI-prone patients with approximately 78.4% sensitivity and 97.4% specificity. In practice, threshold selection should remain context-dependent, particularly given the related-center nature of the validation cohort and the inclusion of urine nitrite positivity as a near-removal updating predictor.

Calibration. Calibration was assessed by plotting predicted probabilities against observed outcomes (Fig. 5) and by goodness-of-fit testing. Predicted risks closely matched observed UTI incidence across risk deciles; the bias-corrected curve lay near the 45° line in all cohorts, with mild underestimation at the high-risk tail. Hosmer–Lemeshow tests were non-significant in the training (χ2 = 6.439, P = 0.598) and internal validation (χ2 = 5.438, P = 0.231) sets, indicating no evidence of lack-of-fit; the related-center validation cohort likewise showed acceptable fit (P > 0.3). Bootstrap-corrected calibration slope, calibration-in-the-large (intercept), and the Brier score are reported in Supplementary Table S2. Together, these results indicate that the model’s absolute risk estimates were acceptably calibrated in the analyzed cohorts, although calibration should be reassessed in broader prospective settings before routine implementation; for example, groups predicted at ~ 20% risk exhibited an observed event rate of roughly 20%.

Clinical utility. Decision curve analysis (DCA) is presented in Fig. 6 for the updated at-removal model. Across clinically plausible threshold probabilities, the model delivered higher net benefit than either “treat-all” or “treat-none” strategies in the training, internal validation, and related-center validation cohorts. In particular, within the 0.10–0.30 threshold band, the model consistently conferred greater net benefit, supporting its potential value for risk-informed surveillance and follow-up while avoiding unnecessary escalation in lower-risk individuals. The pre-removal model also showed net benefit over both default strategies across clinically relevant threshold probabilities, particularly within the range of approximately 0.05–0.30. These findings should be interpreted as evidence of potential clinical utility rather than proof that model-guided management improves patient outcomes.

Risk stratification. Using the prespecified 10% and 20% cut points, the proportions classified as low/intermediate/high risk were 57.9%/15.7%/26.4% in the training set (observed event rates 2.3%/25.0%/46.5%), 61.6%/15.2%/23.2% in the internal validation set (5.9%/20.0%/47.4%), and 62.4%/8.6%/29.0% in the related-center validation cohort (0.8%/22.2%/55.7%), indicating clear monotonic risk separation. In a sensitivity analysis using 5% and 20% as cut points, the low/intermediate/high strata comprised 35.6%/38.0%/26.4% (0.7%/13.1%/46.5%) in the training set, 47.0%/29.9%/23.2% (5.2%/14.3%/47.4%) in the internal validation set, and 53.3%/17.6%/29.0% (0.0%/13.5%/55.7%) in the related-center validation cohort.

Baseline characteristics and predictor analysis

Events per variable assessment. In the training cohort, postoperative UTI occurred in 67 of 382 patients, corresponding to an event rate of 17.5%. For the updated at-removal model including six final predictors, the events per variable (EPV) was approximately 11.2, exceeding the conventional threshold of 10 events per predictor and supporting stable coefficient estimation in multivariable logistic regression. For the five-predictor pre-removal model, the EPV was approximately 13.4. These findings suggest that the number of outcome events in the training cohort was adequate for the final model specifications, although the retrospective design and related-center validation setting still warrant cautious interpretation.

Cohort comparability. Table 1 compares baseline characteristics between the training and internal validation sets, showing broadly comparable distributions, with most variables not statistically different (P > 0.05); a small difference was observed for stent number (P = 0.049), consistent with a largely balanced random split. Table 2 contrasts the training and related-center validation cohorts; modest but meaningful case-mix differences were observed, including sex (male 63.6% vs 53.8%, P = 0.020), age (50.0 vs 54.0 years, P = 0.002), length of stay (9.0 vs 8.0 days, P = 0.034), hypertension status (25.7% vs 34.8%, P = 0.019), preoperative urine nitrite positivity (5.5% vs 13.8%, P < 0.001), DJ stent type (F5 55.2% vs 91.0%, P < 0.001), DJ stent dwell-time distribution (≤ 30 days 30.4% vs 56.7%, P < 0.001), preoperative UTI history (11.8% vs 25.2%, P < 0.001), and nitrite positivity at stent removal (9.4% vs 20.5%, P < 0.001). Despite these differences, overall UTI incidence was comparable between cohorts (≈17–19%). Postoperative residual fragments (> 4 mm) were observed in 57.1% of the training cohort and 41.9% of the related-center validation cohort, representing a significant between-cohort difference (P < 0.001). This difference should be interpreted alongside the broader case-mix differences between cohorts.

Predictor effects. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression results are summarized in Table 3. In univariable analyses, postoperative residual fragments (> 4 mm), urine nitrite positivity at stent removal, longer DJ stent dwell time (31–60 and > 60 vs ≤ 30 days), prior UTI, and moderate-to-severe hydronephrosis were associated with increased postoperative UTI risk (all P < 0.05). After multivariable adjustment, these factors remained significant, while high daily fluid intake (> 3,000 mL/day) emerged as a protective factor. These six variables were incorporated into the updated at-removal nomogram, whereas the pre-removal model excluded urine nitrite positivity at stent removal. For full reproducibility, the model specification (β-coefficients, standard errors, intercept, and exact variable coding) is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Discussion

In this retrospective two-center study, we developed a nomogram to estimate postoperative UTI risk in patients undergoing upper urinary tract stone surgery with indwelling DJ stents and evaluated its performance in an internal validation cohort and a related-center validation cohort. To preserve temporal interpretability, we distinguished a pre-removal model based on antecedent predictors available before the stent-removal encounter from an updated at-removal model that additionally incorporated urine nitrite positivity measured at stent removal. The model showed good discrimination, acceptable calibration, and potential net clinical benefit; in keeping with TRIPOD guidance, calibration slope, calibration-in-the-large, and the Brier score were reported to support the reliability of absolute risk estimates across datasets (Fig. 5; Supplementary Table S2). Moreover, prespecified clinically relevant thresholds (5%, 10%, and 20%) were used to anchor decision-curve interpretation rather than deriving cut-points from the data (Fig. 6). However, these findings should be interpreted as supporting related-center validation rather than broad generalizability across heterogeneous healthcare settings. Accordingly, the nomogram is best viewed as a decision-support tool for structured surveillance and individualized follow-up planning rather than as a directive management instrument.

Key risk factors

The updated at-removal model identified postoperative residual fragments (> 4 mm), DJ stent dwell time, prior UTI, urine nitrite positivity at stent removal, and hydronephrosis severity as independent risk factors for postoperative UTI, while high daily fluid intake was protective. These findings are biologically plausible and generally consistent with existing literature. Postoperative residual fragments (> 4 mm), for example, may harbor bacteria, support biofilm formation, and serve as a persistent nidus for infection, a mechanism supported by meta-analyses in PCNL cohorts9. The residual-fragment threshold used in this study (> 4 mm) was prespecified for operational consistency and was applied uniformly across all cohorts. Nonetheless, the clinical meaning of residual fragments depends strongly on the stone-free definition adopted. A recent systematic review by Quarà et al. emphasized that even so-called clinically insignificant residual fragments, frequently defined as ≤ 4 mm, may be associated with non-negligible risks of complications, regrowth, and reintervention38. Therefore, the predictive effect of postoperative residual fragments in our model should be interpreted in light of our specific > 4 mm operational threshold. This association should also be interpreted in the context of unmeasured stone composition, as infection-related calculi may carry a different microbiological risk profile from metabolically derived stones. Effect sizes were directionally consistent between the development and related-center validation cohorts, although broader generalizability remains to be established (Table 3; Fig. 4; Supplementary Table S1).

Stent dwell time

DJ stent dwell time was also a critical determinant of UTI risk22. Longer stent retention significantly increased infection risk, consistent with the notion that prolonged indwelling facilitates bacterial adherence and biofilm formation23–27. In univariable analysis, both 31–60 days and > 60 days of DJ stent dwell time were associated with higher UTI incidence compared with ≤ 30 days (ORs > 1, P < 0.05). This association persisted after multivariable adjustment, with the risk being particularly pronounced when stent dwell time exceeded 60 days. Notably, the risk gradient across dwell-time categories was monotonic, supporting the clinical relevance of prolonged indwelling duration as a marker of increased postoperative infectious risk (Fig. 6).

A history of UTI before surgery was another robust predictor. Such patients may have underlying anatomical or microbial susceptibilities. Similar to our findings, Mhamid et al. showed that a UTI within 90 days before ureteroscopy tripled postoperative UTI risk28. While earlier studies often focused on severe infections such as urosepsis, our work extends this association to postoperative UTI during the peri-stent period, suggesting that patients with recurrent or recent infections may warrant closer surveillance and more individualized follow-up. The association remained significant after adjustment for nitrite status and DJ stent dwell time, implying that prior UTI may capture host susceptibility and/or persistent colonization beyond perioperative factors (Table 3).

Urine nitrite positivity at stent removal emerged as one of the strongest predictors in the updated at-removal model. Nitrite positivity reflects the presence of nitrate-reducing bacteria and has been reported by Qi et al. (2024) to predict UTI in patients with obstructive ureterolithiasis17. In our cohort, nitrite positivity likely reflected microbiological activity temporally close to the stent-removal encounter rather than a purely antecedent postoperative risk factor. This distinction is important because urine nitrite was measured at stent removal and therefore should be interpreted as part of a dynamic near-removal risk update rather than as a predictor that necessarily precedes all postoperative UTI events. Accordingly, we distinguish between a pre-removal model based on five antecedent variables and an updated at-removal model that incorporates urine nitrite positivity to refine short-term risk estimation immediately around stent removal. This framing better preserves temporal plausibility and more accurately reflects how the nomogram may be applied in clinical practice (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S1).

Hydronephrosis severity was likewise associated with increased UTI risk. Pronounced obstruction provides a favorable environment for bacterial persistence and impairs urinary clearance29. Maeda et al. reported that pre-existing hydronephrosis was a significant risk factor for febrile infection after retrograde pyelography, particularly when no antimicrobial prophylaxis was administered30. Our data suggest that patients with moderate-to-severe hydronephrosis represent a vulnerable subgroup requiring heightened clinical vigilance. Given its plausible link to urinary stasis, hydronephrosis may operate both independently and in conjunction with postoperative residual fragments; nevertheless, the variable retained significance after multivariable adjustment (Table 3).

High daily fluid intake (> 3,000 mL/day) emerged as a protective factor. Increased hydration may plausibly reduce infection risk by diluting urine and promoting urinary clearance, consistent with evidence supporting increased fluid intake for reducing recurrent UTI risk in selected populations31–33. However, this variable was based on follow-up records and patient-reported adherence to hydration advice rather than objectively measured urine output or prospectively monitored intake. Therefore, it may not fully reflect true patient behavior and is subject to potential non-differential misclassification. The observed protective association should thus be interpreted cautiously and viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of a causal intervention effect.

Non-significant factors

Several variables did not show independent predictive value in our analysis. Female sex and diabetes, although frequently cited as risk factors for UTI, were not significantly associated with postoperative infection in our cohort34,35. Female sex demonstrated a numerical trend toward higher UTI incidence but did not reach statistical significance, likely reflecting confounding influences such as prior UTI history or nitrite positivity. Similarly, diabetes did not emerge as a significant factor, suggesting that its potential effect might be offset by perioperative glycemic management or mediated through correlated variables such as colonization status and hydronephrosis. Preoperative urine culture results also failed to predict postoperative events, which may reflect preoperative antimicrobial management and exclusion of patients with uncontrolled preoperative UTI. Operative time and maximum stone diameter were significant in univariable testing but lost significance in the overall model, likely because their influence was captured more directly by postoperative residual fragments and hydronephrosis severity. These observations underscore the value of a multivariable framework that prioritizes direct mechanistic proxies, such as residual fragments and obstruction, over upstream correlates, such as operative time and maximal diameter, thereby reducing redundancy and overfitting (Supplementary Table S1).

Comparison with existing models

Our nomogram showed encouraging discrimination in comparison with previously reported predictive tools for infectious outcomes after endourological procedures. For instance, Liu et al. (2025) developed and externally validated a nomogram to predict sepsis following flexible ureteroscopy, achieving C-indices of 0.88 in the training cohort, 0.87 in the internal validation cohort, and 0.85 in the external validation cohort10. Similarly, Yang et al. (2023) constructed a nomogram to predict urosepsis following retrograde intrarenal surgery, reporting AUCs of approximately 0.887 in the training cohort and 0.864 in the validation cohort18. In contrast to these models, our study focused on postoperative UTI in patients with indwelling DJ stents, a clinically common but more specific postoperative context. However, differences in outcome definition, predictor timing, validation design, and cohort relatedness should be taken into account when comparing discrimination across studies. Beyond discrimination, our model also showed net benefit across clinically relevant threshold probabilities, although these findings should be interpreted cautiously and not as definitive evidence of broader model transportability or clinical impact36,37 (Fig. 6; Supplementary Table S2).

Infectious risk mitigation

The present nomogram should also be interpreted within the broader context of perioperative infection-prevention strategies in stone surgery. Recent evidence synthesis by Gavi et al. showed that antibiotic prophylaxis strategies in stone surgery may differ in effectiveness according to surgical approach and patient risk profile, with clearer benefit in patients with positive preoperative urine cultures39. Our model was not designed to prescribe antibiotic prophylaxis or replace guideline-based antimicrobial decision-making. Rather, it may complement existing prevention strategies by identifying patients who may benefit from closer surveillance, reassessment of urine testing, culture-guided management when clinically indicated, and individualized follow-up during the peri-stent period.

Clinical applications

The nomogram should be used, if at all, as a decision-support aid rather than a directive management tool. The pre-removal model may help identify patients who warrant closer surveillance during stent indwelling, whereas the updated at-removal model may refine short-term risk estimation around the stent-removal encounter. In practice, these estimates could inform the intensity of follow-up, the timing of reassessment, and consideration of additional urine testing or culture-guided evaluation when clinically indicated. However, because the present study was retrospective and observational, these potential applications should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and should not be interpreted as evidence that model-guided management improves clinical outcomes (Fig. 6).

Strengths: This study has several strengths. It addresses a common but understudied complication with a relatively large sample size, applies rigorous statistical methods including LASSO-based variable selection, and evaluates model performance in both internal validation and related-center validation cohorts. The model identifies clinically relevant factors that may support postoperative risk stratification during the peri-stent period. The nomogram format translates the multivariable model into a practical decision-support tool, and decision curve analysis suggested potential clinical utility beyond conventional measures of discrimination and calibration. Transparency is enhanced by providing the complete coefficient set and intercept (Supplementary Table S1) and by reporting optimism-corrected performance via bootstrap resampling (Supplementary Table S2; Supplementary Figure S1).

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design may introduce both information bias and selection bias. In particular, primary analyses relied on a complete-case approach, and exclusion of a substantial proportion of screened patients because of incomplete data may have limited representativeness of the analyzed cohort (Fig. 1; Supplementary Figure S1; Supplementary Table S2). Because excluded records lacked analyzable values for one or more key variables, formal imputation and meaningful included-versus-excluded sensitivity comparisons were not feasible. Second, although validation was conducted in a separate cohort from Hospital B, both cohorts were drawn from affiliated tertiary hospitals within the same regional institutional network. Therefore, this analysis should be interpreted as related-center validation rather than broad external validation, and the model’s transportability to other regions, practice patterns, and patient populations remains uncertain. Third, patients with uncontrolled preoperative UTI or receiving immunosuppression were excluded, restricting applicability to higher-risk populations. Fourth, we did not model time-to-event outcomes nor perform model updating or recalibration in the related-center validation cohort; prospective studies with temporal endpoints and site-specific recalibration are needed to improve transportability (Fig. 5). Fifth, ureteral access sheath use and intraoperative intrarenal pressure were not consistently documented across both centers and therefore could not be evaluated in the present model, although both may influence postoperative infectious outcomes after ureteroscopic procedures. Sixth, stone composition, microbiological species, and antimicrobial resistance profiles were not available in a sufficiently standardized manner for model development. Because infection-related stones and calcium-based stones may differ in postoperative infectious risk, these unmeasured factors may have influenced the observed association between postoperative residual fragments and UTI. In addition, the primary endpoint combined UTI during stent indwelling and UTI within 7 days after stent removal; although both are clinically relevant postoperative infectious events, they may not be entirely mechanistically equivalent. This issue is particularly relevant when interpreting urine nitrite positivity at stent removal, which in our study should be regarded as a near-removal updating variable rather than a predictor that necessarily precedes all postoperative events. Finally, fluid intake was based on reported intake rather than measured diuresis; this may have introduced non-differential misclassification and may limit mechanistic interpretation of the model.

Future directions

Prospective multicenter studies in more heterogeneous healthcare settings are warranted to validate and extend the generalizability of the model. Incorporating microbiological data, antimicrobial resistance profiles, stone composition, and molecular biomarkers may enhance predictive accuracy. Furthermore, embedding the nomogram into electronic health records or web-based calculators could enable real-time individualized risk estimation, bridging the gap between statistical modeling and routine clinical decision-making. A pragmatic impact analysis is warranted to test whether model-informed care pathways reduce UTI events, antibiotic exposure, and resource use. Deployment as a bedside web calculator or EHR-embedded tool could facilitate real-time risk estimation using the coefficients provided (Supplementary Table S1).

Conclusion

Postoperative UTI among patients undergoing upper urinary tract stone surgery with indwelling DJ stents is common and clinically relevant. We developed a parsimonious nomogram incorporating postoperative residual fragments (> 4 mm), DJ stent dwell time, prior UTI, hydronephrosis severity, daily fluid intake, and urine nitrite positivity at stent removal. To preserve temporal interpretability, the model should be understood in two stages: a pre-removal model for postoperative surveillance during stent indwelling and an updated at-removal model for dynamic near-removal risk reassessment. The model demonstrated good discrimination, acceptable calibration, and potential clinical utility in internal and related-center validation. However, given the retrospective design and validation within a related institutional network, this nomogram should be regarded as a potentially useful decision-support tool for structured surveillance rather than a definitive management instrument. Broader prospective validation in more heterogeneous settings and formal impact analyses are required before routine clinical implementation.
