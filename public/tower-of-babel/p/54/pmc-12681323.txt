Availability of multidisciplinary treatment modalities and outcomes among patients with resected early-stage non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Brendan T Heiden, Daniel B Eaton, Jatinder Palta, Su-Hsin Chang, Yan Yan, Ana A Baumann, Theodore S Thomas, Martin W Schoen, Steven Tohmasi, Nikki Rossetti, Mayank R Patel, Daniel Kreisel, Ruben G Nava, Bryan F Meyers, Benjamin D Kozower, Varun Puri, Drew Moghanaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComprehensive, multidisciplinary care is crucial for managing early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), typically treated via lung resection or stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT). We evaluated how variable access to on-site SBRT may be associated with clinical outcomes for patients with stage I NSCLC receiving surgical resection within an integrated health-care system.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study of patients with stage I NSCLC treated with lung resection at Veterans Health Administration (VHA) facilities between 2006 and 2016. The VHA provides thoracic surgery at approximately 100 facilities, whereas lung SBRT is currently available at approximately 20 facilities. We compared short- and long-term outcomes for patients treated at surgery-only facilities vs those at facilities offering surgery and SBRT.ResultsWe identified 6289 patients undergoing lung resection, with 4673 (74.3%) treated at surgery-only sites and 1616 (25.7%) at surgery + SBRT sites. Sociodemographic factors were similar between cohorts. Surgery + SBRT sites showed higher adherence to operative quality metrics and improved patient selection. Short-term outcomes were better at surgery + SBRT sites with lower rates of 30-day major complications, 30-day mortality, and 90-day mortality. With a median follow-up of 6.3 years, 5-year overall survival was higher at surgery + SBRT sites (59.4% vs 56.9%; adjusted hazard ratio 1.12, 95% CI = 1.02 to 1.23).ConclusionShort- and long-term outcomes were better for patients with stage I NSCLC who underwent lung resection at facilities delivering thoracic surgery and SBRT. These findings support recommendations to increase SBRT availability at medical centers offering lung resection to ensure comprehensive multidisciplinary care is provided.

Introduction

Several local therapy options are available for patients with early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), most notably surgical resection and stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT).1,2 Although surgical resection is the preferred treatment for patients with clinical stage I NSCLC, SBRT provides a safer alternative for those at high operative risk due to frailty or comorbidities.

The Veterans Health Administration (VHA), as the nation’s largest integrated health-care network, provides a unique and comprehensive system for studying lung cancer treatment outcomes, including longitudinal care metrics. Although the VHA performs lung cancer resections at approximately 100 facilities, fewer than one-quarter of these facilities also offer on-site SBRT.3 Medical centers offering both surgery and SBRT are theoretically better equipped to consider nonsurgical options when concerns arise about a patient’s operable risk. Meanwhile, medical centers offering only surgery may have more difficulties evaluating a patient’s candidacy for nonsurgical treatment given the need to refer patients to a non-VA community provider for evaluation.4 In the setting of early-stage NSCLC, these challenges may influence the likelihood of a radiation oncology referral altogether.

In this study, we evaluated the impact of limited access to on-site SBRT for patients with stage I NSCLC undergoing surgical resection. We hypothesized that, similar to previous reports, limited access to SBRT would be associated with poor patient selection and outcomes for lung surgery.5,6

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study evaluated patients with early-stage NSCLC who received definitive surgical treatment at a VA medical center between 2006 and 2016. The study cohort was assembled using the VHA Informatics and Computing Infrastructure, which integrates various clinical and administrative datasets within the VA Corporate Data Warehouse, including Oncology Raw and the VA Surgical Quality Improvement Program.7 Veterans diagnosed with NSCLC were identified using the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, Third Edition (ICD-O-3) codes. Surgical treatments were confirmed through International Classification of Diseases, Ninth/Tenth Revision (ICD-9/10) codes.

We evaluated all patients undergoing upfront surgery for clinical stage I NSCLC (≤5 cm, node-negative), as defined by the American Joint Committee on Cancer, 7th edition. We excluded patients who received neoadjuvant therapy, presented with recurrent disease from a previously diagnosed cancer, or did not have an identifiable date of diagnosis. A dedicated team of researchers conducted a manual medical record review, supplemented with natural language processing techniques, over a 24-month period to ensure minimal data missingness, particularly concerning surgical covariates described by our group previously.1,8-17 The study received approval from the St. Louis VA’s Research and Development Committee and institutional review board, which waived the requirement for individual informed consent due to the deidentified nature of the analysis.

Exposure and covariates

The primary exposure was the presence or absence of on-site SBRT at the VA medical center where patients underwent a surgical resection. We queried whether facilities performing lung cancer surgery had SBRT capabilities and, if so, confirmed when these capabilities were first established. Patients were grouped by whether they underwent lung resection at a site offering surgery-only vs surgery + SBRT on the date of surgery. Additional patient-, tumor-, and treatment-related variables were abstracted as previously described.1,8-17

Outcomes

The primary outcome was overall survival, measured from the date of surgery to the date of death or last follow-up. We defined secondary outcomes, including 30-day postoperative mortality, 30-day major complication, 30-day readmission, and 90-day mortality. Additionally, we assessed the relationship between SBRT availability and operative quality, defined using the VA Lung Cancer Operative Quality (VALCAN-O) score. This is a validated 13-point composite measure of operative quality (ie, timely surgery, adequate nodal sampling, anatomic resection, minimally invasive approach, negative margin), with higher scores reflecting higher operative quality and improved risk-adjusted long-term survival.17 We also assessed the relationship between SBRT availability and patient selection, defined using the VA Lung Cancer Operative Mortality (VALCAN-M) score.1 This separately validated composite measure uses 11 readily available treatment-related variables to predict the risk of postoperative mortality, with higher scores representing higher risk-adjusted mortality rates; conversely, lower scores represent improved patient selection.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics for the cohort were reported as mean (SD) for continuous variables, and as absolute numbers (percentages) for categorical variables. For non-normally distributed variables, the median (interquartile range [IQR]) was presented. Missing data elements were reported in the descriptive analysis. Overall survival was assessed using Kaplan-Meier survival curves and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models (controlling for relevant patient-, tumor-, treatment-, and institution-related covariates). Statistical analyses were performed using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc, Cary, NC), with a significance level set at P < .05.

Results

A total of 6289 patients undergoing lung resection between 2006 to 2016 were identified. The mean (SD) age was 67.6 (7.8) years, with 6053 (96.2%) men and 5353 (85.1%) identifying as racially White (Table 1). Current smoking was reported by 3682 (58.5%) veterans and the mean (SD) Charlson score was 6.9 (2.2). The most common surgical procedure was lobectomy (n = 4543, 72.2%), and slightly more than half of procedures during the study period were performed via a thoracotomy (n = 3628, 57.7%). The majority of tumors were adenocarcinomas (n = 3427, 54.5%) and most tumors exhibited higher-grade features (grade II-IV, n = 5124, 81.5%). The rates of major postoperative complications and readmission after surgery were 13.6% (n = 858) and 7.1% (n = 448), respectively. The rates of 30- and 90-day mortality were 2.2% (n = 136) and 4.2% (n = 262), respectively.

Among the study cohort, 1616 (26.7%) and 4673 (74.3%) patients received care at facilities with and without SBRT, respectively (Figure S1). According to the VALCAN-O composite surgical quality score, higher-quality operations were more commonly performed at surgery + SBRT sites vs surgery-only sites (50.8% vs 33.8%, P < .001). According to the VALCAN-M composite surgical risk score, surgery + SBRT sites were more likely to operate on low or very low risk patients compared with surgery-only sites (57.7% vs 69.5%, P < .001).

Short-term outcomes were better at surgery + SBRT sites compared with surgery-only sites (Table 3), including lower rates of 30-day major complication (odds ratio [OR] = 0.830, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.700 to 0.985), 30-day mortality (OR = 0.552, 95% CI = 0.348 to 0.874), and 90-day mortality (OR = 0.588, 95% CI = 0.424 to 0.815). With a median (IQR) follow-up of 6.3 (2.6-11.6) years, 5-year overall survival was higher at surgery + SBRT sites (59.4% vs 56.9%, P = .02, Figure 1; adjusted hazard ratio = 1.12, 95% CI = 1.02 to 1.23). A subanalysis was performed among patients who underwent lobectomy vs sublobar resection, which similarly demonstrated improved overall survival at surgery + SBRT sites (Figure S2).

Finally, we examined differences in outcomes at a select subset of sites that switched from surgery only to surgery + SBRT during the study period. In a pre (n = 246) post (n = 1137) analysis, the relationship between SBRT availability and OS was maintained (adjusted hazard ratio = 0.774, 95% CI = 0.638 to 0.939; Figure S3).

Discussion

This study evaluated the association between on-site SBRT availability—a metric for comprehensive multidisciplinary care—and outcomes among patients with early-stage NSCLC receiving surgical treatment. The findings revealed that patients who received surgery at facilities with on-site SBRT had significantly better outcomes, including higher overall survival rates, lower postoperative mortality, and fewer major complications compared with those treated at facilities without SBRT. These findings highlight improved adherence to surgical quality metrics and better patient selection at facilities able to evaluate patients for nonsurgical options. These results suggest that all facilities providing comprehensive lung cancer care should be equipped to offer both surgery and SBRT to optimize selecting patients for each of these local therapies.

It is notable that the presence of on-site SBRT, available at only approximately one-quarter of VHA medical centers performing lung resections, was strongly associated with enhanced quality of surgical care. The findings from this study provide further evidence of the importance of avoiding specialty gaps at medical centers managing lung cancer. True multidisciplinary care requires effective communication between surgeons, radiation oncologists, and medical oncologists to optimize the development of management plans for complex diseases like NSCLC.18 Prior studies have demonstrated the benefits of multidisciplinary care, including improved overall survival, higher rates of guideline-concordant care, and better patient satisfaction.19-21 It is likely that the study’s findings apply to non-VHA hospitals, given prior work from our group demonstrating similar patterns of care and outcomes between veteran and civilian practice settings.11

The study also found a significant association between the availability of on-site SBRT and improved short-term and long-term outcomes. One contributing factor was that facilities with on-site SBRT operated on lower-risk patients, as evidenced by fewer high-risk patients undergoing surgery (ie, VALCAN-M score).1 This finding suggests a potential role for SBRT availability in enhancing patient selection and multidisciplinary decision-making. SBRT offers a less invasive treatment for high-risk populations, and these findings suggest that the availability of on-site SBRT within the VHA was likely leveraged to improve patient selection for surgery.22

We also observed that facilities with on-site SBRT tended to be higher-volume lung cancer centers. Although the volume–outcome relationship remains somewhat contested within the early-stage NSCLC literature, prior work from our group has not demonstrated an association between surgical lung cancer volumes and outcomes among veterans—likely reflecting the unique VHA practice environment and its ties to academic affiliates.23,24 Importantly, the association observed in this study persisted even after adjusting for facility volume, suggesting that the findings are not solely attributable to volume differences among sites.23

Despite the clear role for SBRT in the multidisciplinary treatment of early-stage NSCLC, the establishment of this service within the VHA (and elsewhere) has been slow. The VHA established a national policy for the delivery of SBRT in 2019 to facilitate expanded access, including proper training and quality assurance requirements for new centers (VHA Directive 1414).25 Recommendations have been made that “radiation oncology services should be maintained where present in the VHA, with consideration for expansion of services to additional facilities.”26 The findings from our study support these recommendations to increase the availability of SBRT at all VA medical centers offering lung cancer care. By expanding SBRT capabilities within the VHA, patients with lung cancer may be able to receive timelier and better coordinated care, ultimately improving their prognosis and quality of life. This expansion of lung cancer services is crucial for ensuring that all veterans have access to the best possible care.

The strengths of this study include its large sample size and the use of comprehensive electronic health data from the VHA. However, the study has limitations, such as its retrospective design and potential for facilities with on-site SBRT to have more resources in general, including higher-quality surgical care. Nevertheless, the findings of poorer patient selection at facilities without on-site SBRT suggest that clinical decisions may be influenced by the lack of SBRT availability. Although the cohort enables comprehensive long-term follow-up across all patients, the treatment period is marked by certain nuances, including a higher prevalence of thoracotomies compared with more contemporary cohorts. Finally, the findings may not be generalizable to all civilian medical systems, including the unique nature of VA cohorts (eg, male, heavy smoking histories).

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the availability of on-site SBRT is associated with improved quality of care and better clinical outcomes for patients with early-stage NSCLC receiving surgical resection. These findings support recommendations to increase the availability of SBRT at all medical centers offering lung cancer resection to ensure that comprehensive multidisciplinary care is provided.
