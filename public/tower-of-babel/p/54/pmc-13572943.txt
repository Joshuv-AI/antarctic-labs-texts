Gender distribution in membership, leadership, and conference participation in the Japanese Society for Hypothalamic and Pituitary Tumors
Authors: Naoko Inoshita, Noriko Makita, Takayuki Matsuo, Masaaki Taniguchi, Hidetaka Suga, Hidenori Fukuoka, Hiroshi Nishioka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gender equity has become an important issue in academic medicine, but data on gender distribution in subspecialty medical societies remain limited. We analyzed gender distribution in membership, leadership positions, and conference participation in the Japanese Society for Hypothalamic and Pituitary Tumors (JSHPT). Membership data as of March 2025 (n = 860) were examined, and gender distribution among speakers and session chairs at the 36th Annual Meeting of the JSHPT was evaluated using the publicly available abstract book. Among all members, 128 (14.9%) were women. Women accounted for 6.6% of councilors and 5.6% of board members and auditors, and only one woman has served as Annual Meeting president among the first 36 annual meetings. At the 36th Annual Meeting, women represented 18.6% of presenters in general oral sessions, but only 5.0% of session chairs in general oral sessions and 3.7% of chairs in other sessions. Women also accounted for 36.4% of members with less than 1 year of membership. These findings suggest that, although women remain underrepresented in leadership positions within the society, female participation may be increasing among newer members, and women are actively participating as conference presenters. Continued efforts to increase opportunities for women to serve as session chairs and to encourage qualified women to apply for councilor positions may contribute not only to a more equitable academic environment, but also to the vitality and future leadership of the field.

Introduction

Gender equity has become an increasingly important issue in academic medicine. Previous studies have reported gender disparities in academic promotion, leadership positions, and representation at scientific meetings [1]. A report from JES We Can, the Gender Equality Promotion Committee of the Japanese Endocrine Society, demonstrated gradual improvement in female participation in academic activities within the endocrine community, although women remain underrepresented in leadership positions [2]. The Japanese Society for Hypothalamic and Pituitary Tumors (JSHPT) is a multidisciplinary academic community including neurosurgeons, endocrinologists, pediatricians, and basic scientists, with strong relevance to clinical and academic endocrinology. Understanding the current gender distribution within this community is important for identifying potential barriers to career advancement and for planning future diversity initiatives. In this study, we analyzed gender distribution in membership, leadership positions, and conference participation in JSHPT as an initial cross-sectional assessment of the current status of the society. Our aim was to provide baseline data to support constructive discussion within the society and to inform future longitudinal evaluation of changes over time. The overall findings of this study are summarized in the Graphical Abstract.

Materials and Methods

Membership data as of March 2025 were obtained from the official registry of the society with permission from the current president of the society and administrative office. The dataset included 860 members. Data were anonymized prior to analysis. Information collected included specialty, membership duration, and leadership roles (councilors, board members and auditors, and presidents). Specialties were categorized as neurosurgery, internal medicine, pediatrics, basic sciences, and others. Gender was inferred from names listed in the membership registry and abstract book. Gender distribution among presenters and session chairs was analyzed using the publicly available abstract book of the 36th Annual Meeting of this society. This study analyzed anonymized membership data and publicly available information and did not involve identifiable personal data; therefore, institutional review board approval was not required. An artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted language tool (ChatGPT, OpenAI) was used only to improve English readability and grammar during manuscript preparation. All outputs were carefully reviewed and revised by the authors, who take full responsibility for the content of the manuscript.

Results

Membership

Among the 860 members of the society, 732 were men and 128 were women, corresponding to a female proportion of 14.9%.

Gender distribution varied by specialty. Neurosurgery accounted for the majority of members but had a relatively low proportion of women (7.6%). Membership duration

The proportion of women was highest among members with less than 1 year of membership (36.4%, 20/55), and gradually decreased with longer membership duration: 18.5% (34/184) for 1–5 years, 13.5% (23/170) for 6–10 years, 14.3% (33/231) for 11–20 years, and 8.2% (18/220) for more than 20 years (Table 1).

Leadership positions

Among the 91 councilors of the society, 6 were women (6.6%). The proportion of members serving as councilors was 11.6% among men (85/732) and 4.7% among women (6/128).

Among the 36 board members and auditors, 2 were women (5.6%). The proportion serving as board members or auditors was 4.6% among men (34/732) and 1.6% among women (2/128).

Among the first 36 annual meetings, only one woman has served as Annual Meeting president. These findings are summarized in Table 1.

Conference participation

Female representation varied across presentation categories at the 36th Annual Meeting (Table 2). Women accounted for 3.1% of symposium speakers (2/64), 25.0% of educational lecture speakers (1/4), 0% of invited lecture speakers (0/1), 18.6% of general oral presenters (19/102), 0% of sponsored seminar speakers (0/6), and 0% of research award speakers (0/4).

Discussion

This study provides a baseline overview of gender distribution within JSHPT. Women accounted for 14.9% of all members, reflecting the male predominance of this field, particularly in neurosurgery. At the same time, the proportion of women was higher among members with shorter membership duration. This pattern likely reflects a recent increase in female enrollment rather than attrition of women within the first several years after registration, although longitudinal follow-up will be required to clarify changes over time.

The present study was intentionally designed as an initial cross-sectional assessment of the current status of gender distribution in the society. Establishing such baseline data is important for promoting constructive discussion within the society and for providing a reference point for future longitudinal evaluation. Rather than retrospectively evaluating past practices, our aim was to document the current structure of participation and leadership as a foundation for future improvement.

In contrast, women were underrepresented in leadership positions. Female representation decreased progressively from general membership to councilors and board members, indicating a leadership gap. Interestingly, the proportion of female presenters in general oral sessions at the 36th Annual Meeting was higher than the overall proportion of female members. In addition, among society members, the proportion who presented in general oral sessions was slightly higher in women than in men (14.8% vs. 11.3%) (Table 3). These findings suggest that women are actively participating in academic activities within the society.

However, this active participation was not reflected in chairing roles at the same meeting. Female representation among session chairs remained low, both in general oral sessions and in other sessions, despite the relatively higher proportion of female presenters. This discrepancy suggests that participation in academic presentations does not automatically translate into visible leadership opportunities. It should also be noted that roles such as session chair and councilor are typically attained after a certain period of society participation, academic achievement, and professional recognition. Therefore, changes in the gender composition of younger or newer members may not be immediately reflected in visible leadership roles. In this sense, some degree of time lag between increased participation and leadership representation is expected. Even so, the present findings suggest the importance of ensuring that active academic participation can be translated into future leadership opportunities.

In our analysis, female representation decreased markedly from general membership to councilor positions, suggesting that the transition to leadership roles may represent an important bottleneck in the academic career pathway. Eligibility for councilor status in this society requires academic achievements such as conference presentations and publications, as well as self-nomination. This indicates that, in addition to supporting academic participation, active encouragement to seek councilor positions may be important, particularly for academically active women who may be less likely to pursue such roles without explicit encouragement or mentorship.

The multidisciplinary composition of JSHPT should also be considered when interpreting these findings. The society includes neurosurgeons, endocrinologists, pediatricians, and basic scientists, and pathways into society participation may differ across specialties. In neurosurgery, many members may join the society relatively early as part of a sustained commitment to this field, whereas in other specialties, some members may become involved after gaining a certain level of clinical or academic experience, for example through collaborative research, presentations, or committee activities. Thus, differences in gender distribution and membership duration across specialties may reflect not only workforce demographics but also differences in pathways into society participation.

In addition, the relatively high proportion of women among newly registered members may suggest that more women are entering this field in recent years, including fields that have traditionally had lower female representation. This may reflect a gradual change in the gender composition of younger generations entering related specialties. It may also reflect broader social and institutional changes that have made academic participation more feasible, such as improved childcare support, greater sharing of caregiving responsibilities within families, and increasing support in workplaces and society. Although the present study did not directly examine these background factors, they may represent an important context for interpreting the observed increase in participation.

A report from JES We Can described efforts within the Japan Endocrine Society to promote women’s academic participation, including increasing opportunities for female chairs and speakers [2]. Similar patterns of active participation by women alongside underrepresentation in leadership positions have also been reported in other academic societies [2, 3]. Our findings therefore likely reflect not only society-specific characteristics but also broader structural issues in academic medicine. In this context, efforts to increase opportunities to serve as session chairs and to actively invite qualified women to consider councilor roles may help bridge the gap between academic participation and leadership visibility.

The aim of the present study is not to retrospectively criticize past practice, but to clarify the current structure of participation and leadership within the society and thereby promote constructive discussion. Such visibility may provide an opportunity to reconsider how roles such as session chairs, committee members, and future councilors are identified and supported. Accumulation of these small changes in awareness may contribute to greater diversity in leadership over time.

Informal mentoring relationships across institutions may also play an important role in supporting early-career researchers. Scientific conferences provide an important environment for cross-institutional communication and mentorship. Encouraging participation and providing guidance to younger members—both women and men—may help strengthen the leadership pipeline. Importantly, such efforts should be viewed not only as a matter of fairness, but also as an investment in the vitality, diversity, and sustainability of the academic society and the future development of the field.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis was limited to a single academic society. Second, gender classification was inferred from names in the membership registry and abstract book, which may have resulted in minor misclassification. Third, conference participation was analyzed primarily using the abstract book of a single annual meeting and therefore does not capture longitudinal changes over time. Nevertheless, this study provides useful baseline data on gender distribution in a subspecialty academic society and may serve as a reference point for future longitudinal evaluation.

Conclusion

Women remain underrepresented in leadership positions within JSHPT. However, increasing participation among newer members and conference presenters suggests a potential future leadership pipeline. Continued efforts to increase opportunities for women to serve as session chairs and to encourage qualified women to apply for councilor positions may contribute not only to gender equity, but also to the academic vitality, sustainability, and future leadership of the field.
