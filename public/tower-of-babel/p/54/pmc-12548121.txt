Exploring feasibility of health-related quality of life assessments and pain diaries in patients undergoing prehabilitation before total knee arthroplasty: a pilot study
Authors: Pascale Gränicher, Michel Kocher, Rob de Bie, Jaap Swanenburg, Johannes Scherr
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrehabilitation prior to total knee arthroplasty (TKA) may mitigate deterioration in health-related quality of life (HRQOL) during the preoperative period and support recovery after surgery. However, little is known about the feasibility of systematically monitoring HRQOL and pain through patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) in this context. This pilot study aimed to evaluate the feasibility of using validated HRQOL questionnaires and daily pain diaries during a prehabilitation programme for patients awaiting (TKA) and up to 12 weeks postoperatively.MethodsTwenty patients (mean age 73 ± 6 years) scheduled for primary unilateral TKA were randomly assigned to an intervention group (IG) or control group (CG). The IG participated in a 4- to 8-week individualised prehabilitation programme involving exercise and education. Feasibility was assessed through the completion rates of daily pain diaries and three HRQOL questionnaires (EQ-5D-5 L, SF-12 and KOOS KRQOL) collected at baseline, preoperatively, and at 6- and 12-weeks postoperatively.ResultsHRQOL questionnaires demonstrated high completion rates across both groups (90%). In contrast, adherence to paper-based pain diaries was suboptimal and notably low (CG: 58%; IG: 47%), falling below the threshold for meaningful analysis. Consequently, pain trajectory over time could not be reliably assessed. No significant group-by-time interaction effects were observed for any HRQOL measure: EQ-5D-5 L (p = 0.67; F = 0.51), KOOS KRQOL (p = 0.72; F = 0.64), SF-12 PCS (p = 0.06; F = 0.50) and MCS (p = 0.36; F = 0.10).ConclusionThis pilot study confirms the feasibility and acceptability of HRQOL questionnaires in older patients undergoing prehabilitation before TKA. However, the low diary completion rates highlight the need for alternative pain-monitoring-approaches – such as electronic formats - to improve data quality in future trials. Preliminary findings suggest potential benefits of prehabilitation, warranting further investigation in larger studies.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier; NCT05314985. Background

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is a common surgical intervention for patients with end-stage knee osteoarthritis who no longer respond to conservative treatments such as pharmacological pain management, physiotherapy, or assistive devices [1]. As the prevalence of advanced knee osteoarthritis rises, the number of TKAs continues to increase – both in Switzerland, where 19,195 procedures were performed in 2022 (a 50% rise since 2013) [2], and across Europe, with projections indicating a 33%−45% increase in countries like France and Italy by 2050 [3, 4].

Most patients expect significant pain relief and improvements in health-related quality of life (HRQOL) following surgery [5, 6]. But despite its routine nature, TKA does not always yield satisfactory results and is associated with highly variable outcomes influenced by factors such as baseline function, psychological status, comorbidities, and patient expectations [7]. Studies have shown that 10%−30% of patients report results that fall short of their personal expectations [8], and approximately 10%−34% of experience ongoing pain following surgery [9, 10]. These figures underscore the need for strategies that can enhance postoperative recovery, improve quality of life, and better align outcomes with patient expectations.

Pain is a critical determinant of HRQOL [11]. While retrospective questionnaires are commonly used to assess pain during medical consultations, they may be limited by recall bias, particularly in dynamic phases such as early postoperative recovery [12, 13] Continuous monitoring using pain diaries offers a more accurate reflection of symptom progression, though adherence can be a challenge. High completion rates of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), including diaries and HRQOL questionnaires, are essential indicators of both, feasibility and patient engagement in clinical research [14, 15].

Prehabilitation – structured physiotherapy interventions before surgery – has shown promise in improving physical function, muscle strength, and reducing postoperative complications and hospital length of stay [16–19]. It may also positively shape patient expectations, which are closely linked to postoperative satisfaction and perceived quality of life [18, 20–22]. However, despite emerging evidence for functional benefits, the impact of prehabilitation on HRQOL remains unclear, and few studies have incorporated systematic pre- and postoperative PROMs monitoring [20].

This is particularly relevant given that most patients enter TKA with high expectations, and greater improvements in HRQOL have been reported, when those expectations are met [23]. Moreover, early postoperative gains in HRQOL may persist long term, as shown in follow-ups up to seven years post-surgery [24]. These findings highlight the importance of capturing changes in self-reported HRQOL over time. Yet, to date, the feasibility of implementing longitudinal HRQOL assessments and pain monitoring tools – such as diaries and validated PROMs – during the prehabilitation phase of TKA remains largely unexplored despite their increasing importance in clinical care and research [25]. Notably, prior research has seldom assessed the feasibility of implementing longitudinal HRQOL assessments and daily pain monitoring during prehabilitation for TKA. This represents a critical gap, as feasible and reliable measurement tools are essential for evaluating intervention effects in both clinical practice and future trials.

The primary aim of this pilot study was to evaluate the feasibility of using validated HRQOL questionnaires and daily pain diaries before and after TKA in the context of an exercise- and education-based prehabilitation programme. A secondary aim was to explore preliminary estimates of the effects of prehabilitation on HRQOL compared to usual care, to inform the design and power calculations of larger-scale trials.

Methods

Trial design

A parallel-group, prospective, controlled pilot trial was conducted at Balgrist University Hospital starting in August 2021. This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was performed according to local ethics committee approval (KEK Zürich; BASEC-NR. 2020–03060), and all participants provided written informed consent. The protocol was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05314985), and the study is reported according to the CONSORT 2010 statement: extension to randomised pilot and feasibility trials [26]. A detailed trial protocol of the study methodology has been published elsewhere [27] and is briefly Summarised below. In line with feasibility trial recommendations, a sample size of 20 was chosen to explore intervention acceptability, protocol adherence, and outcome measurement completion and adherence [28].

Participants

Patients with end-stage knee osteoarthritis who have exhausted conservative treatment options and are scheduled for primary unilateral total knee arthroplasty (TKA) at the Department of Orthopedics in Balgrist University Hospital were recruited for this trial. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged ≥ 18 years, (2) willing to visit for study assessments, and (3) to keep a personal diary from baseline (immediately after indication for TKA was given, min. 4 max. 8 weeks before surgery) to 12 weeks after surgery. To ensure methodological consistency and reduce the risk of confounding in this pilot study, we applied specific exclusion criteria to maintain a relatively homogenous sample. These included: (1) inability to follow study procedures (e.g., due to language barriers), (2) neurological disorders causing significant muscle weakness, (3) obesity grade II or higher (BMI > 35.0 kg/m2), (4) clinically diagnosed depression, (5) patellar instability, (6) patella alta (Caton Deschamps Index > 1.2), and (7) acute pain exacerbation or signs of inflammation. After enrolment, patients were randomly assigned to either the intervention group (IG) or the control group (CG) using a concealed allocation process [27]. Group assignment was performed by an independent study nurse who drew from a set of opaque, sealed, and sequentially numbered envelopes, each containing the pre-determined group allocation. Due to the nature of the study (exercise intervention) and the institutional procedures, blinding of the patients, assessors and physical therapists with regard to group allocation was not possible. Participants were not replaced after withdrawal. Given the study’s focus on exploring the feasibility of using personal diaries and HRQOL questionnaires, and recognising the necessity for pilot testing before embarking on larger-scale studies, a sample size of 20 participants was deemed sufficient.

Intervention

Following the baseline assessment, the IG commenced the prehabilitation intervention immediately, undergoing 1 to 2 Supervised sessions per week along with an additional home exercise programme for a duration of 4 to 8 weeks preceding TKA. The prehabilitation programme was administered by a skilled team of pre-instructed physical therapists to ensure uniform treatment across all participants. The personalised training programme encompassed strength, endurance, mobility, and sensorimotor exercises, with the specific programme structure detailed elsewhere [27]. Exercise selection, intensity, difficulty, and dosage were continuously individualised based on participant’s functional goals, baseline fitness level, pain severity, and comorbidities in accordance with the guidelines of the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) [29]. Conversely, participants in the CG were instructed to maintain their pre-baseline activity levels, refraining from initiating any new forms of therapy or training during the same period.

Outcomes

Both groups underwent assessments at several defined timepoints: baseline, 4 to 8 weeks before surgery (T1); immediately before Surgery, 2 to 10 days after the intervention period (T2), and at follow-ups at 6 weeks (T3) and 12 weeks (T4) postoperatively. The findings regarding the feasibility of the exercise- and education-based prehabilitation intervention and its preliminary effectiveness on knee functioning compared to usual care were previously reported [27]. The timeframe between T1 and the week of surgery was designated as weeks − 8 to 0, with week 0 marking the surgical event. All TKA procedures were performed by the same orthopedic surgical team to ensure consistency.

Due to the clinical setting, the nature of the intervention, and standard hospital procedures, blinding of assessors, patients and therapists to group allocation was not feasible.

The feasibility of using paper-based personal diaries to track daily pain from baseline through the 12-week follow-up was assessed based on documentation rates. A minimum return rate of 60%, as recommended by Baruch et al. [30] and Holtom et al. [31], was defined as the threshold for acceptable compliance. Participants were instructed to complete daily pain ratings using a numeric rating scale (NRS), which ranged from 0 (“no pain”) to 10 (“worst imaginable pain”). The NRS was selected for its simplicity, high compliance, and strong psychometric properties compared to alternatives such as the visual analog scale (VAS) or verbal rating scale (VRS) [32, 33].

During the preoperative period, the diary captured minimum and maximum daily pain scores, pain at rest (i.e., in the absence of movement), and pain under load (i.e., during physical activity or exertion or weight-bearing activities, typically relieved by rest). In the postoperative period, to reduce participant burden and improve adherence, documentation was limited to average pain. This approach aligns with evidence from Stone et al. [34], suggesting that shorter, less demanding diary formats improve long-term compliance.

Additionally, the feasibility of utilising three validated and reliable questionnaires for knee osteoarthritis patients to investigate the impact of prehabilitation on HRQOL was explored [35–37]. The EuroQol-5 Dimensions-5 Levels (EQ-5D-5 L) health survey, recommended for analysing HRQOL in primary TKA patients, comprises a descriptive system assessing mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression, alongside a visual analog scale (EQ-VAS) ranging from 0 to 100, rating general health [35, 38]. The resulting conversion follows the HRQOL convention whereby 1 represents full health and 0 death, and the cutoff point for MIC for the EQ-Index is equivalent to 0.2 [39, 40] Counterintuitively, within the context of mathematical calculation, negative scores can occur, given a scoring range of −0.594 to 1 [35]. The Knee Osteoarthritis Outcome Scale (KOOS) evaluates knee joint function across symptoms, pain, activities of daily living (ADL), function in sport and recreation (sports), and knee-related HRQOL (KRQOL), with scores ranging from 0 to 4 for each subcategory [41–43]. The minimal important change (MIC) is 15.6 points for the section KRQOL [44]. The Short Form 12 (SF-12), a concise version of the Short Form 36, assesses physical and mental HRQOL, with MIC of 2.7 for the physical component (PCS) and 1.4 for the mental component (MCS) [37, 45, 46]. Estimates of prehabilitation effects on HRQOL were assessed using the EQ-5D-5 L and KOOS (KRQOL subdomain) completed at T1 to T4 and the SF-12 administered alongside the performance tests at T1, T2 and T4. The KOOS KRQOL is a knee-specific HRQOL instrument and fits within our focus on HRQOL, alongside the more generic SF-12 and EQ-5D-5 L.

Statistical methods

Patient demographics, clinical characteristics and documentation rates of the diaries are presented using descriptive statistics (mean ± standard deviation (SD), percentage (%) and frequency). An intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis was performed on the data collected at assessment points T1 to T4, and missing values were imputed based on the mean value of the original group assignment. Normal distribution was assessed visually by histograms and the Shapiro-Wilk test, and two-way ANOVA with repeated measures (2 groups, 3 or 4 time points) was applied to explore the effects on the KOOS KRQOL, EQ-5D-5 L and SF-12 scores (PCS and MCS). Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05, and all the statistical procedures were performed using the IBM SPSS 29.0 statistical software package (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Although pain diaries were collected throughout the study period, documentation rates fell below the predefined threshold for reliable analysis per pain dimension (at rest vs. under load). As a result, the available data were insufficient to meaningfully evaluate pain trajectories over time, limiting our ability to draw conclusions regarding changes in pain levels during the pre- and postoperative phases.

Despite limited adherence, available diary data Suggest a steady increase in preoperative pain, peaking shortly before Surgery. In the IG, mean minimal pain rose from NRS 0.0 (week − 8) to NRS 3.2 (week − 1); in the CG from 4.2 (week − 4) to 4.4 (week − 1). Maximal pain in the IG ranged from 5.5 (week − 8) to 5.3 (week − 1), and in the CG from 6.00 (week − 6) to 6.5 (w1). Postoperatively, mean pain declined progressively: in the IG from 5.3 (week 1) to 0.6 (week 12), and in the CG from 6.3 to 1.7.

Discussion

This pilot study aimed to assess the feasibility of using validated HRQOL questionnaires and pain diaries in patients undergoing prehabilitation before TKA. Our findings indicate that the use of HRQOL questionnaires – including EQ-5D-5 L, KOOS KRQOL, and SF-12 – is feasible in this population, as reflected by consistently high completion rates. In contrast, adherence to daily paper-based pain diaries was suboptimal throughout both the pre- and postoperative periods, suggesting a need for improved or alternative data collection strategies, such as digital formats.

Our findings suggest that HRQOL questionnaires are feasible in older TKA patients, while diaries need refinement (e.g., digital formats). Although no statistically significant group differences in HRQOL outcomes were observed, this aligns with the exploratory nature and limited sample size typical of feasibility studies. The value of our study lies in identifying practical challenges and providing direction for optimising future trial designs, particularly regarding patient burden and data completeness in longitudinal monitoring.

Strengths and limitations

The present study serves as a feasibility study for a pilot prehabilitation programme prior to TKA, which was conducted with a limited number of participants. Consequently, interpretations of the results should be confined within this specific context. Furthermore, the stringent inclusion criteria employed may result in a sample that inadequately represents the broader population. Additionally, the application of very strict exclusion criteria meant that only 18 out of 138 potentially eligible patients were ultimately included. This represents a major limitation of the study, as the resulting sample may not reflect the heterogeneity of the general TKA patient population. Although this approach minimises confounding factors, it also diminishes the generalisability of the findings and reduces the representativeness of the sample. While group sizes were small and inclusion criteria strict, this was necessary to reduce heterogeneity in feasibility evaluation. Our results will guide larger trials with broader eligibility.

The execution of postoperative care in this study occurred individually according to current standard care across various private practices and rehabilitation clinics. This resulted in an inability to control variables such as the content or frequency of postoperative therapy, or the influence of other health complications following surgery. Despite this lack of control, the utilisation of personal diaries for close monitoring offered valuable insights into individual postoperative states from a patient’s perspective, as previously noted by Lischetzke et al. [13]. They also reported the distortion that comes with retrospective questionnaires to assess pain since the subjective evaluation of pain sensations changes over time. The recollection of pain experiences therefore tends to diminish accuracy. Moreover, the absence of standardised instructions for pain rating at consistent times of the day, introduces a potential source of bias. Additionally, variations in pain levels following physical exertion versus rest may have impacted reported pain levels.

An inherent limitation of the study was the lack of blinding among patients, therapists, and assessors due to the nature of the intervention. While self-reported questionnaires reduce the risk of observer bias, unblinded assessors may still unintentionally influence patient responses through subtle verbal or non-verbal cues [47]. This could have affected patients’’ perception or reporting, especially regarding subjective outcomes such as pain and quality of life. Although the risk is lower for self-administered measures, the potential for expectancy effects or differential interaction cannot be fully excluded and should be considered in the interpretation of results. Moreover, the revelation of group affiliation to patients upon assignment, potentially introduced the risk of cross-contamination in the CG [48]. Choosing a sham intervention for the CG could mitigate this issue and warrants consideration in future trials.

In addition to the aforementioned limitation, incomplete and irregular completion of diaries by patients resulted in limited data extraction, rendering it insufficient to draw conclusions about experienced pain levels. This underscores the challenge of inadequate adherence and practicability in pain level monitoring, consistent with existing literature highlighting issues of loss to follow-up and nonresponse bias in clinical trials. For instance, Somerson et al. [49, 50] observed an escalating risk of loss to follow-up with extended follow-up periods, with Zelle et al. [51] noting that a 20% loss to follow-up could significantly impact study outcomes. Accordingly, a trend toward improved compliance can be seen as the date of surgery approaches [52].

Notably, this study maintained a documentation rate of 67% or higher for 8 weeks postoperatively in the CG, achieving an overall postoperative documentation rate of 70%. This achievement aligns with trends indicating improved compliance as the surgery date approaches, as well as with observations by Holtom et al. [31] regarding a gradual increase in average response rates over time. While this study faced challenges in pain level monitoring and adherence, it successfully maintained a documentation rate comparable to current standards, highlighting the importance of addressing and overcoming these challenges in future research endeavours.

Clinical implementation

This is one of the first studies to examine long-term diary adherence in older patients undergoing TKA prehabilitation. In our study, we observed that maximum pain was more consistently documented than minimum pain. Surprisingly, we found no evidence indicating that patients encountered greater difficulty reporting minimum pain levels compared to maximum pain. This observation aligns with current literature advocating for the use of standardised pain scales to facilitate more consistent and effective communication of pain levels by patients [53]. Contrary to the assumption that challenges in reporting lower pain levels might underlie the observed differences, we propose that patients perceive maximum pain as more salient or memorable, leading to more accurate documentation.

Furthermore, we noted a disparity in diary completion rates between the CG and IG, both preoperatively and postoperatively. We hypothesized that the higher burden experienced by the IG due to the intervention programme may have diminished their interest or motivation in diary upkeep, potentially affecting postoperative documentation adherence as well. Encouraging patients to complete their diaries in the morning may reduce the perceived burden of PROMs, as it fits smoothly into their daily routine when they are more alert and focused [54].

Recognising the challenges encountered in data collection via paper diaries, transitioning to electronic diaries could offer significant improvements. Electronic diaries, as reported by Fisher et al. [55], offer advantages such as easy data entry via smartphones, secure data storage, superior data quality, accessibility, and timestamping. However, challenges such as digital literacy among elderly participants, increased costs, longer preparation times, and data protection concerns must be addressed [55].

Regardless of the diary format used, fostering a spirit of cooperation and respect between researchers and participants is paramount for improving compliance, as highlighted by Green et al. [56]. Spending additional time at the study’s outset to emphasise the importance of diligent diary maintenance may prove beneficial. Holtom et al. [31] found that incentives increased documentation rates by 10%, while reminders showed mixed results, potentially even decreasing response rates. Despite high questionnaire compliance rates, disparities between diary and questionnaire completion may stem from differences in administration methods and frequency since in this study, the majority of the questionnaires were completed in the presence of the examiner, and, the participants had to complete the questionnaires a maximum of 4 times.

Our findings suggest that while preoperative exercise and education programmes may be beneficial for patients undergoing TKA in terms of knee functioning [27], the effectiveness of such interventions may not be accurately captured through diary documentation alone. Further research is warranted to explore alternative methods of assessing PROMs and to optimise prehabilitation programmes for this patient population. Additionally, strategies to improve diary compliance should be considered to enhance data collection and better understand the impact of prehabilitation on patient outcomes.

Although our study did not detect significant improvements in HRQOL after prehabilitation compared to usual care, trends suggesting favorable effects were observed. Participation in the prehabilitation programme may have heightened expectations of outcomes in the IG, influencing perceived impacts on HRQOL. Exploring correlations between questionnaires, our data mirrored findings from prior studies, reinforcing the validity and utility of the EQ-5D-5 L, KOOS, and SF-12 instruments. For example, Ackerman et al. [57] reported a moderate correlation of 0.58 between the EQ-5D-5 L scores and the KOOS KRQOL subscale. The EQ-5D-5 L, KOOS and SF-12 questionnaires have been validated by several studies and have been utilised in various prior studies, facilitating cross-study comparability [35–37, 58–60].

To enhance HRQOL effectiveness through intervention, structured patient education on self-management, exercise, and coping strategies, as proposed by Mizner et al. [61] and Fransen et al. [62], could yield better outcomes. Moreover, our intervention primarily focused on physical aspects, neglecting emotional needs. Canovas et al. [63] underscored the importance of preoperative psychological and physical assessments and postoperative care in enhancing HRQOL after TKA. Incorporating these factors into interventions may yield significant improvements in questionnaire outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this pilot study demonstrates that HRQOL questionnaires are a feasible and well-accepted tool for monitoring outcomes in patients undergoing prehabilitation before TKA. However, the low adherence to paper-based pain diaries highlights significant limitations in their practicality for daily pain monitoring daily in this population. These findings underscore the need to explore more user-friendly and less burdensome alternatives - such as electronic or app-based solutions - particularly tailored to older adults. While the small sample size precludes firm conclusions about the effectiveness of prehabilitation on HRQOL and pain, the observed trends support the rationale for larger, adequately powered trials to further investigate these outcomes.
