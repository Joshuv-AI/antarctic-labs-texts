Rapid-manufacturing and cost-effective single-layer microfluidic device for high-throughput three-dimensional hydrodynamic focusing
Authors: Ruopeng Yan, Shubin Wei, Yueyun Weng, Rubing Li, Yan Jin, Xun Liu, Jinxuan Hou, Yu Xu, Sheng Li, Du Wang, Haihang Ye, Sheng Liu, Cheng Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrofluidic devices play a crucial role in the widespread application of single-cell analysis, where hydrodynamic focusing stands out due to its simplicity in structure and excellent adaptability to a wide range of flow rates. Owing to the extensive application of soft lithography, polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) is widely used in the fabrication of microfluidic devices. However, challenges arise under high-throughput conditions, where the elastic deformation of PDMS can cause microchannel expansion, diminishing focusing effect. To address this challenge, this work introduces a three-dimensional (3D) hydrodynamic focusing device with simplified single-layer structure, which is fabricated by the double transfer process, specifically designed for fabricating polyurethane acrylate (PUA) microfluidic devices. Notably, this approach eliminates the time-consuming heating procedures, which significantly enhances manufacturing speed by an order of magnitude compared to the soft lithography process. To evaluate the practical focusing performance of the microfluidic device, optical time-stretch (OTS) microscopy is employed for high-throughput imaging of clinical urine samples. Experimental results demonstrate that as the flow rate increases, the focusing efficiency gradually improves in both vertical and lateral directions. At an averaged velocity of 16.7 m/s, the focusing efficiency reaches 98.4% in the vertical direction and 95.0% in the lateral direction. Thus, the amalgamation of simplicity, efficiency, and adaptability positions this technology as a promising tool in the realm of microfluidics, particularly for applications requiring precise cell focusing in high-throughput scenarios. Subject terms: Engineering, Optics and photonics

Introduction

Flow cytometry plays an important role in single-cell analysis with its powerful functions, including cell counting, detection, and sorting, which have been widely used in clinical diagnostics and academic research1–3. With the rapid development of microfluidic technology, microfluidic cytometry, which optimizes portability, cost efficiency, and integration, has attracted widespread attention from both the research and business communities4–6. In the recent past, numerous detection methods have been developed within the microfluidic platform, including lateral/background scattering7, fluorimetry8, Raman spectroscopy9,10, electrical impedance11–13, opto-acoustic imaging14,15, and optical imaging16,17. Meanwhile, the aforementioned detection methods are compatible with different active sorting methods, such as optical tweezers18,19, dielectrophoresis (DEP)20,21, surface acoustic wave (SAW)22,23, and micro-electro-mechanical systems (MEMS)24,25, which further extend their applications in practice. To achieve higher detection accuracy and sorting precision, flow focusing is extremely essential for the distribution of microparticles within a continuous flow, which can ensure that all the particles pass through the detection/actuation window stably. More specifically, the continuously flowing microparticles are restricted by physical forces, i.e., hydrodynamic, inertial, viscoelastic, hydrophoretic, electric, and acoustic forces, which can rapidly and precisely focus the particles to the expected positions within the microchannel26,27.

Therein, hydrodynamic focusing, which employs sheath flow to enclose the microparticles within the core flow, can be deployed in a microfluidic device with simpler construction to simultaneously adapt a wide-scale adjustment on microparticle size and flow rate. Thus, hydrodynamic focusing is compatible with various detection and sorting methods, which have been widely employed in microfluidic cytometry. However, for two-dimensional (2D) hydrodynamic focusing, the samples are only focused in the horizontal plane, which limits its potential to be used in applications with high precision requirements. For example, in a typical imaging cytometry system, numerous details will be missed when the particles are flowing out of the focus plane. To solve this problem, 3D hydrodynamic focusing additionally restricts the particles in a vertical direction, which can further avoid the influence on the signal collection of a single particle. For example, Testa et al. chose poly methyl methacrylate (PMMA) as the base material to construct a 3D microchannel processed by micro-milling and thermal bonding, in which sheath channels were designed with a greater height than the sample channel to simultaneously focus particles in lateral and vertical directions28. Similarly, Sakuma et al. employed wet etching and anodic bonding techniques to fabricate the silicon-glass microfluidic chip where horizontal sheath and vertical sheath were orderly connected to the sample flow to accomplish 3D hydrodynamic focusing29. Even so, multiple-layer microchannel structures are deeply required to realize on-chip 3D hydrodynamic focusing, which also increases the manufacturing difficulty (i.e., multi-step photolithography and ultra-fine alignment) and hinders its potential to be used in practice.

In order to reduce the complexity of fabrication, the single-layer strategy has attracted widespread attention from the academic community26,30,31. One approach involves replacing precise alignment and bonding by fabricating molds through complex processes. For example, Eluru et al. reported a single-layer PDMS microfluidic device with abrupt channel depth variation to achieve hydrodynamic 3D focusing, where a sample inlet and two pairs of sheath inlets are individually connected to constrain the sample flow along the desired location30. However, in that design, the composite mold composed of three layers of SU-8 photoresist on a silicon wafer requires multi-step photolithography, which only slightly reduces the manufacturing difficulty. Another approach aims to use curved channels to generate Dean flow that can induce particle focusing in the vertical direction. Nawaz et al. designed and fabricated “microfluidic drifting” devices that employed a pair of core and sheath inlets connected to a curved channel to constrain the flowing particles in the vertical direction, with an additional pair of sheath inlets connected to the curved channel to achieve horizontal focusing31. Although this method can achieve 3D focusing with submicron precision, the stringent requirements for flow rate configuration (typical flow rates range from 50 μL/min to 550 μL/min) also limit its potential in high-throughput applications.

Additionally, PDMS is a common material used in soft lithography for fabricating microfluidic devices with intricate channel structures17,32–34. Due to the elastic properties of PDMS, severe swelling deformation occurs on the walls of the microchannels under high flow velocities (typical flow velocity >1 m/s), significantly reducing the corresponding focusing effect32,35. Yaxiaer et al. reported that glass devices can reduce flow-induced wall deformation, achieving stable focusing under an ultrahigh flow velocity of 25 m/s35. However, the complex and time-consuming fabrication process has narrowed its application in practice, especially for large-scale deployment. Therefore, developing a microfluidic device for high-speed 3D hydrodynamic focusing, which offers the advantages of a simplified structure and rapid manufacturing, is of great significance for high-throughput microfluidic cytometry.

In this work, we propose a 3D hydrodynamic focusing microfluidic device with a single-layer construction, which can be rapidly fabricated using the double transfer process. Our design resolves this by integrating perpendicularly arranged vertical and lateral sheath channels into a single-layer architecture, leveraging two complementary hydrodynamic mechanisms to achieve efficient 3D confinement: First, for vertical focusing, we adopt an asymmetric T-junction with a gradually narrowing channel. As the sample flow and vertical sheath flow converge in the narrowing channel, the inertial difference between the two streams drives the sample flow to be passively confined to the upper half of the channel cross-section. This inertial-assisted vertical compression avoids the need for multi-layer height differences while ensuring robust vertical focusing. Second, for lateral focusing, two symmetric lateral sheath channels are positioned downstream of the vertical focusing region, where the flow rate of the sheath fluid is set higher than that of the sample fluid. Based on the viscous shear force and pressure gradient of the fluid, the sheath fluid exerts a centripetal squeezing force on the sample fluid, compressing it toward the central axis of the channel. The flow constraining enhancement effect of the sample in the vertical and horizontal directions was verified through numerical simulation. Subsequently, fluid-structure coupled numerical models are created to compare the focusing conditions within microfluidic devices fabricated from PDMS and PUA, respectively. Compared with the PDMS device, where the focusing effect decreases due to serious deformation, the PUA device has good deformation resistance to ensure stable focusing at high velocity. Leveraging the photo-curing characteristics of PUA material, a double transfer process has been developed to enable the rapid fabrication of microfluidic devices within 5 min. After that, hydrodynamic focusing visualization is performed with red ink to investigate the condition of sample flow and evaluate the 3D hydrodynamic focusing effect under different flow rate configurations. Finally, optical time-stretch (OTS) microscopy is used to evaluate the focusing performance of the microfluidic device by imaging a clinical urine sample. The experimental results indicate that, with the increase in flow rate, the focusing efficiency gradually increases in both the vertical and lateral directions, reaching 98.4% and 95.0%, respectively, at 16.7 m/s. This underscores the practical viability and efficacy of our proposed microfluidic device, making it a promising tool for high-throughput applications in single-cell analysis and beyond.

Materials and methods

Design of microfluidic device

To achieve 3D hydrodynamic focusing with a single-layer structure, the microfluidic device is designed with vertical and lateral focusing processes, as illustrated in Fig. 1a. For the vertical focusing process, a pair of sheath and sample inlets are orderly set on a gradually narrowing channel to construct an asymmetric T-junction. When the flow characteristics exhibit a high Reynolds number (a typical number Re>25), the sample flow will be half-surrounded by the sheath flow due to the effect of inertia. As the simulation of flow visualization shown in Fig. 1b indicates, the concentration profiles at the sample inlet and the end of the gradually narrowing channel demonstrate that the sample flow can be compressed into the upper half of the channel section under the action of hydrodynamic inertia. For the lateral focusing process, two sheath inlets are configured on both sides of the narrowing channel, where the compressed sample flow is subsequently sandwiched by two sheath flows. Due to the action of symmetric sheath flow, the sample flow can be further compressed in the lateral direction. Finally, the 3D hydrodynamic focusing result can be observed at the detection window, as shown in the insets of Fig. 1b. Meanwhile, to minimize the absorption of the chip material by the detection light beam, at the detection window, the upper part of the microchannel is encapsulated with a piece of high permeability glass, which reduces the thickness of the microfluidic device and shortens the optical path length. Since glass is a brittle material, this design also avoids the impact of deformation caused by high-speed flow on both 3D focusing and optical detection simultaneously.

In order to optimize the 3D focusing effect, we conducted a numerical investigation on the flow rate configuration and its effect on flow characteristics using finite element simulation software (COMSOL Multiphysics 5.6). As shown in Fig. 1b, the vertical focusing and lateral focusing processes are separately modeled, where the characteristic dimensions of the microchannel at the end of the narrowed channel and the detection window are 145 μm × 50 μm and 100 μm × 50 μm (width × height), respectively. To analyze the effect of flow rate configuration on the vertical focusing process, we maintained the flow rate of the sample flow QS constant at 100 μL/min and simultaneously increased the flow rate of the vertical sheath QV from 100 μL/min to 1 mL/min. As the computed results shown in Fig. 1c indicate, the profile of the sample flow shrinks as the flow rate ratio QV/QS increases, with the contraction in the height direction being more significant than in the width direction. Subsequently, to understand the effect of the lateral sheath on the lateral focusing process, the flow rates of the sample flow QS and the vertical sheath QV were kept constant at 100 μL/min and 300 μL/min, respectively. We parametrically scanned the flow rate of the lateral sheath QL from 100 μL/min to 1 mL/min. Figure 1d shows the changing profile of the sample flow with the increased flow rate ratio QL/QS, where both the width and height concurrently reduce. As the simulation results are carried out, it is demonstrated that the focusing effect can be enhanced by increasing both flow rate ratios of QV/QS and QL/QS.

Since the Reynolds number (Re) is an essential dimensionless parameter which indicates the relative importance of inertial and viscous effects in a flow. The Re can be calculated by:

Re=2(w×h)/(w+h)Vρ/μ

where w and h are the width and height of microchannle, V is the averaged velocity, ρ and μ severally represent density and dynamic viscosity. While the averaged velocity is given by:

V=Q/(w×h)=(QS+QV+2×QL)/(w×h)

where Q is the overall flow rate within the microchannel. We thus conduct a parameterized investigation to clearly distinguish the effects of Re on the final shape of the focused stream by keeping the flow rate ratio constant at QS:QV:QL = 1:3:3 and shifting the overall flow rate Q from 0.5 mL/min to 5 mL/min. With the Re raising from 27.5 to 275.6, the width of sample flow gradually decreases, and the height hardly changes. Thus, with the raising total flow rates, the Re increases, and the focusing effect correspondingly improves.

The effect of deformation on hydrodynamic focusing

Due to the popularity of soft lithography, PDMS is widely used in the fabrication of hydrodynamic focusing microfluidic devices32–34. However, when fluids are injected at high flow rates, the high pressure caused by the liquid flow can cause elastic deformation in the PDMS channels, which decreases the 3D focusing effect. To reduce the effect of elastic deformation on the microchannel’s focusing, we have chosen plastic PUA as the substrate material for the microfluidic device.

To quantitatively evaluate the effect of deformation on focusing, we construct a fluid-structure coupled numerical model to compare the focusing conditions within PDMS and PUA devices when the Re is set to 275.6. In the simulation, the Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, and density for PDMS are respectively set as 2 MPa, 0.45, and 1000 kg/m³, and for PUA, they are 320 MPa, 0.3, and 1050 kg/m³ 36,37. The flow rate ratio is kept identical at QS:QV:QL = 1:3:3. First, we compare the physical conditions of PDMS and PUA devices with a total flow rate of 5 mL/min, corresponding to an Re of 275.6. As shown in Fig. 2a, the PDMS device exhibits severe deformations with a maximum strain of 130.1 μm, where the PDMS bulk with microchannel structure is bonded to glass. For the PUA device, which consists of PUA bulk with microchannels and a smooth substrate, only tiny deformations are observed under the same flow condition, with the strain being less than 90.1 nm, as shown in Fig. 2b. Next, we calculate the variation of stress and strain as the total flow rate increases from 1 mL/min to 5 mL/min, as shown in Fig. 2c. Due to the pressure-driven expansion of the microchannel boundary in the PDMS device under high throughput conditions, the maximum pressure within the PDMS device is slightly less than that in the PUA device. Nevertheless, the PDMS device exhibits much larger deformation than the PUA device.

The flow field model of (d) PDMS and e PUA device which is reconstructed by using strain information. f The profile of sample flow as the change of flow rate for PDMS device model

Subsequently, we use the strain information to reconstruct the microchannel geometry and analyze the effect of deformation on the flow field. For example, Fig. 2d, e respectively show the flow fields of the PDMS and PUA devices after considering deformation, where the boundary conditions are regenerated based on the models shown in Fig. 2a, b. As illustrated in Fig. 2d, due to the deformation of the channel, the streamlines of the sample flow are squeezed to the top of the channel under the action of the vertical sheath. After the action of the lateral sheath at the junction, the streamlines distribute in the two corners of the upper part at the channel outlet. Thus, the deformation of the PDMS channel caused by fluid pressure under high flow rate conditions results in the loss of lateral focusing ability of the PDMS device. As the flow rate increases, the changes in the sample flow profile are shown in Fig. 2f. As the deformation increases with the increase of flow rate, the sample flow gradually squeezes from the center to the side corner of the upper part. Meanwhile, the area of the sample flow profile also gradually increases, indicating that the focusing effect decreases. As shown in Fig. 2e, since the tiny strain within the PUA device is negligible relative to the characteristic size of the channel (125 µm × 50 µm), it has almost no influence on the flow condition. Thus, the PUA device achieves stable focusing at high velocity and meets the requirements of high-throughput applications.

Fabrication of microfluidic device

As shown in Fig. 3a, the PUA-based microfluidic devices are fabricated through a double transfer process. Because PUA (20–60 mJ/m2)37 has a higher surface energy than PDMS (19–21 mJ/m2)36, the liquid PUA will rapidly wet the PDMS surface. Meanwhile, the tiny adhesion force between PUA and PDMS makes it easy to peel off the photocured PUA bulk from the PDMS surface to achieve a patterned transfer process38. Thus, we first fabricate a PDMS mold for manufacturing the base of microfluidic chips. The PDMS components (Sylgard 184, Dow Corning) are mixed at a weight ratio of 1:10 and poured onto a customized silicon wafer on which a patterned SU-8 photoresist with a height of 50 μm is deposited. The patterned PDMS bulk can be peeled off from the silicon wafer after heating at 80 °C for one hour. As shown in Fig. 3b, the scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of the PDMS bulk illustrate that the surface is smooth and well transferred from the SU-8 mold. Subsequently, the inlets and outlet are constructed by punching at reserved locations with a 1-mm puncher and inserting PDMS cylinders with a diameter of 1 mm and a height of 8 mm. To prevent fluidic PUA from spilling over the PDMS mold when PUA is injected, smooth PDMS walls are pasted around the patterned PDMS bulk. Meanwhile, to eliminate the gaps between the cylinder and bulk, a small amount of PDMS mixture is employed to fill the gaps in the PDMS mold, and the whole mold is re-cured by heating at 80 °C for 30 min.

The fabrication of PUA based microfluidic device using double transfer process.

a The workflow of the double transferring process. b, c The SEM images of patterned surfaces of PDMS and PUA. d A snapshot of fabricated microfluidic device (scalebar of 1 cm), the insert displays the microscopic image of detection window (scalebar of 200 μm). e The microscopic image of detection window

Then, we fabricate the PUA device, 0.5-mL of liquid PUA (H807 Jingcheng Co., China) is injected into the PDMS mold using a glue dispenser (YCL-983 Yichuanlong Co., China), in which the selection of PUA materials should take into account the matching of refractive index and the light transmittance, and the comparison process of specific PUA models can be found in Table S1 and Fig. S1. After self-leveling, a standard coverslip with a thickness of 130 μm is cut to 8 × 8 mm and installed at the detection window, and the whole tier is treated with UV radiation for 10 s using a 72-W UV light source (HC35 Jingcheng Co., China). Subsequently, the PUA bulk is peeled off from the PDMS mold after cooling to room temperature (25 °C in a super-clean laboratory). To construct the PUA substrate, 0.1-mL of liquid PUA is uniformly dripped onto a standard slide, and a PDMS bulk with a smooth surface is stamped on it to ensure the level of the PUA surface. After treating with UV photocuring for 10 s and cooling to room temperature, the PDMS bulk is peeled off from the PUA substrate. For bonding the PUA base to the substrate, the PUA base is placed on the substrate with accurate alignment, subjected to uniform pressure, and treated with UV radiation for 15 s. Finally, five stainless steel capillaries are respectively installed at the fluidic inlets and outlet and sealed with resin to prevent leakage, as shown in Fig. 3d. In the proposed workflow, due to the reusability of PDMS mold, the fabricating time for a single microfluidic device can be compressed to within 5 minutes. Compared to the soft lithography method, the manufacturing speed has increased by an order of magnitude, providing an efficient and feasible solution for large-scale applications32–34.

Results and discussion

Hydrodynamic focusing visualization experiment

To investigate the condition of sample flow and evaluate the 3D hydrodynamic focusing effect, we employ red ink to make sample flow visualized, and 4 syringe pumps (Harvard, 11Elite) are severally connected to 4 inlets to control the flow rate configuration. To demonstrate the vertical focusing process, we keep the flow rate of red ink QS at 100 μL/min and increase the flow rate of vertical sheath QV from 100 μL/min to 500 μL/min with a step of 50 μL/min. For each flow rate configuration, we extract the concentration profile before the interaction with lateral sheath at profile a (denoted in Fig. 3e) from 10 gray-scale microscopic images and splice them in Fig. 4a. As shown in Fig. 4d, the averaged intensity profile of the sample flow simultaneously shrinks in lateral and vertical dimensions with the flow rate ratio Qv/ Qs increased. The insert of Fig. 4d statistically analyzes the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the concentration profile under each flow rate configuration. It can be found that with the increase of the flow rate ratio Qv/ Qs, the FWHM also increases, which is consistent with the simulation results in Fig. 1d. However, compared with the simulation results, the intensity curve fluctuates with an asymmetrical variation tendency. On the one hand, the surface of the microchannel may be scratched during the installation of the stainless steel capillaries, and the rough surface may cause disturbance to the sample flow. On the other hand, the manual drilling process may result in an asymmetrical position of the sample inlet within the microchannel, thus making the sample flow imbalanced.

Subsequently, we investigate the effect of lateral sheath on the lateral focusing process. In this experiment, the flow rate of the sample QS and vertical sheath QV is kept identical at 100 μL/min and 300 μL/min, respectively, and the flow rate of lateral sheath QV is increased from 100 μL/min to 500 μL/min with a step of 50 μL/min. The concentration profile of red ink at the detection window (profile b denoted in Fig. 3e) under each flow rate configuration is spliced in Fig. 4b. The averaged intensity profiles are plotted in Fig. 4e, and the insert shows the variation of FWHM, which indicates that the profile shrinks in both the vertical and lateral directions with the flow rate ratio QL/ QS increased. Thus, by increasing both flow rate ratio QV/ QS and QL/ QS, the sample flow profile can be shrunk to achieve the better flow focusing effect.

Finally, we also demonstrate the effect of Re on the hydrodynamic focusing under a constant flow rate ratio, which is chosen as QS:QV:QL = 1:3:3 to balance between throughput and sheath consumption. By increasing the overall flow rate Q from 1 mL/min to 5 mL/min with a step of 0.5 mL/min, the Re can be raised from 55.1 to 275.6, and the profile of sample flow at detection window is slightly shrunk, as shown in Fig. 4c. In Fig. 4f, the averaged intensity profile slightly shrinks with the Re increased from 55.1 to 271.5, and the averaged FWHM raise from 19.2 to 21.5 μm. Therefore, the focusing effect slightly increases as the total flow rate increases, which provides higher accuracy and efficiency for high-throughput 3D hydrodynamic focusing applications, helping to improve optical imaging cytometry performance under high-throughput conditions.

High-throughput OTS microscopy imaging analysis

To evaluate the focusing performance of the microfluidic device in practice, we employ OTS microscopy, which achieves ultra-high-speed image acquisition and is suitable for real-time, high-throughput imaging analysis of fast dynamic processes39–42. The system setup of OTS microscopy is portrayed in Fig. 5a, where a Ti:Sapphire femtosecond pulse laser (Coherent Vitara-T-HP) with a repetition rate of 80 MHz, a center wavelength of 800 nm, and a bandwidth of 40 nm is chosen as the optical source. The ultrashort fs pulse is then stretched to about 7 ns by a 1.55 km single-mode fiber (SMF) in the time domain. After that, the time-stretched pulse is spatially dispersed by a diffraction grating (Thorlabs GR25-1208) with a groove density of 1200 lines/mm to form a 1D rainbow pulse. Next, the 1D rainbow pulse passes through the objective lens (Olympus LCPLN-IR 50×, NA = 0.65) to scan the cells in the microfluidic device, and the pulse containing cell information is collected and recombined by another objective lens and diffraction grating. Finally, the pulse signal is translated to the electrical signal by a 12.5 GHz photodetector (Newport 1544-B-50) and digitized by an oscilloscope with a bandwidth of 13 GHz and a sampling rate of 40 GSa/s (Keysight DSA91304A). With the cell moving at high speed in the microfluidic channel, a 2D image of the cell can be obtained by digitally stacking the pulse signals. The OTS microscopy imaging established in this study is identical to that in our previous work, featuring high spatial resolution superior to 780 nm and a focal depth of ±0.95 μm, whose details are shown in Fig. S2 in the supplementary information.

Then, to demonstrate the universality of our device with clinical samples practically, urine samples from bladder cancer patients are chosen, containing cells of various shapes and sizes such as red blood cells, leukocytes, and epithelium. It should be noted that this study has been approved by the Medical Ethics Committee, Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University, and is conducted in strict accordance with ethical guidelines. Before detection, the urine sample is treated with lysing and rinsing, and the cell concentration of the treated sample is counted with a cell counting chamber at 7 × 107 cells/mL. Subsequently, we raise the total flow rate Q from 1 mL/min to 5 mL/min with a flow rate ratio of QS:QV:QL = 1:3:3, the corresponding average velocity raising from 3.3 m/s to 16.7 m/s, and OTS microscopy is employed to capture cell images. Due to the throughput being determined by both cell concentration and sample flow rate, ranging from 116,667 to 583,333 cells/s in this experiment. And the throughput is usually greater than 100,000 cells/s reported by previous studies on high-throughput microfluidic cytometry. Therefore, the 3D focusing under high-throughput operating conditions is demonstrated with OTS microscopy, which theoretically supports a maximum throughput of 1,000,000 cells/s. However, due to the tiny focal range of OTS microscopy, imaging of cells is very sensitive to the distance from the out-of-focus plane in the microchannel. As shown in Fig. 5b, c, we classify the cells according to whether they are located in the focal plane in the OTS microscopic images. For each flow rate configuration, 100 images were collected as a group, measurements are repeated 5 times, and the vertical focusing efficiency was obtained by calculating the ratio of the total number of focused images to the total number of captured images. As shown in Fig. 5d, the focusing efficiency increases with the raising velocity reaching a maximum of 98.4% at 16.7 m/s, and the maximum variance in all measurements was less than 1.2%.

Afterward, the characteristic dimension of defocused cells at different flow rates is statistically analyzed, as shown in Fig. 5e. Since a part of cells is not a regular spherical shape, here d = (h + w)/2 is employed to define the characteristic dimension of cells, as denoted in the insert of Fig. 5b. In each flow rate configuration, the characteristic dimension of cells smaller than 10 μm dominates in the defocused cells, and characteristic dimension larger than 20 μm is rarely absent from the focal plane. This is due to the fact that the height of the sample flow profile formed by hydraulic focusing is slightly greater than 10 μm. Considering that the volume of cells is different from that of particles, the actual motion range of cells may exceed the sample flow profile, resulting in defocusing. On the other hand, small-sized cells are more likely to overlap with other cells in the height direction leading to being pushed out of the focal plane. In order to exclude the interference caused by the proportion of cells in the sample to the experimental results, the characteristic dimension for all the cells in each group of measurements is counted, as shown in Fig. 5e. Therein, the average proportion of the three characteristic dimension is severally 21.4%, 28.7%, and 49.9%. Therefore, it validates that our device has good focusing ability for cells with different sizes, especially for cells with a characteristic dimension bigger than 20 μm. Meanwhile, it is also demonstrated that the focusing capability of our device is not sensitive to cell shape and can achieve efficient focusing for cells of various shapes. Besides, it should be pointed out that 16.7 m/s is not the limit for both OTS microscopy and the proposed microfluidic device. Due to the pressure generated by the liquid injection at high flow rate, the injection will be severely deformed, which will cause the actual flow rate to be less than the set value of the injection pump. At the same time, the injection pump will stop working due to overload. Although, in the experiment we verified at a maximum safe velocity of 16.7 m/s, the maximum flow rate supported by the microfluidic device can be higher, which can be achieved by using a high-pressure pump for applying higher pressure.

In addition to vertical focusing accuracy, lateral focusing is also significant for accurate single-cell manipulation and detection. We also evaluate the lateral focusing efficiency using OTS microscopy images. Firstly, Fig. 6a shows the situation in which two or more cells are recorded in one continuous collection. When the cells pass through the detection window, the OTS system starts recording, capturing the movement of the cells in 38.4 to 19.8 μs at an averaged velocity of 3.3 to 16.7 m/s. In these representative cases, different kinds of cells with different sizes and shapes flow close to the center of the microchannel, which proves the lateral focusing capability of the microfluidic device. To quantitatively evaluate lateral focusing efficiency, we align and mosaic 100 continuously captured images at different flow configurations and statistically analyze the lateral distribution of the center of cells. As shown in Fig. 6b, the lateral distance is obtained by measuring the distance from the cell center to the boundary of the field of view, where the field of view is calculated as 78.6 μm. Figure 6c shows the lateral distance of the cells changing with increasing flow rate, where the averaged lateral distance is close to 39.3 μm at the center of the microchannel. Meanwhile, the standard deviation of lateral distance decreases with increased velocity, indicating that lateral focusing efficiency increases with increased velocity.

a Two or more cells are recorded in one continuous imaging under different flow rate configuration. b The aligning and mosaicking of continuously captured images at different flow rate. c Effect of flow rate configuration on cell distribution during lateral focusing, the center line indicates averaged lateral distance, the color box represents the average deviation from the center line and the error bar represents stander deviation. d A statistically study of lateral displacement for cells at different velocity, and the error bars indicate stander deviation. e The lateral distribution of cells with different characteristic dimension at different velocity, and the boxes and whiskers severally denote stander deviation and extremum

Subsequently, to quantitatively analyze the effect of increased flow rate on lateral focusing efficiency, we ideally suppose all cells should flow along the central axis of the channel and thus calculate the distance between the cell center and the axis, as shown in Fig. 6d. As the averaged velocity increases from 3.3 to 16.7 m/s, the averaged lateral displacement decreases from 4.61 to 2.51 μm, and the standard deviation simultaneously decreases from 5.87 to 3.76 μm. The lateral focusing efficiency is defined as the ratio of the lateral displacement to the half-width of the microchannel; in this case, it increases from 90.8 to 95.0%, indicating improvements in lateral focusing. Finally, to explore the effect of the size of the cell on lateral focusing, we statistically analyse the lateral distribution of cells with different characteristic dimensions, as shown in Fig. 6e. The cells with a smaller characteristic dimension are more dispersed in the lateral direction, while the bigger cells are more concentrated. This may be due to, during the hydrodynamic focusing process, the center of the smaller cell has a larger range of movement within the profile of the sample flow. Consequently, the center of a relatively larger cell is constrained in less space and will experience a shorter displacement. Meanwhile, as the flow rate increases, the lateral displacement for all the cells with different characteristic dimensions decreases, which is consistent with the simulation results in Fig. 1e. Therefore, the proposed microfluidic device can achieve efficient focusing both in the vertical and lateral directions at high-throughput operating conditions.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we propose a 3D hydrodynamic focusing microfluidic device with only a single-layer construction, relieving the difficulties in production and deployment. Finite element simulations demonstrate that by introducing vertical sheath and lateral sheath perpendicular to the single-layer microchannel structure, the constraints of sample flow in the vertical and lateral directions can be enhanced. Subsequently, fluid-structure couple numerical models are constructed to evaluate the diminishing of focusing effect induced by the elastic deformation of the microchannel. Compared with the PDMS device, where the focusing effect decreases due to serious deformation, the PUA device has good deformation resistance to ensure stable focusing at high velocity. Then a double transfer process is employed for rapidly fabricating the PUA microfluidic device, in which the manufacturing speed can be improved by an order of magnitude compared with the soft lithography process, paving the way for large-scale deployment. To demonstrate the performance of the fabricated microfluidic device, hydrodynamic focusing visualization is performed with red ink, and the experimental results indicate that the focusing effect can be improved by adjusting the flow rate configuration of the sample, vertical sheath, and lateral sheath. Finally, to evaluate the focusing performance in practice, OTS microscopy is employed to evaluate the focusing performance of the microfluidic device by imaging the clinical urine sample. As the experimental results show, the focusing efficiency gradually increases in the vertical and lateral directions with the increase in flow rate, reaching 98.4% and 95.0%, respectively, at 16.7 m/s, demonstrating reliability and precision, particularly in high-throughput conditions. Therefore, the proposed single-layer 3D hydrodynamic focusing microfluidic device not only addresses manufacturing challenges but also demonstrates exceptional performance, making it a promising tool for advancing high-throughput cytometry applications in research, diagnostics, and beyond.
