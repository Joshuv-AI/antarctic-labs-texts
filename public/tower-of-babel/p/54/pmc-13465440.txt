SARS-CoV-2 mRNA XBB1.5 vaccine immunogenicity in kidney transplant recipients
Authors: Mathieu Surénaud, Aurélie Wiedemann, Hakim Hocini, Emile Foucat, Cécile Lefebvre, Tugba Agyar, Emma Jousseaume, Pascaline Tisserand, Céline Pellaton, Millicent Omondi, Wendy Burgers, Giuseppe Pantaleo, Antoine Durrbach, Yves Lévy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the reduced clinical severity of Omicron SARS-CoV-2 variants compared to earlier lineages, kidney transplant recipients (KTR) continue to experience higher SARS-CoV-2 mortality rates than the general population. Seroconversion rates following SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccination remain lower in KTR after three vaccine injections.MethodsWe evaluated anti-SARS-CoV-2 neutralizing antibody activity and cellular responses, as well as whole blood gene expression profiles after XBB.1.5 mRNA vaccination boost in a cohort of KTR.ResultsWe demonstrated that XBB.1.5 mRNA boosting increased both the magnitude and frequency of neutralizing antibody responses against different SARS-CoV-2 variants and found that the neutralizing activity against Wuhan strain or XBB.1.5 subvariant was driven by different anti-Spike binding IgG subclasses. We also demonstrated that Spike-specific CD4⁺ T-cell responses were significantly boosted against Wuhan strain and XBB.1.5 subvariant one month following XBB.1.5 mRNA vaccination. Lastly, we showed that XBB.1.5 boost induced overexpression of genes associated with type I IFN and innate immune responses at day 1, but also activated T and NK-cells at day 3 that persist at day 7 post-vaccination.ConclusionsGlobally, increasing the number of vaccine injections using updated XBB.1.5 mRNA vaccine enhance anti-SARS-CoV-2 immune responses in KTR. However, the monovalent XBB.1.5 mRNA vaccine boost elicited recall of cross-reacting immune responses against the original Wuhan Spike without a clear advantage against Omicron subvariants, consistent with a predominant immunological imprinting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12985-026-03214-1.Keywords: Kidney transplant recipients, XBB.1.5 mRNA vaccination, Anti-SARS-CoV-2 immune responses, Whole blood gene expression profiles

Introduction

Kidney transplant recipients (KTR) are highly susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 infection and are among those most at risk of developing severe COVID-19, long-term complications, or fatal disease [1, 2]. Like other solid organ transplant recipients (SOTR), KTR have lower neutralizing antibody titers than healthy controls against B.1.617.2 (Delta) and B.1.1.529 (Omicron BA.1) after SARS-CoV-2 vaccination [3] and multiple vaccinations are required to increase serological responses [4]. We, and others, have shown variable impact of immune-suppressive therapies in responses to vaccination in KTR [5–7]. Belatacept and mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), a purine synthase inhibitor, severely weaken both humoral and cellular vaccine responses in KTR [8, 9] in contrast to treatment with mTOR inhibitors [10–12]. Despite herd immunity and vaccination recommendations in fragile and immune-suppressed populations, the mortality and morbidity remain still higher in KTR than in the general population in the era of Omicron variants [13]. The emergence of Omicron XBB subvariants has dramatically reduced the efficacy of both SARS-CoV-2 wild-type monovalent and bivalent (wild-type + Omicron BA.5) mRNA vaccines in the general population [14], leading to the development of a new vaccine booster based on spike protein sequence from the XBB.1.5 circulating variant in September 2023. Administration of the updated monovalent XBB.1.5 mRNA vaccine booster to healthy individuals was safe and elicits robust neutralizing antibodies against ancestral (Wuhan) and XBB.1.5 spike proteins but also the more recent BA.2.86 and JN.1 variants [15, 16]. Herein, we evaluated longitudinally humoral and cellular responses after a booster XBB.1.5 vaccination and performed a gene expression analysis in a cohort of highly pre-vaccinated KTR treated with calcineurin inhibitors (CNI).

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

In November 2023, we enrolled 14 kidney transplant recipients (KTR) treated with calcineurin inhibitors from the VACOTRARE longitudinal, single-center cohort [8] to assess the durability of immunity following XBB1.5 mRNA vaccination. Each patient received a single dose of the monovalent Pfizer BioNtech XBB.1.5 vaccine. Inclusion criteria was being > 18 years old and exclusion criteria were pregnancy and nursing. Blood samples were collected at baseline (D0), and at one month (M1), three months (M3), and six months (M6) post-vaccination for peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) and serum isolation. Additional samples for transcriptomic analysis were collected on days 0 (n = 12), 1 (n = 12), 3 (n = 11), and 7 (n = 10).

Determination of anti-nucleocapsid IgG levels

The levels of binding IgG antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid (Nuc) were determined using Luminex-based assays (Luminex Corporation), as previously described [17]. MagPlex beads (Luminex Corporation) were covalently coupled with the SARS-CoV-2 NuC protein. Beads were then incubated 1 h with a 1/300 serum dilution in PBS or a negative control of pre-pandemic pool of human AB serum (BioWest, VWR). After washing twice with PBS-Tween 0.05%, we added anti-human IgG-PE (phycoerythrin) secondary antibody (One Lambda, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to each well and incubated the plate for 45 min. The plate was then washed twice and reading buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added before reading the plate with a Luminex 200 analyzer (Luminex Corporation, BioRad). The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) signal of each serum was divided by the mean negative control MFI, to obtain a ratio. A sample was considered as positive if the ratio was ≥ 6.

Determination of neutralizing activity against spike protein mutations associated with VOCs

The levels of neutralizing IgG antibodies against different SARS-CoV-2 Spike proteins were determined using Luminex-based assays as an ACE2 surrogate neutralization assay, as previously described [18]. Different types of MagPlex beads were covalently coupled with different spike trimer variants (2019nCoV = Wuhan original strain; Delta; Omicron BA.1, BA.2, BA.4, BQ.1, XBB, XBB.1.5, GK.1, EG.5.1, BA.2.86). Beads were then incubated 1 h with serum samples at different dilutions (1:10; 1:30; 1:90; 1:270; 1:810; 1:2,430) in PBS. A pool of pre-COVID-19 pandemic healthy human sera (BioWest Human serum AB males, VWR) was used as negative control. The positive control was a recombinant anti-Spike neutralizing antibody (CHUV, Lausanne). We then added ACE2 mouse Fc fusion protein (EPFL, Lausanne) to the wells at a final concentration of 1 µg/ml and incubated the plates for 1 h. The plates were washed twice with PBS-Tween 0.05% and anti-mouse IgG-PE secondary antibody (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to each well and the plates were incubated for 45 min. The plates were then washed twice and reading buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added before reading the plate with a Luminex 200 analyzer (Luminex Corporation, BioRad). The negative control MFI was considered to correspond to 100% binding of the ACE2 receptor to the bead-coupled spike trimer. The MFI of the well containing the highest concentration (> 1 µg/ml) of commercial anti-spike blocking antibody was considered to represent maximum inhibition. The percent inhibition of the spike trimer protein-ACE2 interaction was calculated as follows: % inhibition = (1 – ([MFI test dilution – MFI max inhibition]/[MFI max binding – MFI max inhibition]) × 100).

Determination of anti-spike immunoglobulin isotype levels

The levels of binding IgG, IgA and IgM antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 Spike (S) were determined using Luminex-based assays (Luminex Corporation). MagPlex beads (Luminex Corporation) were covalently coupled with the SARS-CoV-2 Wuhan Spike trimer protein. Beads were then incubated 1 h with a 1/10 000 serum dilution for IgG, 1/1000 dilution for IgA and 1/100 serum dilution for IgM. After washing twice with PBS-Tween 0.05%, we added goat anti-human IgG-PE (One Lambda, Thermo Fisher Scientific), goat anti-human IgA-PE (Southern Biotech) or goat Fab’2 anti-human IgM (Southern Biotech) secondary antibodies to wells and incubated the plate for 45 min. The plate was then washed twice and reading buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added before reading the plate with a Luminex 200 analyzer (Luminex Corporation, BioRad). Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) signals were analyzed.

Determination of anti-Spike IgG subclass levels

The levels of binding IgG1, IgG2, IgG3 and IgG4 antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 Spike (S) were determined using Luminex-based assays (Luminex Corporation). MagPlex beads (Luminex Corporation) were covalently coupled with the SARS-CoV-2 Wuhan Spike trimer protein. Beads were then incubated 1 h with a 1/1000 serum dilution. After washing twice with PBS-Tween 0.05%, we added mouse anti-human IgG1-PE, anti-human IgG2-PE, anti-human IgG3-PE or anti-human IgG4-PE (Southern Biotech) secondary antibodies to wells and incubated the plate for 45 min. The plate was then washed twice and reading buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added before reading the plate with a Luminex 200 analyzer (Luminex Corporation, BioRad). Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) signals were analyzed.

Characterization of Spike-specific T-cell responses

PBMCs were stimulated by overnight incubation with two pools of 15-mer peptides, overlapping by 10 amino acids, spanning the full spike protein of the SARS-CoV-2 D614G strain (253 peptides) and the XBB.1.5 strain (253 peptides). Additionally, two mini-pools targeting the mutated regions of XBB.1.5 were used. The first mini-pool contained 72 peptides representing the XBB.1.5 mutations, while the second mini-pool included the corresponding 72 wild-type peptides without mutations (Peptides&Elephants, Germany). Unstimulated cells were used as negative controls, and Staphylococcus enterotoxin B (SEB) was included as a positive control to validate assay sensitivity. Antigen-specific responses were background-subtracted using unstimulated conditions. Cell functionality was assessed by intracellular cytokine staining with Boolean gating. Total cytokine-positive T cells were defined as the sum of all IFN-γ, IL-2 and/or TNF-expressing Boolean combinations after background subtraction. The flow cytometry panel included a viability marker, CD3, CD4, and CD8 for T-cell lineage determination, and antibodies against IFN-γ, IL-2 and TNF. A minimum of 250 000 live CD3 cells were acquired on a LSRII Fortessa 4-laser (488, 640, 561 and 405 nm) cytometer (BD Biosciences), and analyzed with FlowJo software version 9.9.6 (Tree Star Inc.). Gating strategy was presented as Supplementary Fig. 1.

RNA extraction, library preparation and sequencing

Gene expression profiles were measured in whole blood collected via cotton swab in Tempus buffer at baseline (D0), D1, D3, and D7. Total RNA was purified using the Tempus™ Spin RNA Isolation Kit (Invitrogen), and globin mRNAs were depleted using the GLOBINclear-Human Kit (Ambion). RNAs were quantified with the Quant-iT RiboGreen RNA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and quality-controlled on a Bioanalyzer (Agilent). mRNA libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Single Cell/Low Input RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina), specifically chosen for its high performance with low-volume samples. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq2000 system.

Bioinformatics and Statistical Analysis Quality control were performed via MultiQC v1.13. Transcript reads were aligned and quantified relative to the GENCODE Human genome Release 47 using Salmon v1.10.244. Raw transcripts were imported using tximport, which accounts for transcript length changes across samples and provides accurate gene-level counts. Normalization was performed using the median-of-ratios method from the DESeq2 package (v1.44.0). To account for the relationship between the variance and the mean inherent in low-input RNA-seq (50 µL of blood), a Variance Stabilizing Transformation (VST) was applied for all exploratory data analyses (e.g., principal component analysis (PCA)). To minimize technical variability, all samples were processed in a single library preparation and sequencing batch, thus precluding technical batch effects.

Pathways induced after vaccination (D1, D3, D7) compared to baseline (D0) were analyzed using Blood Transcription Modules (BTM) [19]. Normalized Enrichment Scores (NES) and False Discovery Rate (FDR) were calculated, with significance set at FDR ≤ 0.05. PCA was performed using FactoMineR v2.11, and heatmaps were generated with the pheatmap package (v1.0.12).

Statistical analysis

Graphpad Prism software version 10 was used for non-linear four-parameter curve-fitting analysis, and for non-parametric statistics and plots, as described in the figure legends. Statistical significance between matched groups was evaluated using nonparametric Friedman test and Dunn’s post test. The nonparametric Spearman’s coefficient was used for correlation analysis. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics

Fourteen KTR treated with CNI were enrolled to receive a single dose of the monovalent Pfizer BioNtech XBB.1.5 vaccine. Demographics, clinical and treatment characteristics are presented in Table 1. Globally, mean age ± SD was 56 ± 11 years, and 43% of the patients were female. All patients were treated with CNI (tacrolimus or cyclosporin). mTOR inhibitor or purine synthase inhibitor (MMF or Imurel) was given to six and eight patients, respectively (Table 1). Mean ± SD graft duration at XBB.1.5 vaccination was 54 ± 26 months. The type of nephropathy, transplant rank and renal function (eGFR) are also presented in Table 1. All patients have previously received three to four COVID-19 vaccinations in accordance with national guidelines. The time line from the last vaccination and the mRNA XBB1.5 boost was 25 months in median. Between month 1 and month 3 after mRNA XBB1.5 boost, two patients experienced a breakthrough infection as determined by anti-Nucleocapsid binding antibody levels (Supplementary Fig. 2). These two patients were excluded from the longitudinal analysis.

XBB.1.5 mRNA boosting increases the magnitude and frequency of neutralizing antibody responders to SARS-CoV-2 variants

One month following mRNA XBB.1.5 boost, a fold increase (median) of baseline neutralizing antibody (NAb) titers was observed against original Original Wuhan (2.8) Delta (2.7), BA.1 (4.1), BA.2 (4.9), BA.4 (5.1), BQ.1 (7.0), XBB (9.9), XBB.1.5 (10.5), GK.1 (14.0), EG.5.1 (11.7), and BA.2.86 (8.2) variants (Fig. 1A). At month 1, mRNA XBB1.5 boosted more nNAb titers against XBB1.5 variant (105 to 1126 IC50) than original Wuhan (470 to 1418 IC50) (Fig. 1B). Median serum NAb titer values (IC50) remained globally stable at 3 months after vaccination for Original Wuhan Strain (1356.5) and all variants tested: Delta (989.5), BA.1 (1451), BA.2 (1454), BA.4 (2118.5), BQ.1 (1058.5), XBB (1033), XBB.1.5 (1019), GK.1 (525.5), EG.5.1 (827.5), and BA.2.86 (983.5) (Fig. 1C). A slight decrease of the magnitude of the responses to all variants was observed 6 months following vaccination (21–39%) as compared to month 3 (Fig. 1C).

The frequency of antibody neutralizers, defined as IC50 > 50 dilutions, against the majority of variants was high at baseline (for example 83% of patients against BA.2) but less than 35% against GK.1 variant. One month after XBB.1.5 mRNA boosting, the rate of responders was above 80% against all the variants but GK.1 (Fig. 1D). Three months post-vaccination the rate of responders against all the variants remained above 80% while a slight decrease for Delta variant was noted at 6 months (Fig. 1D). Of note, 2/12 of the patients were unable to induce NAbs against any of the variants after XBB.1.5 mRNA boosting. These results showed that XBB.1.5 mRNA boosting increased the magnitude of serum NAb titers and the frequency of responders to circulating variants including Omicron variants and that these responses were sustained up to 6 months post-boost for most of the KTR patients included in our study.

As an exploratory analysis, we also look at potential correlations between one month post-vaccination neutralizing antibody responses against XBB.1.5 Spike and relevant clinical variables, such as patient age, time since transplantation, and markers of renal function. This later analysis showed a negative correlation between neutralizing activity against XBB.1.5 variant at M1 and patient age (r = -0.5803; P = 0.0323), a positive correlation between neutralizing activity against XBB.1.5 variant at M1 and time since transplantation (r = 0.6027; P = 0.0255), and no correlation between neutralizing activity against XBB.1.5 variant observed at M1 and markers of renal function (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B, C).

Anti-Spike Ig isotype and IgG subclass binding antibodies are associated with anti-SARS-CoV-2 neutralizing activity after XBB1.5 mRNA boosting

Next, we showed a variable correlation between the magnitude of isotype binding antibodies and neutralizing activities against original Wuhan and XBB.1.5 variant one month following mRNA XBB.1.5 boosting. Anti-Spike (Wuhan) binding IgG, but also to a lesser extend IgA and IgM, were associated with the neutralizing activity against Wuhan strain (Fig. 2A, B, C) and against XBB.1.5 variant (Fig. 2D, E, F). We then quantified the levels of anti-Spike (Wuhan) binding IgG subclasses (i.e., IgG1, IgG2, IgG3 and IgG4) and observed a majority of IgG1 but also a substantial frequency of IgG4 (Supplementary Fig. 4). More specifically, we found that the neutralizing activity against Wuhan strain was driven by anti-Spike (Wuhan) binding IgG from IgG2, IgG1 and IgG4 but not IgG3 subclasses (Fig. 3A, B, C, D). By contrast, the association of anti-Spike (Wuhan) binding IgG with the neutralizing activity against XBB.1.5 variant was driven only by IgG3 subclass (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Spike-specific T cell responses following XBB.1.5 mRNA vaccination are cross-reactives

We evaluated spike-specific T cell responses one month (M1) and three months (M3) following XBB.1.5 mRNA vaccination. To assess cross-reactivity, two peptide pools spanning the full spike protein of the original (Wuhan) strain and the XBB.1.5 variant were tested. At baseline, detectable spike (S)-specific CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells targeting both Wuhan and XBB.1.5 strains were observed in KTR patients. The median (IQR) frequencies of CD4 + T cells specific to Wuhan and XBB.1.5 were 0.41% (0.2–0.6) and 0.39% (0.2–0.6), respectively. Frequencies of CD8⁺ T cells were 0.05% (0.02–0.2) and 0.04% (0–0.2) for Wuhan and XBB.1.5, respectively (Fig. 4A, B). Vaccination with XBB.1.5 mRNA induced a significant increase in Wuhan-specific and XBB.1.5-specific CD4⁺ T cells at M1 (P = 0.01 and P = 0.002 for Wuhan and XBB.1.5, respectively) and declined to baseline frequencies at M3 post-vaccination. Notably, no significant increase in S-specific CD8⁺ T cell responses were observed following XBB.1.5 mRNA vaccination (Fig. 4B). To further investigate the immune response targeting mutated regions of the XBB.1.5 variant, we used mini-pools of peptides encompassing only these mutations. This strategy aimed to determine whether immune responses were differentially directed toward mutated versus wild-type regions. Before vaccination, specific CD4⁺ T cells were detectable and were 0.09% (0.05–0.17) and 0.11% (0.02–0.2) for Wuhan and XBB.1.5, respectively. After vaccination, CD4 + T cell responses against Wuhan and XBB.1.5 were similar (0.18% at M1 and 0.11% at M3) (Fig. 4C). Globally, no significant differences were observed in the magnitude of S-specific CD4 and CD8 T cell responses elicited against the Wuhan and XBB.1.5 strains following XBB.1.5 mRNA vaccination (Supplementary Fig. 6).

XBB.1.5 mRNA booster induces changes in gene expression related to both innate and adaptive immune responses

We then investigated the whole blood gene expression profiles induced by XBB.1.5 mRNA at D1, D3 and D7 after vaccination.

As compared to baseline (D0), we noted at D1 an increased abundance of genes from antiviral interferon responses, activated dendritic cells, innate antiviral response, and viral sensing and immunity, specifically the IRF targets network (Fig. 5A). In more details, we observed upregulation of several genes involved in the regulation and induction of the IFN response, including IFIT1 (Interferon-Induced Protein with Tetratricopeptide Repeats 1), OAS1 (2’-5’ Oligoadenylate Synthetase 1), OAS3 (2’-5’ Oligoadenylate Synthetase 3), RSAD2 (Radical S-Adenosyl Methionine Domain Containing 2), HERC5 (HECT and RLD Domain Containing E3 Ubiquitin Protein Ligase 5), IRF7 (Interferon regulatory factor 7), and STAT1 (Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 1) (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, mRNA vaccine induced overexpression of CXCL10, CCL8, CXCL16 and IL1 chemokines, that are key cytokines in antiviral, monocyte and lymphocyte responses. (Fig. 5B).

On days 3 and 7 post-vaccination, pathways enriched in NK cells and T cell activation were increased (Fig. 5A) such as markers associated with CD8 T cell activation, including CD8A, CD8B, and T cell receptor components (CD3D, CD3G, CD3E, CD247) (Fig. 5C). This activation is accompanied by an increase in the expression of the CXCR3 gene, primarily found on effector T cells and involved in Th1-type immune responses. Furthermore, we observed overexpression of other genes related to T cell activation, such as TCR components (TRBC1, TRAC, TRAV20), signaling molecules (LAG3, ITK, LAT, LEF1, MAL), and lymphocyte development regulators (EOMES, GATA3, LCK, PRKCQ, PTPN22, RASGRP1, RHOH, SH2D2A, TBX21, THEMIS, TXK). This lymphocyte activation is correlated with an increase in the levels of IFNG and IL32 cytokines, as well as IL21RI and L2RG receptors. Besides, the vaccine induced a strong cytotoxic and NK lymphocyte signature, with several upregulated genes such as KLRD1, KLRK1, GNLY (granulysin), NKG7, GEMA, GZMB, GZMH, GZMK and GZMM.

Discussion

In this study, using a Luminex-based assays as an ACE2 surrogate neutralization assay previously shown to match the performance of the gold standard live virus infection assay [18], we demonstrated that XBB.1.5 mRNA boosting increased the magnitude and frequency of NAb responses against the majority of SARS-CoV-2 variants in KTR, and that these responses were sustained up to 6 months post-boost for most of the patients. These results extended previous studies from our group [8] and others [4, 20] by providing insights about the responses to the mRNA XBB1.5 vaccine in this population of patients. Indeed, in our previous study, about 50–60% of KTR treated with CNI had NAb activity against Wuhan, Alpha, Beta, Gamma or Delta variants and about 30% against Omicron after 2 or 3 mRNA vaccine injections [8]. We confirmed here that the frequency of vaccine responders increased following repeated boosts with a vaccine adapted to omicron variants showing that more than 80% of KTR patients are responders. NAb responses to monovalent XBB1.5 mRNA boost are broad against Wuhan and all tested variants, including Omicron XBB, XBB.1.5, GK.1, and EG.5.1 subvariants. However, XBB.1.5 booster elicited also significant neutralizing activity against Wuhan reflecting immunological imprinting [21] related to prior vaccinations with the wild-type monovalent vaccine and the BA.5 bivalent vaccine [22, 23]. Two patients had a history of graft rejection occurring more than 20 months prior to inclusion, both treated with intensified immunosuppressive regimens at the time. Although maintenance therapy was not modified thereafter, a potential long-term impact of such events on immune responsiveness cannot be excluded. Finally, we observed a positive association between time since transplantation and neutralizing antibody responses one month after vaccination. However, given the very small sample size and the exploratory nature of this analysis, this observation should be interpreted with caution and requires confirmation in larger cohorts.

Following XBB.1.5 booster vaccination, IgG1 was the predominant subclass observed in KTR responders, alongside a substantial proportion of IgG4. This isotype profile, detected after 4–6 mRNA vaccine doses, is consistent with the IgG4 class-switch reported after repeated SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccination in both healthy individuals [24] and KTR [25], but not with other vaccine platforms [26]. This shift toward IgG4 has been proposed to reflect prolonged B-cell maturation and ongoing class-switch recombination following BNT162b2 vaccination [24]. Although IgG4 is generally considered to have anti-inflammatory properties and a limited capacity to mediate Fc-dependent effector functions [27], its functional role in antiviral immunity remains to be clarified. In line with previous findings by Juarez et al. [25], we observed an association between anti-Spike IgG4 levels and neutralizing activity. Notably, we also identified a correlation between anti-Spike (Wuhan) binding IgG and neutralization of the XBB.1.5 variant that appeared to be primarily driven by the IgG3 subclass, potentially reflecting its higher binding capacity [28].

Interestingly, despite treatment with calcineurin inhibitors, KTR patients exhibited S-specific functional CD4 + T cell responses against original and XBB.1.5 variant at baseline highlighting the presence of pre-existing immunity. These responses were significantly boosted one month following XBB.1.5 mRNA vaccination. We investigated in more details the CD4 + T cell responses targeting mutated XBB.1.5 epitopes using mini-pools of peptides encompassing only XBB1.5 mutations. We found that the monovalent vaccine did not elicit predominantly responses against mutated regions suggesting that variant-specific booster vaccines did not appear to effectively induce mono-reactive T cell responses [29]. We did not observe a significant increase in baseline S-specific CD8⁺ T cell responses following XBB.1.5 mRNA vaccination. In healthy individuals, mRNA vaccination induces robust CD4⁺ Th1 responses in over 90% of recipients, and CD8⁺ T cell responses are also detected, although with greater inter-individual variability, in approximately 75% of vaccinees [30, 31]. The lack of detectable CD8⁺ T cell response boosting in our study may also be due, at least in part, to the limited sample size.

Globally, the CD4 + T cell responses are reminiscent to antibody responses following monovalent XBB1.5 boost, eliciting recall of cross-reacting responses against the original Wuhan Spike and variants without a clear advantage against Omicron variants.

Gene expression profiles measured in whole blood collected via cotton swab showed that mRNA booster induced at day 1 significant changes in gene abundance from innate immunity predominantly mediated by interferon signaling. The reliability of gene expression profiling from such low-volume, finger-prick blood samples, has been previously validated by our group, demonstrating high correlation and reproducibility between self-collected capillary blood and standard venous sampling [32]. Longitudinal analysis allowed us to characterize the induction of pathways related to adaptive immune responses at D3 which persisted at D7. Several of these genes were described in responses to other vaccination and also in healthy individuals [33]. In particular, previous studies have shown that vaccines like BNT162b2 (Pfizer-BioNTech) enhance interferon-JAK-STAT pathways, leading to the upregulation of IFIT genes and a potential enhancement of response to the vaccine [34]. An association of interferon response after the first dose of mRNA vaccination and antibody response has been reported in healthy individuals [33]. It is interesting to note that we found in immune suppressed KTR changes in gene expression similar to those already described in healthy individuals following COVID-19 vaccination [33]. In contrast the abundance of genes related to adaptive immunity pathways were less pronounced in KTR patients. Albeit, we found significant changes in T-cell activation pathways at D7, only a limited number of B-cell pathways were modified at this time point such as TNFSF13B (TNF receptor superfamily member 13B) or IL21R, which play a key role in B cell development, survival and function. We cannot rule out that the kinetics of B-cell gene expression could be different. Previous studies have shown also in healthy subjects, that COVID vaccination induced a very weak B-cell signature observed on day 5, and only in a subset of individuals [33].

This study has several limitations. The lack of complete infection history in KTR precludes assessment of the impact of prior infections on vaccine-induced immune responses. The limited sample size prevented analysis of the influence of different immunosuppressive regimens, an important question that warrants investigation in larger cohorts. Although designed as an exploratory longitudinal study in a well-characterized KTR cohort, the absence of a healthy donor group limits direct comparison of post-vaccination responses. IgG subclass responses to XBB.1.5 and other Omicron variants, as well as neutralizing activity against more recent variants such as JN.1, were not evaluated. Finally, gene expression was not assessed beyond seven days post-vaccination, potentially missing signatures associated with later adaptive B-cell responses.

Conclusion

Our results showed that the administration of an XBB.1.5 monovalent mRNA vaccine booster in KTR can elicit robust neutralizing antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 Omicron variants and CD4⁺ T-cell responses in association with innate immune response, predominantly mediated by interferon signaling, but also activated T and NK-cells, consistent with observations in healthy individuals. These findings are relevant beyond XBB.1.5 variant as they inform booster vaccine design strategies in immunocompromised hosts. The frequency of boost administration remains to be determined to maintain an efficient anti SARS-Cov-2 protection but avoiding IgG class switching.
