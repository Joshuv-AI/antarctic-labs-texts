The latitudinal pattern of fine root intraspecific trait variation among species in plant communities
Authors: Mengguang Han, Ying Chen, Dayong Gan, Miao Yu, Rui Li, Yunfeng Han, Jiacun Gu, Shuaifeng Li, Jianrong Su, M Luke McCormack, Biao Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntraspecific trait variation (ITV) drives plant species performance in natural communities and can have substantial influences on multiple ecological processes. However, current studies on plant belowground (root) ITV are limited in trait coverage, spatial scale and biome type. Here, by conducting in-situ measurements of root traits in three forests from temperate to subtropics and compiling a large-scale dataset of root traits, we explore the latitudinal pattern of root ITV among species within natural plant community, its drivers and implications. Our results show that intraspecific variation constitute a substantial component of the total variation in root traits, and the physiological trait, root exudation, exhibit the highest ITV among traits. The extent of root ITV tends to increase with root diameter across species, with thick-root species having higher root ITV than thin-root species. We also find a significant latitudinal gradient for species’ intraspecific root trait variation in natural communities, with increasing ITV towards the equator. These findings can simplify the incorporation of root ITV in trait-based ecology, and contribute to understanding of plant species performance and community assembly under environmental changes.Subject terms: Biogeography, Ecosystem ecology

Introduction

Plant traits values are not fixed, but vary across spatiotemporal and organizational scales from organ to ecosystem1,2. In recent decades, quantifying trait variation has been adopted widely as a useful tool to understand ecological processes at different scales, such as individual plant performance, species distribution, community assembly, and ecosystem functions like nutrient cycling and productivity3–5. However, current studies and dominant theories in trait-based ecology are mostly derived from extensive trait comparisons at the species-level6–9, disregarding intraspecific trait variation (ITV). It has traditionally been assumed that trait variation within species is significantly smaller than that among species2,10–12. Yet, recent findings suggest that this may not be always true13–15. In fact, trait variation within communities is ultimately driven by differences among individuals rather than species, and there is mounting evidence that plant species can display high ITV because of both heritable genetic variation and phenotypic plasticity16–18. Several key empirical studies demonstrate that ITV can have serious consequences on plant species coexistence and ecosystem functions19–21. Accordingly, the role of plant ITV in ecological processes has seen a renewed focus in ecology.

Most studies on ITV are conducted on plant aboveground parts (especially leaf traits), where ITV within community accounts for ca. 25% of the total trait variation on average across the globe17. In comparison, within-species variation of root traits remains poorly understood, despite its likely role in regulating the performance of individual plants by mediating belowground resource uptake and pathogen defense3,22. Importantly, while essentially all plant species show some trait plasticity in response to biotic and abiotic environments, previous studies have noted wide differences in their levels of plasticity14,23,24. Thus, variation of root traits among individuals can reflect a species’ ability to adapt to environmental heterogeneity and effectively forage alternative nutrients, influencing both the adaptability and viability of this species within multispecies communities.

Current root studies on ITV are usually restricted to a single or small number of species, few traits of root chemistry and morphology, and limited spatial scales13,15,24,25, which may bias our understanding of the general trends, causes and consequences of intraspecific root trait variation. Variation of root traits across individuals can be strongly influenced by both macro- and micro-environmental heterogeneity in abiotic and biotic factors18,26, such as climatic conditions and nutrient gradients on a large scale, and the mineral nutrient patches, species competition and herbivore attack at smaller scales. Studies have shown that plants growing in cold and nutrient poor-environments tend to have roots featuring a more competitive morphology and high metabolic activity for resource acquisition, exemplified by roots with small diameter, high specific root length (SRL)/area (SRA), and high nitrogen concentration (RN)5,27. At the interspecies level, the diversity in root morphological traits is greatest in the tropics and declines sharply across the sequence of tropical, temperate, and desert biomes28. However, we know little about the biogeographical pattern of intraspecific root trait diversity across species in natural community. Previous studies suggest that climatic suitability might select for increased plant phenotypic plasticity, whereas stressful conditions (e.g., cold and/or dry environments) can impose limits on such intraspecific variation (including for both plant morphology and physiology2,29,30). On the other hand, the degree of individual competition (i.e., within species variation) is also reported to be positively regulated by species diversity of the community19. In this context, a comprehensive, multiple-species, and multi-trait test regarding root ITV is still lacking.

In addition, we face the limitation that studies on roots are significantly imbalanced among different categories of traits31. Earlier research has mainly focused on morphological and chemical traits, leaving out the critical importance of physiological traits (e.g., root exudation [RE]) in mediating plant performance and species coexistence32,33. For instance, RE can enhance the bioavailability of essential soil nutrients by solubilizing minerals or attracting beneficial microbes to support plant growth or alter the competitive balance among species34,35.

Although root physiological traits are receiving increasing attention36, no studies have yet explored their intraspecific variation, as well as the similarities or differences to levels of variation with other root traits. An additional important matter is the influential role of plant phylogenetic relatedness in shaping the differences of root traits across species28,37. For example, species in the evolutionarily ancient lineages (e.g., Magnoliids) generally have thick roots, whereas those in more recent lineages (e.g., Rosids) show the opposite pattern38. This phenomenon might be facilitated by a decline in atmospheric CO2, leading to plants having higher water requirements and a diminished reliance on mycorrhizal fungi28,38. However, whether the magnitude of root ITV among different species is constrained by phylogeny is still an open question.

Here, we focus on identifying the structure of root trait variation across ecological scales and the latitudinal pattern of root ITV among species in plant communities on a large scale, by conducting in-situ measurements in three geographically distant forests and by compiling a large scale dataset of root ITV at the species level in natural habitats. For the in-situ measurements in forests differing in terms of climate, species composition and richness, and soil nutrient conditions, we target seven root traits associated with chemistry, morphology, architecture and physiology (RN, root tissue density [RTD], root diameter [RD], SRL, SRA, branching intensity [BI] and RE; 51 species in total). For large scale analysis, we target four readily available root traits in worldwide databases (RN, RTD, RD, and SRL; 478 species in total). All these root traits underpin the main dimensions of root functions and are linked to multiple ecosystem processes such as community assembly and nutrient cycling3,5,39. Specifically, we decompose root trait variation across four nested ecological scales, i.e., individuals, species, plant functional types, and sites, and quantify the relative contribution of intraspecific variation to overall root trait variation based on our in-situ measurements. We also evaluate root ITV in a number of root traits on a relatively large scale, and attempt to reveal the latitudinal gradient of root ITV among species in natural plant communities and explore how climate and species attributes influence root ITV. We hypothesize that the degree of intraspecific root trait variation among species within community increases from high to low latitudes, given the contrasting effects of environmental filtering (inhibition) and plant competition (facilitation) on the conspecific trait diversity (Fig. 1).

Results

Within-species variation contributes substantially to root trait variation

By decomposing root trait variation at four ecological scales from individual to site, within species (i.e., intraspecific) explained an average of 41 ± 7% (mean ± standard error) of total trait variation across all seven root traits, but varied substantially among traits (from 20 ± 0.2% of SRL to 61 ± 0.4% of RE) (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 1). On the other hand, intraspecific variation represented by coefficient of variation (CV) also differed among root traits, of which RN exhibited the smallest and RE the largest variation, respectively (Fig. 2c), and the mean value was 0.25 across all root traits. The ITV of RE was significantly higher than that of other root traits (P < 0.001). The extent of ITV varied greatly among species for these chemical, morphological, architectural, and physiological traits (Fig. 2c). However, we did not observe a strong impact of plant phylogeny on root ITV across species, as shown by the weak phylogenetic signals (Supplementary Table 1). This result also held true for woody and nonwoody species, as well as arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) and ectomycorrhizal (EcM) species (Supplementary Table 1).

Root intraspecific trait variation in community exhibits significant latitudinal gradient

The extent of root ITV generally increased from the temperate to subtropical forest sites. Species in the temperate site, MES, exhibited significantly lower root ITV than species in the subtropical sites, SNJ and TYH (Fig. 3a–g and Supplementary Table 2), and this pattern was consistent for nearly all chemical, morphological, architectural, and physiological traits. The ITV of RD and SRL among species in a site increased significantly with decreasing absolute latitude (Fig. 3j, k). Accordingly, there were significant positive linkages between the ITV of RD (and SRL) with mean annual temperature (MAT) and precipitation (MAP) (Supplementary Fig. 2c, d, g, h). However, the ITV of RN and RTD did not show a significant latidudinal pattern. The latitunal patterns of root ITV were influenced by fine root type (Fig. 4). The increasing trend of root ITV among species within community with decreasing absolute latitue held true for the 1st–3rd root trait data and the absorptive root trait data (Fig. 4g–i, o–q), while it was not for the pooled fine root trait data (Fig. 4j–m). In addition, the ITV of RD and SRL increased significantly with the mean diameter of root at the interspecies level (Fig. 5c, d; P < 0.05). For our in-situ measurements, such links between the diameter of root and the extent of root ITV also appeared for most traits (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Discussion

Using the in-situ root trait dataset of 51 tree species from temperate and subtropical forest sites, we were able to decompose sources of root trait variation on a relatively large scale. Across four nested ecological scales from the individual to site levels, the greatest root trait variation mainly occurred at the within-species level and the interspecies level (Fig. 2b), which underlines the importance of both species turnover and individual performance in determining above- and below-ground trait diversity2,18. Unlike leaves, where the majority of trait variation occurs among species15,40, within-species variation contributed as much or more to root trait variation than interspecies variation did. On average, the proportion of total trait variation contributed by intraspecific variation for root traits (41%) was 1.3–1.6 times that for leaf traits (25–32%) within and among communities17. Soil properties and conditions vary spatially below the centimeter scale, forcing individual plants to adjust their root systems to specific edaphic conditions and adapt to soil heterogeneity26. Accordingly, root trait expression has been found to be significantly influenced by mineral nutrient patches41,42. This contrasts with leaves for which the main resource, atmospheric carbon dioxide, is fully available throughout the canopy despite some variation in light availability and microclimatic conditions22. The large root ITV implies that using a single root trait value at the species level as optimal solution for a specific environment might be problematic43.

The importance and magnitude of intraspecific variation depended on the considered root trait. The rate of root exuded carbon (i.e., RE) showed greater variation among individuals of a species compared with morphological traits, such as RD and SRL (Fig. 2b, c and Supplementary Fig. 1). On one hand, in contrast to physiological traits, root morphological traits were more phylogenetically conserved (Supplementary Table 3). Such evolutionary constraints can result in the latter to be more dissimilar between species than within species44,45. On the other hand, there is a tradeoff in the cost-benefit of plant plasticity in response to environmental cues, such as resources distribution, plant-plant competition, and herbivory attack46. The plasticity of physiological traits has been found to be less costly and reversible than that of morphological traits, and then ITV of physiological traits might be favored47. Further test on this finding is still needed as our focus on root physiology is not comprehensive enough. For instance, the potential impact of seasonal dynamics on plant physiology might be crucial in high latitudes, and root physiological traits also include nutrients uptake and respiration rate.

The amount of observed intraspecific variation varied greatly among species (Fig. 2c). We found that species with thick roots tended to have higher root ITV compared with species with thin roots (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 3). This finding still held true when considering only absorptive root data (1st order root and 1st–3rd order root branch) (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). Hence, plant species that display traits considered to be more acquisitive or opportunistic (e.g., strategies represented by low RD and high SRL) might feature a lower degree of intraspecific variation of root traits in contrast to species with conservative strategies (characterized by high RD and low SRL). In comparison with thick-root species, thin-root species are more likely to occur in relatively less stable environments, such as cold temperate and boreal climatic regions5,27,28, also as evidenced by the positive relationship between RD and MAT/MAP (Supplementary Fig. 5c, g). The abiotic filtering under such stressful conditions can constrain the dissimilarity of traits among individuals of the same species48 (see more discussion below). On the other hand, the variance of root traits has been proved to be largely influenced by changes in anatomical structures of root49,50. Fine roots are mainly composed by primary tissues, with the cortex making up the majority of their structure49. The cortical cells are generally unstable in terms of metabolic activity and responsiveness to environmental conditions, and they can undergo cell number, cell volume or cell wall modifications to adapt to the environment51,52, which further determines root traits expression. Then, species with thick root (i.e., having more cortical cells53) might have relatively high flexibility that allows for wide adjustments (expressed as more diverse trait values among individuals) to environmental stimuli such as resource availability or biotic interactions. When categorizing species into different plant functional types, for example, between species associated with AM and EcM fungi, or between species of woody and nonwoody plants, we did not find consistent differences in the ITV of different root traits (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). This could be associated with the absence of notable differences in RD between species of different functional types (Supplementary Fig. 8), indicating that different plant functional types share similar belowground functional needs when adapting to environments.

Additionally, the ITV of root chemical, morphological, architectural, and physiological traits were positively correlated with the ITV of RD across species (Supplementary Fig. 9). Diameter is a core trait that links to root construction, maintenance, persistence, and function50,54. For example, the length and surface area of a root per unit biomass invested (i.e., SRL and SRA, respectively) increase with decreasing diameter50,55. Therefore, the leading role of diameter in structuring interspecific differences of diverse root traits also plays a key role in determining the magnitude of ITV of root traits. Remarkably, for most root traits, the extent of root ITV (represented by CV) was not influenced by the number of individuals across plant species in the current study (Supplementary Fig. 10). However, this does not mean that a small number of individuals is enough to obtain reliable root ITV of certain plant species within a community. Considering both cost and workload, a sufficient sample size remains essential for increasing the accuracy and stability of the estimated root ITV. Taken together, these findings provide a tractable and promising approach for estimating intraspecific root trait variation across different species, which has the potential to simplify the inclusion of root ITV at different ecological scales in trait-based ecology.

The extent of root ITV among species in natural plant communities exhibited a significant pattern increasing from high to low latitudes for most root traits (Fig. 3a–g, j, k), and this result remained the same when only considering absorptive root data (Fig. 4g–i, o–q). To the best of our knowledge, no study has systematically reviewed or comprehensively evaluated the latitudinal pattern of species’ root ITV in community, and the patterns we observed here may tentatively be explained from aspects of abiotic influences and biotic interactions. First, the degree of plant species’ phenotypic plasticity has been shown to vary in response to climate conditions30,56,57, and the latitude integrates variation of climatic attributes, such as MAT and MAP (Supplementary Fig. 11), which we found to be positively associated with root ITV across study sites (Supplementary Fig. 2). This result is similar to the global trends of the degree of leaf morphological and physiological variation within species30. In general, phenotypically plastic organisms can incur costs in production and maintenance of expressed trait values under the environment, and such costs may increase when plants face stressful conditions58. Therefore, trait dissimilarities among individuals could be limited when costs outweigh the benefits of phenotypic plasticity in cold and dry environments46,59. However, it is contrasting for plants in warm and moist environments, because such favorable conditions allow for a wider range of root morphological and physiological variations29. Second, root traits generally serve as key indicators of plant’s ability to forage for resources. They play a pivotal role in determining whether a species can establish a competitive edge within the community60. On a large scale, species’ ITV of most root traits studied here within a community increased towards the equator in natural habitats, which is a pattern that coincides with the striking latitudinal plant diversity gradient61. According to the ‘individual variation’ theory19, stronger competition within species (i.e., high intraspecific variation) than between species can promote coexistence and maintain high plant species diversity. To adapt to fine-scale variation in the availability of multiple soil resources, enhancing root ITV has the potential to expand the ecological breadth of a species62–64, and this adaptability is particularly significant within a highly competitive community. Thirdly, thick roots are not as successful in higher latitudes, because they are more susceptible to seasonal freezing, water stress, and fluctuating soil resources; however, thin roots can rapidly respond to such unstable conditions28,41. It has been found that cold and dry environments (e.g., temperate and boreal forests) hold less thick-root species than warm and moist environments (e.g., tropical and subtropical forests)28. Then the increasing pattern of root ITV from high to low latitudes can also be partly attributed to the differences in plant community composition in the relative proportions of thick- and thin-root species, and that thick roots exhibited higher ITV (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 3). Overall, the latitudinal pattern of ITV of root traits among species in natural community could be jointly shaped by environmental filtering and plant-plant (individuals) competition (Fig. 1).

It is important to note that the latitudinal patterns of root ITV among species in plant community differed between the absorptive-root and fine-root data, which were derived from distinct source studies (Fig. 4f–q). For many plant species, the diameter-defined fine roots (≤ 2 mm in diameter) generally include absorptive and transport fine roots, two distinct pools of fine roots that differ in form and function. The absorptive fine roots involve primarily in the acquisition and uptake of soil resources, whereas the transport fine roots have low metabolic activity and are typically structurally reinforced and responsible for transporting resources49. The undetectable amount of transport fine roots in the diameter-based fine roots might result in biases in estimating root ITV. On the other hand, the limited latitude range of the 1st order root data may restrict the large scale patterns of root ITV that can be observed (Fig. 4b–e). Therefore, the order-based fine root classification is strongly advocated in root traits research to improve comparability between plant species and study sites49,65. In plant ITV research, another key issue is elucidating how individuals of the same species vary in their responses to environmental gradients24. Due to the limited widespread species in our root ITV data (Supplementary Fig. 12) and we primarily focused on species’ ITV within community, testing root trait plasticity of specific species against an environmental gradient is not feasible and falls beyond the scope of our study.

Collectively, our work shows that intraspecific variation is an important driving force in forming root trait diversity, and physiological traits are likely to be highly plastic in response to biotic and abiotic environments among different categories of root traits. The general trends of root ITV with the size of RD among species, i.e., that thick-root species have higher root ITV compared to thin-root species, provide a convenient way to predict patterns and relative magnitudes of ITV for specific root traits across species. Our results highlight clear correlations between climatic conditions and intraspecific variations of many root traits among species in natural plant communities. The expected change in climate may thus influence root traits expression within species and, in turn, also have dramatic impacts on species coexistence, community assembly, and other ecosystem functions. These findings shed light on understanding the primary forces that shape plant diversity and ecosystem function from a belowground perspective. We advocate for the explicit consideration of root ITV in future trait-based studies, particularly within tropical and subtropical plant communities.

Methods

In-situ measurements

Three geographically distant forest sites were selected to study intraspecific variation of root traits, including the Maoershan Forest Research Station in Heilongjiang province (45 °21′ N, 127 °34′ E; 400–500 m a.s.l.), the southern slope of the Shennongjia Mountain in Hubei province (31°32′ N, 110 °49′ E; 1200–1800 m a.s.l.), and the Taiyanghe Nature Reserve in Yunnan province (22 °34′ N, 101 °70′ E; 1467–1587 m a.s.l.) (Fig. 2a). The forest community in northeast China (Maoershan, MES) is mainly composed of deciduous broadleaved species, the forest community in central China (Shennongjia, SNJ) is a mixture of deciduous and evergreen broadleaved species, and the forest community in southwest China (Taiyanghe, TYH) is mainly composed by evergreen broadleaved species. MAT and MAP of the three sites are 2.8 °C and 723 mm (MES, temperate of China), 10.6 °C and 1330 mm (SNJ, north subtropics of China), and 17.7 °C and 1548 mm (TYH, south subtropics of China).

Absorptive root samples were collected at the individual tree level. In order to evaluate root ITV within the community, we sampled the most abundant tree species, determined by species abundance and the cumulative stem basal area in each forest site. In total, we sampled 51 species from 25 families and 41 genera, of which 14 species were sampled at MES, 18 species at SNJ, and 20 species at TYH (Supplementary Table 4). The Acer Pictum occurred in both MES and SNJ. At each forest site, the selected species can be categorized into different symbiotic status (or called plant functional types in this study) according to laboratory observation and public database66,67, including EcM, AM, ericoid mycorrhizal (ErM), and dinitrogen fixing (root nodules, Nfixing).

Individual trees were randomly distributed spatially within the forest site. For most species, five individuals (visibly healthy trees) were sampled to acquire the intraspecific variation of root traits (Supplementary Table 4). For some species, only 3–4 individuals were successfully sampled due to wildlife destruction to root exudates collection device or species distribution at extreme locations where root exudates could not be collected. One intact root branch (i.e., the first three order roots, an approximation of absorptive roots) of each individual tree was collected to measure root traits of interest. During collection we carefully dug the surface soil to locate target roots and then traced these roots back to the trunk of the parent tree to confirm their identity.

Root trait measurements

For in-situ collection of root samples, we measured seven core root traits related to root chemistry, morphology, architecture, and physiology, i.e., RN, RTD, RD, SRL, SRA, BI, and RE, which are tightly related to plant carbon economy and resource acquisition68.

Root exudates were collected by following the soil-hydroponic hybrid protocol of Phillips et al. (2008)69 in-situ. Briefly, the targeted first three order roots (i.e., absorptive roots) were excavated from surface soil carefully so that the intact root branch remained connected to the parent tree. The roots were then washed gently with deionized water and incubated in a syringe filled with carbon-free nutrient solution (0.1 mM KH2PO4, 0.2 mM K2SO4, 0.2 mM MgSO4, and 0.3 mM CaCl2). After 24 h, the solution was recovered from the syringe and thereafter stored and transported to laboratory in a cooler box. Afterwards, the root branch was rinsed gently with deionized water and harvested for measuring other traits. The in-situ collection of root exudates was conducted on consecutive rainless days in July (SNJ, subtropical forest), August (MES, temperate forest), and October-November (TYH, subtropical forest), depending on the workload in the field and laboratory and the time permission of the Nature Reserves. Total carbon in the collected solution was determined using TOC analyzer (multi N/C 3100; Analytikjena Inc., Germany). The flux rate of RE was calculated by dividing the total carbon by incubation time and root dry weight.

In addition to RE, the collected root branches were used to determine other fine root traits. They were firstly scanned to get high resolution images at 300–600 dpi, and then analyzed using WinRHIZO software (WinRHIZO PRO 2004b; Regent Instruments Inc., Canada), to obtain the data of total length, total surface area, total volume, average diameter (RD), and root tip number. Afterwards, all root samples were oven dried at 65 °C to constant weight. Tissues were then ground and RN was determined using an elemental analyzer (Vario EL III; Elementar Inc., Germany). The RTD was calculated as root dry mass divided by total volume, SRL/SRA was calculated as total root length/area divided by root dry mass, and BI was calculated as root tip number divided by root total length.

Literature data of intraspecific root trait variation in community

To acquire a holistic view of intraspecific variation of root traits among species in natural plant communities on a relatively large scale, we further conducted a literature search. Here, we focused our analyses on four traits: RN, RTD, RD, and SRL, which are informative of root carbon economy and nutrient acquisition, and reported widely. The literature dataset of intraspecific root trait variation was mainly built from the Fine Root Ecology Database (FRED)70, GRooT database31, and our in-situ measured root trait data. As the intraspecific variation (represented by CV) of root traits cannot be derived directly from the databases, we screened source studies that reported RN, RTD, RD, and SRL in FRED and GRooT. Because we focused on root ITV among species in natural multispecies communities and explored its latitudinal pattern, to obtain a reasonable estimate as much as possible and increase the comparability of ITV among sites, the studies should meet the following criteria: (1) Studies were carried out in natural field conditions and reported root traits of multiple dominant species (generally ≥ 3) of the typical local plant community. Therefore, monoculture plantings, common gardens, single species research, pot/greenhouse studies, and environmental change experiments were out of consideration; (2) Studies reported the root ITV, or the mean value and standard deviation of root traits for each species, or the mean value, standard error, and sample size of root traits for each species. The number of individuals per species shall be more than one; (3) 1st order root, 1st–3rd order root branch, or pooled fine roots (diameter ≤ 2 mm) were the targets. In addition, we further searched relevant studies from the China National Knowledge Infrastructure and the Web of Science, and found that five of them met the requirements (see Supplementary Tables 7 and 8 and Supplementary Fig. 13). We obtained a total of 24 studies, and finally compiled the literature dataset of the intraspecific variation of RN, RTD, RD, and SRL of 478 plant species from 32 sampling sites (also including our in-situ experiments in three forest sites in China; see Fig. 2a), which were distributed in diverse biomes (Supplementary Fig. 14).

In each sampling site, root ITV of each species was derived directly from source studies, or calculated as Standard deviationMean or Standard error×Sample sizeMean, i.e., the CV. Coefficient of variation quantifies variability as a proportion of the mean and is dimensionless, making it ideal for comparing data with different units (e.g., various root traits). Scientific names of species were standardized by querying names using the Taxonomic Name Resolution Service (https://tnrs.biendata.org/). The symbiotic status of species was confirmed according to the original study and two public databases66,67. When symbiotic status was not available at the species level, we categorized a species as belonging to the symbiotic status of other species in the same genus. In the literature dataset, 85% of species are AM or EcM species, and the remaining 15% were N-fixing, ericoid mycorrhizal, non-mycorrhizal species, or species whose symbiotic state cannot be determined (Supplementary Fig. 15). We also categorized all target species into woody and nonwoody plants, 78% of which were woody (Supplementary Fig. 15). For all sampling sites, we extracted the geographic information (latitude and longitude) and climatic factors (MAT and MAP), which were used to determine the type of Whittaker biome of each sampling site (Supplementary Fig. 14).

Data analysis

To partition the sources of root trait variation at different ecological scales (i.e., within species, between species, between plant functional types, and between sites), we performed variance partitioning by fitting a linear mixed effect (LME) model using our in-situ measured complete root trait data. In the LME model, the nested classification hierarchy of the random effect term was as follows: Site/Functional type/Species. This LME model was fitted using the ‘lme’ function of the R package NLME71. Afterwards, a variance component analysis was performed on this model using the “varcomp” function of the R package APE72. The unexplained variance was used to represent the contribution of within-species variation (i.e., intraspecific variation) to total root trait variation1,40. Notably, the original data of each root trait was normalized by conducting log10 transformation prior to carrying out these analyses. To complement trait variance analysis, the extent of root ITV for each species was also quantified by the CV, which was used for further analysis.

Evolutionary constraint has been suggested to sometimes be stronger than ecological filtering in structuring interspecific differences of root traits28,73,74, yet we know little about how plant phylogeny influences the extent of root ITV among species. To address this, we constructed a phylogenetic tree of all the species pruned from the backbone phylogeny of vascular plants provided by Zanne et al. (2014)75 (Supplementary Fig. 15), using the “phylo.maker” function of the R package V.PHYLOMAKER76, and all the species were available in the mega-tree. Then we calculated the phylogenetic signal (Blomberg’s K value) using the ‘phylosig’ function of the R package PHYTOOLS77, to evaluate the influence of plant phylogeny on the changes of the extent of root ITV among different species. A larger K value indicates greater phylogenetic conservatism of the variable78.

For our in-situ measurement root trait data, we analyzed the differences of the extent of root ITV (represented by CV for each species) among species of the three forest sites (MES, SNJ, and TYH). As the ITV of some root traits exhibited phylogenetic signal (despite it was weak), we performed phylogenetic generalized least square (PGLS) analyses to correct for non-independence in species data due to shared evolutionary history, using the ‘gls’ function of the R package NLME71. Briefly, the variance-covariance structure derived from the phylogenetic tree of the 51 target species over the three forest sites was incorporated in the model (Supplementary Table 2). Afterwards, the Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference post hoc test was applied to compare root ITV among sites, using the ‘glht’ function of the R package MULTCOMP79.

Using the literature dataset of root ITV, we analyzed the latitudinal gradient of root ITV among species in community. Specifically, we fitted the relationships between root ITV and absolute latitude across all study sites. As the number of species was different among sites, some species appeared in more than one sites (41 out of 478 species), and the target root type was different among studies (1st order root, 1st–3rd order root branch and pooled fine roots [diameter ≤ 2 mm]), we fitted an LME model using the “lmer” function of the R package LME480, setting “site ID” and “root type” as random factor and absolute latitude as fixed factor. To account for the influence of phylogeny, the first ten phylogenetic eigenvectors were extracted to represent evolutionary relatedness between species8,81. The phylogeny of 478 plant species across all sites was transformed to a phylogenetic distance matrix using the “cophenetic” function, and the distance matrix is then subjected to eigenvalue decomposition using the “cmdscale” function of the R package STATS. The phylogenetic eigenvectors were then added as covariates in the LME model (Supplementary Table 5).

As the target fine root type was different among studies, including 1st order root, 1st–3rd order root branch and pooled fine roots (diameter ≤ 2 mm), we further explored influences of fine root type on the latitudinal gradient of root ITV. Specifically, we fitted the relationships between root ITV and absolute latitude based on root trait data of (1) the 1st order root only, (2) the 1st–3rd order root branch only, (3) both the 1st order root and 1st–3rd order root branch (i.e., the absorptive root), and (4) the pooled fine roots (diameter ≤ 2 mm), respectively. LME models were constructed as abovementioned using the “lmer” function of the R package LME480, setting “site ID” and “root type” as random factor and absolute latitude as fixed factor, and the phylogenetic eigenvectors were also added as covariates in the model to account for the influence of phylogeny. Similar analyses were conducted to explore the relationships between root ITV and MAT/MAP across sites.

RD is deemed a core trait that links the variances of root traits across species28,50,82, reflecting central aspects of plant resource foraging strategies7, and thus might be also associated with the degree of belowground intraspecific variation. We explored the interspecific pattern of root ITV, and how is it related to RD of species (i.e., the mean diameter across individuals of the species). RD has been observed to differ significantly among biomes; for example, tropical trees generally exhibit thicker root than boreal trees27,28, and our data also confirmed the positive relationships between RD and MAT/MAP (Supplementary Fig. 5c, g). Therefore, an LME model was fitted by setting “biome type” and “root type” as random factor, and RD as fixed factor. Similarly, phylogenetic eigenvectors were added as covariates within the LME model to account for the influence of evolutionary relatedness between species (Supplementary Table 6).

Finally, to compare the differences in the extent of ITV among different root traits, the Kruskal–Wallis test was performed, as the number of species that reported root ITV (represented by CV) was different among root traits. All statistical analyses were completed using R v.4.3.283.
