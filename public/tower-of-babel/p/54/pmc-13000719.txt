Deletion of STIM1 in Treg cells protects against lung fibrosis and associated cardiovascular complications in a pre-clinical mouse model
Authors: Nagaraja Nagre, Balaji Srinivas, Nicholas Richards, Ryan Washington, Gregory Nicholson, Yan Sanders, Beata Kosmider, Khalid Matrougui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a progressive and incurable lung disease characterized by excessive tissue remodeling and impaired gas exchange. Evidence highlights the critical interplay between immune regulation, calcium signaling, and redox biology implications in the pathogenesis of fibrotic lung disease. Regulatory T-cells (Tregs), known to modulate cardiovascular and immune function, are diminished in IPF, contributing to inflammation and tissue damage. Here, we demonstrate that the stromal interaction molecule 1 (STIM1), a key regulator of intracellular calcium homeostasis, is significantly upregulated in Treg cells isolated from IPF patients and mice subjected to bleomycin-induced lung injury. This upregulation is associated with increased Treg cell apoptosis, reduced Foxp3 expression, and exacerbation of pulmonary fibrosis and cardiac remodeling. Using Treg-specific STIM1 knockout mice (TregSTIM1−/-), we show that genetic deletion of STIM1 preserves Treg cell survival and function, attenuates pulmonary fibrosis, and significantly reduces cardiac fibrosis and endothelial dysfunction. Importantly, TregSTIM1−/- mice displayed preserved pulmonary endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) phosphorylation and nitric oxide (NO) signaling, indicating protection of endothelial redox balance. The maintenance of NO bioavailability correlated with reduced parenchymal resistance, improved lung compliance, and enhanced survival during chronic fibrotic stress. Our findings uncover a novel STIM1-dependent mechanism regulating Treg cell viability and nitric oxide (NO) signaling during pulmonary fibrosis. These results reveal a previously unrecognized link between Treg survival and endothelial NO-dependent redox homeostasis in fibrotic lung disease. By preserving endothelial function and redox balance, deletion of STIM1 in Treg cells protects against fibrosis-associated pulmonary and cardiovascular complications, identifying STIM1 as a potential therapeutic target for restoring immune–redox balance in IPF.

Introduction

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a progressive and fatal interstitial lung disease characterized by excessive extracellular matrix deposition, alveolar architectural distortion, and irreversible loss of pulmonary compliance [1,2]. Despite extensive biomedical research, the initiating causes of IPF remain poorly defined. This maladaptive remodeling progressively increases lung stiffness, impairs gas exchange, and ultimately leads to respiratory failure [3]. Accumulating evidence supports a model in which IPF is driven not by classical inflammation alone, but by dysregulated wound-healing processes that fail to resolve after injury [4,5]. Recurrent damage to alveolar epithelial cells promotes sustained fibroblast activation, myofibroblast differentiation, and excessive deposition of collagen and other matrix components. Multiple profibrotic signaling pathways, including transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), Wnt/β-catenin, platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), and mechanotransduction signaling, have been implicated in fibroblast persistence and matrix accumulation [6,7]. However, the mechanisms by which these pathways become chronically activated and escape normal resolution remain unclear.

Currently, two antifibrotic agents, pirfenidone and nintedanib, are approved for the treatment of IPF. Although these therapies slow the rate of decline in lung function, they do not reverse established fibrosis, halt disease progression, or substantially improve long-term survival [8,9]. Their clinical utility is further limited by adverse effects, including gastrointestinal intolerance, skin reactions, fatigue, and hepatotoxicity [10]. The modest efficacy of available therapies underscores a critical gap in our understanding of the fundamental mechanisms underlying fibrotic remodeling in IPF.

Emerging evidence indicates that immune cells play a pivotal role in shaping fibrotic responses beyond traditional inflammatory paradigms [11]. Among these, regulatory T (Treg) cells have gained increasing attention as critical modulators of tissue repair, immune tolerance, and fibrosis [12]. IPF is associated with altered Treg cell frequency in patient lungs; however, their precise role in fibrosis initiation versus progression remains controversial [13]. Whether Treg dysfunction actively promotes extracellular matrix accumulation or impairs the resolution of lung injury in IPF remains unknown.

Importantly, the mechanisms that regulate Treg cell stability and function within the fibrotic lung remain largely unexplored. Defining how immune dysregulation intersects with fibrotic remodeling represents a critical knowledge gap with significant therapeutic implications.

Regulatory T cells also play an essential role in cardiovascular structure and function, including the regulation of cardiac fibrosis and remodeling [14]. Stromal interaction molecule 1 (STIM1), an endoplasmic reticulum–localized calcium sensor, coordinates calcium-dependent signaling pathways implicated in multiple cardiovascular diseases. Molkentin and Hulot demonstrated that heart failure can result from either STIM1 overexpression or deletion in cardiomyocytes [15,16]. We previously reported that mice lacking STIM1 specifically in vascular smooth muscle cells are protected against hypertension-induced vascular dysfunction [17], while endothelial-specific STIM1 disruption leads to arterial endothelial dysfunction. In immune cells, deletion of STIM1 and STIM2 in T cells, including Treg cells, protects mice from multiple sclerosis [18], whereas combined deletion of STIM1 and STIM2 specifically in Treg cells induces multiorgan inflammation [19], highlighting the context-dependent and cell-specific roles of STIM1 signaling.

In this study, we aimed to elucidate the role of STIM1 in Treg cells in lung and heart fibrosis, as well as its impact on vascular function in a mouse bleomycin model.

Materials and method

Animals and tissues from patients

All experimental procedures are conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Old Dominion University (ODU) has approved them. We bred STIM1flox/flox mice (Strain #:023350, B6.Cg-STIM1tm1Rao/J, Jackson Laboratory, USA. These mice carry loxP sites flanking exon 2 of the Stim1 gene, enabling conditional mutations in studies of store-operated Ca2+ entry and Ca2+ homeostasis. They are crossed with Cre-mice for Foxp3 (Strain #:016959, B6.129(Cg)-Foxp3tm4(YFP/icre)Ayr/J, Jackson Laboratory, USA). Foxp3YFP/Cre mice feature YFP and iCre downstream of the Foxp3 gene, enabling the study of regulatory T (Treg) cell development. YFP expression is observable in FOXP3+ Treg cells, and breeding with loxP-flanked sequence mice leads to Cre-mediated recombination in Treg cells, causing deletion of the floxed sequences in the offspring, to generate mice lacking STIM1, specifically in Treg cells. C57BL/6 J (WT) mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory, USA, and were housed in a sterile, ventilated facility at ODU under standard husbandry. Mice of 12-14 weeks of age were used in this study. All the animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Old Dominion University. In the present study, we only used male mice.

De-identified human lung tissues were obtained from Temple University, and the Institutional Review Board approved the study.

Bleomycin fibrosis mouse model

Bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis was modelled by intratracheal (i.t.) injection of Bleomycin (Ble: 1.5 U/kg in 50 μl: B2434, Millipore Sigma, USA) to WT, Stim1flox/flox and TregStim1−/ mice under anaesthesia with ketamine and xylazine. Control mice received 50 μl of PBS via i.t. injection. On day 14, after bleomycin injection, mice were euthanized, and lung tissue was collected.

To induce persistent fibrosis, Stim1flox/flox and Tregtim1-−/- mice received three bleomycin (1U/kg) injections, given bi-weekly. Mortality was recorded, and a Kaplan-Meier survival curve was constructed. The surviving mice were euthanized two weeks after the third bleomycin injection.

Measurement of lung mechanics

Lung mechanics were measured using the FlexiVent system and FlexiWare software (SCIREQ, Montreal, Canada). After Ble- exposure, mice were anesthetized using ketamine/xylazine. Anesthetized mice were intubated intratracheally with a calibrated 18½ G catheter and ventilated at a lower tidal volume (10 ml/kg) and 150 breaths/minute for 10 min. Tissue parenchymal resistance (G), parenchymal elastance (H), and compliance (Cst) were assessed using FlexiWare software, according to manufacturer recommendations.

Analysis of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF)

BALF was collected from mice by lavaging the lungs with 3 ml of PBS, 1 ml at a time. Cytokine levels, specifically IL-10, IL-1β, and TGF-β1, in the BALF were quantified using an ELISA kit (R&D Systems) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Lung tissue processing

The right mainstem bronchus was tied off with a 4-0 silk suture, and the right lung was cut and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until further use. The left lung was inflated with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 20 cm H2O and fixed overnight at 4 °C. The left lobe was processed at the ODU histology core facility for haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Masson's trichrome staining to assess lung injury and fibrosis.

Hydroxyproline assay

The collagen content in the mouse lung was determined by a hydroxyproline assay kit (MAK008, Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, the right lungs were homogenized in PBS and hydrolyzed in 6 N HCl at 120 °C overnight. Diluted samples were incubated with 4-(dimethylamino) benzaldehyde (DMAB) for 90 min at 60 °C, and the oxidized hydroxyproline content was determined by measuring the absorbance at 560 nm.

Isolation of treg cells

The whole lung tissue was digested in digestion buffer (1 mg/ml Dispase and 0.1 mg/ml DNase I in Hanks' balanced salt solution), and the cell suspension was passed through a 40-μm nylon mesh cell strainer to get the single cell suspension. According to the manufacturer's instructions, Treg cells were isolated from this cell suspension using EasySep™ Mouse CD4+CD25+ Regulatory T Cell Isolation Kit (18783A, STEMCELL Technologies, USA).

Western blot analysis

Aliquots of freshly frozen de-identified human lung tissues were obtained from Temple University, which the Temple University Institutional Review Board approved. Lung tissues (from humans and mice) and Treg cells were lysed in Pierce™ IP Lysis Buffer (ThermoFisher Scientific). The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and the protein concentration in the supernatant was determined by the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories). For the immunoblot, 40 μg of the total protein was resolved in SDS-PAGE and electroblotted onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk in PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 for 30 min at room temperature. The membrane was incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies. The membrane was then developed using ECL Western Blot Substrate (ThermoFisher Scientific). The following primary antibodies were used: anti-STIM1 (5668, 1:500), anti-FoxP3 (12632, 1:500), from Cell Signaling Technology Inc., anti-p-eNOS (612393, 1:500), anti-eNOS (610296, 1:500) from R &D Biosciences, anti-caspase-3 (1:1000, NB-100-56708, Novus Biologicals), and anti-β-actin (SC-47778, 1:1000, Santa Cruz Biotechnology).

Pulmonary arterial endothelial function

Pulmonary artery reactivity in all groups of mice was evaluated using a DMT wire myograph. The lungs were collected from all groups of mice and immediately placed in cold PSS solution, carefully cleared of connective tissue around the lung arteries, and cut into rings (2 mm in length). The lung arteries were mounted in small vessels in a dual chamber myograph (DMT myograph; AD Instruments Ltd., Oxford, UK) to measure isometric tension. After a 30-min equilibration period in PSS solution bubbled with CO2 at 37 °C (pH = 7.4), arteries were stretched to their optimal lumen diameter for active tension development. After 1 h of incubation time, artery rings were pre-constricted with phenylephrine (PE 10−8 - 10−4 M) (Sigma- Aldrich, P6126-50G, CAS 61-76-7), and when a steady maximal contraction was reached, cumulative dose-response curves were obtained for acetylcholine (ACh, 10−8 - 10−4 M) (Sigma- Aldrich, A6625-25G, Lot#BCBH3758V).

Picrosirius Red staining

Picrosirius Red staining was carried out to determine collagen deposition (fibrosis) in the hearts of all the groups of mice. Paraffin-embedded specimens of selected tissues were cut into 4-5 μm thick sections. Deparaffinized samples were rinsed with Milli-Q water three times. The paraffined samples were then incubated with the Picoro-Sirius Red (PSR) as per the manufacturer's protocol detailed in the kit (NOVAULTRA SIRIUS RED STAIN KIT, Catalog number: NC0249910). Finally, samples were dehydrated, mounted, and slides were observed under the bright field microscope (20X) (Olympus, IX73 U-TB190 Japan), and collagen deposition (Fibrotic area) was evaluated using ImageJ software.

Immunostaining

Human lung tissue sections were deparaffinized, hydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. The sections were stained for Foxp3 using an anti-Foxp3 antibody (1:200, ab20034, Abcam) and for STIM1 using anti-STIM1 (1:100, ab108994, Abcam). Sections were then incubated with fluorochrome-labeled species-specific secondary antibodies, and the resultant stained sections were imaged using an Olympus IX73 fluorescent microscope.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical calculations for significant differences were performed using one-way or repeated measure two-way ANOVA plus Tukey's post hoc test for multiple comparisons as appropriate in all experiments. Comparisons are considered statistically significant when p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software version 10.6.

To assess whether the observed reduction in Foxp3 protein in IPF lungs corresponds to a decrease in Treg abundance, we performed immunofluorescence staining on lung tissue sections from healthy controls and IPF patients. Foxp3 immunostaining showed fewer Foxp3+ cells in IPF lung sections compared to healthy lungs, indicating a reduction in Treg numbers rather than just a change in Foxp3 expression (Fig. 1B). Dual immunofluorescence analysis further revealed elevated STIM1 expression within Foxp3+ cells in IPF lungs. In contrast, Foxp3+ cells in healthy lung tissue exhibited comparatively low STIM1 expression (Fig. 1B, merged). These results collectively indicate a decreased Treg presence in IPF lungs and identify upregulated STIM1 expression in IPF Tregs.

We then examined the levels of STIM1 and Foxp3 in isolated Treg cells from WT (C57BL/6J) male mice 14 days after bleomycin (1.5 U/kg) exposure. We observed a significant increase in STIM1 expression in lung Treg cells, accompanied by a simultaneous decrease in Foxp3 expression (Fig. 1C). Based on these findings from patients and mice, we hypothesized that deleting STIM1 in Treg cells could protect mice from pulmonary fibrosis, pulmonary vascular endothelial dysfunction, and cardiac fibrosis. To understand the specific role of STIM1 in pulmonary fibrosis, pulmonary vascular endothelial function, and cardiac fibrosis, we generated mice that do not express STIM1 specifically in Treg cells (TregStim1-/-). We compared them to their control counterparts (Stim1flox/flox). We isolated Treg cells and Epithelial cells from the lungs of Stim1flox/flox and TregStim1-/- mice and confirmed the deletion of STIM1 specifically in Treg cells in TregStim1−/- mice (Fig. 2A), not in Epithelial cells (Fig. 2B) using Western blot analysis. We hypothesize that STIM1 expression in Treg cells is crucial in regulating cell survival. We assessed the frequency of CD4+CD25+FoxP3+ Treg cells in the lung single-cell suspensions from Stim1flox/flox and TregStim1-/-mice on day 14 following bleomycin administration using flow cytometry. Our results demonstrate a significant decrease in the number of Treg cells in Stim1flox/flox mice after exposure to bleomycin, compared to the Treg cell count in TregStim1−/- mice subjected to the same treatment. To investigate the critical long-term role of STIM1 in Treg cells in chronic, progressive pulmonary fibrosis, we conducted a study with substantial translational relevance. Using Stim1flox/flox and TregStim1−/- mice, we administered repeated intratracheal doses of bleomycin over several weeks, mimicking the prolonged progression of pulmonary fibrosis observed in patients. Remarkably, TregStim1−/- mice exhibited a significantly higher survival rate than Stim1flox/flox mice, highlighting the long-term therapeutic potential of targeting STIM1 in Treg cells to treat pulmonary fibrosis (Fig. 6A). While Stim1flox/flox mice suffered severe pulmonary injury and fibrosis, TregStim1−/- mice demonstrated markedly reduced pulmonary damage/Injury (Fig. 6B–D) and Pulmonary fibrosis (Fig. 6C–E). Discussion

Pulmonary fibrosis is a well-established and complex immunopathological disease driven not only by aberrant extracellular matrix deposition, but also by profound disturbances in immune homeostasis [20]. While early paradigms emphasized macrophage-driven inflammation and fibroblast-myofibroblast transition, accumulating evidence indicates that failure of immune regulatory mechanisms plays a central role in sustaining chronic inflammation and fibrotic remodeling [21]. In this study, we identify STIM1-dependent Treg cell apoptosis as a previously unrecognized mechanism linking immune adaptation failure to pulmonary fibrosis, pulmonary vascular endothelial dysfunction, and secondary cardiac fibrosis.

Regulatory T cells are well established as critical suppressors of excessive immune activation in the lung, and multiple studies have reported reduced Treg cell numbers or impaired Treg function in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and in experimental models of lung fibrosis [22,23]. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying Treg cell instability and fibrotic lung disease remain poorly defined. Our findings extend this literature by identifying STIM1 upregulation as a conserved feature of Treg cells in both human IPF lungs and bleomycin-induced fibrosis, linking intracellular calcium signaling machinery to Treg reduction in fibrotic disease.

STIM1 is classically recognized as a key regulator of store-operated calcium entry [24,25]. Prior studies have shown that STIM1-mediated calcium signaling is required for T cell activation, differentiation, and cytokine production, including in Treg cells [26,27]. Notably, genetic disruption of STIM1 or ORAI1 in T cells has been reported to impair immune responses and alter Treg development under homeostatic conditions [[27], [28], [29]]. In contrast, our data reveal a context-dependent, maladaptive role for STIM1 signaling in Treg cells during chronic inflammatory stress, in which increased STIM1 expression correlates with Foxp3 loss and apoptosis rather than immune suppression. These findings suggest that while basal STIM1 activity may be required for Treg function, excessive STIM1 signaling in fibrotic environments may drive Treg destabilization and death.

Consistent with this concept, Treg-specific deletion of STIM1 preserved Treg survival following lung injury and conferred marked protection against pulmonary fibrosis. Our study extends these findings by identifying STIM1 as a molecular checkpoint controlling Treg survival, rather than simply Treg abundance or suppressive capacity. Importantly, the associated shift toward an anti-inflammatory cytokine profile, characterized by increased IL-10 and reduced IL-1β and TGF-β1, is consistent with prior work showing that Treg-derived IL-10 restrains profibrotic macrophage activation and fibroblast signaling [30].

Beyond parenchymal fibrosis, pulmonary vascular dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a critical determinant of disease severity and mortality in pulmonary fibrosis [31,32]. Endothelial dysfunction, impaired nitric oxide signaling, and vascular remodeling are associated with pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure in this population [[33], [34], [35]]. While immune-mediated mechanisms of vascular injury have been proposed, direct links between Treg dysfunction and pulmonary endothelial impairment have remained limited. Our findings demonstrate that protection of Treg integrity via STIM1 deletion maintains pulmonary endothelial function and eNOS signaling, supporting the emerging concept that immune regulatory failure contributes to vascular injury in fibrotic lung disease. Nitric oxide (NO) is a central regulator of vascular redox homeostasis, and disruption of eNOS-derived NO signaling is known to promote oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction. The preservation of eNOS phosphorylation and NO bioavailability observed in TregSTIM1−/- mice, therefore, suggests that maintaining Treg survival contributes to stabilization of endothelial redox balance during pulmonary fibrosis. These findings highlight a previously underappreciated link between immune regulatory pathways and NO-dependent redox signaling in the pulmonary vasculature. It is not clear yet how Treg cells regulate endothelial function. Whether a direct interaction between Treg cells and endothelial cells is still unknown. Also, Treg cells near endothelial cells may release factors, such as IL-10. Treg cells may reduce the expression of adhesion molecules, such as VCAM-1, on endothelial cells, thereby inhibiting endothelial inflammation. However, our data are consistent with prior reports showing that Treg depletion exacerbates endothelial dysfunction in other inflammatory vascular contexts [[36], [37], [38]].

Strikingly, STIM1 deletion in Treg cells also limited secondary cardiac fibrosis, underscoring the systemic consequences of immune dysregulation initiated in the lung. Cardiac fibrosis has been described in association with chronic lung disease and pulmonary hypertension, [27,[39], [40], [41]] yet its immunological underpinnings remain incompletely understood. Our data support a model in which Treg apoptosis during lung injury amplifies systemic inflammatory signaling, contributing to distal organ fibrosis. This concept is consistent with growing evidence that immune Treg cells mediate tissue fibrosis [12,42,43].

Importantly, the protective effects of Treg-specific STIM1 deletion were sustained in a chronic, repetitive bleomycin model that more closely recapitulates the progressive nature of human pulmonary fibrosis. The durability of protection observed in TregStim1−/- mice highlights the therapeutic relevance of targeting Treg survival pathways, rather than transient inflammatory signals, for long-term disease modification.

Collectively, our findings identify STIM1 as a previously unrecognized regulator of Treg cell survival in fibrotic lung disease, linking dysregulated calcium signaling to immune failure, fibrosis, vascular dysfunction, and cardiac remodeling. By preserving Treg viability and immune homeostasis, STIM1 deletion limits profibrotic signaling and protects cardiopulmonary structure and function. These protective effects are associated with preservation of endothelial nitric oxide signaling and maintenance of vascular redox homeostasis, suggesting that immune regulatory pathways influence NO-dependent redox balance during fibrotic lung disease. These results extend existing paradigms of pulmonary fibrosis pathogenesis and suggest that Treg-intrinsic STIM1 signaling represents a novel, immune-centered therapeutic target for pulmonary fibrosis and its associated cardiovascular complications.

Future studies should identify upstream inflammatory or metabolic signals that drive STIM1 upregulation in Treg cells under fibrotic stress and elucidate how STIM1-dependent calcium signaling intersects with transcriptional and apoptotic programs that govern Foxp3 stability. In addition, delineating how STIM1-regulated Treg dysfunction influences macrophage polarization, dendritic cell activation, and endothelial responses, including the regulation of endothelial nitric oxide signaling and vascular redox homeostasis, will be essential for translating these findings into targeted immunomodulatory therapies. Ultimately, strategies aimed at preserving Treg survival by modulating STIM1 signaling may offer a novel avenue to halt or slow the progression of pulmonary fibrosis and its systemic sequelae.
