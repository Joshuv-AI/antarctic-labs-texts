Geostatistical point process modelling of HIV prevalence using disease progression and CD4 cell count dynamics
Authors: Exaverio Chireshe, Retius Chifurira, Jesca Mercy Batidzirai, Knowledge Chinhamu, Ayesha BM Kharsany
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryKwaZulu-Natal, South Africa, remains the epicentre of the HIV epidemic, characterized by pronounced spatial and demographic heterogeneity. Capturing geographic variation in HIV burden and disease progression is critical for informing targeted public health responses. We used Bayesian geostatistical models to examine spatial patterns of HIV prevalence and immunologic decline, with CD4 cell count dynamics used as temporal indicators of disease progression. Data were obtained from two population-based cross-sectional surveys conducted in uMgungundlovu Municipality under the HIV Incidence Provincial Surveillance System. Spatial variation in HIV prevalence was modelled using geostatistical point-process methods with enumeration area structure and spatially continuous random effects. CD4 outcomes were analyzed in relation to demographic, socio-economic, and clinical factors, with spatial clustering assessed using posterior exceedance probabilities. Distinct hotspot areas were identified, reflecting local variation in HIV risk and progression. These findings highlight the value of spatial modeling for guiding geographically targeted HIV prevention and treatment strategies.Subject areas: Environmental health

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) remains a major global public health challenge, with an estimated 39 million people living with HIV worldwide, two-thirds of whom reside in sub-Saharan Africa.1 South Africa continues to represent the epicentre of the epidemic, accounting for approximately one-fifth of the global HIV burden.2 Within South Africa, the province of KwaZulu-Natal (KZN) has consistently recorded the highest HIV prevalence nationally, exceeding 25% among adults in several population-based surveys.2,3 Despite extensive investments in prevention and treatment programmes, including the widespread rollout of antiretroviral therapy (ART), the epidemic in KZN remains deeply entrenched, particularly among young women and socioeconomically vulnerable populations.4

HIV transmission and prevalence are not uniformly distributed but display marked spatial heterogeneity shaped by demographic, behavioral, and structural determinants.5,6 Traditional surveillance systems, such as household and antenatal clinic surveys, provide crucial prevalence estimates but often conceal fine-scale spatial variations in risk. Consequently, spatial epidemiology has emerged as an essential discipline for revealing hidden geographic disparities and identifying areas of elevated transmission or unmet need. Bayesian geostatistical models, in particular, have proven to be highly effective in quantifying spatial heterogeneity by jointly estimating the effects of individual-level covariates and spatially structured random effects. Such models enhance estimation precision in data-sparse regions and improve the reliability of disease mapping.7,8 Although widely applied to malaria, tuberculosis (TB), and other infectious diseases,8,9 their use in HIV research remains comparatively limited, particularly in analyses integrating biological indicators of disease progression alongside prevalence data.

Most spatial analyses of HIV have focused primarily on serostatus, treating prevalence as a static outcome rather than a reflection of underlying disease dynamics,10 yet HIV infection evolves over time, with immunological decline and treatment uptake shaping both individual and population-level outcomes. CD4 cell count, a key marker of immune function, provides valuable insight into disease progression and clinical status. Historically, thresholds such as <200 and <350 cells/μL have defined stages of advanced disease and guided ART initiation. Although median CD4 counts at ART initiation have improved nationally, from approximately 396 to 473 cells/μL between 2013 and 2023, a considerable proportion of individuals continue to present with advanced disease (CD4 ≤200 cells/μL), particularly among men.11 In KZN, similar patterns persist, with many patients experiencing opportunistic infections and poor outcomes linked to late diagnosis and immune suppression.12 ART exposure, meanwhile, serves as a critical proxy for treatment access and immune recovery. Incorporating CD4 and ART markers within spatial models thus enables the capture of temporal dynamics of infection, linking prevalence to disease progression even in cross-sectional datasets.

Population-based survey data further demonstrate the spatial concentration of HIV in KZN. The sixth South African National HIV Prevalence, Incidence, Behaviour and Communication Survey (SABSSM VI, 2022) reported provincial prevalence at approximately 16% in KZN, with the highest burden observed among adults aged 25–49 years2 Fine-scale Bayesian analyses have revealed distinct clustering within the province, with prevalence among young women exceeding 45%, even in the era of universal ART access.5 Across sub-Saharan Africa, similar geographic heterogeneity has been observed, with spatial clustering of HIV prevalence linked to migration, urban-rural gradients, social inequities, and delayed ART initiation.13,14 Parallel analyses of national and provincial CD4 datasets confirm that late diagnosis and poor ART uptake remain key drivers of disease progression, reinforcing the need to integrate immunological and spatial analyses to better characterize epidemic dynamics.15,16

Recent work in KZN by Chireshe et al.17 applied a joint multivariate Bayesian latent variable model to CD4 cell counts and TB diagnosis among HIV-positive individuals. Their findings demonstrated strong correlations between immunological decline and opportunistic co-infections, underscoring the value of integrating biomarkers into statistical models of HIV outcomes. Yet, such approaches have rarely been extended to prevalence modelling or geostatistical frameworks capable of capturing fine-scale spatial heterogeneity and temporal dynamics. Similarly, while earlier spatial analyses have mapped HIV prevalence, few have incorporated CD4 trajectories and treatment proxies as temporal indicators of disease progression within a unified geostatistical framework.

Despite advances in Bayesian disease mapping, important research gaps remain. Existing spatial HIV models seldom integrate biological and treatment-related variables such as CD4 dynamics or ART exposure, limiting their capacity to capture immunological deterioration and clinical severity. Moreover, most studies employ areal models that aggregate data to administrative boundaries, thereby obscuring fine-scale heterogeneity evident at the individual or household level. These limitations hinder the development of precision-targeted interventions in high-burden regions.

While Bayesian geostatistical models based on the integrated nested Laplace approximation (INLA)/SPDE framework have been widely applied to HIV prevalence mapping, they have largely treated prevalence as a static spatial outcome, without explicitly linking infection patterns to biological markers of disease progression. The present study introduces a conceptual and methodological advance by integrating empirical Bayes (EA)-assisted estimation within a geostatistical point-process SPDE framework in which CD4 cell count and ART exposure are incorporated as temporal proxies related to disease duration and clinical progression within cross-sectional prevalence data. Unlike conventional HIV geostatistical models that estimate spatial risk surfaces alone, this approach jointly models infection presence with multiple CD4-based submodels, including continuous CD4 levels, advanced disease thresholds, and ART status, thereby enabling the spatial characterization of not only where HIV prevalence is high but also where infections are likely to be immunologically advanced or treatment delayed. In doing so, the framework bridges the gap between static prevalence mapping and longitudinal disease progression modelling in settings where longitudinal follow-up data are limited or unavailable.

By incorporating disease-progression and CD4 cell count dynamics as temporal proxies within a geostatistical point-process framework, this study contributes to a more dynamic and biologically informed understanding of the HIV epidemic. The resulting spatial representations identify locations where both infection and advanced disease burden are concentrated, providing critical insights for the design of geographically targeted prevention, diagnosis, and treatment interventions. This integrative approach advances spatial HIV epidemiology by connecting prevalence mapping with immunological and treatment data, thereby enhancing the evidence base for effective HIV control strategies in high-burden settings such as KZN, South Africa.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Table S4 presents the weighted HIV prevalence and mean log-transformed CD4 cell counts by demographic, socioeconomic, and behavioral covariates among participants from the Vulindlela and Greater Edendale areas in the uMgungundlovu Municipality, KZN. The overall weighted HIV prevalence was 35.8% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 34.9–36.6), and the mean log-transformed CD4 count among HIV-positive participants was 6.13 (standard deviation [SD] = 0.65).

Raw, weighted HIV prevalence estimates at the EA level ranged from 0% to approximately 70%. For mapping purposes, these estimates were grouped into standardized prevalence classes, with values ≥ 30% representing the highest category, to facilitate visual comparison across figures (Figure S4). While this provides an initial spatial overview, prevalence estimates in areas with small sample sizes are inherently less stable. To address this limitation, Bayesian spatial smoothing and posterior exceedance probability analyses were subsequently conducted.

Based on the geostatistical HIV prevalence model, several demographic, behavioral, and clinical covariates were significantly associated with HIV-related outcomes. Age was strongly associated with advanced HIV outcomes, with individuals aged 25–44 years showing substantially higher odds compared with those aged 15–19 years. The highest odds were observed among individuals aged 35–39 years (AOR = 13.4; 95% CrI: 11.5–15.8), followed by those aged 30–34 years (AOR = 12.1; 95% CrI: 10.4–14.1) and 40–44 years (AOR = 12.6; 95% CrI: 10.7–14.9). Male sex was associated with lower odds of advanced disease compared with female sex (AOR = 0.29; 95% CrI: 0.27–0.32).

Sociodemographic characteristics also played a role. Being single was associated with 1.5 times higher odds of adverse outcomes compared to those married (AOR = 1.49; 95% CrI: 1.36–1.65). Educational attainment showed a gradient: individuals with incomplete secondary (AOR = 1.51; 95% CrI: 1.40–1.62), primary schooling (AOR = 1.51; 95% CrI: 1.30–1.75), or no schooling (AOR = 1.69; 95% CrI: 1.36–2.09) had significantly higher risks, whereas tertiary education was protective (AOR = 0.56; 95% CrI: 0.48–0.65).

Behavioral factors were also significantly associated with adverse HIV outcomes. Alcohol use (OR = 1.19; 95% CrI: 1.09–1.31), ever being sexually active (AOR = 2.30; 95% CrI: 1.98–2.67), having multiple sexual partners (three partners: OR = 1.27; 95% CrI: 1.01–1.27), reported condom use (AOR = 1.59; 95% CrI: 1.36–1.60), and forced first sex (AOR = 1.31; 95% CrI: 1.08–1.60) were all associated with higher odds of adverse HIV-related outcomes.

Healthcare-related and clinical variables were strongly associated with adverse HIV outcomes. Accessing healthcare was associated with higher odds (AOR = 1.29; 95% CrI: 1.20–1.39), likely reflecting care-seeking among symptomatic individuals. A history of TB was associated with more than 2-fold higher odds of adverse HIV outcomes (AOR = 2.23; 95% CrI: 2.04–2.44), while prior sexually transmitted infection (STI) diagnosis was also associated with higher odds (AOR = 1.49; 95% CrI: 1.31–1.68).

The survey year (2015 vs. baseline) was associated with slightly lower odds of adverse outcomes (AOR = 0.85; 95% CrI: 0.79–0.93), consistent with temporal improvements in HIV prevention and treatment coverage. This temporal effect likely reflects programme-scale changes between survey waves, including expanded ART eligibility, increased HIV-testing coverage, and earlier linkage to care. As such, survey year acts as a proxy for evolving health system and prevention contexts rather than an individual-level causal exposure, and should be interpreted accordingly.

The analysis of predictors of severe immunosuppression (CD4 <200 cells/μL) revealed several significant demographic, socio-economic, and clinical associations. Older age was strongly associated with low CD4 counts. Compared with adolescents aged 15–19 years, individuals aged 30–34 years had 1.68 times higher odds of CD4 <200 (95% CI: 1.02–2.78), rising to over 2-fold higher risk among those aged 35–39 years (AOR = 2.07, 95% CrI: 1.24–3.45) and 40–44 years (AOR = 2.17, 95% CrI: 1.29–3.63). Men were almost twice as likely as women to present with severe immunosuppression (AOR = 1.90, 95% CrI: 1.57–2.28), highlighting gender disparities in HIV care access and health-seeking behavior.

Socioeconomic indicators were inversely associated with severe immunosuppression. Individuals reporting salary or wage income had lower odds of CD4 <200 (AOR = 0.75, 95% CrI: 0.56–0.99), with a similar pattern observed among those receiving pensions or social grants (AOR = 0.75, 95% CrI: 0.55–1.01).

Clinical variables were also strongly associated with advanced immunosuppression. Prior TB diagnosis was associated with higher odds of CD4 <200 (AOR = 1.38, 95% CrI: 1.15–1.64). Participants on ART had higher odds of severe immunosuppression (AOR = 1.94, 95% CrI: 1.59–2.38). This association reflects confounding by indication, whereby ART initiation commonly occurs at advanced stages of disease, rather than a detrimental effect of treatment. Viral non-suppression showed the strongest association, with unsuppressed individuals having nearly 10-fold higher odds of CD4 <200 compared with those who were virally suppressed (AOR = 9.43, 95% CrI: 7.49–11.87).

Taken together, these results highlight that older age, male gender, TB co-infection, and viral non-suppression are key drivers of advanced HIV disease (AHD) in this population, while stable income may mitigate risk through improved healthcare access. The paradoxical association between ART status and low CD4 counts emphasizes the persistent challenge of late ART initiation, despite wide-scale availability of treatment.

In the continuous CD4 model, several covariates showed statistically credible associations. Older age groups had lower CD4 on the log scale compared with the reference age group: ages 25–29 (β = −0.08, 95% CrI: −0.16 to −0.01), 30–34 (β = −0.12, 95% CrI: −0.20 to −0.05), 35–39 (β = −0.14, 95% CrI: −0.22 to −0.06), 40–44 (β = −0.14, 95% CrI: −0.22 to −0.06), and 45–49 (β = −0.12, 95% CrI: −0.20 to −0.03). Male sex was associated with substantially lower CD4 (β = −0.24, 95% CrI: −0.27 to −0.20). By contrast, respondents reporting pension/grant income (β = +0.10, 95% CrI: 0.05–0.16) or salary/wage (β = +0.097, 95% CrI: 0.04–0.15) had higher CD4. Shorter residence in the community (<1 year) and prior TB diagnosis were associated with lower CD4 (β = −0.11, 95% CrI: −0.18 to −0.03 and β = −0.06, 95% CrI: −0.09 to −0.03, respectively). Compared with the reference year, observations from 2015 had slightly higher CD4 (β = +0.03, 95% CrI 0.003–0.06). The modest increase in CD4 levels observed in 2015 is consistent with improved treatment uptake and earlier diagnosis over time. Notably, being on ART was associated with lower CD4 (β = −0.186, 95% CrI: −0.22 to −0.15), and viral non-suppression showed the largest negative association (β = −0.55, 95% CrI: −0.58 to −0.51). Back transformed to the original CD4 scale, these coefficients correspond roughly to a 42% lower CD4 for viral non-suppression, 17% lower for ARV use, 21% lower for males, and 10% higher CD4 for those reporting pension or salary income. These patterns are consistent with disease progression markers (low CD4 with high viral load) and possible confounding by indication for ART (persons on ART may have started with lower CD4).

Across all models, older age (30–44 years), male sex, TB co-infection, and viral non-suppression consistently predicted adverse HIV outcomes, whereas salaried income, pension support, and viral suppression were protective. These patterns highlight the combined demographic, behavioral, and clinical determinants influencing HIV infection and disease progression in KZN.

After accounting for these covariates, residual spatial structure remained, suggesting geographically patterned heterogeneity beyond individual-level effects.

Spatial random effects and hyperparameters

The spatial random effects from the fitted Bayesian hierarchical model captured the residual spatial dependence in HIV prevalence after adjusting for individual- and community-level covariates. Figure 2 presents the posterior spatial field summaries, including the posterior mean, SD, and the 95% CrI bounds (lower and upper). The posterior mean map revealed a smooth spatial surface of the underlying HIV risk, indicating that the model effectively smoothed local prevalence estimates across neighboring enumeration areas (EAs). Areas in the north-eastern and south-eastern regions of the study area exhibited higher posterior mean values, suggesting residual HIV risk that persists beyond what is explained by the fixed effects.

Spatial distribution of predicted HIV prevalence and uncertainty estimates

The posterior SD map showed relatively higher uncertainty in peripheral or sparsely sampled EAs, while central and densely populated EAs exhibited smaller SD values, reflecting greater model confidence in those estimates. The 95% CrI maps (lower and upper bounds) further illustrated the range of plausible posterior values, with narrower intervals in areas of high data support and wider intervals in regions with sparse observations. Overall, the spatial field summaries demonstrate that the model adequately captured local spatial dependencies, providing a robust basis for identifying spatially structured variation in HIV prevalence.

The three Bayesian geostatistical models consistently demonstrated spatial heterogeneity in HIV-related outcomes, with clustering patterns varying according to outcome specification. The joint EA + SPDE model identified moderate residual spatial dependence, with clustering operating primarily at short to intermediate distances (posterior median spatial range ≈ 20 km; 95% CrI: 8–58 km; posterior mean ≈ 12 km) and a spatial standard deviation of 0.27 (95% CrI: 0.14–0.48), suggesting the influence of unmeasured local geographic factors beyond the individual-level predictors.

In the continuous CD4 model (log-transformed CD4), spatial variation was minimal (spatial SD = 0.03; 95% CI: 0.01–0.07), indicating that most of the variability in CD4 counts was captured by demographic and clinical covariates. By contrast, the binary model of severe immunosuppression (CD4 < 200 cells/μL) exhibited stronger localized clustering (spatial SD = 0.06; 95% CrI: 0.01–0.20), highlighting geographically concentrated pockets of individuals at highest risk of disease progression.

Together, these hyperparameter estimates indicate that HIV prevalence and CD4-defined advanced immunosuppression exhibit comparable short-range spatial dependence, with clustering operating primarily at community-level scales (within approximately 20 km). The relatively smaller EA-level variance compared with the SPDE spatial field suggests that continuous geographic variation, rather than purely administrative-area differences, accounts for much of the remaining unexplained heterogeneity.

Spatial heterogeneity in HIV prevalence

The spatial random field derived from the EA + SPDE model revealed pronounced geographical variation in HIV prevalence. The posterior exceedance probability map (Figure S5) identifies EAs where the estimated prevalence exceeds the regional mean threshold, highlighting areas of elevated risk after spatial smoothing. To further characterize the spatial extent and intensity of high-prevalence areas, the continuous exceedance grid map (Figure S6) illustrates localized clusters of elevated risk across the study area. Grid cells with exceedance probabilities greater than 0.8 were interpreted as having significantly higher HIV prevalence, while probabilities below 0.2 indicated lower-risk areas.

Spatial clustering was further assessed using the Getis-Ord Gi∗ statistic (Figure 3), which identified nine statistically significant hotspot clusters (Gi∗>1.96) and fifteen coldspot clusters (Gi∗<−1.96). Hotspots were concentrated primarily in the northwestern and southwestern sectors of the municipality, whereas coldspots were more prevalent along the southern margins, revealing a northwest-south gradient in HIV risk intensity. Although areas of high exceedance probability were observed across multiple grid cells, only a subset formed statistically significant Gi hotspots, reflecting the fact that elevated prevalence does not necessarily imply spatial clustering when neighboring values are considered.

Sensitivity analyses across exceedance probability thresholds of 0.75, 0.8, and 0.9 (Table S5) showed that three hotspot clusters and eight coldspot clusters were consistently identified, with the remaining grid cells classified as not significant. This indicates that the observed spatial clustering is robust to reasonable changes in threshold selection. Together, the exceedance probability maps and Gi∗ analyses demonstrate that the identified hotspots reflect genuine localized elevations in HIV prevalence rather than artefacts of threshold choice or uneven EA distribution.

Finally, these results suggest that spatially structured factors, such as population mobility, access to health services, and socioeconomic variation, contribute to the clustering of HIV infection across Vulindlela and Greater Edendale.

Model diagnostics and performance

To evaluate model adequacy, convergence, and predictive reliability, a series of diagnostic checks were conducted for all fitted models, including the geostatistical HIV prevalence model (EA + SPDE), the continuous CD4 model (log-transformed CD4 count), and the binary CD4 < 200 cells/μL model.

Convergence and calibration

Posterior convergence diagnostics indicated stable model estimation across all models. Near-zero Kullback-Leibler divergence (KLD) values, minimal mean-mode differences, and smooth, unimodal posterior density distributions (Figures 4, 5, and 6) confirm numerical stability and reliable INLA optimisation.

Discussion

This study applied complementary geostatistical and hierarchical Bayesian frameworks to examine predictors and spatial clustering of HIV disease progression, measured by CD4 count and severe immunosuppression (CD4 <200 cells/μL), in KZN, South Africa. Across model specifications, consistent demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical determinants of disease progression were identified, while spatial analyses revealed localized clusters of elevated and reduced HIV risk.

Across all models, several covariates consistently emerged as credible predictors of HIV infection and disease progression. Age was a dominant factor: adults aged over 30 years exhibited the highest adjusted odds of HIV infection and advanced immunosuppression, alongside lower mean CD4 counts compared with adolescents. Male gender was linked to lower odds of HIV infection but higher likelihood of severe immunosuppression and reduced CD4 levels, reflecting delayed health-seeking and later entry into care. Socioeconomic factors, particularly salaried employment and pension support, were protective, corresponding to higher CD4 counts and slower immunologic decline. Clinically, ART use and viral suppression were associated with better immune recovery, while detectable viral load and TB co-infection predicted significantly lower CD4 counts and greater odds of advanced disease. Collectively, these patterns across the geostatistical and disease-progression models underscore the intertwined demographic, behavioral, and clinical drivers of HIV outcomes in KZN.

Overall, the results demonstrate that HIV risk and disease progression are shaped by both individual-level determinants and spatially structured contextual factors. While demographic, socioeconomic, behavioral, and clinical covariates explained much of the observed variation, residual short-range spatial clustering persisted after adjustment. This remaining spatial dependence likely reflects unmeasured local influences, such as access to healthcare, transmission networks, mobility, and community-level socioeconomic conditions. Together, these findings underscore the need to combine individual-focused interventions with geographically targeted, place-based strategies in high-burden settings.

In the CD4 < 200 cells/μL model, older age was consistently associated with severe immunosuppression, with individuals aged 30–44 years showing progressively higher odds of advanced disease compared with adolescents. This age gradient is consistent with national laboratory surveillance indicating that, although median CD4 cell counts have increased over time, the proportion of samples with CD4 ≤ 200 cells/μL has remained relatively stable, particularly among older adults and men who tend to present later for care.11 Similar patterns have been reported in analyses of AHD in South Africa, which document persistent late presentation among older populations despite universal treatment policies.18 Taken together, these findings suggest that older age is associated with greater likelihood of severe immunosuppression, potentially reflecting delayed testing and linkage to care among older adults.

Gender effects differed across model specifications. In the binary CD4 model, men had nearly double the odds of severe immunosuppression, consistent with prior studies reporting that men more frequently present with advanced disease and lower CD4 counts, often in the context of lower HIV testing uptake and delayed healthcare engagement.19,20 In contrast, in the spatial EA + SPDE model, men were less likely to be classified with adverse outcomes, which may reflect higher background HIV prevalence among women observed in population-based surveys.3 Similar discrepancies across modelling frameworks have been documented elsewhere and likely reflect differences between markers of disease progression and population-level prevalence surfaces.

Stable income sources, including salary or wage employment and pensions or social grants, were associated with lower odds of low CD4 counts. This pattern is consistent with evidence linking socioeconomic stability to improved engagement with HIV care and more favorable immunologic profiles.21,22,23 Conversely, economic insecurity has been repeatedly associated with later presentation and interruptions in care in observational studies.

A prior TB diagnosis was associated with lower CD4 levels, in keeping with the well-documented overlap between TB and AHD. Programmatic and surveillance analyses consistently report high TB prevalence among individuals with severe immunosuppression,24 supporting the observed association and highlighting the importance of integrated TB-HIV screening and management strategies in populations with advanced disease.

The observed positive association between ART use and low CD4 likely reflects confounding by indication, where individuals initiating ART at late disease stages are captured before immune recovery. Similar trends have been described in South African cohorts, where substantial proportions of ART initiators begin treatment with CD4 < 200 cells/μL despite universal test-and-treat policies.19,25 Conversely, viral non-suppression was strongly associated with low CD4, consistent with longitudinal evidence that ongoing viremia hinders immune reconstitution and accelerates disease progression.26,27 Importantly, the association between ART use and low CD4 should not be interpreted as a harmful or causal effect of treatment. Rather, this pattern reflects late treatment initiation and reverse causality, whereby individuals initiating ART are more likely to present with advanced immunosuppression prior to immune recovery. In this cross-sectional setting, ART exposure therefore captures treatment history and clinical indication rather than treatment efficacy.

The use of CD4 cell count in this analysis should be interpreted cautiously. CD4 levels are not treated as direct or causal measures of time since HIV infection, but rather as imperfect, population-level indicators of disease progression and clinical severity. Biologically, untreated HIV infection is characterized by progressive immune depletion over time, making CD4 informative, on average, about stage of disease. However, this relationship is substantially modified by ART initiation, viral suppression, treatment adherence, and individual heterogeneity in immune response. In cross-sectional data, low CD4 among individuals on ART may reflect delayed diagnosis or late treatment initiation rather than prolonged infection duration, representing a classic example of confounding by indication. Accordingly, CD4-based indicators in this study are used descriptively to characterize spatial variation in immunological severity among prevalent infections, rather than to infer individual-level infection timelines. ART exposure and viral suppression were modelled separately to help distinguish spatial patterns related to treatment access from those associated with advanced immunosuppression, although residual confounding cannot be fully eliminated. Consequently, the spatial patterns identified should be interpreted as reflecting gradients of clinical severity and treatment delay, rather than precise estimates of time since infection.

Behavioral and psychosocial factors were also strongly associated with adverse HIV outcomes. Alcohol use, sexual debut, multiple partnerships, and forced first sex were significantly associated with poorer HIV-related indicators. Hazardous drinking has consistently been associated with poor ART adherence and immunologic decline in prior studies, potentially operating through behavioral and psychosocial mechanisms rather than direct causal pathways.28,29 Multiple sexual partnerships and early sexual debut likely indicate increased exposure within high-prevalence sexual networks, consistent with findings from other South African spatial analyses of HIV clustering.3,30 The positive association between reported condom use and adverse outcomes is consistent with reverse causality, whereby individuals already diagnosed with HIV or perceiving higher risk are more likely to report condom use.31 Similarly, forced first sex and sexual violence have been associated with increased HIV vulnerability and poorer treatment engagement, potentially mediated by long-term psychological and social stressors.32,33 Taken together, these associations highlight the need for integrated HIV responses that address substance use, gender-based violence, and sexual health alongside biomedical care.

Education and marital status effects followed expected gradients. Individuals with lower educational attainment and single or non-partnered status were more likely to exhibit adverse outcomes, consistent with literature linking social disadvantage and weaker health literacy to poorer engagement in HIV services.34 Marriage or stable partnerships may offer psychosocial support that facilitates adherence and viral suppression, while low education often limits health system navigation.

Mobility and short duration of residence were associated with lower CD4 in the continuous model, corroborating research showing that newly mobile or recently settled individuals often face fragmented care, interrupted ART, and poorer treatment engagement.35,36 In rural hyper-endemic settings, migration events have been particularly common among HIV-positive persons,37 further highlighting how population movement and transience contribute to suboptimal immune recovery and disease progression.

Spatial modelling revealed modest but distinct short-range clustering of advanced immunosuppression, with an estimated median spatial range of approximately 20 km. Using Getis-Ord Gi∗ statistics, we identified nine significant hotspot clusters (Gi∗ > 1.96) and fifteen coldspot clusters (Gi∗ < −1.96). Hotspots were mainly located in the northwestern and southwestern sectors of the study area, indicating localized concentrations of elevated HIV prevalence, whereas coldspots were primarily situated toward the southern margins, representing zones of lower prevalence. This spatial pattern supports growing evidence from spatial epidemiology and phylogenetic studies showing micro-scale heterogeneity in HIV transmission and disease progression across KZN.5,38,39

The identification of discrete clusters underscores the value of spatially explicit approaches for epidemic control. Hotspot-targeted interventions, such as intensified testing, community ART delivery, and linkage programmes, have demonstrated cost-effectiveness and improved outcomes when deployed in localized high-risk settings.13,39

The estimated median spatial range of approximately 20 km suggests that residual clustering of HIV prevalence and advanced immunosuppression operates at local community scales rather than broad administrative levels. This spatial scale is consistent with clinic catchment areas, routine mobility patterns, and shared healthcare environments, implying that geographically targeted interventions, such as community-based testing, decentralized ART delivery, and outreach focused on high-risk neighborhoods, may be more effective than district-wide approaches. These findings support precision public health strategies that prioritize micro-geographic targeting within identified hotspot zones.

Overall, this study integrates individual, behavioral, and spatial determinants to provide a comprehensive understanding of HIV disease progression in KZN. The convergence of demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical factors underscores the multifactorial nature of AHD. By coupling Bayesian geostatistical modelling with disease progression indicators, this work generates actionable insights for refining HIV surveillance and guiding geographically targeted interventions aimed at improving prevention and care outcomes.

Public health implications

Our results support three key policy priorities for strengthening South Africa’s HIV response. First, targeted testing and early treatment strategies should prioritize older adults and men to address persistent age- and sex-based disparities in disease progression. Second, integrating TB prevention and screening within HIV programs remains critical to forestall immune decline. Third, addressing structural barriers through transport support, social grants, and economic interventions could enhance access and retention in care.

The observed spatial clustering further highlights opportunities for geographically targeted service delivery, consistent with global shifts toward precision public health. While CD4 testing is no longer required for ART initiation under universal test-and-treat policies, maintaining CD4 surveillance remains vital for identifying individuals at risk of opportunistic infections.40 The relatively short spatial range observed in our models (≈20 km) suggests that residual HIV-related heterogeneity operates over community-level distances, supporting strategies that concentrate interventions within small, high-risk catchments.

Building on these spatial insights, hotspot identification can guide specific programmatic actions. These include deploying mobile or community-based HIV-testing units in high-prevalence areas, prioritizing rapid ART initiation and differentiated service delivery for populations at greatest risk, integrating TB screening and treatment within co-affected communities, and targeting CD4 monitoring to detect advanced immunosuppression. By translating geospatial evidence into actionable strategies, programs can allocate resources more efficiently and maximize impact at the community level.

Strengths of study

A major strength of this study lies in its application of a Bayesian geostatistical point-process (SPDE) framework, which accounts for spatial correlation structures while simultaneously adjusting for individual-level covariates. By jointly analyzing both binary (CD4 <200 cells/μL) and continuous (log-transformed CD4) outcomes, we captured distinct dimensions of HIV disease progression, offering a more nuanced view of immune suppression. The inclusion of behavioral and socioeconomic covariates further strengthened the analysis by highlighting structural and individual-level determinants of disease dynamics.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. The use of cross-sectional data limits inference on temporal dynamics and precludes causal interpretation of CD4 decline and treatment effects. CD4 cell count and ART status were used as population-level proxies of disease progression rather than direct measures of infection timing, and are influenced by treatment initiation, adherence, viral suppression, and individual immune response. Although multiple imputation was used to address missing data, some residual bias may remain. Important factors such as ART adherence, duration on treatment, co-infections, and viral subtype variation were not explicitly modelled, contributing to potential residual confounding. In addition, spatial analyses were conducted at enumeration-area level, which may mask finer-scale heterogeneity within communities. Finally, the data reflect epidemiological conditions from earlier survey periods and may not fully represent current HIV prevalence patterns.

Future directions

Future analyses could leverage longitudinal data and updated spatial datasets to better characterize temporal evolution and spatial variation in HIV burden and disease progression.

Sources of data and study population

This study draws on secondary data obtained from two consecutive population-based cross-sectional surveys conducted under the HIV Incidence Provincial Surveillance System (HIPSS). The HIPSS programme was designed to monitor temporal changes in HIV prevalence and incidence in KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa. The first survey was implemented between 11 June 2014 and 18 June 2015, followed by the second between 8 July 2015 and 7 June 2016. Data collection was undertaken in two contrasting communities within the uMgungundlovu District, Vulindlela, a predominantly rural area, and Greater Edendale, a peri-urban locality.

In total, 20,048 participants aged 15–49 years were included across both survey waves, 9,812 individuals (6,265 women and 3,547 men) in 2014, and 10,236 (6,431 women and 3,805 men) in 2015. To address incomplete information (missing data), multiple imputation was performed using the mice package in R, applying suitable models for both categorical and continuous variables. All participants were retained in the final analytical dataset. Although some bias cannot be entirely excluded, the robust sampling design and appropriate handling of missing data enhance the credibility and generalisability of the results.

The study focused on individuals aged 15–49 years, aligning with international HIV surveillance standards and UNAIDS reporting conventions. This age group encompasses the most sexually active and economically productive segment of the population, who bear a disproportionate share of HIV, tuberculosis (TB), and sexually transmitted infection (STI) burdens. Concentrating on this demographic ensures comparability with national and global estimates while providing policy-relevant insights for targeted intervention strategies in high-prevalence settings such as KwaZulu-Natal.

Method details

Data collection and quality assurance

To ensure representativeness, HIPSS employed a multistage probability sampling design. Out of 600 available EAs, 591 with at least 50 households were eligible for selection. From these, 221 EAs were randomly chosen in 2014 and 203 in 2015. Within each EA, households were systematically sampled, and one eligible individual per household was randomly selected after obtaining written informed consent.41 Geographic coordinates were recorded for each sampled household to enable spatial analyses and minimize potential selection bias.

Comprehensive quality assurance procedures were implemented throughout field operations. Daily supervision was carried out during the first month of data collection, followed by monthly reviews for six months and quarterly assessments thereafter. Data were captured using the Mobenzi Researcher system (Durban, South Africa), which allowed real-time oversight of fieldwork, data accuracy, and adherence to study protocols. Automated validation checks within the system identified inconsistencies for immediate correction. All biological samples were processed in accredited laboratories, providing laboratory-confirmed HIV test results to ensure epidemiological validity. Final datasets underwent centralized quality control and verification prior to analysis.41

Variable definition and preprocessing

This study examined spatial patterns of HIV prevalence using geostatistical modelling informed by both biological and contextual variables. In this study, CD4 cell count is interpreted as a marker of immune status and stage of HIV disease rather than a direct measure of time since infection. While lower CD4 levels generally reflect more advanced disease, CD4 is influenced by antiretroviral therapy, viral suppression, survival bias, and health-seeking behaviour. Accordingly, CD4-based measures are used as imperfect temporal proxies to provide cross-sectional insight into disease progression, with ART use and viral load status included to account for treatment-related effects.

To capture aspects of disease progression within the cross-sectional framework, temporal proxies were derived from immunological markers of HIV infection. Specifically, CD4 cell count was used as a key indicator of immune system status and disease severity. Two CD4-based proxies were included: a continuous log-transformed measure of CD4 count and a binary variable distinguishing individuals with CD4 counts below or above 200 cells/mm3. The 200 cells/mm3 CD4 threshold is widely recognized as a clinical marker of advanced immunosuppression and is commonly used to indicate progression to AIDS.42 These proxies provided a temporal dimension for interpreting HIV burden and immune function across space, compensating for the lack of longitudinal data.

The analysis incorporated covariates across multiple domains (Table S1). Demographic factors included age group, sex, marital status, educational attainment, and main source of income. Behavioural factors comprised alcohol consumption, ever having had sexual intercourse (“sex ever”), age at sexual debut, number of lifetime sexual partners, and condom use at last sexual intercourse. Sex ever captures exposure to sexual debut, whereas condom use reflects protective behaviour conditional on sexual activity; these variables represent distinct constructs and were therefore retained as separate covariates.

Structural factors reflected socioeconomic vulnerability and access to healthcare, encompassing food insecurity (assessed through skipped meals, reduced meal size, and running out of money for food), experience of forced first sexual intercourse (measured through a self-reported survey item asking whether the respondent’s first sexual experience was against their will), migration status (living away from home), and degree of community attachment (length of residence in the community).

Access to healthcare was measured as self-reported utilization of health services within the preceding 12 months and includes both general healthcare visits and HIV-related services, including ART clinic attendance. In addition, two binary indicators, current antiretroviral (ARV) treatment status and viral load suppression, were included to explicitly account for engagement in HIV care and treatment.

Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) derived from a non-spatial logistic regression model. Despite conceptual overlap between some behavioural and structural variables (e.g., sexual behaviour indicators and food insecurity measures), all adjusted VIF values were below 2, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity among the included covariates.

Geostatistical modelling of HIV prevalence

To account for spatial dependence and heterogeneity in HIV prevalence across EAs, we employed Bayesian hierarchical logistic regression within the SPDE framework using R-INLA. This approach approximates the underlying continuous spatial random field as a Gaussian Markov random field (GMRF) through a triangulated mesh, enabling efficient representation and computation of spatial processes.

A two-dimensional mesh covering the study area was constructed to serve as the spatial domain for the model. The mesh was refined in regions with higher EA density, allowing better capture of local variation while maintaining computational efficiency. The resulting mesh comprised 713 nodes, with minimum and maximum edge lengths of 0.030° (∼3.3 km) and 0.101° (∼11.2 km), respectively, ensuring adequate spatial resolution while avoiding computational overload. The vertices of the mesh define reference points for approximating the continuous Gaussian field, which facilitates estimation of HIV prevalence at both sampled and unsampled locations throughout the study area (Figure S2). A detailed description of the full modelling workflow, including mesh construction, SPDE specification, prior distributions, fixed effects, and posterior exceedance probability computation, is provided in Supplementary Methods S1.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted using the full EA + SPDE model specification to assess the robustness of spatial parameter estimates to mesh resolution. In addition to the primary mesh (minimum edge length ≈ 3.3 km, maximum edge length ≈ 11.2 km, cutoff ≈ 3.3 km), a coarser mesh (minimum edge length ≈ 4.4 km, maximum edge length ≈ 22.2 km, cutoff ≈ 4.4 km) and a finer mesh (minimum edge length ≈ 1.1 km, maximum edge length ≈ 5.6 km, cutoff ≈ 1.1 km) were fitted. Estimated spatial ranges were stable across meshes, varying between approximately 7.1 km (fine mesh) and 13.6 km (coarse mesh), with minimal variation in the marginal standard deviation of the spatial field (0.23–0.24). These results indicate that inference on residual spatial dependence is robust to reasonable mesh choices when accounting jointly for EA-level and continuous spatial random effects (Table S2).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Bayesian spatial modelling and model comparison

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.0) using the Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA) framework for Bayesian hierarchical inference. HIV status and CD4-derived outcomes were modelled at the individual level with fixed effects for demographic, behavioural, and structural covariates together with spatial random effects. For HIV prevalence, three competing spatial model specifications were evaluated: (i) an EA-only model incorporating unstructured EA random effects, (ii) an SPDE-only model representing continuous spatial dependence, and (iii) a combined EA + SPDE model including both discrete and continuous spatial components. Model performance was compared using the Watanabe–Akaike Information Criterion (WAIC), Deviance Information Criterion (DIC), and mean log score (MLS), with lower values indicating improved fit and predictive performance.

For CD4-derived disease progression proxies, Gaussian models were fitted for log-transformed CD4 counts, while Bernoulli models with logit links were fitted for binary immunosuppression thresholds (CD4 < 200 cells/μL and CD4 < 350 cells/μL). Antiretroviral treatment status and viral load suppression were included as covariates to account for treatment-related effects on CD4 trajectories. Binary model performance was evaluated using WAIC and DIC.

Sampling weights (weight_individual) were incorporated through the INLA weights argument to obtain population-representative estimates. Weights were normalized to sum to the total sample size and were applied at the likelihood level. Consistent with current INLA implementation, weighting was not applied directly to latent random effects. Spatial clustering and unequal sampling density were additionally accounted for through the inclusion of EA random effects and continuous SPDE spatial fields.

Model diaglistanostics and posterior assessment

Model adequacy and predictive reliability were assessed using Conditional Predictive Ordinate (CPO) and Probability Integral Transform (PIT) diagnostics. Uniform PIT distributions and the absence of extreme log(CPO) values were interpreted as evidence of satisfactory predictive calibration. Posterior stability and convergence were evaluated through comparison of posterior means and modes and by examining Kullback–Leibler divergence (KLD) values, with values near zero indicating stable posterior approximations. Posterior density distributions of fixed effects and spatial hyperparameters, including range, variance, and precision parameters, were further inspected to confirm smoothness, unimodality, and overall model stability.
