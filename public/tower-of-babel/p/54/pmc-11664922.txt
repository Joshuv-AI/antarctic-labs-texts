Ubiquitin's Conformational Heterogeneity as Discerned by Nuclear Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy
Authors: David Beriashvili, Gert E. Folkers, Marc Baldus
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractVisualizing a protein's molecular motions has been a long standing topic of research in the biophysics community. Largely this has been done by exploiting nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (NMR), and arguably no protein's molecular motions have been better characterized by NMR than that of ubiquitin (Ub), a 76 amino acid polypeptide essential in ubiquitination‐a key regulatory system within cells. Herein, we discuss ubiquitin's conformational plasticity as visualized, at atomic resolution, by more than 35 years of NMR work. In our discussions we point out the differences between data acquired in vitro, ex vivo, as well as in vivo and stress the need to investigate Ub's conformational plasticity in more biologically representative backgrounds.

Ubiquitin, Ubiquitination, and Ubiquitin's Conformational Heterogeneity

Research over the last 50 years has firmly established that post‐translational modifications (PTMs) are critical modulators of cellular order and function.[ 1 , 2 , 3 ] Although diverse, PTMs function by altering a substrate's intrinsic molecular motions facilitating novel interactions and triggering specific cellular outcomes (see, e. g. Ref.[ 4 , 5 ]) Our understanding of these induced structural modulations has predominately come from static snapshots obtained by X‐ray crystallography and more recently cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo‐EM). However, a significant drawback of static snapshots is that they report only on changes in a motion's amplitude leaving us blind to motional rates which are now emerging as an important factor in facilitating intermolecular (e. g., protein‐protein) interactions.[ 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 ]

Given the importance of visualizing both the temporal and spatial aspects of protein motion, tremendous efforts have been expended to acquire such data by nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (NMR), room‐temperature X‐ray crystallography, and more recently cryo‐EM.[ 8 , 9 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 ] In the current context, we will largely focus on the use of NMR given the relative ease of acquiring data without the challenge of protein crystallization and sample freezing in cryo‐EM at sufficiently fast cooling rates [19] which, in the latter case, may limit the ability to detect ps to ns motions which are known to be critical for protein function. [20]

Nearly all NMR methods reporting on protein motion have been debuted on ubiquitin (Ub), an essential player in ubiquitination.[ 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 ] These NMR derived structural insights taken together with an ever increasing wealth of biochemical data pertaining to ubiquitination have facilitated an nearly unprecedented understanding of Ub's molecular motions.[ 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 ] Herein, we present a summary of these observations as revealed by NMR. Subsequently, we raise the point that despite all this effort our understanding of these events is likely limited given that most of the studies were performed in vitro and not in a complex cellular environment. Finally, we discuss the important role in vivo NMR will play in helping us better understand Ub's motion's and possibly their relation to function.

Brief Overview of Ubiquitination

Ubiquitination constitutes a complex regulatory system involved in modulating nearly all facets of cellular function from protein turnover and homeostasis control to cellular division.[ 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 ] Ubiquitination involves the reversible post‐translational modification of substrates, predominantly proteins with Ub, which itself is a highly conserved 76 amino acid protein encoded for by a multi‐gene family. Ub is synthesized de novo either as a monomeric‐ribosomal protein precursor fusion (encoded by the UBA52/80 gene) or as a covalently‐linked polymer comprising 3 to 10 Ubs linked head to tail (encoded for by the UBB and UBC genes).[ 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 ]

Substrate conjugation is facilitated through the collective action of three enzyme classes respectively termed ubiquitin‐activating enzymes (E1), ubiquitin‐conjugating enzymes (E2), and ubiquitin ligases (E3). [44] E1 enzymes, of which there are 2 within humans, serve to activate free monomeric Ub through ATP hydrolysis, subsequently transferring it to an E2.[ 45 , 46 ] The human genome encodes approximately 40 different E2 enzymes, which when charged with Ub either directly transfer it to a substrate or complex with an E3 enzyme. [47] E3 s are by far are the most populous group of enzymes, with more than 600 encoded within humans, and are predominately responsible for substrate‐specific Ub tagging. [48] Collectively these enzymes work to link a substrate's nucleophilic primary amine side chain to Ub's C‐terminal glycine via an iso‐peptide bond.

The degree of substrate ubiquitination ranges greatly–from mono‐ubiquitination to decoration with multiple monomeric, or even poly‐Ub species with the latter colloquially known as chains. Chains comprise multiple Ubs linked together via iso‐peptide linkages formed between the C‐terminus of a distal Ub and one of 7 internal lysine moieties (K6, K11, K27, K29, K33, K48, and K63) or the N‐terminal methionine of a proximal Ub (Figure 1A).[ 1 , 44 ] Ub chains can either be homotypic (Ub's are linked through the same lysine) or heterotypic (Ub's are linked through different lysines).[ 34 , 49 , 50 ] Recent work has further revealed the existence of non‐canonical ubiquitination where Ub is tethered to substrates through its serine or threonine side‐chain hydroxyl groups. [1] Finally, Ub can also be post‐translationally modified itself–e. g., phosphorylation and acetylation amongst a host of other modifications.[ 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 ]

Experimental evidence clearly shows that the large array of Ub modifications constitutes a code with each modification eliciting a highly specific outcome.[ 1 , 44 ] For example, substrates conjugated with K48‐linked chains are targeted for degradation by the proteosome, whereas those conjugated with K63‐linked chains are involved in non‐proteasomal events such as DNA damage response, and monomeric Ub phosphorylated at position 65 is involved in mitophagy.[ 34 , 56 , 57 ]

Ubiquitination is regulated by nearly 100 deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs). The discovery of which was spurred on by early experimental evidence indicating that cellular homeostasis was reliant on maintaining a near constant concentration of free monomeric Ub, without a concomitant increase in de novo Ub biosynthesis.[ 58 , 59 , 60 ] This taken with the observation that cellular Ub was produced as a polymer suggested that cells produced specific enzymes with specificity for iso‐peptide bonds. With time it emerged that the production, distribution, and abundance of Ub was indeed tightly controlled by the collective action of nearly 100 DUBs sre which are sub‐divided into 2 families (cysteine and metalloproteases) and specifically recognize ubiquitinated substrates cleaving their iso‐peptide linkages in order to maintain requisite Ub levels.[ 37 , 49 , 50 , 58 , 60 , 61 , 62 , 63 ]

Structural Aspects of Ubiquitin and Ubiquitination

The question of how Ub selectively interacts with E1, E2, E3 enzymes or with other substrates in both its free and conjugated forms is one that arose naturally with answers being sought through investigation of Ub's structural properties. Ub is unique, in that it is one of the largest PTMs in terms of size. Early NMR and X‐ray crystallography structures revealed that Ub adopted a canonical β‐grasp fold comprising 5 β‐sheets, a single α‐helix, and 310‐helix (Figure 1A) with a collective solvent exposed surface area exceeding 8000 Å2.[ 42 , 64 ] Structural studies of monomeric Ub and mono/poly ubiquitinated substrates revealed that recognition occurs by targeting various Ub surfaces,[ 28 , 33 , 49 , 50 , 57 , 61 , 63 , 65 , 66 , 67 , 68 , 69 , 70 , 71 , 72 , 73 ] thus broadly implicating both Ub's size and its ability to form higher‐order conjugates as determinants in recognition.

Early experimental evidence strongly suggested that the above explanation was not all encompassing. In 1989, Crooke's group, building on the 1987 NMR work of Weber et al., engineered 2 Ub disulfide cross‐linking mutants with cysteines at positions 4/14 and 4/66 respectively. Subsequent testing of their viability in E1 ubiquitination and proteolysis assays[ 74 , 75 ] revealed that while the 4/14 mutant behaved identical to wild type Ub (WT−Ub), the 4/66 mutant was 80 % less proteolytically active. Consequently, a series of NMR experiments were performed to determine whether disulfide cross‐linking induced structural changes could explain this observation. NMR data showed no significant structural perturbations, leading the authors to speculate that 4/66 crosslinking, which happened across a large surface area, likely constrained Ub's motions. With time, as more structure elucidation techniques matured renewed efforts to study Ub's dynamics were made.[ 22 , 23 , 76 , 77 ] At an atomic‐level the vast majority of the insights, both for free monomeric Ub and its higher order assemblies including chains were obtained by NMR.

Conformational Heterogeneity and Dynamics of Free Monomeric Ubiquitin as Revealed by NMR

The discovery and characterization of Ub by biochemical and molecular biology means was concomitant with the development of NMR methodologies. Ub presented as an attractive test‐system for the NMR community due to its small size, well dispersed signals, biological importance, ease of recombinant production, and purification at scale. [78] Consequently, Ub has featured prominently in NMR literature including in papers detailing the development of 3D structure determination methods, pulse sequences enabling interrogation of the temportal and spatial aspects of protein motions, and structural ensemble elucidation workflows.[ 22 , 23 , 26 , 77 , 79 , 80 , 81 , 82 ] The following section details insights garned into Ub's structural plasticity by the various sub‐fields of NMR.

Free Monomeric Ubiquitin in Solution

Early NMR studies revealed Ub to be highly rigid, thermally stable, and resistant to pH‐dependent denaturation.[ 83 , 84 ] The first 3‐dimensional (3D) structure of Ub in solution was solved by the Bax group in 1998 (Figure 1A) through a combination of nuclear Overhauser effect (NOE‐ through space) and residual dipolar couplings restraints (RDCs‐PDB ID: 1D3Z). [64]

The initial foray into solution Ub conformational dynamics was made in 1992. [24] Backbone amide spin relaxation and Nuclear Overhauser effect (NOE) measurements interpreted via Lipari‐Szabo formalism, suggested that Ub exhibited fast dynamics (sub‐nanosecond) with the degree of motion correlating with the degree of backbone amide/peptidyl carbonyl hydrogen bonding. The last 4 C‐terminal residues were found to be nearly unrestricted in their motion. Subsequent, backbone amide T1/T2 spin relaxation and NOE measurements performed by Tjandra et al., also revealed unrestricted motion for the last 4 C‐terminal residues, with L8‐T12 sampling motions on the order of 50 ps, and E18, I23, N25, and I36 being exchanged broadened. [25] Similar conclusions were also reached by Lienin et al. [85]

The idea that Ub interchanged between heterogenous conformations whilst maintaining the same general fold, on a ps to ns time scale, was unambiguously demonstrated by Lindorff‐Larsen et al. [23] who proposed a novel structure refinement methodology accounting for both structural restraints (NOE‐based) and dynamics information (encoded in Lipari‐Szabo formalism) to yield structural ensembles. For Ub, the approach resulted in 128 individual structures with significant Cα RMSDs (>1 Å) being observed for residues in the β1‐β2 loop, β4‐310 helix, and the C‐terminus regions. Consideration of the aggregate NMR data through the generalized Lindemann criterion, which reports on protein rigidity, lead Lindorff‐Larsen et al., to report that while Ub's backbone is highly rigid both its core and exterior side chain atoms exhibit liquid‐like properties. [23]

It soon became evident that protein motions (Figure 1B) occurring on the ps to ns timescales were too fast to be associated with the large conformational changes necessary for molecular recognition events.[ 80 , 86 ] Motions occurring on slower timescales (i. e., >ns) in Ub had already been observed in 1999 by de Alba et al., who reported that the conformational exchange broadening of I23 and N25 occurred on a >ns timescale. [87] Mills and Szyperski observed that in addition to I23 and N25, E24 and V70 sampled motions on >ns time scale. [88] Subsequently, Dittmer et al., reported that the exchange rate of I23’s conformational broadening was 4200 sec−1 ‐obtained via T2 relaxation dispersion experiments. [89] Interestingly, Massi et al., who conducted amide T1ρ relaxation dispersion experiments on Ub, reported that I23, N25, T55, and V70 exchanged at a rate of 25000 sec−1, suggesting that the conformational broadening of a single residue could be subject to several motional regimes simultaneously. [82] Within a few years technological advancements revealed that molecular dynamics simulation guided by an assortment of relaxation and RDC NMR data allowed to visualize Ub motions sampling μs timescales (Figure 1B).[ 86 , 90 , 91 , 92 , 93 , 94 , 95 ] In 2001 Tolman et al., reported that Ub's β1‐β2, α2‐β3, and C‐terminal loop regions underwent motions sampling μs timescales. [96] A more in‐depth exploration of Ub μs motions was presented by Lakomek et al., who showed that solvent exposed residues and those undergoing minimal backbone hydrogen‐bonding exhibited greater degrees of μs motion. [93]

Near simultaneously, the existence of distinct Ub conformations was reported by Kitahara et al. [97] Under high pressure conditions 2 Ub conformers exchanging at a rate of >104 sec−1 were observed (Figure 2). [98] The new conformer accounted for 15 % of Ub's population at 293 K/1 bar and was dubbed “active” because its last 6 C‐terminal amino acids versus the previously reported terminal 4 were observed to exhibit unrestricted motion. This novel finding was attributed to the destruction/weakening of backbone hydrogen bonds between the C‐terminal tail and β5 strand (67–72) residues. Hydrogen bond weakening/disruption was also observed for residues in the contiguous β‐strand face (β‐5‐67‐72, β1‐T7, and β4‐49‐50) a process likely driven by cavity expansion and possible water entry. In 2005, Kitahara et al., solved the 3D structures of the two conformers with the high pressure conformer having an expansive central cavity created by displacement of the α‐helix and C‐terminal tail in opposite directions (Figure 2). [99] The magnitude of displacement was significant with Cα root mean square deviations (RMSD) of up to 4 Å for residues in the α‐helix/following loop (26–40) and >4 Å for the 6 C‐terminal residues. The α‐helix's motion was observed to occur at the rate of 10 sec−1.

Given reports that several of Ub's segments exhibited motions occurring on the slower μs timescale, [100] Lange et al., performed an exhaustive set of RDC measurements complemented with molecular dynamics simulations (Figure 3A). [77] The acquired data was subsequently analyzed via a protocol accounting for both NOE and RDC restraints. [77] The largest motional amplitudes were observed for residues in the β1‐β2, β3‐β4, β4‐α2, and α2‐β4 loop regions. All RDC derived structures were readily found back in distinct Ub‐substrate high resolution X‐ray crystallography structures. Lange et al., took this to indicate that substrates specifically bound intrinsically populated Ub conformers, possibly through conformational selection. Lange et al., further reasoned that populating these conformations through stochastic motions would be entropically prohibitive, and that rather Ub cycled through the various conformers via a collective motion. Constrained molecular dynamics simulations (MD) revealed that Ub indeed exhibitted a collective pincer like motion that was centered on the β1‐β2 and β3‐α2 loops and took place on the timescale of hundreds of ns. Taken together, Ub solution‐state NMR studies indicate that free monomeric Ub is in a constant state of conformational flux with small amplitude (local) motions being coupled with slower larger amplitude collective ones (Figure 3A/B).

Crystalline Monomeric Unconjugated Ubiquitin

Much like the solution‐state NMR community, solid‐state NMR (ssNMR) researchers also heavily investigated Ub. For the ssNMR community, Ub was attractive due to ease of crystallization and wealth of existing spectroscopic data. Additionally, it exhibited a degree of conformational heterogeneity rarely seen in such a small system, thereby making it ideal for the development of ssMR methods reporting on molecular motions.

Ub's microcrystalline structure has been solved by various solid‐state NMR methods.[ 101 , 102 , 103 , 104 ] The structures share a similar fold to that observed for Ub in solution, with minor changes like the absence or presence of the 310‐helix, a motif with known conformational heterogeneity. In terms of discerning Ub's conformational heterogeneity, the first ssNMR studies focused on motions sampling time scales from ps to 10’s of μs. The findings mirrored those reported by the solution‐state community; namely, that Ub's side‐chain atoms had higher motional freedom than backbone atoms with the latter's mobility being correlated with the degree of backbone hydrogen bonding.[ 27 , 105 ]

Relaxation measurements on microcrystalline Ub performed by Schanda et al., are of note. They did not observe correlations for L8, T9, E24, N25, and for residue 72–76 in cross polarization based ssNMR experiments. [26] Transverse relaxation experiments revealed that residues neighboring those missing exhibited faster relaxation rates, implicating the ajoining regions in dynamic interplay. The same was observed for I23. Backbone amide coupling measurements revealed the presence of ns molecular motions within the β1‐β2, α1‐β3, and β4‐α2 loop regions. Similar to Lakomek et al., [93] Schanda et al., also observed that the motions of residues whose sidechains were oriented towards the protein's core were more restrained. [26]

SsNMR studies using microcrystalline Ub also revealed that different precipitating agents stabilized different Ub polymorphs.[ 108 , 109 , 110 , 111 , 112 ] The earliest observation of this was made by Seidel et al., who observed stark changes in correlation positions between Ub crystalized with poly‐ethylene glycol and 2‐methyl‐2,4‐pentanediol, respectively. The structures varied in backbone RMSD by 2.2 Å with appearance and disappearance of peaks in the β1‐β2 loop regions and chemicals‐shift perturbations for residues in nearly all other loop regions. Subsequently, Fasshuber et al., further reported that structural heterogeneity was even evident amongst crystals prepared with the same precipitating agent. [111] Based on ssNMR restraints Fasshuber et al., modeled Ub's structure, with the occupancy of the 10 lowest‐energy conformer's being best fit by a tri‐state model with regions exhibiting the greatest backbone RMSDs matching almost identically with Lange et al's., [77] solution‐state work.

Ma et al., further showed that backbone amide nitrogen relaxation rates were dependent on the precipitating agent and that the entirety of Ub exhibited a μs rocking motion which persisted regardless of the precipitant used. [105] Subsequently, Lakomek and Penzel et al., experimentally confirmed that the rocking motion's occurred at 1–2 μs with residues largely exhibiting a small amplitude of motion (up to 4° ‐assuming diffusion in a cone). [113] However, certain regions like the β1‐β2 loop were observed to exhibit amplitudes of motion between 6° to 14° which was interpreted to be evidence of the collective pincer like‐motion proposed by Lange et al. [77] Additionally, motions with an amplitude between 6° to 10° sampling a 1–2 μs timescale, were also observed for E24, D52 and G53 echoing Massi et al's., findings. [82] It is important to note that there are significant discrepancies in motional rates both amongst different solid‐state studies and more generally with solution‐state NMR work. These variations may be caused by the fact that different precipitating agents in complex with Ub preferentially form different crystal structures characterized by space groups with different protein‐lattice interactions.[ 114 , 115 ]

Functional Relevance of free Monomeric Ub Conformational Heterogeneity

Considering the above information the question naturally arises whether Ub's structural heterogeneity has a functional relevance especially in terms of substrate binding and if so, what are the mechanisms governing these interactions.

As mentioned earlier, Lange et al., [77] had reasoned that the probability of all Ub residues being in the perfect position for a binding event as a result of stochastic motions was small, and moreover that the entropic costs would be prohibitive. Consequently, they performed a principal component analysis and guided simulations which revealed the presence of a collective pincer‐like motional mode centered on the β1‐β2 and β4‐α2 loops (Figure 4A). They compared the conformational space sampled by Ub over the course of this motional mode with that sampled by Ub in substrate‐bound structures in the PDB. The comparison showed that collective motion permitted Ub to visit all conformations populated in the deposited high‐resolution crystallographic structures. Taken together, the data was taken to be strongly indicative of the idea that substrates bound an intrinsically populated high affinity Ub conformer‐a mechanism colloquially known as conformational selection.

The hypothesis that Ub undergoes collective motions was subsequently confirmed by other studies.[ 95 , 116 ] Of these, the perhaps most noteworthy showed that Ub exhibited collective motions occurring on different times scales, including the slower μs timescale.[ 95 , 117 ] This slower collective motion was centered on a peptidyl flip between D52 and G53 which disrupted G53’s hydrogen bond to E24 engendering a global allosteric switch (see also Figure 4B/C/D). Comparision with a bioinformatics screen revealed that the peptidyl‐flip mode structures were also readily found back in Ub‐substrate structures deposited in the PDB. Interestingly, already in 2005 Massi et al., had speculated that E24, D52, and G53 might be involved in a dynamic interplay. Notably, Huang et al., had also observed these motions on microcrystalline Ub already in 2011, but neither group concluded that they were observing concerted collective motion. [118]

While the idea that Ub exhibited collective motions became widely accepted, substrate binding to Ub through conformational selection has remained under discussion in the literature. Wlodarski and Zagrovic et al., conducted a multidimensional scaling comparison of Lange's et al's., [77] RDC structures with Ub‐substrate crystallographic structures. [119] The analysis revealed that the majority of RDC derived structures did not populate substrate‐bound conformations and those that did had significant RMSD differences particularly at the binding interfaces. The differences were taken to be an indication of an induced fit mechanism–one where the substrate initially bound a “close‐enough” Ub conformation with subsequent induced conformational changes leading to the desired final high affinity conformer. Experimental evidence of the induced‐fit mechanism was first reported by Philips et al. [76] Philips et al., set out to produce a highly potent Ub‐mimetic DUB inhibitor. They started by determining, via a bioinformatics screen, whether different DUB families discriminated between Ubs on the basis of conformation. The screen revealed that the two major families (Ub C‐terminal hydrolases‐UCH and Ub specific proteases‐USP) were selective for specific β1‐β2 loop conformations. Subsequently, a computational approach was used to determine a series of core residue mutations postulated to stabilize these specific loop conformations. The mutants were screened by using phage‐display with subsequent assays revealing several mutants with 50‐to‐250‐fold increase in USP affinity. Further interferometry experiments revealed binding kinetics that were best fit by an induced fit mechanism. The structures of these high affinity mutants were solved by X‐ray crystallography revealing that the mutants did not seemingly adopt the desired fixed β1‐β2 loop conformation. Subsequent backbone amide relaxation dispersion experiments revealed that these core mutants exhibited significantly slower dynamics which in part precluded obtaining definitive NMR restraints for the β1‐β2 loop region. The mutants were readily incorporated into chains both in vitro and in vivo, but could not replace endogenous Ub in yeast, thus suggesting that the rate of conformational interchange possibly plays an important function in modulating Ub's broader signaling.

The group that performed the work described in Philips et al., subsequently employed a similar strategy to create Ub‐mimetic inhibitors selective for USP7. [120] First, a set of Ub mutants with altered core residues was created followed by a second round of computation to identify a series of surface residue mutations (surface mutants). The high affinity core mutants’ β1‐β2 loop regions sampled a much wider range of conformations than WT Ub (interestingly not adopting the hinge‐like pincer motion proposed by Lange et al. [77] ). Room temperature crystallography data, acquired by the same group, but in a much later study confirmed that the core mutant exhibited a higher degree of conformational heterogeneity than WT Ub. [121] The surface mutant with the highest USP7 affinity was however not at all structurally heterogeneous, with its β1‐β2 loop adopting the predominate WT “up” conformation, a highly perplexing observation given that the β1‐β2 loop “down” conformation was the one allegedly preferred by USP7 as per the bioinformatics screen. Interestingly, the affinity mutant had more segments sampling slower μs dynamics further purporting the notion that the rate of conformational exchange might be an important modulator of biological function. Work along similar lines was also reported by Ernst et al., however in that paper the motional properties of Ub‐mutants were not investigated. [122]

Both solution and ssNMR data in complex with molecular dynamics simulations show that Ub is a structurally heterogenous protein that seemingly samples its conformational space through a series of collective motions occurring on the order of 100’s of ns to μs.[ 77 , 100 , 117 ] From the standpoint of substrate interactions, the structural bio‐informatic screens indicate that Ub's binding partners, like DUBs, select for specific Ub‐conformations thereby implicating Ub's structural flexibility in being a higher order control mechanism. [123] However, attempts to create conformationally restrained versions of Ub as tools to target specific target substrates, yielded experimental data that by in large contradicted the results obtained from the bioinformatic screens.[ 120 , 121 , 123 ] This taken with the non‐viability of these “restrained” mutants within yeast cells indicates that while conformational restriction is seemingly important, other factors also play a role in regulating Ub‐substrate interactions.[ 120 , 121 , 123 ] The fact that the various mutants exhibit a slowing of conformational dynamics suggests that this may have important functional implications, but further investigations are likely needed. It is further noteworthy that the Ub L43 A mutant, which exhibits a completely different cellular behavior than WT−Ub, also has more segments sampling slower motions relative to WT Ub. [124]

Conformational Heterogeneity and Dynamics of Polymeric Ub Species

Polymeric Ub assemblies known as Ub‐chains, serve as discrete signaling markers in ubiquitination pathways. [44] As discussed above, these chains are believed to differ in topology enabling highly selective substrate binding. This section discusses NMR derived insights which suggest that substrates not only target a specific topology but also differentiate between conformers.

NMR Based Structural Insights into K48 and K63 Ub Chains

Investigation of Ub‐chain structures by NMR was initiated to reconcile differences between the X‐ray crystallography structures for dimeric (Ub2) [125] and tetrameric (Ub4). [126] For K48‐linked Ub chains the X‐ray crystallography structures suggested a symmetrical dimer with interfacial contacts centered on L8, I44, and V70, whereas the tetramer was shown to adopt an open conformation with the distal moieties’ L8, I44, and V70 residues being solvent exposed. In 1997, Lam et al., attempted to reconcile these differences by using solution‐state NMR, but already in the first experiment they did not observe the closed conformation of K48‐Ub2 revealed by X‐ray crystallography. These conflicting findings spurred on further work by Varadan et al., who showed that K48‐Ub2 engendered a pH dependent conformational switch, where at acidic pH electrostatic repulsion limited interface formation resulting in an “open‐chain” conformation and at neutral or alkaline conditions dimished leading to collapse into a “closed” conformation with a symmetrical L8, I44, V70 solvent excluding interface being formed. [127] Further, Varadan et al., showed that at neutral pH, K48‐Ub4’s 2 distal moieties adopted a closed conformation with the proximal moiety remaining in the open conformation. [127]

Subsequently, Ryabov and Fushman utilized backbone amide NOE and transverse relaxation experiments to investigate the dynamic nature of K48‐linked Ub2 chains at both acidic and neutral pH (4.5 & 6.8). [128] At acidic pH there were significantly fewer non‐covalent interactions between the ubiquitin moieties (the “open” conformation). The fitting of all relaxation data, regardless of pH, was indicative of a model where the cognate K48‐Ub2 domains tumbled in unison whilst exhibiting interdomain motions occurring on a 10 ns timescale. In 2013, the Fushman group completed their research studies into K48‐chain structure by determining their conformational occupancy as a function of pH. [81] Through spare regularization of backbone amide residual dipolar couplings (RDCs) they came to the conclusion that the “closed” state of K48‐Ub2 was predominantly populated at physiological pH (around 65 %) with a sub‐set of “open” chain conformations being present. Interestingly, only open chain structures were found back in the PDB for substrate bound K48‐Ub2 structures which is suggestive of preferential conformer substrate selection.

In 2004, Varadan and Assfalg et al., reported that Ub‐chain topology was dependent on linkage type and that substrates discriminated on the basis of chain topology. [129] They reported that the solution structure of K63‐Ub2 was devoid of interfacial contacts, with chemical shift perturbations being observed only for C‐terminal residues in the distal Ub and lysine 63 in the proximal moiety. Correspondingly, the dimer was described to adopt an extended conformation with hydrophobic patch residues (L8, I44, and V70) being solvent exposed at physiological pH. To test whether linkage‐specific conformations influenced substrate binding, a pull‐down assay was preformed, where K48‐ and K63‐ dimeric and tetrameric chains were treated with the ubiquitin associated (UBA) domain of Rad23 (Rad23 is highly selective for K48‐Ub4). The results indicated that UBA‐Rad23 bound nearly an order of magnitude more to K48‐Ub4 than K63‐Ub4 chains, and 2 orders of magnitude more to K48‐Ub4 than to free monomeric Ub, thus supporting that idea the substrates could discriminate between different chains. A further experiment revealed that cognate Ubs within K63‐linked chains could be bound by substrates through their hydrophobic patches in line with notion that substrates target various topologies even within homotypic chain types. The observation that K63‐linked chains were elongated molecules devoid of a stable non‐covalent inter‐subunit interfaces was also reported by Tenno et al., through the use of NMR and small angle x‐ray scattering (SAXS). [130]

Ye et al., expanded on Fushman's work by utilizing Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) experiments to track changes in Ub‐chain conformation ensembles upon substrate addition. [131] They reported that substrates could remodel chain configuration or alternatively select for pre‐existing Ub conformations. [131] Liu and Jiang et al., obtained similar results through the combined use of NMR and small angle X‐ray scattering. [132] They concluded that K63‐Ub2 populated at least 2 “closed” conformations (collectively accounting for 30 % population) and a predominate singly populated open state. The closed states were found to be selected for by different zinc‐finger containing proteins which induced significant peak broadening in Ub upon binding. In our opinion this again serves as evidence that alterations in the rate of conformational interchange may play an important role in modulating Ub‐substrate binding. It is important to highlight that Liu and Jiang et al's, data was obtained with consideration of their earlier data showing that free monomeric Ub formed noncovalent dimers at high concentrations. [133] As an aside, in 2012 Liu et al., conducted 1H, 15N‐HSQC and backbone amide transverse relaxation experiments on increasing concentrations of Ub. [133] Analysis revealed that Ub formed non‐covalent dimers interfacing along their respective contiguous β‐sheet faces (residues 4–12, 42–52, and 62–71) with a KD of 4.9 mM. Paramagnetic relaxation experiments (PRE) revealed that the interaction was not static with cognate Ub's sampling a rather large conformational space. [133] Liu and Jiang et al’s., consideration of these earlier findings in their later work is important in a global sense has it confirms that the various events described by NMR are not due to non‐specific interactions, but rather to the ascribed specific structure dependent events.

NMR Based Structural Insights into Atypical Ub Chains

So far, NMR studies have predominantly been conducted on K48 and K63‐linked chains compared to atypical chains, namely those linked through K6, K11, K27, K29, and K33. We attribute this observation to the fact that K48 and K63 chains where discovered first.[ 61 , 62 , 134 ]

Backbone heteronuclear single quantum coherence (HSQC) and X‐ray crystallography experiments conducted on recombinantly produced 13C, 15N‐labelled K6‐Ub2 dimers, revealed formation of a compact dimer with an asymmetric interface with the Ubs interfacing through the β1‐β2 loop, residues 42–50 (I44 patch), and 68–74 of the proximal Ub, and β1‐β2 loop and 30–40 (I36 patch) of the distal moiety. [31] The lack of chemical shift perturbations for residues 42–50 in the distal Ub suggested that further chain propagation was possible. 1H, 15N‐HSQC measurements conducted on a K6‐Ub3 trimer, with exclusively the middle Ub 15N‐labelled, revealed perturbations for residues in both the I36 and I44 patches supporting this hypothesis. Moreover, the middle Ub spectra exhibited significant peak doubling and chemical shift perturbations for residues in the I44 patch, T13‐T14 and the β1‐β2 loop region. Since such observations where not previously observed for Ubs interacting through the I44 patch, Hospenthal et al., reasoned that this might be a hallmark of I36 patch‐based interactions. NMR experiments with a zinc‐finger Ub‐binding domain selective for Ub's I36 patch revealed exchanged broadening of β1‐β2 loop residues and very strong chemical shift perturbations for residues T12 through to T14 supporting this hypothesis. Hospenthal et al., thus proposed that interactions through the I36 patch seemingly involve L8 which located in the highly dynamic β1‐β2 loop, causing the loop to sample two distinct conformations‐loop‐in and loop‐out (Figure 5A).

1H, 15N‐HSQC experiments on recombinantly produced isotope‐labelled K11‐Ub2 dimers, revealed an interface involving residues 5–15, 29–36, 53, and 69–76 on the distal ubiquitin [28] with a pH engendered conformational switch leading to loss of interfacial contacts (centered on K27 and K33) under acidic conditions. [127] Significant perturbations were observed for L8 and V70 and ascribed either to iso‐peptide linkage induced conformational change or to the presence of higher order K11‐Ub polymers with an possible asymmetrical interface. Coupled with crystallography data, the chemical exchange broadening of E24 and G53 at pH 7.2 was attributed to interface formation induced conformational heterogeneity, possibly because of the peptidyl conformational switch centered on D52 and G53 (see. Refs.[ 117 , 118 ]). Interestingly, in 2013 Castañeda et al., reported that Boc‐Lys11‐Ub had nearly identical perturbations to K11‐Ub2, suggesting that the chemical shift perturbations reported by Bremms et al, [28] were likely caused by linkage formation rather than interdomain contacts.

In 2016, Castañeda et al., presented an in‐depth NMR‐based characterization of K27‐Ub2 chains. [71] They reported that in K27‐Ub2 chains both the distal and proximal moieties exhibited a significant number of peak perturbations, likely due to the buried nature of K27, but few indications of an interdomain interface. Interestingly, correlations ariasing for G53 and E24, which are generally reported to be exchange broadened, were quite prominent which is line with the notion of a conformational switch centered around these residues (see. Refs[ 117 , 118 ]). The distal and proximal moieties were observed to tumble as a single molecular moiety with no alterations in ps to ns dynamics. However, the C‐terminus of the distal moiety was surprisingly more rigid and an increase in μs dynamics for residues in the α‐helix and adjacent β‐strand on the proximal moiety was evident. Sparse‐linearization of RDC data revealed that K27‐Ub2 populated at least 2 major conformers; one where the hydrophobic patches of both Ubs were oriented towards each other and another where the proximal moieties’ hydrophobic patch was fully solvent exposed.

NMR‐based investigations of K29/K33 chains are few. Michel and Elliot et al., reported the first NMR‐based insights into K29/K33 chain structure. The spectra for both distal and proximal Ubs in K29 and K33 dimers generally mirrored those observed for Ub in K63‐linked dimers, suggesting that both chain types adopted open conformations. It is noteworthy that residues adjacent to the linkage point exhibited peak splitting, indicating the presence of slow (possibly on the second time scale) exchanging species. [135]

Recently, Schneider et al., performed an exhaustive solution NMR‐based investigation of Ub K6, K29, and K33 dimers with the goal of determining their respective conformational ensembles. [136] The dimers were produced through an azide‐alkyne cycloaddition yielding triazole‐linked dimers with the linkage type previously being found to be minimally invasive in nature. [136] The conformational ensembles were generated based on MD simulations and paramagnetic relaxation experiments with subsequent validation via amide exchange, chemical shift perturbation analysis, and backbone amide transverse relaxationexperiments. Analysis of the ensembles revealed thatdetermined dimer structues did not sample all conformations reported in substrate‐bound structures deposited in the PDB. This observation is noteworthy because it suggests that binding to these specific dimers also occurs through ligand‐induced conformational change in addition to conformational selection. Additionally, the similarity of PRE profiles, regardless of linkage type, was striking as it argues against existence of dominant conformers.

A Combined View of Ub Chain Structure and Dynamics through NMR

Polymeric Ub assemblies are a key part of the Ub code. Taken together the presented NMR data suggests that substrates interact with Ub chains through both topology and conformer selection‐much like observed for monomeric Ub. The structural and dynamic properties of cognate Ubs are influenced by the position and number of linkages and augmented with chain wide motions‐as an example, K48‐dimers tumble as single molecular [128] with substrates binding to both domains, whereas within K63 chains constituent Ub moieties behave like “beads on a string” [129] with substrates binding cognate Ub's. Observations that apo‐chain structures are found back in respective Ub‐chain‐substrate structures, but do not explain all substrate‐bound structures argues for both conformational selection and substrate induced conformational change binding mechanisms.

Conformational Heterogeneity and Dynamics of Post‐translational Modified Ub

Recent literature has revealed that Ub itself can be post‐translationally modified with modifications ranging from phosphorylation and acetylation to ADP‐ribosylation.[ 72 , 138 , 139 ] Emerging NMR evidence suggests that posttranslationally modified Ub molecules are structurally diverse and constitute discrete signaling classes.

Ub Phosphorylation

Studies have revealed that Ub's threonine and serine hydroxy sidechains can be phosphorylated in vivo.[ 51 , 61 , 140 ] Perhaps the most well known Ub phosphorylation event occurs on S65 (Ub−S65P) within the context of the PARKIN activation pathway.[ 138 , 140 ] To better understand the molecular underpinnings of Ub−S65P, Wauer et al., conducted an extensive NMR‐based study. [141] Biological activity assays and NMR showed that Ub monomers and K63‐chain are phosphorylated at residue 65 (Ub−S65P). Backbone amide HSQC experiments of Ub−S65P revealed significant peak multiplicity, indicative of two conformers exchanging at a rate of 2 s−1. Spectra of the major conformer (70 %) resemble that of WT Ub, whereas spectra of the minor conformation exhibi significant chemical shift perturbations. Similar chemical shift perturbations were observed for K63‐Ub−S65P containing dimers, trimers, and tetramers.

Backbone amide HSQC spectra of the minor conformer revealed a very pronounced peak for R74, which is generally weak for WT Ub due to chemical exchange. This taken together with TALOS+ predictions suggested that the minor conformer underwent a 2 amino acid register shift in the last β‐strand thereby retracting its C‐terminal tail. Subsequent, HNCO experiments with long transfer times (to detect inter β‐strand hydrogen bonds), revealed that the minor conformer had a completely altered backbone hydrogen bonding pattern especially for residues in the β1, β3 and β5 strands.

While Ub‐S65P was not observed to inhibit E1 enzyme activity it was observed to impair the function of certain E2 and E3 enzymes, thus suggesting that Ub phosphorylation may act a higher order regulation mechanism as it influences ubiquitination cascades beyond just the Parkin pathway. [141] To determine which conformer was causative a phospho‐mimetic Ub was produced with its spectra matching near perfectly with the major conformer. This result echoed those reported those by Philips et al., [76] showing that Ub core mutants could be incorporated into chains but were not sufficient as a molecular replacements of endogenous Ub.

Dong et al., further showed that Ub−S65P engenders a pH‐dependent conformational switch (Figure 5B/C). [137] At pH 7.4, 2 peaks were revealed by 1D 31P NMR experiments‐one representing the retracted state (register shifted) and another the WT conformation‐the former's intensity decreased upon addition of acid indicating that the retracted state's phosphate is unprotonated. FRET experiments of K63‐Ub−S65P chains revealed alterations in long and short range contacts as well as loss of FRET interactions upon titration with acid and base echoing Ye et al’s., findings that K63 chains populate a variety of conformations. [131]

In 2017 Gladkova et al., detected via chemical exchange saturation transfer (CEST) experiments that WT−Ub had a low populated conformer state (0.68 % at 328 K) whose spectra were nearly identical to that of the retracted Ub−S65P conformer. [32] To determine the functional relevance of the retracted state a retracted mutant was created with the mutant avidly binding PINK1. In situ phosphorylation assays revealed that the retracted mutant was phosphorylated nearly 2 orders of magnitude faster than WT−Ub, indicating that PINK1 preferred this nearly “invisible” conformer.

Ub Acetylation

It has recently emerged that Ub's lysine residues can be acetylated in vivo.[ 55 , 142 ] To investigate the structural aspects of Ub acetylation, Kienle et al., utilized genetic‐code expansion to recombinantly produce all 7 mono‐acetylated versions of Ub. [72] Backbone amide HSQC experiments revealed no peak multiplicity for any of the 7 acetylated Ubs, indicating that no slow exchanging conformers were populated. In the same work, Kienle et al., utilized Ub‐mutant based ubiquitination assays supplemented with NMR experiments to reveal that acetylation had different functional effects based on which lysine was acetylated. For example, K48 acetalytion acted as a charge shield limiting electrostatic based interactions, whereas acetylation of K11 induced allosteric changes in the I44 hydrophobic patch that seemingly induced significant changes in Ub's functionality.

Ub Carbamylation

In 2021, mass spectrometry and NMR experiments revealed that Ub's K6, K33, K48, and K63 were susceptible to carbamylation.[ 53 , 143 ] Carbamylation propensity and stability were observed to correlate with lysine side‐chain pKa. However, a thorough understanding of potential structural and functional implications is currently lacking.

A Summary of Ub PTM Conformational Heterogeneity

As discussed in Section 1.5, the post‐translational modification of Ub itself presents as a significant expansion of the Ub code. Solution‐state NMR studies all allude to structural alterations that seemingly influence Ub's function. However, a thorough understanding of induced dynamic alterations and their functional implications still requires further studies both in vitro as well as in a cellular setting to elucidate their biological relevance (vide infra).

In Cell NMR–A Novel Tool for Studying Ub In Situ

Significant experimental evidence gathered in vitro suggests that Ub is structurally heterogeneous. However, direct atomic‐level evidence of Ub conformational heterogeneity and corresponding functional relevance in vivo is largely lacking. Given that acquiring such data in vivo at atomic resolution is a persistent problem hindering the entire field of structural biology, the NMR community has made a concerted effort to establish methodologies and workflows that facilitate data acquisition in vivo. In keeping with NMR tradition these studies have heavily made use of Ub as a test system.[ 5 , 145 , 146 , 147 , 148 , 149 , 150 , 151 , 152 ]

Solution‐State NMR Studies of Ub in Cell

Ub has been utilized in a number of early in‐cell NMR studies, which for the purpose of this review will not be discussed as data was acquired either in bacteria or in Xenopus laevis embryos.[ 153 , 154 , 155 ] Ub is highly attractive as a model system for in cell studies due to its near unparalleled biophysical characterization and high cellular abundance which is important due to NMR being a low senstivity technique.[ 58 , 62 , 156 ] In the context of human cells, the first in cell NMR study utilizing Ub was performed by Inomata et al., in 2009,[ 151 , 155 ] who utilized solution‐state NMR to acquire an 1H‐15N HSQC of isotopically labelled Ub, with L8, I44, and V70 mutated to alanine (Ub‐3 A) delivered to cells through the action of a virally derived cell‐penetrating peptide ligated to Ub‐3 A's C‐terminus (via a peptide linker which subsequently turned out to be cleaved off by cellular DUBs). The cellular concentration of delivered Ub was gauged to be on the order of 20–30 μM. The decision to use Ub‐3 A was made because of solution‐state NMR's intrinsic size limitation; the mutations was sought to limit transient hydrophobic patch interactions that would have otherwise rendered Ub spectrally invisible; something observed in earlier work on Ub in Xenopus laevis eggs. [155] and was later also reported for Ub in bacterial lysates. [157] The in‐cell mutant Ub NMR spectrum compared well with an equivalent in vitro spectrum and later NMR work confirming that the 3D structure of Ub‐3 A (PDB ID 5zd0) [158] largely agrees with the wild‐type structure (PDB ID 1d3z). Inomata et al., further conducted backbone amide hydrogen‐deuterium exchange experiments to gauge conformational exchange rates within human cell lysates at various time points. The exchange rates for the mutant Ub lysates were 15–22 faster than in vitro, suggesting that Ub exhibits a higher degree of structural heterogeneity ex vivo than previously thought, likely through partaking in both specific and non‐specific interactions, although the effects of molecular crowding on the interaction rates cannot be excluded.

In 2015, Majumder et al., visualized isotope labelled Ub electroporated into HeLa cells after a period of recovery through the application of a TROSY modified pulse sequence. [149] The pulse sequence was specifically designed to optimally detect signals arising from slow tumbling molecular assemblies (>100 kDa).[ 159 , 160 ] Majumder et al., thus observed nearly all Ub correlations in cell with correlations for V5, K27, K29, and Q40 exhibited a significant increase in intensity relative to WT‐UB with D39 being exchange broadened. These spectral changes were attributed to quinary interactions taking place between Ub and RNA. To confirm this Ub was titrated with RNA in vitro. The resulting spectrum was identical to that recorded in cell. A further interesting observation was that many in cell correlations exhibited peak multiplicity which was taken to be indicative of conformational exchange occurring on at least a ms time scale; this observation was attributed to conformational exchange between free and conjugated Ub species, however no experimental evidence to support this hypothesis was provided.

Recently, Kadavath et al., observed peak multiplicity for Ub electroporated into cells, similarly ascribing this to conformational exchange occurring on a ms timescale. [161] However, Kadavath et al., recorded the various backbone amide HSQC spectra immediately following electroporation. This is a striking experimental choice given studies showing that post‐electroporation recovery is vital to facilitating endogenous treatment of electroporated Ub by the host cell.[ 162 , 163 ] As such recording spectra immediately post‐electroporation detected perhaps not the most biologically relevant state.

A significant draw back of all the aforementioned studies is that solution‐state NMR studies are restricted to macromolecules that tumble freely in the cytosol or undergo minimal weak interactions with other cellular components, in particular larger biomolecules or complexes. Given that such interactions are critical for the functioning of Ub, our group has sought to overcome these limitations by utilzing solid‐state NMR (ssNMR), which is less restricted to molecular size, to visualize the structural properties of proteins inside human cells. In an effort to even visualize the structural properties of lowly populated Ub modifications we have sought to increase sensitivity by utilizing dynamic nuclear polarization. [152] Our conjoined efforts have not only demonstrated that Ub can indeed be observed under DNP‐ssNMR conditions but, more recently, also suggest that Ub exhibits differential dynamics based on its sub‐cellular localization.[ 164 , 165 ]

Conclusions and Perspective to the Future

Structural evidence, predominately acquired under in vitro conditions by solution and solid‐state NMR implicates Ub's conformational plasticity in being a key determinant of its functionality. In its most basic form, free monomeric Ub can be thought of being in a state of constant conformational flux (Figure 6A) with rapid local small‐amplitude motions being coupled to larger yet slower collective ones.[ 23 , 77 , 80 , 116 , 117 ] In higher order assemblies, such as chains, cognate Ub flux persists (Figure 6B) being influenced by both the position and number of linkages, plus assembly wide motions.[ 28 , 31 , 71 ] Regardless of the modification type, studies suggest that substrates discriminate not only between modification‐specific topologies but also the populated conformers.[ 81 , 131 , 136 ] It is has been proposed that both conformational selection [77] and substrate‐induced conformation change [119] mechanisms are at play in Ub‐substrate interactions.

Although remarkable and highly insightful work, almost none of it was performed in vivo. The few NMR experiments performed in environments approaching the complexity of the cellular lumen,[ 149 , 151 ] particularly the ex vivo hydrogen‐deuterium NMR exchange and in‐cell CRINEPT experiments, suggest a significant underestimation of Ub's plasticity and its penchant for partaking in non‐specific interactions in vivo. Given, the ever‐increasing evidence that Ub itself can itself be post‐translational modified (Figure 6C) the notion of conformational undersampling evident within the reported in vitro data is in fact quite expectant. In the context of NMR, significant efforts are being expended to overcome the various technical limitations that have historically impeded the execution of NMR experiments in vivo using both solution and solid‐state NMR. Ultimately, we believe that in‐cell NMR will emerge as an important tool in unraveling the functional implications of Ub structural plasticity in vivo.
