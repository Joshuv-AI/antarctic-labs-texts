Integration of genome and transcriptome reveals core genes and allele-specific expression genetic variants associated with immune differentiation of taurine ancestry dairy cattle from indicine ancestry cattle
Authors: Quanzhen Chen, Siqian Chen, Yongjie Tang, Qingyao Zhao, Xilai Zhu, Li Zhi, Xiao Feng, Huaming Mao, Kaixing Qu, Ying Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDisease is a key factor to shorten productive lifespan of dairy cattle. Shifting from classical ‘treating existing diseases’ to ‘preventing diseases before they occur’ is a challenge in high-producing dairy cattle. Immunity plays a crucial role in defending against disease. Substantial evidence indicates that the immune function of taurine ancestry cattle and indicine ancestry cattle are distinct. Therefore, this study aims: (1) to identify core immune genes (CIG) and allele-specific expression (ASE) variants by comparing taurine ancestry dairy cattle with indicine ancestry cattle; and (2) to provide candidate markers for future validation and then used in genomic selection for enhanced immunity in taurine ancestry dairy cattle.ResultsHere, transcriptome and genome data generated by us from the livers and spleens of taurine ancestry dairy cattle (Holstein and Xinjiang Brown) and indicine ancestry cattle (Dehong humped and Dengchuan), along with publicly downloaded transcriptome and genome data of immune cells (monocytes, CD4+ T cells, and B cells) from Holstein and Nelore were systematically analyzed. We constructed transcriptomic-based immune- and disease-scores to assess immunity between taurine ancestry dairy cattle and indicine ancestry cattle. Taurine ancestry dairy cattle exhibited lower immune-scores (T cell receptor signaling, etc.) and higher disease-scores (Staphylococcus aureus infection, etc.) compared to indicine ancestry cattle. A total of 184 CIG underlying the score differences were identified. Furthermore, we performed ASE analysis and verified the identified ASE genes and ASE SNPs by using large-scale expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) data from immune tissues and cells. A total of 1,255 ASE genetic variants related to the CIG were identified in this study. These variants were significantly enriched in active promoters and enhancers in the spleen. Finally, we verified the association between CIG and health traits in 27,214 Holstein bulls and 101 Holstein cows.ConclusionsThis study uses high-throughput omics data to provide new insights into the digitalization of immunity, defined as quantifying immune- and disease-related pathway activity through gene expression levels. Our study also provides candidate markers for future functional validation and genomic selection strategies aimed at enhancing immunity in Holstein cattle.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-11936-9.

Background

Over the past decades, the average milk yield of dairy cattle has increased significantly [1]. However, this gain coincides with rising culling rates (from 35.1% in 2006 to 38% in 2019) [2, 3]. One of the main reasons for culling is health problems [4]. The most economical and effective approach of diseases control should shift from ‘treating existing diseases’ to ‘preventing diseases before they occur’. Enhancing immunity is central to this strategy. Immunity is the comprehensive ability of individuals to resist pathogen invasion, remove antigenic foreign bodies, and monitor and maintain the body’s healthy homeostasis. A recent study reported that individuals with higher African ancestry exhibited stronger antiviral abilities than those with higher European ancestry [5]. The study calculated immune-related pathway activity scores to support this finding. To date, using scores of immune-related pathways to assess immunity in cattle is not determined yet.

The major dairy cattle breeds, such as Holstein, and dual-purpose cattle breeds, such as Brown Swiss, are taurine ancestry cattle (Bos taurus taurus). Compared with taurine ancestry dairy cattle, indicine ancestry cattle (Bos taurus indicus) have shown higher resistance to ticks and Babesia parasites [6–11]. Bos taurus taurus and Bos taurus indicus are two main subspecies of cattle [12, 13]. The main difference in external body morphology between the two cattle subspecies is that Bos taurus indicus has a prominent hump. Friedrich et al. found that immune systems and production traits are key selection targets in taurine ancestry dairy cattle and indicine ancestry cattle [14]. Additionally, a recent study revealed that DNA sequence variations lead to widespread transcriptomic differences in the immune cells of taurine ancestry dairy cattle (Holstein) compared to indicine ancestry cattle (Nelore) [15]. China has diverse cattle genetic resources. Most improved and imported dairy, beef, and dual-purpose breeds are of taurine ancestry, while indigenous breeds have indicine ancestry [16]. For example, Xinjiang Brown cattle were developed through crossbreeding of Kazakh cattle with introduced breeds, including Brown Swiss, followed by long-term selective breeding [17]. It was recognized as a dual-purpose breed of China in 1983 [18, 19]. Genome-wide analysis indicates that Xinjiang Brown cattle derive approximately 37% of their ancestry from Brown Swiss and 63% from Kazakh cattle [20]. Thus, Xinjiang Brown cattle represent a taurine ancestry hybrid population. Archaeological evidence suggested that Bos taurus indicus dispersed from the Indian subcontinent to East Asia between 3,500 and 2,500 years before present [8]. Yunnan, as a frontier zone connecting China with South and Southeast Asia, represents an admixture zone for Bos taurus taurus and Bos taurus indicus. For example, Dehong humped cattle and Dengchuan cattle are indigenous breeds found in Yunnan province, China. Studies have shown that Dehong humped cattle and Dengchuan cattle are predominantly of indicine ancestry [16, 21–24]. Additionally, Dengchuan cattle are the unique dairy-type yellow cattle in Southwestern China and are nearly endangered [25].

Allele-specific expression (ASE) refers to the unequal expression between the two alleles of a gene and is a common event in the transcriptome [26]. In pigs, ASE has been used to identify candidate genes affecting meat quality by integrating eQTL [27]. Moreover, in ruminants, ASE has also been used to uncover the genetic mechanisms regulating the formation of meat quality [28] in cattle and fat deposition in sheep tails [29]. However, current research on immunity in cattle remains limited. The spleen is the largest secondary lymphoid organ and plays a crucial role in both innate and adaptive immunity [30]. It serves as a reservoir for a diverse array of immune cells, including T cells, B cells, and natural killer cells, highlighting the organ’s immunological significance. The spleen provides a specialized environment for immune cell maturation, antigen presentation, and immune regulation. Through these functions, it contributes to host defense against pathogens. The liver is a key organ for energy metabolism and plays a crucial role in determining cattle production performance. Meanwhile, the spleen and liver are both involved in immune response and connected anatomically and physiologically [31]. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to select representative taurine ancestry dairy cattle and indicine ancestry cattle as research subjects, and to identify core genes and immune-related ASE SNPs using whole genome sequencing (WGS) and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of the liver and spleen (see Additional file 1: Fig. S1). Our study provides insights into breeding healthier and more sustainable dairy cattle, and highlights immune-related candidate markers for future validation and incorporation into genomic selection.

Results

Genome and transcriptome characterization of taurine ancestry dairy cattle and indicine ancestry cattle

The sampling sites for taurine ancestry dairy cattle (Holstein and Xinjiang Brown cattle) and indicine ancestry cattle (Dengchuan and Dehong humped cattle) were shown on a map of China (Fig. 1a). In addition to the RNA-seq and WGS data generated in this study, we also obtained publicly available RNA-seq data from the spleen and liver tissues of two healthy adult Holstein bulls and two Hereford bulls, as well as from immune cells (monocytes, B cells, and CD4+ T cells) of three healthy adult female Holstein and Nelore cattle (indicine ancestry cattle). Additionally, publicly available WGS data was acquired from three Nelore cattle. After adapter and quality filtering, RNA-seq libraries yielded over 2.5 billion mapped reads (mean 51 million per sample) with an average mapping rate of 92.06%. WGS libraries yielded over 3.1 billion clean reads (mean 0.2 billion per sample) with an average mapping rate of 99.48% (see Additional file 4: Tables S1–S2). Admixture analysis of the genome (K = 2) clearly separated the taurine ancestry dairy cattle from indicine ancestry cattle (Fig. 1b). When Nelore cattle were included, the results indicated that Dehong humped cattle predominantly exhibit indicine ancestry, and the genetic composition of Dengchuan cattle is more closely related to indicine than to taurine ancestry (see Additional file 1: Fig. S2a), consistent with previous findings [16, 21–24]. Gene expression profiles revealed three dominant clusters: spleen, immune cells, and liver (top left of Fig. 1c). Clustering within each tissue also showed clear separation by cattle breed (top right of Fig. 1c). Moreover, the cluster patterns indicated that gender differences were less pronounced than inter-breed differences, particularly in spleen tissue (see Additional file 1: Fig. S2b). To further explore the evolution of gene expression in the spleen and liver, phylogenetic trees were constructed using RNA-seq data. The observed patterns align with the genomic structure analysis, suggesting coevolution between gene expression and DNA sequence during bovine evolution (Fig. 1d). Notably, the total tree length indicates liver transcriptome evolution is faster than spleen (Fig. 1e), consistent with long-term selection for production traits.

Identification of spleen and immune cells specifically highly expressed genes shared by taurine ancestry dairy cattle and indicine ancestry cattle

We conducted differential expression analysis for the tissues and immune cells within each cattle breed separately. The specifically highly expressed genes (log2-fold change > 1 and adjusted P value < 0.05) in the spleen, liver, monocytes, CD4+ T cells, and B cells of each cattle breed are shown in Table S3 (see Additional file 4). Among these genes, the highest numbers are observed for those shared by taurine ancestry dairy cattle and indicine ancestry cattle (red boxes in Fig. 2a, b) and also for those unique to Holstein cattle (blue boxes in Fig. 2a, b). Specifically, a total of 1,414 genes are BSSH and a total of 1481 genes are BSLH (Fig. 2a). Holstein and Nelore cattle share 2,119, 1,263, and 912 immune cell specifically highly expressed genes in monocytes, CD4+ T cells, and B cells, respectively (Fig. 2b). We refer to these genes collectively as immune-cell-specific highly expressed genes shared by Nelore and Holstein cattle (ICHSNH). We also identified specifically highly expressed genes in the spleen and liver of the Holstein bulls and Hereford bulls, respectively (see Additional file 4: Table S3). The results showed more than 90% of BSSH and BSLH could be identified across breeds and genders (Fig. 2c).

Taurine ancestry dairy cattle exhibit lower transcriptome immune-scores and higher disease-scores than indicine ancestry cattle both in spleen tissue and immune cells

To quantify the immunity of taurine ancestry dairy cattle and indicine ancestry cattle, we systematically calculated scores of immune- and disease-related pathways through GSEA and GSVA, namely immune- and disease-scores, based on the RNA-seq data. The results of GSEA and GSVA performed on BSSH both showed the pattern of immune-scores, such as T cell receptor signaling, was Holstein < Xinjiang Brown < Dengchuan < Dehong humped cattle, while disease-scores (excluding Chagas disease), such as Staphylococcus aureus infection, exhibited the opposite pattern (Fig. 3a, b and see Additional file 4: Tables S4−S5). Similar patterns were observed in GSEA performed on all filtered genes of the spleen (see Additional file 1: Fig. S3a and Additional file 4: Table S4). Additionally, the results of GSEA performed on BSSH and all filtered genes of spleen showed that the pattern of scores for adaptive immune response was Holstein < Xinjiang Brown < Dengchuan < Dehong humped cattle, while the pattern for innate immune response followed the opposite trend (Fig. 3c and see Additional file 4: Table S4). Moreover, the scores for inflammation response and acute inflammation response were higher in taurine ancestry dairy cattle compared to indicine ancestry cattle (Fig. 3c, d and see Additional file 4: Table S4, S6). These findings suggest that taurine ancestry dairy cattle exhibit poorer immunity compared to indicine ancestry cattle.

To support this conclusion, we performed GSEA on both BSSH and all filtered spleen genes for the four cattle breeds (with the Holstein cattle being bulls), and the resulting patterns were similar to those obtained from female Holstein cattle (see Additional file 1: Fig S3b–d and see Additional file 4: Table S4). Furthermore, the same analysis was conducted for RNA-seq from monocytes, B cells, and CD4+ T cells of female Nelore and Holstein. Specifically, we performed GSEA and GSVA for ICHSNH and all filtered genes of monocytes, B cells, and CD4+ T cells. The results demonstrated that the disease-scores (inflammatory bowel disease, etc.) were higher and the immune-scores (adaptive immune response, etc.) were lower in Holstein cattle compared to Nelore cattle (Fig. S4−S6 and see Additional file 4: Tables S3, S7, S10).

Identification of core genes underlying immune differentiation of taurine ancestry dairy cattle from indicine ancestry cattle

We performed WGCNA for BSSH across the four cattle breeds in two separate analyses: one with female Holstein cattle and another with Holstein bulls. We found the co-expression patterns were very similar (Fig. 4a and see Additional file 2: Fig. S7). The module 2 was positively correlated with Dehong humped and Dengchuan cattle and negatively correlated with Xinjiang Brown and Holstein cattle. The genes of this module were significantly enriched in immune-related processes, including immune response, adaptive immunity, and innate immunity (Fig. 4b). Based on |MM| and |GS| (Fig. 4c and see Additional file 4: Table S11), we identified 184 genes shared by taurine ancestry dairy cattle and indicine ancestry cattle (Fig. 4d). We defined these genes as core immune genes (CIG). Protein-protein interaction analysis of CIG identified 10 hub genes with high connectivity, including ZAP70, LCK, GRAP2, PTPRC, CD3D, LCP2, CD247, TRAT1, CD3G, and EOMES (Fig. 4e and see Additional file 2: Fig. S8). Except for TRAT1, the expression of these hub genes was lower in taurine ancestry dairy cattle and was specifically highly expressed in immune-related tissues as supported by data from 8,558 samples across 109 tissue types of cattle (see Additional file 3: Fig. S9-10) [32]. Additionally, the expression of these hub genes was significantly positively correlated with immune-scores and negatively correlated with disease-scores (Fig. 4f). For example, ZAP70 was significantly positively correlated with scores of the T cell receptor signaling pathway (R = 0.96, P = 8e-07) and negatively correlated with scores of the Staphylococcus aureus infection pathway (R = −0.95, P = 3.2e-06). Motif enrichment analysis identified TWIST1 as the most significantly enriched transcription factor (TF) regulating CIG. The TWIST1 was specifically highly expressed in spleen, with its average expression levels increased in the following order: Holstein, Xinjiang Brown, Dengchuan, and Dehong humped cattle (Fig. 4g).

Because immune and production traits typically exhibit an antagonistic relationship, we also identified gene co-expression modules for BSLH as a contrast to the identified CIG. The co-expression modules 1, 4, 5, and 6 were significantly correlated with Holstein cattle, which exhibited opposite correlations with the other three breeds (see Additional file 3: Fig. S11a). These modules were enriched in biological functions related to milk production, including carboxylic acid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and lipid metabolism (see Additional file 3: Fig S11b). Using criteria of |MM| >0.5 for modules (1, 4, 5, and 6) and |GS| >0.2 for Holstein cattle, we identified 984 genes, which are referred to as potential lactation-associated genes (PLAG) (see Additional file 3: Fig. S11c and see Additional file 4: Table S12). TF motif enrichment analysis for promoters of PLAG revealed HNF4A, a central regulator of glucose and lipid metabolism [33, 34], was significantly enriched (see Additional file 3: Fig. S11d). The HNF4A was specifically highly expressed in the liver and showed the highest expression level in Holstein cattle (see Additional file 3: Fig. S11e).

Impact of shared ASE genes on complex traits and identification of immune-related ASE SNPs

To reveal variants with cis-regulatory effects on the expression of CIG, we performed ASE analysis. We identified 6,778 ASE genes in the spleen and 5,624 in the liver (FDR < 0.05; see Additional file 5: Table S13). The number of spleen ASE genes increased in the following order: Holstein, Xinjiang Brown, Dengchuan, and Dehong humped cattle (Fig. 5a and see Additional file 3: Fig S12a). The CattleGTEx is a comprehensive large-scale public database with valuable information on tissue-wise expression levels, eQTL, and transcriptome-wide association [32]. By integrating eGenes (genes with significant cis‐eQTLs) from immune tissues, immune cells, and liver tissue in CattleGTEx, we observed that ASE genes were significantly overrepresented among eGenes of blood, macrophages, monocytes, leukocytes, lymph nodes, and liver (see Additional file 3: Fig. S12b) [32]. Given the four cattle breeds exhibited different linkage disequilibrium (LD) decay rates, with the highest rate observed in Holstein cattle and the lowest in Dehong humped cattle (see Additional file 3: Fig. S12c). Therefore, we analyzed ASE genes shared by Holstein and other breeds. The shared spleen ASE genes were significantly enriched in GWAS signals and QTL regions of health traits, such as somatic cell score (SCS) and tick resistance, while the shared liver ASE genes were significantly enriched in GWAS signals and QTL regions of production traits and metabolic disease, such as milk butterfat production and ketosis (Fig. 5b, c). Moreover, the liver ASE genes shared by Holstein cattle and other cattle breeds in the PLAG significantly enriched in production-related QTLs (Fig. S12d). Notably, the spleen ASE genes shared by Holstein cattle and other cattle breeds in the CIG significantly enriched in disease-related QTLs (Fig. 5d).

Additionally, we identified a total of 32,976 spleen ASE SNPs (FDR < 0.05; see Additional file 5: Table S14). Validation using eQTL data from immune tissues and cells in CattleGTEx revealed that the effect sizes of cis-eQTLs in blood, macrophages, monocytes, leukocytes, and lymph nodes were significantly positively correlated with the regression coefficients of ASE SNPs (Fig. 5e-f and see Additional file 3: Fig. S12e-g). Given that tissues with similar transcriptional profiles share genetic regulatory mechanisms [35], the above results confirm the robustness of the ASE SNPs identified in this study. Of the 184 identified CIG, 126 genes are spleen ASE genes, encompassing a total of 1,255 ASE SNPs. Therefore, these ASE SNPs (n = 1,255) are considered immune-related ASE SNPs. Only 128 of these have been recognized as eQTLs in immune tissues and cells in CattleGTEx (Additional file 5: Table S15). For example, twelve ASE SNPs within the ZAP70 gene body are also macrophage eQTLs and exhibit consistent regulatory effects in spleen and macrophages (Additional file 3: Fig. S12h). To explore the potential regulatory roles of the immune-related ASE SNPs, we performed chromatin-state enrichment analysis. The immune-related ASE SNPs were significantly enriched at active promoters and enhancers in the spleen (Fig. 5d). In total, 434 immune-related ASE SNPs were located in active regulatory elements (Additional file 5: Table S15). For example, the ASE SNP rs43065424 (NC_037342.1:g.47781598 A > G) resides in the active promoter region at a highly conserved position within the GATA3 binding motif (Fig. 5d), implying that the derived allele may weaken GATA3 binding. Furthermore, it is worth noting that rs43065424 is located in the exon region of the gene LOC616948. LOC616948 is a homolog of the human TRIM5 gene, known for its antiviral E3 ubiquitin ligase activity [36]. The lead eQTL of LOC616948 gene in cattle blood is rs209160572 (P < 1e-18, Fig. 5I) [32]. The relationship between rs209160572 and the expression of LOC616948 gene in CattleGTEx blood was consistent with our study (Fig. 5h). These results indicate that among the 1,255 immune-associated ASE SNPs (e.g., rs134919307) not identified by the CattleGTEx project as immune tissue/cell eQTLs, some may complement its findings (Fig. 5h and see Additional file 5: Table S15).

Verification of immune core gene functions in Holstein cattle herds

To assess the association between CIG and health traits in Holstein cattle, we integrated GWAS summary datasets for 44 complex traits from 27,214 Holstein bulls. We observed that immune-related gene sets (BSSH and CIG) showed significant enrichment of GWAS signals for SCS (Fig. 6a). Furthermore, integrating blood transcriptome data, dairy herd improvement (DHI) records, and routine blood data from 101 Holstein cattle revealed that the hub genes and TF of CIG were negatively correlated with somatic cell count (SCC) and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (Neu/Lym) (Fig. 6b). High SCC and Neu/Lym are indicators of poor udder health and increased inflammation in cows, respectively [37, 38]. For instance, CD247 was significantly negatively correlated with SCC and ZAP70 was significantly negatively correlated with Neu/Lym (P < 0.05, Fig. 6c).

In contrast, metabolism-related genes sets (BSLH and PLAG) were significantly enriched in GWAS signals for production traits (e.g., milk yield, protein percentage, and protein) as well as body conformation traits (e.g., body depth and fore udder attachment) (Fig. 6a). The hub genes and TF of PLAG showed positive correlations with milk production traits (Fig. 6b). For example, CAD was significantly positively correlated with milk yield and CTH was significantly positively correlated with protein percentage (P < 0.05, Fig. 6c). Additionally, a higher proportion of genes significantly correlated with SCC and the Neu/Lym ratio was found in the spleen compared to the liver, while a higher proportion of genes significantly correlated with milk production was found in the liver compared to the spleen (Fig. 6b).

Discussion

Up to date, it has not been determined how to digitize the immunity of dairy cattle. In humans, individuals with higher African ancestry exhibit stronger antiviral capabilities by analyzing scores of antiviral response pathways [5]. Therefore, we constructed transcriptomic-based immune- and disease-scores to assess immunity between taurine ancestry dairy cattle and indicine ancestry cattle. Host immunity is classically divided into innate and adaptive immunity. During young adulthood, when the immune system is fully developed, adaptive immunity becomes the primary defense against diseases [39]. In our study, indicine ancestry cattle have higher scores for the adaptive immune response while taurine ancestry dairy cattle scored higher for the inflammatory response and innate immune response. Taurine ancestry dairy cattle scored higher for innate immune response probably because of the production stress. Innate immunity, as the first line of defense, often triggers inflammation [40, 41]. Additionally, as corroborated by our findings, dual-purpose cattle are generally more adaptable to environmental challenges than single-purpose breeds [19]. Therefore, it highlights the need to balance milk production, reproduction, and disease resistance in breeding programs to enhance profitability and promote animal welfare.

The immunity- and disease-scores were calculated based on gene expression, making identification of core genes crucial for understanding the basis of score differences. Tissue-specific genes play critical roles in tissue-related biological processes, such as cholesterol homeostasis in the liver and neurological processes in the brain [42]. Similarly, our study shows that tissue-specific genes significantly enrich GWAS signals related to their functions. Therefore, we identified CIG based on genes that are highly expressed in spleen tissue. These genes may contribute to immune differences between taurine ancestry dairy cattle and indicine ancestry cattle. The hub genes (ZAP70, LCK, CD247, CD3D, and CD3G) of CIG have been utilized to construct a predictive model for nasopharyngeal carcinoma, indicating that higher expression of these genes is associated with longer survival [43]. Meanwhile, the hub genes of CIG are core components of the T cell receptor signaling pathway [44, 45]. TWIST1 was the most significantly enriched transcription factor regulating ICG, and is known for its roles in tumor immune microenvironment formation and inflammation regulation [46–48]. These findings indicate that cross-subspecies/breed comparisons can further narrow down candidate genes influencing complex traits in Holstein cattle.

Elucidating the genetic variants that regulate phenotype is essential for the genome selection of dairy cattle. ASE is a powerful signal that can be used to investigate multiple molecular mechanisms underlying phenotype differences, such as cis-regulatory effects [49]. ASE analysis is also an effective method for experimental design with small sample sizes. It is worth noting that the immune-related ASE SNPs are significantly enriched in active promoters and enhancers in the spleen, suggesting that the ASE SNPs within active regulatory regions may regulate gene expression by modulating transcription factor binding. Among these, rs43065424 maps to a conserved GATA3 binding locus, suggesting that the derived allele may attenuate GATA3 binding and downstream transcriptional activation. GATA3 is a master regulator of type-2 immunity, orchestrating the differentiation of naive T cells into Th2 cells [50, 51]. Therefore, we speculate that the genetic variant rs43065424 in the active promoter region modulates transcription factor GATA3 binding, which may influence the activity of the T cell receptor signaling pathway. Furthermore, the lack of overlap between immune-related ASE SNPs and CattleGTEx’s eQTLs may be attributed to several factors: (1) CattleGTEx predominantly represents Holstein cattle, whereas Xinjiang Brown, Dengchuan, and Dehong humped are underrepresented; (2) CattleGTEx includes fewer than 40 spleen tissue samples, but eQTL identification requires a relatively large sample size; (3) biological differences may exist between spleen and other immune tissues or cell types.

Studies have shown that cows tend to increase blood levels of β-hydroxybutyrate during the transition period [52–54]. However, the limited ability of the liver to metabolize β-hydroxybutyrate often results in hyperketonemia, also known as ketosis. Our study found that the QTLs associated with ketosis were significantly enriched in the shared liver ASE genes and the QTLs associated with tick resistance were significantly enriched in the shared spleen ASE genes. This suggests that selecting specific tissues related to the trait under investigation helps to uncover the mechanisms underlying the formation of complex traits. Overall, our study provides novel insights into cis-regulatory genetic variation associated with immunity in cattle, complementing CattleGTEx findings and advancing efforts toward a healthier and more sustainable livestock industry.

Conclusion

Our study systematically compared immune- and disease-scores between taurine ancestry dairy cattle and indicine ancestry cattle based on the transcriptomes of immune tissue and cells. We identified 84 CIG and 1,255 immune-related ASE genetic variants underlying the score differences. Our study could contribute to producing healthier and more sustainable dairy cattle.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Here, we selected two Chinese taurine ancestry dairy cattle breeds (Holstein and dual-purpose Xinjiang Brown) and two indigenous indicine ancestry cattle breeds (Dehong humped and Dengchuan). For each breed, three healthy adult individuals were randomly selected. The liver and spleen tissues were collected from each animal, yielding a total of 24 samples. All animals were privately owned by farms in their respective regions in China: Xinjiang Brown cattle (males) sampled in the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region; Dengchuan cattle (males) sampled in the Dali City, Yunnan Province; Dehong humped cattle (males) sampled in the Dehong Prefecture, Yunnan Province; and Holstein cattle (females) sampled in the Beijing Municipality. Dehong humped and Dengchuan cattle were reared on mountain pastures. Holstein and Xinjiang Brown cattle were intensively farmed in confinement. All sampling was performed in the same season. Samples were collected aseptically, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until use. All animal procedures were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at China Agricultural University (AW90211202‑1‑1).

RNA-seq library construction

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Takara, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions to ensure minimal RNA degradation. The mRNA was enriched using magnetic beads with Oligo (dT) and sheared. The cDNA was synthesized using random hexamers, end-repaired, and ligated to A base and sequencing adapters. Then, cDNA libraries were obtained by polymerase chain reaction amplification and sequenced using the Illumina Hiseq NovaSeq X Plus platform with 150 bp-paired reads (Novogene Inc., Beijing, China). The RNA-seq data for the three Holstein cattle liver tissues have already been published [55].

Whole genome sequencing

Genomic DNA was isolated from liver tissues of each individual using the Magnetic Universal Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions The purified DNA was subsequently submitted to Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) for DNA library preparation, and WGS was performed using the DNBSEQ-T7 platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads to achieve approximately 10x coverage.

RNA-seq mapping and gene expression quantification

The adapter sequences and low-quality bases were filtered using Trimmomatic (version 0.39) [56]. The processed reads were aligned to the Ensembl 110 version of the ARS-UCD1.2 reference genome using STAR aligner (version 2.7.9) with default parameters [57]. Gene expression levels were quantified as counts using featureCounts [58]. Genes with counts of more than 6 in 20% of the samples were used for further analysis. After filtering, 17,893 genes were retained for further analysis. Sequencing depths were normalized for library size using the Trimmed Mean of M-values (TMM) method implemented in edgeR (v4.4.1) prior to downstream analysis [59]. The log2-transformed values of TMM-normalized CPM (counts per million) + 1 for each gene were then obtained.

Clustering and differential expression analysis of samples

Dimensionality reduction and sample clustering were performed using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding with the Rtsne (version 0.17) R package [60]. To control for genetic and environmental variation and allow more accurate identification of genes specifically highly expressed in tissues and immune cells, we performed differential expression analysis within each cattle breed using the edgeR (version 3.42.4) package [59]. Specifically, genes with significant overexpression (log₂-fold change > 1 and adjusted P < 0.05) in a given tissue or immune cell type were identified by comparing that tissue or cell type to all others. The UpSetR package was then employed to identify the genes that were specifically highly expressed in spleen, liver, monocytes, CD4+ T cells, and B cells shared by taurine ancestry dairy cattle and indicine ancestry cattle [61].

Gene expression phylogenies

We constructed phylogenic trees for taurine ancestry dairy cattle (Holstein and Xingjiang Brown cattle) and indicine ancestry cattle (Dengchuan and Dehong humped cattle) based on pairwise distance matrices using the neighbor-joining method in the ape (version 5.8) R package [62]. We calculated the average expression level for each gene in each cattle breed. Pairwise distance matrices were estimated as 1 − ρ (ρ is Spearman’s correlation coefficient of breeds). The branch length of phylogenetic trees was assessed with 1,000 bootstrap analysis.

Whole genome sequencing variant calling

Raw sequencing data underwent quality control, involving the removal of adapter sequences and low-quality bases, using SOAPnuke (version 2.1.7) [63] and fastp (version 0.23.1) [64]. The processed reads were aligned to the bovine ARS-UCD1.2 reference genome using BWA-MEM (version 0.7.17) [65]. The resulting sequence alignment map (SAM) output was converted to a binary alignment map (BAM) using SAMtools (version 1.3.1) and subsequently sorted by coordinates with SAMtools ‘sort’ [66]. Duplicate reads were marked with Picard ‘MarkDuplicates’ (version 2.18.29) (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard). GATK (version 3.8) HaplotypeCaller was run in GVCF mode on the pre-processed BAM files [67]. The average sequencing depth was approximately 7.39× coverage, determined using GATK’s DepthOfCoverage tool. GATK’s CombineGVCFs was used to aggregate the GVCF files, followed by joint genotyping with GATK’s GenotypeGVCFs to produce the final multi-sample variant call format (VCF) file. GATK’s SelectVariants was then applied to extract the final multi-sample VCF file containing SNPs. These SNPs underwent hard filtering using GATK’s VariantFiltration. Finally, variants were filtered with BCFtools (version 1.11) to exclude those with missing genotype call rates greater than 10%. Only biallelic SNPs with a minor allele frequency > 1% were retained [68].

Compare the scores of immune- and disease-related pathways across breeds

We systematically calculated scores of immune- and disease-related pathways, namely immune- and disease-scores, based on the transcriptomes to assess immunity. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) refers to using a predefined set of genes to evaluate the distribution trend of these genes within a ranked list of genes based on their association with a phenotype, thereby determining their contribution to the phenotype. The R package fgsea (version 1.26.0) [69] was used to perform GSEA to determine which biological pathways were enriched or depleted among the genes ranked by log2-fold change, and the corresponding normalized enrichment scores (NES) for each pathway were calculated. Specifically, differential expression analysis was performed between each pair of breeds, and genes were ranked by log2-fold change before applying GSEA. The activity scores of pathways were also calculated using the ‘gsva’ method in the R package gene set variation analysis (GSVA, version 1.34.0) [70]. It starts by calculating an expression-level statistic that brings gene expression profiles with different dynamic ranges to a common scale. The information of immunity- and disease-related pathways was obtained from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, https://www.genome.jp/kegg/) and Gene Ontology (GO, https://www.geneontology.org/) databases.

Transcription factor motif analysis

We conducted the motif enrichment analysis for the promoter regions (1500 bp upstream of and 500 bp downstream from the transcriptional start sites) of selected genes using ‘findMotifsGenome.pl’ module in HOMER (version 4.11) software with the default motif database [75].

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was conducted separately on breeds shared and spleen specifically highly expressed genes (BSSH) and breeds shared and liver specifically highly expressed genes (BSLH) using the WGCNA R package (version 1.70.3) [76]. A soft threshold of 17 and 13 was applied for BSSH and BSLH, respectively, to identify gene co-expression modules highly correlated with the breeds. We screened important genes based on |Module Membership| (|MM|) > 0.5 and |Gene Significance| (|GS|) > 0.2. The protein-protein interaction network analysis was performed via Cytoscape (https://cytoscape.org/). The ‘degree’ calculated by Cytoscape was used to identify hub genes and ‘degree’ indicates the connectivity of a gene with other genes.

Allele specific expression analysis

Allele-specific read counts for SNPs were obtained using GATK ‘ASEReadCounter’ [67] with the following settings: --U ALLOW_N_CIGAR_READS -minMappingQuality 255 --minBaseQuality 10. Only SNPs located within the gene body, supported by at least 10 reads per allele, and exhibiting a minor allele frequency of at least 2%, were retained. These SNPs were subsequently employed for ASE gene detection. Only SNPs that were heterozygous in at least three individuals were considered for ASE SNPs detection. ASE gene detection was conducted in both the spleen and liver for each cattle breed using ASEP (Allele-Specific Expression Analysis in a Population, version 0.1.0) [77]. The ASE_detection function from the ASEP package was utilized to identify significant ASE genes (FDR < 0.05), with detection parameters set to unphased, adaptive, and a resample rate of 1000. We detected spleen ASE SNP by calculating a binomial P-value to compare the observed ratio with the expected ratio under the null hypothesis, followed by multiple testing correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg approach [32]. SNPs with FDR < 0.05 were considered significant ASE SNPs. Regression coefficients for ASE SNPs were estimated using the lm function in the stats R package.

Other downstream bioinformatics analyses

Fisher’s exact test was conducted on spleen and liver ASE genes to determine whether they are significantly enriched among the eGenes identified in immune-related tissues and liver from the cattle genotype-tissue expression atlas (CattleGTEx, https://cgtex.roslin.ed.ac.uk/) [32]. Additionally, annotations of genomic chromatin states in cattle spleen were used for enrichment analysis [78]. To determine whether immune-related ASE SNPs are significantly enriched in these regions, compared to all SNP loci used for the ASE test, we performed Fisher’s exact test. We used a web tool (JASPAR-A database of transcription factor binding profiles) to predict transcription factor binding sites flanking (± 8 bp) immune-related ASE SNPs located in active regulatory elements. LD decay analysis was performed using the PopLDdecay tool (version 3.43) [79]. We conducted QTL enrichment analysis using regioneR package (Permutation test: 1000) [80]. The QTL were downloaded from CattleQTLdb (Release 54, August 25, 2024). GO and KEGG enrichment analysis were performed using the ClusterProfiler R package (version 4.8.3) [81]. Utilize the UpSetR to visualize and analyze the number of shared and specific genes [61].
