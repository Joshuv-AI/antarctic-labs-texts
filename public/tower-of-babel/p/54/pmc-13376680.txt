Interpretable machine learning for predicting school absenteeism at-scale in Brazil: evidence from 22,000 schools
Authors: Abílio Nogueira Barros, Felipe Vieira Roque, Tiago Paulino, Augusto Schmidt, Flavia Galvani, Rafael Oliveira, Leonardo Brandão Marques, Diego Dermeval, Anita Gea Martinez Stefani, Marisa de Santana da Costa, Emanuel Marques Queiroga, Elthon Oliveira, Cristian Cechinel, Thales Vieira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
School attendance is a fundamental prerequisite for children’s and adolescents’ academic, social, and personal development. Yet, despite decades of research and policy efforts, rates of problematic school absenteeism remain persistently high. This study investigates the predictive potential of school-level factors to identify schools at secondary level in Brazil at risk of elevated absenteeism. Using data from 22,476 schools, we consolidate three main categories of predictors: school infrastructure, human resources, and regional characteristics, combined with attendance records and demographic information. Five machine learning models were compared, and Random Forest was selected due to its balance of predictive performance, interpretability, and statistical confirmation through the DeLong test. Building on this, we apply interpretable machine learning techniques, particularly SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), to reveal how the importance of predictors varies across different school complexity levels, defined as the structural and organizational conditions under which schools operate. Furthermore, we conduct counterfactual “what-if” scenarios, systematically perturbing key features to explore how policy-relevant adjustments could shift schools from high to low absenteeism risk. Our results highlight how structural, staff-related, and contextual features interact differently depending on school complexity, offering actionable insights to inform more equitable educational interventions and policy decisions.

Introduction

School attendance is essential for the development of academic, social, occupational, and civic skills in children and adolescents1. Conversely, absenteeism correlates with reduced academic achievement, lower test scores in reading and mathematics, decreased literacy, grade repetition, social isolation, both internalizing and externalizing behavioral issues, involvement with the juvenile justice system, and school dropout2. School dropouts face increased risks of psychiatric, occupational, marital, and economic challenges in adulthood3,4. The definition of problematic absenteeism or truancy is debated; some works used a 10–11% prevalence rate in the U.S when using a criterion of one missed day over the past 30 days5. Problematic absenteeism and school dropout are also significant global problems with varying prevalence rates6.

The quality and integrity of educational data are fundamental for developing evidence-based policies that lead to meaningful improvements in education7. Among the many indicators collected in education systems, attendance stands out as a critical measure of student engagement, resource allocation, and overall institutional characteristics. Accurate records go beyond mere statistics: they reflect how students interact with their learning environments and offer essential insights for shaping their academic and professional futures. When data are unreliable, policymakers risk drawing flawed conclusions, resulting in inefficiencies and missed opportunities to improve educational outcomes. Ensuring data reliability is therefore paramount to fostering accountability and supporting informed decision-making8.

High dropout and school attrition rates affect not only educational performance indicators but also exacerbate social inequalities, restricting young people’s access to future academic and professional opportunities9. Understanding the factors that influence school retention is therefore essential for the improvement of public policies in education. Socioeconomic factors and individual student grades have traditionally been the primary focus of research on this issue1. However, beyond individual characteristics, schools differ substantially in their structural and organizational conditions. In this study, such differences are captured by the notion of school complexity, operationalized through the School Management Complexity Index (ICG), which summarizes factors such as school size, number of shifts, educational stages offered, and the complexity of the highest level provided10.

In the past decade, predictive modeling techniques such as decision trees, Random Forests (RF)11, Neural Networks, such as Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP)12, and Support Vector Machines (SVMs)13 have been widely applied in educational contexts. When relying solely on students’ demographic and academic information, such as grades and personal information, these models have achieved accuracy levels of approximately 70–75%14,15. Beyond prediction, interpretable machine learning plays a crucial role in explaining model outputs, offering insights into why specific students may be at risk and what underlying factors contribute to their struggles. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)16, an explainable Artificial Intelligence technique that attributes importance values to features based on cooperative game theory, has been widely used to interpret the most influential predictors in machine learning models17. Such information allows educators to design proactive and personalized interventions aimed at improving student success. For instance, studies have shown the effectiveness of early warning systems powered by machine learning in enhancing student retention across diverse disciplines18. Building on these findings, there is a pressing need to develop approaches that not only predict student outcomes but also integrate school-level information in order to develop strategies to aid decision-makers, like infrastructure investments or human resources. Such approaches would strengthen institutional capacity to design more effective, evidence-based interventions and policies, ultimately improving student retention and academic achievement.

This study aims to evaluate the predictive potential of integrating attendance records with demographic information and factors / characteristics at school level to identify high schools in Brazil that are at risk of elevated absenteeism. Specifically, it investigates whether ensemble learning methods can deliver accurate and interpretable predictions across different school subgroups. To ensure transparency, model interpretability is addressed through SHAP, which provides detailed insights into the contribution of each predictor. Beyond interpretability, we introduce a counterfactual perturbation method that systematically tests whether changes in individual or combined school-level characteristics are sufficient to flip a school’s predicted risk status. By combining boosting-based models, SHAP-driven interpretability, and counterfactual simulations, this study enhances the early identification of schools potentially at risk and offers empirical signals that can support reflection and diagnosis.

From this perspective, the study is guided by two Research Questions (RQ):RQ1: How can interpretable machine learning methods be used to analyze school characteristics of different complexity levels and reveal how combinations of school-level factors influence absenteeism risk?RQ2: To what extent can interpretable machine learning approaches provide actionable insights into the key infrastructural and organizational drivers of absenteeism, thereby supporting targeted school and policy-level interventions?

Related works

Recently, extensive research has focused on utilizing interpretable machine learning techniques and predictive modeling within educational data. The study by Krüger et al.18 evaluates interpretable models in the classification of student performance. Real-world data and interpretability metrics were employed with a focus on ethics and transparency. The results highlight the usefulness of interpretable in understanding the factors that impact academic achievement. Furthermore, Melo et al.19 applied interpretable techniques to predict school dropout among students, proposing an explainability index based on a literature-derived checklist. This approach proved effective in identifying dropout profiles and supporting more ethical and transparent educational decisions.

To extract informative educational metrics from a national database, Macarini et al.20 introduce a nationwide project that monitors Uruguayan high school students’ academic paths utilizing K-means clustering. The authors employ association rules and Machine Learning to develop a visualization tool that offers government officials interactive methods for examining and visualizing the nationwide K-12 educational data. Hernández-Leal et al.21 explore governmental data from elementary and middle schools in Norte de Santander, Colombia. The study integrates student enrollment, institutional evaluations, and national assessment data from 2013 to 2018 to investigate performance patterns. Using techniques such as decision trees, clustering, and feature ranking with XGBoost, the authors extract eight relevant educational patterns. Beyond the findings, the paper discusses the main challenges of implementing LA at national and regional levels and proposes solutions tailored to the Latin American context. Skedgell and Kearney1 investigate the combined influence of core academic and demographic factors on predicting problematic absenteeism in youth throughout various developmental stages. Utilizing CART analysis, the study distinguishes youth subgroups at heightened risk for varying levels of school absenteeism. The results help pinpoint at-risk youth to enhance evaluation, prevention, and intervention strategies.

The work of Bulut et al.22 analyzed ninth-grade student data using tree-based and deep learning models, showing the superior performance and interpretability of the former. The study concluded that a student’s sense of belonging is a key factor in predicting school dropout. In addition, Queiroga et al.23 used open data from Brazil’s Basic Education Assessment System (SAEB) and the National School Census (INEP) to identify eight school-related factors that influence both student performance and educational equity. This analysis enabled the visualization of disparities across regions, states, and schools, facilitating the evaluation of existing policies and the identification of schools in need of targeted investments. The public policy recommendations suggested by this study include establishing a robust data quality infrastructure and encouraging initiatives focused on measuring and promoting educational equity. Masci et al.24 apply tree-based machine learning methods to analyze student achievement using PISA 2015 data across nine countries. Their approach models school value-added and captures non-linear effects and interactions among school-level factors, highlighting cross-country differences in educational outcomes and the value of interpretable models in educational analysis. Cannistrà et al.25 propose an early-warning framework for student dropout that integrates Learning Analytics with a conceptual model of dropout determinants. Using administrative student data, the study compares multiple statistical and machine learning approaches, accounting for the hierarchical structure of educational data, and demonstrates robust and accurate dropout predictions while emphasizing theoretical interpretability.

Finally, Queiroga et al.26 developed a machine learning methodology using data from 79,165 high school students in Espírito Santo to predict the risk of failure and dropout. The predictive models generated serve as an early warning system, allowing educators and school administrators to implement proactive and personalized interventions to support at-risk students. Also, Arévalo-Cordovilla17 analyzed 2,225 engineering students in Ecuador by integrating Moodle interactions, academic history, and demographic data to predict academic performance. Using Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE)27 for class balancing, they compared seven classifiers and a stacking ensemble. LightGBM achieved the best results (AUC=0.953, F1=0.950), while interpretability analysis confirmed early grades as key predictors. Their fairness evaluation showed consistency across gender, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status, highlighting the importance of integrated data and interpretable models for identifying at-risk students.

Soma et al.28 conducted a longitudinal study of 453 first-grade students to identify predictors of frequent absenteeism. Using Cox regression, they examined absenteeism in relation to BMI, lifestyle, sleep quality, physical activity, academic performance, and school health room visits. Lower grade point averages and frequent illness-related health room use predicted absenteeism for both genders, while insufficient physical activity and poor sleep quality were additional risk factors for girls. Keppens29 examined links between school absenteeism and academic achievement using time-stamped attendance records for 62,841 secondary students. The study assessed how the timing and reasons for absences affect end-of-year exam results. It found that unexcused absences, sickness-related absences, and school exclusions all harm performance. Unexcused absences are especially damaging at the beginning and end of the year, while sickness-related absences have stronger effects near year’s end. The study underscores the need for temporally aware attendance monitoring and predictive early-warning systems that can identify risk linked to different absenteeism trajectories.

Previous studies in educational data mining and predictive analytics have focused primarily on understanding and predicting student-level absenteeism and dropout, often using demographic, academic, or behavioral indicators. These works provided valuable insights into which factors influence individual student outcomes, highlighting, for instance, the importance of belonging, early grades, and school-related resources. Our study advances this line of research by shifting the focus from individual students to the institutional level, analyzing absenteeism patterns across schools with varying complexity levels. Usually, the aggregate and open data from school and educational systems management platforms are the only ones available to dropout analysis. Utilizing interpretations in conjunction with ensemble learning techniques, we not only predict which schools are at higher risk of absenteeism, but also interpret the main contributing factors in a context-sensitive manner. This approach allows for identifying which resources, infrastructure, or organizational variables have the greatest influence on absenteeism across different school complexity groups. Consequently, our methodology provides a framework for actionable, evidence-based decision-making at the school level, offering guidance to policymakers and administrators on where to allocate resources and interventions to effectively mitigate absenteeism. In this way, our work complements and extends existing literature by integrating predictive accuracy, interpretability, and institutional granularity.

Data collection, feature selection, and preparation

The construction of the database was based on the consolidation of three sources: (i) the Sistema Gestão Presente (SGP)30,31, containing attendance records of high school students for the year 2024; (ii) the 2024 Basic Education Census; and (iii) the 2024 ICG10. These data originate from official educational information systems provided by the National Institute for Educational Studies and Research Anísio Teixeira (INEP) and the Brazilian Ministry of Education.

After merging the three data sources, the consolidated dataset initially comprised approximately 350 variables. These attributes originate mainly from the School Census and include detailed information on infrastructure, human resources, pedagogical resources, administrative organization, and regional context. It is important to emphasize that not all these variables are explicitly listed in tables, as many are highly granular or derived from similar institutional dimensions.

Table 1 provides a summary of six levels of the school Management Complexity Index, categorized in ascending order from the least to the most complex. Each level is defined by the predominant characteristics found in at least two-thirds of the schools within that group. The classification is the result of a statistical model that synthesizes four key variables: school size (total enrollment), number of operational shifts, the quantity of educational stages offered, and the complexity of the highest stage available (based on the theoretical age of the students). The features used in the absenteeism prediction models encompass three main categories: school infrastructure, human resources, and regional characteristics. Table 2 presents examples of representative variables, rather than an exhaustive list of all features used in the models.

Infrastructure-related features include the number of classrooms (qt_classroom_used), the number of air-conditioned classrooms (qt_classroom_aircon), and technological resources such as the number of portable computers per student (qt_pc_student), multimedia projectors (qt_projector), and television sets (qt_tv). Additionally, the presence of distance learning mediation is captured by in_distance_learning.

Human resources features include the number of high school teachers (qt_teacher_hs), the number of pedagogical support staff such as coordinators and educational counselors (qt_pedagogical_staff), and the number of general service personnel, including janitors and kitchen staff (qt_general_staff and qt_food_staff).

Regional characteristics are represented by the tp_location feature, indicating whether the school is located in an urban or rural area, which may influence accessibility, student attendance patterns, and resource availability.

Table 3 shows that several school-level features present consistently high standard deviations across all ICG categories. In particular, infrastructure-related variables such as qt_projector, qt_tv, and qt_pc_student, exhibit dispersion values that are often several times larger than their respective means. This pattern suggests the presence of highly uneven resource distributions, with a small number of schools displaying extreme values. Staffing-related features, particularly qt_pedagogical_staff and qt_general_staff, present markedly higher mean values in ICG 1 when compared to the remaining ICGs. In the initial preprocessing stage, only schools with valid data across all three sources were considered. From the Census, a total of 29,993 institutions reported offering secondary education. After merging this information with the SGP data, the number was reduced to 22,476 schools, ensuring consistency and integrity in the analyses resulting in the final sample.

The School Census dataset underwent manual curation, through which only variables directly related to high school were retained, since SGP records currently refer exclusively to this educational stage. Variables associated with elementary education, Youth and Adult Education (EJA), and school infrastructure not directly linked to high school were excluded.

Subsequently, quantitative variables were standardized by dividing their values by the number of enrollments per school, ensuring comparability across institutions of different sizes. The SGP dataset was used to generate the target variable for a binary classification problem. Student attendance was categorized according to the absenteeism ranges proposed by Kearney32: Low (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$>95\%$$\end{document}) indicates high attendance, Moderate (91–95%) reflects occasional absences, Significant (81–90%) denotes frequent absences, High (71–80%) signals a concerning pattern, and Severe (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\le 70\%$$\end{document}) represents a high risk of dropout.

Based on these distributions, schools where more than 50% of students fell into the Low and Moderate ranges were assigned to class 1 (low absenteeism risk). Conversely, schools with more than 50% of students in the Significant, High, or Severe ranges were assigned to class 0 (high absenteeism risk). We tested alternative thresholds of 30%, 40%, and 60%. In all of these scenarios, the class balance deteriorated substantially. The results indicate a clear separation between the two classes across all ICG levels. The 50% threshold was adopted as a majority-class rule to ensure that the assigned label reflects the dominant absenteeism profile within each school. This prevents a school from being classified as high-risk based on a small subset of severely absent students and is consistent with our policy-oriented objective of detecting systemic absenteeism, where absenteeism affects most students. This majority-based decision rule parallels the logic adopted in ensemble learning, where the final outcome is determined by the dominant class across multiple predictions33, and has also been used in other domains to define prevailing patterns in segmented data34.

After merging with the ICG data, the consolidated dataset was partitioned into six distinct subsets according to the school management complexity range. Each subset retained the same attribute structure, enabling comparable analyses across different contexts of complexity. Modeling and evaluation

Random forest feature selector

We performed a variable selection using a Random Forest (RF)–based feature importance strategy, applied independently to each of the six datasets segmented by school management complexity levels (ICGs). At this stage, all 294 attributes obtained after the mutual-information-based filtering step were retained as candidate predictors and provided as input to the RF models.

For each ICG subset, an RF classifier was trained using the full feature set, and feature importance scores were extracted based on the mean decrease in impurity. These importance values quantify the relative contribution of each attribute to the classification task. The selection process was implemented using a fixed importance threshold, defined as CUTOFF = 0.01. Only variables whose normalized importance exceeded this cutoff were retained. Due to differences in data distributions and structural characteristics across ICGs, the number of retained variables varied slightly between subsets. However, on average, this procedure resulted in approximately 35 selected features per ICG.

Machine learning models

To enable a principled comparison between linear and non-linear classification strategies, we evaluated five supervised learning algorithms representing two distinct modeling paradigms: a linear probabilistic model (Logistic Regression) and four tree-based ensemble models (Random Forest, XGBoost36, LightGBM37, and CatBoost38). All models were trained using a grid-search procedure with cross-validation, ensuring that comparisons are methodologically consistent and that performance differences can be attributed to model characteristics rather than tuning artifacts.

Logistic Regression (LR). Logistic Regression was adopted as a linear baseline classifier. LR models the conditional probability of class membership using the logistic (sigmoid) function applied to a linear combination of predictors. Model parameters are estimated by maximizing the log-likelihood function, typically using gradient-based optimization algorithms such as lbfgs or coordinate descent (as implemented in liblinear). LR assumes a linear relationship between predictors and the log-odds of the outcome. Unlike tree-based models, it does not automatically capture non-linearities or higher-order feature interactions unless these are explicitly engineered. However, its coefficients are directly interpretable as log-odds ratios, which makes LR a transparent reference model for comparison. Hyperparameters were optimized over the regularization strength \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C \in \{0.01, 0.1, 1, 10\}$$\end{document} and solvers lbfgs and liblinear, with the maximum number of iterations set to 1000 to ensure convergence.

Random Forest (RF). Tree-based models partition the feature space recursively into homogeneous regions by selecting splits that maximize class purity. These models are non-parametric and do not assume linear relationships or specific data distributions. They naturally capture non-linear effects and complex feature interactions. Random Forest is a bagging-based ensemble method that constructs multiple decision trees using different subsets of features and data samples. Each tree is grown by recursively selecting splits that reduce impurity, and final predictions are obtained by aggregating predictions across trees using majority voting. This strategy reduces variance relative to a single decision tree and provides robustness to multicollinearity and outliers. The RF configuration was optimized using n_estimators=100, max_depth=10, max_features=sqrt, min_samples_split=5, and min_samples_leaf=2.

Gradient Boosting Models. Gradient boosting methods build trees sequentially rather than independently. Each new tree is trained to correct the residual errors of the previous ensemble by minimizing a differentiable loss function using gradient descent in function space. This sequential optimization framework often produces stronger predictive performance than bagging methods, although it is more sensitive to hyperparameter configuration.

XGBoost optimizes a regularized objective function that combines training loss with a complexity penalty on tree structure, helping to control overfitting while improving predictive accuracy. LightGBM adopts a leaf-wise tree growth strategy combined with histogram-based feature binning, which improves computational efficiency and scalability when handling large datasets. CatBoost incorporates ordered boosting and specialized mechanisms for handling categorical features, reducing prediction shift and mitigating overfitting during training. For these gradient boosting models, hyperparameters such as tree depth, learning rate, and number of boosting iterations were tuned through systematic search procedures.

Feature importance and Mean Decrease in Impurity (MDI). For tree-based models, feature importance was computed using the Mean Decrease in Impurity (MDI). In classification trees, impurity is commonly measured using the Gini index, defined as one minus the sum of squared class proportions within a node. When a feature is used to split a node, the impurity of the resulting child nodes is typically lower than that of the parent node. The reduction in impurity therefore, indicates how much that split improves class separation.

MDI quantifies the importance of a feature by aggregating the impurity reductions produced by all splits that use that feature across all trees in the ensemble. Features that consistently produce larger reductions in impurity are interpreted as more influential in the model’s decision-making process. This measure provides an interpretable way to identify which variables contribute most strongly to the predictive structure of tree-based models.

Data splitting and cross-validation

To robustly evaluate model performance and prevent data leakage, each ICG dataset was split into training and testing subsets using a 75/25 stratified split (maintaining class proportions) before model training. Within the training set, a stratified 5-fold cross-validation framework (StratifiedKFold with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n\_splits=5$$\end{document}, shuffle=True, random_state=42) was employed to optimize model (all five models preseted in previous section) hyperparameters and to assess performance consistently across folds. This ensured that each fold preserved approximately the same percentage of samples from each target class as in the full dataset.

Class balancing

Given the imbalance in the target variable, we applied the SMOTE prior to feature selection, ensuring that the identification of relevant variables was not biased by class imbalance. Specifically, we used \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k\_neighbors=5$$\end{document} when generating the synthetic samples, after which feature selection and model training were conducted on the balanced dataset.

Performance evaluation metrics and statistical analysis

To evaluate model performance, we computed a comprehensive set of metrics, including Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-score, in addition to the standard AUC-ROC. These metrics provide complementary perspectives on the models’ predictive capabilities, accounting not only for overall correctness (Accuracy) but also for the balance between false positives and false negatives (Precision and Recall) and their harmonic mean (F1-score).

Furthermore, the DeLong test39 was applied to assess the statistical significance of differences in AUC-ROC values between top-performing models. In our study, this involved comparing the two leading classifiers, Random Forest and CatBoost, for each ICG level. The DeLong test evaluates whether observed differences in ROC-AUC are likely to reflect true differences in predictive performance rather than random variation, providing a formal statistical basis for model comparison. Using this approach, we ensured that model selection was informed both by absolute performance metrics and by the statistical significance of observed differences.

Additionally, we developed a validation procedure to investigate how simultaneous changes in multiple school characteristics modify model predictions. The procedure consisted of systematically perturbing these variables in different combinations, always starting from schools classified as high absenteeism risk and verifying whether the prediction shifted to low risk.

For example, we first tested combinations involving two features, such as qt_class_hs, and qt_classroom_aircon, and computed the change in model output under this joint perturbation. Next, we extended the combination by including a third feature, for examples, qt_enroll_tech_hs, and again evaluated whether the prediction changed. This process continued step by step, progressively adding more features, until ultimately considering all selected variables together. At each stage, we compared the perturbed prediction with the baseline to determine whether the combination was sufficient to move the model from high to low absenteeism risk.

By applying this systematic strategy, we were able to assess not only the individual impact of features but also how their combined effects operate in practice, offering a more realistic perspective on the types of structural or organizational adjustments that could effectively reduce absenteeism.

Interpretability study

To further interpret the predictive models, we employed SHAP, a widely used method that decomposes a model’s predictions into the contribution of each feature, allowing us to understand both the direction (positive or negative) and the intensity of their impact on absenteeism risk. This analysis was performed separately for each school complexity category defined by the ICG (School Management Complexity Index), which ranges from ICG 1 (least complex schools) to ICG 6 (most complex schools). We analyze the top five influential features for each model to identify potential relationships. By adopting this structure, we can uncover how the importance and influence of features systematically vary according to school complexity, instead of assuming that the same drivers operate uniformly across all contexts.

The analysis of feature importance across all ICG models reveals that certain variables consistently appear as major contributors to school absenteeism, suggesting systematic influences that transcend specific school complexity levels. Notably, features such as qt_pc_student (student laptop availability), qt_pedagogical_staff (pedagogy professionals), qt_teacher_hs (number of high school teachers), and qt_class_hs (number of high school classes), are present in five of the six models. This indicates that, even if their importance varies across ICGs, these factors consistently contribute to absenteeism outcomes, reflecting their systemic relevance in educational contexts.

The differences observed between the variables highlighted by the SHAP analysis (Figs. 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8) and those identified through RF feature importance (Fig. 9) arise from the distinct internal mechanisms underlying these interpretability methods. Random Forest feature importance is computed using the Mean Decrease in Impurity (MDI), which quantifies how much, on average, a given feature reduces node impurity when it is used to split the data across the ensemble of trees. As such, this measure reflects the structural role of features in building the decision trees and emphasizes variables that frequently contribute to partitioning the feature space. In contrast, SHAP values are grounded in cooperative game theory and measure the marginal contribution of each feature to the final model prediction. SHAP assesses how changes in a feature’s value influence the predicted outcome, accounting for interactions and nonlinear effects. Consequently, SHAP highlights features that have the strongest impact on predictions at the output level, which may differ from those most frequently used in tree splits. These differences are therefore expected and should be interpreted as complementary perspectives: Random Forest importance provides insight into the model’s internal decision structure, while SHAP offers a more direct explanation of how individual features drive absenteeism risk predictions.

The consistent presence of these variables among the most relevant predictors suggests that they are strongly associated with absenteeism patterns in the schools analyzed. While the results indicate that infrastructure, staffing, and pedagogical conditions are closely associated with absenteeism patterns, the analysis is observational in nature. Accordingly, the findings are best understood as highlighting areas that may merit closer examination by policymakers and school leaders, rather than as direct evidence of the effects of specific interventions. In this sense, the identified associations can serve as useful signals for prioritizing diagnostic efforts and for formulating hypotheses to be tested in future research using appropriate causal or experimental designs.

Specifically, the results in Fig. 10 present the Flip Rate for transitions from high absenteeism to low absenteeism. It is important to emphasize that these features should not be interpreted as direct causal drivers of absenteeism. Discussion

The results of this study highlight the importance of interpretability in predictive modeling applied to school absenteeism. Beyond achieving good predictive performance, models that provide transparent explanations of their outputs are essential for decision-makers in the educational domain. By understanding why certain schools are at higher risk of absenteeism, policymakers and school administrators can design interventions that are both targeted and evidence-based. This aligns predictive modeling not only with academic interest but also with actionable educational policies. At the same time, it is important to point out that the model identifies predictive associations rather than causal effects, and therefore its outputs should be interpreted as signals to support reflection and diagnosis, rather than as direct prescriptions for policy action.

The sensitivity analysis proved particularly valuable by identifying which features have the greatest influence on the model’s predictions. For instance, the number of air-conditioned classrooms emerged as a key factor in some scenarios. However, this variable is likely to function as a proxy for broader socioeconomic and infrastructural conditions, rather than as a direct lever that policymakers should manipulate in isolation. As such, there is a nontrivial risk that decision-makers unfamiliar with the limits of predictive modeling could interpret these results as recommendations to invest in specific items, without recognizing the underlying contextual factors implicitly encoded in the data.

To mitigate this risk, any operational use of the proposed methodology should be accompanied by clear communication strategies that explain the associative nature of the findings, the potential presence of proxy variables, and the fact that predictive machine learning alone cannot identify effective policy interventions. Instead, the insights generated by interpretability techniques should be combined with qualitative inquiry, contextual expertise, and, where feasible, causal evaluation designs. In this sense, interpretability bridges the gap between predictive accuracy and informed decision-making, not by dictating interventions, but by helping direct attention toward the conditions under which absenteeism risk tends to be higher and where further investigation is warranted.

Another important finding of this work is the benefit of adopting a distinct analytical approach for schools at different ICG. Treating schools as a homogeneous group can obscure important differences in the drivers of absenteeism. By contrast, our analysis demonstrated that the relevance of features varies systematically across ICG levels. This was confirmed both in the feature importance analysis (see Fig. 9) and in the sensitivity analysis (see Fig. 10), where different variables became more salient depending on the school’s complexity category. For example, while infrastructure-related features such as qt_classroom_aircon were more relevant in higher ICG levels, other socio-demographic or resource-related variables were more influential in lower ICG contexts. These findings emphasize that policy interventions should be tailored to school complexity levels rather than applying a one-size-fits-all strategy.

Based on these results, we can address our two research questions:

RQ1: How can interpretable machine learning methods be used to analyze school characteristics of different complexity levels and reveal how combinations of school-level factors influence absenteeism risk?

Our results suggest that different ICG levels present distinct features as most important, as evidenced in both the feature importance analysis (Fig. 9) and the SHAP visualizations. These analyses indicate that the impact of specific factors varies with the complexity of schools, confirming that school heterogeneity shapes the drivers of absenteeism risk. Without this type of interpretability analysis, it would be difficult to disentangle how staff-related, regional, and infrastructure variables influence absenteeism differently across contexts. For instance, while qt_pedagogical_staff consistently appears as an important predictor in ICG levels 3 to 6, it does not play a relevant role in ICG levels 1 and 2. This finding highlights the necessity of tailoring interventions according to school complexity, as the relevance of features is not uniform across the educational system.

RQ2: To what extent can interpretable machine learning approaches provide actionable insights into the key infrastructural and organizational drivers of absenteeism, thereby supporting targeted school and policy-level interventions?

By systematically perturbating (see Table 6 and Fig. 10) combinations of features, our analysis demonstrates that certain configurations can shift the predicted outcome from high absenteeism risk to low absenteeism risk. This counterfactual perspective not only identifies which variables are individually important but also reveals how their interactions shape absenteeism outcomes. Furthermore, we show that these combinations are not uniform across ICG levels: schools with higher complexity require different sets of feature adjustments compared to schools with lower complexity. This highlights that actionable interventions must be tailored to the infrastructural and organizational context of each school group. Consequently, interpretable machine learning provides concrete and context-specific insights that can support decision-makers in designing more effective, targeted strategies to reduce absenteeism. From our results in Table 6, we can infer that effective policies should prioritize both structural and staff-related factors. Specifically, the number of teachers and classrooms, along with the presence of air conditioning, emerged as key drivers capable of shifting schools from high absenteeism to low-risk categories. After perturbation, these features produced the strongest outcome changes, reinforcing their potential as actionable levers for intervention.

Our research extends the literature on predictors of school absenteeism by explicitly examining how school quality indicators and infrastructure conditions interact to influence attendance outcomes. While Skedgell et al.1 investigated the role of core academic and demographic factors across developmental stages, their analysis did not account for the combinatorial effects of multiple school-level characteristics. In contrast, our approach employs counterfactual perturbation experiments to identify whether changes in combinations of features are sufficient to reverse a school’s predicted status from high risk (absenteeism-prone) to low risk. This methodological innovation highlights not only the marginal importance of individual predictors but also their joint capacity to alter outcomes, offering a more policy-relevant perspective.

The findings further support that school quality is a key determinant to model absenteeism patterns, complementing prior findings such as those reported in40, which emphasize that parental engagement, cultural alignment, and school quality can substantially influence student attendance. By situating infrastructure-related variables alongside organizational dimensions, our work demonstrates that absenteeism cannot be fully explained by demographic or family-level factors alone, but must also be understood through the lens of school-level quality and resource allocation. In line with this perspective, we show that improvements in school infrastructure (e.g., availability of climatized classrooms) and staffing (e.g., adequate number of teachers) function as critical interventions to mitigate absenteeism. These findings are consistent with broader evidence that strengthening school environments and resources can positively affect attendance outcomes41,42.

Concerning policy implications, this work directly connects to broader strategies to mitigate absenteeism. Regional development initiatives, addressing disparities in infrastructure, teacher quality, and resources, support our evidence that school facilities and staffing impact attendance. Similarly, economic support policies like scholarships, free meals, uniforms, or transport reduce financial barriers tied to school quality, promoting student participation43. By emphasizing infrastructure, human resources, and their connection with regional and economic contexts, our work advances to a more informed understanding of school-level absenteeism risk.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that predictive modeling, when combined with interpretability techniques, can be applied to large-scale administrative education data to better understand patterns associated with school-level absenteeism risk. Beyond building accurate classifiers, we implemented a counterfactual approach to reveal which features, when perturbed, are most critical for shifting schools from high to low absenteeism risk, thus pointing to concrete avenues for intervention. In parallel, we applied SHAP analysis to provide a detailed interpretation of feature contributions across different levels of ICG, extending the model’s utility from prediction to explanation. Together, these strategies illustrate the type of insights that interpretable ML methods can generate when applied to absenteeism data at scale.

It is important to emphasize that these findings should be understood as illustrating the diagnostic and exploratory potential of interpretable ML, rather than as identifying causal effects or prescribing concrete policy actions. The predictive influence of a variable does not imply that modifying that variable would change absenteeism outcomes, and in several cases the most influential features likely operate as proxies for broader contextual or socioeconomic conditions that are not directly observed. Thus, the usefulness of the approach depends fundamentally on the nature, quality, and granularity of the administrative records available.

Despite these contributions, some limitations must be acknowledged. A central constraint of the current work lies in the level of granularity adopted: the analyses were performed at the school level, rather than focusing on individual students. While this perspective allows us to capture structural and contextual factors, it inevitably overlooks student-level heterogeneity, such as personal, family, or socio-emotional conditions that also play a critical role in absenteeism. This aggregation may mask important micro-level patterns, thereby limiting the precision with which interventions can be designed and evaluated.

As a direction for future work, we propose extending the analysis to the student level, integrating richer datasets that combine school census information with individual attendance histories, socio-demographic characteristics, and possibly external contextual factors (e.g., neighborhood socioeconomic indicators). This finer granularity would allow not only for more precise predictions but also for the design of highly tailored interventions that directly address the needs of specific groups of students.We seek to evaluate other model interpretability techniques such as Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations and Anchors. Additionally, future studies could explore advanced fairness-aware techniques to ensure that recommendations do not inadvertently reinforce existing inequalities across student populations.
