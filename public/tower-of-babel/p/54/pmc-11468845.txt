Recognition of language barriers in comprehending non-communicable disease management among rural elderly people in the DIMAMO surveillance area: a case of AWI-Gen participants
Authors: Reneilwe G Mashaba, Kagiso P Seakamela, Masenyani O Mbombi, Livhuwani Muthelo, Precious M Makoti, Solomo SR Choma, Eric Maimela, Cairo B Ntimana
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLanguage plays a critical role in health communication, particularly in the management and understanding of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) among elderly populations. This study aimed to explore the language barriers that affect elderly participants’ comprehension of NCDs, focusing on how these barriers impact their understanding of disease causes, symptoms, treatment, and self-management. The study also investigated how linguistic differences between healthcare workers and patients influence the effectiveness of health interventions in a rural South African context.MethodsThis qualitative phenomenological study was conducted with elderly participants aged 60 years and above, all of whom have chronic diseases. In-depth face-to-face interviews were conducted using an interview guide during the AWI-Gen 2 research feedback sessions in the Ga-Dikgale area. Data analysis was performed using QSR NVivo 10 to identify key themes.ResultsFive major themes emerged regarding participants’ understanding of NCDs and the perceived language barriers. These include Limited Knowledge and Awareness of NCDs, Misconceptions about NCDs, Mixed perceptions regarding NCD causes and treatment, Discrepancies in healthcare workers’ communication, and the Importance of interpreters in facilitating NCD-related communication.ConclusionThe study revealed significant misconceptions about diabetes and hypertension, as well as traditional practices like bloodletting, which were perceived to relieve hypertension symptoms. It also identified critical communication barriers, including limited time for health practitioners to explain diagnoses and treatments. The findings emphasize the need for interpreters and culturally sensitive communication strategies to improve health literacy and care. Collaborative efforts between traditional and western health practitioners are recommended to address these issues and enhance community health interventions.Keywords: Language barriers, Diabetes mellitus, Hypertension

Introduction

Language plays a significant role in health communication and research [1]. Linguistic differences between the researcher and the participants could result in various challenges which may affect participants’ recruitment and understanding of consent and pose difficulties in the dissemination of findings by the researcher to the participant [2]. In addition, language barriers between patients and healthcare providers were reported to have negative implications, which include patient dissatisfaction, negative effects on the quality of healthcare and patient safety, and a counterbalance of equal access to treatment and healthcare amongst patients. Other studies have reported that patients affected by the language barrier are more likely to consume more health services and experience more adverse events [1, 3]. A study conducted in Ethiopia reported that patients affected by language barriers suffered preventable medical errors, low health-seeking behavior, additional treatment cost, increased length of hospital stays, weak therapeutic relation, social desirability bias, less confidence, and dissatisfaction with the healthcare [4].

Engaging the community from which a study is conducted, especially in the design, conduct, and dissemination of research outcomes is becoming a norm internationally [5–7]. A study by Sacristan et al., (2016) reported the need to do a post-study evaluation of the patient experience as part of improving patients’ involvement in clinical research [8]. However, this was measured from the researcher’s perspective [8]. To measure the impact of obstacles preventing optimal management of NCDs it is important to solicit participant’s perspectives and understanding to identify language gaps, the results of which could inform and shape how future studies research is conducted and how to best engage the participants to maximize their understanding of research outcomes.

Although the researchers may produce alternative ways to breach the language barriers, using interpreters and translations, some concepts may be lost in translation and the same may not be implemented in public health facilities due to infrastructural and financial limitations. As a result, the language barrier may affect the understanding of disease pathogenesis, and the impact and role of medication, which may subsequently affect lifestyle choices and self-management of chronic conditions and medical adherence by the participants. In addition, the negative effects associated with language barriers may also have a negative impact on the life expectancy and cost relating to primary health services.

The DIMAMO area, a location from which the present study was conducted has a high prevalence of diabetes (between 7% and 28% versus national prevalence of 11.3%) [9–11] and hypertension (45.8% versus national prevalence of 38%) [12, 13]. This motivates for studies that investigate possible contributors to these high prevalence. Can the high prevalence be as a result of lack of understanding of the conditions and their related risk factors? More so that language plays a significant role in the understanding of health conditions and subsequently the health seeking behavior of the elderly participants. Thus, the study aimed to explore the participants’ perspective on barriers including language barriers, which may hinder the understanding of individual chronic diseases, and interventions thereof in elderly people residing in Limpopo province, South Africa. The findings of the present study will assist in addressing a critical gap in healthcare communication, especially among vulnerable populations. Chronic conditions that were considered in the present study were diabetes mellitus and hypertension.

Methods

Study design

The present study was qualitative and designed as descriptive phenomenological research. A phenomenological approach to the description of a common meaning for several individuals of their conscious lived experiences of a concept or phenomenon [14–16]. Additionally, the approach focused on the description of a common phenomenon/concept that the participants had in common, which the researchers are aware of but do not have in-depth knowledge about [14]. The concept the participants shared in the present study was non-communicable diseases and perceived language barriers. The consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) guiding principle was used in reporting the present study findings [14, 17].

Sampling and study setting

The study was conducted during the Africa Wits INDEPTH Partnership for Genomic Research (AWI-Gen) research findings feedback session held in the Dikgale area. The Dikgale area forms part of villages that fall under the Dikgale Mamabolo Mothiba Population Health Research Centre (DIMAMO PHRC) surveillance area. DIMAMO site is in rural areas about 30 km North of Polokwane, Limpopo province, South Africa, refer to Fig. 1 [18]. Participants of the AWI-Gen phase 2 aged 40 years and above study were purposively selected to form part of the current study population, refer to Fig. 2. All participants who took part in the present study had at least one chronic condition. The majority of the AWI-Gen participants were unemployed (77%) and had primary school (53.3%) as their highest level of education [19]. Consent was sought from the prospective participants. A heterogeneous sample approach was employed to collect a wide range of viewpoints regarding language barriers and their effect on participants’ comprehension of chronic disease pathophysiology and its associated implications for disease self-management. Heterogeneous sampling is a purposive sampling technique used to capture a wide range of perspectives relating to the thing that one is interested in studying [20, 21]. This technique seeks to search for variations in perspectives, ranging from those conditions that are viewed to be typical to those that are extreme in nature [20, 21].

Study area

Participants selection

Research questions

The main research question was, what are the perceived language barriers relating to the understanding and management of chronic conditions? Sub-questions included the following: What are the participants’ understanding of NCDs’ (hypertension and diabetes) pathogenesis? What are the perceived language barriers relating to NCDs and how do they affect self-management of NCDs? What interventions are best suited to address the language barrier in the selected community?

Data collection and analysis

Data was collected through qualitative face-to-face in-depth interviews using an interview guide, between September and October 2023 in the Dikgale area, Limpopo province, South Africa during the AWI-Gen 2 results feedback and community engagement. Three principal authors [RGM (BSc), CBN (MSc), and KPS (MSc)] performed semi-structured one-on-one interviews in a private room with each participant using the primary local language in the region, Sepedi. Three authors conducted the interviews, in Sepedi, which is the local language that is also used by the health care professionals in the area. Although this was the case, there still existed subtle but important linguistic differences that may affect health communication [22, 23]. Health professionals and researchers, have academic and clinical backgrounds, which influenced the way certain medical and research terminologies are framed. Participants, on the other hand, often come from non-medical, rural backgrounds with a limited understanding of such terminologies. Despite efforts to communicate health concepts (e.g. disease pathogenesis and treatment), the participant may not fully understand due to educational and contextual gaps. Therefore, conducting the interviews in the local language ensured that the participants first understood the questions that were asked and had the opportunity to express themselves in the best possible way. The answers that the participants gave to these questions where compared with literature to determine the level of deviance from the actual known pathogenesis, definitions and the risk factors of diabetes mellitus and hypertension. The deviations thereof were used to determine the understanding the participants have of these conditions which speaks to language barriers.

Before the interviews were done, the primary authors clarified the reason for conducting the interviews, the voluntary nature of participation, and the freedom to withdraw at any moment. Two authors [PMM (BSc) and RGM (BSc)] translated and transcribed word-for-word the interview’s audio recordings. The transcripts were anonymized before entering them into QSR NVivo 10 (QSR International, Warrington, UK) to aid in analysis. The interview data was analyzed using a deductive content analysis method to develop themes.

Results

In-depth interviews were conducted until data saturation was reached in Participant 15. Most of the participants were females and all aged above 60 years. The results section below is organized around themes of the perceptions of participants relating to their understanding of non-communicable diseases and perceived language barrier. These themes, identified through deductive analysis include, Limited Knowledge and Awareness of NCDs, Misconceptions about NCDs, Mixed perceptions regarding NCDs causes, symptoms, and treatment, Discrepancies about the HCW communicating the NCDs diagnosis and treatment options, Importance of interpreters for understanding the NCDs, diagnostic and treatment terms (see Table 1).

Summary of themes

Limited knowledge and awareness of NCDs

Although most participants were on treatment for diabetes mellitus and /or hypertension, there remained limited knowledge and awareness of these conditions. When asked about their understating of the cause of DM, participants indicated a lack of comprehensive understanding of the causes and symptoms of diabetes mellitus. Some (3) respondents acknowledge a lack of formal education on the subject and in general as the contributing factor to the lack of understanding of the disease:

“I don’t really know, people who have diabetes they just lose strength and collapse and then become better when they are given water mixed with sugar or a small amount of milk and the person would be better.” (Participant 6, female).

“I don’t know because I depend on the information I receive from those who know about diabetes mellitus.” (Participant 1, female).

Misconceptions influencing the understanding of NCD`s

Mixed conceptions relating to diabetes mellitus and hypertension were also noted. Some (6) participants understood diabetes mellitus as a condition that mostly affected males more than females and that it presented with sexual malfunctions: “I don’t know but what I have heard is that it affects males more causing them to not… they can’t really sexually perform.” (Participant 1, female).

We noted the influence of cultural beliefs on the understanding of hypertension. Some respondents refer to hypertension as a traditional condition from the olden days, given a new Western name and therefore requiring traditional treatment instead of Western medication. This included self-treatment methods like releasing blood/bloodletting to relieve symptoms.

“…it was there in the past because it was indicated by dizziness, which was managed by using traditional herbs, steaming or sometimes they reduce blood by pricking and cutting and the hypertension gets cured.” (Participant 15, female).

There was a common belief amongst the participants that high blood pressure meant that one had a lot of blood in the body which needed to be reduced for one to experience some sort of relief from the symptoms. “When a person has high blood, it means that a person has a lot of blood in their body.” (Participant 6, female)

It is just a traditional condition from the olden days… just that given a new name. (Participant 14, female)

Some participants associated hypertension with eating too much beetroot, which was believed to increase the quantity of blood. In addition, feeling the heat in the body, cracks on the skin, heavy shoulders, problems with the voice, and nosebleeds were also associated with high blood pressure: “I don’t really know but I think is eating too much beetroot because it increases the blood… I also saw cracks on my body and my shoulders got heavy I knew that I had hypertension”.” (Participant 15, female).

Mixed perceptions about NCDs (perceived causes, symptoms and treatment)

Several (8) participants attribute diabetes to excessive sugar consumption, poor dietary choices such as eating big portions of food, and genetic inheritance:

“It is caused by the food that we eat that is not good for our bodies… and eating too much sugar and eating food that contains a lot of starch… these are what causes diabetes mellitus.” (Participant 7, female).

“They are caused by the food we eat …at first the food we eat causes high blood and if you don’t go for a medical check-up and treatment, the body gets defeated by high blood then it elevates to diabetes mellitus.” (Participant 2, female).

“There is DM that is a result of inheritance… there are families who have it and there are families who don’t have it… and there is that one that occurs as a result of eating food that is not good for your body.” (Participant 11, female).

“This disease comes in many ways… diabetes comes in many ways that I can’t really explain… but there are food products that are not adaptable to our bodies and when you are forced to eat those foods it can also cause diabetes Mellitus.” (Participant 10, female).

Although not in specific terms, some participants suggested a connection between hypertension and diabetes: “They are caused by the food we eat …at first the food we eat causes high blood and if you don’t go for a medical check-up and treatment, the body gets defeated by high blood then it elevates to diabetes mellitus”. (Participant 5, female)

Perceived causes of high blood pressure were mostly attributed to psychosocial factors. Emotional challenges, stress, worrying, overthinking, living in fear, family problems, passing through difficult times, food choices, and obesity are associated with an increase in blood pressure: “As I see it… when a person has high blood …is when they have stress and worrying and living in fear causes hypertension…also… maybe the food that we eat can also cause hypertension”. (Participant 3, female)

“I also had this kind of problem of hypertension, but I didn’t consult anywhere, I used to be overweight and had excess bleeding which I don’t have anymore maybe it was because of being overweight… according to the way I think being overweight can cause hypertension.… yes, because hypertension is an old condition which was only found mostly in older people but in the present moment it is seen in young people as well.” (Participant 14, female).

Participants described diabetic symptoms to include weight loss, loss of appetite, mood changes, darkening of the skin, dryness in the mouth, frequent urination, and perceived sexual performance issues in males.

“You as a person would already know that you have the disease, you will see changes in your body, such as losing weight, dizziness, and getting tired easily.” (Participant 3, female).

Most (11) participants mentioned as an intervention and treatment strategy the need to get checked up in the clinics and take pills/medication and self-injecting for treatment as recommended by health practitioners. On the other hand, cultural remedies, such as water mixed with sugar or a small amount of milk, were mentioned by other participants as solutions to alleviate diabetic symptoms. In addition, some participants mentioned the need for dietary modifications to manage diabetes.

“If you don’t do regular checkups or attend the clinic you will suffer from these conditions, even if it is HIV… if you don’t attend you won’t get better if you don’t attend the clinics. So this disease grows more when people don’t do regular checkups… because if you are just at home you don’t know if you are sick… but if you go to the clinic they will check you and let you know if you are sick…”.(Participant 8, female).

“…become better when they are given water mixed with sugar or a small amount of milk and the person would be better.” (Participant 13, female).

Discrepancies about the HCW communicating the NCDs diagnosis and treatment options

When asked about their knowledge, and experiences of language barriers in receiving healthcare, some participants noted discrepancies in the explanations provided at clinics, with some feeling that the information was limited and rushed. Some participants drew from their own experiences where they indicated that medical professionals did not use diagnostic tools or took less time to explain conditions to them leading to a desire for more detailed explanations and comprehensive communication particularly during the diagnosis of new health conditions: “ one day I went to the clinic and the doctor had those stereoscopes on his necks and did not used them on me… I asked why he did not use them on me and … he stood up touched me with them and went back to the table to write something on a paper and gave me pills and told me when to drink them…he did not explain anything to me….” (Participant 6, female).

… According to me, I think some improvements are needed especially when we go find out our health status, therefore health professionals should explain in detail so that we can understand. This is because if you don’t understand that you have DM and how it affects your body you disregard the advice and diagnosis due to the lack of understanding. (Participant 15, female).

Some participants reported that clinics are usually packed thus limiting the time that the healthcare professionals spend with each participant, a situation which subsequently inhibits the healthcare provider’s time to provide detailed explanations. This is regarded as a barrier, which has a negative impact on how the participants interpret and comply with medical advice.

“…, In clinics, they do not have time to tell us how these diseases are formed or the lifestyle changes needed to manage them is not that they are unable is just they don’t have time. They only explain when you ask … is just that they never explain… they never call us aside to explain this disease.” (Participant 4, male).

Importance of interpreters for understanding the NCDs, diagnostic and treatment terms

Participants recognize the potential benefits of having interpreters emphasizing the need for a deeper understanding of the causes and management of their health conditions. Science communicators are seen as valuable for enhancing comprehension, particularly when medical terminology is not fully understood. Understanding one’s health condition is perceived as empowering, encouraging individuals to take better care of themselves. Detailed explanations are linked to improved understanding and adherence to health advice.

“According to me, I think some improvements are needed especially when we go find out our health status, therefore the health professionals should explain in detail so that we can understand. This is because if you don’t understand that you have DM and how it affects your body you disregard the advice and diagnosis due to the lack of understanding”.” (Participant 8, female).

“At the clinics, they explain so that we understand even with the food we also understand the cause which I am satisfied with. I don’t think we need interpreters, because the nurse explain in our native language so I don’t see a need for an interpreter.” (Participant 13, female).

Discussion

The present study aimed to explore the participants’ perspective on language barriers relating to their understanding of chronic conditions. The findings of the present study identified a gap in knowledge and awareness of how NCDs develop or affect the body among the elderly participants. This was attributed to a lack of formal education with some participants reporting a reliance on other people, who may not be healthcare professionals, for knowledge. Being knowledgeable about NCDs and related treatment has been linked with better adherence to treatment, attainment of metabolic outcome goals, reduction in mortality and disability, improved quality of life, and reduced health care costs [24–28]. On the other hand, the lack of knowledge has been reported to lead to unsatisfactory medical outcomes due to a lack of correct remedial self-management on the part of the patient, and the failures of the health care providers [27, 29]. Although other studies reported that knowledge does not necessarily lead to risk-reducing behavior, as people may engage in unhealthy behaviors despite knowledge of their risks [27, 30], the importance of knowledge remains vital. This is because one’s knowledge and understanding of their chronic condition could inform the day-to-day risk evasion behavior, reduce the rate of disease progression, improve quality of life, and increase the life expectancy amongst the elderly population [31, 32]. Thus, there is a need for educational programs to educate the elderly of chronic condition for better self-management.

The findings of the present study further highlighted that some participants held the belief that diabetes predominantly, affected males and was associated with sexual dysfunction. Although some studies reported sexual dysfunctions to be associated with diabetes in men [33, 34], this does not suggest that DM affects men more than women and that women are immune to related complications. On the contrary, studies conducted in the DIMAMO area and sub-Saharan Africa reported DM to be more prevalent in women than men although the global prevalence shows a higher prevalence in men than women [35–37]. Although DM is established as a contributing factor for sexual dysfunctions [38], the stereotypes related to it may affect the health-seeking behavior among men, thus depriving them of the opportunity to learn about how to manage the condition [39]. Therefore, targeted educational interventions should be implemented to address the misconceptions about DM, with the goal of correcting gender-biased perceptions and providing accurate information about its diverse manifestations.

The participants in the present study linked DM with factors such as excessive sugar and fat consumption, poor dietary choices, and genetic inheritance. It is also noteworthy that the participants had limited knowledge of insulin or its role in DM. The participants further mentioned that the development of high blood pressure was associated with emotional challenges, stress, worrying and overthinking, living in fear, family problems, passing through challenging times, food choices, and obesity. Similar findings of perceived lifestyle and psychosocial causes of DM was reported in other studies [40, 41]. Although more needs to be done to educate the elderly about the causes of NCDs, the recognition of obesity, lifestyle, and psychosocial factors as potential causes of DM is notable, thus indicating an awareness of the impact of lifestyle on health.

Previous studies reported high blood pressure as “madi a magolo” which in the local Sepedi language translates to an excessive amount or too much blood [40–42]. In agreement with previous studies, the findings of the present study noted that most participants shared the same sentiments whereby high blood pressure was referred to as one having too much blood in the body. Furthermore, the participants associated high blood pressure with physical symptoms, such as cracks on the skin, heavy shoulders, voice problems, nosebleeds, and, interestingly, the consumption of beetroot. Similar views of blood pressure were reported in a study by Peltzer et al. [41], where participants said “madi a magolo is when the person has too much blood wandering in the body causing dizziness and headache This was further associated with head and nosebleeds as was the case in the present study. The notion held by the elderly participants of high blood pressure as too much blood “madi a magolo,” speaks to the barrier of language between the health practitioners and patients which may lead to misguided self-management practices which may bring more harm than good to the participants.

The findings of the present study revealed diverse perceptions of diabetic symptoms, which included weight loss, loss of appetite, mood changes, darkening of the skin, dryness in the mouth, frequent urination, and perceived sexual performance issues in males. Most participants mentioned as an intervention and treatment strategy the need to be checked up in the clinics and take pills/medication and self-injecting for treatment as recommended by health practitioners. Additionally, dietary modifications were acknowledged to manage diabetes. On the other hand, there seemed to be cultural beliefs and remedies that the participants believed had a therapeutic impact on hypertension. The main cultural intervention noted was bloodletting. Bloodletting therapy (BLT), also known as phlebotomy, blood donation, or collateral pricking therapy, is a practice of treating diseases through the removal of tiny amounts of blood from patients and has been used around the world for many centuries [43–45]. Although some trials that investigated the effect of bloodletting therapy on blood pressure compared to antihypertensive drugs revealed that blood pressure was significantly reduced in the BLT group [46, 47], the long-term risk of this practice remains unclear. Conversely, some medical scholars reported that bloodletting negatively affected more people than it helped [48], with associated risks including, an increased risk of thrombotic complications [49], risk of infection from the tools used to create the incisions for bleeding [50], and possible death due to blood loss. The findings of the present study highlight the coexistence of conventional, cultural perspectives and practices that need further investigation. Cultural believes may influence behaviors around seeking medical care in ways that inhibited health seeking [51].

The findings of the present study showed different views relating to communication and language barriers in healthcare settings. Some participants expressed satisfaction with the current practices at clinics and did not see the need for interpreters, especially when they receive prescribed medication and are told how to use them. These results are similar to those of a study conducted in Johannesburg [52]. Although the study focused on attitudes, cleanliness of the clinic, safe and effective care, infection control, and the availability of medicines and not language barriers, it reported general satisfaction among participants aged 50 years and above [52]. Conversely, some participants highlighted the need for interpreters and science communicators to facilitate a deeper understanding of health conditions. Furthermore, they highlighted that sometimes health practitioners take minimal time to explain conditions or rush through the process. Participants perceived time constraints at clinics, suggesting that healthcare professionals may lack sufficient time to offer detailed explanations, potentially affecting patient compliance with medical advice. Poor communication has been reported to lead to negative outcomes such as discontinuity of care, compromised patient safety, inefficient use of valuable resources, and patient dissatisfaction [53].

Limitations and strengths of the study

This research reflects the viewpoints of purposefully selected individuals from the rural black community residing in the DIMAMO area of Limpopo province, South Africa. Consequently, it is focused on a specific population and geographic location and cannot be generalized to other populations. Furthermore, the sample selectin of participants was based on those who were present on the AWI-Gen phase 2 results feedback session. However, the findings of the present study shed light on the experiences of elderly residents, specifically regarding language barriers in research and primary healthcare facilities within the DIMAMO area of Limpopo province, South Africa. Given the increased susceptibility of older individuals to age-related diseases, the study explored the effects of language barriers on the understanding and management of chronic conditions.

Conclusion

The present study found misconceptions about chronic diseases such as diabetes and hypertension. These misconceptions are related to perceptions that diabetes affects mostly males, and that hypertension refers to an increased quantity of blood. Additionally, most participants were found to use bloodletting practice to relieve hypertension symptoms in addition to the treatment they receive from health care facilities. We noted a barrier of communication that was related to a lack of time on the health practitioner site. Some participants highlighted the need for interpreters and science communicators to facilitate a deeper understanding of health conditions. Given the findings of the present study, we recommends the following; Increasing knowledge-sharing activities such as seminars conducted by healthcare professionals and all researchers to equip the elderly population with context-specific and accurate knowledge in the language that they understand and dispel myths and misconceptions with the hope that the knowledge they receive would affect how they self-manage their conditions. Furthermore, knowledge-sharing seminars between health practitioners (western) and traditional practitioners on the community’s health beliefs and practices, NCD treatment, etc. to produce culturally sensitive intervention programs. This is because community members consult both sides of practices for help and a balance of knowledge between the two schools of thought would benefit the participants positively. The findings of the present study highlight the need to intensify communication between patients and health practitioners to bridge the language gap.
