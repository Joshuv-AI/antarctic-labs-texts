Biomimetic M1 macrophage Membrane–Modified liposomes Co-delivering ponatinib and ginsenoside Rg3 for inhibition of breast cancer lung metastasis
Authors: Lei Quan, Yue Gao, Chen Zhang, Yue Ding, Sijuan Sun, Xinran Tai, Qingqing Yu, Dong Wang, Yumei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreast cancer ranks among the most prevalent malignant tumors in women worldwide, with lung metastasis accounting for over 90% of fatalities. Despite this high mortality, effective targeted therapies remain insufficient. Although ponatinib (PNT) shows potential for treating breast cancer lung metastasis (BCLM), its delivery requires optimization. This study aims to construct a biomimetic nanomedicine for targeted delivery of PNT, thereby efficiently treating BCLM.MethodsGinsenoside Rg3 (Rg3) can serve as a substitute for cholesterol as a membrane support material while also acting as an adjuvant in cancer treatment. We replaced cholesterol with Rg3 to prepare PNT-Rg3-Lipo liposomes, extracted the cell membrane of M1 macrophages to coat it on the surface of PNT-Rg3-Lipo, and obtained biomimetic nano MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo. The characterization of biomimetic nanomedicines was conducted, and the functionality of biomimetic nanomedicines was evaluated at both animal and cellular levels.ResultsThe macrophage membrane-modified MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo retains most proteins found on M1-type macrophages, including the characteristic proteins integrin α4 and β1. This macrophage membrane-modified MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo reduces macrophage phagocytosis, while enhancing uptake by metastatic breast cancer cells. In vitro, macrophage membrane-modified MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo exhibits a stronger cytotoxic effect on breast cancer cells compared to free PNT. In vivo, MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo shows a lower hemolysis rate, demonstrating its safety. Macrophage membrane modification guides liposomes to lung metastatic sites, leading to localized drug release and consequential inhibition of breast cancer lung metastases.ConclusionThe biomimetic macrophage membrane-coated liposomes, MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo, which we have innovatively prepared, exhibit promising therapeutic effects against breast cancer lung metastases.Keywords: Macrophage membrane, Ponatinib, Ginsenoside Rg3, Lung metastasis of breast cancer, Targeted delivery

Introduction

Breast cancer ranks as one of the prevalent forms of malignancy in women globally and stands as a primary contributor to the majority of cancer-related fatalities. The elevated mortality rate of breast cancer can be attributed to various factors, with metastasis to crucial organs being recognized as the primary underlying cause [1,2]. Reports indicate that 20% to 30% of individuals diagnosed with breast cancer may experience metastasis following their initial diagnosis and primary tumor treatment, and roughly 90% of cancer-related fatalities can be attributed to the spread of the disease to distant organs [3]. Patients with breast cancer who do not have metastasis have a five-year overall survival rate exceeding 80% [4]. Nonetheless, the occurrence of distant metastasis can result in a substantial decrease in this rate, reducing it to approximately 25% [5]. Unlike primary tumors, which can typically be surgically addressed through standard care protocols, the secondary foci of breast cancer are less amenable to surgical intervention. As a result, chemotherapy and radiotherapy currently stand as the primary treatment modalities for metastatic breast cancer [6]. Regrettably, the current drugs used to treat metastasis exhibit low delivery efficiency, with a significant portion failing to effectively reach the site of metastasis.

PNT is a third-generation oral tyrosine kinase inhibitor used in the treatment of leukemia. In the earlier stages of this study, our research group utilized high-throughput screening (HTS2) in conjunction with gene signatures associated with BCLM to identify potential anti-metastatic pharmaceutical compounds. We identified and validated the potential of PNT in suppressing BCLM and identified c-Jun as a potential target for therapeutic intervention in BCLM [7,8]. Furthermore, by employing a high-throughput screening method utilizing tumor immune phenotypic expression profiles, we have ascertained that PNT selectively inhibits the kinase activity of p38α mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), resulting in decreased phosphorylation of signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1). Subsequently, this modulates the expression of chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand (CXCL) 1 and CXCL2, ultimately reducing their secretion and the recruitment of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) from the bone marrow into the tumor microenvironment [9]. PNT demonstrates favorable therapeutic effects for various diseases, but its off-target effects may lead to cardiovascular issues, skin reactions, and gastrointestinal problems. Therefore, a significant challenge in current drug application is enhancing the drug's specificity, enabling targeted release at specific sites to achieve optimal treatment outcomes.

Liposomes are well-established nanomaterials used in drug and gene delivery [10]. Some of the benefits of liposomes include their high loading capacity, ease of preparation, and exceptional biocompatibility [6,11]. Nevertheless, they do not effectively home in on small, non-vascularized metastatic sites through the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effects. Macrophages are the part of the innate immune system that are known for phagocytizing or engulfing or eliminating the foreign materials, bacteria, virus or any pathogen from the human body by using the recognition receptors such as toll-like receptors, mannose receptors or scavenging receptors. The M1 phenotype of macrophages is characterized by its pro-inflammatory nature, as it releases a range of pro-inflammatory cytokines, reactive oxygen species, and reactive nitrogen intermediates [12]. These collective actions play a vital role in eliminating pathogens and damaged, unhealthy tissues. Many studies have demonstrated that M1 macrophages exhibit an affinity for cancerous cells, making them potentially valuable as carriers for delivering drugs in tumor therapy. The ability to target M1 macrophages is attributed to their surface membrane proteins, and macrophage-derived exosomes can also inherit this targeting capability. Moreover, it is possible to extract macrophage membranes for the purpose of coating various nanoparticles, thereby enhancing their targeting capabilities. Hence, the utilization of nanoparticles coated with macrophage membranes has been harnessed in the development of diverse therapeutics [13,14]. The application of a macrophage membrane coating allows cargo molecules to actively target inflamed sites by evading immune cell recognition. These particles not only travel through the bloodstream, similar to red blood cells and neutrophils, but they also possess the ability to selectively home in on tumor tissue. This homing mechanism relies on the interaction between α4 and β1 integrins on macrophages and vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) found on various cancer cells [15]. In this context, the use of a macrophage cell membrane coating on nanoparticles presents significant promise for drug delivery.

Ginsenoside Rg3 is a membrane-stabilizing substance with the ability to substitute for cholesterol in the lipid bilayer. Studies suggest that the glycosyl portion of Rg3 becomes exposed on the surface of liposomes containing Rg3, facilitating interaction with the highly expressed glucose transporter protein 1 (Glut1) on tumor cells. This interaction leads to the selective absorption of Rg3-lp/DTX by 4T1 cells and increased accumulation at tumor locations [16]. Furthermore, ginsenoside Rg3 exhibits dual anti-tumor and immune-regulatory properties, offering potential as a supplementary agent in cancer treatment. Research indicates that ginsenoside Rg3 enhances cellular toxicity and induces apoptosis in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells by inhibiting the nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) signaling pathway and modulating the expression of TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand/B-cell lymphoma-2, thereby potentiating the efficacy of paclitaxel [17,18]. Transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) has been recognized as a substantial catalyst for tumor metastasis [19,20]. Rg3 inhibits the release of TGF-β by tumor cells, regulates the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, and prompts activated cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) to transition into a dormant state, thus reducing the density of stromal barriers [21].

In this study, we successfully prepared macrophage membrane-mimetic PNT liposomes, enhancing its active targeting capability. Specifically, we encapsulated PNT and Rg3 within the liposomes, creating PNT-Rg3-Lipo. RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to induce the formation of M1-type macrophages. Subsequently, macrophage membranes were extracted and used to coat the PNT-Rg3-lipo liposomes. This article aims to investigate the enhancement of liposomes through macrophage membrane modification to improve their targeting capabilities for metastatic lesions, thereby inhibiting BCLM (Scheme 1). This study evaluates the inhibitory effects of MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo on metastasis in the 4T1 breast cancer cell line and the breast cancer lung metastasis mouse model.

Materials and methods

Materials

Ponatinib (PNT) was purchased from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Ginsenoside 20 (S)-Rg3 was purchased from Jingcui Tiancheng Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China). Cholesterol was provided by Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Egg yolk lecithin was purchased from AVT Pharmaceutical Tech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Coomassie brilliant blue stain solution and decolorization solution were supplied by Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Coumarin-6 (C6) was purchased from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Hoechst 33342 was obtained from Hengyu Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). 1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindotricarbocyanine iodide (DiR) and D-luciferin potassium were obtained from Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Dalian, China). CCK-8 was obtained from Baoguang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Chongqing, China). The 0.25% trypsin-EDTA was purchased from Servicebio (Wuhan, China). Penicillin and streptomycin were supplied by HyClone (Logan, UT, USA). All other chemicals and solvents used in this study were of reagent grade or high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) grade.

Cell culture

The 4T1-luc cells, RAW264.7 cells and MDA-MB-231 cells were purchased from the Shanghai Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China), and were verified to be error-free. Luc-4T1, MDA-MB-231 and RAW264.7 cells were developed in Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; ExCell Bio, Shanghai, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (HyClone, Logan, UT, USA). These cells were incubated at 37°C in a CO2-enriched environment with a 5% concentration of CO2.

Animals

Female BALB/c mice (20 ± 2 g) were provided by Beijing Huafukang Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China) and maintained under standard housing conditions of the Department of Experimental Animals, Chengdu University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Sichuan, China).

Ethics statement

All experiments involving animals were conducted according to the ethical policies and procedures approved by the ethics committee for Experimental Animal Welfare of Chengdu University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (approval number: 2023037).

Preparation and characterization of PNT-Rg3-Lipo

PNT-Rg3-Lipo was prepared using the thin film dispersion method. In brief, PNT, Rg3, and phospholipids (1:3:10, w/w/w) were dissolved in anhydrous ethanol. Subsequently, the mixture was subjected to evaporation under reduced pressure within a round-bottom flask, to form of a lipid film. The film was scattered into phosphate buffer saline (PBS, pH 7.4) at 45°C and underwent probe sonication for 3 min to produce PNT-Rg3-Lipo. Additionally, conventional PNT liposomes were prepared by dissolving PNT, cholesterol, and phospholipids (1:4:20, w/w/w) in anhydrous ethanol using the same method as above. The size and zeta potential of the prepared liposomes were measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS) with the LITESIZER 500 (Anton Paar, Graz, Austria). The morphology of the liposomes was observed under transmission electron microscopy (TEM) using the HT7800 instrument (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

The encapsulation efficiency (EE) and loading efficiency (LE) of PNT and Rg3 in PNT-Rg3-Lipo were determined using HPLC (LC-2030C, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). In simple terms, a 0.5 mL PNT-Rg3-Lipo solution was mixed with 1.5 mL of methanol, subjected to 10 min of sonication, and then filtered through a 0.45 μm microfilter. A 10 μL aliquot of the filtered solution was injected into the HPLC using a C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) to quantify the content of PNT and Rg3. PNT detection wavelength, 254 nm; flow rate, 1.0 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA)-containing water (Phase A) and acetonitrile (Phase B) with a gradient elution procedure: 0-10 min, from 90% A to 0% A, 10-12 min, 0% A, 12-15 min, from 0% A to 90% A, 15-18 min, 90% A. Rg3 detection wavelength, 203 nm; flow rate, 1.0 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% phosphoric acid-containing water (Phase A) and acetonitrile (Phase B) with a gradient elution procedure: 0-20 min, from 35% A to 55% A.

Additionally, the in vitro release patterns of PNT-Rg3-Lipo were ascertained using the dialysis method in PBS at varying pH values of 7.4, 5.8, and 4.8. In a nutshell, 2 mL of freshly prepared PNT-Rg3-Lipo was enclosed within dialysis bags with a molecular weight cut-off of 3500 Da. These bags were subsequently placed in 30 mL of different release media containing 1% Tween 80 and shaken at 150 rpm at 37°C. At specific time points, extract 1 mL of the release solution, and subsequently substitute it with 1 mL of freshly preheated release medium. The drug concentration in the release media was quantified using the previously mentioned HPLC analysis.

Isolation of macrophage membrane (MM)

M1 macrophages were induced by incubating RAW264.7 cells with DMEM media containing 10% FBS and LPS (1 μg/mL) for 24 h. The membranes of M1 macrophages were extracted, following the previously outlined method, with a slight adjustment [22]. The M1 macrophage membrane was acquired through the utilization of the Membrane and Cytosol Protein Extraction Kit (Cat. No. P0033; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). In summary, the harvested cells were dispersed in buffer solutions designed for membrane protein extraction and then chilled in an ice bath for 15 min. Following this step, the cell suspension was moved to a glass homogenizer and subjected to approximately 30 homogenization cycles. The homogenate was centrifuged at 700 g for 10 min at 4°C to remove cell nuclei and intact cells. Subsequently, the resulting mixture underwent centrifugation at 14,000 g for 30 min at 4°C to obtain the cell membranes. To procure MM vesicles, the macrophage membranes that had been extracted were initially subjected to 5 min of ultrasonication and subsequently extruded 10 times through a 400 nm polycarbonate porous membrane using an Avestin mini extruder (Model LF-1; Avestin, Ottawa, ON, Canada). The collected MM vesicles were preserved in PBS at 4°C.

Preparation and characterization of MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo

MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo were fabricated by coating PNT-Rg3-Lipo with MMs by ultrasonic method. Briefly, MM vesicles and PNT-Rg3-Lipo were mixed at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio of macrophage membrane to liposome, and sonicated for 5 min.

Characterization of proteins

To begin with, the protein profiles of RAW 264.7 cells, macrophage membranes, and MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo were assessed through an SDS-PAGE electrophoresis assay. The membrane proteins were isolated from RAW 264.7 cells, macrophage membranes, and MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo using a moderate RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). By contrast, a control was established with PNT-Rg3-Lipo lacking membrane decoration. The isolated membrane proteins were separated on a 10% Bis-Tris, utilizing a Bio-Rad electrophoresis system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The electrophoresis was conducted at 80 V for 0.5 h, followed by 100 V for 1 h. Lastly, the obtained polyacrylamide gel was stained with Coomassie brilliant blue for 3 h to enable visualization.

Cellular uptake

In vitro assessments were conducted to evaluate cell uptake in metastatic 4T1 cancer cells and murine RAW 264.7 macrophages. To prepare the fluorescent formulations (C6-Lipo, C6-Rg3-Lipo, and MM/C6-Rg3-Lipo), a trace amount of C6 was incorporated to substitute PNT, while strictly maintaining the identical lipid ratios and core manufacturing conditions. Specifically, C6 was first dissolved in suitable solvents strictly according to the manufacturer's instructions. The dissolved C6 was then thoroughly mixed with other lipid excipients. Following this, the mixtures underwent our standard liposome preparation procedure, which includes rotary evaporation to form a lipid film, hydration with PBS at 45°C, sonication in an ice bath for 3 min, and subsequent filtration through a 0.45 μm microfilter. For the biomimetic coating, the extracted M1 macrophage membrane vesicles were mixed with the fluorescent liposomes, followed by 10 min of probe sonication in an ice bath to yield MM/C6-Rg3-Lipo. The C6 content in each sample was subsequently quantified using HPLC to ensure an equivalent final C6 concentration of 100 ng/mL across all groups to meet the standardized dosage requirements for the in vitro assays. After incubation of cells with different drug formulations for 4 h, the cells were stained with Hoechst 33342 to label the cell nuclei. The uptake of free C6, C6-Lipo, C6-Rg3-Lipo, and MM/C6-Rg3-Lipo in metastatic 4T1 cancer cells and RAW 264.7 macrophages were visualized using laser confocal scanning microscopy (LCSM) (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Subsequent quantitative analysis was conducted with the fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA).

In vitro cytotoxicity

The cell-killing potential of each liposome group on 4T1 and MDA-MB-231 cells was assessed through the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. In simple terms, 6 × 103 cells were placed into each well of a 96-well plate. After 24 h, different concentrations, spanning from 0.001 μg/mL to 12 μg/mL, of free PNT, Rg3-Lipo, PNT-Lipo, PNT-Rg3-Lipo, and MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo were introduced into their respective wells. Unprocessed cells were employed as the negative control. Subsequently, after an additional 24 h of incubation, cell viability was assessed through the CCK-8 assay.

Western blot

After subjecting 4T1-luc cells to various treatments for 24 h, they were washed with PBS and subsequently subjected to lysis in a lysis buffer for 30 min. Protein concentrations were determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Kangwei Century Biotechnology, Beijing, China). Each set of protein samples was placed into the designated protein lane, separated by electrophoresis on a polyacrylamide gel utilizing the tris/glycine buffer system, and then transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. Anti-integrin α4 (ab75760; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-integrin β1 (ab179471; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-STAT1 (14994; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-p-STAT1 (8826; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-c-Jun (9165; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-p-p38 (4511; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-p38 (8690; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), and anti-GAPDH (60004-1-Ig; Proteintech, Wuhan, China) were incubated overnight at 4°C. The peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (GB23301 or GB23303; Servicebio, Wuhan, China) was applied, and the signals were visualized by introducing enhanced chemiluminescence western blotting detection reagents from Sizhengbai Biological Technology (Beijing, China). The lung tissue protein was acquired through grinding in a lysis buffer containing both a proteinase inhibitor and a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail, with subsequent steps following the previously outlined procedure.

Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

RNA was isolated from 4T1 cells subjected to various treatments for 24 h, employing TRIzol (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Reverse transcription was performed. Quantitative real-time PCR analyses using the comparative cycle threshold (CT) method were performed on fluorescence qPCR instruments (BIO-RAD, Hercules, CA, USA). After an initial incubation at 95°C for 3 min, amplification was performed for 40 cycles at 95°C for 10 s, 60°C for 10 s and 72°C for 20 s. The mouse CXCL1 primer pair was 5′-TCCAGAGCTTGAAGGTGTTGCC-3′ and 5′-AACCAAGGGAGCTTCAGGGTCA-3′. The mouse CXCL2 primer pair was 5′-CATCCAGAGCTTGAGTGTGACG-3′ and 5′-GGCTTCAGGGTCAAGGCAAACT-3′. The mouse CCL2 primer pair was 5′-CCAGCAAGATGATCCCAATGAGT-3′ and 5′-CATTAGCTTCAGATTTACGGGTC-3′. The mouse CXCL11 primer pair was 5′-CGCCCCTGTTTGAACATAAG-3′ and 5′-CTGCTGAGATGAACAGGAAGG-3′. The mouse GAPDH primer pair was 5′-CATCACTGCCACCCAGAAGACTG-3′ and 5′-ATGCCAGTGAGCTTCCCGTTCAG-3′.

In vitro blood compatibility tests

The hemolysis assay for MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo was carried out employing the in vitro direct contact method, as previously outlined [23]. To put it simply, fresh whole blood from the experimental rabbits was thinned by mixing it with a 0.9% (w/v) saline solution, resulting in a 2% red blood cell suspension. Every group sample was thinned to a concentration of 10 μg/mL of PNT. Subsequently, 750 μL of each group sample was combined with an equivalent volume of a 2% red blood cell suspension. The samples were placed in a stable-temperature water bath set at 37°C for a duration of 3 h, after which they were subjected to centrifugation at 1500 rpm for 15 min in a centrifuge. The absorbance of the clear liquid above the precipitate was assessed at 545 nm using a microplate reader (VARIOSKAN LUX; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to ascertain the amount of hemoglobin released from the ruptured red blood cells. The untreated negative control consisted of 0.9% (w/v) saline solution, while the positive control was double-distilled water. The hemolysis rate for each group sample was determined using formula 3.

In vivo distribution of MM/PNT-Rg3-lipo in lung metastasis model

To create the lung metastasis model for breast cancer, mice were injected with 4T1-luc cells via the tail vein at a concentration of 2 × 105 cells per mouse. On day 14, the mice received an intraperitoneal injection of D-luciferin potassium salt (10 μl/g), and an in vivo imaging system (IVIS Spectrum, PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) was employed for imaging to assess the development of lung metastasis. While conducting in vivo imaging, each drug group was tagged with the DiR lipophilic probe. The DiR-labeled formulations (DiR-Lipo, DiR-Rg3-Lipo, and MM/DiR-Rg3-Lipo) were prepared using the exact same protocols as the C6-labeled formulations, with DiR dissolved in appropriate solvents according to the manufacturer's instructions prior to mixing with lipid excipients. Subsequently, these drug groups were intravenously administered to mice at a dosage of 1 mg/kg (n = 3). Imaging was performed at intervals of 1.0, 2.0, 4.0, 8.0, 12.0, and 24.0 h to monitor the drug distribution within the body. Following the last imaging session, the mice were humanely euthanized, and specimens of the tissues were gathered. The IVIS Spectrum imaging system was utilized to visualize the distribution of DiR, DiR-Lipo, DiR-Rg3-Lipo, and MM/DiR-Rg3-Lipo in key organs to confirm their potential for targeting metastatic lungs.

In vivo anti-metastatic efficacy measurements

Following the methods mentioned above, establish a breast cancer lung metastasis model. After injecting 4T1-luc cells into the mice via the tail vein, the mice were randomly allocated into six groups, each comprising eight individuals. Commencing on day 4, the mice received intravenous injections of the following substances every other day: PBS, free PNT, Rg3-Lipo, PNT-Lipo, PNT-Rg3-Lipo, and MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo. Each group received a PNT dosage of 10 mg/kg, and the mice's body weight was monitored throughout the experiment. On day 25, the mice were humanely euthanized, and photographs of lung tissues from each group were taken to document the quantity of macroscopic metastatic nodules in the lungs to assess the impact on breast cancer lung metastasis inhibition. The weight of lung tissue for each group was assessed for the computation of the lung organ index. Concurrently, lung tissue samples were subjected to H&E staining to detect metastatic foci.

Statistical analysis

The data is reported in the format of mean ± standard deviation (SD). Two-group comparisons were assessed through a two-tailed Student's t-test. Multiple group comparisons were conducted using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post-hoc test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, with p < 0.01 signifying high significance.

Results

Characterization of MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo

The MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo preparation comprises a three-step process: (i) the preparation of PNT-Rg3-Lipo, (ii) isolating macrophage membranes, and (iii) cloaking the macrophage membrane onto PNT-Rg3-Lipo (Scheme 1). PNT-Rg3-Lipo is created via a thin-film dispersion technique using PNT, Rg3, and egg yolk phospholipids. DLS analysis shows that the hydrodynamic diameter of PNT-Rg3-Lipo is 104.4 ± 1.48 nm (Fig. 1A), with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.078 ± 0.017 and a zeta potential of 33.9 ± 0.1 mV. In the span of more than 10 d, the average diameter of PNT-Rg3-Lipo exhibits little to no change, demonstrating its excellent long-term stability (Fig. S1). Analysis using HPLC reveals that the encapsulation efficiency (EE) of PNT in PNT-Rg3-Lipo is 96.16 ± 0.59%, and that of Rg3 is 80.65 ± 3.29%, implying that PNT and Rg3 are nearly entirely encapsulated within the liposomes (Table 1). TEM measurements reveal that PNT-Rg3-Lipo appears as nanoscale spherical particles (Fig. 1B).

An in vitro release experiment was carried out to determine the sensitivity of PNT-Rg3-Lipo to acidic environments. PNT-Rg3-Lipo was introduced into release media with pH levels of 7.4, 5.8, and 4.8, and at various time intervals, samples were extracted for HPLC analysis to assess the release of PNT and Rg3 from PNT-Rg3-Lipo. The findings reveal that in acidic environments with pH levels of 4.8 and 5.8, PNT and Rg3 are released rapidly, whereas the release is comparatively slower in neutral conditions with a pH of 7.4. After 12 h, the cumulative release percentages of PNT from PNT-Rg3-Lipo under pH conditions of 4.8 and 5.8 amount to 64% and 52%, respectively, which are significantly greater than the release rate observed at pH 7.4 (Fig. 1C). Therefore, we speculate that in the weakly acidic environment of the tumor microenvironment, PNT-Rg3-Lipo undergoes a phase transition, leading to lipid membrane instability and facilitating rapid drug release.

At the same time, PNT-Lipo were formulated using the thin-film dispersion method to serve as a control for future experiments. The hydrodynamic diameter of PNT-Lipo is 162.02 ± 2.37 nm (Fig. 1A), with a PDI of 0.262 ± 0.003. TEM analysis shows that, like PNT-Rg3-Lipo, PNT-Lipo is composed of nanoscale spherical particles (Fig. 1B). Additionally, PNT-Lipo exhibits sustained stability in PBS with a pH of 7.4 for a duration of 10 d (Fig. S1).

RAW264.7 cells were exposed to LPS to induce their transformation into M1-type macrophages. Macrophage cell membranes were isolated using a reagent kit and then processed through ultrasonication to form macrophage membrane microvesicles. These microvesicles were mixed with PNT-Rg3-Lipo and underwent ultrasonication. After membrane decoration, the hydrodynamic MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo diameter was 130.28 ± 3.40 nm, which increased by 25 nm compared with PNT-Rg3-Lipo diameter, and PDI was 0.198 ± 0.015 (Fig. 1A). In PBS with a pH of 7.4, the particle size of MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo remained constant over 10 d, indicating good stability over time (Fig. S1). The zeta potential of MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo measured 8.1 ± 0.2 mV, which was notably less than the zeta potential of the unmodified PNT-Rg3-Lipo as indicated in Table 1. TEM observations demonstrated that the structures of MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo were of a nano-sized core-shell morphology (Fig. 1B).

Following that, SDS-PAGE electrophoresis was employed for the analysis of protein profiles in M1-type macrophages, macrophage membranes, and MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo. The macrophage membrane in MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo preserved the majority of proteins from the M1-type macrophage cell membrane, whereas no protein signal was observed in PNT-Rg3-Lipo. (Fig. 1D). There are reports indicating that macrophages can engage with metastatic cancer cells through the interaction of α4β1 integrin and VCAM-1 on the tumor cell surface [24]. To validate the quality of the extracted macrophage membrane, Western blot analysis was employed to detect two distinctive proteins, integrin α4 and β1, in M1-type macrophage cells, the extracted macrophage membranes, and MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo (Fig. 1E). β-actin was used as a reference to showcase the purity of the extracted cell membranes. Distinctive protein signals of integrins α4 and β1 were detected in M1-type macrophage cells, the extracted macrophage membranes, and MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo, affirming the presence of these integrin markers. Furthermore, the β-actin signal was readily discernible in M1-type macrophage cell samples, but exhibited minimal visibility in the extracted macrophage membranes and MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo, signifying a greater degree of purity in the isolated macrophage membranes. This evidence indicates successful camouflage of PNT-Rg3-Lipo with macrophage membranes in MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo.

In vitro assessment of immune-evasive functions and targeted delivery

Assessing the influence of M1-type macrophage modification on cellular uptake in metastatic breast cancer 4T1 cells. A fluorescent probe, C6, was used in place of PNT for liposome preparation, at a concentration of 100 ng/mL for every group. Free C6, C6-Lipo, C6-Rg3-Lipo, and MM/C6-Rg3-Lipo were co-incubated with 4T1 cells for 4 h. Cell uptake was then qualitatively observed using LCSM and quantitatively analyzed through FACS. In the LCSM images, the green signal corresponds to the different drug groups, while the blue signal corresponds to the cell nuclei. As shown in Fig. 2E, it is apparent that MM/C6-Rg3-Lipo demonstrates a higher uptake by 4T1 cells when compared to free C6, C6-Lipo, and C6-Rg3-Lipo, as indicated by a more pronounced green fluorescence signal. Rg3 functions not only as an adjuvant drug but also serves as an active targeting ligand and a membrane-stabilizing material. The hydrophilic part of Rg3, which includes the glucose moiety, is theoretically expected to protrude from the liposome surface. This characteristic makes it a suitable ligand for the overexpressed glucose transporter 1 (Glut1) in TNBC. This suggests that, once Rg3 is incorporated into liposomes, it may have the potential to target and capture circulating tumor cells (CTCs) [17]. The LCSM findings reveal that C6-Rg3-Lipo exhibits a more robust green fluorescence signal in comparison to both free C6 and C6-Lipo. Additionally, FACS analysis shows that in 4T1 cells, the cell uptake signal of C6-Rg3-Lipo is 1.68 times greater than that of C6-Lipo, indicating an improved uptake of C6-Rg3-Lipo by 4T1 cells. The cellular uptake signal of MM/C6-Rg3-Lipo surpasses that of C6-Rg3-Lipo by a factor of 1.25 (Fig. 2A and C). These findings are consistent with an enhanced tumor-cell interaction mediated by the biomimetic macrophage membrane coating. To further elucidate the underlying active targeting mechanism, a competitive blocking experiment was conducted. As illustrated in Fig. S2, LCSM observations revealed a stark reduction in intracellular fluorescence when 4T1 cells were pre-incubated with free MM vesicles. In alignment with the imaging data, quantitative FACS analysis showed that the MFI of the MM pretreatment + MM/C6-Rg3-Lipo group was significantly attenuated (Fig. S2B and C). This dual evidence from qualitative imaging and quantitative analysis strongly supports the conclusion that the macrophage membrane coating facilitates targeted delivery through specific receptor-ligand interactions.

A growing body of evidence indicates that nanostructures enveloped in macrophage membranes have the potential to suppress macrophage phagocytosis [25]. Hence, we evaluated the uptake of MM/C6-Rg3-Lipo by mouse RAW 264.7 macrophages through LCSM and FACS analysis (Fig. 2B–D, and F). C6-Rg3-Lipo displayed a more pronounced green fluorescence signal when contrasted with MM/C6-Rg3-Lipo. FACS analysis indicated a decrease of roughly 53% in the internalization of MM/C6-Rg3-Lipo by macrophages in comparison to the C6-Rg3-Lipo group. These findings are consistent with a reduced macrophage internalization, demonstrating that the biomimetic coating effectively confers immune-evasive functions to the liposomes.

The influence of MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo on the metastatic tumor microenvironment and the mechanisms involved

The enhanced cellular uptake of MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo, driven by the macrophage membrane coating, directly led to a higher intracellular accumulation of PNT and Rg3. Consequently, this elevated drug level laid the foundation for the increased cytotoxicity and the effective modulation of downstream signaling pathways observed later.

The cytotoxicity of MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo on 4T1 cells was evaluated utilizing the CCK-8 assay. Following 24 h of incubation with 4T1 cells, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were determined as follows: 1.207 μg/ml for free PNT, 0.52 μg/ml for PNT-Lipo, 0.45 μg/ml for PNT-Rg3-Lipo, and 0.37 μg/ml for MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo (Fig. 3A). These findings suggest that combining PNT and Rg3 increases cytotoxicity against 4T1 cells. Furthermore, the modification with macrophage membrane further amplifies the cytotoxicity against 4T1 cells. The cytotoxicity of the various formulations against metastatic breast cancer cells of the MDA-MB-231 cell line was comparable to that against 4T1 cells (Fig. S3). Additionally, we confirmed the cytotoxicity of Rg3-Lipo against both 4T1 and MDA-MB-231 cells, with IC50 values of 72.89 μg/ml and 74.93 μg/ml, respectively, for these two cell lines (Fig. S3).

The cytotoxicity of MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo on 4T1 cells and its regulatory mechanisms on the tumor microenvironment. (A) Cytotoxicity assessment in 4T1 cancer cells. (B) Conducting a Western blot analysis to compare the expression levels of p-STAT1, STAT1, c-Jun, p-p38, and p38 in 4T1 cells following various treatment regimens. Semi-quantitative results of p-STAT1/STAT1 (C), c-Jun (D), and p-p38/p38 (E) relative levels in 4T1 cells determined by Western blot. Tumors can be broadly classified into two categories: "cold" tumors, characterized by minimal T cell infiltration and inflammation, and "hot" tumors, marked by high T cell infiltration, primarily influenced by the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines [26]. An attribute of hot tumors is the abundance of TH1-type chemokines, such as the recruitment chemokine CXCL11. On the other hand, cold tumors exhibit an accumulation of immunosuppressive chemokines in the microenvironment, such as CXCL1 and CXCL2. Presently, numerous studies are centered on transforming "cold" tumors into "hot" tumors in order to improve responses to immunotherapy [26]. The research group previously identified and verified ponatinib as an effective anti-tumor drug that can suppress the expression of CXCL1 and CXCL2 by inhibiting the p38α-STAT1 signaling pathway, thereby inhibiting the infiltration of MDSCs into the tumor. Furthermore, PNT predominantly inhibits JUN transcription, hastening the degradation of c-Jun protein, which, in turn, restrains the expression of BCLM-related genes via the ERK/c-Jun signaling pathway [7]. It has been reported that Rg3 can hinder the activation of STAT3 and mitigate the release of C-C chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2), which is a tumor-derived chemokine pivotal in the formation of tumor metastatic foci [16]. Therefore, to elucidate the mechanism of action of MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo, we employed western blotting to assess the expression of p-STAT1, p-p38, and c-Jun in the 4T1 cell line after treatment with various drug formulations (Fig. 3B). The results demonstrated that in 4T1 cells, PNT treatment led to a reduction in p-STAT1 expression, which was significantly lower in the MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo group compared to the free PNT group (Fig. 3C). Moreover, PNT-Rg3-Lipo and MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo were more effective in downregulating p-p38 protein expression compared to the free PNT group (Fig. 3E). The expression of c-Jun protein was significantly decreased in the free PNT, PNT-Lipo, PNT-Rg3-Lipo, and MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo groups (Fig. 3D). This indicates that MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo has a better effect on downregulating p-STAT1 and p-p38 compared to the free PNT group.

Furthermore, to elucidate the combined effects of PNT and Rg3, we used quantitative PCR to measure the expression levels of downstream chemokines CXCL1, CXCL2 in the p38α-STAT1 signaling pathway, and two additional chemokines, CXCL11 and CCL2. The results demonstrated that Rg3 significantly downregulated the expression levels of chemokines CCL2 and CXCL2, while PNT significantly downregulated the expression levels of CCL2, CXCL1, and CXCL2, and upregulated CXCL11 expression. When used in combination, compared to PNT monotherapy, the combination significantly downregulated mRNA expression levels of CCL2, CXCL1, and CXCL2, while upregulating CXCL11 mRNA expression (Fig. S4). This indicates that Rg3 enhances the pro-tumor immune effects of PNT by downregulating the expression of chemokines CCL2, CXCL1, and CXCL2 and upregulating the expression of CXCL11. We also employed quantitative PCR to analyze the mRNA expression levels of the four chemokines in the 4T1 cell line after treatment with various formulations (Fig. 3F, G, H and I). The results indicated that PNT-Rg3-Lipo and MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo were more effective in downregulating the expression levels of chemokines CCL2, CXCL1, and CXCL2 and upregulating CXCL11 expression compared to the free PNT group. This effect can be attributed to the combination of PNT and Rg3 and possibly enhanced uptake of PNT-Rg3-Lipo and MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo by 4T1 cells.

Hemocompatibility and targeting ability of MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo towards established lung metastases

Hemocompatibility is a crucial safety measure for evaluating biomaterials, especially those directly in contact with blood [23]. Hence, the hemocompatibility of each drug group was evaluated through an in vitro direct contact approach. Visual hemolysis images demonstrated that a PNT concentration of 10 μg/mL, PNT-Lipo, PNT-Rg3-Lipo, and MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo exhibited no significant hemolysis compared to free PNT (Fig. 4A and B). These results indicate that PNT-Lipo, PNT-Rg3-Lipo, and MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo are non-hemolytic.

The investigation into the targeting capability of MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo was conducted in a mouse model featuring confirmed lung metastases. After injecting 4T1-luc cells into the mouse tail vein, a bioluminescence imaging (BLI) was conducted 14 d later to verify the establishment of a model simulating breast cancer lung metastasis (Fig. 4C). Later, free DiR, DiR-Lipo, DiR-Rg3-Lipo, and MM/DiR-Rg3-Lipo were introduced via the mouse's tail vein. Imaging to track the distribution of each drug within the mouse's body was carried out at various time intervals post-administration (Fig. 4C). As shown in Fig. 4C, free DiR and DiR-Lipo did not exhibit significant accumulation in the lungs within 24 h after administration. However, there was an improved accumulation of DiR-Rg3-Lipo and MM/DiR-Rg3-Lipo at the metastatic sites. To further confirm the targeting activity of MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo, mice were euthanized 24 h after administration, and ex vivo tissues were subjected to bioluminescence and chemiluminescence imaging. As observed in Fig. 4D, T1-luc formed lung nodules in lung tissues, displayed as blue-green fluorescence. MM/DiR-Rg3-Lipo exhibited significantly higher enrichment on lung nodules compared to the other three groups. Therefore, the results indicate that MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo possesses targeting ability towards established lung metastases.

The inhibitory effect of MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo on TNBC lung metastasis

On day 0 of the experiment, 4T1-luc cells were intravenously injected into the mouse tail vein to induce TNBC lung metastasis. The mice were randomly divided into 6 groups, and starting on day 4 of the experiment, they were intravenously administered the drugs every 2 d for a total of 11 doses. The PNT dosage was 10 mg/kg (Fig. 5A). After the final dose, the lung tissues were extracted, weighed, photographed, and the number of lung nodules was recorded. From Fig. 5B and C, it can be observed that the PBS group had the highest number of lung nodules, confirming the successful establishment of the lung metastasis model. PNT-Rg3-Lipo and MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo also exhibited lower lung organ coefficients compared to free PNT (Fig. 5D). Additionally, PNT-Lipo, PNT-Rg3-Lipo, and MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo alleviated the weight loss induced by the lung metastasis model (Fig. 5E).

Impact of MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo on the tumor microenvironment and its mechanisms in vivo

Fig. 6A shows the results of the lung tissues of mice from different treatment groups stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). As shown in Fig. 6A, both PNT-Rg3-Lipo and MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo significantly reduce lung nodules compared to other groups. To investigate the underlying mechanisms, we analyzed the expression of p-STAT1, p-p38, and c-Jun in lung tissue nodules of mice treated with different drug regimens using Western blot (Fig. 6B). Quantitative analysis of the results indicates that in lung nodules, p-STAT1 expression is reduced after PNT treatment, and MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo further lowers p-STAT1 compared to free PNT (Fig. 6C). There is a trend of decreased p-p38 protein expression in MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo group compared to the free PNT group (Fig. 6E). Moreover, the protein expression of c-Jun is decreased in the MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo group compared to the free PNT group (Fig. 6D). This suggests that MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo exhibits superior effects in downregulating p-STAT1, p-p38, and c-Jun compared to free PNT. Similarly, we used quantitative PCR to analyze the mRNA expression levels of four chemokines in lung nodules after different drug treatments. The results show that MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo is more effective in downregulating CCL2 mRNA expression levels and upregulating CXCL11 mRNA expression levels compared to the free PNT group (Fig. 6F and I). In lung nodules, the free PNT group significantly decreases the mRNA expression levels of CXCL1 and CXCL2 compared to the PBS group, and PNT-Lipo, PNT-Rg3-Lipo, and MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo exhibit a trend of further downregulation of CXCL1 and CXCL2 mRNA expression levels compared to free PNT, with MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo showing the most significant downregulation trend (Fig. 6G and H). This indicates that MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo can enhance the delivery of therapeutic drugs to lung metastatic sites, allowing drug PNT to exert its therapeutic effect more effectively by inhibiting the p38 MAPK/STAT1 pathway and subsequently regulating downstream chemotactic factors.

Discussion

Transmissible diseases continue to be a leading contributor to cancer-related deaths. Among the vital organs impacted by breast cancer metastasis, the lungs are one of the most common sites [27]. Metastatic lesions frequently render surgery impractical, and the limited retention of most drugs at metastatic sites hinders their effectiveness [28]. Biomimetic nanodrug delivery systems have shown benefits in addressing metastatic tumor therapy. Previous research in our team has indicated that PNT holds potential for treating breast cancer lung metastasis; however, its off-target effects may result in various adverse reactions. Therefore, enhancing the targeting of PNT becomes a crucial step in advancing its clinical use. Rg3 can not only replace cholesterol in the cell membrane but also function as an additional anticancer agent. Shen-Yi capsules, predominantly composed of ginsenoside Rg3, received approval in China in 2000 and have been employed in clinical practice alongside chemotherapy to enhance the therapeutic outcomes for breast and lung cancer patients [29,30]. In this study, PNT and Rg3 were co-encapsulated in liposomes. The extracted M1 macrophage membrane was enveloped around liposomes loaded with PNT-Rg3-Lipo, forming biomimetic MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo. Since macrophages "homing" on the lung metastatic sites, MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo can target the lung metastatic sites and release therapeutic drugs PNT and Rg3 in the lung tissue, thereby achieving the inhibition of metastatic tumor colonization.

Cancer development and progression go hand in hand with changes in the neighboring stromal tissue. Cancer cells can actively shape their microenvironment by releasing a variety of cytokines, chemokines, and other signaling molecules. This leads to a reprogramming of the neighboring cells, empowering them to have a decisive impact on tumor survival and advancement [31]. Research has demonstrated the pivotal role of chemokines and their receptors in influencing the metastatic destination of tumor cells [32]. Among the chemokines released by TAMs, CXCL1 stands out as the most prevalent. CXCL1 is known to enhance the migratory and invasive capabilities of breast cancer, and it induces epithelial-mesenchymal transition in both mouse and human breast cancer cells [33]. The chemokine CXCL2 facilitates the infiltration of M2 macrophages and promotes tumor cell metastasis [34]. Additionally, CCL2 exhibits robust chemotactic properties, recruiting monocytes and macrophages. Numerous studies provide compelling evidence that the overexpression of CCL2 fosters tumor metastasis, invasion, and immune evasion. Nevertheless, some research findings suggest that CCL2 expression can also trigger infiltration of anti-tumor inflammatory monocytes. The various mechanisms through which CCL2 controls the tumor immune microenvironment are tightly regulated by tumor cells, immune cells infiltrating the tumor, and the surrounding tumor stroma [35].

STAT1 possesses the capacity to elevate the expression of numerous pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and enzymes, including but not limited to tumor necrosis factor-α, cyclooxygenase-2, and inducible isoform of nitric oxide synthase, all of which can potentially contribute to the initiation of tumorigenesis [36]. The p38 MAPK plays a role in promoting both metastasis and resistance to chemotherapy [37]. In this study, the biomimetic MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo releases the drug at the site of transfer, thereby enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of PNT. Specifically, PNT inhibits the p38 MAPK-STAT1 signaling pathway and cooperatively regulates the expression of chemokines CXCL1, CXCL2, CCL2 and CXCL11 with Rg3 in the tumor microenvironment, thereby inhibiting the progression of metastasis.

Conclusions

In summary, we have developed macrophage membrane-modified PNT-Rg3-Lipo liposomes (MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo) that can be targeted to breast cancer lung metastatic sites. The macrophage membrane modification enhances the uptake of liposomes by metastatic 4T1 cells, while reducing macrophage uptake. Additionally, it augments cytotoxicity against 4T1 cells. In a mouse model of breast cancer lung metastasis, MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo exhibits improved targeting of lung metastatic lesions, releasing the loaded drugs PNT and Rg3 into the metastatic microenvironment. PNT achieves inhibition of the p38/STAT1 signaling pathway and, in synergy with Rg3, regulates the expression of chemokines CXCL1, CXCL2, CCL2, and CXCL11, thereby suppressing breast cancer lung metastasis. In conclusion, we have successfully prepared a biomimetic MM/PNT-Rg3-Lipo, capable of efficiently delivering PNT and Rg3, offering a promising avenue for the clinical application of the potential therapeutic agent PNT in breast cancer lung metastasis.
