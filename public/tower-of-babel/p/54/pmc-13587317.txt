Liver transplant for paediatric acute liver failure: insights from a newly established paediatric liver transplant program in a developing country
Authors: Meranthi Fernando, Suchintha Tillakaratne, Bhagya Gunathilake, Rachini Withanage, Chamila Liyanage, Aruna Weerasuriya, Chinthaka Appuhamy, Eranga Ganewatte, Liyanage Ranaweera, Pamalee Herath, Rohan Siriwardana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLiver transplantation (LT) is the cure for medically refractory acute liver failure (ALF). In resource-limited settings, establishing a paediatric LT program for paediatric ALF (PALF) encounters unique challenges.MethodsThis study describes the initial 5-year experience of the paediatric LT (PLT) program at the Colombo North Centre for Liver Diseases (CNCLD), Sri Lanka (March 2020 to March 2025). Data from all PALF cases (n = 35) were retrospectively analyzed.ResultsA total of 120 children were referred to LT and 35 (29%) were due to ALF. Median age was 8 years 9 months (range: 2 months – 13 years) and 63% were male. Common etiologies included Wilson disease (23%) and autoimmune liver disease (20%). Spontaneous recovery occurred in 43% (15) while 34% (12/35) underwent LT and 23% (8) died without LT. Of 12 transplant recipients, 8/12 received living donor liver transplants (LDLT) (5/8 related donors, 3/8 altruistic – Buddhist monks), and 4/12 received deceased donor liver transplants (DDLT). The median time from listing to transplant was 72 h. Post-transplant survival in one and three months was 75% and 66% respectively. There were three immediate post-LT deaths due to primary non-function of deceased donor grafts.ConclusionPALF comprised a substantial proportion of referrals in Sri Lanka’s nascent PLT program. The majority of LT for PALF were LDLT with favorable outcomes compared to DDLT. Optimum utilization of a budding LT program with timely referrals and availability of altruistic donors have played key roles in the success of the PLT program in a resource limited setting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07252-0.Keywords: Liver transplantation, Paediatric acute liver failure, Newly established liver transplant programme, Sri Lanka, Living donor liver transplant outcomes.

Introduction

Liver transplantation (LT) is a highly specialized treatment that requires a multidisciplinary healthcare team, advanced infrastructure, and a constant supply of liver donors [1–3]. These prerequisites are closely linked to a country’s socioeconomic status [1, 4] As nations progress in medical technology and specialized care, they often encounter substantial challenges in developing and maintaining sustainable transplant programs [3].

Sri Lanka is a developing country located in Southeast Asia, with an estimated population of 22 million [5]. Although it has a well-established primary healthcare system, resulting in health indices comparable to those of developed nations in the region, tertiary care services and an effective referral system between different levels of healthcare are still under development [6–8]. Sri Lanka firstly established its paediatric liver transplant (PLT) program in 2020 in comparison to the world’s first liver transplant performed in 1963 [9].

Currently, the Colombo North Centre for Liver Diseases (CNCLD) is the only specialized center providing paediatric liver transplantation services for the entire country, and the program remains in its early developmental phase and reached the timeline of initial 5 years of service by March 2025 [1, 3, 10].

Acute liver failure (ALF) accounts for 10–20% of pediatric liver transplants in high-volume centers in Western countries [11]. In contrast, data from the Indian subcontinent indicate that only 2 out of 100 pediatric liver transplants are performed for liver failure, reflecting huge disparity between two healthcare systems [12] Only a limited number of pediatric ALF (PALF) etiologies have specific medical therapies [13], and management is predominantly supportive, aimed at promoting liver regeneration [14, 15]. However, once hepatic necrosis exceeds the liver’s regenerative capacity, timely liver transplantation becomes the only life-saving option [16]. In children, ALF progresses rapidly, providing a narrow window for assessment and transplantation [14, 16–18]. Successful outcomes depend on optimal pre-transplant care, timely referral, and emergency LT once listed for surgery. Even in highly specialized centers, performing liver transplantation for PALF remains a significant challenge due to difficulties in assembling the team, infrastructure, logistics, ethical approval for donation and finding a suitable graft at emergency situations. Outcomes are also documented to be less favorable compared to elective LTs.

The initiation of a pediatric liver transplant program within a developing healthcare system presents distinct and significant challenges. Addressing these requires context-specific solutions that align with the country’s unique circumstances and available resources. This report outlines the challenges faced and outcomes observed during the early stages of the liver transplant program at the CNCLD in Sri Lanka.

Methods

All the records of the children referred with PALF to CNCLD, over a period of 5 years (March 2020 – March 2025) were prospectively maintained following institutional approval and patient consents. Acute liver failure was defined using paediatric acute liver failure study group (PALFSG) diagnostic criteria which included: the absence of chronic liver disease; biochemical evidence of acute liver injury and coagulopathy Prothrombin Time (PT) ≥ 20 or International Normalized Ratio (INR) ≥ 2.0 regardless of the presence or absence of encephalopathy, or PT ≥ 15 or INR ≥ 1.5 in the presence of encephalopathy not corrected by vitamin K [16]. Institutional approval was obtained to extract data from the already maintained records and the patient database. centres database. All study subjects were less than 16 years of age at the time of referral.

At the clinical set up, on referral to CNCLD, all the paediatric patients with ALF were evaluated promptly to delineate a cause in parallel to their intense clinical management.

Diagnostic work up was mainly focussed on treatable causes such as infections, autoimmune liver diseases and Wilson diseases as per the age-based evaluation approach by North American Society for Paediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (NASPGHAN) guidelines 2022 [19]. The patients who were confirmed to have treatable causes were commenced on their respective treatment promptly.

Patients were monitored with INR, blood gas lactate, level of encephalopathy and cerebral oedema in view of their sensorium and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS). Patients with worsening GCS, coagulopathy, or lactate despite standard supportive care with disease specific medications, were listed for liver transplant, following multidisciplinary discussion. King’s Wilson index (KWI) was used additionally to assist listing for LT for clinical cases of Wilson disease. KWI of 11 or more with progression of the disease despite copper chelators were considered as an indication for LT following Multi-Disciplinary Team (MDT) discussion and clinical assessment [19].

All the children with ALF were managed as per the standard guidelines with supportive care including parenteral vitamin K, blood products guided by thromboelastography, fluid restriction to minimise cerebral oedema, organ support, prophylactic antibiotics, and antifungals along with albumin infusions and diuretics. Plasma exchange was considered in limited occasions especially when timely liver grafts were not available.

Family members were kept informed about the potential need for liver transplant on their first counselling at the time of admission. The need for LT was discussed in a multidisciplinary specialist meeting. When the clinical condition deemed necessary for transplantation patients were listed for deceased donor graft following confirmation of the brain death. As the likelihood of receiving a DDLT graft is limited, living donor transplants were offered for all indicated ALF patients and the same time family counselling is continued to find a related donor. When a suitable family member is not available for donation, altruistic donors were considered from the unit’s living liver donor registry.

The ‘living donor liver registry’ at CNCLD is open for an any adult a who would volunteer or come forward on altruistic basis, to donate part of the liver for a child or an adult requiring liver transplant, in the absence of a suitable related living donor from the patient’s family or delayed receipt of an deceased donor on the waiting list. This is not advertised and totally comes on voluntary basis, and the medical panel will choose the recipient anonymously to avoid organ misuse.

All potential altruistic donors underwent a comprehensive psychosocial evaluation by an independent multidisciplinary team. This assessment included evaluation of donor motivation, understanding of risks, capacity for informed consent, and donation proceeded only after the independent assessment team was satisfied that informed consent was freely given. Listed donors were matched to the potential patients by the MDT team, solely considering the medical grounds, Further, the consent from the next of kin is obtained from the altruistic donors and they would go through ethical approval process from the ministry of health. In our observation, it had been Buddhist monks who mostly come forward voluntarily as altruistic donors due to religious beliefs’.

Living donors were evaluated using previously described criteria [20]. In the event for living liver donation, emergency ethical approval was obtained from the ministry of health for the organ donation.

Primary end points of this study were LT, recovery from ALF with medical care or death with or without LT. The results were analysed using descriptive statistics for the demographic characteristics, aetiology of ALF, outcome pathways in terms of medically recovered, liver transplant required or died without liver transplant. The subjects who required liver transplant were further analysed for the donor type and transplant outcomes. Data analyses were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) for Windows (SPSS 22.0) statistical software package with the use of n (actual number, percentage and the p value where the significance is kept as p < 0.005).

Results

In total, 120 patients were referred for liver transplants during this period. Out of this, 35 (29%) patients were referred with acute liver failure. Of them 63% were males (Fig. 2).

Majority of the patients (85%) with ALF belonged to the lower income category according to World Health Organization (WHO) classification [21]. The median age was higher in the ALF group (6.5 vs. 3.5 years, p = 0.108), with a similar male predominance in both groups. Wilson’s disease was the commonest underlying diagnosis among ALF patients (42%), whereas biliary atresia (32%) and Wilson’s disease (26%) were the leading diagnoses in the non-ALF group. Of the 35 patients that presented with ALF, 23% (8/35) had genetically confirmed Wilson disease, 20% (7/35) were autoimmune liver disease. Of the 12 cases of autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) 10 were type 1 AIH and 2 were type 2 AIHs. The diagnosis was made with the liver biopsy evidence in all cases either and was assisted by raised IgG and auto-antibodies.

In this cohort, 34% (12/35) were due to indeterminate causes and 23% (8/35) were due to other causes including metabolic diseases other than Wilson’s disease; Neimann Pick Type C disease (1), Galactosemia (1), PFIC (1), drug induced liver disease (1), Paracetamol poisoning (1), Hepatitis A – viral hepatitis (2), Budd-Chiari syndrome (1) Table 1.

On evaluation of the clinical features 88% (31/35) had jaundice, 80% (28/35) had hepatic encephalopathy at the time of referral and 20% (7/35) had bleeding manifestations. Fever was observed in 74% (26/35) while 23% (8/35) had chest infections, 20% (7/35) had hypotension.

Of the biochemical parameters 20% (7/35) had hypoglycaemia, 40% (14/35) had low K, 28% (10/35) had low Phosphate. Ventilator care was required due to progressive cerebral oedema in 5/35 (14%) children prior to LT. Blood ammonia levels were routinely not done due to non-availability. only one patient underwent therapeutic plasma exchange pending LT.

The number of PALF patients who recovered with supportive care with or without specific medical management was 43% (15/35). The rest of the 20/35(57%) were eventually diagnosed as progressive, medically refractory ALF and were listed for LT (Table 2). Of them 40% (8/20) died without LT. The reasons for not having liver transplant were, unsuitable to go through LT due to late referral and haemodynamic instability (3/8), unavailability of a suitable liver graft (3/8) and other reasons (2/8 – died due to COVID-19 infection while awaiting LT and LT contraindicated due to Neiman Pick type C) (Table 2).

Out of the 35 patients presented with ALF, 20 were listed for LT and 12 (34%) were able to undergo LT. Of them 67% (8/12) were living donor LT (LDLT) while 33% (4/12) were deceased donor liver transplants (DDLT). Out of the LDLTs 62% (5/8) were from related donors while 38% (3/8) were from altruistic donors (Fig. 2). All three altruistic donors were Buddhist monks. Median time taken from listing to LT was 72 h (range 24 h – 14 days) in LDLT while it was 48 h, 72 h, 72 h and 4 days in four cases of DDLT.

The cold ischemic time for the LDLT was minimal as grafts were implanted immediately after cold perfusion. The warm ischemic time for the LDLT was 48 min ( 40–55 min). The cold ischemic time for the four DDLT grafts ranged from 6 h to 9 h. The warm ischemic time ranged from 45 min to 60 min.

Mean age of the children who underwent LT was 6 years and 6 months (5 month – 13 years) Out of the 12 children who underwent LT due to ALF 58% (7/12) were males. Time taken from listing to LT was 72 h (range 24 h – 14 days) 8% (1/12) had a right lobe graft, 67% (8/12) were left lobe grafts and others were 25% (3/12) were left lateral grafts. Median GRWR was 1.55 (1.1–4.4) and median weight of the grafts were 254 g (176 –600 g), Median hospital stay after the transplant was 21 days (8days – 66 days).

The main post-operative complications in this cohort were primary non-function of the graft (3/12), infections (12/12), rejection (4/12) and acute kidney injury (2/12).

Overall, 3-month mortality for patients presenting with acute liver failure was 34% (12/35).

Sixty percent (12/20) mortality was reported in patients who progressed to indication for transplant.

There were 8 living donors in total who donated for 8/12 cases of paediatric acute liver failure patients. There were no mortalities in this cohort, and all donors were discharged from the hospital within 7–10 days of hospital stay. The main morbidities were post-operative pain (8/8), surgical site infection 1/8 and a chest infection 1/8. Of the outcomes of LDLT all children who had LDLT survived and the 1-year survival after LDLT was 100%.

Discussion

Liver transplantation for acute liver failure presents significant challenges and is associated with poorer outcomes compared to other indications for LT in children [16, 17]. In paediatric patients, ALF progresses rapidly, leaving a narrow window for timely transplantation [17]. In countries with well-established DDLT programs, obtaining a cadaveric graft within the critical window is challenging as with previous data, the waiting time is 4 days (range: 2–10) for emergency patients [10] However, in our setting, due to poor awareness of the public about deceased donation and lack of proper system to coordinate the distribution of diseased donor grafts are still in its early stages of development, the prompt availability of deceased donor grafts remains a major constraint for timely LT especially in ALF. Against this backdrop, managing a newly established LT program in which 29% of referrals present with ALF have posed a significant challenge [20–22].

In our program 8/12 PALF grafts were from living donors. The successful implementation of a DDLT program requires a well-structured and extensively developed healthcare network and infrastructure [22]. The lack of such systems has contributed to the limited success of DDLT programs in regions such as Asia and Africa [23]. As we experienced in our setting, living donor liver transplantation emerges as a viable alternative, particularly for paediatric recipients, given the relative technical simplicity of donor hepatectomy and its favourable safety profile [24]. Though it is important to setup DDLT programs long-term, changing the whole healthcare infrastructure and system that suits effective DDLT program is a task that align with the development of the whole country. Rather more practical approach in resource-limited settings would be to concentrate on efforts to enhance LDLT, which can be easily achieved, by individual efforts enhancing human resources and infrastructure within a single dedicated centre. This targeted strategy offers a feasible pathway for advancing liver transplantation programs in countries like ours.

In our experience at the initial stages of the program living donors had a better post-transplant outcome compared to DDLT. Of the total 4 deceased donor grafts, 3 had primary non-function and died. As both DDLT and LDLT were undertaken concurrently throughout the study period, the favourable outcomes observed with LDLT cannot be explained by a preceding phase of DDLT experience alone. Superior graft outcomes have been previously reported with living donors [25] This observation also highlights the possible shortcomings of DDLT splits in developing healthcare systems. Limitations in donor maintenance in the intensive care units, delays in organ retrieval and quality maintenance in organ preservation solutions all could have led to poor outcomes in back table liver splits performed in our setting.

In our experience, the median time from listing to liver transplantation was 72 h. Related donors were available for 5/12 patients, while the remaining 3/12 were altruistic donors, consisting of Buddhist monks who had pre-registered with our centre for organ donation. As a transplant unit, we maintain an altruistic donor registry, where donors undergo comprehensive medical and psychological evaluations before being included in the database [26, 27]. This follows ethical approval from the ministry of Health in view of their voluntary decision on donating part of liver. This system enabled rapid donor-recipient matching, significantly reducing the time to transplantation. Although altruistic donation remains a topic of ethical debate due to concerns regarding donor safety and the potential for commercial transactions, it plays a critical role in saving lives, particularly in paediatric acute liver failure cases [26, 27]. Once the donor from the donor pool is matched with the recipient, there is a pathway to obtain rapid ethical approval for the liver transplant. Establishing and maintaining a fully evaluated and pre-registered altruistic donor database is a viable strategy for countries without well-developed DDLT programs, especially for super-urgent cases requiring immediate transplantation.

Our referral patterns were unique, with 29% of LT referrals being for ALF. This proportion is significantly higher than that reported in other available data, reflecting traditional physician attitudes and awareness in the absence of a liver transplant program [11, 13, 16]. For many years, chronic liver disease (CLD) and failed Kasai procedures were considered terminal conditions in Sri Lanka, with no curative options available. Consequently, these patients were typically managed with palliative care. As LT is still a relatively a new development in the country, awareness remains limited, and many physicians and primary care providers continue to perceive CLD as an untreatable condition [4]. In contrast, ALF presents as an acute, life-threatening event, prompting urgent referrals to major medical centres. This pattern has led to the eventual transfer of ALF patients to the transplant centre, highlighting the need for greater awareness and education regarding liver transplantation as a viable treatment option.

Although mortality was numerically higher in the CLD group than in the PALF group, this finding should be interpreted with caution, given the small sample size. The PALF cohort was generally older, and, given the programme’s evolving nature and resource limitations, transplantation was offered preferentially to patients considered likely to benefit from the procedure. In contrast, the CLD cohort included a substantial proportion of children with biliary atresia, who are often younger and may present with more advanced disease and greater technical challenges at transplantation. These factors may partly explain the observed mortality pattern, which differs from that reported in larger published series.

LDLT remains the most viable option for PALF in countries with developing healthcare infrastructure. A carefully regulated altruistic donor pool can significantly enhance the likelihood of timely liver transplantation, mitigating the challenges posed by limited access to deceased donor grafts. As a country transitions toward establishing a paediatric liver transplant program, shifting traditional attitudes from the pre-transplant era requires time and systematic efforts. Increasing awareness among healthcare providers and the public is essential to ensure the successful integration of liver transplantation as a standard treatment modality for paediatric liver disease.

Limitations

We admit the limitations of this study of being a small cohort. Further, in the initial patient selection for liver transplant, the very sick patients could not be enrolled due to limited resources, and very young patients were not referred for the transplant program due to its initial stages.

Conclusion

PALF comprised a sizable proportion of referrals in Sri Lanka’s budding PLT program. The majority of LT for PALF were LDLT with favorable outcomes. Optimum utilization of an emergent LT program with timely referrals by general pediatricians is crucial. The availability of altruistic donors due to unique cultural impact have played key roles in the success of the PLT program in a resource limited setting with underdeveloped deceased donor system.
