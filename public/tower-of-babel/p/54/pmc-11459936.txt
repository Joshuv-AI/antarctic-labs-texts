The optic nerve sheath in hypertensive disorders of pregnancy and perinatal outcomes: a cohort study
Authors: Marina Félix da Mota, Melania Maria de Amorim, Mário Diego Teles Correia, Leila Katz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreeclampsia is implicated in 14% of maternal deaths worldwide, mostly due to complications such as intracranial hemorrhage and cerebral edema. Cerebral edema increases intracranial pressure, which can be predicted by ultrasonographic measurement of the optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD). Greater diameters have been reported in women with preeclampsia and eclampsia; however, data are lacking on the possible association with maternal and neonatal adverse outcomes. This study aimed to determine whether there is an association between hypertensive disorders of pregnancy and the ONSD, and between this measurement and maternal and neonatal adverse outcomes.MethodsThis was a cohort study involving 183 women in the third trimester of pregnancy or within 24 h following childbirth, distributed as follows: control group (n = 30), gestational hypertension (n = 14), chronic hypertension (n = 12), preeclampsia without severe features (n = 12), preeclampsia with severe features (n = 62), superimposed preeclampsia (n = 23) and eclampsia (n = 30). The participants underwent ocular ultrasonography, and data on maternal and neonatal outcomes were collected from the medical records. To compare the groups, analysis of variance was used for the normally distributed numerical variables and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for variables with non-normal distribution. Two-tailed p-values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant.ResultsOverall comparison between the seven groups showed no statistically significant difference in the mean ONSD (p = 0.056). Nevertheless, diameters were significantly greater in the eclampsia group compared to the control group (p = 0.003). Greater diameters were associated with maternal admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) (p < 0.01) and maternal near miss (p = 0.01). There was no association between ONSD and admission to the neonatal ICU (p = 0.1), neonatal near miss (p = 0.34) or neonatal death (p = 0.26).ConclusionsNo association was found between ONSD and the hypertensive disorders of pregnancy in the overall analysis; however, ONSD was greater in women with eclampsia compared to controls. Greater diameters were associated with maternal admission to the ICU and maternal near miss. These findings suggest a potential use for bedside ultrasound as an additional tool for stratifying risk in patients with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy. Background

Hypertensive disorders constitute the most common medical complication of pregnancy, incurring high medical and social costs [1]. Preeclampsia is implicated in 14% of maternal deaths worldwide [2], with studies suggesting that neurological complications were the direct cause of death in 30–70% of these cases, particularly as a result of cerebral edema and intracranial hemorrhage [3]. Although the origin of cerebral edema in preeclampsia/eclampsia is primarily vasogenic due to an increase in hydrostatic pressure, cerebral hyperperfusion and blood–brain barrier dysfunction, an ischemic/cytotoxic component has also been found in 20–25% of the women. The implied mechanism in such cases involves impaired cerebral autoregulation leading to vasospasm, reduced blood flow and areas of ischemia and cell death [4, 5].

Cerebral edema appears to be the cause of the increase in intracranial pressure (ICP) in women with preeclampsia and eclampsia [6]. Intracranial hypertension is a severe complication that may be present in neurological and non-neurological diseases. Recognizing it quickly is crucial in allowing the appropriate treatment to be instituted [7]. The gold standard diagnostic method for this involves placement of an intracranial catheter, an invasive and not risk-free surgical procedure [8]. Non-invasive alternatives for cases in which signs of an increase in ICP are present include computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, transcranial Doppler ultrasound, near-infrared spectroscopy and visual evoked potentials. These methods, however, are expensive, of limited availability, or require specific equipment and/or trained professionals to carry them out and interpret them [9].

Ocular ultrasound has been validated and used for over ten years as a non-invasive method in this scenario, and is considered a practical, cost-effective approach with a minimal risk of complications [10]. When ICP increases, the cerebrospinal fluid flows to the perineural space and the subarachnoid space, increasing the pressure around the optic nerve, leading to expansion of the dural sheath and an increase in its diameter, principally in the anterior segment (3 mm behind the ocular globe), which can be measured using an ultrasound scanner almost immediately after the event [11] (Fig. 1). A thickness of 5 mm has classically been considered a reasonable cut-off point suggestive of intracranial hypertension (ICP > 20 mmHg); however, other authors have proposed higher values [12]. Because it has been impossible to compare studies with each other due to their differences in methodology and populations, there is still no consensus on the upper limit of normality for the optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) [12, 13].

Ultrasound measurement of the optic nerve sheath diameter

A pilot study conducted in 2012 (n = 51) found that 19% of the patients with preeclampsia had an ONSD > 5.8 mm, an abnormality that was not found in the control group of healthy pregnant women. There was no association between the severity of preeclampsia and the ultrasound findings, and no cases of eclampsia were included in that study [14]. Since then, some studies have compared women with preeclampsia or eclampsia to others without the hypertensive disorder, with results showing a greater frequency of diameters suggestive of elevated ICP in the two former groups [15, 16]. The majority of studies found no association between ONSD and the presence of severe features of preeclampsia [6, 16, 17], although a study conducted in Egypt reported conflicting findings [18].

At the present time, in the literature databases evaluated, only one prospective study was found whose objective was to analyze the association between the ONSD and maternal and neonatal complications. Those authors found that greater diameters on the day of delivery were associated with neurological symptoms (mean 5.8 mm; p < 0.001), admission of the newborn infant to a neonatal intensive care unit (ICU) (mean 5.2 mm; p = 0.016) and indication for an emergency Cesarean section (mean 5.1 mm; p = 0.001) [19]. In addition to the paucity of studies with a similar objective, there are no published data on other spectrums of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy such as gestational or chronic hypertension and superimposed preeclampsia.

Therefore, the primary objective of the present study was to determine whether ultrasound measurement of the ONSD is associated with different hypertensive disorders of pregnancy and maternal and neonatal outcomes. Maternal outcomes included mode of delivery, ICU stay, maternal near miss and maternal death. Birthweight, 1st and 5th minute Apgar scores, neonatal ICU admission, neonatal near miss and neonatal death were assessed as neonatal outcomes. The secondary objectives were to describe the biological, sociodemographic, clinical, and obstetric characteristics of women with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy admitted to high-risk maternities.

Methods

Study design

The cohort included women in the period ranging from 28 weeks of pregnancy to 24 h postpartum. Women without any hypertensive disorder formed a control group, while the other groups consisted of women with gestational hypertension, chronic hypertension, preeclampsia with severe features, preeclampsia without severe features, superimposed preeclampsia and eclampsia. The participants were recruited between August 2021 and September 2022 while hospitalized in the antepartum unit, maternity ward or maternal ICU of two university teaching hospitals in northeastern Brazil. The internal review board of the Instituto Materno Infantil Professor Fernando Figueira approved the study protocol under reference number 4.846.476 on July 14, 2021 (CAAE: 48,639,521.4.0000.5201). All the participants, or their legal guardians in the case of participants under 18 years of age, gave their written consent. If participation of a woman younger than 18 years of age was authorized by her parent/legal guardian, she was then asked to sign a written assent form.

Sample

Convenience sampling was used to select the participants. The following inclusion criteria were applied: women in the third trimester of pregnancy (≥ 28 weeks) and women who had vaginal delivery or Cesarean section within the preceding 24 h. This study considered that women in the period that ranged from 28 weeks of pregnancy to 24 h postpartum could be placed together in a single group. This decision was based on the pilot study conducted in 2012 that found no significant change in ONSD in participants with preeclampsia within the period from pregnancy up to the first 72 h following delivery. Indeed, that study detected a subsequent progressive reduction in values towards normal only between the third and seventh postpartum day [14]. To enable patients in whom postpartum eclampsia was their first manifestation of a hypertensive disorder to be included in the study, the recruitment period was extended in these cases to 24 h after the seizure. The exclusion criteria were: prior cerebrovascular disease, epilepsy, hyperthyroidism, glaucoma, neuroma or optic neuritis, ocular lesion, prior ocular surgery and any other condition that constituted a contraindication to ocular ultrasound.

The diagnostic criteria of the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) were used to classify the participants and allocate them in each group [20]. Gestational hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg on two or more occasions, at least four hours apart, after 20 weeks of pregnancy in a woman whose blood pressure was previously normal. When these clinical parameters were identified before 20 weeks of pregnancy, chronic hypertension was diagnosed. Preeclampsia was defined as the new onset of hypertension and proteinuria characterized by ≥ 300 mg/dl of protein per 24-h urine collection, protein/creatinine ratio of 0.3 mg/dl or more in a single urine sample or dipstick reading of at least 2 + (update recommended by the ACOG in view of the high false-positive rates found when a lower cut-off point was used). Diagnosis can also be made in the absence of proteinuria if there are severe features of preeclampsia present: thrombocytopenia (platelet count < 100 × 109/l); renal insufficiency (creatinine > 1.1 mg/dl or a doubling of the serum creatinine concentration in the absence of other renal diseases); impaired liver function (increase in transaminases to twice the normal level); pulmonary edema; or new-onset headache unresponsive to medication. The presence of visual disturbances or severe hypertension (≥ 160 × 110 mmHg) confirmed within an interval of minutes in a woman with gestational hypertension is also indicative of a diagnosis of preeclampsia with severe features. Eclampsia was defined by new-onset tonic–clonic, focal or multifocal seizures in the absence of other causative conditions.

Since there are no robust studies including a large population to enable sample size to be adequately calculated for the association between the ONSD and maternal and neonatal outcomes, this is an exploratory study. The plan was to include a cohort of 180 women, allocated to each group according to the final diagnosis on their medical records.

Overall, 183 participants were included, distributed as follows:

Data collection

The participants were interviewed at recruitment and data were collected on their age, skin color, weight and height as measured at the last prenatal visit, place of residence (rural/urban) and the presence of neurological symptoms such as scotomas, phosphenes and headache (including maximum intensity level) in the week preceding their inclusion in the study. Number of pregnancies, parity, maximum SBP, maximum DBP, and maximum mean blood pressure (MBP) in the 24 h after admission to the ICU or hospital, the degree of proteinuria, and gestational age at the time of admission and at the time of delivery were recorded according to the data available on the patient records.

An experienced investigator performed transorbital sonography at each patient’s bedside in the relevant unit (antepartum unit, ward or ICU). An ultrasound scanner was used at each participating hospital, together with its respective high frequency linear transducer (Phillips model HD7 XE with a 3–12 MHz probe or a GE Healthcare NextGen LOGIC™ with a 5–12 MHz probe) and the device was configured to visualize structures up to 5–6 cm deep. The patients were placed in the supine position, with their eyes closed and head in the neutral position elevated to 30 degrees. After filling the preorbital space with gel, the transducer was gently placed on the temporal region of the upper eyelid and slightly tilted caudally and medially until an image of the orbit was obtained on the axial axis, showing the entry point of the optic nerve into the ocular globe. The image was frozen until cross-sectional measurement of the optic nerve sheath 3 mm from its entry into the ocular globe, as previously recommended [6–14]. The procedure was performed three times on each eye, with the final value recorded in millimeters corresponding to the mean of all the measurements, as established in previous studies [21].

Maternal outcomes (mode of delivery, ICU stay, maternal near miss and maternal death) and neonatal outcomes (birthweight, 1st and 5th minute Apgar scores, neonatal ICU admission, neonatal near miss and neonatal death) were investigated according to the medical records. Maternal near miss is defined, according to the World Health Organization (WHO), as an event when a woman nearly dies but survives severe complications during pregnancy, childbirth or within 42 days of termination of pregnancy. The criteria used to define it have already been validated [22]. Neonatal near miss is defined as an event during which a newborn almost dies but survives severe complications in the first 28 days of life. The defining criteria were birthweight < 1,750 g, gestational age < 33 weeks or 5th minute Apgar < 7, as already validated [23]. The presence of one criterion was sufficient for diagnosis.

Statistical analysis

Measures of central tendency and dispersion were used to describe the numerical variables. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to evaluate the normality of data distribution. For numerical variables with a normal distribution and with equal variance, as shown using Levene’s test or the Bartlett test, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. When distribution was not normal, the Kruskall-Wallis test was used for multiple comparisons followed by the Conover test for paired comparisons or the Mann–Whitney test for comparisons between two groups. The categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages and were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as pertinent. Throughout the statistical analysis, two-tailed p-values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Overall, 802 patients were evaluated for eligibility. Of these, 596 did not meet the inclusion criteria and 7 refused to participate in the study. Of the 199 women initially selected, 16 had at least one exclusion criterion, leaving 183 women who were included in the study (Fig. 2).

Flowchart depicting participant recruitment and follow-up

No losses occurred throughout the study; however, information on neonatal death was not obtainable in 7.1% of cases because the newborn infant had been transferred to another hospital. There was no difference between the groups with respect to skin color or place of residence. The age of participants differed significantly between the groups, being higher in the groups of women with chronic hypertension and superimposed preeclampsia (p = 0.009). There was a statistically significant difference in body mass index (BMI) between the groups (p = 0.002), with BMI being higher in the group of women with superimposed preeclampsia. The groups also differed with respect to the number of pregnancies (p = 0.004) and number of women in their first pregnancy (p = 0.014), with a greater frequency of first-time pregnant women in the groups of preeclampsia with severe features, and eclampsia. There was no difference between the groups with respect to gestational age at delivery or in the frequency of diabetes mellitus or gestational diabetes. Headache, phosphenes and blurred vision were more common in the groups of women with preeclampsia with severe features, superimposed preeclampsia and eclampsia, while scotomas were the only neurological symptoms for which there was no statistically significant difference between the groups (Table 1 and Fig. 3).

In the overall analysis, the maximum levels of SBP, DBP and MBP varied significantly between the groups during the first 24 h following admission to hospital or to the ICU. Maximum SBP measurements were higher in the eclampsia group compared to the other groups, whereas maximum DBP and MBP were significantly lower in the control group in relation to the other groups. In the paired analysis, MBP was higher in the eclampsia group compared to the other groups except for the group of women with superimposed preeclampsia (Table 1).

In the general comparison between the groups, no significant differences were found with respect to ONSD (Table 2). However, when each group was compared individually to the control group, diameters were found to be greater in the eclampsia group (p = 0.003). There were no other significant differences between the healthy controls and the patients with other forms of hypertensive disorder in this analysis (p = 0.46 for gestational hypertension; p = 0.25 for chronic hypertension; p = 0.21 for preeclampsia without severe features; p = 0.19 for preeclampsia with severe features; and p = 0.95 for superimposed preeclampsia).

A separate analysis was performed of the women with ONSD ≥ 5 mm, i.e. taking the cut-off value mostly used in the literature into account. There was a greater frequency of participants with ONSD ≥ 5 mm in the eclampsia group, both in the overall inter-group analysis (p = 0.03) and when each group was compared to the control group (p = 0.003). The frequency of women with diameter ≥ 5 mm was similar in all the other groups compared to the control group (p = 0.69 for gestational hypertension; p = 0.66 for chronic hypertension; p = 0.65 for preeclampsia without severe features; p = 0.15 for preeclampsia with severe features; and p = 0.40 for superimposed preeclampsia).

An association was found between ONSD and the presence of maternal near miss (mean diameter 5.1 mm ± 0.58; p = 0.01) and maternal admission to the ICU (mean diameter 5.1 mm ± 0.56; p < 0.01). At a cut-off point of 5 mm (53 participants and 28.9% of the sample), there was a higher frequency of headache (p = 0.008), admission to the ICU (p = 0.0001) and delivery at gestational age < 34 weeks (p = 0.01). First-minute Apgar score < 7 was also more common in newborns of women with ONSD > 5 mm; however, there was no difference with respect to admission to the neonatal ICU, neonatal near miss or neonatal death (Table 3).

Discussion

In the present study, comparison between the seven groups of participants (six groups of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy and a control group) showed no significant difference in mean ONSD (p = 0.056). However, when each group was compared individually to the control group, significantly greater values of ONSD were found in the eclampsia group (p = 0.003). Greater diameters were associated with admission of the patient to the ICU (p < 0.01) and maternal near miss (p = 0.01). Women whose ONSD was > 5 mm were also more likely to experience headache (p = 0.008), require ICU care (p = 0.0001), deliver at gestational age < 34 weeks (p = 0.01) and to have a newborn whose 1st minute Apgar score was < 7 (p = 0.009).

The increase in ONSD found in the women with eclampsia compared to the healthy controls corroborates earlier studies. Ever since the first study showing greater ONSD in women with preeclampsia [14], irrespective of the presence of severe features, some studies with differing methodologies have been conducted. One of these studies reproduced the methodology of the original 2019 study and reported similar results [16], while others compared only cases of preeclampsia with severe features to a control group, and detected greater diameters in the severe preeclampsia group [21, 24, 25]. Studies on eclampsia have also shown that ONSD is greater in this group of women compared to controls with no hypertensive disorder [15], with one study having found no difference between women with eclampsia and those with preeclampsia with severe features [26].

On the other hand, a recent study that stratified pregnant women into five groups (control, preeclampsia without severe features, preeclampsia with severe features and neurological symptoms, preeclampsia with severe features without neurological symptoms, and eclampsia) found no statistically significant difference between them [6]. That was the first study to use the most up-to-date ACOG criteria for hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (published in 2020), which were also applied in the present study, and the first to report conflicting results. An interesting finding reported by those authors was the high incidence of ONSD > 5.8 mm in the control group (42.1%), while in the present study no change was found in any of the healthy controls.

Possible explanations for the discrepancies between the results reported in the literature concern the different diagnostic criteria used to classify the participants, the moment at which testing was performed (before, during or after magnesium sulfate therapy or delivery) and the technique of testing (the expertise of the professional, the quality of the image obtained, the number of measurements performed on each eye, the choice of the axis in which the images were obtained, and the markers used to demarcate the margins of the optic nerve sheath). In the present study, two intensive care physicians whose experience exceeded 80 ONSD measurements including patients with normal and increased intracranial pressure performed the exams, as previously suggested [27], thus minimizing the possibility of measurement bias.

In a methodological narrative review of 63 articles on ultrasound of the optic nerve published in 2021, four different ultrasound marker positions had been used to delineate the optic nerve sheath, entailing different sensitivities to detect changes in ICP between individuals [28]. Some authors attribute the hyperechogenic bands seen at the exam to the pia mater, others to the subarachnoid space, and others to the dura mater and the pia mater, arguing that the subarachnoid space would only appear between these bands in cases of increased ICP. An observational cohort study compared two forms of measuring the ONSD using its internal or external limit with the dura mater and, although the values found were significantly different, no change was found in the diagnostic accuracy for ICP > 20 mmHg when the respective cut-off points of 5.3 mm and 6.8 mm were considered [29]. The present study used the external limit as reference; however, the methodology sections of many publications fail to state the measurement reference used.

The possible effect of the ethnicity of the population evaluated on aspects of optic nerve sonography remains under debate. Healthy individuals from different countries have already been evaluated to determine the mean ONSD in each population [10, 30] and differing results have already been described for the same nationality. On the other hand, a study conducted in Colombia recorded means of 3.96 mm (transversal axis) and 4 mm (sagittal axis) in its population, while another described a mean of 4.5 mm in the same setting [31]. Likewise, values such as 3.46 mm [32] and 5.1 mm [33] have already been suggested as mean diameters in the Chinese population.

The influence of age, sex and weight on the results obtained has also been evaluated in other studies, again with conflicting results [10]. With respect to the interference of the pregnancy, a study conducted in Australia found no difference in the measurements obtained during and following pregnancy in healthy women [34]. A review of the PubMed, ScIELO and Google Scholar databases conducted in April 2024 using the descriptors optic nerve AND ultrasonography AND Brazil failed to find any study with a similar objective conducted in the Brazilian population up to the present time, including in pregnant women.

The time at which the exam is performed may also be a relevant factor that varies from study to study. A pilot study conducted in 2012 found no statistically significant change in ONSD in participants with preeclampsia up to the first 72 h after delivery, with a progressive reduction in values between the third and seventh postpartum day [12]. Notwithstanding, recent studies comparing the ONSD during magnesium sulfate therapy showed differing results with respect to the interference or not of the drug on optic nerve ultrasound findings over the first 24 h of treatment [35–37]. Therefore, the ideal moment at which to evaluate patients and to what extent the differences in methodology in this respect could have affected the different results obtained up to now are questions that as yet remain unanswered.

A literature review conducted in April 2024 using the PubMed, ScIELO and Google Scholar databases and the descriptors preeclampsia AND optic nerve revealed only one publication in which the association between the ONSD and maternal and neonatal outcomes was investigated. Greater diameters were found in patients with neurological symptoms and in cases in which the medical team decided to perform an emergency Cesarean section. No association was found between the ONSD and neonatal admission to the ICU or neonatal death [19]. Although the present sample was insufficient to evaluate the outcome of maternal death, an infrequent event even in hypertensive disorders, an association was found between the ONSD and other outcomes such as maternal admission to the ICU and maternal near miss. Diameters > 5 mm were also associated with a greater frequency of headache, delivery at gestational age < 34 weeks and 1st minute Apgar score < 7. The results found suggest a potential for the use of bedside ultrasonography as an additional tool for stratifying the risk of patients with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy.

Some of the limitations associated with this study include the convenience sampling method, the use of two different ultrasound scanners, the absence of examiner blinding, and the acquisition of images within one single moment, with patients’ final diagnosis being that on their medical records. Blinding the examiners was not possible, both because of the location of the participants and because of the impossibility of blinding to the continuous infusion of magnesium sulfate in the patients who received that treatment during the exam in their hospital bed. Nevertheless, the absence of any statistically significant difference in the ONSD between the groups in the overall analysis supports the belief that the examiners knowledge of the group allocation did not affect measurements. In addition, information on neonatal death during hospitalization could not be obtained in 7.1% of cases because the newborn infants had been transferred to other hospitals.

Procedures adopted to assure the internal validity of the study included the selection of a control group, standardization of the measurement technique between the examiners with respect to the anatomical markers involved (external limit to the dura mater) and the requirement to repeat the measurement three times. We understand that the external validity of a study depends fundamentally on the sample and it should be taken into consideration that the present results are from hospitalized Brazilian women in the period ranging from 28 weeks of pregnancy to 24 h postpartum, so external validity (generalizability) may be limited. The absence up to the present time of studies evaluating the ONSD in the Brazilian population and aimed at defining the pattern of distribution of this variable in healthy individuals, principally pregnant women, limits the extrapolation of these results to other settings.

Future studies with methodology that includes ultrasound monitoring of participants during hospitalization could clarify questions regarding possible changes in the patient’s clinical condition and corresponding ultrasound results, even defining the effect of the timing of magnesium sulfate infusion on the measurements obtained in patients with preeclampsia with severe features and eclampsia, a subject that remains highly relevant and under debate among the various authors.

Conclusions

Overall analysis showed no association between ultrasound measurement of the ONSD and the hypertensive disorders of pregnancy; however, a statistically significant difference was found between the mean measurements of the eclampsia and control groups. Greater diameters were associated with maternal admission to the ICU and maternal near miss. There were no cases of maternal death in this cohort. There was no association between ONSD and admission to the neonatal ICU, neonatal near miss or neonatal death. Diameters > 5 mm were associated with headache, maternal admission to the ICU, delivery at gestational age < 34 weeks and 1st minute Apgar scores < 7. These findings suggest a potential for the use of bedside ultrasonography as an additional tool for stratifying risk in patients with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy.
