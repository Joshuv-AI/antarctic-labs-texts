High triglyceride-glucose index is associated with diabetes mellitus in people with HIV in Southwest China
Authors: Liangjia Wei, Chunxing Tao, Xinju Huang, Ruby Congjiang Wang, Jianhua Che, Aidan Nong, Rongye Huang, Zhiman Yan, Lijing Huang, Jiao Qin, Lincong Chen, Sanqi An, Junjun Jiang, Lixian Xiong, Hao Liang, Lijuan Bao, Bingyu Liang, Li Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, an emerging marker of insulin resistance, has shown promise in predicting various noncommunicable diseases. This study investigated the association between the TyG index and the incidence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) within a 13-year retrospective longitudinal cohort of people with HIV (PWH).Methods:This retrospective cohort study utilized data from the China National Free ART Program (CNFAP) in Guangxi, enrolling PWH who initiated ART between 2010 and 2022. The primary exposure, TyG index, was calculated as ln [TG (mg/dl) × FBG (mg/dl)/2]. The outcome, T2DM, was defined as two consecutive FBG measurements at least 7 mmol/l. Gaussian mixture model (GMM)-based multitrajectory analysis identified TyG trajectories. Multivariate Cox proportional hazard models were used to estimate the associations between TyG trajectories and the risk of T2DM.Results:A total of 15 370 patients were included, with 69.9% men, and a median age of 45 at ART initiation. Over 63 232 person-years of follow-up, 22.7% (3493/15 370) developed T2DM.The ‘high Inverted U-shape’ TyG trajectory was significantly associated with T2DM [ adjusted hazard ratio (aHR): 2.37, 95% confidence interval (CI): 2.21–2.54]. Additionally, the TyG index outperformed FBG in predicting T2DM at both 5 and 10 years (5-year AUC: 0.640 vs. 0.621, P = 0.003; 10-year AUC: 0.655 vs. 0.592, P < 0.001).Conclusion:This study highlights the clinical value of the TyG index as a predictive marker for T2DM in PWH, suggesting its potential for early risk stratification.

Introduction

Long-term antiretroviral therapy (ART) is associated with an increased risk of chronic diseases, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and alterations in body composition [1]. Research has shown that PWH exhibited a higher prevalence of diabetes and an elevated risk of mortality [2–4]. Birabaharan et al. reported that the overall prevalence of type 2 diabetes (T2DM) in PWH was 22.1%, significantly higher than the 14.9% observed in the general population [5]. A multicentre clinical trial in China showed that diabetes prevalence among 1415 PWH was considerably higher (2.62 per 100 person-years) than that of an age-matched general population (0.43–1.55 per 100 person-years) [6]. This elevated prevalence highlights the growing concern of diabetes as a significant public health issue for PWH [7].

Diabetes is a metabolic disorder caused by a combination of genetic and environmental factors, characterized by impaired insulin sensitivity, insulin deficiency, and dysregulated glucose metabolism [8]. Insulin resistance refers to a diminished biological response to insulin in target tissues, such as adipocytes, hepatocytes, and skeletal muscle cells, which impairs glucose uptake and utilization throughout the body. It is markedly associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease and T2DM [9,10]. Insulin resistance often develops several years before the onset of T2DM and is regarded as a precursor, playing a critical role in disease progression [9,11]. The aging of PWH, the long-term side effects of ART, and the complex interplay between inflammatory states and immune activation collectively contribute to an increased risk of insulin resistance [12–14]. Meanwhile, metabolic diseases associated with insulin resistance may be partially driven by inflammation and immune dysfunction caused by chronic HIV infection during ART [15]. Therefore, we hypothesized that insulin resistance plays a crucial role in the development of diabetes among PWH.

The hyperinsulinaemic–euglycaemic clamp (HIEC) and homeostasis model assessment-insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) are commonly used to assess insulin resistance [16,17]. HIEC has been considered the gold standard for determining insulin resistance. However, its complexity, high cost, and invasiveness all limit its use for small, rather than large-scale, cohort studies [10,16]. HOMA-IR is a model that reflects the relationship between glucose and insulin dynamics under fasting conditions, requiring serum insulin measurements, and is relatively expensive [17,18]. In recent years, the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index has emerged as an alternative indicator for assessing insulin resistance [19]. TyG index is calculated as Ln [fasting triglycerides (mg/dl) × fasting glucose (mg/dl)/2] [10,20]. The TyG index eliminates the complexity, high cost, and invasiveness associated with the hyperinsulinaemic–euglycaemic clamp technique [16,21]. Furthermore, the TyG index has demonstrated superior predictive ability for the prevalence of T2DM compared to HOMA-IR [22]. The TyG index has become a simple tool for detecting insulin resistance in the general population and assessing cardiovascular disease risk [10]. Multiple cohort studies have consistently found a significant positive correlation between the TyG index and TyG-related parameters and diabetes risk across diverse populations [23–26]. Although some studies have shown that the TyG index effectively predicts the onset of diabetes, most of them have focused exclusively on the general population. This association between TyG trajectory and diabetes risk in PWH remains unclear.

We determined the association between TyG index trajectories and T2DM risk using retrospective longitudinal cohort data from 2010 to 2022. This study aims to inform the development of more effective screening strategies and personalized treatment approaches to improve health outcomes in this population.

Methods

Study sites and participants

This retrospective cohort study used data from the China National Free ART Program (CNFAP) in Guangxi Province, a southwestern province with the highest HIV burden in China [1]. Participants were recruited from designated HIV treatment hospitals and clinics in Nanning City, Qinzhou City, Chongzuo City, and Fangchenggang City, all of which are part of the national HIV care system. PWH who initiated ART between 1 January 2010, and 31 December 2022, were eligible for inclusion. Details of exclusion criteria were presented in Supplementary Figure S1.

Data collection and covariates

Trained healthcare personnel collected data at each site, with routine quality control procedures to ensure accuracy and completeness. Glucose levels were determined using the glucose oxidase enzymatic method, and triglyceride levels were measured using the glycerol phosphate oxidase-peroxidase (GPO-POD) enzymatic method, both performed on standardized automated biochemical analysers following national laboratory quality control protocols.

Sociodemographic data included age, sex, ethnicity, education level, occupation, and marital status. Clinical data included HIV transmission route, ART initiation regimen, year of ART initiation, time from diagnosis to ART initiation, duration of ART, WHO clinical stage at baseline, initial ART regimen, co-trimoxazole use history, HCV co-infection, and history of sexually transmitted infections (STIs). Laboratory data included CD4+ cell count, HIV viral load, haemoglobin (Hb), total glyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), and fasting blood glucose (FBG). Notably, baseline viral load measurements are typically recorded within a window of 6 months to 1 year after ART initiation due to clinical practice patterns in many treatment sites, especially in resource-limited settings. This study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration and was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Guangxi Medical University (Ethical Review No. KY0294).

Definition of exposure

The primary exposure was the TyG index, a surrogate marker of insulin resistance, calculated as:

TyG = [TG (mg/dl) × FBG (mg/dl)/2]

TG and FBG levels were measured every 3 months during the ART follow-up, in order to capture any changes over time. All participants were instructed to fast for at least 12 h before blood collection for laboratory testing. These regular measurements enabled assessment of longitudinal trends in the TyG index. To examine the long-term changes in TyG, we employed a Gaussian mixture model (GMM)-based multitrajectory model to identify distinct TyG trajectories over time.

Definition of outcome variable

The primary outcome was incident type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), defined as two consecutive FBG measurements at least 7 mmol/l, with at least 30 days between tests and both measurements occurring within 180 days. This approach aligns with American Diabetes Association (ADA) guidelines adapted for PWH [27].

Statistical methods

Handling missing data

For variables with missing data (e.g. ethnicity, marital status, education, occupation, HIV transmission route, co-trimoxazole use, sexually transmitted infection (STI) history, viral load, WHO clinical stage, Hb, TC) (Table S1), we assumed data were missing at random. Multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was performed.

Descriptive analysis

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (%). Pearson's chi-square test was used to compare baseline characteristics between the TyG trajectory groups.

Association analysis

We assessed the association between baseline TyG index and T2DM using Cox proportional hazards models, with a bidirectional stepwise selection for covariate adjustment. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) and penalized splines were applied to detect nonlinear associations and determine optimal cut-off points.

Kaplan–Meier curves and Log-rank tests assessed differences in cumulative T2DM incidence between TyG trajectory groups. Multivariate Cox regression models estimated the adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) for the risk of T2DM across trajectories, adjusting for sociodemographic and clinical variables. To minimize confounding, 1 : 1 nearest-neighbour propensity score matching (PSM) was performed with a calliper of 0.05. We employed logistic regression models to identify factors associated with the ‘High inverted U-shaped’ TyG trajectory pattern.

We performed subgroup analyses stratified by year of ART initiation, age, sex, ethnicity, marital status, education, occupation, time from diagnoses to ART, duration of ART, WHO clinical stage at baseline, initial ART regimen, ART regimen switching, viral load at baseline, CD4+ cell count at baseline, HCV infection status and transmission route to evaluate the association between TyG trajectories and the risk of T2DM. Likelihood ratio tests were employed to assess potential interactions across subgroups.

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted a series of sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the study findings. First, Poisson regression models enabled the estimation of the association between different TyG trajectories and the incidence of T2DM, with time offset and robust variance. Second, we conducted additional analyses by sequentially excluding participants with baseline TC levels exceeding 5.2 μmol/l and TG levels exceeding 1.7 μmol/l, FBG levels exceeding 5.6 μmol/l, missing baseline viral load measurement and any missing baseline covariate data, respectively. Third, to account for competing events such as death, transfer out and loss to follow-up, we treated them as competing risks and applied a Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model. Finally, T2DM events occurring within the first year of follow-up were excluded to minimize the potential for reverse causation.

Predictive performance analysis

We evaluated the discriminative performance of the TyG index, FBG and TG for predicting T2DM using time-dependent ROC curves and Harrell's C-index. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was compared at 2, 5 and 10 years.

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.1). A two-sided P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

Fifteen thousand three hundred and seventy participants were included in the final analysis. Among these participants, 39.1% were aged over 50 years, and approximately half were farmers (51.6%). Most initiated ART between 2009 and 2017 (57.8%) and had received ART for less than 5 years (61.2%). Regarding HIV-related characteristics, 83.7% acquired HIV through heterosexual contact, and 91.6% reported no history of sexually transmitted diseases. At baseline, 52.4% had CD4+ cell counts less than 200 cells/μl, 61% had viral loads less than 51 copies/ml, and 48.4% were in WHO clinical stage I. For ART treatment, 68.9% received EFV-based therapy, while 27.4% experienced ART switching (Table 1).

Triglyceride-glucose index trajectory analysis

A two-group trajectory model was identified as optimal for describing TyG index patterns over a 100-month follow-up (model fit presented in Table S2). The ‘Low stable’ group exhibited a low TyG index that slightly decreased during the first 40 months of ART, then gradually increased but remained below 9. The ‘High inverted U-shape’ group started with a relatively high TyG index, which increased over the first 60 months of ART before gradually declining but still remained above 9 (Fig. 1). Approximately three-quarters (73.5%) of participants were classified into the ‘Low stable’ group, and 26.5% into the ‘High inverted U-shape’ group.

Triglyceride-glucose trajectories over 100 months of follow-up after antiretroviral therapy initiation among people with HIV from 2010 to 2022 in Guangxi Province of China.

Correlates of high inverted U-shape triglyceride–glucose index trajectory

Multivariable analysis identified several factors significantly associated with membership in the ‘High inverted U-shaped’ group: age at ART initiation: 35–49 years (aOR: 1.47; 95% CI: 1.32–1.64); at least 50 years (aOR: 1.23; 95% CI: 1.08–1.39), female sex (aOR, 0.69; 95% CI: 0.63–0.76); ART initiation period 2018–2023 (aOR: 0.70; 95% CI: 0.64–0.77); ART duration: 5–10 years (aOR: 0.64; 95% CI: 0.58–0.71); at least 10 years (aOR: 0.67; 95% CI: 0.57–0.78); LPV-based ART regimen (aOR, 1.64; 95% CI: 1.41–1.90); HCV co-infection (aOR, 0.56; 95% CI: 0.44–0.70); baseline TG at least 1.7 μmol/l (aOR, 2.79; 95% CI: 2.58–3.01) and baseline FBG at least 7 μmol/l (aOR, 1.57; 95% CI: 1.40–1.75) (Table S3).

With prolonged ART duration (≥60 vs. ≥60 and <100 months), Among patients with an inverted U-shaped trajectory, the proportion on LPV-containing regimens decreased from 49.2 to 33.3% (P < 0.001) (Table S4), while viral nonsuppression (≥51 copies/ml) declined from 17.9 to 7% (P < 0.001) (Table S5), and severe immunosuppression (CD4+ <200 cells/μl) reduced from 28.7 to 7.8% (P < 0.001) (Table S6).

Incidence of type 2 diabetes mellitus and characteristics of the two triglyceride-glucose trajectory groups

During 63232 person-years of follow-up, 3493 participants developed diabetes mellitus, with an incidence rate of 5.5 per 100 person-years (95% CI: 53–57) . A significantly higher proportion of the participants in the ‘High inverted U-shape’ developed T2DM (41.3%) compared to the ‘low stable’ group (16%).

Association between triglyceride-glucose index and the risk of type 2 diabetes

When categorizing the TyG index as an ordinal variable, individuals in the highest quartile (9.1 ≤ TyG < 10.9) had a significantly increased risk for developing T2DM according to the Cox proportional hazards models (aHR: 4.81; 95% CI: 4.17–5.55, P < 0.001), relative to those in the lowest quartile (6 < TyG < 8.3) (Table 2). This pattern was mirrored in the RCS regression analyses, which showed a nonlinear association between the risk of TyG and T2DM, with increased risk of T2DM as TyG levels increased (P for nonlinear = 0.002). An inflection point was identified at a TyG index of 8.7, as shown in Figure S2. Further, the Cox proportional hazards model showed that participants with a TyG index at least 8.7 had a 2.08-fold higher risk of developing T2DM compared to those with a TyG index less than 8.7 (aHR: 2.08, 95% CI: 1.90–2.28) (Table 3). After excluding participants with baseline FBG less than 5.6, the association remained statistically significant (Table 3).

Association between triglyceride-glucose trajectory and the risk of diabetes mellitus

As shown in Fig. 2a, participants in the ‘High inverted U-shaped’ group exhibited a significantly higher cumulative incidence of T2DM compared to those in the ‘Low stable’ group (P < 0.001). This difference remained significant, even after propensity score matching (P < 0.001, Fig. 2b). The Cox proportional hazards model showed that participants in the ‘High inverted U-shaped’ TyG trajectory group faced a significantly elevated risk of T2DM compared to those in the ‘Low stable’ group (aHR: 2.37, 95% CI: 2.21–2.54, P < 0.001) (Table 4). Following propensity score matching, the association remained robust (aHR: 2.31, 95% CI: 2.12–2.52%, P < 0.001) (Table 4).

Subgroup analysis

The association between a ‘High inverted U-shaped’ TyG trajectory and T2DM was most sensitive among PWH aged over 50 years (P for interaction < 0.001) (18–34: aHR: 2.33, 95% CI: 2.06–2.65, 35–49: aHR: 2.25; 95% CI: 2.05–2.47, 50+: aHR: 3.15, 95% CI: 2.58–3.85), those occupation as farmer (P for interaction < 0.001) (farmer: aHR: 2.89, 95% CI: 2.45–3.39; unemployment: aHR: 2.37, 95% CI: 2.30–2.76; other: aHR: 2.23, 95% CI: 2.03–2.44), and those with low educational attainment (P for interaction = 0.003) (primary school or below: aHR: 2.88, 95% CI: 2.44–3.39; junior high school: aHR: 2.42, 95% CI: 2.16–2.71; high school or above: aHR: 2.15, 95% CI: 1.92–2.40) (Table S9). Moreover, no interactions were found between TyG trajectories and the other subgroup variables (Table S9–S11).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the findings:

First, Poisson regression indicated an aHR of 2.36 (95% CI: 2.20–2.54%) (Table S12). Second, the results from a competing risks model remained consistent with the Cox regression (Table S13). Thirdly, after excluding participants with baseline TG <1.7 mmol/l, TC <5.2 mmol/l, FBG <5.6 mmol/l, or missing data, the association persisted with aHRs ranging from 1.82 to 2.45 (Table S14). Finally, excluding participants who developed T2DM within the first year after ART further strengthened the association (aHR: 2.61; 95% CI: 2.39–2.86) (Table S14).

Predictive value of fasting blood glucose, triglycerides, and triglyceride-glucose for type 2 diabetes mellitus incidence

The TyG index showed noninferior predictive performance to FBG for overall T2DM risk (C-index: 0.644 vs. 0.647; P = 0.558). In contrast, TG alone had significantly lower predictive accuracy (C-index: 0.556; P < 0.001 vs. TyG) (Table S15). Time-dependent analyses using Harrell's C-index showed: (1) 2-year prediction: TyG inferior to FBG (0.658 vs. 0.674; P = 0.015), but better than TG (0.658 vs. 0.559; P < 0.001); (2) 5-year and 10-year prediction: TyG outperformed both FBG (5-year: 0.640 vs. 0.621; P = 0.003; 10-year: 0.655 vs. 0.592; P < 0.001) and TG (5-year: 0.640 vs. 0.562; 10-year: 0.655 vs. 0.567; both P < 0.001) (Figure S3). Results remained largely consistent across age and sex strata (Figures S4–S8).

Discussion

This study represents one of the largest cohort studies to examine the association between TyG, TyG trajectory, and the risk of T2DM among PWH. Our findings indicated that both a high TyG index at baseline and a high TyG trajectory were significantly associated with an increased risk of developing T2DM. Moreover, the TyG index outperformed FBG in predicting T2DM at both 5 and 10 years. These findings highlight the TyG index as a practical and clinically relevant biomarker for identifying high-risk individuals, allowing for earlier intervention in PWH.

We found a nonlinear association between baseline TyG and T2DM, with an inflection point at 8.7, consistent with prior studies [25,28]. When stratified by this threshold, a TyG index greater than 8.7 was associated with an elevated risk of T2DM. This finding was in line with a recent systematic review that identified a nonlinear dose–response relationship between the TyG index and T2DM, with a steeper risk gradient above a TyG value of 8.6 [29]. This review indicated that a TyG range of 8.7–8.9 could predict incidence of T2DM with a sensitivity of 54% and specificity of 71% [29], and another study proposed 8.52 as the optimal cut-off for diagnosing insulin resistance [30]. These findings suggest that a TyG index of around 8.7 may represent a critical threshold for identifying PWH at an elevated risk of T2DM.

Our study also found that the association between an inverted U-shape TyG trajectory and T2DM was more pronounced among older PWH. Aging is known to exacerbate chronic inflammation and immune system deterioration [31], both of which can intensify insulin resistance. Furthermore, frailty, a common concern in older adults, has been linked to increased T2DM risk [32,33], and previous research suggests that a high TyG index may contribute to the development of frailty [34]. Behavioural factors, including physical inactivity and alcohol consumption, which are more prevalent in older populations, may further compound diabetes risk [35,36]. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions in older PWH, including regular TyG monitoring, frailty assessments, and lifestyle modifications to reduce metabolic risk.

Understanding the factors contributing to an inverted U-shaped TyG trajectory is essential for early identification and prevention of T2DM in PWH. We found that individuals initiating ART before 2018 were more likely to follow this trajectory, potentially due to delayed treatment initiation (Supplementary Material: Table S16), which is associated with persistent inflammation and subsequent insulin resistance [14,37,38]. Additionally, patients receiving lopinavir (LPV)-containing regimens had a higher likelihood of developing a high TyG trajectory, likely due to the known metabolic toxicity of LPV, including its effects on insulin sensitivity [39–41]. Interestingly, a longer ART duration was associated with a reduced likelihood of following the high TyG trajectory, possibly reflecting the protective effects of sustained viral suppression and immune restoration [38,42]. While previous studies revealed an association between HCV infection and increased risk of insulin resistance [43,44], our results revealed an inverse relationship. The finding may be explained by the high treatment coverage and cure rates of HCV infection in this region [45], as successful HCV eradication has been shown to significantly reduce insulin resistance [46]. These findings underscored that early ART initiation, optimized regimen, and treatment of HCV infection may mitigate the development of insulin resistance and reduce long-term T2DM risk in PWH.

Consistent with studies in the general population [47], our findings confirmed the TyG index as a superior long-term predictor of T2DM compared to FBG or triglycerides alone. In this study, FBG was more predictive of short-term (2-year) diabetes risk, whereas TyG outperformed FBG in predicting T2DM at 5 and 10 years. This may be explained by the fact that FBG reflects late-stage prediabetes, while the TyG index captures early insulin resistance [19], which often precedes hyperglycaemia by several years. Furthermore, by combining TG and FBG, the TyG index integrates two critical metabolic risk factors, enhancing its predictive utility [48]. These findings support incorporating the TyG index into routine diabetes screening in PWH, providing a broader window for early detection and intervention.

This study had several limitations. First, the cohort was derived primarily from Guangxi, China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions or populations. Second, T2DM diagnosis relied solely on FBG measurements, without the inclusion of oral glucose tolerance tests (OGTT) or glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c). This decision was made because ART can interfere with haemoglobin structure, reducing the reliability of HbA1c in PWH. Although OGTT provides valuable postprandial glucose information, it is often impractical in large retrospective cohorts due to cost and invasiveness. FBG remains a feasible and cost-effective option in resource-limited HIV care settings. Future studies could incorporate OGTT and HbA1c in subpopulations to better capture glucose abnormalities. Third, data on HOMA-IR and HbA1c were not available, which prevented direct comparisons of TyG with HOMA-IR and HbA1c. However, prior research suggested that TyG may outperform HOMA-IR in detecting insulin resistance [49,50]. Fourth, we lacked data on the use of glucose-lowering medications, which could have influenced the classification of T2DM. Fifth, BMI data were unavailable. While weight gain is a common risk factor for insulin resistance, previous studies suggest that T2DM in PWH may occur even at lower BMI levels [51]. Future prospective studies should routinely collect obesity-related measures to better elucidate the interplay between body composition, TyG trajectories, and T2DM risk in PWH.

In conclusion, this study found that a high TyG trajectory is significantly associated with increased T2DM risk in PWH. These findings underscore the importance of regular TyG monitoring as a predictive tool for diabetes prevention in this population. Interventions aimed at reducing triglyceride and FBG levels, promoting early ART initiation, adopting healthier lifestyles, and addressing insulin resistance may help mitigate the burden of T2DM among PWH.
