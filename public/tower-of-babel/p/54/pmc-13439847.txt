Effects of positive pressure on tracheobronchomalacia and collapse reversal
Authors: Evelise Lima, Maria A M Nakamura, Ascédio J Rodrigues, Eduardo L V Costa, Samia Z Rached, Marcio V Y Sawamura, Pedro R Genta, Rodrigo A Athanazio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSymptoms in patients with Mounier-Kuhn Syndrome (MKS) may arise from associated tracheobronchomalacia (TBM). Although continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is a recognized therapeutic option, there are few evidences regarding its clinical effectiveness. This study aims to identify TBM in patients with MKS via bronchoscopy, evaluate whether CPAP can reverse airways collapse, and determine the optimal pressure. Furthermore, this research analyzes the impact of CPAP on pulmonary ventilation and investigates the prevalence of obstructive sleep apnea–hypopnea syndrome (OSAHS) within this population.MethodsThis is an open exploratory intervention study with involving patients diagnosed with MKS. Data collection included sociodemographic and clinical variables, alongside a review of previous diagnosis test. Patients underwent full-night polysomnography. Subsequently, bronchoscopy and electrical impedance tomography (EIT) were performed concurrently with CPAP titration to address airways collapse. For each affected region, the positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) level was determined using three methods: observational analysis during bronchoscopy “expert assessment”), post-hoc analysis of recorded bronchoscopy images (“objective assessment”), and EIT-based analysis “EIT-derived PEEP”).ResultsTwelve patients consented to participate. OSAHS was observed in 90%, and malacia in 91.66%. Twelve procedures were performed for CPAP titration (five in the tracheal and seven in the bronchi). Expiratory luminal area with PEEP 0 was smaller than the one with the PEEP selected based on the “objective assessment” (p-value < 0.001) and based on the “objective assessment” (p-value 0.002). EIT demonstrated changes in the distribution of pulmonary ventilation with CPAP.ConclusionTBM is highly prevalent in patients with MKS. Importance of the study

Although continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) has been proposed as a therapeutic option for patients with tracheobronchomalacia (TBM), its effectiveness in opening collapsed airways has not been previously demonstrated. In this exploratory study, we propose a novel method to quantify tracheobronchial collapse using bronchoscopy and to assess the impact of CPAP on airways dynamics and pulmonary ventilation. This approach may facilitate the diagnosis of TBM and promote the reversal of tracheobronchial collapse, thereby improving the homogeneity of pulmonary ventilation. Our findings contribute to the existing literature by providing evidence supporting the physiological benefits of CPAP in patients with TBM, while also establishing a framework for future investigations. Furthermore, this manuscript is of direct relevance to the journal’s readership, as it describes a reproducible bronchoscopic method with potential clinical applicability in patients with secondary TBM of diverse etiologies.

Introduction

Mounier-Kuhn Syndrome (MKS) or congenital tracheobronchomegaly is a rare disease characterized by dilatation of the trachea and main bronchi and is commonly associated with recurrent respiratory infections [1–3]. It is characterized by the enlargement of the transverse diameter of the trachea, right and left main bronchi, beyond 3.0 cm, 2.4 cm, and 2.3 cm, respectively [4, 5].

Although the cause is not well known, histological changes found in the airways, such as a decrease in the thickness of the muscular mucosa, atrophy, and absence of longitudinal elastic fibers and smooth muscle cells, may be responsible for the tracheobronchial structural deformities, such as tracheobronchomegaly, tracheobronchomalacia (TBM), intercartilaginous diverticula, bulges, and dilatations in the airways walls [1, 2, 6, 7].

The main signs and symptoms include coughing, voluminous sputum, dyspnea, and wheezing with recurrent respiratory infections [1, 3]. It is commonly associated with comorbidities such as bronchiectasis, gastroesophageal reflux disease, chronic pulmonary obstructive disease, and obstructive sleep apnea–hypopnea syndrome (OSAHS) [1, 8, 9].

It is believed that some of the symptoms associated with MKS are due to TBM, which is observed in most patients. TBM is defined as a 50% decrease in the tracheobronchial sectional area that occurs mainly during expiration as a consequence of muscle weakness and/or destruction of the support structures and cartilaginous rings [10–12].

Its primary clinical impact is expiratory airflow obstruction, which leads to air trapping and impaired cough efficacy. These factors compromise bronchial hygiene, thereby facilitating microbial colonization and recurrent respiratory infections [10, 12]. Respiratory endoscopy remains the gold standard for the diagnosis of TBM [13–15].

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) has been proposed as a therapeutic intervention for TBM [16, 17]. By acting as a pneumatic stent, CPAP maintains airway patency, reduces airflow obstruction, and improves secretion clearance and bronchial hygiene [12, 16–19]. However, clinical evidence demonstrating the effectiveness of this treatment remains limited.

Therefore, this study was designed to objectively identify TBM in patients with MKS using bronchoscopy, and to evaluate the capacity of CPAP to mitigate or reverse airway collapse. Furthermore we sought to determine the optimal pressure for this intervention, analyze the effect of CPAP on pulmonary ventilation, and investigate the prevalence of OSAHS within this patient population.

Methods

This is an open exploratory interventional study with one arm consisting of patients diagnosed with MKS in outpatient care at the Pulmonary Division of the Heart Institute (InCor) of the Medical School of the University of São Paulo.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Scientific and Ethics Committee of the Heart Institute) and registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03101059). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles for medical research involving human subjects as outline in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Bronchoscopy

For the procedure, a custom CPAP nasal mask was fitted to the patient’s face. The mask included a small port that allowed passage of the videobronchoscope (Olympus Standard Q180), which has a 4.9-mm external diameter and a 2.0-mm working channel.

Fentanyl (0.5–2 μg/kg) and midazolam (0.03–0.05 mg/kg) were administered intravenously, with propofol boluses (20–40 mg/dose) used as needed, to achieve light sedation (Ramsay scores between 2 and 3), important to minimize the potential effects of deep sedation on muscle tone reduction and to avoid worsening collapse of both the upper and lower airways [21].

Following airway inspection, bronchial hygiene, and identification of tracheal and/or bronchial malacia, pressure titration was initiated. During this phase, three experienced bronchoscopists independently performed real-time visual assessments to determine the minimum positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) required to reverse airway collapse. Through expert consensus, we established the minimum PEEP necessary to achieve complete reversal of collapse in the trachea and main bronchi (referred to as “expert assessment”). A reduction of at least 50% in airway collapse was considered indicative of malacia reversal (Fig. 1).

The bronchoscopic procedures were video-recorded, and subsequent image analysis was performed using Image Processing Toolbox (Matlab®, Mathworks, Natick, MA). This software enables pixel-based quantification of a selected region of interest (Fig. 2). When calibrated to a known reference distance, pixel counts provided a proportional representation of the airway cross-sectional area. This objective analysis allowed for the identification of the minimum pressure required to reverse airway collapse, hereafter referred to as the “objective assessment”.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or medians with interquartile range, as appropriated. The normality of data distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. A paired analysis was performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test to evaluate differences between expiratory pixel measurements and the inspiration/expiration ratio at 0 cmH2O and the PEEP identified by “objective assessment”.

Pearson's correlation coefficient was used to evaluate the strength and direction of the associations between the “objective assessment” method (the gold standard for the present study) and the other titration methods. Furthermore, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to compare the mean PEEP values obtained across the three proposed methods. All statistical analyses were conducted using the Stata software, version 15.1 (StataCorp, College Park, TX), with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Discussion

TBM is highly prevalent among patients with MKS. In this study, the application of positive airway pressure led to a significant increase in expiratory airway luminal area, complete reversal of malacia across all analyzed collapsed segments and improved distribution of pulmonary ventilation.

A proportional relationship was observed between airway luminal expansion and increasing CPAP levels. Muscle weakness and flaccidity of the tracheobronchial supporting structures allowed the airway lumen to expand proportionally to the applied pressure.

Regarding collapse reversal, we observed a significant correlation between the PEEP levels selected based on the “objective assessment” (considered the gold standard) and those determined by “expert assessment”. This finding suggests that both the diagnosis of malacia and the pressure required for its reversal can be reliably determined by a trained expert.

EIT demonstrated that positive pressure titration can improve the distribution of ventilation. In some cases, the PEEP required for this purpose was insufficient to reverse tracheobronchial collapse, indicating that positive pressure may benefit respiratory physiology, even without complete reversal of airway collapse. EIT findings also suggested that inappropriately high pressures may induce deleterious effects, such as pulmonary hyperinflation. Therefore, the empirical choice of high expiratory pressure to open the airways may be associated with this risk.

OSAHS was the most common comorbidity in patients with MKS, corroborating findings from previous studies [1, 8, 9]. Given its high prevalence, the presence of this condition does not appear to correlate with the clinical presentation of MKS. This observation suggests that the structural weakness identified in the lower airways may also be present in the upper airways.

Chest CT findings were consistent with those obtained by bronchoscopy. However, dynamic evaluation during bronchoscopy combined with EIT provided additional relevant information, including airway behavior and pulmonary ventilation patterns during CPAP titration.

In this study, we developed a method that enabled quantification of tracheobronchial collapse through bronchoscopy and assessment of the effects of CPAP on the airways and pulmonary ventilation. This approach allowed the diagnosis of TBM and reversal of tracheobronchial collapse, leading to a more homogeneous distribution of pulmonary ventilation. Although CPAP has been proposed as a therapeutic option for the management of patients with TBM, with symptomatic improvement and reduction in respiratory infections [13, 16–19], no study has yet demonstrated its effectiveness in reversing airway collapse.

The present findings therefore provide novel and potentially practice informing evidence supporting a reproducible method that may be adopted by bronchoscopists and further evaluated in patients with secondary TBM of diverse etiologies. Nevertheless, the long term impact of chronic CPAP therapy on patient-centered outcomes, including quality of life and survival, was not assessed and warrants further investigation. From a practical standpoint, a pragmatic initial strategy would involve nocturnal CPAP use to enhance mucociliary clearance, complemented by intermittent daytime application on demand. With ongoing advances in portable CPAP technologies, extended—or even continuous—daytime use may become a realistic and scalable therapeutic option.

Our study has several limitations. First, MKS is a rare disease, which limited the sample size. In addition, some patients with MKS followed at our center had severe airway compromise, precluding their inclusion. Considering that most patients had OSAHS and underwent pressure titration using a nasal mask under sedation, the PEEP levels identified may have overestimated the actual pressure required to reverse airway collapse. This may be related to sedation-induced reduction in muscle tone, potentially increasing the collapsibility of both the upper and lower airways. Other limitations include the absence of a control group and the technical challenges associated with performing CPAP titration. Although the presence of the bronchoscope may theoretically limit airflow and increase airway pressure, we do not consider this to represent a major limitation given the relative size of the device compared with the airway caliber of these patients. Furthermore, patient attrition during the study may have introduced selection bias.

Conclusion

TBM can be objectively diagnosed using bronchoscopy. CPAP promoted reversal of airway collapse and improved the distribution of pulmonary ventilation. These results support a potential therapeutic role for CPAP in TBM, further studies are required to establish its long-term impact on clinically relevant outcomes.
