Research trend on quantum dots for tumor diagnosis and therapy: a bibliometric analysis
Authors: Yonghui Feng, Danni Wang, Zhongqing Wang, Hui Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As nanoscale luminous materials, quantum dots (QDs) have gained extensive application in tumor diagnosis and therapy. Their size-tunable optical properties, compact nanoscale dimensions, high surface area, and excellent biocompatibility render them particularly suitable for tumor diagnosis and therapy, encompassing bioimaging, biosensing, drug delivery, as well as photothermal and photodynamic therapy applications. To systematically catalog the scientific advancements in QDs in the tumor-related research field, we first conducted a comprehensive bibliometric analysis of relevant studies published between 2002 and 2024. This bibliometric analysis revealed annual publication trends, highly productive journals, global collaboration networks among leading countries/regions and institutions, impact references, and the research hotspots and frontiers within the field. Co-occurrence cluster analysis of high-frequency keywords identified seven principal research domains: classification of QDs, properties of QDs, photochemotherapy, delivery, biosensing, bioimaging, and functionalization. Temporal visualization of high-frequency keyword networks further indicated that future research priorities will focus on green synthesis methodologies and clinical translation of next-generation QDs with enhanced biocompatibility and targeting capabilities. We hope to provide a systematic bibliometric perspective for cross-field researchers to promote the advancement of QDs in the tumor research field.

Introduction

Quantum dots (QDs) are nanoscale semiconductor particles with unique optical and physical properties such as tunable emission behavior, high quantum yield, superior photostability, and good biocompatibility[1]. Owing to these remarkable merits, QDs have shown significant application value within the biomedical field, particularly for tumor diagnosis and therapy[2–4]. QDs are highly desirable for bioimaging and biosensing in tumor diagnosis due to their photochemical stability and robust fluorescence emission[5]. Their compact nanoscale dimensions enable efficient cellular membrane penetration, facilitating tumor cell labeling in imaging applications. For biosensing applications, QDs have shown great sensitivity and specificity in detecting various tumor biomarkers at the molecular level[6,7]. Beyond diagnostic applications, QDs exhibit therapeutic potential for tumors through drug delivery, photothermal therapy (PTT), and photodynamic therapy (PDT)[8,9]. As drug delivery vehicles, QDs have been proven to have potential in tumor-specific targeting, microenvironment penetration, and controlled drug release[10]. Furthermore, QDs can generate localized heat and produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) upon irradiation, synergistically enhancing tumor ablation efficiency[11]. Despite the aforementioned advantages of QDs for tumor diagnosis and therapy, the potential toxicity of QDs remains a significant challenge for their clinical application[12]. Over the past decade, numerous review articles have been published on QDs in oncology[13]. However, with the continuous advancement of materials science and nanotechnology, research trends and hotspots of QDs in tumor-related research are also continuously evolving. Recently, bibliometric analysis has become a popular research method due to the growing number of publications available for analysis[16–18]. Bibliometrics, an emerging interdisciplinary field that integrates mathematical and statistical methods for quantitative analysis of scientific literature, enables researchers to rapidly identify research hotspots and development trends within a specific field[19,20]. In contrast to traditional narrative reviews, which tend to rely on expert opinions to highlight particular research fields subjectively, bibliometric analysis provides an objective, comprehensive, and quantitative summary of topics grounded in the best available information[21]. Its applications span scientific topic selection, discipline evaluation, and policy formulation[22,23]. Zhang et al systematically explored the prospects and developing trends in the field of tumor burden and immunotherapy through an innovative bibliometric analysis[24]. By employing bibliometric tools, Wang’s study not only identified current challenges and underexplored territories in the triple-negative breast cancer field but also illuminated emerging directions for the development and clinical application of biomarkers to enhance therapeutic strategies and precision management[25].

Conducting bibliometric analysis typically involves utilizing Web of Science (WoS) databases to download publication data, followed by using visualization software tools like Citespace and VOSviewer to extract and analyze publication data to create a knowledge map[26]. VOSviewer supports multiple data sources, such as WoS, Scopus, and PubMed. It can process large-scale literature datasets and automatically extract information, including keywords, authors, and institutions, to generate a co-occurrence matrix. Applying these tools enables researchers to intuitively grasp the knowledge structure and research dynamics in a specific subject.

Despite the benefits of bibliometrics mentioned earlier, there remains a notable absence of bibliometric analyses concentrating on QDs in cancer research fields. We have conducted a comprehensive bibliometric analysis of publications regarding QDs and tumors from 2002 to 2024, including the identification of the research hotspot, the main contributors, and the research trends with high-frequency keywords. This work has been developed and reported in line with the TITAN criteria[27].

Methods

Data source and search strategy

WoS is a comprehensive academic database with a wide range of high-quality journals across various disciplines, providing extensive research on scientific topics[28,29]. It features robust citation tracking capabilities that allow researchers to evaluate the impact and trends within the literature. The journals in WoS undergo a rigorous selection process to ensure their academic quality, and the database is regularly updated to ensure that users have access to the latest research findings[30]. This study systematically searched the Science Citation Index Expanded Database within the WoS Core Collection. The search query utilized was TS = “Quantum Dot$” and TS = (“Neoplasm$” or “Tumo$r*” or “Cancer$” or “Carcinoma$” or “Malignanc*”or “Malignant” or “Oncolog*”). No restrictions were applied to the publication time frame, and both non-English publications and non-article types were excluded from the results. The search was performed on 26 July 2024, and all bibliometric data were downloaded on the same day as the search to mitigate the effects of database updates. We excluded 87 non-English publications and 2363 derived from early access, book chapters, editorial material, and retracted publications. Finally, 7309 publications were extracted, each with information on publication year, journal, countries/regions, institutions, keywords, and references (Fig. 1).Figure 1.The workflow diagram of bibliometric analysis of QDs in the tumor research field.

The workflow diagram of bibliometric analysis of QDs in the tumor research field.

Bibliometric data analysis

VOSviewer (version 1.6.20), a well-established bibliometric analysis tool, was used to visualize the co-occurrence networks of QD in the tumor research field[29]. Our methodological framework incorporated multiple analytical dimensions: examination of citation patterns across various journals and publications to understand their intellectual linkages; mapping collaboration networks among different countries/regions and institutions; keyword co-occurrence analysis to identify dominant research themes and emerging trends. We also performed descriptive statistics on publication years, journals, countries/regions, and institutions, providing a comprehensive overview of the research landscape. By integrating these methodologies and tools, we aim to conduct an in-depth analysis of the applications of QDs for tumor diagnosis and therapy while assessing the impact of related literature in this interdisciplinary field.

Keyword analysis

A bibliometric keyword analysis was conducted on 7039 extracted publications related to QDs in the tumor research field, and 17 258 keywords were identified. “QDs” appeared as the predominant keyword (n = 5553), followed by “neoplasms” (n = 1535), “cells” (n = 1516), and “drug delivery” (n = 1023). Additionally, 10 keywords exceeded 500 mentions, including of “therapeutics” (n = 852), “gold nanoparticles” (n = 787), “graphene quantum dots (GQDs)” (n = 782), “toxicity” (n = 746), “probes” (n = 715), “carbon quantum dots (CQDs)” (n = 699), “fluorescence” (n = 662), “in-vivo” (n = 659), “delivery” (n = 627), and “in-vitro” (n = 533).

To comprehensively map the intellectual landscape of QD research directions in the tumor field, we conduct a co-occurrence analysis of keywords using VOSviewer with a frequency threshold of 40 occurrences, yielding 237 high-frequency keywords from the 7039 publications. As shown in Figure 7, eight keywords were located at the center of the visualization network map, including QDs, neoplasms, cells, fluorescence, fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET), gold nanoparticles, delivery, and imaging. Discussion

Global publication trend

Annual publication volume, journals, collaborative networks of countries/regions, and institutions were connected to present a comprehensive overview of the global publication trend of QDs in the tumor research field over the last 20 years. According to the bibliometric analysis results mentioned above, there has been a clear and consistent increase in the annual publication volume related to QDs in the tumor research field from 2005 to 2018, followed by a plateau observed until 2023. Three distinct evolutionary phases were also revealed, including the embryonic phase (2002–2007), the rapid growth phase (2008–2014), and the prosperity phase (2015–2023).

Among the countries/regions engaged in collaborative research within this field, China ranks first, followed by the United States and India, indicating substantial progress made by China. Furthermore, Chinese institutions hold the largest share of the collaboration network, which explains why China contributed the most publications related to QDs in the tumor research field. Notably, the publication output from the United States is less than one-third of that from China, while the difference in total citation counts between the two countries is relatively small. Regarding average citations per article, the United States significantly surpassed China, demonstrating that the United States maintained a leading position in both aspects of research quality.

Research hotspots and cutting-edge topics

Keyword co-occurrence analysis effectively reflects the individual research hotspots, while emerging keywords can indicate cutting-edge topics within the specific research field. By conducting a co-occurrence cluster analysis of the high-frequency keywords, the network visualization map shows seven main research clusters of QDs in the tumor research field from 2001 to 2023 (Fig. 7): #1 “classification of QDs” (red cluster), #2 “properties of QDs” (blue cluster), #3 “photochemotherapy” (green cluster), #4 “delivery” (purple cluster), #5 “biosensing” (yellow cluster), #6 “bioimaging” (cyan cluster), #7 “functionalization” (orange cluster). We further summarized the seven clusters into three subjects: Characteristics of QDs, tumor diagnosis and tumor therapy (Fig. 9). The research hotspots of each cluster are discussed in the following section.Figure 9.Graphical representation of the seven primary research clusters of QDs in the field of tumor research.

Classification of QDs

Cluster 1 (red cluster) is the largest one, comprising 45 high-frequency keywords. This cluster includes prominent nodes such as QDs, gold nanoparticles, GQDs, CQDs, carbon nanotubes, nanostructures, size, nanocomposites, CdTe, and others. From our perspective, the high-frequency keywords in cluster 1 mainly represented the classification of QDs based on their composition and structure, which are critical for their application in tumor research. QDs are semiconductor nanocrystalline materials ranging from 2 to 10 nm. Typical QDs are type-II core-shell heterostructures, such as CdTe and CdSe QDs. CdSe QD consists of a CdSe luminescent core, encapsulated by ZnS monolayer as a semiconductor shell to enhance photoluminescence intensity[31]. The size and composition of the core materials can be precisely regulated, enabling the customization of QDs for versatile applications in tumor diagnosis and therapy. Owing to their versatile applications in the tumor research field and growing considerations regarding their cytotoxicity, researchers have shifted focus toward exploring alternatives to traditional semiconductor inorganic QDs. Among these alternatives, carbon-based QDs (e.g., GQDs and CQDs) have emerged as promising candidates, which exhibit superior water solubility and improved biocompatibility[32,33]. Notably, our bibliometric analysis reveals that the average publication year for GQDs and CQDs (2019 and 2020) was nearly five years later than those for CdTe and CdSe QDs (2014 and 2015), highlighting the evolving compositional trends of QD research for tumor diagnosis and therapy. Conventional inorganic semiconductor QDs (e.g., CdTe and CdSe QDs) with remarkable photoluminescence properties are very attractive for in vitro tumor diagnostics. In contrast, novel types of QDs (e.g., GQDs and CQDs) with low biological toxicity are more suitable for in vivo tumor imaging and drug delivery[34]. The rational classification of QDs will facilitate the selection of more appropriate QDs for specific applications in tumor research.

Properties of QDs

Cluster 2 (blue cluster), encompassing 41 high-frequency keywords, mainly focuses on the fundamental properties of QDs, with a dual emphasis on their optical and physicochemical characteristics. The optical properties are represented by keywords such as fluorescence, luminescence, and photoluminescence, whereas physicochemical properties typically involve toxicity and water solubility. The distinctive optical merits of QDs, characterized by their broad absorption spectra combined with exceptionally narrow emission bandwidths, enable multicolor labeling using a single excitation source. Furthermore, their luminescent properties can be precisely controlled via size modulation due to the quantum confinement effect[35]. This size-dependent emission tuning manifests as a blue shift when particle dimensions decrease, a feature highly relevant for multiplexed bioanalytical applications. Wu et al developed a multiplexed fluoroimmunoassay capable of simultaneously detecting cytokeratin 19 (CYFRA 21-1), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), and neuron-specific enolase (NSE) in a single sample for lung cancer diagnosis[36]. Compared to traditional fluorescent dyes, QDs demonstrate exceptional resistance to photochemical degradation and photo-bleaching, offering significant advantages for molecular and cellular-level applications in bioimaging and biosensing[5]. Despite their diagnostic potential, the clinical translation of QDs faces significant challenges due to inherent toxicity concerns. The toxicological mechanism of QDs primarily arises from the release of heavy metal ions, generation of ROS, disturbance of organelles, and alterations of gene expression profile[37]. A study by Chong et al synthesized environmentally friendly GQDs and systematically evaluated their toxicity[38]. Comprehensive toxicological profiling revealed minimal cytotoxicity in vitro and favorable biodistribution profiles in vivo. Furthermore, the murine models demonstrated fast renal clearance of GQD without significant accumulation in organs. QDs have been proposed as diagnostic and theranostic agents for tumor research due to their unique properties. Their high photoluminescence quantum yield and exceptional photostability make them highly desirable as diagnostic probes for biomarker sensing and real-time tumor tracking[5]. However, minimizing QD toxicity remains challenging and is essential for broader clinical applications in tumor diagnosis and therapy.

Photochemotherapy

Cluster 3 (green cluster) comprises 41 high-frequency keywords, among which therapeutics, photochemotherapy, breast neoplasms, and PTT had frequencies exceeding 300. In recent years, research on QDs for cancer therapy has shifted towards non-invasive phototherapy methods, particularly PTT and PDT[33]. Phototherapy is widely investigated for treating breast neoplasms due to the accessibility of the breast tissue to light exposure[39]. In PTT, QDs act as light-absorbing agents that convert light energy into localized heat, with subsequent induction of protein destruction and tumor cell apoptosis. For instance, one study developed an intelligent nanovehicle by grafting the GQDs onto the pores of mesoporous carbon nanoparticles and loading doxorubicin as a model drug, achieving a significant synergistic chemo-photothermal effect for superficial and deep tumor tissues[40]. PDT, another phototherapy approach, is a two-stage procedure in which the photosensitizer is excited with light absorption, followed by generating ROS. These ROS amplify cytotoxic effects on tumor cellular components (e.g., membranes, proteins, and nucleic acids), triggering localized tumor cell death[41]. Regarding the application of QDs in PDT, they can serve directly as PSs or be conjugated with other molecules to enhance PDT efficacy. Combining PTT and PDT enables synergistic therapeutic effects, promising to improve the overall therapeutic outcome[42,43]. Wang et al employed IR820-conjugated GQD for synergistic PTT and PDT against Glioblastoma. In this system, GQD served as the photothermal agent, covalently conjugated to the photosensitizer IR-820[44].

Delivery

Cluster 4 contains 32 high-frequency keywords, including drug delivery, delivery, DNA, doxorubicin, and systems, referring to applying QDs as versatile delivery platforms. Drug delivery systems are greatly desired for precise tumor targeting, optimal drug delivery efficiency, and controlled drug release profiles[45,46]. QDs demonstrate exceptional potential for targeted drug delivery, benefiting from their compact size and high specific surface area. Their surface chemistry, characterized by abundant free amine and carboxylic acid groups, allows multiple drug-binding possibilities via electrostatic interactions or covalent linkages[47]. Additionally, their ultra-small size facilitates tumor tissue penetration and enhanced cellular membrane permeability. QD-based delivery systems primarily utilize two targeting mechanisms: passive accumulation via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect and active targeting through ligand functionalization[48]. Recent advancements in nanotechnology have accelerated the development of QD-based platforms capable of both precise tumor-targeting and controlled therapeutic agent release. A representative study developed CdZnSeS QD-based nanoplatforms co-loaded with botulinic acid and ceranib-2 as theragnostic probes for prostate cancer and leukemia[49]. These probes exhibited significantly lower half-maximal inhibitory concentrations against HL-60 leukemia and PC-3 prostate cancer cells compared to conventional formulations, as evidenced by drug release studies. Notably, fluorescent properties of QDs enable them to serve as imaging-guided drug delivery systems for real-time monitoring of key pharmacokinetic processes, including biodistribution, intracellular uptake, drug release, and systemic clearance[50]. A recent study by Hashemzadeh et al pioneered the potential of CDs in the gene delivery application[51]. The team engineered a brain-targeted nanovector incorporating polyethyleneimine-functionalized CDs, specifically designed to achieve blood-brain barrier penetration for precise delivery of CRISPR-Cas9 plasmid DNA to knock out the GFP gene efficiently.

Biosensing

Cluster 5 focuses on the biosensing applications of QD and comprises 37 high-frequency keywords. Nine of these keywords: probes, in vitro, FRET, electrochemiluminescence (ECL), biomarkers, sensors, biosensing techniques, signal amplification, and sensitive detection exhibit frequencies over 300. As excellent luminescent materials, QDs have garnered significant interest in developing highly sensitive biosensors for early cancer diagnosis. Specific biomarkers highly associated with tumor growth and metastasis can serve as indicators of carcinomas[52–55]. The unique optical and electronic properties of QDs enable their use as probes for detecting various tumor biomarkers, such as antigens, antibodies, nucleic acids, circulating tumor cells, and extracellular vesicles[56–58]. Compared to traditional organic fluorophores, QDs exhibit superior performance due to their broader excitation wavelength range, narrower emission wavelength, higher brightness, and longer photoluminescence lifetimes[7]. These attributes position QDs as ideal fluorescent probes for enhancing sensitivity and multiplexing capabilities in cancer diagnostic assays. FRET, the most prevalent mechanism in QD-based fluorescent biosensors, involves a distance-dependent energy transfer process from an excited-state donor to a proximal ground-state acceptor[59]. QDs have been employed as both FRET donors and acceptors for biomarker sensing. When employed as donors, their emission spectra need to overlap with the absorption spectra of an appropriate acceptor. Through the strategic integration of fluorescent QDs and DNA aptamers with magnetic nanoparticles, a multifunctional diagnostic nanoplatform was engineered. This rationally designed system demonstrated specific targeting of lung cancer-derived exosomes through aptamer-mediated recognition and quantitative profiling of epithelial-mesenchymal transition biomarkers via fluorescence detection[60]. Wang et al developed an ECL biosensor using CDs enriched with luminol as molecular emission centers. This design achieved an 80-fold enhancement in ECL intensity, enabling a wide detection range and ultra-low detection limits for MCF-7 breast cancer cell analysis[61].

Bioimaging

Cluster 6 focused on the diagnostic potential of QDs in bioimaging, with only 22 high-frequency keywords. The dominant terms of cells, in vivo, and imaging are the only three keywords with frequencies exceeding 300, indicating the cluster’s scope concentrated on cellular and in vivo imaging technologies based on QDs. These non-invasive methods leverage QDs to visualize internal cellular structures, dynamics of physiological processes, and pathological abnormalities (e.g., vascular blockages or tumor tissues), providing critical diagnostic insights[62]. At the cellular level, Xing et al synthesized fluorescent CQDs with switchable organelle-targeting capabilities, achieving specific labeling of cell membranes and endoplasmic reticulum through lipid-rich organelle affinity[63]. The fluorescent CQDs with cell membrane-targeting functionality enable real-time monitoring of membrane morphological dynamics during cell division and apoptotic processes. For in vivo applications, conventional visible-light QDs face limitations from tissue scattering and autofluorescence. To address this issue, near-infrared (NIR) QDs have been strategically adopted to minimize the strong absorbance and light scattering of tissue and blood[64]. A study by Qu et al synthesized chiral NIR-II fluorescent Ag2S QDs, revealing that surface chirality governs their primary localization and passive tumor accumulation in the orthotopic kidney tumor model[65]. Moreover, NIR-emitting QDs demonstrate enhanced tissue penetration depth (up to 5–7 cm in biological tissues) due to reduced photon scattering at longer wavelengths[32]. A novel NIR-II fluorescent probe based on core-shell PbS/CdS QDs was synthesized by Zhang et al in the organic phase[66]. This probe emitted bright fluorescence at 1600 nm, enabling rapid, real-time in vivo vascular and tumor imaging with a high tumor-to-normal tissue ratio, thereby providing a new tool for biomedical research.

Functionalization

Cluster 7 (red cluster) represents a functionalization process focused on surface modification of QDs through targeting ligands or functional coatings, which is a critical step to enhance their luminescence efficiency and biocompatibility[67]. Primary targeting ligands such as folic acid (FA), antibodies, and peptides, along with functional coatings (e.g., polymers, chitosan, polyethylene-glycol [PEG]) for QDs, were included in the high-frequency keywords of cluster 7. A persistent challenge in applying QDs for tumor diagnosis and therapy lies in achieving target specificity to minimize damage to normal cells and reduce the associated side effects on patients. This necessitates the conjugation of QDs with tumor-specific molecules and offers promising prospects for tumor cell targeting. Among the tumor-specific molecules, FA stands out as a preferred ligand due to its selective binding to folate receptors overexpressed on the surface of cancer cells. Bwatanglang et al demonstrated this strategy by developing chitosan-encapsulated QDs as drug carriers, which were loaded with 5-fluorouracil[68]. In this delivery system, FA was conjugated to the Mn-ZnS QD to enhance targeting efficacy for folate receptors expressing malignant tissues. Compared to free 5-fluorouracil, the FA-modified Mn-ZnS QDs exhibited significantly enhanced apoptosis in the MDA-MB231 breast cancer cells in vitro and 4TI breast cancer cells in vivo. Similarly, antibody- or aptamer-decorated QDs have shown exceptional specificity in tumor cell recognition for image-guided therapies[64]. Regarding functional coatings, PEGylation proved to be an effective strategy for enhancing the stability and biocompatibility of QDs. As demonstrated by Han et al, decorating the QDs-based drug nanovectors with a PEG corona significantly reduced nonspecific interaction while prolonging blood circulation time[69]. Furthermore, metalloproteinase-9 overexpression in tumor tissues enables selective detachment of the nanovectors’ PEG corona, achieving programmed drug release for precision therapy of pancreatic cancer.

Emerging frontiers of QDs in the tumor research field

Emerging keywords highlight cutting-edge topics within the specific research field. The temporal evolution map of high-frequency keywords reveals that the development of eco-friendly and biosafe QDs, as well as their clinical translation, represent the emerging frontiers and future research directions.

Carbon-based QDs

The number of publications related to carbon-based QDs has experienced rapid growth in recent years, underlining their gradual replacement of heavy metal-synthesized QD in biomedical research[70]. Notably, carbon-based QDs, including GQDs, CQDs, carbon nanodots, and carbonized polymer dots, can be synthesized via eco-friendly processes employing natural carbon precursors[71]. The inherent carbon skeleton structures provide them with excellent biocompatibility, low toxicity, and biodegradability, demonstrating significant potential in the field of tumor research[33,72]. In addition to the carbon-based QDs, there are also emerging green QDs attracting significant attention in tumor research. For instance, Li et al developed a light-driven synthesis strategy to synthesize sulfur QDs for fluorescence cell imaging[73]. Compared with widely reported thermal synthesis methods, this light-driven synthesis method exhibited enhanced eco-friendliness and operational simplicity.

Clinical translation of QDs

Although significant progress has been made in QD research for oncology, clinically approved QD-based products remain extremely limited[74]. Notably, emerging studies have reported promising QD-based applications with substantial clinical potential[75,76]. For example, a recent study developed boron-doped CQDs as a biocompatible nanoplatform for targeted cancer theranostics, demonstrating efficacy and biocompatibility through histopathological validation[77]. From our perspective, critical challenges must be addressed before QDs can achieve clinical translation. First, the development of standardized and scalable QD synthesis protocols is essential to ensure optimal QD properties and batch-to-batch reproducibility. Second, a comprehensive understanding of the interactions between QDs and physiological systems remains imperative, particularly biodistribution, retention kinetics, and clearance mechanisms. Last but not least, rigorous assessment of long-term health implications from chronic QD exposure constitutes a prerequisite for clinical advancement.

Limitation

Although bibliometric analysis is becoming increasingly popular and practical in research evaluation, this study also has some inherent limitations. First, the publications for analysis are only extracted from WOS, excluding data extracted using other search engines such as Scopus, PubMed, or Google Scholar. Thus, it is possible that publications appearing only through one of these other databases have been missed. Second, although we conducted manual keyword annotation to improve consistency and accuracy, limitations remain. Conceptual overlap, evolving terminology, and discipline-specific language nuances still pose challenges. Third, citation lag presents a temporal constraint, as recently published works may not have had sufficient time to accumulate citations, resulting in an underestimation of their academic impact.

Conclusions

We conducted a bibliometric analysis of QDs in tumor research, revealing current trends and future directions. This is the first analysis to examine QD research trends in tumors over the last two decades. This study analyzed annual publication volume, collaboration networks, and references to identify global trends in knowledge diffusion. High-frequency keyword clusters revealed that the hotspots of QD applications in tumor research lie in photochemotherapy, delivery, biosensing, and bioimaging. Compared to other nanomaterials (e.g., carbon dots, liposomes, gold nanoparticles), the unique fluorescent properties of QDs enable them to function as multifunctional theranostic platforms capable of rapid diagnostics, imaging-guided delivery, and synergistic photochemotherapy. The time-based network map indicated that future advancements will focus on the green synthesis of novel QDs for improved clinical applications. This bibliometric analysis presents the versatile applications and transformative prospects of QDs in tumor research, while motivating further exploration and innovation to accelerate their clinical translation.
