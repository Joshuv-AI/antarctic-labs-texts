Floating clavicle with medial clavicular fracture causing diagnostic dilemma - A case report and literature review
Authors: Pengkun Yu, Zhang Chi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importanceA rare case of a fracture of the medial third of the clavicle on the basis of the “floating clavicle” was hereby reported. To the best of our knowledge, this complex clavicle fracture has not yet been published. This article aims to describe, for the first time, a case of the diagnosis and treatment of this special clavicle fracture.Case presentationThe 26-year-old male patient suffered from a right sternoclavicular dislocation, a fracture of the right medial third of the clavicle, and a dislocation of the right acromioclavicular joint due to a car accident. In the course of treatment, he was missed diagnosis, and after active adjustment of treatment plan, good therapeutic effect was achieved.Clinical discussionA special case of a floating clavicle was hereby reported. The missed diagnosis of sternoclavicular dislocation will cause great difficulties in the management of the disease. Based on the condition and clear diagnosis of the patient, surgical treatment was actively recommended for the patient. The selection of the implant for surgical treatment varied due to its specificity. A comprehensive literature review is also provided.ConclusionClavicular bone fracture is common in clinical practice. At the same time, the injury of sternoclavicular joint and acromioclavicular joint should be vigilant. For the complex clavicle fracture reported in this paper, the computed tomography scan with 3D reconstruction can assist in diagnosis. In the case of definite diagnosis, the use of a contralateral distal clavicular plate in combination with the conventional clavicular hook plate is an appropriate choice.

Introduction

Floating clavicle is the injury pattern describing simultaneous ipsilateral dislocation of sternoclavicular and acromioclavicular joints [1]. The combination of both is extremely rare, with approximately 40 cases reported in the literature [2]. It was first described by Porral in 1831. Then, Beckman reported 16 cases in 1924 and the 17th case was added by Gearen and Petty in 1982. Sanders and associates treated 6 patients in 1990, and other isolated cases were subsequently published [2]. A special case of a floating clavicle was hereby reported, in which a traumatic dislocation of the acromioclavicular joint and the sternoclavicular joint was accompanied by a fracture of the medial third of the ipsilateral clavicular bone. However, no cases have been reported in the literature specifically linking the floating clavicle to the fracture of the intra-clavicular 1/3 [3]. The diagnosis and treatment of this disease were hereby reported, and certain treatment experience and guidance were provided. The work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria.

Case report

A 26-year-old male construction worker was sent by ambulance to the emergency department of our hospital after accidentally colliding with a car while riding a motorcycle. There was no nausea, vomiting, unconsciousness disorder and other discomfort at that time. During the evaluation, the patient's vital signs were relatively stable, with a convex mass visible at the proximal end of the right clavicle, a displaced projection at the distal end, tenderness of the joint at both ends of the clavicle, a snap of pressure and a bone rub sensation visible in the middle of the clavicle. The sensation and blood flow in the right upper limb were found normal. His range of motion could not be detected due to the pain in the right shoulder. According to the injury described by the patient, Shoulder joint X-ray films were routinely taken (Fig. 1a). In the treatment of hospitalized patients, the computed tomography scan with 3D reconstruction was routinely required (Fig. 1b). After inviting a consultation，thoracic surgeons were combined with chest CT to rule out traumatic complications such as lung contusion and traumatic hemopneumothorax.

Combining clinical symptoms and auxiliary examinations, the final diagnosis was: i) Right distal clavicle fracture; ii) Fracture of right intra-clavicular1/3; and iii) Dislocation of right sternoclavicular joint. The patient underwent surgery on the third day after admission.

Procedure

The patient was put under general anesthesia. A 12 cm incision was made along the clavicular direction with the fracture as the center. The sternoclavicular ligament and the costoclavicular ligament were repaired using absorbable sutures, and the articular cartilage disc was reconstructed. The distal clavicular locking plate was reversed-placed on the medial clavicular bone and in front of the sternal manubrium to fix the sternoclavicular joint and the fracture of the medial 1/3 of the clavicle (Fig. 2). The distal clavicle fracture was reduced by the hook plate (Fig. 3). Several locking screws were successively inserted to check the accuracy and security of the reduction of fractures and dislocations. Discussion

Traumatic floating clavicle is a rare injury with simultaneous dislocation of both ends of the ipsilateral clavicle. The reason may be the “first rib pivot theory” proposed by Maruyama et al. [4], which believes that the first rib matters considerably in the injury mechanism of the “floating clavicle”. When the violence from the anterolateral side of the shoulder acts on the acromion, the acromion and Scapula suddenly descend, and the Joint capsule and coracoclavicular ligament are torn off, making the acromioclavicular joint dislocation backward or upward. When transmitted inward along the shoulder peak, violence pushes the clavicle towards the first rib, causing the proximal clavicle to protrude forward under the lever, and thereby resulting in bipolar dislocation of the anterior sternoclavicular joint [5]. Combined with the injury factors and condition description of the patient in the car accident, it was speculated that the injury mechanism of the patient was that the car accident caused the patient to land on the right shoulder, which first led to the fracture of the distal clavicle. The violence continued inwards pushing the clavicle towards the first rib, resulting in an anterior dislocation of the sternoclavicular joint due to leverage. Finally, after the patient fell to the ground, the direct force of the impact on the medial clavicle resulted in a fracture of the medial third of the clavicle. In the past, the traumatic dislocation of the sternoclavicular joint was easy to be misdiagnosed and missed [6]. Anterior dislocation of the sternoclavicular joint is more common, and posterior dislocation also occurs. More importantly, dislocation of the sternoclavicular joint may damage important structures in the mediastinum, resulting in fatal consequences [7]. The gold standard imaging technique is a computed tomography scan, with 3D reconstruction when available, which will aid in preoperative planning. An AP as well as a serendipity view (30° ascendant X-ray) should be included, to facilitate the assessment of anteroposterior displacement [8]. Although there is a lack of treatment options for floating clavicles, most authors agree on surgical treatment for clavicle injuries, with a particular emphasis on young and active patients.

Kirschner wire tension band fixation was used earlier in the treatment of sternoclavicular dislocation. However, it has been reported that the fracture and displacement of Kirschner needle may cause internal fixation failure or other organ damage, or even slip into the chest cavity and cause death. In this case, this technique is usually not used alone [9].

Clavicular hook plate is used to fix sternoclavicular joint by lever principle and conforms to elastic fixation principle. But considering that this method is easy to cause mediastinum injury, and tail hook will cause wear on the sternum [10].

The repair and reconstruction technology of ligaments is more in line with the biomechanical requirements of the sternoclavicular joint, making the joint structure more stable and striving to restore its original physiological function, gradually becoming the mainstream surgical technique [11].However, these methods cannot fix Clavicle fracture. We believe that the distal clavicle plate is an excellent choice. The left distal clavicle plate was placed anteriorly on the sternum and anterior clavicle, which could be well attached to the anterior surface of the clavicle and fixed the sternoclavicular joint. Simultaneously, because the anterior plate conforms to the anatomical shape of the clavicle, it can be used to reduce the fracture of the medial third of the clavicle. Due to the larger diameter of the clavicle cross-section, plate placement in front of the clavicle provides greater grip on the clavicle [12]. For the “floating clavicle”, literature has pointed out that acromioclavicular joint dislocation (Rockwood III type) can be passively reduced in the case of a fixed sternoclavicular joint [13]. Following this principle, the sternoclavicular joint and the medial third of the clavicular fracture were fixed with the reverse placement of the contralateral distal clavicular plate. At this time ，this complex fracture can be regarded as a simple distal clavicle fracture. The following points should be emphasized when using this method in the operation: i) Choose to place the healthy side steel plate in reverse and try to repair the sternoclavicular ligament and costoclavicular ligament during the surgery; ii) Try to restore the role of the articular disc in maintaining the normal occlusal relationship of the sternoclavicular joint by repairing the articular disc during the operation. Repairing these soft tissues is of considerable significance, since the intraoperative repair of damaged and torn articular disc ligament and articular capsule can effectively reduce the possibility of postoperative recurrence of dislocation of sternoclavicular joint [14]; iii) When using an electric drill to fix the sternum handle during surgery, the action should be gentle enough to avoid damaging the clavicle and important blood vessels and nerves under the sternum handle, especially important tissues in the mediastinum. iv) When fixing the sternoclavicular joint, the screw length should be appropriate; v) Avoid strong fixation during fixation, since this method will make local internal fixation directly attached to the bone surface, which is not conducive to soft tissue growth and repair, and is also one of the reasons for the recurrence and re-dislocation after removal of internal fixation. However, there are still shortcomings in this treatment plan. The treatment plan sacrifices the micro motion function of the sternoclavicular joint. In the later stage, there may be discomfort such as joint degradation, arthritis, and even unbearable pain, which requires secondary surgical treatment [15]. The patient was asked to strictly implement the rehabilitation exercise plan and timely remove the internal fixation to make up for the treatment defects of the program. Comprehensive consideration was given to remove the implant after 12 months of surgery and ultimately achieve good results.

Conclusion

Floating clavicle is relatively rare in clinical practice, and dislocation of the sternoclavicular joint is easily misdiagnosed, which should be taken seriously by clinical physicians. CT + 3D reconstruction has important significance in the diagnosis of thoracoclavicular joint dislocation. The sternoclavicular joint is a micro motion joint, and this principle should be followed in the selection of intraoperative plants and conservative treatment. Herein, a rare and complex clavicular fracture and dislocation was reported. For this case, the contralateral distal clavicular plate was adopted to fix the sternoclavicular joint and the fracture of the medial third of the clavicular bone. The conventional clavicular hook plate was used to fix the distal clavicular fracture. Lastly this case of special complex clavicular fracture and dislocation was well treated.
