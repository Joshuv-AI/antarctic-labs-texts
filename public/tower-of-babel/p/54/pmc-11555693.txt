The role of prokaryotic argonautes in resistance to type II topoisomerases poison ciprofloxacin
Authors: Alina Galivondzhyan, Dmitry Sutormin, Vladimir Panteleev, Andrey Kulbachinskiy, Konstantin Severinov
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Argonaute proteins are programmable nucleases found in all domains of life. Eukaryotic argonautes (eAgos) participate in genetic regulation, antiviral response, and transposon silencing during RNA interference. Prokaryotic argonautes (pAgos) are much more diverse than eAgos and have been implicated in defense against invading genetic elements. Recently, it was shown that pAgos protect bacterial cells from a topoisomerase poison ciprofloxacin, raising a possibility that they may play a role in DNA replication and/or repair. Here, we discuss possible models of pAgo-mediated ciprofloxacin resistance. We propose that pAgos could (i) participate in chromosome decatenation as a backup to topoisomerases; (ii) participate in the processing of DNA repair intermediates formed after topoisomerase poisoning, or (iii) induce SOS response that generally affects DNA repair and antibiotic resistance. These hypotheses should guide future investigations of the involvement of pAgos in the emergence of resistance to ciprofloxacin and, possibly, other antibiotics.

Introduction

Argonaute proteins are ubiquitous nucleic acid-guided nucleases found in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes [1–7]. Eukaryotic Argonaute proteins (eAgos) are highly conserved and found in most species (except for some fungi [8]). eAgos use small RNA molecules as guides to recognize complementary RNAs. Upon recognition, eAgos either cleave their targets or recruit various protein partners [7,9,10]. This process, referred to as RNA-guided RNA interference, has a variety of functions, including regulation of gene expression and antiviral defense [11–15]. Structurally, eAgos consist of N-terminal, L1, PAZ, L2, MID, and PIWI domains. The PAZ and MID domains anchor, respectively, the 3′- and 5′-ends of guide RNA, while the N-terminal domain helps with the loading of guides and target RNA unwinding. The PIWI domain contains a conserved DEDX (X = D, H, K, N) motif that binds divalent magnesium or manganese ions and catalyzes the nucleolytic cleavage of targets [2,5,16–19].

Prokaryotic Argonautes (pAgos) are much more diverse and much less studied [3,6,20,21]. pAgos are present in 17% and 25% of, respectively, bacterial and archaeal genera, with some species encoding up to four pAgo paralogs. pAgos can be divided into three classes: ‘long-A’ and ‘long-B’ pAgos have domain architecture resembling that of eAgos, while ‘short’ pAgos contain only the MID and PIWI domains [20]. Most long-A pAgos contain an intact DEDX motif, while all long-B and short pAgos harbor amino acid substitutions that inactivate the nuclease activity. Catalytically inactive long pAgos and short pAgos are frequently co-encoded with putative nucleases, NADases, and transmembrane effectors [3,20–22]. Thus, these pAgos may function as sensors that recognize their targets and subsequently activate associated effectors that have global effects on cell physiology.

Most of the studied catalytically active long pAgos bind small guide DNAs (smDNAs) and target DNA [21,23–28]. Examples of pAgo nucleases that bind (i) small guide RNAs (smRNAs) to target DNA (MpAgo from Marinitoga piezophila [29], AmAgo from Alteromonas macleodii [30]), (ii) smDNAs to target RNA (PliAgo from Pseudooceanicola lipolyticus [31]), and (iii) smRNAs to target RNA (pAgos from Asgard archaea, the likely ancestors of eAgos [32]) are also known. In contrast to CRISPR effector complexes, pAgos interact exclusively with single-stranded targets. Thus, the DNA targets of pAgos must be pre-melted for efficient recognition and may become available for binding during DNA replication, recombination, and repair [21,23,24,33,34].

Functions of prokaryotic Argonaute proteins

pAgos provide host defense against mobile genetic elements

The uneven distribution of pAgos among prokaryotic phyla suggests that they spread via horizontal gene transfer, which is a common mechanism of distribution of host defense systems [3,5,20]. Indeed, pAgo genes are often associated with various phage defense systems [3,35] and protect cells from mobile genetic elements such as plasmids and bacteriophages. Several catalytically active pAgos, including TtAgo from Thermus thermophilus, CbAgo from Clostridium butyricum, MjAgo from Methanocaldococcus jannaschii, and PfAgo from Pyrococcus furiosus, all of which bind smDNAs and recognize DNA targets, inhibit plasmid transformation or eliminate resident plasmids in host species or when heterologously produced in Escherichia coli [21,27,28,36]. When expressed in E. coli, CbAgo, AmAgo and EmaAgo from Exiguobacterium marinum decrease phage titers and delay lysis of infected cultures [25,30,36]. The protective action of catalytically active pAgos likely depends on degradation of targets recognized by complementary smDNAs. These smDNAs are generated through a joint action of pAgos and cellular DNA repair and recombination machineries (the RecBCD complex in E. coli) [25,36].

Long catalytically inactive pAgos and short pAgos also protect cells from plasmid transformation or phage infection. The RsAgo from Rhodobacter sphaeroides (binds smRNA guides to target DNA) represses transcription of plasmid genes in its host and induces plasmid degradation in E. coli, likely with the participation of host-encoded nucleases [37]. Target recognition by other inactive pAgos induces effector proteins that either deplete NAD+/NADH, degrade cellular DNA, or disrupt the cell membrane. By killing the host cell, they prevent plasmid or phage propagation in clonal populations [38–42].

pAgos increase bacterial resistance to ciprofloxacin

Ciprofloxacin (Cfx) is a quinolone antibiotic that poisons bacterial type IIA topoisomerases (DNA gyrase and Topo IV in E. coli). These enzymes manipulate DNA topology by capturing two segments of DNA, introducing a transient double-strand break (DSB) in one segment, transferring another segment through the DSB, followed by religation of the first segment. Type IIA topoisomerases can alter the supercoiling level by capturing two segments of the same DNA strand or decatenate DNA by capturing DNA segments from replicated chromosomes. Cfx traps intermediate type IIA topoisomerases complexes in which the catalytic tyrosine residues of the enzyme are covalently linked to the 5′-ends of cleaved DNA. This prevents the completion of the catalytic cycle, inhibits the DNA relaxation [43,44] and leads to the formation of stable DSBs, which leads to SOS response and inhibition of cell division [45,46].

Intriguingly, several recent observations showed that pAgos increase bacterial resistance to Cfx. Deletion of the TtAgo gene greatly increases Cfx sensitivity of T. thermophilus [47]. Conversely, heterologous expression of SeAgo from Synechococcus elongatus or LrAgo from Limnothrix rosea in E. coli increases the minimal inhibitory concentration of Cfx ∼8–16-fold [48]. A PIWI-RE protein from Pseudomonas stutzeri, which is distantly related to pAgos, was also shown to protect host cells from Cfx [49]. The mechanism(s) of pAgo-mediated protection of cells from Cfx is unknown. Hypothesis 1: pAgos backup topoisomerases during decatenation of replicated genomes

The ability of active pAgos to perform targeted DNA cleavage/introduce DSBs suggests that they may directly assist chromosome decatenation during or after replication (Figure 1A). The DNA gyrase is the only type II topoisomerase in T. thermophilus and is thus likely involved in both supercoiling and decatenation of DNA. The ability of TtAgo to suppress cell filamentation when the gyrase is inhibited by Cfx suggests that it can substitute for the essential gyrase function, presumably by introducing DNA breaks, which allows decatenation even in the absence of the functional DNA gyrase [47]. Consistently, expression of catalytically active SeAgo or LrAgo in E. coli made cells more resistant to Cfx and also compensated for a gyrase knockdown [48]. A T. thermophilus strain with an inactivating mutation in the active site of TtAgo was more susceptible to Cfx than the wild-type strain [47]. Similarly, a catalytically-dead SeAgo mutant does not protect E. coli from Cfx (Figure 2A) (see Supplementary Methods for details). These data support direct involvement of the nucleolytic activity of pAgos in Cfx resistance.

Sequencing of smDNA guides associated with TtAgo in T. thermophilus and with several pAgos expressed in E. coli (CbAgo, SeAgo, LrAgo, EmaAgo) demonstrated that they preferentially originate from the replication termination (ter) regions of the chromosome (and, in the case of T. thermophilus, the megaplasmid) [25,36,47,48]. This suggests that pAgos may specifically recognize the ter region, and, by introduction of DNA breaks there, participate in the regulation of replication termination. Furthermore, mass spectrometry analysis of TtAgo complexes pulled-down from T. thermophilus showed that it is associated with DNA gyrase, Topo I, and proteins involved in recombination and processing of stalled replication forks (see also below) [47]. At least some of these interactions disappear after DNase treatment and are thus mediated by DNA [47]. Whether TtAgo or other pAgos can directly interact with the DNA gyrase and other replication/repair proteins remains to be tested. Taken together, these data suggest that pAgos may co-operate with DNA topoisomerases during late stages of DNA replication and substitute for their activity in the presence of Cfx. Specifically, pAgos may relieve positive supercoils accumulating between converging replication forks or resolve (pre)catenanes that accumulate in replicated circular chromosomes when the DNA gyrase activity is inhibited by Cfx. Testing of possible effects of pAgos on Cfx resistance of bacteria with linear chromosomes, where no replication fork collisions occur at the end of replication [50,51], could help unveil whether their effects are limited to the termination step of replication.

Some observations are inconsistent with direct substitution of type II topoisomerases by pAgos. Notably, deletion of the TtAgo gene does not change the sensitivity of T. thermophilus to novobiocin [47], a compound that inhibits the ATPase activity of topoisomerases and suppresses their DNA cleavage activity without accumulation of DSBs [52,53]. Likewise, SeAgo does not protect E. coli cells from novobiocin treatment (Figure 2B) (see Supplementary Methods for details). Moreover, the abovementioned T. thermophilus strain with a catalytic mutation of TtAgo was still slightly more resistant to Cfx than an isogenic strain harboring a deletion of the TtAgo gene [47], suggesting that the TtAgo role in decatenation may be to recruit recombination factors (such as the RecBCD homolog AddAB, Rad52, and a XerCD-accessory factor ArgR (XerA)) to the ter region. In other words, pAgos may suppress the effects of Cfx indirectly, by alleviating the consequences of DNA damage resulting from topoisomerase poisoning by Cfx.

Hypothesis 2: pAgos are involved in processing of DSBs formed upon topoisomerase poisoning

The covalent topoisomerase-DNA complexes stabilized by Cfx must be resolved to initiate the DSB repair process. According to the current model, in E. coli, the Lon protease degrades trapped topoisomerases and exposes modified DNA ends to exonuclease VII (ExoVII), which cleaves the link between the remaining peptide(s) and DNA and releases the 5′-end for further processing by RecBCD [54–56]. pAgos may also participate in the processing and/or repair of DSBs and thus increase resistance to Cfx (Figure 1B). When expressed in E. coli, pAgos are actively loaded with smDNAs originating from regions around engineered chromosomal DSBs and promote DNA degradation in these regions [36,57]. Furthermore, the distribution of smDNAs associated with pAgos at the whole-genome level is asymmetric around the Chi (crossover hot-spot instigator) sites, which are recognized by the RecBCD complex during DSB processing. A higher smDNAs density is observed downstream of Chi, where nuclease activity of RecBCD is expected [25,36,48]. Regions around the E. coli ter sites from which the most abundant smDNAs originate are also flanked by Chi sites, indicating that these smDNAs are produced by RecBCD from double-stranded ends formed during replication termination [25,36,48]. This suggests an interplay between the processing of DSBs by RecBCD and smDNAs loading into pAgos.

Mutations in RecBCD components dramatically change the profile of smDNAs associated with CbAgo in E. coli, making it Chi site-independent [36]. Furthermore, pAgos do not increase Cfx resistance in E. coli recA and recBD mutants, providing direct evidence that pAgos contribute to homologous recombination/DSB repair mediated by RecBCD [48]. In line with that, and as mentioned above, TtAgo was co-immunoprecipitated from T. thermophilus with components of various DNA repair pathways (AddAB, Rad52/Rad22, RecJ, UvrB), suggesting its involvement in the processing of recombination substrates and/or products [47].

The ability of SeAgo and LrAgo to provide resistance to Cfx in E. coli [48] suggests that they co-operate with heterologous host factors. In E. coli treated with Cfx, the SeAgo- and LrAgo-bound smDNAs preferentially originate from chromosomal regions between Chi sites and adjacent gyrase cleavage sites (GCSs) located downstream of Chi (Figure 3). In such regions, increased activity of RecBCD is expected due to repair of Cfx-induced DSBs [48]. We have performed similar analysis for Topo IV cleavage sites (TCSs) formed in the presence of Cfx [58] and found that the abundance of smDNAs produced from regions between Chi sites and adjacent downstream TCSs is also significantly increased (Figure 3) (see Supplementary Methods for details). Importantly, since there is only a partial overlap between GCSs and TCSs, pAgos might contribute to RecBCD-dependent processing of DSBs generated by both the DNA gyrase and Topo IV.

There are three non-mutually exclusive mechanisms on how pAgos could contribute to the repair of DNA damage caused by poisoning of type IIA topoisomerases. First, pAgos could cleave DNA around the Cfx-trapped topoisomerase complexes and thus initiate the repair process. In this way, pAgos can recapitulate the activity of Lon-protease and ExoVII, i.e. generate protein-free recombinogenic DNA ends [54,55,59]. If so, one can expect that some pAgos could suppress mutations in the Lon and/or ExoVII genes. However, our preliminary analysis of E. coli strains with knockouts of the xseA or xseB genes encoding the subunits of ExoVII showed that they are equally sensitive to Cfx both in the absence and in the presence of SeAgo, implying that pAgos may be acting in the same repair pathway as ExoVII (Figure 2D,E) (see Supplementary Methods for details). Further experiments are needed to decipher the possible functional interplay between pAgos and the ExoVII pathway. Second, pAgos might assist the RecBCD helicase-nuclease translocating from DSBs towards Chi sites in generating single-stranded recombination intermediates. Third, pAgos might recruit DNA repair factors and nucleases (including ExoVII, RecBCD/AddAB, and other nucleases) to DNA lesions and DSBs [47]. The direct involvement of pAgos in DSB repair is challenged by the fact that, while suppressing the effects of Cfx, they do not protect cells from other types of DNA damaging agents causing DSBs. For example, TtAgo does not affect the sensitivity of T. thermophilus to 4-nitroquinoline N-oxide, methyl methanesulfonate, cisplatin, and mitomycin C, while SeAgo does not change the sensitivity of Synechococcus elongatus to 4-nitroquinoline 1-oxide and nitrofurazone [47,48]. SeAgo also does not change the sensitivity of E. coli to mitomycin C (Figure 2C) (see Supplementary Methods for details). Since ciprofloxacin is a synthetic drug that does not exist in nature [60], it would be highly informative to determine whether pAgos can protect cells from natural topoisomerase poisons such as Microcin B17 or albicidin [61,62]. Moreover, deletion of the SeAgo gene does not increase susceptibility of S. elongatus to Cfx while its overexpression actually leads to increased sensitivity. This indicates that the role(s) a particular pAgo plays likely depend(s) on the interplay with different DNA repair pathways of the host and may change upon their horizontal transfer [48]. It also remains to be established whether pAgos may act as sensors of in vivo DNA damage other than the one caused by topoisomerase poisoning [63].

Hypothesis 3: pAgos mediate the SOS response

The effects of pAgos on cell survival in the presence of Cfx may result from activation of various DNA repair pathways by persistent DNA damage. pAgos likely randomly sample and cleave cellular DNA, in either guide-independent or guide-dependent ways [21,26,28,34,36,47]. The level of pAgo-inflicted cleavage must be low, as no fitness cost of pAgo expression and no self/non-self-discrimination mechanisms have been reported. Yet, this cleavage may induce low levels of SOS response that could be beneficial for cells under DNA damaging conditions (Figure 1C). Indeed, CbAgo expression induces SOS response in E. coli, while expression of its catalytically-inactive mutant does not, suggesting that the nuclease activity is essential for SOS response induction [64].

pAgos may either generate single-stranded gaps by nicking one of the DNA strands or DSBs by targeting closely located sites on opposite strands. In the first case, pAgos may induce SOS response through the RecF-dependent pathway [65]. If so, RecF depletion would decrease the level of SOS response stimulated by pAgos, which needs to be tested. In the second case, pAgo may induce SOS response via the RecBCD-dependent pathway. Consistently, pAgos do not protect the cells from Cfx when RecBCD is mutated (see above) [48]. Both pathways would lead to the assembly of activated RecA filaments and expression of SOS regulon genes [66].

A constantly elevated level of SOS response might increase cell resistance to DNA damage and cause persistence (phenotypical resistance without acquisition of mutations) to Cfx by pre-activating DNA repair machinery and elevating expression of homologous recombination machinery genes [67,68]. The SOS response increases the mutation rate [69] and the same effect may be expected for bacteria expressing DNA-targeting pAgos. Pertinent to Cfx resistance, increased mutation rates may stimulate resistance acquisition due to triggered adaptive mutagenesis, a phenomenon described for SOS-induced quinolone persisters [70]. Finally, continuous low-level SOS response may affect horizontal gene transfer and recombination [71,72], which could increase adaptation to antibiotics at the population level. Additional experiments in strains unable to induce SOS response are required to evaluate the contribution of pAgo-driven SOS response to fluoroquinolone resistance.

Conclusions and further directions

The phenomenon of pAgo-mediated resistance to ciprofloxacin is far from being fully understood. We propose that pAgos can either: (1) resolve (pre)catenanes by introduction of DNA breaks, (2) participate in RecBCD-dependent processing of Cfx-induced DSBs, or (3) elevate tolerance levels to DNA damage by induction of low-level SOS response. None of these non-mutually exclusive hypotheses fits all available experimental data. Moreover, the existence of a universal mechanism is challenged by the variable effects of pAgos on Cfx resistance in different bacteria (E. coli and T. thermophilus vs S. elongatus), which likely depend on species-specific DNA repair pathways.

Physical and functional interactions between pAgos and DNA repair factors require further investigation. Some gyrase-interacting proteins (GyrI, MurI, and YacG among others) can protect bacteria against Cfx by either interfering with drug binding or inhibiting the gyrase binding to DNA [73–77]. TtAgo co-immunoprecipitates with DNA gyrase [47], though it remains to be determined whether they interact directly and, if so, whether this interaction is necessary for Cfx resistance. Be that as it may, the demonstrated ability of pAgos to increase resistance to Cfx in diverse bacteria is concerning, showing that pAgos may increase resistance acquisition while their susceptibility to horizontal transfer may facilitate the spread of resistance in both environmental and clinically relevant bacteria.

Perspectives

pAgos are programmable nucleases that protect cells from foreign genetic elements and induce Cfx resistance. The latter makes them a potentially important target for control of antibiotic resistance acquisition in pathogenic bacteria.
