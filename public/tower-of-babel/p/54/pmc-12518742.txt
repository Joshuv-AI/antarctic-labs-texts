Exploring the barriers and facilitators of nutrition and dietary intake among school-aged children in the urban low-income setting of Addis Ababa
Authors: Yimer Mihretie Adugna, Abebe Ayelign, Tadesse Zerfu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUND/OBJECTIVESHealthy eating supports mental clarity, physical development, and focus in the classroom. However, government policy, cultural customs, education, family income, and food access, influence children's nutrition, which in turn affects school-age children's growth, development, and academic achievement. This study aimed to explore the barriers and facilitators influencing the nutritional and dietary intake of school-age children in urban low-income settings in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.SUBJECTS/METHODSA total of 10 schools (6 public and 4 private) from 2 subcities in Addis Ababa participated in a qualitative phenomenological study conducted in May 2024. Focus group discussions, key informant interviews (KIIs), and in-depth interviews (IDIs) with 92 participants were used to gather data. A total of 77 participants (41 male, 36 female) participated in 6 focus groups, 10 IDIs, and 5 KIIs from the Ministry of Agriculture, Ministry of Education, Save the Children, Water, Sanitation and Hygiene (WASH), and Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI). Using MAXQDA software, native language data were transcribed, translated, and then thematically analyzed.RESULTSTwo major themes emerged from the study's findings. The fisrt one is barriers to healthy nutrition and dietary intake in school-aged children, including lack of awareness, inadequate and unhealthy dietary practices, financial hardship and time constraints within households, parental lack of awareness, food accessibility issues, poor infrastructure, and inconsistent policies. These factors were described as potential contributors to challenges in children's nutrition. The second one is facilitators, which include school meal programs, community engagement, family support, physical activity, and policy support for healthy eating, all of which are seen as promoting healthier dietary behaviors and possibly improving children's focus and participation in school activities.CONCLUSIONSeveral factors influence children's diet and nutrition. Improving children's nutritional and general well-being requires focused nutrition education, policy interventions, and the utilization of coordinators.Keywords: Children, focus groups, interviews as topic, nutritional status, socioeconomic factors

INTRODUCTION

Consuming a balanced diet with essential nutrients such as proteins, fats, vitamins, and carbohydrates is crucial for good nutrition, promoting growth and cognitive development, and preventing malnutrition [1,2]. In urban low-income areas such as Addis Ababa, children face socioeconomic barriers such as poverty, poor environmental conditions [3,4], and limited access to nutrient-rich foods, hindering proper nourishment [5,6]. These factors contribute to undernutrition, negatively affect children’s physical and mental health, and are worsened by poor feeding habits, inadequate sanitation, and limited nutritional knowledge [7,8].

Many school-age children in Addis Ababa’s urban low-income areas struggle with inadequate nutrition, which is vital for growth and development [9]. Economic hardship, food insecurity, and poor environmental conditions contribute to this issue [10]. A lack of nutritious food affects children's health, academic performance, and overall well-being [11]. Addressing these challenges is essential for improving their future prospects.

A number of facilitators can encourage children in these environments to eat healthier food and consume more food. Community-based initiatives that provide meals during school hours, including school feeding programs, have demonstrated promise in alleviating acute nutritional deficits [12]. However, there are several barriers to healthy eating, including financial constraints, cultural habits, and limited nutritional awareness [13,14]. Families with low incomes struggle to afford nutrient-rich foods [15], whereas food insecurity—driven by fluctuating prices and weak distribution systems—makes healthy meals less accessible [16,17]. Additionally, poor sanitation and a lack of clean water increase the risk of infection, impairing nutrient absorption and overall health [18,19].

To overcome barriers to adequate nutritional intake, strict management through government programs that improve sanitation and hygiene conditions and nutrition education activities can be crucial [20]. These initiatives, which are backed by both domestic and foreign organizations, have the potential to increase the accessibility and availability of nutrient-dense foods, improve families' knowledge of good eating habits, and foster an atmosphere that is more conducive to children's development [21,22].

Although several studies in Ethiopia and the broader sub-Saharan region have focused on the nutritional status of school-aged children, most have used primarily quantitative approaches or concentrated on rural settings [23,24]. Few studies have examined the lived experiences and contextual challenges shaping dietary practices in urban low-income environments. Moreover, the literature often overlooks the perspectives of caregivers, community members, and institutional actors, who play vital roles in children’s nutritional outcomes.

This study is important for understanding the barriers to and facilitators of healthy eating in urban low-income settings. By exploring these factors, we gain deeper insights into the complex interplay of socioeconomic, cultural, and environmental conditions that influence children's nutritional status [14,25]. This knowledge is essential for creating focused interventions and policies that will enhance the nutritional intake and general health of Addis Ababa's school-age children, thereby assisting in the mitigation of nutrition-related issues in this susceptible group. Thus, this study aimed to explore the barriers affecting and facilitators that promote the nutritional and dietary intake of school-age children in the urban low-income setting of Addis Ababa.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

Study area

The study was conducted from April 1 to May 10, 2024, in 10 primary schools—6 public schools and 4 private schools—located in the Kolfe Keraniyo and Nifas Silk Lafto subcities of Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. The majority of the households in these areas are considered low income, with many earning below the national poverty line, and living in overcrowded or substandard housing lacking basic infrastructure such as sanitation and piped water [26,27].

Theoretical framework

This study uses the social ecological model (SEM) [24] to explore the barriers and facilitators affecting child nutrition. SEM helps understand the factors shaping dietary behaviors at multiple levels, from individual choices to societal influences (Fig. 1). It highlights key elements such as personal knowledge, family support, school environments, food access, and policy interventions. By addressing both challenges and enablers, SEM helps identify nutritional disparities and guides the design of multilevel interventions, such as school meal programs, to improve nutrition in vulnerable populations. It also considers cultural and social factors, linking childhood nutrition to long-term health and academic outcomes.

Study design

A community-based qualitative study explored the barriers and facilitators affecting children's nutritional status. A phenomenological approach [25] was used to examine parents' and caregivers' experiences with factors influencing children's dietary intake.

Study population

This study involved school-age children living in Addis Ababa, along with their parents or caregivers, school directors, teachers, and representatives from government and nongovernment organization officials.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

• Participants aged 18–57 yrs.

• Participants aged 18–57 yrs.

• Representations from various socioeconomic backgrounds, including officials and experts from relevant ministries, Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI), Water, Sanitation and Hygiene (WASH) programs, and non-governmental organizations (NGOs).

• Representations from various socioeconomic backgrounds, including officials and experts from relevant ministries, Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI), Water, Sanitation and Hygiene (WASH) programs, and non-governmental organizations (NGOs).

• Parents, caregivers, or community members with experience in child nutrition or food preparation.

• Parents, caregivers, or community members with experience in child nutrition or food preparation.

Exclusion criteria

• Individuals under 18 yrs of age, unwilling to participate, or unable to provide informed consent.

• Individuals under 18 yrs of age, unwilling to participate, or unable to provide informed consent.

• Those with severe illness, health conditions, or communication difficulties affecting participation.

• Those with severe illness, health conditions, or communication difficulties affecting participation.

Sample size and sampling technique

Two urban low-income subcities, Kolfe Keraniyo and Nifas Silk Lafto, were purposefully selected for this study on the basis of their high population density, socioeconomic vulnerability, and relevance to the research objectives. These areas reflect typical urban challenges in Addis Ababa related to nutrition and WASH, making them suitable for exploring lived experiences in such contexts. Within each subcity, 5 woredas were randomly selected, and participants were purposefully chosen for their first-hand experiences and roles in child nutrition and school environments.

Data collection was guided by the principle of thematic saturation. Saturation was considered reached when no new themes or insights emerged from the data. This point was observed after the 6th focus group discussion (FGD) and the 10th in-depth interview (IDI), at which stage subsequent data collection confirmed recurring patterns without yielding additional themes [28,29]. The final sample included 92 participants: 77 in 6 FGDs, 5 key informant interviews (KIIs), and 10 IDIs with parents, school directors, teachers, and nutrition focal persons. The data were reviewed daily to determine if further interviews were needed.

Data collection and analysis

Data collection was carried out by the principal investigator (PI) and 7 experienced research assistants, one female and 6 males at various locations, on the basis of participant availability. The 6 research assistants were assigned to take notes during the FGDs, and one researcher was assigned to the IDIs and KII after brief training on research ethics by the PI. FGDs took place in schools during meetings, whereas IDIs and KIIs were held in schools and different government and nongovernment offices. The authors carefully developed the interview guides in English with the support of a fluent English teacher and translated them into Amharic. The interview guide was pretested on 5% of the study population, who were not included in the main study, before the actual data collection.

Individual IDIs and KIIs

Before each interview, basic participant information such as sex, age, nationality, work status, and marital status was recorded. A male data collector with a BSc degree conducted KIIs and IDIs via semistructured guides [30]. Each IDI and KII lasted between 30 and 90 min. The interviews were recorded to capture the participants' responses accurately. The recordings were later transcribed into English and securely stored on the researcher’s personal computer, with translations carefully made to maintain the original meaning.

FGDs

Each FGD, consisting of 6–12 participants, was facilitated by a trained moderator with support from a note-taker. The discussions lasted between 50 and 105 min. The research assistants included 5 males and one female, all of whom held master's degrees. Using semistructured guides [31,32,33], discussions explored the factors affecting children's nutritional status. Participants: parents, children, teachers, and school officials were purposively selected on the basis of experience. The groups were mixed in terms of sex but shared similar backgrounds. Sessions were held in neutral settings, recorded, and transcribed for accuracy. The guide was translated into Amharic and pretested. Sociodemographic data were collected, and participants were coded (IDI1–IDI10, KII1–KII5, FGD1–FGD6) to ensure confidentiality.

Data analysis

The authors transcribed all the IDI, KII, and FGD recordings verbatim in Amharic and translated them into English. The accuracy of translation was ensured by having a second bilingual researcher review a subset of translated transcripts. Any discrepancies between the original Amharic meaning and the English translation were discussed and resolved collaboratively. This process helped ensure that the participants' meanings were preserved. A total of 6 FGDs, 10 IDIs, and 5 KIIs were transcribed, managed, and coded via VERBI Software (2020), following predefined codes from the SEM [34], which helped categorize the barriers and facilitators affecting children's nutrition at different levels: individual, interpersonal, community, organizational, and policy.

Both deductive and inductive approaches were used during the coding process. A codebook was developed manually by the PI, drawing on initial readings of the data and the SEM framework. The draft codebook was reviewed by coauthors, and changes were made through ongoing discussions. To support consistency in how the codes were applied, 2 members of the team coded a portion of the transcripts separately and then compared their work. Any differences were discussed and resolved together, helping to ensure a shared understanding. Although no formal statistical testing for intercoder reliability was performed, this collaborative approach helped maintain the accuracy and trustworthiness of the coding process.

Themes and subcategories were then grouped to reflect broader patterns across the data. Thematic analysis helped identify the main ideas, as well as important differences and similarities in what participants shared. Key findings were organized around major themes, whereas smaller themes were interpreted in the context of literature. The selected quotes were translated into English to preserve their original meaning and enhance the credibility of the findings.

Data quality assurance

The study ensured validity and reliability through credibility, dependability, transferability, and trustworthiness. Dependability was reinforced by participant-verified transcripts and a documented audit trail [35,36]. Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement; the triangulation of FGDs, KIIs, and IDIs; and peer debriefing. Transferability was supported by detailed contextual descriptions, whereas purposeful sampling captured diverse perspectives. Confirmability was maintained by grounding findings in participant responses, tracking biases in a journal, and undergoing external auditing. MAXQDA software [37] ensures accurate data coding and organization, strengthening the study’s integrity.

Ethics

The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the College of Natural and Computational Sciences of Addis Ababa University approved the study on February 20, 2023, with the reference number CNCSDO/515/15/2023. All participants and guardians were informed about the study and provided written consent. Participation was voluntary, with the option to withdraw anytime without consequences. Confidentiality was ensured, with only aggregated data used. The participants received full details on the study’s purpose, benefits, and potential risks.

Individual-level barriers

In this study, individual-level barriers were identified as factors inherent to the school children themselves. These barriers were classified as 1) inadequate and unhealthy dietary practices and 2) lack of awareness.

1) Inadequate and unhealthy dietary practices

The majority of participants across FGDs and IDIs reported that school-aged children regularly skip meals and rely on unhealthy foods, which negatively affects their health, learning, and concentration in class. Skipping breakfast was especially common in public schools, often because of household food insecurity. In addition, children frequently consume sugary snacks, sweetened drinks, and deep-fried items in place of nutritious meals [39]. These habits were observed across income levels.

“Children often skip meals or eat sugary snacks, which makes it hard for them to focus in class.” (FGD1, 36-yr-old male, teacher)

However, a few participants reported that despite limited food availability, some children remained focused in school because of high motivation or strong family support.

“Even though my daughter sometimes leaves without eating, she still does well. She says she want to be a doctor and pushes herself hard.” (IDI7, 42-yr-old male, school director)

2) Children’s lack of awareness

Many participants highlighted that school-aged children often lack basic knowledge about nutrition, which affects their ability to make healthy food choices. This was a common concern raised across focus groups and interviews, with parents, teachers, and school officials noting that children tend to prefer sugary snacks and fast foods simply because they do not understand what constitutes a balanced diet.

Most participants emphasized the need for early nutrition education in schools, suggesting interactive lessons, awareness campaigns, and greater parental involvement to help children develop better habits. Without such exposure, they noted that children are unlikely to choose foods that support their growth and learning.

“My children don’t understand healthy eating and often reject nutritious foods such as injera and meat for fast foods such as biscuits and candy.” (FGD3, 56-yr-old female, secretary)

However, a few participants shared more hopeful perspectives. Some children, especially those involved in school clubs or nutrition-focused programs, were more aware of healthy options and even encouraged their peers to make better choices. One teacher described an exceptional case in which a student began teaching her younger siblings about balanced meals after attending a nutrition club in a school.

Interpersonal-level barriers

In this study, interpersonal barriers were highlighted as follows: 1) financial hardship and time constraints within households and 2) lack of awareness.

1) Financial hardship and time constraints within households

The majority of participants consistently identified financial hardship as a major barrier to children’s nutrition. Rising food prices, limited household income, and inflation were frequently cited across FGDs and IDIs. Families often resort to cheaper, less nutritious staples, whereas fruits, vegetables, and protein-rich foods remain unaffordable [40]. These economic difficulties were compounded by parents’ demanding work schedules, which limited time for meal preparation and meal diversity.

“After work, I’m often too tired to cook, so we settle for quick meals such as noodles.” (FGD1, 45-yr-old male, bank teller)

“Food has become so expensive in Ethiopia that it’s hard to serve children fresh, diverse meals.” (IDI1, 50-yr-old male, school director)

However, a few participants described coping strategies, such as bulk purchasing seasonal produce, preparing meals in advance, or relying on community support programs to maintain healthier food choices despite financial limitations.

2) Lack of parental awareness

The majority of participants noted that many parents give their children an unbalanced diet since they are unaware of it and frequently rely on staples such as pasta, rice, or fir-fir rather than a variety of foods. The participants also emphasized that parental education affects the quality of diets because, despite having completed basic education, many caregivers lack precise nutritional information.

“Parental education matters—not just income but knowing how to provide the right nutrition.” (IDI10, 46-yr-old female, school director)

A few participants shared that some parents are becoming more proactive, seeking out information from health centers or school-based programs. These parents were described as making more diverse and balanced food choices, even within limited means.

A noteworthy experience was shared by a mother, who explained that after regularly watching a local TV show about child nutrition, she began changing the way she prepared meals for her family. She introduced more vegetables and protein sources into her diet and noticed improvements in her child’s energy and concentration at school.

Community-level barriers

The community-level barriers in this study include 1) economic barriers and food accessibility and 2) infrastructure and community support for nutrition.

1) Economic barriers and food accessibility

Most participants said that economic challenges make it very difficult for families to obtain nutritious food. The cost of fresh produce, meat, and other healthy ingredients continues to rise, and many low-income households cannot afford to prepare balanced meals. The participants also mentioned that food prices are pushed even higher by intermediaries. Seasonal changes also contribute to this problem; for example, fruits often become more expensive shortly after the harvest period. These issues directly affect how well children eat and grow.

“The rising cost of food makes it hard to provide nutritious meals. I often choose cheaper, less healthy options just to keep everyone fed.” (FGD3, 56-yr-old female, secretary)

Some participants mentioned home gardening, local cooperatives, and community programs as ways to access affordable, healthy food. One mother shared that a neighborhood garden project helped families obtain fresh vegetables, improving their children's diets.

2) Infrastructure and community support for nutrition

Most participants reported that many schools lack facilities and community resources to support access to nutritious food and ensure food safety. This makes it difficult for students to maintain a balanced diet. The few available shops mostly sell affordable, processed, sugary snacks such as biscuits and candies, which lack nutritional value and contribute to fatigue, poor concentration, and long-term health issues such as obesity and dental decay.

“Children rely on processed snacks due to the lack of shops offering affordable, nutritious food near schools.” (IDI8, 28-yr-old female, school director)

At the same time, a few schools have started small feeding programs or partnered with local farmers to improve food access. One director described a school garden project that provided fresh vegetables and encouraged nutrition education.

Most participants raised concerns about inconsistent access to clean water, which worsens hygiene and makes safe food preparation difficult. Inadequate storage systems prevent proper washing of raw foods, increasing the risk of contamination and waterborne illnesses such as diarrhea and typhoid.

“It is clear that children must have access to clean water. We also believe that they should consume clean, safe food.” (KII, 38-yr-old female, ministry of agriculture official)

A few participants described small but effective measures, such as using sealed storage containers or collecting rainwater, to improve access to clean water and help prevent contamination.

Organizational-level barriers

Organizational-level barriers involve a lack of accountability and organizational support.

1) Lack of organizational support and responsibility

Most participants felt that government agencies and NGOs provided limited support to low-income families. Few children benefit from school meal programs, and the absence of dormitories makes it harder for vulnerable students to access consistent food and education.

“While there are programs that help families, their reach is often limited and doesn’t cover all our needs” (FGD2, 52-yr-old female, housewife)

At the same time, some participants acknowledged isolated efforts, such as temporary feeding programs or NGO-led campaigns, although these efforts were not seen as sustainable or widespread.

Policy/enabling environment level barriers

These barriers include 1) a lack of effective food price control policies and 2) a lack of education and supportive policies.

1) Lack of effective food price control policies

Most participants identified weak food pricing regulations as a major barrier to children’s nutrition. The participants explained that fluctuating prices, caused by middlemen and weak oversight, make nutritious meals unaffordable for low-income families, pushing them to rely on cheaper, unhealthy alternatives. The lack of government action to stabilize prices and monitor food quality was also a shared concern, with some vendors reportedly adding unsafe ingredients to increase profits. These issues harm children’s health, cognitive function, and school attendance, ultimately affecting their long-term growth and success.

“Better policies around food pricing, especially for healthy options, are crucial for families like mine.” (FGD1, 33-yr-old female, accountant)

“There is a need for policy improvements to make nutritious food affordable and accessible for all.” (IDI15, 47-yr-old male, school director)

A few participants acknowledged government efforts, such as subsidies for staple items, but noted that these initiatives are limited in scope and rarely include fresh or nutrient-rich foods.

2) Lack of education and supportive policies

Most participants emphasized the lack of nutrition education for both students and parents. They noted that without proper guidance, families struggle to make healthy food choices. Many recommended school-based programs that include nutrition lessons, parental engagement, gardening, and cooking demonstrations to promote better diets and active living.

“Policies should aim for equitable distribution of food and continuous nutrition education for both parents and students.” (IDI14, 45-yr-old male, school director)

A few participants shared encouraging examples, such as schools that organize nutrition days or invite health workers to speak about balanced diets. These efforts, although limited, were seen as effective in raising awareness and improving habits.

Theme 2: Facilitators of nutrition and dietary intake: FGDs and IDIs

Individual-level facilitators

In this study, individual-level facilitators are personal factors such as understanding nutrition and being physically active. These factors help children develop healthier nutrition and eating practices.

1) Physical activity and nutrition

Most participants highlighted the importance of regular physical exercise in improving children's appetite, digestion, and energy levels. Parents and teachers explained that active children tend to eat better, stay focused in class, and perform well both physically and academically.

“When they do not engage in physical exercise, their nutrition and dietary practices decrease.” (FGD6, 49-yr-old male, agricultural expert)

Some participants noted that limited school facilities or safety concerns restrict children's opportunities for regular activity. A few shared positive examples, such as schools with organized sports or community spaces, encourage active play.

Interpersonal level facilitators

Interpersonal-level facilitators in this study included 1) family support in nutrition and 2) education and media on nutrition.

1) Family support in nutrition

Most participants agreed that parents play a key role in shaping children's eating habits. Despite financial and behavioral challenges, many parents try to provide balanced meals. However, children often prefer packaged snacks, which creates tension at home, as parents attempt to promote healthier choices without much support from schools or the community.

“I’ve been trying to encourage them to enjoy healthier options by making meals more appealing.” (FGD4, 32-yr-old male, private worker)

A few participants shared creative strategies, such as involving children in meal preparation or offering rewards for choosing fruits and vegetables. While these efforts are not always successful, they reflect a strong parental commitment to better nutrition.

2) Education and media on nutrition

Many participants noted that both social and traditional media play a growing role in nutrition education. Platforms such as Facebook, TikTok, Telegram, and TV help raise awareness and guide families in making healthier choices. These tools were seen as especially useful for parents seeking practical tips on affordable, nutritious meals.

“TV programs such as ‘Mirtu Gebeta’ help families plan healthier meals on a budget.” (FGD3, 37-yr-old male, agricultural expert)

Some participants noted that access to such media varies and that not all families benefit equally. Nevertheless, these programs are widely appreciated for their potential to improve food choices and promote nutrition awareness at both home and school.

Community-level facilitators of nutrition

The community-level facilitators in this study include 1) community engagement and collaboration, 2) access to and equity in nutrition, and 3) cultural and social influences.

1) Community engagement and collaboration

Most participants agreed that community engagement remains limited. However, they highlighted that programs such as the Productive Safety Net Program (PSNP) and school-feeding initiatives play important roles in supporting low-income families. These efforts help reduce food insecurity and improve children’s nutritional outcomes.

“Numerous families received assistance from the safety net program, which helps them support and care for their children.” (FGD4, 48-yr-old male, teacher)

A few participants felt that access to these programs was uneven across communities. Others reported that some schools have begun working with parents or local leaders to expand support at the school level.

2) Access and equity in nutrition

Most participants highlighted access to and equity in nutrition as key community-level facilitators. They emphasized the importance of making safe and nutritious food available to all, especially vulnerable, groups. Some schools use their land to grow fruits and vegetables, which are then added to school meals to increase their nutritional value.

“Community gardens serve as educational platforms offering workshops on planting, crop care, and healthy meal preparation, helping families incorporate more fresh produce into their diets.” (FGD4, 53-yr-old male, teacher)

While these practices were appreciated, a few participants noted that not all schools have the space, knowledge, or support to implement such initiatives. Others noted that families in urban areas often lack access to land or tools for home gardening.

3) Cultural and social influences

Most participants emphasized the positive role of cultural festivals such as Meskel, Genna, Fasika, and Timket in shaping children’s eating habits. These events often include traditional dishes made with meat, dairy, and grains, which provide children with nutritious meals while strengthening social bonds, family unity, and ethnic identity.

“During festivals, we celebrate with a variety of traditional dishes (a variety of dishes scoop with injera), and this exposure helps them appreciate our culinary heritage.” (FGD4, 31-yr-old male, agricultural expert)

Some participants noted that these benefits are short-lived, as such meals are limited to special occasions. Others noted that not all families can afford these foods during holidays, which may highlight economic disparities even in festive contexts.

Organizational-level facilitators

The organizational-level facilitators in this study included 1) school-based nutrition and meal programs and 2) community-led nutritional support services.

1) School-based nutrition and meal programs

Most participants emphasized that school-based nutrition programs, especially government-led school-feeding initiatives, are essential for improving children’s health, concentration, and academic performance. Teachers reported noticeable differences in focus and classroom participation among students who received regular, balanced meals, particularly those with vegetables and protein.

“Children who eat balanced meals are more focused and perform better in class.” (IDI14, 52-yr-old male, school director)

However, a few participants noted that in some schools, meals are irregular or lack variety, limiting their impact. Others raised concerns about poor food quality or a lack of trained staff to manage the programs. These findings show that while school meal programs are widely valued, their effectiveness depends on consistency, quality, and proper implementation.

2) Community-led nutritional support services

Some participants identified community-led nutrition support programs as valuable in improving children’s diets, although these initiatives are not yet widespread or equally accessible across schools. They emphasized the importance of local organizations working together and respecting cultural traditions to encourage healthier eating habits.

“Training local vendors in healthy cooking using fresh ingredients can significantly improve the quality of meals offered.” (FGD3, 28-yr-old male, merchant)

Other participants noted that such programs are not yet available in many communities and called for more resources and support to increase their availability and impact.

Policy and enabling environment-level facilitators

The policies and enabling environment-level facilitators in this study include 1) policy support for healthy eating and 2) health promotion and public nutrition campaigns.

1) Policy support for healthy eating

Most participants emphasized the importance of stronger policies on food labeling and packaging to ensure that families receive accurate nutritional information. They suggested training programs to increase awareness and empower families to make healthier choices.

“By providing food labels in languages that everyone can understand, families are able to make more informed decisions about what they’re feeding their children.” (FGD3, 38-yr-old male health officer)

Some participants noted concerns with policy enforcement, as many products have incorrect or incomplete labels, and some traditional items remain unlabeled. Others highlighted the need for policies that also regulate the advertising of unhealthy foods to children.

2) Health promotion and public health campaigns

Most participants highlighted the important role of public health campaigns promoting breastfeeding, exercise, healthy eating, school lunches, and maternal–child health programs. These initiatives help train caregivers on balanced meals, food hygiene, and safe cooking, improving children’s nutrition and academic performance.

“Healthy eating campaigns can empower families to make better food choices by raising awareness about nutrition’s impact on health.” (FGD2, 37-yr-old female, housewife)

Some participants noted that while health campaigns exist in Addis Ababa, they often overlook nutrition, leaving low-income communities with limited awareness and guidance on essential dietary issues. Others stressed the need for ongoing support and follow-up training to ensure lasting improvements in nutrition and hygiene practices.

DISCUSSION

This study revealed that inadequate and unhealthy dietary practices, including skipping meals, reducing children’s nutritional intake and contributing to academic difficulties such as poor attendance, reduced concentration, and a greater risk of repeating grades or dropping out. In Addis Ababa, where economic constraints and limited access to school feeding programs are common, these issues appear to be especially pronounced. This finding aligns with prior research indicating that meal skipping adversely affects children’s health and learning outcomes [41,42].

In this study, a lack of a balanced diet, limited nutritional awareness, and high consumption of processed and fast foods were identified as major nutritional challenges for school-aged children. The issue is particularly acute in Addis Ababa because of the widespread availability of cheap, unhealthy street food near schools, limited parental time for meal preparation, and low levels of food literacy among both children and caregivers [43]. These patterns mirror findings from other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where similar dietary habits are becoming increasingly common due to rapid urbanization and the growing influence of Western-style food marketing [44]. Studies from LMICs also highlight that inadequate dietary intake in school-aged children contributes to poor academic performance, stunted growth, and rising cases of both undernutrition and overweight [45].

Additionally, evidence shows that improving food literacy in children is critical for shaping lifelong dietary habits [46], yet such efforts are limited in informal settlements such as those in Addis Ababa. Risky food behaviors and physical inactivity, both of which are increasing in many urban settings, have also been linked to long-term health consequences [47].

The study also revealed that parental knowledge plays an important role in shaping children's consumption of ultra-processed foods, which are known to pose various health risks. In Addis Ababa, this issue may be especially pronounced compared with other urban settings in LMICs. Many parents in cities face unique challenges, including limited access to nutrition education, long and inflexible working hours, and economic pressures. These conditions often lead to greater reliance on inexpensive, ready-to-eat foods that are widely sold throughout the city, particularly near schools. Similar patterns have been observed in other LMICs, where limited parental knowledge and constrained resources contribute to poor dietary choices and increased consumption of unhealthy foods [48,49,50]. The easy availability of these foods, combined with limited affordable healthy alternatives, makes it even more difficult for families to make informed and nutritious food choices.

In addition, the study revealed that poor family relationships, including weak communication and lack of support, were linked to unhealthy eating habits among school-age children. These findings are consistent with other research in urban settings of LMICs, where strained family dynamics often lead to poor dietary choices [51]. In Addis Ababa, the situation may be worsened by factors such as economic stress and overcrowded households, which place additional pressure on family interactions and reduce the capacity of caregivers to provide consistent nutritional guidance and support.

Moreover, peer pressure and the lack of effective policies to regulate food marketing to children appear to have a significant influence in this context, and this finding supports earlier research highlighting the powerful role of peers in shaping unhealthy eating preferences, even in rural and low-income settings [52]. Additionally, a study in Addis Ababa reported that adolescents’ food choices are more strongly influenced by peer norms and perceived food safety than by the density of street vendors, despite the widespread availability of unhealthy foods near schools [53]. Similarly, findings from LMICs such as Greater Tunis show a clustering of unhealthy food outlets and marketing around schools, reinforcing poor dietary habits through both environmental exposure and social pressures [54]. While some high-income countries and a few LMICs have introduced mandatory restrictions such as front-of-package warning labels, bans on advertising unhealthy foods to children, and restrictions on school food sales, many countries, including Ethiopia, still rely on voluntary guidelines or have no regulatory framework in place [55].

This study revealed that economic and time constraints are significant barriers to the nutritional and dietary intake of school-age children in the urban low-income setting of Addis Ababa. Specifically, the findings indicated that high food costs and seasonal price fluctuations severely limit both the affordability and accessibility of nutritious foods. These challenges are particularly acute in Addis Ababa, where many families rely on informal employment with irregular income, making it difficult to budget for consistent food purchases [56]. Additionally, the rapid pace of urbanization has led to the expansion of informal food markets and the widespread availability of cheap, ultra-processed street foods, which are often more accessible and appealing to children than are healthier alternatives [57]. As a result, families frequently struggle to monitor their children’s diets or prepare balanced meals, especially due to extended working hours and limited household resources, conditions that are intensified by the city’s high cost of living and weak enforcement of nutrition-related policies such as the Food-Based Dietary Guidelines (FBDGs) introduced by the Ministry of Health in 2022 [58]. These findings align with broader research from other LMICs, which shows that economic hardship, food insecurity, and limited policy implementation contribute significantly to poor dietary patterns and nutritional outcomes among children [59,60].

The findings of this study indicate that poor infrastructure and limited community support further hinder access to nutrient-dense foods for households in low-income settings. This issue is largely driven by the absence of essential services and facilities, including health clinics, food aid programs, and centers for nutrition education. In Addis Ababa, densely populated neighborhoods lack nearby health clinics, food assistance programs, or nutrition education centers, making it harder for families to obtain the support needed to improve dietary practices [52,60,61]. Compared with other LMIC urban settings, the challenges in Addis Ababa are intensified by a fragmented urban planning system and limited coordination between the health, education, and social protection sectors, which further weakens the delivery of nutrition-related services [62,63].

The study reveals that food prices in Addis Ababa are not effectively regulated by existing laws or policies. A key issue is the unregulated role of intermediaries such as brokers and merchants, which enables price manipulation and contributes to volatile food costs. This challenge is particularly pronounced in Addis Ababa because of the widespread presence of informal food markets and limited governmental oversight. Unlike some other urban areas in LMICs where food supply chains are more structured, Addis Ababa’s fragmented distribution system makes it difficult to monitor pricing and uphold fairness. As a result, many households, especially those in low-income neighborhoods, struggle to access affordable and nutritious food, particularly during periods of inflation or supply disruptions [41,64,65].

According to the findings of this study, multiple facilitators, such as physical activity and nutrition, were identified as key factors in promoting the nutritional and dietary intake of school-age children in the urban low-income setting of Addis Ababa. The participants emphasized that regular exercise supports a healthy diet, enhances mental well-being, and helps maintain a healthy weight by reducing symptoms of anxiety and depression. In Addis Ababa, where formal mental health services and recreational facilities are limited, physical activity provides a critical outlet for children to improve both their physical health and their psychological health. These findings align with previous research [66], which highlights the positive role of physical activity in improving both physical and psychological health. From the perspective of LMICs, where access to formal healthcare and mental health services is often limited, facilitators such as physical activity and good nutrition are especially important in Addis Ababa. They provide accessible and cost-effective strategies to improve overall well-being and prevent noncommunicable diseases among urban populations facing resource constraints.

In this study, support from family members and access to nutrition education emerged as important interpersonal facilitators. Family encouragement plays a vital role in reinforcing healthy eating habits, especially for children in low-income households in Addis Ababa, who are frequently exposed to processed and street foods. In urban areas where families face economic pressure and limited time for meal preparation, positive reinforcement from caregivers can significantly influence children’s dietary choices. Additionally, digital platforms such as television, Facebook, and TikTok have become increasingly important sources of nutrition information for both parents and children. These platforms offer accessible and low-cost ways to increase awareness and promote healthy behaviors, especially in settings where formal nutrition education is limited. This finding aligns with those of previous studies highlighting the value of family influence and digital tools in promoting nutrition awareness in LMICs [67,68,69].

Moreover, in this study, cultural influences, access to nutritious food, and community engagement played significant roles in shaping children’s dietary practices. In Addis Ababa, major religious and cultural festivals such as Meskel, Genna, Fasika, and Timket provide opportunities for children to access a variety of traditional, nutrient-rich foods that are often not part of their everyday meals [70]. These events not only improve dietary diversity but also help strengthen food-related cultural identity and family involvement. In school settings, peer interactions during shared meals and snacks also influence children's eating behaviors through social modeling and informal learning [71].

Community engagement was identified as a key facilitator, with participants emphasizing the importance of collective knowledge sharing and the development of locally appropriate solutions to improve dietary practices. In Addis Ababa, community involvement supports families in addressing challenges such as food insecurity and limited nutrition awareness, particularly in low-income neighborhoods. Initiatives such as the PSNP demonstrate the role of organized efforts in supporting household nutrition and well-being. Similar forms of community-based engagement are found in other urban areas across LMICs, where local participation helps strengthen food systems and promote healthier eating habits [72]. These findings suggest that expanding community-led programs and culturally responsive approaches can contribute meaningfully to sustaining healthy diets in Addis Ababa and similar settings [73].

According to this research, school-based nutrition programs were identified as key organizational facilitators, offering children regular meals that support their health, increase school attendance, and enhance cognitive function. In Addis Ababa, where many families face economic hardship and food insecurity, such programs are especially valuable. They not only help reduce dropout rates but also contribute to improved concentration and academic performance among students from low-income households. These findings are consistent with evidence from other LMICs, where school feeding initiatives have been shown to play a critical role in supporting child development and educational outcomes [6,74].

This study also revealed that policy support plays a crucial role in promoting healthy eating. In Ethiopia, the Ministry of Health introduced the FBDGs in 2022 as part of the national efforts to address malnutrition [40]. While this is a positive step, implementation has remained limited, especially in urban settings such as Addis Ababa. Feasibility challenges, limited public awareness, cultural factors, and weak enforcement of food safety laws have slowed progress. In many parts of the city, particularly in low-income neighborhoods, families have limited exposure to these guidelines, and informal food markets operate with little regulation. Similar difficulties in turning national policy into practical change have been observed in other LMICs, where multisectoral collaboration and strong enforcement mechanisms are essential. This finding aligns with previous research highlighting the importance of integrated, multisectoral approaches for improving children’s nutritional and health outcomes [75].

This study’s strength lies in its qualitative approach, which provides a deep understanding of the barriers to and facilitators of nutrition among school-aged children in urban low-income settings. The diverse perspectives of the FGD, IDI and KII participants enriched the findings, making them more relevant. Aligning with both local and international research further strengthens credibility. However, the qualitative nature limits generalizability, and participant biases such as social desirability and recall bias may have influenced the responses. Logistical constraints also affected the sample size, and the focus on immediate challenges may have overlooked long-term dietary impacts.

In conclusion, this study highlights the serious nutritional challenges faced by school-aged children in low-income areas of Addis Ababa. Many of these difficulties stem from unhealthy eating habits and a limited understanding of what a balanced diet looks like. Families often struggle with financial hardship, a lack of time, and limited knowledge about nutrition, making it harder to prioritize healthy meals. At the community level, rising food prices, poor infrastructure, and limited access to support services further complicate the situation. Moreover, schools and other organizations often lack the resources or accountability to respond effectively, and national policies around food pricing and nutrition education remain weak.

However, the study also points to hopeful solutions. Nutrition education, encouragement from family, school meal programs, and traditional food practices can all play a positive role in improving children’s diets, especially when this support is well coordinated and sustained.
