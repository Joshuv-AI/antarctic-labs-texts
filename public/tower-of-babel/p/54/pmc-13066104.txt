Challenges in establishing ambulatory surgery in a Moroccan general surgery department
Authors: Kenza Elouali, Hamza Sekkat, Mahdi Bahij, Abdellah Moufid, Ben lahsen Mohamed, Youssef Achour, Samira El Fakir, Youness Bakali, Mouna Mhamdi El Alaoui, Mohammed Raiss, Farid Sabbah, Abdelmalek Hrora
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmbulatory surgery has become widely used in high-income countries because it is safe, cost-effective, and optimizes hospital resources, yet its development remains limited in many low- and middle-income settings, including Morocco. This study evaluated the feasibility and outcomes of ambulatory digestive surgery in a Moroccan tertiary care hospital and sought to identify factors associated with failure of same-day discharge. Over one year, all adult patients scheduled for elective ambulatory digestive procedures were prospectively included, and their perioperative outcomes were analyzed. Among 595 procedures performed in 2022, 266 were initially planned as ambulatory, but 64 ultimately required unplanned overnight hospitalization, resulting in an effective ambulatory rate of 33.9%. Complication and readmission rates were low, and no 90-day mortality was observed, supporting the overall safety of this approach. However, ambulatory failure was frequently associated with advanced age, comorbidities, prior abdominal surgery, and, importantly, social factors such as illiteracy, lack of caregiver support, and distance from the hospital. These findings indicate that while ambulatory digestive surgery is feasible and safe in this setting, its broader implementation is strongly influenced by non-medical barriers. Strengthening perioperative education, reinforcing family involvement, and adapting organizational infrastructure are essential to expand ambulatory surgery in similar healthcare contexts.Keywords: Ambulatory surgery, Day surgery, Low- and middle-income countries, Morocco, Failure factors, Perioperative care, Health systemsSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Ambulatory surgery (AS), defined as scheduled surgical procedures conducted under appropriate anesthesia allowing same-day discharge without increased patient risk, has become a cornerstone of surgical care in high-income countries (HICs) due to its benefits in reducing nosocomial infections, thromboembolic events, and overall healthcare costs, while optimizing resource utilization and staff workflow1–4. In HICs, AS constitutes a significant proportion of surgical procedures, with rates ranging from 25% to 65%5–7.

In contrast, the implementation of AS in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) remains limited. A systematic review by Patel et al.8 highlighted the sparse literature on perioperative care pathways in LMICs, noting that most studies were single-center and focused on elective surgeries in tertiary hospitals. Barriers to implementation included cost constraints and lack of resources8. Furthermore, a systematic review assessing surgical volumes in LMICs found that the average number of surgeries performed was significantly below the Lancet Commission on Global Surgery’s benchmark of 5,000 procedures per 100,000 population annually, with an average of only 877 surgeries, indicating substantial unmet surgical needs9.

In Morocco, the adoption of ambulatory surgery remains at an early stage, with limited evidence regarding its feasibility and clinical outcomes. The absence of structured AS programs, dedicated facilities, and standardized perioperative pathways continues to present major organizational challenges10. In addition to these structural limitations, several non-medical factors, such as high rates of patient illiteracy, insufficient postoperative support, and geographic inaccessibility, further impede the effective implementation and expansion of AS within the national healthcare system11.

Given these challenges, there is a critical need to evaluate the feasibility of AS in Moroccan healthcare settings. This study aimed to assess the implementation of an ambulatory surgery program in a digestive surgery unit of a Moroccan tertiary hospital by evaluating patient outcomes, identifying factors contributing to ambulatory surgery failure, and proposing potential institutional improvements. We hypothesized that non-medical factors would be the primary contributors to failure in this context.

Methods

This observational study was conducted and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines12. It took place in the Digestive Surgery Department of a tertiary-level university hospital that serves as a national referral center, performing approximately 1,000 general surgery procedures annually. Operating within a resource-limited environment characterized by high patient flow, limited bed capacity, and prioritization of emergency and oncologic cases, the department faced significant logistical constraints that motivated the implementation of ambulatory surgery. During the study period, no dedicated ambulatory infrastructure, such as separate admission, recovery, or postoperative observation units, was available. The primary objective was to evaluate the outcomes of ambulatory digestive surgeries, while the secondary objective was to identify factors associated with the failure to complete ambulatory surgery in selected patients.

Study design

This was a prospective, observational study based on a systematically maintained institutional database for ambulatory surgery. No experimental intervention was performed, and no identifiable personal data were used beyond standard anonymization protocols.

Study setting and population

The study included all consecutive adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) scheduled for ambulatory digestive surgery in the general surgery department of our institution between January 1, 2022, and December 31, 2022. A total of 266 patients were selected from the 595 admissions during this period. All surgical procedures were performed by a team of six experienced digestive surgeons.

Inclusion criteria required that procedures be non-emergency, scheduled for completion in under one hour, and not involve digestive tract suturing. However, exceeding the planned operative time of one hour was not considered an exclusion criterion if the surgery remained within the ambulatory care framework. Patients were excluded only if they were under 18 years of age or not scheduled for digestive ambulatory surgery. All included patients were followed for a minimum of 90 days postoperatively.

Ambulatory surgery was considered successful when the patient was discharged on the same day of the procedure without requiring overnight hospitalization. Conversely, failure of ambulatory surgery was defined as any circumstance necessitating an unplanned overnight stay beyond the day of surgery. No missing data were encountered.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the cohort. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages (n, %), while continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), as appropriate. A univariate analysis was performed using the chi-square test. P-values of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using Jamovi.

During the study period, 266 patients were scheduled for an ambulatory surgery, with an average age of 49,3 years +/- 15,5 years. The sex ratio of patients operated on was 1,01 with a slight female predominance (50.4%) (134 women and 132 men). Nine patients (3.4%) had a history of cardiac pathology, 13 patients had a history of hypertension (4.9%), 6 patients were diabetic (2.3%) and 42 patients (15.8%) had a history of previous abdominal surgery. Residents of the region where the hospital is located represented the majority, with 201 patients (75.6%), compared with 65 patients (24.4%) from other regions. Parietal surgeries represented the most frequently treated pathology, accounting for 34.2% of cases (91 patients), followed closely by proctologic procedures at 33.8% (90 patients), and lithiasis-related surgeries at 29.7% (79 patients). A small proportion of patients (2.2%; 6 cases) underwent surgery for various other pathologies (one abdominal lipoma, one inguinal cutaneous cyst, one synovial cyst, two rectal polyps, and one case of hidradenitis suppurativa). Except for proctologic procedures, laparoscopy was the preferred surgical approach, with 70.3% of interventions (121 out of 172) performed laparoscopically. The overall conversion rate to open surgery was low, at 1.7% (2 out of 121).

The overall complication rate was 1.87% (5 patients), including two serious complications (0.74%). These comprised one case of postoperative atelectasis with desaturation requiring admission to the surgical intensive care unit, and one postoperative seroma following umbilical hernia repair that necessitated percutaneous drainage. The remaining complications consisted of postoperative chest pain, a surgical site infection, and an infection following anoplasty. The readmission rate was 1.12%, and no 90-day mortality was observed in this cohort.

In sixty four patients (24%) of those scheduled for ambulatory surgery, a prolonged hospital stay was necessary for those reasons: Thirty-eight patients (59,38%) lived outside the hospital area, Fifteen patients were illiterate (23,44%). A univariate analysis was performed to identify factors associated with ambulatory surgery failure. The mean age of patients in the successful ambulatory group was 48.2 ± 15.6 years, compared to 52.9 years in the failure group. This difference was statistically significant (p = 0.02). Ambulatory surgery failure occurred in 31.3% of female patients (42 out of 134), compared to 17.4% of male patients (23 out of 132), indicating a significant association between female sex and ambulatory failure (p = 0.008).

Patients with a history of comorbidities experienced a significantly higher failure rate (40.2%; 37 out of 92) than those without such histories (16.1%; 28 out of 174) (p = 0.001).

Cardiovascular comorbidities, including hypertension, were particularly associated with poor outcomes: 77.8% of patients with cardiac history (7 out of 9) failed ambulatory surgery versus 22.6% (58 out of 257) in the non-cardiac group (p = 0.001). Similarly, patients with diabetes had a failure rate of 66.7% (4 out of 6) compared to 23.5% (61 out of 260) among non-diabetic patients (p = 0.01). A prior surgical history was associated with a significantly higher failure rate (47.6%; 20 out of 42) compared to those without prior surgeries (20.1%; 45 out of 224) (p = 0.001). Performance status was also relevant: patients with a performance score (PS) of 2 or 3 had a 50% failure rate (2 out of 4), versus 25.0% (63 out of 252) among those with a score of 0 or 1 (p = 0.006).

Regarding the surgical approach, ambulatory surgery failure occurred in 33.3% of patients who underwent an open procedure (17 out of 51), compared to 29.4% of those who had a laparoscopic approach (35 out of 119). This difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.07).

Finally, among patients undergoing abdominal wall surgery (n = 91), the failure rate was higher: 35.2% (32 cases). However, this association did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05).

Discussion

Advances in anesthetic techniques have been pivotal to the expansion and safety of ambulatory surgery. The development of short-acting anesthetic and analgesic agents, improved airway management, and the use of multimodal analgesia have enabled faster recovery and reduced postoperative complications13. Furthermore, the implementation of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery principles, in the 1990 s, emphasizing opioid-sparing anesthesia, regional blocks, and early mobilization, has markedly improved perioperative outcomes and patient satisfaction, and also broadened the spectrum of procedures amenable to same-day discharge, extending AS candidacy to patients with significant comorbidities previously deemed unsuitable for outpatient care8,14.

Recent analyses show a steady rise in the adoption of ambulatory surgery across OECD countries, particularly for high-volume procedures15. In France, national data indicate that 43% of digestive surgeries were performed on an ambulatory basis in 2021, reflecting a significant increase over the past decade16. In the United States, data shows that more than 65% of surgical procedures occur in ambulatory surgery centers (ASC), a 2.1% increase compared with 20227. Conversely, in South Asia, ambulatory surgery accounts for less than 20% of surgical interventions17,18.

Despite the global expansion of ambulatory surgery, its implementation across African countries remains limited and inconsistently reported19. In Morocco, available data are scarce. A national multicentric study reported that although 43% of colonic resections for cancer were performed laparoscopically, ambulatory management was rarely documented, indicating minimal integration of AS practices in digestive surgery20. Similarly, an audit conducted at Hassan II University Hospital in Fès between 2009 and 2012 showed that among 2,171 patients undergoing hernial and proctologic procedures, only 270 (12.24%) were treated on an ambulatory basis, underscoring the limited adoption of same-day discharge despite its proven feasibility21.

In 2022, 266 of the 595 procedures performed in our department (44.7%) were initially scheduled as ambulatory surgeries. However, with 64 cases ultimately failing to achieve ambulatory management, the effective rate of ambulatory surgery decreased to 33.9%. Although this proportion remains modest, it is still higher than previously reported Moroccan figures and exceeds those from other Sub-Saharan African studies. In Burkina Faso, for instance, a feasibility study conducted at a pediatric university hospital found that 103 out of 515 elective cases (20%) were successfully managed as ambulatory procedures22. The highest reported rate to date comes from Uganda, where the Kyabirwa Surgical Center, the country’s first standalone ambulatory surgery facility, achieved 64.6% of the 1,355 procedures performed between 2019 and 2021 as ambulatory general surgeries23.

The slow development of ambulatory surgery in Africa, and particularly in Morocco, is attributable to a combination of structural, financial, and cultural barriers. Inadequate government funding, an underdeveloped reimbursement system, and a shortage of beds contribute to this delayed expansion24. Systemic challenges also play a role: the absence of dedicated AS units prevents effective separation of outpatient and inpatient circuits20; operating room scarcity and prioritization of emergency and oncologic cases restrict AS availability22,23; and the lack of trained personnel in outpatient perioperative management further limits growth8. The absence of standardized national guidelines and perioperative pathways contributes to inconsistent practices and reduced confidence in same-day discharge8. Weak postoperative follow-up systems, limited integration with primary care, and poor accessibility in rural areas further complicate safe ambulatory care22,23.

Social determinants represent an additional barrier. Illiteracy and low health literacy affect patient comprehension of perioperative instructions, increasing risks of inadequate postoperative management and readmission22. Many patients lack reliable family or social support to manage care at home post-discharge, which is essential for safe outpatient recovery20. Geographic inaccessibility is also a major barrier; patients from remote areas often face long travel distances and lack safe, timely transportation, which discourages same-day discharge25. Finally, financial factors further impact AS adoption: inadequate institutional incentives reduce hospital willingness to prioritize ambulatory pathways26, while patients face indirect costs such as transportation expenses and income loss, making same-day discharge less feasible23.

Cultural perceptions also influence AS acceptance. Many patients and families associate prolonged hospitalization with better care, perceiving outpatient surgery as less safe22. Studies show that improved patient education and shared decision-making can mitigate these concerns and enhance acceptance of AS models27. Low functional health literacy has been associated with poorer postoperative recovery in day-surgery settings, underscoring the need for tailored counseling28. Shared decision-making consistently reduces anxiety and decisional conflict while enhancing patient satisfaction and trust—factors that support acceptance of early discharge29. It may also address surgeon hesitation, particularly fears of medico-legal complications after discharge8.

Despite these challenges associated with establishing AS programs, the broader ambulatory surgery center market in the Middle East and Africa is experiencing robust growth: valued at USD 2.68 billion in 2023 and projected to reach USD 3.99 billion by 2030, driven by enhanced healthcare access and technological innovation30. In South Africa alone, ASC revenues totaled USD 539.3 million in 2023, with orthopedics and otolaryngology leading outpatient surgical volumes. These trends underscore both the emerging promise of AS in African health systems and the imperative for comprehensive national data collection and standardized reporting mechanisms to guide its sustainable expansion30.

In our series, the feasibility and safety of AS were demonstrated by a low complication rate (1.87%) and a 90-day readmission rate of 1.12%, consistent with international data31–33. Most large studies report complication rates between 1 and 5% and unplanned readmissions below 2%. A prospective Canadian study by Fortier et al. found an unanticipated hospital admission rate of 1.1% following ambulatory procedures31. Similarly, Mezei and Chung reported a 1.2% readmission rate and 2.1% emergency visits within 30 days post-surgery in a cohort of more than 17,000 patients32. Similar results have been reproduced in more recent studies, where minor complications such as wound infections, urinary retention, and postoperative pain are the main causes of unplanned healthcare contact33.

Chao et al. demonstrated that laparoscopic surgery in low- and middle-income countries is safe, effective, and cost-efficient, but its broader adoption remains limited by scarce equipment, inadequate maintenance, and insufficiently trained personnel, resulting in uneven access across LMIC hospitals34. Similar challenges exist in Morocco, where the expansion of laparoscopy still requires strengthened training, certification, centralization, and standardization, and where procedures are largely concentrated among a small number of surgeons20. In our series, laparoscopy accounted for 70.3% of procedures, reflecting both the suitability of minimally invasive techniques for ambulatory pathways and the high laparoscopic activity of our department, which routinely treats high-risk patients. In a recent study from our center, 86.6% of major rectal cancer surgeries were performed laparoscopically, underscoring our unit’s established expertise in minimally invasive surgery35. This expertise, combined with the predominance of short, standardized procedures typical of ambulatory surgery, likely explains the high proportion of laparoscopic cases in our cohort. Our low conversion rate (1.7%) and low 90-day readmission rate align with international data, including a meta-analysis of over 1.5 million laparoscopic cholecystectomies reporting a 30-day readmission rate of 3.3%36, supporting laparoscopy as a safe and feasible component of ambulatory pathways in our setting.

The incidence of ambulatory care failure reported in English-speaking countries ranges from 0.9% to 9.4%37, which remains substantially lower than the 24% failure rate observed in our cohort. In these settings, failures are largely attributed to a combination of medical, surgical, and social factors leading to complications at discharge, unplanned rehospitalization, or cancellation of the outpatient procedure33,37,38. Reported determinants of delayed discharge include female sex, advanced age, heart failure, prolonged operative time (> 120 min), anesthesia technique, postoperative pain, and absence of caregiver support39. Interventions such as improved nursing protocols, adequate assistance, proactive pain management, and standardized discharge criteria have been recommended to reduce failures40,41. In contrast, the risk factors for prolonged hospitalization identified in our study were predominantly non-clinical and included the absence of a primary care provider for postoperative follow-up (e.g., for anticoagulant management), inadequate pain control, and social determinants such as lack of a caregiver, patient illiteracy, and transportation difficulties.

These findings highlight the distinct contextual barriers faced in our healthcare environment compared to those reported in high-income countries, emphasizing the need for strategies tailored to local realities. Our study provides important insights into the underlying causes and contributing factors to the limitations observed in the implementation of ambulatory surgery within the Moroccan healthcare system. To our knowledge, this work represents the first investigation specifically focused on identifying the barriers to deploying AS in our national context. A key strength of our study lies in its prospective design, which allowed for systematic and detailed collection of patient data over a defined period, thereby enhancing the reliability of our findings. Furthermore, we critically appraised existing literature and contextualized our results within the realities of the Moroccan healthcare infrastructure, benefiting from the input of experienced surgical and health systems experts. Our primary aim was to provide evidence-based insights that could guide healthcare practitioners in developing tailored patient management strategies adapted to local conditions.

Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. The absence of a standardized AS selection protocol meant that eligibility was based primarily on procedure type, without predefined social or demographic exclusion criteria. This pragmatic approach reflects the absence of structured AS pathways in our setting. Second, the single-center design limits generalizability to other institutions in Morocco or LMICs. Third, the lack of long-term follow-up prevents assessment of delayed complications or readmissions beyond 90 days. Future multicenter studies with larger samples and extended follow-up are needed to confirm and expand upon our findings.

​​Based on our findings and supported by international literature, the implementation of ambulatory surgery (AS) programs in low- and middle-income countries requires a multifaceted approach addressing systemic, social, and operational barriers. Comprehensive patient education is essential to improve health literacy, enhance adherence to perioperative protocols, and reduce postoperative complications; culturally adapted materials and community health worker involvement can help overcome literacy gaps25. Improving access also requires expanding infrastructure through dedicated AS units, satellite centers, and mobile surgical teams to increase capacity in underserved areas23. Financial support mechanisms—such as travel vouchers, conditional cash transfers, and incentives favoring outpatient over inpatient care—are crucial to reducing indirect costs and promoting AS adoption42. In parallel, the development of standardized, context-adapted perioperative pathways and performance indicators can ensure consistent care quality and strengthen provider confidence8. Finally, partnerships with professional societies and local stakeholders are key to supporting provider training, building community trust, and aligning technical resources with evolving clinical standards26.

By addressing these multifaceted challenges through a comprehensive and culturally informed approach, LMICs can enhance the effectiveness and reach of ambulatory surgery programs, ultimately improving surgical care access and outcomes for their populations.

Conclusion

Our study confirms that ambulatory surgery is both clinically feasible and generally safe within our Moroccan tertiary care setting, supported by low complication and readmission rates. However, the program’s broader success remains significantly constrained by systemic, logistical, and social barriers, as evidenced by the fact that nearly one-quarter of patients (24%) could not complete their surgical procedure in an ambulatory setting. The principal challenges identified relate not to medical contraindications but to social determinants of health, including patient illiteracy, lack of caregiver support, and geographic inaccessibility.
