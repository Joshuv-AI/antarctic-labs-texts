Molecular-level freezing and melting dynamics of aqueous lithium chloride solutions using in-situ Raman spectroscopy
Authors: Hyeonji Park, Andrew Wang, Juwon Kim, Amol Pophali, Dilip Gersappe, Taejin Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLithium chloride (LiCl) aqueous solutions are widely used in chemical, biological, and industrial applications due to their high solubility, hygroscopicity, and antifreeze properties. This study investigates how Li+ and Cl− ions influence the structural and dynamic behavior of water molecules during freezing and melting processes. In-situ Raman spectroscopy was employed to analyze these solutions with varied concentrations and temperature ramping rates. Vibrational features, including OH stretching, HOH bending, and low-frequency lattice modes, were monitored to elucidate ion-induced changes. For the 1.5 M LiCl solution, a visual indicator, methylene blue, was used to track freezing and melting transitions. A unique behavior of LiCl, characterized by chain-like aggregation, was observed at low temperatures in the liquid phase, where increasing ion concentration with decreasing temperature coincided with the disappearance of the HOH bending band. The spectral shifts correlated with increased salt concentration, confirming freezing point depression and two-step structural transformations. This work deepens the understanding of ion-specific effects on the vibrational behavior and phase transitions of water.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-61621-4.Keywords: Lithium chloride, Water, In-situ Raman spectroscopy, Phase transition temperaturesSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science, Physics

Introduction

Lithium chloride (LiCl) aqueous solutions are electrolyte solutions that are useful in both chemical and biological applications. For example, LiCl can increase water permeability in hydrophobic polymers because it is highly hydrophilic1. It also acts as a biological activator of the canonical Wnt signaling pathway, which promotes dentin sialophosphoprotein (DSPP) mRNA expression and is therefore used in dentin repair2. LiCl solutions are 1:1 electrolytes with high solubility in water, and the lithium ion (Li+) has a high charge density. These aqueous alkali halide systems are typically implemented in the production of lithium-ion batteries. LiCl is also used for dehumidification and in desiccant solutions as it is less corrosive and less volatile than other salts, meaning it has a lower vapor partial pressure, which is beneficial for regeneration processes3. Lithium salts also exhibit concentration-dependent antifreezing effects. LiCl is often added to hydrogels or other chemical components to improve performance in low-temperature environments. Several studies have demonstrated the antifreezing effect of LiCl by incorporating it into hydrogels such as a poly(SBMA-co-HEAA), poly(vinyl alcohol), tannic acid, polyacrylamide, and polyacrylamide/carboxymethyl cellulose hydrogels to lower their freezing points4–6. LiCl solutions were also responsible for the dehydration effects of hydrogels7 and enhancing the ionic conductivity of conductive hydrogels8,9.

LiCl solutions have been characterized and studied for their unique characteristics to disrupt hydrogen bonds and form ionic confinement. X-ray Raman scattering (XRS) revealed that increasing LiCl concentration gradually disrupted the hydrogen-bond network of water, with LiCl ions primarily affecting hydrogen bonds within their first hydration shell10. Raman scattering measurements have shown that LiCl solutions resembled bulk water at high temperatures, but at low temperatures their structure diverged as ionic confinement suppressed the natural clustering of water molecules11. Based on these findings, one study reported that increasing the LiCl concentration decreased the hydrogen atoms (H–H) intermolecular distance and distorted the hydration shell around Li⁺ ions, whereas it remained unaffected with the Cl−. It has also been previously reported that LiCl did not form ion pairs even at the highest concentration at room temperature12. However, it was found that although LiCl showed weaker ion pairing and clustering than other lithium-based salts such as Li2SO4, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations suggested that Cl− can be present in the first hydration shell of Li+13. Interestingly, ion pairing becomes a dominant feature of electrolyte solutions at lower temperature conditions14. It has been reported that increasing salt concentration promoted anion-cation interactions and ion pair formation, which can weaken the hydrogen bond disruption through colligative properties. Thus, at low temperatures, ion pairing can hinder ions from disrupting hydrogen bonds. A study has also stated that the antifreezing behaviors of lithium salt solutions depended on the mobility of water molecules within the ions’ hydration shells and solely on the anion identity15. Since each study offers a different explanation of ion behavior in relation to antifreezing properties, it is important to investigate the antifreezing effects of LiCl at various concentrations and low temperatures.

The thermodynamics and structural dynamics of LiCl-water system under supercooled conditions have shown to be strongly affected by the concentration of the LiCl with involved water of hydration16. The Li-water radial distribution shown to be more affected by the high- and low-density water phases than the Cl-water, with well-defined water shell surrounding them even on phase transition16. To understand the effects on the phase transition under freezing or thawing process, Monnin et al. provided a phase diagram of the LiCl+H2O system clarifying the structural differences17. The developed thermodynamic phase diagrams and empirical models could effectively explain the eutectic and peritectic behavior of the system. However, the concentrations required to attain these phase transitions are substantially higher than those examined in the present work. In an investigation by Dubois et al., Raman spectroscopy was effectively used to showcase the LiCl-hydrates, hydrohalite and eutectic points during heating, but understanding the phase transition phenomenon of water solely by these could lead to complexity18. In addition, formation of each LiCl-hydrate could be function of the cooling/heating rates and salt concentration, and the peak assignment could be a debatable factor.

In this study, we applied in-situ Raman spectroscopy to investigate the freezing and melting behavior of LiCl solutions. These results demonstrate that LiCl concentration and ramping rate critically influence its freezing and melting behavior, providing mechanistic insight into its antifreezing properties. In addition, visualization tests using methylene blue (MB) as an indicator, considering MB aggregation at higher concentrations, further support this understanding. These results elucidate how LiCl modifies hydrogen bonding to impart antifreezing behavior, advancing insight into its role in relevant applications.

Results and discussion

Effects of LiCl concentration on their freezing and melting behavior

Raman spectroscopy was employed to analyze the molecular structure of water in LiCl solution. Three relevant regions have been identified: (1) intermolecular lattice vibrations below 400 cm− 1 which associate with the hydrogen bond network, (2) the HOH bending peak at ~ 1640 cm− 1, (3) OH stretching peaks between ~ 2800 and ~ 4000 cm− 1, comprising (i) symmetric stretching (2800–3300 cm− 1) and (ii) asymmetric stretching (3300–3900 cm− 1)19–21. Variations in these peaks will be analyzed to determine the phase transition temperatures of LiCl solutions as a function of concentration and ramping rate.

Figure 1 shows that across all three concentrations (0.1, 1.5, and 3.0 M), the OH stretching band exhibits low intensity on the left shoulder (Isym) and high intensity on the right shoulder (Iasym) at room temperature. In the liquid state, water molecules form the hydration shell around Li⁺ and Cl⁻ ions, enhancing asymmetric OH stretching. Analysis of the Raman spectra provided two distinct freezing phase transition temperatures for the 1.5 M and 3.0 M concentrations (Fig. 1b, c). The first phase transition temperature in freezing was identified by the emergence of a peak near 217 cm− 1 and changes in the symmetric OH stretching region, indicating the onset of ice formation. Below the first transition temperature under freezing, the HOH bending peak progressively decreased in intensity with further cooling, and the second phase transition temperature was defined by the temperature at which the HOH bending peak disappeared. In contrast, the 0.1 M LiCl solution exhibited features of both transitions simultaneously at the first phase transition temperature, which is similar to pure water (Fig. S1). Regardless of concentrations, the symmetric OH stretching peak undergoes a gradual redshift upon cooling below the first phase transition temperature (Fig. S2), where it reflects a densification of the ice lattice under these conditions22. Table 1 summarizes the first and second phase transition temperatures in freezing of the three LiCl solutions. The freezing point decreases with increasing LiCl concentration at both first and second phase transition temperatures. This observation aligns with previous studies, confirming that LiCl induces antifreezing behavior by weakening the hydrogen bond network in water21.

The spectral features obtained from in-situ Raman spectroscopy provide a precise molecular fingerprint of the freezing behavior of LiCl solutions, linking the observed phase transitions to concentration-dependent changes in the ice environment and LiCl-rich domains. The first phase transition in freezing happens when water freezes into ice crystals, while LiCl concentration increases in the liquid part which can be called a freeze-concentrated LiCl-rich domain as shown in Fig. 2 (a) and (b). The interpretation of this low-temperature region can be considered in the context of the established H2O-LiCl phase diagram, which includes eutectic behavior and possible hydrated LiCl phases under sufficiently concentrated low-temperature conditions16–18. However, our results did not show the emergence of new hydrate-specific peaks or a clear shift of the HOH bending band between the first and second phase transition temperatures in freezing. Instead, the intensity of the HOH bending band gradually decreased in intensity and eventually disappeared. This behavior suggests that, as freezable water is progressively incorporated into the ice phase, the remaining water molecules associated with the LiCl-rich domains become increasingly bound, confined, or immobilized through strong ion-ion interactions, rather than forming a distinct Raman-detectable crystalline hydrate phase under our experimental conditions. Prior studies have reported that the local coordination environment around Li+ changes with increasing LiCl concentration23 and that enhanced Li+-Cl− correlations, including ion-pairing or chain-like ion-associated structures, can develop in concentrated LiCl solutions24,25. Thus, we interpret the gradual loss of HOH bending signal as being consistent with the development of highly concentrated, ion-aggregated LiCl-water domains in which the mobility and Raman-detectable population of liquid-like water are progressively reduced (Fig. 2c).

Overall, as the water freezes and ice crystals form, Li⁺ and Cl⁻ ions congregate in the liquid part, leading them to form an ionic network. This result is consistent with reports that Li-Cl forms a chain-like structure at high concentrations at room temperature24,25. Zhang et al. reported that Li-Cl forms a chain-like structure above 8 mol/kg (molal) and confirmed that Li⁺ and Cl⁻ ions form ion-ion structures as concentration increases by calculating the respective coefficient for the ions and water molecules24. As the LiCl concentration increases, Li(H2O)2+ and Li(H2O)3+ populations are more dominant than Li(H2O)4+. Singh et al. reported molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of LiCl solutions at varying concentrations, showing that the number of Li+-Cl− ion pairs increases (Li+-Cl−-Li+-Cl−-Li+)25. The Raman results validated the distinctive ion-water interactions of LiCl in solution, demonstrating their direct role in governing its freezing behavior. The initial freezing point depression arises primarily from the presence of dissolved ions, while the subsequent development of freeze-concentrated, ion-associated LiCl-rich domains further restricts the mobility of residual water molecules and contributes to the suppression of complete ice crystallization at lower temperatures.

The melting behavior of the three LiCl concentrations followed a reversible pathway relative to freezing, as shown in Fig. 3. Specifically, (i) the HOH bending peak intensity gradually increased from the first phase transition temperature, and (ii) the lattice vibration peak and symmetric OH stretching peak disappeared at the second transition temperature in melting. As in freezing, two distinct phase transition temperatures were observed only for the 1.5 M and 3.0 M solutions, while the 0.1 M solution exhibited a single transition. Furthermore, as summarized in Table 1, higher LiCl concentrations resulted in a larger decrease in melting temperatures, consistent with the concentration-dependent trends observed during freezing. During melting, the water molecules progressively replace Cl− ions in the LiCl-aggregated liquid, leading to the reorganization of water molecules within the Li+ solvation shells. The observed reversibility of freezing and melting indicates that LiCl influences water structure through reversible hydrogen-bond reorganization, without inducing permanent structural changes. This reversibility underscores the reliability of LiCl solutions under freezing-melting cycling, highlighting their potential relevance for antifreeze design and cold-environment applications.

Effects of ramping rate on freezing and melting behavior of 1.5 M LiCl solution

The influence of cooling rate on the freezing behavior of aqueous solutions has been widely investigated in earlier studies26–28. Freezing involves the nucleation of ice crystals, a process governed by kinetics rather than solely by thermodynamics. It was reported that freezing was observed at higher temperatures with a lower cooling rate, due to an increase in the probability of ice nucleation26. Bogdanova et al. reported that the cooling rate affected the onset time of crystallization, whereas the overall crystallization time showed only a slight dependence on cooling rate28. The authors concluded that once an ice nucleus was formed, crystallization proceeded rapidly. In contrast to this conclusion, Gokhale observed that the freezing temperature can be further depressed depending on the specific cooling rate27. Using pure water, the author observed that cooling rates less than 5 °C/min depressed the freezing temperature, whereas higher rates above 5 °C/min show the opposite trend. Unlike the freezing temperature, recent studies have shown that the melting temperature is independent of the heating rate29. However, published studies on the effect of cooling rate have primarily focused on pure water, whereas experiments using salt solutions (e.g., LiCl) are relatively uncommon.

Figure 4(a-c) shows the freezing behavior of 1.5 M LiCl solution at cooling rates of 0.5, 5, and 100 °C/min down to -80 °C, respectively. Under the current experimental conditions, the first phase transition temperature of the 1.5 M LiCl solution (-20 °C ~ -30 °C) was not affected by cooling rates. The ice densification process was also consistently observed regardless of cooling rate, as evidenced by the redshift of the symmetric OH stretching peak with decreasing temperature (Fig. S3). The results suggest that freezing in these systems is governed mainly by thermodynamic nucleation conditions, specifically the equilibrium freezing point depression and the ice nucleation energy barrier, which are only weakly affected by moderate changes in the ramping rate. Figure 4(d-f) shows the effect of the ramping rate on the melting process, which reveals that the ramping rate also has less impact on the melting point of 1.5 M LiCl solution under our experimental condition. All three ramping rates showed the melting point at -10 °C by observing changes in the OH stretching peak. In short, our experiment conditions for in-situ Raman spectroscopy conclude that the depression in freezing and melting points is mainly affected by the ion presence in water, less by the ramping rate.

Visualization of the freezing and melting behavior of water and 1.5 M LiCl solution by applying methylene blue as an indicator

Methylene blue (MB) exhibits unique solution behavior depending on its concentration and solvent environment. MB molecules can exist either as individual monomers or as aggregated species30,31. Their distinct absorption and scattering characteristics make MB particularly useful for visually distinguishing between the liquid and solid states of aqueous solutions through color change. In frozen aqueous systems, MB molecules can be excluded from the growing ice lattice and locally enriched in unfrozen liquid regions or ice grain boundaries, where aggregation can lead to purplish coloration. Therefore, MB was employed in this study as a complementary visual probe to compare the freezing and melting behavior of water and 1.5 M LiCl solution.

During freezing, the aqueous MB solution displayed a progressive color change, with a purple coloration initially appearing at the vial bottom and edges, then propagating upward as ice formation progressed (Fig. 5(a)). This color change is attributed to the aggregation of MB molecules at ice grain boundaries. Considering the freezing behavior, it can be inferred that during ice formation, water molecules expel MB molecules from the growing ice lattice. Upon a complete freezing, the solution exhibited a uniform purple appearance within two minutes. When allowed to melt at room temperature, the sample near the exterior walls of the vial gradually transitioned from purple to blue as melting progressed. As shown in Fig. 5(b), at 20 min, melting initiated from the bottom, where the ice first converted to liquid water. As time passed, the remaining ice progressively rose toward the surface, and at 50 min, only a small fraction of residual white ice remained. These observations indirectly suggest that MB molecules are largely excluded from the ice phase, implying that the ice predominantly contains water molecules. In contrast, the 1.5 M LiCl solution containing MB developed a weaker purple coloration during freezing, retaining a more bluish appearance compared with the DI water sample (Fig. 5(c)). For instance, even after 180 s, the LiCl solution remained predominantly blue, whereas the DI water sample had already developed a distinct purple color at 90 s. While similar edge-localized color features were observed in the LiCl solution, the overall intensity of MB aggregation was reduced in the LiCl solution, consistent with ion-induced suppression of ice grain boundary formation. During melting, the LiCl solution transitioned from light purple to blue, and compared with DI water, a faster melting rate was observed. Notably, no floating ice was visible at the surface, indirectly suggesting that the kinetics of the ice-to-liquid transition were relatively accelerated in the presence of LiCl (Fig. 5d).

Visualization of (a) freezing behavior and (b) melting behavior of MB+water solution (MB 8 mg/L), and (c) freezing behavior and (d) melting behavior of MB + 1.5 M LiCl solution (MB 8 mg/L). When freezing, the vial sample container was dipped into the liquid nitrogen and photos were taken per 30 s. In melting, the vial sample was set at room temperature, and photos were taken per 5–10 min.

The freezing and melting behaviors of LiCl solutions with MB were further examined using a temperature-controlled stage, which allowed for detailed observation of color changes due to the small sample volume and precise temperature control. As shown in Fig. 6(a), the DI water sample showed a noticeable color change beginning between − 10 °C and − 20 °C, consistent with previous Raman spectroscopy studies20. At -20 °C, the solution appeared light blue, which gradually transitioned to a more intense purple color at -30 °C. During melting as shown in Fig. 6(b), the color change occurred over the range of -10 °C ~ 0 °C, with the blue coloration being most prominent in 0 °C ~ 10 °C. As shown in Fig. 6(c), the 1.5 M LiCl solution exhibited more distinct color transitions compared with the DI water sample. Two successive color phase changes were observed. The first transition from clear blue to light blue occurred between − 23.5 °C ~ -30 °C, and since the first transition, the second transition showed gradient color changes from light blue to purple from − 30 °C to -100 °C. In addition to the findings presented in Fig. 5, the results in Fig. 6(c) indicate that the liquid and solid phases coexist over a long period at low temperatures in the 1.5 M LiCl solution as compared to DI water. This gradient color changes also support the gradient decrease of HOH bending intensity, shown in Fig. 1(e). During melting, the LiCl solution sample showed a color transition from purple to blue in the temperature range of -50 °C to -40 °C. As the temperature increased further, the blue coloration gradually became lighter. These color changes during melting support the previously noted Raman spectroscopic results. The LiCl solution can show an apparently lower melting temperature in the observed color transition because the ionic interactions stabilize the remaining ice clusters at lower temperatures, effectively prolonging the solid-liquid coexistence and producing a more gradual melting process compared with DI water. In short, the phase transition temperatures observed in Fig. 6(c) and (d) were in good agreement with those shown in Figs. 1 and 3, respectively. These findings are fundamentally crucial, as they demonstrate that the presence of Li+ and Cl− ions delays the complete formation of ice, with MB-derived color variations providing a clear visual distinction between the freezing behaviors of water and LiCl solutions.

Raman spectroscopy of freezing and melting behavior of water and 1.5 M LiCl solution by applying MB as an indicator

In addition to the observed color variation from visualization experiment results shown in Figs. 5 and 6, in-situ Raman spectroscopy was employed to further characterize the freezing behaviors of (a) MB + Water and (b) MB + 1.5 M LiCl solutions. Previous studies have shown that certain dyes can interact with ice and at sufficiently high concentrations, inhibit ice growth32. To minimize perturbation during Raman measurements, MB was used at a low concentration of 0.04 mg/L, and the water-associated Raman features and transition temperatures were consistent with those of the corresponding MB-free water/LiCl solutions.

As shown in Fig. 7(a) and (b), an additional broad feature appears in the range of 3800 cm− 1 and 7000 cm− 1, which is assumed to be due to the fluorescence of MB. It is hypothesized that MB aggregation kinetics can be related to the intensity changes of Raman fluorescence band during freezing. In addition, it was confirmed that MB did not affect phase transition temperatures and Raman peak features derived from water molecules. For the MB+water sample, a broad and high-intensity peak is observed near 4100 cm− 1, at room temperature. The peak intensity decreases gradually down from room temperature to -20 °C, where the first phase transition temperature shows ice formation, then drops rapidly below − 20 °C (Fig. 7a). This behavior matches the temperature range observed in the visualization results (Fig. 6a). In the case of the 1.5 M LiCl solution, the peak intensity changes slowly from room temperature to the first phase transition temperature (-30 °C) and the intensity decreased from − 30 °C to -40 °C and all the way down to -120 °C (Fig. 7b), corroborating that LiCl ions delay complete ice formation and subsequent crystal growth. Recent work on methyl orange has further shown that organic dyes can exhibit freezing-induced optical changes, supporting the broader concept of dye-based probes for monitoring liquid-solid phase evolution33. However, because dye-based probes can depend on concentration, pH, or salt environment, we used only MB for our study. Future work using multiple dyes may help distinguish general freeze-concentration behavior from dye-specific optical or ice-interaction effects.

Conclusions

In-situ Raman spectroscopy was applied to systematically investigate the freezing and melting behavior of LiCl solutions with varied concentrations and ramping rates. The results revealed two distinct phase transition temperatures in both freezing and melting, captured through changes in three major spectral features of water molecules. In freezing, the first transition was indicated by the growth of the lattice vibration peak and the symmetric OH stretching peak, signaling ice crystallization, while the second was characterized by a gradual reduction of the HOH bending peak intensity, associated with the increasing local concentration and aggregation of LiCl ions. Thus, LiCl can exhibit a unique chain-like aggregation behavior during freezing, as inferred from the progressively concentrated liquid fraction and corresponding Raman spectral changes associated with ion clustering. Melting proceeded in the reverse order, underscoring the ion-specific modulation of water structure, as evidenced by the slower relaxation of Raman peaks in LiCl solutions compared with DI water. Higher LiCl concentrations consistently depressed the phase transition temperatures, whereas cooling rates were not the major factor to affect the phase transition temperature. Complementary visualization experiments using MB further corroborated these findings, as the dynamic color changes, due to liquid and solid phases, were more pronounced in LiCl solutions compared with DI water. Therefore, MB served as a visual indicator and a sensitive optical/Raman probe for monitoring phase transition behavior. Taken together, these results provide a molecular-level framework for fundamentally understanding how LiCl ions modify water structure and phase transitions, with broad implications for antifreezing strategies, energy storage, and environmental applications.

Materials and methods

Materials

Three different concentrations of lithium chloride (LiCl, Purity > 99%, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), including 0.1, 1.5, and 3.0 M, were prepared by using deionized (DI) water (Milli Q with 18.2 MΩ). Methylene blue (MB, Fluka Analytical, USA) was employed as an indicator for differentiating colors between liquid and solid. Its concentration was prepared at 8 mg/L for the visualization tests and 0.04 mg/L for the in-situ Raman spectroscopy experiments.

In-situ Raman spectroscopy

The changes in the molecular structure of the prepared salt solutions over a varied temperature range (room temperature (RT) to -140 °C) were investigated by Raman spectroscopy (visible Raman, 532 nm) using a HORIBA Xplora Plus Raman microscope (Horiba, USA) and a cryo-stage system (THMS600, Linkam Scientific Instrument Co. Ltd., UK). Liquid nitrogen was pumped from a dewar to the THMS600-PS stage to cool the system using an LNP pump at varied cooling rates (0.5, 5, 100 °C/min). The liquid sample (100 µl) was tested in a quartz crucible (15 mm diameter) mounted inside the cryo-stage. Melting experiments were conducted at the same rates as the freezing, reversibly from − 140 °C to room temperature. The operating parameters used to collect the final Raman spectrum for the samples at each temperature condition were kept consistent as follows: acquisition time of 10 s, accumulation of 5 scans, laser output power of 5.6 mW, and 1200 gr/mm grating. During Raman measurements, the laser was focused within the liquid or frozen sample rather than on the quartz crucible bottom or the Linkam cell window. Also, since the confocal Raman microscope was used, spectral contributions from cell or crucible were negligible in the spectral regions. All collected spectra were processed by using Origin software. The spectra were normalized from 0 to 1 based on the peak intensity and smoothed using the Savitzky-Golay method with a 2 of polynomial order and 8 of window size. The peak position was determined as the wave number corresponding to the maximum intensity within the known region.

Probing phase transitions in LiCl solutions via MB-enabled visualization

Concentrated MB solutions (8 mg/L) were prepared, and two solvent conditions (DI water and 1.5 M LiCl aqueous solutions) were used for the visualization experiments according to Fig. S4. For each condition, vials (capacity 20 mL) were filled with 10 mL of the solutions. The vials were then rapidly frozen by immersing in liquid nitrogen and photographed every 30 s of the freezing process. After freezing experiment, the vials were further transferred to room temperature and photographed every 5 ~ 10 min during the thawing process. The two samples were also tested and photographed in the THMS600 cryo-stage system, with temperatures recorded. In the cryo-stage system visualization experiments, a 250 µL solution was used to fill the volume of the sample holder (quartz crucible). For usage in the Raman spectroscopy experiment, since the broad peak from 8 mg/L of MB solution was too dominant compared to the peaks from water molecules, the MB concentration was decreased to 0.04 mg/L for a better analysis to monitor peak changes for both water molecules and MB with varying temperature (Fig. 7(a) and (b)). The in-situ Raman spectroscopy supports the visualization test by monitoring the peak changes derived from water and methylene blue.
