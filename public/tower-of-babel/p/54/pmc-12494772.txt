Nano-curcumin potentially ameliorates hormonal function and follicular counts following the vitrification and transplantation of rat ovarian tissue
Authors: Hendy Hendarto, Satria Audi Hutama, Relly Yanuari Primariawan, Firas Farisi Alkaff, Bambang Satrio Utomo, Widjiati Widjiati, Nao Suzuki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ovarian tissue cryopreservation followed by transplantation may damage the ovaries, potentially impeding fertility restoration. The notable antioxidant properties of curcumin highlight its potential as a therapeutic agent for enhancing fertility restoration. The use of nano-curcumin to improve ovarian tissue transplantation outcomes after vitrification for fertility preservation remains unknown. This study aimed to evaluate endocrine function, by measuring estradiol (E2), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), and ovarian follicle count in rats subjected to vitrification and transplantation. Thirty female rats were categorized into three groups (n = 10/group): control (Group 1), untreated (Group 2), and nano-curcumin treated (Group 3; 2 mg/kg/day for 14 days). The rats were euthanized, and blood samples and ovaries were collected for analysis. The FSH level in the treated group (0.99 (0.97-1.00) mIU/ml) was significantly lower compared to that in the other groups, with a significant difference, particularly against Group 2 (p < 0.05). E2 and AMH values were higher in the treated group, with increased primordial and total follicle counts, though not statistically significant (p = 0.069 and p = 0.051). The administration of nano-curcumin may improve hormonal function and increase the number of follicles in rat ovaries after vitrification and transplantation.

Introduction

Fertility preservation in patients with cancer is gaining significant attention in both research and clinical practice1–5. Cancer therapy has improved the survival rates of patients; however, the success of this therapy negatively impacts fertility, as chemotherapy and radiotherapy procedures are gonadotoxic, thereby damaging the ovarian follicles, which are the main functional unit of the female reproductive system3,6,7. Fertility preservation procedures have been developed to protect the reproductive organs from damage caused by gonadotoxic therapy. One of the essential options for fertility preservation is ovarian tissue cryopreservation (OTC) followed by ovarian tissue transplantation (OTT), which primarily focuses on preserving fertility for children, adolescents, and individuals who lack sufficient time to undergo alternative fertility preservation treatments8,9.

Dead primordial follicle cells were found after vitrification and transplantation, resulting in a decrease of up to 65% and disruption of fertility restoration10. Ischemia and hypoxia are commonly identified as significant barriers to successful OTT, making revascularization and apoptosis prevention crucial factors in this process11. Multiple methods have been employed to minimize these risks and improve the recovery of ovarian function. These include the administration of hormonal supplementation, antioxidants, cell therapy, and various other approaches6,12. The study by Zhao et al. found that curcumin, a traditional medicine that has been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), activates granulosa cell proliferation and differentiation, and oocyte growth through phosphatidylinositol 3 kinase (PI3K)13,14. In its nano-formulated form, curcumin demonstrates better absorption, enhancing its biological and pharmacological effects15,16. Curcumin (C₂₁H₂₀O₆) is an essential bioactive compound found in the root of the turmeric plant (Curcuma longa). Its chemical structure consists of two aromatic rings linked by a seven-carbon chain containing a β-diketone group, which gives curcumin its strong biological activity. This structure enables curcumin to act as a potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory agent. It neutralizes reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reduces the expression of inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6. Due to these properties, curcumin has been widely studied for its therapeutic potential in protecting tissues from oxidative and inflammatory damage17.

The idea of using nano-curcumin, which can activate PI3K/PTEN signals in ovarian tissue14, can potentially improve the survival and development of ovarian follicles and support the endocrinal system. The problem statement raised in this study is that the use of nano-curcumin to improve OTT outcomes after vitrification for fertility preservation remains unknown and has not been thoroughly investigated.

This study aimed to assess endocrine function, specifically focusing on the levels of follicle-stimulating hormone FSH, estradiol (E2), anti-Mullerian hormone (AMH), and the number of ovarian follicles in rats that underwent vitrification and subsequent transplantation. A comparison was made between those treated with nano-curcumin and those that did not receive this treatment.

Results

Hormonal function analysis

The results indicated that the median E2 levels in groups 1 and 2 were similar, measuring 101 [44–216] ng/ml and 101 [62–174] ng/ml, respectively, with no significant difference observed between the two groups (p > 0.05) (Table 1). Additionally, in the treatment group (group 3), which received nano-curcumin injections, E2 levels were 139 [83–246] ng/ml, also showing no significant difference in group 2 (p > 0.05). While the difference in E2 levels was not statistically significant, there was a noticeable trend towards higher E2 levels in group 3 compared to those in groups 1 and 2. This suggests the potential of nano-curcumin injections, known for their anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, to enhance granulosa cell function, thus aiding in the normalization of hormone balance during follicle development.

The median FSH levels in groups 1 and 2 were measured at 1.01 [1.01 ± 1.02] mIU/ml and 1.01 (1.00-1.01) mIU/ml, respectively, showing a notable difference (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1). These outcomes signify the effective operation of endocrine function in the central compartment of experimental rats following OTT. Moreover, the FSH level in group 3, which received nano-curcumin injections (0.99 (0.97-1.00) mIU/ml), was lower compared to that in groups 1 and 2, with a significant variance specifically against group 2 (p < 0.05). These findings suggest that nano-curcumin injections could potentially enhance the endocrine function of the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis. This indicates ongoing communication between the central nervous system and the ovaries, suggesting that despite its lesser impact on the E2 hormone parameter, an elevation in E2 levels might trigger a centrally regulated decrease in FSH levels through negative feedback.

This study revealed that the median AMH levels in group 2 (20.9 (15.3–33.7) ng/ml) did not significantly differ from those in group 1 (37 (28.3–42) ng/ml) (p > 0.05). The AMH levels observed in group 2 indicated the functionality of the transplanted follicles and ovarian growth, confirming the success of the frozen vitrification and auto-transplantation methods employed. Furthermore, in group 3, which received nano-curcumin injections, the median AMH levels (27.7 (9.5–43.6) ng/ml) did not exhibit significant differences compared to those in group 2 (p > 0.05). However, there was a tendency for higher AMH levels in the nano-curcumin-receiving group than in other groups without these injections. This suggests the potential of nano-curcumin, recognized for its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, to enhance the quality and function of ovarian follicles.

Follicle count analysis

The purpose of this histopathological examination was to assess ovarian follicle count, allowing for the evaluation and analysis of improvements in folliculogenesis. Histological preparations of the ovarian samples were stained with HE. Group 2 rats, which underwent ovariectomy, vitrification, and transplantation, demonstrated macroscopically healthy and viable ovaries. In contrast, in group 3, two ovaries were non-developed and unsuitable for histological examination. Consequently, these two ovarian samples were excluded from the analysis. In this study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of all follicles present in each group, including primordial, primary, secondary, tertiary, and Graafian follicles, for a thorough assessment (Table 2; Fig. 2).

In this study, compared to group 1, group 2 exhibited a reduction in individual follicles and overall follicle count. Notably, group 2 had no primordial follicles. Conversely, group 3, which received nano-curcumin injections, showed the presence of primordial and primary follicles, although in lower numbers than group 1. Despite the lower count, group 3 showed superior follicle findings than did group 2, indicating more positive results in terms of follicle presence and quantity.

To evaluate post-vitrification and transplantation viability, we specifically assessed primordial and primary follicles and the overall follicle counts in each group, focusing on comparing groups 3 and 2 (Fig. 2D–F). There were no considerable differences in the number of primordial and primary follicles between groups 3 and 2. When examining the total follicle counts, although group 3 displayed better total numbers than did group 2, we did not observe a significant difference. However, a trend toward significance emerged across all three examinations. These findings align with our hormone analysis, which showed increased E2 levels and a noteworthy decrease in FSH levels. This consistency reinforces the trends observed in follicle examinations, suggesting a positive relationship between hormone levels and follicular development following vitrification and transplantation, especially with nano-curcumin injections as treatment.

Discussion

Currently, the American Society for Reproductive Medicine has eliminated the “experimental” designation and recommends considering OTC and OTT as primary options for specific patients in need18. In Europe, over 4,500 instances of OTC have been recorded, while Japan has performed over 200 similar procedures12. In fertility preservation methods involving the freezing of ovarian tissue, the main causes of diminished ovarian function are cryoinjury during the tissue freezing process (OTC) and ischemic damage occurring after transplantation of ovarian tissue (OTT). Angiogenesis following transplantation is pivotal for mitigating apoptosis and reinstating ovarian functions. To address this critical requirement, it is imperative to minimize ischemic damage by fostering neovascularization19. These factors significantly impact the health of the tissue and its capacity to function effectively, especially in supporting post-transplantation follicular development.

During the processes of OTC and OTT, the ovarian tissue is inevitably subjected to cryoinjury and ischemic damage, which negatively impact the ovarian reserve and restoration of function11. Oxidative stress contributes to ovarian damage and follicular loss. Studies have highlighted that post-transplant ischemia is the primary cause of ovarian impairment. Various studies have focused on growth factors, hormones, and antioxidants to enhance revascularization and mitigate oxidative stress, thereby improving the restoration of ovarian function. This protective mechanism involves leveraging the tissue’s response to ischemia to facilitate tissue healing11,12,19,20. The intricate interplay among oxidative stress, inflammation, and immunity underscores the therapeutic potential of curcumin. Oxidative stress is characterized by an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and the presence of antioxidants. This imbalance results from either excessive production of ROS or diminished capacity of detoxification systems21.

Curcumin, a bioactive constituent found in the roots of Curcuma longa, serves various purposes—as a spice, food coloring, and an ingredient in traditional herbal medicines22. This compound has garnered approval from the US FDA as “generally recognized as safe”13. Numerous studies investigating curcumin, particularly its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory properties, highlighted its potential as a therapeutic agent23. However, several challenges hinder its clinical application, including low bioavailability, poor solubility in water, limited absorption of its active components, and rapid metabolism. One approach to enhancing its efficacy involves engineering curcumin into nanoparticle formulations to optimize its functionality15. In addition to improved bioavailability, several studies have demonstrated the in vivo safety of nanocurcumin. Histopathological evaluations of non-ovarian tissues, including liver, kidney, and lungs, showed no significant morphological changes following treatment with nanocurcumin formulations supporting its low systemic toxicity and favorable safety profile42.

In this study, we observed an increase in the average E2 levels, followed by a decrease in the average FSH levels among the groups receiving nano-curcumin injections. Despite no significant difference in the median E2 levels between groups 3 and 1, these findings highlight the successful development of transplanted mouse ovaries and affirm the effectiveness of the techniques used (vitrification, frozen storage, and transplantation) in restoring hormonal balance. Consistent with other studies, several studies have reported increased E2 levels with curcumin therapy paired with reduced FSH levels15,24,25. The observed outcome might result from the effects of the curcumin component, speculated to exert a direct estrogenic impact, or it might emerge following its metabolism26.

Although no statistically significant difference was noted, our results indicated enhanced AMH outcomes compared to those in the control group. AMH and E2 play vital regulatory roles in follicular growth and FSH activity. This hormone is manufactured by granulosa cells in early-developing follicles and serves as an indicator of both the number of growing follicles and the ovarian reserve27. Studies have shown that in a mouse model of premature ovarian failure, the injection of curcumin led to an elevation in AMH expression within granulosa cells. This effect was attributed to the inhibition of oxidative stress, apoptosis, and ovarian injury achieved through the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway and the PI3K/Akt signaling mechanism28. Oxidative stress affects the synthesis of steroid hormones, including E2 and AMH, in ovarian granulosa cells. This effect is mediated through lipid peroxidation in granulosa cells, and both E2 and AMH serve as crucial predictors of the ovarian response29. By administering nano-curcumin and subsequently suppressing oxidative stress, we anticipated improvements in both E2 and AMH levels. These enhancements are expected to influence central feedback mechanisms, leading to a decrease in FSH levels. These findings suggest the potential of nano-curcumin injections, known for their anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, to enhance granulosa cell function, thus aiding in the normalization of hormone balance during follicle development.

Our analysis of follicle counts corresponded with the hormone results we collected. Although the differences were not statistically significant, a positive trend was observed in the group that received nano-curcumin injections, indicating an increase in primordial follicles and overall number of follicles. There is extensive research on the use of curcumin for improving folliculogenesis in infertility therapy14,22,24,28. A previous study documented that curcumin possesses the potential to aid in the formation of primary ovarian follicles and promote the growth of follicles throughout folliculogenesis30. Numerous studies have indicated that curcumin has the potential to enhance folliculogenesis by inhibiting oxidative stress through the PI3K/PTEN pathway and by diminishing inflammatory factors14,22,31.

We suggest that the main mechanism contributing to the enhancement of ovarian quality after transplantation with curcumin is associated with its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects (Fig. 3). The sequence begins with the ovariectomy process, which induces ischemia in the ovarian tissue, followed by freezing and thawing procedures that can result in cryoinjury due to exposure to cryoprotectant agents. These processes cause ovarian tissue to undergo osmotic stress and inflammation, ultimately contributing to oxidative stress. Subsequently, ovarian transplantation results in ischemia and reperfusion injury. Simultaneously, intracellular oxidation mechanisms become active, intensifying the oxidative stress (2). Furthermore, ischemia-reperfusion injury in transplanted ovarian tissue leads to the production of oxygen free radicals32. Oxidative stress induces damage to the ovaries, leading to DNA damage and lipid peroxidation20,33. DNA damage stress harms healthy cells and triggers the signaling pathway of PI3K/PTEN, leading to a state of “burn out”34,35. Additionally, oxidative stress induces lipid peroxidation, initiates apoptosis, and causes loss of follicles36. This impacts the folliculogenesis process, influencing the production of hormones such as AMH and E2.

Curcumin has been documented to mitigate oxidative stress by exerting antioxidant effects. Additionally, curcumin has been reported to promote proliferation and oogenesis while inhibiting ovarian cell apoptosis24,37. Previous studies have indicated that curcumin can enhance the regulation of folliculogenesis by exerting anti-inflammatory effects, notably by reducing the expression of TNF-α and IL-622,31,38,39. Curcumin is suggested to be useful for preserving fertility by modulating folliculogenesis via the PI3K/PTEN pathway22. Curcumin can also positively influence oocyte development by improving ovarian hormone levels and inhibiting oxidative stress and apoptosis24. The intraperitoneal administration of nano-curcumin has been reported to alleviate ovarian distress caused by ischemia-reperfusion40. Additional studies on the effects of antioxidants on the ovaries have suggested that they can mitigate the risk of ischemia-reperfusion damage. Moreover, antioxidants enhance follicle survival by reducing the expression of TNF-α, IL-6, VEGF-a, and the Bax/Bcl2 ratio41.

Several previous studies have demonstrated that curcumin, particularly in nanoparticle form, exerts protective and regenerative effects on ovarian tissue. Systemic administration of nanocurcumin has been shown to reduce ischemia-reperfusion injury by enhancing endogenous antioxidant levels such as SOD and GSH, while simultaneously suppressing oxidative stress and apoptosis. Similar outcomes have been reported in models of chemically induced ovarian damage, where curcumin helped preserve folliculogenesis and supported vascular integrity through the modulation of VEGF and GDF-9 expression (3).Other natural antioxidants have also shown beneficial effects. Resveratrol primarily acts through SIRT1-mediated mitochondrial protection, while melatonin is known for its free radical scavenging and anti-apoptotic properties in ovarian models. These agents offer different mechanisms of protection, contributing to ongoing interest in antioxidant-based strategies for ovarian tissue preservation (4).

Several previous studies have indicated that curcumin’s effects on the female reproductive system are not confined to follicle count, but also involve hormonal regulation, preservation of ovarian tissue architecture, and protection against oxidative damage. Significant increases in estradiol levels and reductions in FSH levels have been observed in animal models with reproductive dysfunction following curcumin administration, suggesting enhanced ovarian endocrine feedback14,28,30. Improvements in vascularization and reduced granulosa cell damage have also been reported, even when follicle number remained unchanged22,25. Additionally, curcumin has been shown to upregulate key regulators of folliculogenesis and angiogenesis, such as VEGF and GDF-9, which play essential roles in follicle development and maturation24. Furthermore, as illustrated in our proposed pathophysiological model (Fig. 3), these beneficial effects are likely mediated through multiple mechanisms including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and pro-angiogenic pathways that synergistically contribute to the survival, functional recovery, and regeneration of ovarian tissue following vitrification and transplantation.

The utilization of curcumin nanoparticles aims to enhance absorption capacity, and the transperitoneal route was chosen because of its potential for superior and quicker local absorption in the ovaries15,40. Therefore, our goal was to improve the effectiveness of absorption and accumulation within the ovaries. Based on our research, we deduced that intraperitoneal administration of nano-curcumin can potentially enhance hormonal function and increase the number of follicles in rat ovaries subjected to vitrification and transplantation. We propose that nano-curcumin achieves these improvements through its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and proangiogenic effects. Further investigations are needed to determine the optimal dosage of curcumin and establish evidence supporting its potential molecular pathways.

Conclusion

Intraperitoneal injection of nano-curcumin shows promise for improving hormonal function and follicle count in rat ovaries that have undergone vitrification and transplantation. Nano-curcumin can trigger these improvements by harnessing its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and proangiogenic effects.

Methods

Animals and groups

Thirty female rats (Rattus norvegicus) with regular estrous cycles were housed in clean, well-maintained cages with adequate ventilation, and consistent lighting. The rats were purchased from the Animal Care of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine Universitas Airlangga where they were bred and raised under animal welfare regulations and guidelines of the Universitas Airlangga. The rats were provided with uniform food and water supply. The lighting schedule followed a 12-hour dark and 12-hour light cycle. The rats were three months old, with a mean weight of 200–250 g. Subsequently, the rats were categorized into three groups for the study (n = 10/group). Group 1 was the control group and underwent sham surgery. Group 2 underwent ovariectomy, followed by vitrification of the ovarian tissue and subsequent transplantation into the ovarian fossa. Group 3 underwent ovariectomy, followed by vitrification of the ovarian tissue and transplantation with nano-curcumin administered intraperitoneally. The dose of nano-curcumin was 2 mg/kg administered daily for 14 days. At the end of the experiment, rats were euthanized with an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). Once a surgical plane of anesthesia was confirmed, blood samples were collected via cardiac puncture. Death was verified by cessation of respiration and heartbeat, as well as fixed dilated pupils. All methods were conducted in accordance with the animal welfare regulations and guidelines at the Universities Airlangga, and with Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines.

Ovariectomy

The rats were administered an analgesic containing ketamine (0.5 cc) and xylazine (0.5 cc), following established handling and injection protocols. Subsequently, a bilateral ovariectomy was performed using an abdominal incision. After ovariectomy, the rats received postoperative care involving the administration of analgesics and antibiotics. The “Sham” group underwent laparotomy to mimic the surgical procedure without undergoing ovariectomy or vitrification.

Ovarian tissue vitrification

The ovaries were halved and placed in 15 ml of minimum essential medium. The vitrification process used the Cryo-tissue method (Kitazato, Japan), employing the Cryotop Vitrification Kit VT 601-TOP. The medium was brought to room temperature (25–27 °C) and categorized into three culture dishes. Equilibration was achieved by immersing the ovaries in the medium until they settled at the bottom of the dish; this process took 10 min for each liquid. Subsequently, the ovaries were labeled and cryopreserved in a tube containing liquid nitrogen at a temperature of − 196 °C. Thawing and transplantation

The ovaries were removed from the liquid nitrogen tube and transferred to a thawing solution medium that had been pre-warmed to 37 °C. This allowed the tissue to separate from the cryo-tissue. Afterward, the thawing procedure progressed in line with Kitazato’s thawing method, employing VT602 thawing media.

The transplantation process involved the insertion of ovaries into the ovarian fossa of rats.

Nano-curcumin

Nano-curcumin was synthesized at the Pharmacology Laboratory, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Universitas Airlangga. Nano-curcumin was synthesized using the ionic gelation method with a weight ratio of 5:1:1 for chitosan, tripolyphosphate (TPP), and curcumin (Merck No. 8.20354.0010), respectively. Chitosan was dissolved in 60 mL of 1% acetic acid and stirred at 1,000 rpm for 30 min, followed by sonication for 10 min and filtration. TPP was dissolved in 5 mL of distilled water, while curcumin was dissolved in 3 mL of 97% ethanol and sonicated for 5 min.

The chitosan solution was placed under magnetic stirring, then mixed with the curcumin solution for 30 min at 1,000 rpm. TPP solution was subsequently added dropwise, and the mixture was stirred for 3 h at the same speed to allow nanoparticle formation. A 20 mL stock of nano-curcumin was prepared by diluting in 5 mL ethanol and 20 mL distilled water, followed by sonication for 75 min.

Particle size analysis (PSA) was performed using a Nano Particle Size Analyzer (BK-802 N; BIObase) at the Institute of Life Sciences, Technology, and Engineering (LIHTR), Universitas Airlangga. The synthesized nano-curcumin showed an average particle size of 22.03 nm, with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.2052, D10 of 9.11 nm, D50 of 15.86 nm, and D90 of 27.57 nm.

Hormonal function evaluation

Fourteen days after the procedure, the rats were euthanized, and blood samples (1.5 cc) were extracted from their heart veins. Blood samples were collected without anticoagulants and centrifuged to obtain the serum. The extracted serum was subsequently analyzed for FSH, E2, and AMH levels using competitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay method.

Follicle count

The collected ovaries were processed to evaluate follicular growth by staining them with hematoxylin and eosin (HE). Subsequently, the follicles were classified into growth stages—primordial, primary, secondary, and tertiary. Quantification was performed using Image Raster 3 software, calibrated previously at 200× magnification.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.0 for Windows; GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to evaluate data distribution. Because the data for all continuous variables were skewed, the data were presented as medians [interquartile ranges]. The Mann–Whitney U test was used for statistical analyses. In all analyses, a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
