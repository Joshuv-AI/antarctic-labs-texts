Incidence and predictors of moderate to severe postoperative pain after cesarean delivery among parturients receiving spinal anesthesia at the selected southern Ethiopian Governmental Hospital 2023: a multicenter prospective single-armed cohort study
Authors: Dejene Moreda Jiru, Yonas Sagni Doba, Hunde Amsalu Name
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInadequate postoperative pain management predisposes patients to delayed recovery, impairs mother–infant interaction, prolongs hospital stay, and increases the risk of chronic pain development. In resource-limited settings, postoperative pain management remains challenging because of inadequate administration of analgesics and a high patient-to-nurse ratio, which limits adequate pain assessment and timely management.ObjectiveTo assess the incidence and predictors of moderate to severe postoperative pain after cesarean delivery among parturients receiving spinal anesthesia at two Southern Governmental teaching Hospitals between March1 and May 30, 2023.MethodsA multicenter prospective cohort study involving 205 parturients was conducted after ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board. Participants were selected using a systematic sampling technique. Postoperative pain severity was assessed using the Numeric Rating Scale within the first 24 h after surgery. Binary logistic regression was used to identify independent risk factors for postoperative pain. A p value of 0.05 was considered the cutoff point to test for statistical significance in multivariate logistic regression analysis.ResultsIn our study, the incidence of moderate to severe postoperative pain after cesarean delivery was 71.2% within the first 24 h. On the basis of the multivariable analysis, preoperative anxiety (AOR: 2.849, 95% CI: 1.276, 6.359), previous cesarean delivery (AOR: 3.571, 95% CI: 1.536, 8.300) and transverse incision (AOR: 6.965, 95% CI: 2.469, 19.652) were significantly associated with moderate to severe postoperative pain after cesarean delivery. Abdominal field blocks were associated with a reduced likelihood of postoperative pain (AOR = 0.035, 95% CI: 0.009–0.139).ConclusionA high proportion of parturients experienced moderate to severe postoperative pain after cesarean delivery. Preoperative anxiety, previous cesarean delivery, and transverse incision were significant predictors of postoperative pain, whereas abdominal field blocks had a protective effect. Routine postoperative pain assessment and the implementation of multimodal analgesic strategies, including regional analgesic techniques, are recommended to improve post-cesarean pain management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03954-1.Keywords: Cesarean delivery, Postoperative pain, Spinal anesthesia, Postoperative analgesia, Parturients

Introduction

A cesarean delivery is one type of delivery in which a surgical incision is made into the mother’s abdomen and uterus [1]. It is one of the most commonly performed procedures in obstetric practice for saving the lives of mothers and their newborns. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that the regional cesarean delivery rate should not exceed 10–15%; however, the percentage of cesarean delivery is significantly elevated globally [1, 2].

Pain is defined as a sensory and emotional experience associated with actual or potential tissue damage or is described in terms of such damage [3]. It is an important issue in cesarean delivery [4]. Poor management of pain is common and can have serious consequences. Untreated postoperative pain may result in clinical and psychological changes that increase morbidity and mortality while also decreasing quality of life [5].

Approximately 20% of parturients develop moderate to severe postoperative pain following cesarean delivery [5]. Moderate to severe postoperative pain after surgery can delay recovery and return to daily activities, affect maternal psychological well-being, increase hospital stays, develop chronic postoperative pain, prolonged opioids use, complicate breastfeeding, all of which increase medical costs and are emerging as public health concerns [6–8].

Owing to patients' expectations of postoperative pain and the low nurse-to-patient ratio, which restricts the assessment of pain and the administration of effective pain relief medications, postoperative pain management can be particularly difficult in low-income nations [9].

A study conducted in Ethiopia among surgical patients showed that postoperative pain remains severe and inadequately managed. Identifying perioperative factors associated with moderate to severe postoperative pain may help in designing targeted interventions to reduce patient suffering [10].

Very little is known about the incidence and predictors of postoperative pain in developing countries [11–13]. In particular, no studies have examined the incidence and predictors of postoperative pain after cesarean delivery among patients receiving spinal anesthesia in Ethiopia. Few studies have reported the overall incidence of postoperative pain among surgical patients undergoing different procedures and compared different methods of analgesia Therefore, in resource-limited settings, determining the incidence and predictors of postoperative pain is essential for planning appropriate interventions and optimizing the use of available analgesic modalities.

Materials and methods

Study design and area

A multicenter prospective single-arm cohort study was conducted from March 2023 to May 2023 at two southern Ethiopia governmental teaching hospitals; Dilla University Referral Hospital (DURH) and Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (HUCSH). DURH is located in the Gedeo Zone, Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples’ Region (SNNPRS), Ethiopia. HUCSH is located in Hawassa city, Sidama Region, Ethiopia.

Population

The study population included all parturients who underwent cesarean delivery under spinal anesthesia at Dilla University Referral Hospital and Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital from March 1 to May 30, 2023. Parturients who had preexisting cognitive dysfunction and failed spinal anesthesia were excluded from this study.

All parturients approached for the study provided written informed consent and none declined participation. Four parturients were subsequently excluded due to failed spinal anesthesia (which was an exclusion criterion), resulting in a final sample size of 205.

Sample size and sampling technique

Sample size calculation

The sample size estimate was based on a previous study of the incidence of post cesarean pain conducted at the University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (UOGCSH), Ethiopia during the first 24 h, in which the incidence was 85.5 [14].

A single proportion formula with a 95% level of significance and a 5% margin of error for incomplete or contingency data was used as the parameter.

n = (Zα) 2 P (1-P)/d2…Where n is the sample size Z is the statistic corresponding to the level of confidence, P is expected prevalence and d is precision (corresponding to effect size).

The initial calculated sample size was 190. To account for an anticipated 10% non-response rate, 19 participants were added, giving a final sample size of 209.

Methods of data collection

Through the use of a pretested structured questionnaire, data were collected. The questionnaire was prepared in both the English language and the Amharic language. A pretested questionnaire was administered to 5% of the parturients at one hospital to ensure that question were clear, understandable, and culturally appropriate. After the patient arrived at the postanesthesia care unit, the presence of postoperative pain was assessed at the 2nd, 6th, 12th, and 24th postoperative hours via the NRS-11.

Preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative data were collected by 2 trained BSc anesthetists and 2 BSc midwives. The data collectors provided informed consent, interviewed the patients, reviewed the charts, and documented pain severity via the NRS-11.

The structured questionnaire used for data collection is provided as Additional File 1, including socio-demographic, clinical, and perioperative variables, as well as the NRS-11 and S-STAI tools used for outcome and exposure assessment.

Study variables

The dependent variable was the incidence of postoperative pain, categorized as no to mild pain and moderate to severe postoperative pain. The independent variables were sociodemographic (age, BMI, religion, educational status, marital status, employment status), preoperative factors (ASA classification, current smoker, preoperative anxiety, analgesics given, previous scars, parity, blood type, coexisting disease), intraoperative factors (analgesia given, incision type and length, duration of surgery, emergency or elective cesarean delivery) and postoperative factors (types of analgesia given).

Operational definition

A numerical rating scale with 11 points (NRS-11) is a valid and reliable pain assessment tool in which the numbers are assigned from 0–10 to represent the severity of pain: 0 = no pain, 1–3 = mild pain, 4–6 = moderate pain, and 7–10 = severe pain30. In this study, the NRS-11 was preferred because of its simplicity of understanding by parturients. A tolerable pain threshold is defined as a treatment threshold of a pain score with a cutoff point of NRS-11 ≥ 4, which is considered to identify patients with moderate to severe pain [15, 16].

Moderate to severe postoperative pain

When a patient experiences moderate to severe postoperative pain in the first 24 postoperative hours with a cutoff point of NRS ≥ 4, this cutoff point was identified as the tolerable pain threshold [16].

Preoperative anxiety

S-STAI scores indicate anxiety and no anxiety [17].

There is anxiety

Patients who score on the S-STAI > 44 [17].

Low/no anxiety

Patients who score on the S-STAI ≤ 44 [17].

The six-item short-form of the STAI produces scores similar to those achieved via the full 20-item STAI. It has standard reliability and produces scores that are similar to those produced with the full-form across subject groups, indicating normal and elevated levels of anxiety. A brief questionnaire decreases the time taken to complete the questionnaire and the time taken to score the result [18].

Data quality assurance

The data collectors received brief orientations on the assessment tools as well as training on the goals and applicability of the study to ensure the quality of the data. Data were gathered via a structured and pretested questionnaire after the data collectors had received training. Each questionnaire was reviewed by the investigator throughout the data collection process to ensure that it was complete and appropriate.

Data analysis procedure

The data were coded and then entered, cleaned, and edited via Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 26. Using SPSS, basic descriptive statistics such as frequency and cumulative frequency were calculated for sociodemographic variables and the incidence of postoperative pain, respectively. A numeric rating scale was used for the assessment of pain severity. Binary logistic regression was used to identify independent risk factors for postoperative pain. The data were tested for a normal distribution by using the Shapiro‒Wilk test and a histogram for continuous variables. The median and interquartile range were used for nonnormally distributed variables. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test was used for goodness-of-fit. Multicollinearity was checked by the tolerance value and variance inflation factor (for each independent variable). The associations between independent factors and the outcome variable were determined at the 95% CI with bivariate and multivariate binary logistic regression. The strength of the statistical association was measured by an adjusted odds ratio and a 95% confidence interval. Variables with a p value less than 0.25 in the bivariate binary logistic analysis were included in the multivariate binary logistic regression analysis, and a p value of 0.05 was considered the cutoff point to test for statistical significance. In multivariate binary logistic regression, variables with a p value of 0.05 were statistically significant. The results were presented in text, tables, charts, and graphs.

Among the 209 study participants who were recruited for this study, four participants were excluded because of failed spinal anesthesia. A total of 205 participants were included in the analysis. Data were gathered from 130 parturients at HUCSH and 75 parturients at DURH.

Perioperative factors

Among 205 parturients, 177 (86.3%) were ASA II. A total of 113 (55.1%) parturients had no previous cesarean delivery scar. Additionally, 145 (70.7%) were multiparous, and 83 (40.5%) had blood type O. Most parturients, 153 (74.6%), were term pregnancies. Emergency cesarean delivery accounted for 167 (81.5%) cases performed under spinal anesthesia, and 174 (84.9%) parturients underwent a transverse incision. Among the 205 parturients included in the analysis, 174 (84.9%) had a duration of surgery of less than 60 min.

A single analgesic agent was commonly used to manage postoperative pain among the study participants, which may have contributed to the high rate of postoperative pain following cesarean delivery. Abdominal field blocks, such as transversus abdominis plane (TAP) blocks and ilioinguinal/iliohypogastric (IL/IH) nerve blocks, are established regional analgesic techniques for postoperative pain management after cesarean delivery. However, these techniques were performed in only 25 (12.2%) parturients, while 180 (87.8%) did not receive any abdominal field block (Table 2).

The severity of postoperative pain according to the NRS score

The proportion of parturients reporting moderate to severe postoperative pain on the numerical rating scale (NRS) was 71 (34.6%) at 2 h, 125 (61.0%) at 6 h, 108 (52.7%) at 12 h, and 84 (41.0%) at 24 h following cesarean delivery. The median (interquartile range) pain scores were 3 (0–4) at 2 h, 5 (2–7) at 6 h, 4 (2–7) at 12 h, and 2 (1–4) at 24 h postoperatively. The incidence of moderate to severe postoperative pain was highest at 6 h compared with 12 and 24 h.

Incidence of postoperative pain

In this study, 71 (34.6%) of the participants experienced moderate to severe pain during the first 2 h after a cesarean delivery, and 134 (65.4%) patients reported experiencing moderate to severe pain during the first 6 h after a cesarean delivery. The incidence of postoperative pain during the first 12 h was 141 (68.8%) participants who reported moderate to severe pain. Within the first 24 h after a cesarean delivery, 146 parturients (71.2%) experienced moderate to severe pain (Figs. 2).

Predictors of moderate to severe postoperative pain after cesarean delivery

Multivariable logistic regression analysis showed that preoperative anxiety, history of previous cesarean delivery, transverse skin incision, and use of abdominal field block were significantly associated with moderate to severe postoperative pain after cesarean delivery.

Parturients with preoperative anxiety had higher odds of experiencing moderate to severe postoperative pain compared with those without anxiety (AOR = 2.85, 95% CI: 1.28–6.36). Similarly, those with a history of previous cesarean delivery had increased odds of moderate to severe pain compared with those without a prior cesarean delivery (AOR = 3.57, 95% CI: 1.54–8.30). Transverse skin incision was also associated with higher odds of moderate to severe postoperative pain compared with vertical incision (AOR = 6.97, 95% CI: 2.47–19.65).

In contrast, the use of abdominal field blocks was associated with lower odds of moderate to severe postoperative pain (AOR = 0.04, 95% CI: 0.009–0.139), suggesting a potential protective association (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, the incidence of moderate to severe postoperative pain increased over time, affecting 34.6% of parturients within 2 h, 65.4% at 6 h, 68.8% at 12 h, and 71.2% at 24 h after cesarean delivery. This pattern is clinically expected and likely reflects the gradual resolution of spinal anesthesia and limited duration of postoperative analgesic coverage. The high proportion of women experiencing significant pain within the first postoperative day highlights the need for improved early postoperative pain management.

The overall incidence of moderate to severe postoperative pain within 24 h (71.2%) in this study is lower than that reported in a cross-sectional study from northwest Ethiopia, where 85.5%(95% CI: 81.4%, 89.0%) of parturients experienced moderate to severe pain during the first postoperative day [19]. Differences may be explained by variations in study population and anesthesia techniques, as their study included both spinal and general anesthesia, whereas our study included only parturients who underwent spinal anesthesia. In contrast, a prospective longitudinal study from Brazil reported a slightly higher incidence (78.4%) (CI: 95%: 75.9%–80.8%) of moderate to severe postoperative pain after cesarean delivery [20]. Differences in healthcare settings, analgesic protocols, and pain assessment approaches may account for this variation.

Studies conducted in high-income countries have generally reported lower incidences of moderate to severe postoperative pain. For example, a prospective cohort study from the United States reported a prevalence of severe postoperative pain of 10.9% within 36 h after cesarean delivery [21]. Similarly, a retrospective study from Duke University, North Carolina, found that 53% of parturients experienced moderate to severe pain within 48 h [22]. These lower rates are likely related to the routine use of neuraxial opioids, multimodal analgesia, and structured pain assessment protocols, which differ from routine practice in many low-resource settings, including Ethiopia.

Lower pain incidences have also been reported in some African settings. A prospective descriptive study from Uganda documented lower rates of moderate to severe pain at zero, six, and twenty-four hours (23%, 39%, and 29%) respectively after cesarean delivery [23]. In that study, the use of pethidine and morphine in addition to non-opioid analgesics and early pain assessment before the full resolution of spinal anesthesia may have contributed to the lower reported pain levels. In contrast, postoperative pain management in our study relied mainly on diclofenac and tramadol, indicating limited application of multimodal analgesia. Importantly, parturients who received abdominal field blocks were significantly less likely to experience moderate to severe postoperative pain, highlighting the benefit of regional analgesic techniques.

Preoperative anxiety was a significant predictor of moderate to severe postoperative pain in this study. Parturients who experienced preoperative anxiety were 2.8 times more likely to develop moderate to severe postoperative pain after cesarean delivery than parturients who did not experience preoperative anxiety. This finding is consistent with evidence from prospective studies conducted in northwest Ethiopia [14], Brazil [20], and the United States [22], which have shown that preoperative anxiety increases the likelihood of postoperative pain. Anxiety may heighten pain perception by activating stress-related neuroendocrine pathways and reducing pain tolerance. These findings underscore the importance of routine preoperative anxiety screening and appropriate psychological support, which may contribute to improved postoperative pain control and overall maternal outcomes.

In this study, transverse skin incisions were significantly associated with moderate to severe postoperative pain following cesarean delivery. Women who underwent transverse incisions were nearly seven times more likely to experience moderate to severe pain compared with those who had vertical incisions (AOR = 6.97, 95% CI: 2.47–19.65). This finding is consistent with a study from northwest Ethiopia, which reported higher odds of moderate to severe pain with Pfannenstiel incisions compared with midline incisions (AOR = 3.2, 95% CI: 1.3–8.0) [14].

However, evidence from randomized controlled trials is mixed. A prospective, double-blind trial conducted in Thailand reported higher VAS scores in the Pfannenstiel group among women undergoing repeat cesarean delivery, but ultimately found no meaningful difference in overall postoperative pain severity between vertical and Pfannenstiel incisions [24]. Differences in study design, patient populations, analgesic regimens, and surgical context.

In addition, the observed association in this study may have been influenced by unmeasured confounders, such as variation in postoperative analgesic administration, use of regional techniques (e.g., abdominal wall blocks), or the relatively small number of patients in the vertical incision group. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted with caution, and a causal relationship cannot be established.

In this study, a history of previous cesarean scars was significantly associated with moderate to severe postoperative pain. Parturients with prior cesarean delivery were 3.6 times more likely to experience moderate to severe pain compared with those without a previous scar (AOR = 3.57; 95% CI: 1.54–8.30). This finding is consistent with previous studies, which reported that women with a history of cesarean delivery were 2.3 times more likely to develop moderate to severe postoperative pain (AOR = 2.3; 95% CI: 1.1–5) [14]. Similarly, another study demonstrated that parturients undergoing repeat cesarean delivery had a 1.54-fold increased risk of postoperative pain compared with those undergoing primary cesarean delivery (AOR = 1.54; 95% CI: 1.18–2.02) [22]. This association may be related to more extensive surgical dissection, scar tissue formation, and altered pain sensitivity in repeat cesarean deliveries.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study identifies associations between selected factors and postoperative pain outcomes; however, causal relationships cannot be established due to the observational study design. First, there was no standardized postoperative analgesic strategy or protocol across participants, which may have influenced the observed incidence and severity of postoperative pain. Second, as this was a hospital-based study conducted in Ethiopia, the results may not be fully generalizable to other populations, particularly those outside Ethiopia or East Africa, where patient characteristics, clinical practices, and resource availability may differ. Third, details of the surgical techniques used for cesarean delivery and the spinal anesthetic technique were not fully standardized or described, and institutional practices may vary from those in other settings. Fourth, postoperative pain was assessed using a single pain score without differentiation between pain at rest and during activity, and activity limitations or functional outcomes were not evaluated. Finally, although widely used in clinical research, pain scores are subjective and may not fully capture the multidimensional nature of pain and its impact on recovery.

Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insight into postoperative pain and its associated factors following cesarean delivery in a low-resource setting, where such data remain limited. The findings may help inform future research and guide improvements in perioperative pain management strategies in similar contexts.

Conclusion and recommendations

The incidence of moderate to severe postoperative pain after cesarean delivery was 71.2% within the first 24 h. Parturients who had preoperative anxiety, a history of previous CS, transverse incisions, and those who did not receive an abdominal field block had moderate to severe postoperative pain after cesarean delivery.

Preoperative anxiety should be assessed during the preoperative assessment, and appropriate interventions should be provided. Clinicians should pay special attention to parturients who have preoperative anxiety and a history of previous C/D and transverse incisions. Abdominal field blocks should be provided to minimize the incidence of postoperative pain after cesarean delivery.

Postoperative pain should be regularly assessed using standardized pain scales, and analgesic management should be guided by the severity of pain to ensure timely and adequate pain relief.
