Exosomes derived from umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells alleviate jaw bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells senescence and restore osteogenic differentiation potential
Authors: Hongkun Li, Nuo Xu, Shuaichen Li, Xinjing Wang, Anping Li, Hengxin Wang, Shuang Wu, Sunxin Zhou, Chuan Cai, Tong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe age-related functional decline of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells significantly impairs bone regeneration capacity. Exosomes derived from umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (UCMSCs) have emerged as promising therapeutic agents in regenerative medicine and anti-aging research due to their bioactive cargo and low immunogenicity. This study investigated the rejuvenating potential of UCMSCs-derived exosomes (UCMSC-Exos) on senescent jaw bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (JBMMSCs) and their ability to enhance bone repair in aged rats.MethodsSenescent JBMMSCs were treated with UCMSC-Exos, and their effects on cellular senescence, proliferation, migration, and osteogenic capability were assessed using senescence-associated beta-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining, CCK8 assays, scratch assays, alizarin red S staining, alkaline phosphatase staining, RT-qPCR, ELISA and western blotting. The underlying mechanisms were explored through western blot analysis of autophagy markers and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. For in vivo evaluation, calvarial defect models were established in aged rats, and the bone repair efficacy of UCMSC-Exos was assessed by micro-CT, histological staining, immunohistochemical staining for OCN and ALP, and immunofluorescence staining for OPN and OSX.ResultsUCMSC-Exos treatment markedly attenuated cellular senescence in JBMMSCs, as demonstrated by decreased SA-β-gal-positive cells and downregulation of key senescence-associated proteins (p53, p21, and p16) and pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and TNF-α). The exosome-treated cells showed significant improvements in proliferative capacity, migratory ability, and osteogenic differentiation potential compared to untreated controls. Mechanistically, UCMSC-Exos restored autophagy through modulation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, as demonstrated by altered protein expression. In rat calvarial defect models, UCMSC-Exos treatment resulted in better bone repair in aged rats.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated that UCMSC-Exos could rejuvenate senescent JBMMSCs by activating autophagy via the regulation of PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, and enhance bone repair in aged rats. UCMSC-Exos hold potential as a cell-free therapeutic tool for anti-aging treatments and regenerative medicine, particularly for age-related skeletal disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04587-w.Keywords: Autophagy, Bone regeneration, Cellular senescence, Exosomes, Mesenchymal stem cells

Background

The functional decline of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) during aging, characterized by reduced proliferation, migration, and osteogenic differentiation capacities, significantly impairs bone tissue regeneration [1–3]. This contributes to age-related skeletal disorders such as osteoporosis and delayed fracture healing [4–7]. In craniofacial regions, these impairments adversely affect the repair of craniofacial bone defects caused by tumor resection, infection and severe trauma [8, 9]. Recent advances in regenerative medicine have highlighted mesenchymal stem cells-derived exosomes (MSC-Exos) as a promising therapeutic alternative [10–12]. These nanovesicles retain the regenerative potential of their parent cells while eliminating the risks associated with whole-cell transplantation [13, 14]. Studies indicate that the therapeutic effects of stem cells are primarily mediated through paracrine mechanisms, with MSC-Exos delivering bioactive cargo (proteins, mRNAs, and miRNAs) to modulate recipient cell functions [15]. However, the anti-aging effects and underlying mechanisms of MSC-Exos on senescent jaw bone marrow MSCs (JBMMSCs) remain incompletely understood.

Cellular senescence not only affects cell function but also induces the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), which leads to chronic inflammation and propagates senescence to neighboring cells through the secretion of high levels of proinflammatory factors [16–18]. In contrast, young cells secrete “young signals” that can restore the function of senescent cells [19]. It has been reported that the rejuvenation of aged progenitor cells can be achieved by exposing them to a youthful systemic environment [20]. Recent studies have consistently emphasized the significant roles of exosomes in modulating cellular senescence [21, 22]. For example, a study demonstrated that young exosomes secreted by stem cells from human exfoliated deciduous teeth (SHED-Exos) can alleviate the aging phenotypes of aged cells and maintain their capacity [22]. This indicates that paracrine factors from young MSCs play crucial roles in counteracting aging. Exosomes offer distinct advantages over mesenchymal stem cells, including easier preparation, enhanced safety profiles, and greater stability against environmental reprogramming, which collectively render them particularly attractive for anti-aging therapeutics. Among various MSC sources, umbilical cord MSCs (UCMSCs) exhibit superior paracrine activity and represent a promising option for stem cell-based therapy [23–25]. Nevertheless, the rejuvenating effects of UCMSC-derived exosomes (UCMSC-Exos) and their mechanisms require further investigation.

The PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway plays a key role in cellular senescence [26]. Hyperactivation of this pathway suppresses autophagy, leading to the accumulation of abnormal proteins and increased oxidative stress, thereby promoting cell senescence [27, 28]. Conversely, the activation of autophagy has anti-aging effects and can extend the lifespan of many model organisms [29, 30]. As previous studies have shown, restoring autophagy via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway can alleviate brain aging as well as the senescence of MSCs [31, 32]. While MSC-Exos have been shown to regulate multiple signaling pathways, their role in counteracting senescence in JBMMSCs via PI3K/AKT/mTOR inhibition and autophagy induction remains unclear.

Based on the critical role of cellular senescence in age-related bone regeneration impairment and the emerging therapeutic potential of MSC-derived exosomes, this study was designed to evaluate the rejuvenating effects of UCMSC-Exos on senescent JBMMSCs. Specifically, we aimed to investigate whether UCMSC-Exos could restore the activity of senescent JBMMSCs through autophagy modulation, to elucidate the involvement of PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in this process, and to validate the therapeutic potential of UCMSC-Exos in aged rat calvarial defect models. These investigations were expected to provide new insights into developing exosome-based therapies for age-related skeletal disorders.

Materials and methods

Isolation, culture, and identification of JBMMSCs

Primary rat JBMMSCs were isolated from young (8 weeks old) and aged (22 months old) male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats. All procedures involving animals were approved by the Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of Beijing MDKN Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Approval number: MDKN-2024-072) and were conducted in strict accordance with the experimental animal care and use guidelines of the Beijing Animal Control Committee. Following euthanasia by intraperitoneal sodium pentobarbital administration (150 mg/kg), the rats were immersed in 75% ethanol for 10 min and then transferred to a biological safety cabinet. Under sterile conditions, the mandibles were dissected out, and the soft tissues and teeth were removed using sterile instruments. The bones were then thoroughly rinsed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 5% penicillin-streptomycin. The bone marrow cavity was repeatedly flushed with a 1 ml syringe containing α-MEM (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and the flushing fluid was collected. After centrifugation at 800 × g for 3 min, the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was resuspended in complete culture medium (α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin) and transferred to a culture dish. The flushed mandible were divided into small bone slices using a bone rongeur and transferred into a tube containing 0.1% type II collagenase solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The tube was placed on a shaker and incubated at 37 °C, 200 rpm for 90 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded, and the harvested cells and digested bone slices were transferred to the culture dish containing the cells previously collected from the marrow flushing fluid, followed by incubation in a 37 °C, 5% CO₂ incubator. Non-adherent cells were removed after 48 h, and the medium was replaced every 3 days. When the adherent cells reached 80–90% confluence, they were passaged at a 1:2 ratio. The bone slices were discarded during the second passage, and JBMMSCs from passages 2 to 5 were used for the study. The cellular morphology was observed under microscopy for both young and aged cells.

The cells were characterized for negative expression of surface marker antigens CD45, CD34, and CD11b, and positive expression of surface marker antigens CD73, CD90, and CD105 using flow cytometry. Subsequently, the cells’ multilineage differentiation potential was evaluated using Alizarin Red S, Oil Red O, and Alcian Blue staining methods for osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation, respectively, as described previously [33].

Isolation and identification of UCMSC-Exos

Human umbilical cord MSCs (UCMSCs) were obtained from iCell Bioscience Inc. (HUM-iCell-e009, Shanghai, China). UCMSC-Exos were isolated by serial centrifugation with ultracentrifugation as previously described [22]. When the cells reached 70–80% confluence, they were washed twice with PBS and then cultured in exosome-free medium for 48 h. The supernatant of UCMSCs was first centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min to remove the cells and subsequently centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min to remove the apoptotic bodies and debris. Subsequently, the supernatant was centrifuged at 10 000 × g for 30 min and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA) to pellet microvesicles. The clarified supernatant was then ultracentrifuged (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) at 120 000 × g for 70 min to obtain the exosome pellet, which was washed with PBS and re-centrifuged under identical conditions. Finally, the exosomes pellet was resuspended in PBS and stored at − 80 °C.

The morphology of the isolated exosomes was examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Hitachi HT7700, Japan) following negative staining with 2% uranyl acetate. Size distribution and particle concentration were determined using the ZetaView (Particle Metrix, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol as previously described [34]. The protein concentration of exosomes enriched fraction was quantified by a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the product manual. The expression of the tetraspanins CD63 and CD81, the endosomal sorting complex required for transport TSG101, and the endoplasmic reticulum marker calnexin was analyzed by western blotting using standard protocols.

Exosomes uptake assay

UCMSC-Exos were fluorescently labeled with PKH26 (Beyotime, Nanjing, China) for 10 min at room temperature and subsequently re-isolated according to the standard exosome purification protocol. After 24 h of culture, JBMMSCs were washed twice with PBS and incubated with PKH26-labeled exosomes for 24 h. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature, followed by staining with SF488-conjugated phalloidin (1:200 dilution; Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 20 min to visualize actin filaments. After three PBS washes, nuclei were stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Beyotime, Nanjing, China) for 5 min. Cellular uptake of PKH26-labeled exosomes was then visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) assay

JBMMSCs were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells per well. When the cells reached 60% confluence, cellular senescence was assessed using the Senescence β-Galactosidase Staining Kit (Beyotime, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 5 min. After washing three times with PBS, the SA-β-galactosidase staining working solution was added, and the samples were incubated at 37 °C in a CO₂-free incubator for 16–18 h. Subsequently, the reaction solution was then aspirated, and PBS was added to terminate the enzyme reaction and wash the cells. The cells were observed and photographed under a microscope. For quantitative analysis, six random fields were captured per well using a microscope. The percentage of SA-β-gal-positive cells was calculated as: (number of blue-stained cells/total number of cells observed) × 100%.

Proliferation assay

The effects of different concentrations of UCMSC-Exos on the proliferation of JBMMSCs were assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8; Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). Briefly, JBMMSCs were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 10³ cells per well and treated with UCMSC-Exos at concentrations of 0, 25 µg/mL, and 50 µg/mL. On days 0, 1, 3, and 5 , the culture medium was removed, and CCK-8 was added to each well according to the manufacturer’s instruction (90 µL fresh medium and 10 µL CCK-8 reagent per well). After incubation for 3 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2, the absorbance of each well was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).

Cell migration assay

To evaluate the effect of UCMSC-Exos on the migratory ability of JBMMSCs, the Scratch-Wound assay was performed. Briefly, JBMMSCs were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Once the cells reached 100% confluence, the monolayer was scratched using a 200-µL pipette tip. The cells were then washed with PBS to remove debris and smooth the edge of the scratch, and subsequently cultured in medium containing 1% serum supplemented with different concentrations of UCMSC-Exos (25 µg/mL and 50 µg/mL) or an equal volume of PBS. The cells were observed and photographed at 0 h and 24 h using a microscope (Nikon Instruments, Japan). The migration area (%) was assessed as follows: Migration area (%) = (A0 – An) / A0 × 100%, where A0 represents the initial scratch area (t = 0 h) and An represents the residual scratch area at the measurement time point (t = 24 h).

Osteogenic differentiation assay and alizarin red S staining

JBMMSCs were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured overnight until 80% confluence was reached. The medium was then replaced with osteogenic differentiation medium (α-MEM containing 10% FBS, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, 50 mg/L ascorbic acid, and 1 µM dexamethasone) supplemented with 25 µg/mL or 50 µg/mL UCMSC-Exos or an equal volume of PBS. The induction medium was replaced every two days. To evaluate osteogenic differentiation, cells were stained with alizarin red S on day 21 after osteogenic induction. Specifically, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, washed three times with PBS, stained with 2% alizarin red S (OriCell, Guangzhou, China) for 30 min, washed with ddH₂O, and then observed and photographed under a microscope.

Alkaline phosphatase staining

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining was performed on JBMMSCs after 7 days of osteogenic induction using an ALP staining kit (Beyotime, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the culture medium was aspirated, and the cells were washed with PBS. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. After fixation, the cells were washed again with PBS, and fresh ALP staining working solution was added. The cells were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. The reaction was terminated by washing with distilled water, and the cells were subsequently observed and photographed under a light microscope.

Measurement of autophagic flux

To assess autophagy flux, cells were infected with Ad-Plus-mCherry-GFP-LC3 adenovirus (C3011, Beyotime, Nanjing, China) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 20 for 24 h, as per the manufacturer’s instructions. After 24 h, the medium was replaced and exosomes or an equal volume of PBS were added, followed by incubation for 48 h. The number of yellow and red puncta, representing autophagosomes and autolysosomes, respectively were observed using a confocal microscope (LSM900, ZEISS, Germany).

Quantitative real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Target gene expression analysis was performed via RT-qPCR. In brief, total RNA was extracted using Trizol (TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan), and its quantity and purity were confirmed using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA). The RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the Primer Script® RT reagent kit (TaKaRa, Japan). The PCR reaction was carried out using SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ (TaKaRa, Japan) on a CFX96™ real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA). The primer sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Western blotting

Cells or UCMSC-Exos were lysed with RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors (PMSF, Beyotime, China). Western blotting was performed as previously described [35]. Antibodies used for western blotting analysis included: CD63 (1:1000; HA722731, Huabio), CD81 (1:5000; 84644-1-RR, Proteintech), TSG101 (1:1000; 84334-4-RR, Proteintech), Calnexin (1:1000; 81938-1-RR, Proteintech), OPN (1:1000; abs159925, Absin), ALP (1:1000; abs158623, Absin), RUNX2 (1:1000; ET1612-47, Huabio), P16 (1:1000; A23882, Abclonal), P21 (1:1000; ab109199, Abcam), P53 (1:5000;60283-2-Ig, Proteintech), LC3 (1:2000; 14600-1-AP, Proteintech), Beclin1 (1:1000; 11306-1-AP, Proteintech), P62 (1:1000; 5114, CST), PI3K (1:1000; AF1729, Beyotime), p-PI3K (1:1000; AF3242, Affinity), AKT (1:1000; 9272, CST), p-AKT (1:1000; 4060, CST), mTOR (1:1000; 2972, CST), p-mTOR (1:1000; 2971, CST), and β-actin (1:5000; 20536-1-AP, Proteintech). The secondary antibodies used were HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse antibodies (diluted at 1:5000). The immunoreactive proteins were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate kit (Absin, Shanghai, China). ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used to quantitatively analyze the protein expression.

ELISA assays

The ELISA analysis of IL-6, TNF-α and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) secretion levels was performed using cell culture supernatants collected from JBMMSCs with or without UCMSC-Exos treatment. The assays were conducted following the manufacturer’s protocols for Rat IL-6, Rat TNF-α, and Rat IGF-1 ELISA kits (Abclonal, China).

RNA-sequencing analysis

RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis was performed by Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). In brief, total RNA was isolated using the TRIzol® Reagent following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Subsequently, the RNA quality was assessed using the 5300 Bioanalyzer (Agilent) and quantified using the ND-2000 (NanoDrop Technologies). The RNA-seq transcriptome library was prepared using the Illumina® Stranded mRNA Prep (San Diego, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The libraries underwent quality assessment using the Qubit 4.0 and were sequenced on the NovaSeq X Plus platform (PE150) using the NovaSeq Reagent Kit. Prior to read mapping, raw reads underwent filtering to obtain high-quality sequencing data. The DESeq2 algorithm was applied to calculate the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) [36]. DEGs with |log2FC| ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05 were considered to be significantly differentially expressed genes.

Animal model and study design

This experiment was approved by the Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of Beijing MDKN Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Approval number: MDKN-2024-072; Date of approval: April 20, 2024; Title of the approved project: “Exosomes derived from umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells alleviate jaw bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells senescence and promote osteogenic differentiation”), and was in compliance with the Guidelines for ethical review of animal welfare of Laboratory animals issued by the China National Standardization Management Committee (publication No. GB/T35892-2018). The work has been reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. The UCMSC-Exos carrier used for in vivo studies was the bilayer non-cross-linked collagen membrane scaffold (Bio-Gide®, Geistlich Pharma, Wolhusen, Switzerland), as previously described [37]. The membranes were cut into small pieces (6 mm × 6 mm) using sterile scissors and placed in a 48-well plate with the “rough” side facing up. PBS containing 50 µg of exosomes was added to the wells, while the control group received an equivalent volume of PBS. The membranes were incubated at 4 °C under sterile conditions for 4 h to allow the exosomes to be absorbed by the collagen membranes [37]. Subsequently, the rat calvarial defect models were immediately established and the membranes were implanted. The experimental animals included five 8-week-old male SD rats (250–300 g) and five 22-month-old male SD rats (650–750 g), all of which had been adapted for one week prior to the experiment. After anesthesia with pentobarbital sodium, the 5 mm diameter calvarial defect was created on both sides of the sagittal suture of the skull using a 5-mm outer-diameter drill. In the young rat group, one side of the calvarial defect was treated with a membrane containing PBS (Young-vehicle; n = 5). In the aged rat group, one side of the calvarial defect was treated with a membrane containing PBS (Aged-vehicle; n = 5), while the other side was treated with a membrane containing UCMSC-Exos (Aged + Exo; n = 5). The rough surface of the membrane faced the calvarial defect, and the smooth surface faced the soft tissue. Four weeks after surgery, all rats were euthanized with pentobarbital sodium, and the craniums were harvested for micro-CT scanning and histopathological evaluation.

Micro-CT analysis

Four weeks after surgery, the rats were euthanized, and the craniums were harvested and fixed in 4% formaldehyde solution for 48 h. The specimens were scanned using a micro-CT scanner (SCANCO Medical AG, Bruttisellen, Switzerland) under the following conditions: 70 kV, 200 µA, with the layer thickness of 11.4 μm. Three-dimensional images were reconstructed using the CTVol software, and regions of interest (ROIs) were selected using CTAn software to analyze the bone volume/total volume (BV/TV) at the bone defect site. In addition, bone coverage was calculated using ImageJ software.

Histological analysis

After fixation in 4% formaldehyde, the samples were decalcified in 10% EDTA solution for over 4 weeks. Following decalcification, the samples were washed with tap water and then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. The embedded samples were sectioned into 5 μm thick slices using a microtome (Leica RM 2035, Germany) in a direction perpendicular to the sagittal suture, longitudinally through the centers of the bilateral calvarial defects, and perpendicular to the parietal bone. The sections were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome to observe the regenerated new bone and the repair efficacy.

Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence staining

For immunohistochemical staining, sections were incubated in 3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol for 15 min to reduce nonspecific staining caused by endogenous peroxidase. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with goat serum on ice for 40 min. Primary antibodies against ALP (1:200; abs158623, Absin) and OCN (1:200; 23418-1-AP, Proteintech) were incubated overnight at 4 °C. The secondary antibody used was rabbit anti-goat IgG, incubated for 30 min. For immunofluorescence staining, sections were permeabilized and blocked, then incubated with anti-OPN (1:200; AF7662, Beyotime) and anti-OSX (1:200; sc-393325, Santa Cruz) antibodies overnight at 4 °C. On the following day, the sections were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. After washing with PBS, DAPI was used to stain the nuclei. Quantification of the number of positively stained cells was performed using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed at least three times. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The results were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for statistical analysis. Differences between two groups were tested using the Student’s t-test. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characterization of JBMMSCs and UCMSC-Exos

Microscopic observation revealed that the isolated and cultured JBMMSCs exhibited adherent growth characteristics. Cells derived from young rats displayed a smooth surface morphology with slender spindle shapes, arranged in a whirlpool pattern (Fig. 1A). In contrast, cells derived from aged rats demonstrated distinct senescence-related morphological alterations, including cellular enlargement, flattened fried-egg-like morphology, and poorly defined cellular boundaries (Fig. 1E). Tri-lineage differentiation experiments demonstrated that both young and aged JBMMSCs possessed multilineage differentiation potential, albeit with differences in their differentiation capacities. Following 21 days of osteogenic induction, Alizarin Red S staining indicated the formation of mineralized nodules in JBMMSCs, with young cells exhibiting greater calcium deposition (Fig. 1B and F). After 28 days of adipogenic induction, Oil Red O staining showed the presence of lipid droplets in JBMMSCs, which was more pronounced in aged cells (Fig. 1C and G). Alcian Blue staining demonstrated the formation of cartilage by JBMMSCs after 28 days of chondrogenic differentiation, with young cells displaying greater cartilage extracellular matrix (Fig. 1D and H).

Additionally, flow cytometry revealed positive phenotypic markers for CD73, CD90, and CD105, while CD45, CD34, and CD11b were negative (Fig. 1I). For the UCMSC-Exos, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observations showed that UCMSC-Exos had a typical cup-shaped bilayer membrane structure (Fig. 1J). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) showed that the particle size of UCMSC-Exos was approximately 60–150 nm (Fig. 1K). Western blotting analysis confirmed that the isolated particles were positive for classical exosome markers (CD63, CD81, and TSG101) and negative for Calnexin (Fig. 1L), indicating no contamination of cellular components in the isolated exosomes. After incubating JBMMSCs with PKH26-labeled UCMSC-Exos, red fluorescence was observed within the cells under the microscope, indicating that UCMSC-Exos were effectively internalized by JBMMSCs (Fig. 1M).

UCMSC-Exos mitigated JBMMSCs senescence and enhanced the proliferation, migration, and osteogenic capacity

JBMMSCs derived from aged rats exhibited a higher proportion of SA-β-galactosidase-positive cells, with significant upregulation of senescence biomarkers p53, p21, and p16 (Fig. 2A-H). Furthermore, the aged JBMMSCs exhibited elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and TNF-α) along with downregulated expression of IGF-1 (Fig. 2I-N). Results from CCK-8 and scratch-wound assays indicated that the proliferation and migration capabilities of the aged JBMMSCs were significantly lower than those of young JBMMSCs (Fig. 2O-Q). These alterations validated the establishment of the senescent JBMMSC model. Following osteogenic induction, ALP staining and alizarin red S staining revealed diminished ALP activity and calcium deposition in senescent JBMMSCs (Fig. 3A-B). Consistent with these findings, the expression of osteogenesis-related markers ALP, OPN, and RUNX2 was significantly downregulated (Fig. 3C-I).

After 48 h of treatment with UCMSC-Exos, the number of SA-β-galactosidase-positive cells in senescent JBMMSCs was significantly reduced, and the expression of senescence biomarkers p53, p21, and p16 was also significantly decreased (Fig. 2A-H). Moreover, the treatment downregulated pro-inflammatory cytokine levels (IL-6 and TNF-α) while upregulating IGF-1 expression (Fig. 2I-N). Treatment with UCMSC-Exos enhanced the proliferation rate and migration capacity of senescent cells, increased ALP activity and calcium deposition, and significantly upregulated the expression of osteogenesis-related markers ALP, OPN, and RUNX2 (Figs. 2O-Q and 3A-I). Collectively, these results demonstrated that UCMSC-Exos could rejuvenate senescent JBMMSCs and improve their proliferation, migration, and osteogenic capacities.

UCMSC-Exos enhanced autophagy in JBMMSCs

To determine whether UCMSC-Exos could enhance autophagy in senescent JBMMSCs, western blotting was used to detect autophagy-related proteins, including Beclin1, P62, and LC3. Compared to the young cells, the older cells exhibited a lower LC3-II/LC3-I ratio and Beclin1 expression, while p62 was upregulated (Fig. 4A-D). After treatment with exosomes, the impaired autophagy was improved, with increased LC3-II/LC3-I ratio and Beclin1 expression, and decreased p62 expression.

Additionally, to assess the effect of UCMSC-Exos on autophagic flux, we infected the cells with Ad-mCherry-GFP-LC3. The results indicated that, compared to the young group, the older group had fewer yellow dots (indicating autophagosomes) and red dots (indicating autolysosomes). However, after treatment with exosomes, the number of yellow and red dots increased, indicating enhanced autophagic flux (Fig. 4E-F). To further confirm that the increase in LC3-II was due to enhanced autophagic flux rather than impaired degradation, we treated the cells with chloroquine (CQ). The results showed that the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio and Beclin1 expression still increased, while p62 expression decreased (Fig. 4G-J).

To elucidate the essential role of autophagy in UCMSC-Exos-mediated senescence alleviation, we co-cultured JBMMSCs with the autophagy inhibitor 3-methyladenine (3-MA). Western blot analysis demonstrated that 3-MA treatment effectively blocked UCMSC-Exos-induced autophagy in senescent JBMMSCs, evidenced by increased accumulation of P62, reduced expression of Beclin1, and decreased LC3-II/LC3-I ratio (Fig. 4K-N). Further results showed that autophagy inhibition significantly attenuated the anti-senescence effects of UCMSC-Exos, with the increase in SA-β-gal-positivity cells (Fig. 4O). Western blot analysis demonstrated corresponding upregulation of P53, P21, and P16 in the combination group compared to UCMSC-Exos monotherapy (Fig. 4P-S). These results collectively establish that autophagy activation is essential for UCMSC-Exos to alleviate cellular senescence.

UCMSC-Exos modulated the gene expression pattern of senescent JBMMSCs

To elucidate the potential molecular mechanisms by which UCMSC-Exos rescue the function of senescent JBMMSCs, we performed RNA sequencing. After UCMSC-Exos treatment, we identified 431 upregulated genes (2-fold, p < 0.05) and 862 downregulated genes (< 0.5-fold, p < 0.05) (Fig. 5A). Downregulated genes included senescence-related Mmp13, Il1a, Il6, Serpine1, and Tnf. Upregulated genes included anabolic and cell vitality-related Col11a1, Col11a2, Foxo1, Ccnd1, and Ccna1. Next, we performed GO functional enrichment analysis on these DEGs, which showed enrichment in terms such as positive regulation of biological process, positive regulation of cellular process, regulation of cell population proliferation, cell activation, extracellular space, and protein binding (Fig. 5B). KEGG analysis revealed enrichment in the NF-kappa B signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, PI3K/AKT pathway and cellular senescence pathways (Fig. 5C). Subsequently, we used Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) to mine the RNA-seq data. The results showed that treatment with UCMSC-Exos reduced the enrichment score of the “PI3K/AKT pathway” module (Fig. 5D), while increasing the enrichment score of the “autophagy” module (Fig. 5E). Since the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway has previously been implicated in the senescence alleviation of MSCs [38–41], it is reasonable to believe that the signaling process may play a key role in the exosome-mediated restoration of vitality in senescent JBMMSCs. Further considering the upregulation of autophagy, we speculate that UCMSC-Exos restore vitality in senescent JBMMSCs by modulating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. In summary, UCMSC-Exos treatment alleviated the senescence phenotype and enhanced cellular vitality in senescent JBMMSCs, which may be associated with the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway.

UCMSC-Exos restored vitality in senescent JBMMSCs via downregulation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway

To explore whether the anti-senescence effect of UCMSC-Exos on senescent JBMMSCs was mediated by downregulating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway to activate autophagy, we assessed the expression levels of proteins in the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. The western blotting results revealed that p-PI3K, p-AKT, and p-mTOR were upregulated in senescent JBMMSCs, while the ratios of p-PI3K/PI3K, p-AKT/AKT and p-mTOR/mTOR were significantly decreased after UCMSC-Exos treatment (Fig. 6A–D). To further validate the regulatory mechanism of PI3K/AKT/mTOR, we co-cultured senescent JBMMSCs with the PI3K activator (740-YP; 10 µM) for 24 h. Western blotting results showed that, due to the facilitating effect of 740 Y-P on PI3K phosphorylation, the downregulation of PI3K and its downstream AKT and mTOR phosphorylation by UCMSC-Exos was abolished (Fig. 6E–H). Importantly, the activation of PI3K/AKT/mTOR also reversed the anti-senescence effects of UCMSC-Exos. The reduction in SA-β-gal-positive cells achieved by exosome treatment was reversed upon co-treatment with 740 Y-P (Fig. 6I), indicating inhibition of UCMSC-Exos’ anti-senescence effects. Similarly, significant upregulation of senescence-associated proteins (p53, p21, and p16) was observed in the combination treatment group compared to exosome treatment alone (Fig. 6J, M, N and O). Importantly, the combination treatment group showed accumulated p62 and decreased LC3II/LC3I ratio (Fig. 6J-L), demonstrating suppression of exosome-induced autophagy that likely diminished the anti-senescence effects mediated by the exosomes. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that UCMSC-Exos may restore cellular vitality in senescent JBMMSCs through PI3K/AKT/mTOR-mediated autophagy activation.

UCMSC-Exos enhanced new bone formation in aged rat calvarial defects

To investigate the therapeutic potential of UCMSC-Exos in bone repair, the rat calvarial defect models were established, and membranes containing exosomes or PBS were implanted into the defect area (Fig. 7A). All animals recovered well from the surgery. High-resolution micro-CT scans were performed at 4 weeks post-implantation to evaluate new bone formation. The results showed that the young group exhibited the fastest bone healing rate. In the aged group, the side without exosome treatment showed only minimal new bone formation, while the side implanted with the membrane containing 50 µg exosomes showed significantly more new bone formation at the calvarial defect site (Fig. 7B). Analysis revealed that the Young-vehicle group had the highest BV/TV (24.05 ± 6.28%), while the Aged-vehicle group had the lowest BV/TV (9.45 ± 3.87%). The Aged + Exo group showed a significant increase in BV/TV (13.83 ± 3.07%,) (Fig. 7C). Similar trends were observed for bone coverage area (Fig. 7D). The Young-vehicle group demonstrated the highest bone coverage (82.3 ± 5.1%), while the Aged-vehicle group showed the lowest (28.6 ± 3.9%). Notably, UCMSC-Exos treatment restored bone coverage in aged rats to 52.4 ± 4.2% (p < 0.01 vs. Aged-vehicle). These findings indicated that UCMSC-Exos could enhance bone repair in bone defects.

Histological analysis demonstrated improved bone repair with UCMSC-Exos treatment

Subsequently, H&E staining and Masson’s trichrome staining were performed to evaluate the bone regeneration quality. Consistent with micro-CT results, young rats demonstrated the greatest new bone formation. In aged rats, PBS-treated sites showed the lowest osteogenic capacity, whereas exosome-treated sites displayed significantly enhanced bone maturation. In the H&E images, newly regenerated bone can be observed through the deeper light red-stained areas, and the blue-purple color highlights the nuclei of the cells. The area of new bone and the presence of newly formed cells can be clearly visualized in all three groups (Fig. 8A-B). The young group displayed the most advanced bone healing, with extensive new bone formation surrounded by minimal connective tissue. In contrast, the Aged-vehicle group exhibited markedly delayed bone regeneration. However, exosome treatment significantly improved bone repair, as evidenced by an increased number of new bone formation areas and a higher density of osteocytes embedded in deep lacunae. These findings suggest that exosomes could accelerate bone regeneration and maturation. Masson’s trichrome staining revealed newly formed bone in all groups (Fig. 8C). In aged rats, the Aged-vehicle side had the least new bone formation and fewer red-stained areas, indicating low bone maturity. In contrast, the exosome-treated side exhibited more osteoid deposition, with the red-stained areas increasing, indicating superior bone repair and maturation.

Discussion

The global demographic shift towards an aging population presents a formidable healthcare challenge. According to the World Health Organization’s 2024 projections, the global population aged 65 years or older is expected to nearly double from 17 to 33% between 2024 and 2054, indicating accelerated population aging worldwide [42]. This aging trend is particularly concerning given its strong association with multiple chronic conditions including cardiovascular diseases [43, 44], neurodegenerative disorders [45, 46], and skeletal disorders [47, 48], all of which significantly compromise quality of life in elderly populations.

Bone represents a remarkably dynamic organ, characterized by a rigid cortical exterior housing a highly active bone marrow compartment. The bone marrow niche contains diverse immune cells, stem cells, osteoprogenitors, and an intricate network of cytokines and growth factors that regulate tissue homeostasis [49]. Under physiological conditions, MSCs maintain a quiescent state, but rapidly activate in response to biological signals following tissue injury, undergoing proliferation and subsequent recruitment to damaged sites for tissue regeneration [50]. However, with increasing age, the function of the skeleton deteriorates, and cellular senescence within the bone reduces its regenerative capacities [51–53]. The age-related changes underscore the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies targeting aging processes in bone.

Recent research has increasingly recognized exosomes as critical mediators of intercellular communication, particularly in modulating aging processes and tissue regeneration. Among these, UCMSC-Exos have demonstrated remarkable therapeutic potential due to their diverse cargo of regulatory molecules (e.g., miR-21-5p, miR-17-5p, and miR-199a-3p). These molecules exhibit multifaceted biological activities, including inflammation suppression [54], cellular senescence amelioration [55], and tissue repair enhancement [56]. In this study, we investigated the rejuvenating effects of UCMSC-Exos on senescent JBMMSCs and in the calvarial defect models of aged rats, as well as the underlying mechanisms. Our findings revealed that UCMSC-Exos significantly alleviated the senescence level of JBMMSCs derived from aged rats and enhanced their proliferation, migration, and osteogenic capabilities. In vivo experiments demonstrated that UCMSC-Exos significantly promoted bone repair in aged rats.

Specifically, SA-β-gal staining revealed that UCMSC-Exos could ameliorate the senescence phenotype of senescent JBMMSCs. RT-qPCR and western blotting results confirmed the downregulation of senescence-associated genes and proteins, including p16, p21, and p53. Furthermore, analysis of inflammatory factors and growth factors showed that UCMSC-Exos treatment decreased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and TNF-α) and increased IGF-1 expression. These coordinated changes in senescence markers, inflammatory mediators, and growth factors collectively demonstrate the multimodal anti-senescence effects of UCMSC-Exos on aged JBMMSCs. Further assessment of cellular capabilities showed that, following treatment with UCMSC-Exos, CCK8 assays indicated enhanced proliferative capacity of senescent JBMMSCs, scratch assays demonstrated improved migratory ability, and alizarin red S and ALP staining results suggested increased osteogenic potential. RT-qPCR and western blotting analyses also revealed upregulation of osteogenesis-related genes and proteins, further supporting the notion that UCMSC-Exos promote osteogenesis in senescent JBMMSCs. Importantly, we found that UCMSC-Exos treatment enhanced autophagy in senescent JBMMSCs. By inhibiting autophagosome-lysosome fusion using chloroquine (CQ), we observed that exosome treatment induced LC3II accumulation in senescent JBMMSCs, demonstrating that exosomes enhance autophagic flux rather than impair autophagic degradation. To further validate the essential role of autophagy activation in UCMSC-Exos-mediated functional recovery of senescent JBMMSCs, we utilized 3-MA, a specific Class III PI3K inhibitor that blocks autophagosome formation. 3-MA treatment effectively suppressed UCMSC-Exos-induced autophagy, and this inhibition reversed the anti-senescence effects of UCMSC-Exos, as evidenced by increased SA-β-gal-positive cells and elevated expression of senescence markers (P53, P21, and P16) compared to cells treated with UCMSC-Exos alone. Collectively, these findings elucidated that UCMSC-Exos may alleviate the senescence phenotype by promoting autophagy in senescent JBMMSCs. It is well established that autophagy declines with age, leading to impaired protein homeostasis [57]. Conversely, enhancing the compromised autophagy in senescent cells can rejuvenate cells and even extend lifespan [58–60]. UCMSC-Exos may similarly bolster JBMMSCs’ functionality by enhancing autophagy.

To further explore the molecular mechanism underlying UCMSC-Exos’ effects on senescent JBMMSCs, we performed RNA sequencing to examine the gene expression patterns in senescent JBMMSCs treated with UCMSC-Exos. The results showed significant changes in the expression of multiple genes following exosome treatment. Bioinformatics analysis indicated that these differentially expressed genes were involved in the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Considering the relationship between the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and autophagy, as well as its important role in senescence [26–28, 61–63], we speculate that the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway may play a role in UCMSC-Exos promoting autophagy to restore the vitality of senescent JBMMSCs. Western blotting results showed that the expression levels of p-PI3K, p-AKT, and p-mTOR were excessively activated in senescent cells, while these protein levels were significantly reduced after exosome treatment. It has been previously reported that mTOR, positively regulated by PI3K, is a negative regulator of autophagy [64]. In this study, exosomes may promote autophagy levels by downregulating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. Furthermore, co-treatment with the PI3K agonist 740 Y-P abolished exosome-mediated downregulation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and subsequent autophagy activation. This intervention significantly attenuated the anti-aging effects of exosomes, providing evidence that UCMSC-Exos rejuvenate senescent JBMMSCs by inducing PI3K/AKT/mTOR-mediated autophagy.

Beyond autophagy, growth factors and inflammation related pathways are intimately linked to the senescence process and aging-related bone regeneration disorders [65, 66]. In line with this, our observations of reduced IL-6 and TNF-α levels, coupled with elevated IGF-1 expression, together with the enrichment of NF-κB and TNF signaling pathways in KEGG analysis, point to additional potential mechanisms. These observations suggest that, beyond the effects on autophagy, UCMSC-Exos may rejuvenate senescent JBMMSCs through a multimodal mechanism involving the modulation of growth factor signaling and inflammatory pathways.

Increased researches showed that exosomes have the potential to restore the function of senescent organisms [19, 22, 35]. In this study, we established the calvarial defect models in aged rats and combined UCMSC-Exos with a clinically approved membrane (MEM) to investigate their potential to enhance bone regeneration in aged rats, aiming to provide an accessible and clinically translational osteogenic regenerative strategy. Prior research has shown that osteogenic extracellular vesicles (Osteo-EVs) possess good affinity with MEM, resulting in significantly enhanced bone regeneration efficacy [37]. Our study revealed that compared to young rats, aged rats exhibited significantly reduced bone repair capabilities. In fact, despite the use of MEM, bone repair in aged rats remained minimal. However, MEM combined with UCMSC-Exos demonstrated superior bone repair effects. Micro-CT results showed that, compared to the side without exosome treatment, the bone defect side treated with UCMSC-Exos in all aged rats exhibited better bone repair effects. H&E staining and Masson’s trichrome staining results also consistently supported that UCMSC-Exos promoted new bone formation in aged rats. Similarly, the UCMSC-Exos treated group exhibited elevated expression of OCN, ALP, OPN and OSX in aged rats. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that UCMSC-Exos could promote bone repair in aged rats, providing a promising direction for the treatment of skeletal diseases in elderly patients.

While our study demonstrated that UCMSC-Exos alleviate the senescence of JBMMSCs and promote their functional recovery, it is important to acknowledge some limitations. For instance, UCMSC-Exos contain many bioactive components, and the specific growth factors or microRNAs secreted by UCMSC-Exos that play a targeted therapeutic role have not yet been clearly identified. The current study has not analyzed the molecular cargo profile of the UCMSC-Exos or evaluated the roles of the components in the UCMSC-Exos. Therefore, it is worth further exploring the mechanisms by which UCMSC-Exos restore the activity of senescent JBMMSCs. Additionally, this study focused exclusively on aged models for evaluating UCMSC-Exos treatment, and thus did not incorporate young treatment groups to assess whether the therapeutic efficacy of UCMSC-Exos is age-dependent. Therefore, systematic investigations are warranted in future comparative studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we demonstrated that UCMSC-Exos could rejuvenate senescent JBMMSCs and enhance their proliferation, migration, and osteogenic differentiation capabilities. This mechanism may be associated with the activation of autophagy and the regulation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. Our in vivo experiments revealed that UCMSC-Exos promoted bone repair in aged rats. Our study indicated that UCMSC-Exos hold potential therapeutic applications in anti-aging treatments and regenerative medicine and may serve as a novel cell-free therapeutic tool for treating age-related skeletal diseases.
