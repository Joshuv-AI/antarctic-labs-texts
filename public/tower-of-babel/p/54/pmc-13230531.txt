A single-nucleus RNA-seq dataset of the colon in Pink1-deficient and wild-type mice
Authors: Muhammad Junaid, Soo Jung Park, Yiseul Bae, Eun Jeong Lee, Su Bin Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PTEN-induced kinase 1 (Pink1), a familial Parkinson’s associated gene, is a key regulator of mitochondrial and cellular energy homeostasis. Mutations in Pink1 disrupt mitophagy and perturbations in gastrointestinal homeostasis. This suggests the possibility that Pink1 deficiency may influence neurodegenerative processes by altering gut-to-brain signaling mechanisms. To facilitate investigation of gut-specific consequences of Pink1 deficiency, we generated a single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) dataset from gut tissue of wild-type (WT) and Pink1 knockout (KO) mice. We identified major cell populations such as goblet cells, immune cells, and colonocytes, and characterized their transcriptional profiles. For technical validation, we utilized a publicly available murine gut (snRNA-seq) dataset. We then applied anchor-based label transfer and confirmed cell-type assignments via random forest classification. This rigorously validated dataset provides a robust resource for exploring shifts in cell-type composition and transcriptional alterations associated with Pink1 loss.

Background & Summary

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by both motor and non-motor symptoms1–3. Among the earliest non-motor manifestations are gastrointestinal (GI) disturbances, which can precede motor dysfunction by decades4,5. This temporal gap has created intense interest in the gut-brain axis as a contributor to PD pathogenesis. In this context, PTEN-induced kinase 1 (Pink1), a gene linked to early-onset PD, is a key regulator of mitochondrial quality control and cellular energetics6,7; mutations or loss of Pink1 disrupt mitophagy and alter the cellular landscape of the GI tract8–10. Notably, Pink1−/− mice show limited spontaneous neurodegenerative or motor phenotypes under baseline conditions, suggesting that additional factors may be required for disease manifestation11. Environmental triggers, such as intestinal infection, can unmask PD-like features in Pink1−/− mice through immune-related mechanisms, including mitochondrial antigen presentation and cytotoxic T-cell responses12. These observations underscore the importance of defining the intestinal landscape of Pink1 deficiency at baseline, prior to the emergence of overt disease-related changes.

To investigate gut-specific consequences of Pink1 deficiency, we generated single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) profiles from intestinal tissue of wild-type (WT) and Pink1 knockout (KO) mice. Differential gene expression analysis was performed by comparing all Pink1 KO cells collectively to all wild-type cells, identifying the top upregulated transcripts in Pink1 KO cells (Fig. 2a), and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment highlighted apoptotic and programmed cell-death related pathways as significantly overrepresented (Fig. 2b,c). Furthermore, at the dataset level, we examined the expression of the innate immune marker Cd14 and the B cell lineage marker Ms4a1 (Cd20)15,16, using average log-normalized expression and the percentage of expressing cells per group (Fig. 2d). While Pink1 expression was reduced in knockout samples as expected, Cd14 and Ms4a1 expression levels were comparable between WT and Pink1 KO tissues. Next, rank-based gene set enrichment analyses (GSEA) revealed strong positive enrichment for cell differentiation, cellular developmental processes, and responses to stimuli in Pink1 KO (Fig. 2e). In contrast, negatively enriched processes showed weaker, non-significant trends, including metabolism and immune-related terms (Supplementary Fig. S1e). Overall, it highlights that mitochondrial dysfunctions driven by Pink1 deficiency can lead to metabolic and immune-related reprogramming in the colon. To further characterize the transcriptional consequences of Pink1 KO in stem cells and colonocytes, we performed the cell-type-specific differential expression analyses (Fig. 3a,b). Comparison of significantly up- and down-regulated genes identified a subset of shared differentially expressed genes (DEGs) regulated in the same direction, such as Egr1, Fos, and Txnip (Fig. 3c). Correlation and heatmap plot of common DEGs demonstrate a concordant fold-change pattern between stem cells and colonocytes (Fig. 3d,e). GO enrichment analyses revealed that upregulated pathways in both populations were enriched for the regulation of phosphorylation, kinase activity, and metabolic processes, while downregulated genes included processes associated with circadian rhythm and hormone signaling, particularly in colonocytes (Fig. 3f,g). Methods

Mice

Pink1 knockout (KO) mice (C57BL/6 background) were generously provided by Prof. Xiaoxi Zhuang (University of Chicago, Chicago, IL, USA)20. Fifteen-week-old male KO mice were used, with age- and sex-matched wild-type (WT) littermates as controls. Animals were maintained in a specific-pathogen-free facility on a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. All procedures were approved by the Ajou University School of Medicine IACUC. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. This study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Experimental detail

Isolation of single nuclei

Mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation. The colon was detached from mesenteric tissue, flushed with ice-cold PBS, opened longitudinally, and minced. Nuclei isolation was performed with minor modifications to a published protocol21. Briefly, tissue pieces were immediately transferred to nuclei isolation buffer (0.25 M sucrose, 25 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM Tris-HCl, dithiothreitol, 0.1% Triton), micro-dissected, and then Dounce-homogenized. The homogenate was passed through a 40-µm cell strainer and centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 8 min at 4 °C. After removing the supernatant, the nuclei pellet was resuspended in 500 µL of 0.5% BSA and filtered a second time through a 40-µm cell strainer. Nuclei were stained with SYTOX Green (Invitrogen), and single nuclei were sorted by flow cytometry (FACSAria III; BD Biosciences) directly into tubes pre-filled with ice-cold methanol, then immediately stored at −80 °C. FACS-based sorting was employed to enrich for intact singlet nuclei and to remove debris and aggregates, thereby improving sample purity and reducing ambient RNA contamination.

Library preparation and sequencing

For each genotype, FACS-sorted single nuclei from the colon tissues of three mice were pooled to generate one sample for analysis. The pooled nuclei were then loaded into a single channel of the Chromium Controller (10x Genomics) using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit (v2 chemistry), targeting a recovery of ~10,000 nuclei. Libraries were prepared with the Next GEM Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit v3.1 (Dual Index) following the manufacturer’s instructions: after GEM-RT cleanup, cDNA was amplified (14 cycles), enzymatically fragmented, end-repaired, A-tailed, ligated to adapters, and indexed by PCR (Dual Index Kit TT Set A, PN-1000215). Library size distribution was assessed on an Agilent Bioanalyzer High Sensitivity DNA chip. Libraries were sequenced by Macrogen (Seoul, Korea) on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten in paired-end mode, using a 10x-compatible configuration (R1 28, i7 10, i5 10, R2 90).

Single-nucleus RNA-seq data processing and analysis

Each dataset was processed using Seurat (v5.3.0) in R (v4.2.1). After loading the data into R, we created a Seurat object and annotated metadata to distinguish WT from Pink1 KO samples. The datasets were then merged, yielding a total of 21,154 nuclei before quality control (QC) filtering. Nuclei with >10% mitochondrial gene content and with fewer than 200 or more than 3,000 detected features were removed to exclude low-quality cells and potential doublets (Supplementary Fig. S1a, c). After QC, 20,981 nuclei were retained for downstream analysis, including 8,856 nuclei from WT and 12,122 from Pink1 KO. The merged dataset was LogNormalize with a scale factor of 10,000, and highly variable features were identified using the “vst” method by selecting the top 2,000 variable features. The first 20 principal components were used to construct a shared nearest neighbor graph (FindNeighbors). Then we performed clustering with the Louvain algorithm at a resolution of 0.2, followed by t-SNE dimensionality reduction. We applied Seurat’s “IntegrateLayers” function with canonical correlation analysis (CCA) to correct for batch effects and identify shared cell populations across samples (Supplementary Fig. S1b, d). Differential gene expression between WT and Pink1 KO was performed using the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Initially, cell type annotation was performed based on the canonical markers, then label transfer was conducted using a published male mouse colon reference dataset (Drokhlyansky et al.). Although FACS allows for the precise isolation of single nuclei, we recognize that the physical stress of sorting, such as shear forces, can sometimes impact more fragile cell populations. To ensure our data remained unbiased, we compared the cellular makeup of our wild-type samples against the reference dataset after label transfer analysis.

Functional enrichment analyses

Gene ontology (GO) functional enrichment was performed using clusterProfiler::enrichGO (biological process (BP); OrgDb = org.Mm.eg.db) on significantly up- and down-regulated genes at adjusted p < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.25, using all the detected genes as the universe. Multiple testing correction was performed using Benjamini-Hochberg, with FDR < 0.05. Furthermore, GSEA was performed using GO (BP) terms on all genes ranked by log2 fold-change between WT and Pink1 KO. Biological pathways with minGSSize = 10, maxGSSize = 500, BH correction, and FDR < 0.05 were considered significant. Enrichment direction was interpreted by normalized enrichment score (NES): NED > 0 indicated enrichment among genes upregulated in Pink1 KO, and NES < 0 indicates the enrichment among genes downregulated in Pink1 KO.

Data Records

Raw and processed snRNA-seq data for WT and Pink1 have been deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus22 (GEO; accession GSE310832). This repository includes raw FASTQ files and gene-by-cell count matrices files with sample metadata. Meanwhile, the processed Seurat objects used in this study, including the filtered and integrated RDS files (WT_Pink1KO.rds) and the label-transferred validated object (Validation_WT_Pink1KO.rds) generated using the public reference dataset, are available from figshare (10.6084/m9.figshare.30702425.v1). These files contain the actual and validated cell types used for annotation in downstream analyses. Both files are well-structured and support the reusability for downstream analyses.

Technical Validation

Benchmarking with reference dataset

To benchmark the technical validity of our lab-generated snRNA-seq dataset (WT and Pink1 KO, male), we used a published reference, the male mouse colon atlas from Drokhlyansky et al.17. Raw count matrices from the mouse colon reference were loaded with ReadMtx and converted to a Seurat object. We merged author-supplied metadata (all.meta.txt) by cell barcode and subset to male samples (Sex = “M”). Cells were kept if present in both the matrix and metadata tables. Cell-type labels used the provided annotation field (reference identities), and t-SNE were computed for visualization (Fig. 2a)

Anchor-based label transfer and validation

Our WT and Pink1 KO colon snRNA-seq object (wt_pink1ko) and the male-colon reference were normalized and feature-selected in parallel. We computed anchors with FindTransferAnchors (normalization.method = “LogNormalize”, reduction = “pcaproject”, reference.reduction = “pca”) and projected/query-mapped labels with MapQuery. This yielded predicted cell-type identities and per-cell prediction scores for all nuclei (Fig. 4b,c). A histogram of label-transfer prediction scores confirmed strong transcriptional similarity between reference and query cell types (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. S2). Finally, we trained a supervised random forest (RF) classifier on reference data and compared its predictions against the Seurat-transferred labels in the query datasets (Fig. 4e). High agreement between RF-predicted and transferred annotations further confirms the robustness of our label-transfer strategy.

Further to evaluate the accuracy of each label, we also summarized per-class recall, showing that all transferred cell-type labels achieved recall values above 0.75 (Fig. 5a). We then identified the most informative genes for classification using two important metrics, mean decrease accuracy (MDA) and mean decrease Gini (MDG), which highlight biologically plausible markers (Fig. 5b,c). We also quantified the relative abundance of transferred subtypes within major compartments and compared the WT with Pink1 KO (Fig. 5d). Interestingly, we observed an immune shift in Pink1 KO characterized by macrophage enrichment (+14.3 pp; 44.8% to 59.2%) and B-cell depletion (−15.6 pp; 33.7% to 18.2%), with T cells largely stable (+1.2 pp). Furthermore, the stem-like fraction (Stem_1/Stem_2) was reduced in Pink1 KO (−7.7; 49.2% to 41.5%), consistent with a shift away from a progenitor-like state. Next, to quantify the accuracy of our manual annotation, we compared predicted reference cell types and subtypes to our marker-based cell identities using high-confidence predictions (score ≥ 0.5). The resulting confusion matrix shows high row-normalized agreement between our manual labels and the reference-predicted identities (Supplementary Fig. S3a). Furthermore, we also confirmed that cells assigned by label transfer maintain the expression of canonical markers shown in Fig. 1e, ensuring that technical validation aligns with the biological identity (Supplementary Fig. S3b). For example, predicted immune clusters (macrophages, B_cells, and T_cells) specifically expressed Ptprc and Lyz2, while predicted stromal population (fibroblasts, myocyte_1) expressed Col1a1, Acta2, and Pdgfra.
