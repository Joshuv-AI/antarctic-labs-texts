Components of the Atrial fibrillation Better Care pathway for holistic care of patients with atrial fibrillation: a win ratio analysis from the COOL-AF registry
Authors: Rungroj Krittayaphong, Sukrit Treewaree, Gregory Y H Lip
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsCompliance with integrated care based on the Atrial fibrillation Better Care (ABC) pathway has been associated with improved clinical outcomes. The primary objective of this study was to compare clinical outcomes of AF patients according to the compliant status of each component of the ABC pathway in a hierarchical win ratio approach.Methods and resultsWe studied AF patients in the COOL-AF registry. Each patient was followed every 6 months until 3 years. A win ratio analysis was performed, as not all clinical outcomes are equivalent. The hierarchical outcomes were (1) all-cause death, (2) intracranial haemorrhage (ICH), (3) ischaemic stroke/systemic embolism, (4) non-ICH major bleedings, and (5) acute myocardial infarction or heart failure. We also assessed win ratio and win proportion variance over the follow-up time, and the variations over time. A total of 3405 patients (mean age 67.8 ± 11.3; 41.8% female) were studied. Win ratio of ABC-compliant (all three components) vs. ABC-not-compliant was 1.57 (1.35–1.83), P < 0.001. When adding time in therapeutic range (TTR) data for compliant criteria for those who were on warfarin, the win ratio increased to 2.28 (1.89–2.75), P < 0.001. The A-compliant group (plus TTR data), B-compliant, and C-compliant had the win ratio of 1.81 (1.51–2.12), 1.82 (1.53–2.16), and 1.39 (1.18–1.62), all P < 0.001, compared to not compliant group.ConclusionManagement of AF patients according to each component of the ABC pathway is associated with better clinical outcomes compared to those non-compliant to ABC pathway. This finding underscores the importance of a holistic management approach strategy for AF patients.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is one of the conditions leading to a high rate of mortality and morbidity worldwide.1 Despite using oral anticoagulants (OAC) for stroke prevention, a significant proportion of AF patients still develop clinical complications associated with AF or adverse outcomes related to treatment.2 Bleeding complications tend to be more frequent in Asians compared to non-Asians.3,4 Non-cardiovascular cause of death contributes approximately half of all deaths.5 Besides, AF patients also have an increased risk of cardiovascular complications, such as heart failure.6

Given the need to manage AF patients, guidelines have advocated a holistic or integrated care approach, based on the Atrial fibrillation Better Care (ABC) pathway.7,8 The ABC pathway comprises the following7: A: avoid stroke with anticoagulation; B: better symptom and atrial fibrillation management with patient-centred, symptom directed decisions on rate control or rhythm control; and C: cardiovascular risk factor and comorbidity management, including attention to lifestyle factors and psychological morbidity. Management adherent to the ABC pathway has been shown to reduce the risk of AF related complications.9–12

Since the significance and impact of each clinical outcome are not the same, we believed that the analysis of the impact of a treatment strategy should be in a hierarchical order.13 Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to compare clinical outcomes of AF patients according to the compliant status of each component of the ABC pathway as well as all ABC components combined. The secondary objective was to perform sensitivity analysis with the inclusion of a time in therapeutic range (TTR) ≥ 65% criterion for those who were taking warfarin as a measure of the A-compliant criteria.

Methods

Study population

We studied AF patients in the COhort of antithrombotic use and Optimal INR Level in patients with non-valvular atrial fibrillation in Thailand (COOL-AF) registry. Patients in the COOL-AF registry were aged older than 18 years with AF documented by 12-lead ECG or ambulatory monitoring. The following conditions were excluded: (1) rheumatic mitral valve disease, (2) mechanical heart valves, (3) cannot come for follow-up, (4) not willing to participate, (5) life expectancy < 3 years, (6) AF from transient and reversible cause, (7) haematologic disease that increased risk of bleeding, (8) ischaemic stroke within 3 months, and (9) current hospitalization. The study was approved by the Central Research Ethic Committee. All patients provided written informed consent before the participation. All methods were conducted in accordance with the principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Conference on Harmonization for Good Clinical Practice Guidelines.

Data collection

We collected the following baseline data; age, sex, weight, height, underlying disease such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidaemia, smoking, history of coronary artery disease (CAD), coronary revascularization, ischaemic stroke or transient ischaemic attack (TIA), heart failure, chronic kidney disease, type and duration of AF, AF symptoms, vital signs, physical examination data, ECG data, laboratory data such as fasting plasma glucose, haemoglobin A1C, lipid profile, serum creatinine, alanine aminotransferase, international normalized ratio (INR) for those who were on warfarin, and echocardiographic data. The data were recorded in the web-based system. Data verification was performed at the central site. Queries were sent to the investigators of each site for clarification of data whenever needed.

We collected follow-up data every 6 month until 3 years. Clinical outcomes during follow-up were recorded including death, cause of death, ischaemic stroke/systemic embolism (SSE), bleeding, myocardial infarction, and heart failure. We encourage the site staff of all study sites to make attempt to complete the follow-up data. The follow-up data were retrieved from the data in the medical record. We also performed telephone follow-up every 6 month to confirm the data in the medical record and to verify the status of the patients if the follow-up data are missing.

Each component of the CHA2DS2-VASc score was scored and recorded as C = congestive heart failure (1 point); H = hypertension (1 point); A = age ≥ 75 years (2 points); D = diabetes mellitus (1 point); S = stroke or TIA (2 points); V = vascular disease (1 point); A = age 65–74 years (1 point); and Sc = female sex (1 point). The HAS-BLED score was calculated according to the method described by Pisters et al.14 In patients treated with warfarin, the TTR < 65% was defined as labile INR. History of prior stroke, prior bleeding, and drug or alcohol use was self-reported by the patients. Patients with HAS-BLED score ≥ 3 were classified as high bleeding risk.14 ABC pathway data were collected according to its original description.7 ‘A’ for avoid stroke is defined by the appropriate use of OAC; ‘B’ for better symptom management as defined by the European Heart Rhythm Association (EHRA) symptom scale of no more than 2; and ‘C’ for cardiovascular and comorbidity management is defined the appropriate use of medications for hypertension, CAD, ischaemic stroke, heart failure, and diabetes (Figure 1). European Heart Rhythm Association symptom scale15 was recorded at baseline and follow-up. For follow-up data, EHRA was recorded during the clinic visit or by telephone.

Description of study population.

Outcomes

The hierarchical outcomes were (1) all-cause death, (2) intracranial haemorrhage (ICH), (3) ischaemic SSE, (4) non-ICH major bleedings, and (5) acute myocardial infarction or heart failure. Ischaemic stroke was defined as acute onset of focal neurological deficit that lasted more than 24 h for ischaemic stroke, and <24 h for TIA. Whether positive or negative, imaging data from computerized tomography brain scan or magnetic resonance imaging were required to be uploaded into the web-based system. We used major bleeding criteria from the definition of the International Society of Thrombosis and Haemostasis criteria.16 ICH was defined as the bleeding within the skull that could be intracerebral bleeds, subarachnoid bleeds, subdural bleeds, and epidural bleeds. Microbleeds and secondary haemorrhagic transformation were not included.17 Heart failure event was defined as a hospital admission or a presentation of the patient for an urgent, unscheduled visit, with a primary diagnosis of heart failure, whereby the patient exhibits new or worsening symptoms of heart failure on presentation, has objective evidence of new or worsening heart failure, and receives initiation or intensification of treatment specifically for heart failure.18

The source document was uploaded into the web-based system for the verification of clinical outcomes. All source documents, including imaging for those who had stroke or TIA, were sent to the adjudication committee for confirmation of the clinical outcome.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of patients were presented as means and standard deviation for continuous data, and frequency and percentages for categorical variables, respectively. The incidence rates of each clinical outcome were presented as incidence rate per 100 person-years with a Poisson 95% confidence interval (CI) and a two-sided P-value.

Win ratio is an emerging concept for analysing composite outcome in clinical trials, which focuses on the severity of the outcome and timing of occurrence instead of solely relying on time-to-first event in conventional analysis.13,19 We performed win ratio analysis by comparing the group with compliant to each category of ABC pathway, namely A-compliant vs. A-not-compliant, B-compliant vs. B-not-compliant, and C-compliant vs. C-not-compliant. Then, we compared compliance of the three components, i.e. ABC-compliant vs. ABC-not-compliant.

For the comparisons between A-compliant vs. A-not-compliant, all patients with A-compliant group were paired with all patients with A-not-compliant group. In each pair, the A-compliant group was counted as a winner when a patient in the A-not-compliant group developed all-cause death before the A-compliant group. In contrast, the A-compliant group was considered a loser if a patient in this group developed all-cause death first. If neither of these conditions were met, we assessed the next outcome in the prespecified hierarchical order until we determined the winner or loser. The sequence of the hierarchical outcomes tested was as follows: (1) all-cause death, (2) intracranial haemorrhage, (3) SSE, (4) non-ICH major bleedings, and (5) acute myocardial infarction or heart failure. If the winner or loser could not be determined, the pair was a tie. The win ratio was calculated by dividing the number of win pairs by the number of lose pairs. The value of win ratio > 1.00 indicated that the A-compliant group had better outcomes compared to the A-not-compliant group. Win ratio, 95% CI, two-sided P-value, and numbers of wins, losses, and ties were calculated with WINS package in R.20 The analysis of B-compliant vs. B-not-compliant, C-compliant vs. C-not-compliant, and ABC-compliant (compliant of the three components) vs. ABC-not-compliant was performed in a similar manner.

Sensitivity analysis was performed by (1) adding TTR ≥ 65% in the A-compliant (and ABC-compliant) criteria for those who were on warfarin before the win ratio analysis. Time in therapeutic range was calculated by the Rosendaal method21; (2) win ratio analysis comparing patients with ABC-compliance of 1, 2, and 3 criteria to 0 criterion compliant group; (3) adding conventional analysis of ‘survival free from composite outcomes’ and ‘survival free from death’ with Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank test and hazard ratio and 95% CI from the Cox model; and (4) adding the analysis on the time updated models to adjusted for the changes in A-, B-, C-, and ABC-compliant during the follow-up period. This is to test whether the changes in ABC-compliant status during the follow-up period on the results of the win ratio analysis. By doing this, we reset the group according the changes on ABC-compliant status during the follow-up period and run the win ratio analysis.

Win ratio and win proportion could vary over the follow-up time as demonstrated by Oakes23 and Finkelstein and Schoenfeld.22 Specifically, the beneficial effect of the treatment that contributes to wins may be driven by different outcomes at each time point in the study period. The win proportion of a group was defined as the number of wins for that group divided by the total number of pairs.24 We used WIN package to calculate win ratio and win proportion for each group over the follow-up time to investigate the changes in the contribution to wins by each outcome. All analyses were done using R version 4.2.3 (www.r-project.org), IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and MedCalc Statistical Software version 20 (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium).

Results

A total of 3405 patients [mean age 67.8 ± 11.3; 1424 (41.8%) were female] were studied. A number of patients with A-compliant, B-compliant, C-compliant, and ABC-compliant were 2561 (77.9%), 2608 (76.6%), 2345 (68.9%), and 1453 (42.7%), respectively. Outcomes

The median follow-up duration was 35.9 (Interquartile range 34.8, 36.0) months. A number of patients who developed all-cause death, intracranial haemorrhage, SSE, non-ICH major bleedings, and acute myocardial infarction or heart failure were 380 (11.16%), 70 (2.06%), 134 (3.94%), 129 (3.79%), and 275 (8.08%) patients, respectively. The incidence rate of all-cause death, intracranial haemorrhage, SSE, non-ICH major bleedings, and acute myocardial infarction or heart failure was 4.21 (3.80–4.65), 0.78 (0.61–0.98), 1.51 (1.26–1.78), 1.45 (1.21–1.73), and 3.17 (2.80–3.56) per 100 person-years, respectively.

Win ratio analysis

Figure 2 demonstrates the main analysis of the win ratio. All patients in the A-compliant group were paired with all patients in the A-non-compliant group, resulting in 2651 × 754 = 1 998 854 matched pairs. The A-compliant group had a total of 348 394 wins (17.4%), while experiencing 365 249 losses (18.3%) and 1 285 211 ties (64.3%). The win ratio was 0.95 (95% CI: 0.80–1.14; P 0.600), with all-cause death as a major contributor to wins (53.3%). Additional analysis was performed by adding TTR ≥ 65% for those who were on warfarin as a definition of A-compliant, demonstrating that the A-compliant group (plus TTR > 65% data) had more wins (21.5%) than losses (11.9%) resulting in a win ratio of 1.81 (1.51–2.12), P < 0.001. The win ratio of B-compliant vs. B-not-compliant and C-compliant vs. C-not-compliant was 1.82 (1.53–2.16), P < 0.001 and 1.39 (1.18–1.62), P < 0.001, respectively.

Sensitivity analysis that was performed for the comparisons of number of compliant factors vs. not compliant to any of the three factors showed that as the number of compliant factors increased from 1, to 2, and to 3 compliant factors, the win ratio increased indicating that the compliance to each component contributes the incremental benefit in the reduction of clinical outcomes (Figure 5). Additionally, the quality of A-compliance as indicated by adding the TTR data was also a major contribution to wins over losses (Figure 5).

Discussion

The results of this prospective multicentre nationwide study of AF patients demonstrate that the management according to the ABC pathway based on the hierarchical outcomes of death, ICH, SSE, non-ICH major bleeding, and myocardial infarction or heart failure had more number of wins than losses with a significant win ratio. Secondly, quality of INR control has to be considered by adding TTR ≥ 65% into the criteria of A-compliant. Thirdly, compliance to each component of the ABC pathway had an incremental value for the benefit of the win ratio.

Although many physicians perceive that giving OAC to reduce ischaemic stroke risk is the most important strategy for AF management, a residual risk of adverse outcomes remains even amongst anticoagulated AF patients.25 Hence, the need to go beyond OAC alone and the need for an integrated care approach have been shown to be associated with improved clinical outcomes.26–28 One meta-analysis has also shown the similar direction of benefit of ABC pathway.11 Despite the main focus of AF management is to consider stroke prevention strategy, the holistic care using the ABC pathway provides an integration of not only stroke prevention in the A component, but also the B component of better symptom management and C component of cardiovascular risk factor and comorbidity management into consideration as well.29 It has been shown that compliance to the ABC pathway had a better outcome compared to the non-compliant group.11,30

Despite the main analysis of our study was in a similar direction of the report from previous studies on the ABC pathway, we have added many novel findings. The conventional reporting of composite endpoints in clinical studies has an inherent limitation in that it emphasizes the first event, which may be the outcome of lesser clinical importance. The win ratio for reporting composite endpoints can overcome this problem, is easy to use, and gives priority to the more clinically important event such as death.13,19 The win ratio analysis of our report confirms the benefit of ABC pathway compliance, even extending to each component of the ABC pathway.

However, quality of anticoagulation control using the TTR for patients who were on warfarin contributes to the benefit on the hierarchical outcomes compared to the non-compliant group with the win ratio of the win ratio of 1.81 (1.51–2.12), P < 0.001. If we do not consider the TTR factor, A-compliance did not demonstrate a benefit over the not compliant group with a win ratio of 0.95 (95% CI: 0.80–1.14; P = 0.600). This finding emphasizes the results from the previous studies that TTR should be used to guide management in patients receiving warfarin to improve clinical outcomes.31,32 Since Asian AF patients tend to have a poorer TTR compared to non-Asians,32,33 attempts should be made to improve quality of warfarin use in Asian population.

There were some important findings from our study. First, each component of the ABC pathway (A, B, and C) had an independent value for the benefit according to the win ratio analysis. The win ratio of A-compliant (plus TTR) and B-compliant was relatively similar [1.81 (1.51–2.12), P < 0.001 and 1.82 (1.53–2.16), P < 0.001] and higher than C-compliant [1.39 (1.18–1.62, P < 0.001]. Secondly, quality of the ABC management may be of more important than just receiving treatment alone as reflected by the win ratio of A-compliant vs. A-not-compliant [0.95 (95% CI: 0.80–1.14; P 0.600) without TTR data and 1.81 (1.51–2.12), P < 0.001)] with TTR data. Thirdly, each component had an incremental effect on the risk reduction when combined. Fourthly, since findings on the proportion of wins from each outcome of our study indicated that the major contributors of wins in the ABC management group were death and myocardial infarction or heart failure, and the cause of death was non-cardiovascular more than cardiovascular cause,5 we should provide holistic care approach to all AF patients and not to focus on the use of OAC alone.

The profiles of AF population in the COOL-AF registry are similar to the AF population reported from the national data in Thailand34 and similar to the AF population from other reports such as GARFIELD,35 ORBIT-AF,36 and FUSHIMI registry37 in terms of age and sex distribution and the proportion of the underlying disease such as hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidaemia. Our population had a broad range of demographic, reimbursement system, and educational status reflecting AF population in the real-world setting. Average age was 67.8 ± 11.3 years, ranged 21–99 years, and 41.8% were female. The reimbursement systems were civil servant 46.9%, universal coverage 42.8%, social security 4.4%, self-pay 5.2%, and private insurance 0.1%. Education statuses were primary school 47.5%, secondary school 18.8%, university 16.9%, no education 6.5%, and not available 10.5%.

Win ratio analysis has been proposed as the alternative method of the conventional analysis of the Kaplan–Meier curves and the Cox model analysis. The advantages of the win ratio analysis are potentially better way of analysing composite endpoints to account for the hierarchy of clinical significance of their components and the inclusion of recurrent events. Its advantages are counterbalanced by several fallacies such as weighting each hierarchal component equally and ignoring ties.38 In our study, despite we used win ratio as the primary analysis, we also showed conventional analysis by the Kaplan–Meier curves and Forest plot of the Cox model analysis. The results of the conventional analysis also support the findings from the win ratio analysis.

Limitations

There are some limitations of this study. First, this study is from Thailand and the enrolment period was 2014–2017. The main OAC used remains warfarin, which may limit generalizability of the results of this study. Secondly, the participating study sites were university hospitals or large hospitals rather than primary care hospitals. Thus, the results of this study may not reflect AF patient in the primary care setting. Thirdly, there might be some selection bias during the enrolment despite we emphasized to the site investigators the need to enrol consecutively. Fourthly, we did not include weight loss and alcohol/nicotine cessation in our study despite the original description of the ABC pathway mentioned about the behavioural modification. However, it is not clearly stated the weight loss definition for the consideration of compliant or not compliant. For alcohol and cigarette cessation, it is difficult to find the data in the medical record during the follow-up.

Conclusion

Management of AF patients according to each component of the ABC pathway provides better clinical outcomes compared to those non-compliant to ABC pathway, when considering outcomes in a hierarchical win ratio approach. This finding underscores the importance of a holistic management approach strategy for AF patients.
