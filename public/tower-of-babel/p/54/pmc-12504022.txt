The Role of Interventional Radiology in Renal Trauma: A Narrative Review
Authors: Ryoichi Kitamura, Takaaki Maruhashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis review highlights the role of interventional radiology in the management of renal trauma, emphasizing its contributions to imaging-based diagnosis, injury classification, and treatment. It discusses the indications for and clinical outcomes of transcatheter arterial embolization, as well as the management of renal artery dissection and urinary extravasation. Transcatheter arterial embolization has demonstrated favorable outcomes, even in high-grade injuries, and repeat procedures have proven effective for managing rebleeding. Renal artery dissection may be treated with stent placement to preserve renal function. Although urinary extravasation often resolves spontaneously, persistent cases may require ureteral stenting or percutaneous drainage. Additional interventional radiology-based interventions, including embolization and cryoablation, have also been reported. As a minimally invasive, organ-preserving treatment modality, interventional radiology plays a vital role in supporting non-operative management strategies for renal trauma.Keywords: angiography, endovascular procedures, kidney, urinary fistula, stents

Introduction

Renal trauma has an incidence rate of approximately 2.06 cases per 100,000 person-years in Japan [1] and accounts for up to 5% of all trauma cases [2]. In Japan, blunt trauma, typically from motor vehicle accidents, is the most common cause of renal injury [3].

The kidney, as a retroperitoneal organ enclosed by Gerota's fascia, is relatively well protected. Therefore, blunt renal trauma rarely leads to significant hemorrhage or requires emergency surgical intervention [3]. Recent advancements in diagnostic imaging and endovascular techniques have facilitated a shift toward minimally invasive approaches for managing renal trauma [4].

Current treatment approaches for renal trauma include TAE to control bleeding, drainage procedures to manage urine leakage, and surgical intervention when necessary. Notably, the majority of cases can now be managed non-surgically using interventional radiology (IR), underscoring its role in organ preservation [5, 6]. We aimed to provide a comprehensive overview of IR in the management of renal trauma, highlighting its effectiveness, safety, and potential complications.

Methods

This narrative review was conducted to provide a comprehensive overview of the current understanding and management of renal and ureteral injuries, with a particular focus on the role of IR.

A structured literature search was performed using MEDLINE and PubMed databases. The search strategy included the following keywords: “kidney,” “renal trauma,” “ureteral injury,” “trauma,” “embolization,” “renal artery dissection,” and “endovascular therapy.” Articles published from January 1992 to October 2024 were considered, with inclusion limited to studies published in English.

The inclusion criteria for this review encompassed original research articles, review articles, and case reports focusing on renal and ureteral injuries and their management, particularly those involving endovascular or minimally invasive techniques. Studies addressing the treatment of complications such as urinary leakage, as well as the use of embolization or drainage procedures, were also included. Articles focusing solely on unrelated topics were excluded.

Titles and abstracts of the retrieved articles were screened for relevance, and full-text reviews were conducted to confirm eligibility based on the inclusion criteria.

Imaging Evaluation and Classification of Renal Trauma

Contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) is currently considered the gold standard for evaluating renal trauma [7-9]. In 2018, the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) updated its renal injury classification system for the first time in nearly 30 years [10]. The revised classification categorizes renal injuries from grade I to V based on detailed criteria across 3 domains: imaging findings, intraoperative observations, and pathological features (Table 1). Diagnosis is primarily based on imaging findings obtained through CECT. Notably, a thorough evaluation of vascular injuries is essential, and the inclusion of an excretory phase is recommended for a comprehensive assessment.

Previous studies have demonstrated that incorporating a delay of approximately 9 minutes between the portal venous and excretory phases optimizes the detection of urinary extravasation, a critical factor in evaluating renal trauma. However, in hemodynamically unstable patients or those with severe vascular injuries, acquiring excretory phase images during the initial assessment is not necessary, as management should prioritize life-threatening injuries [11].

The following considerations are important when interpreting CECT findings in the evaluation of renal trauma:

•Assessment of both kidneys: Evaluation should include not only the injured kidney but also the contralateral kidney. In patients with bilateral renal injuries, the 2018 AAST classification recommends increasing the injury grade by one level [10, 12].

•Differentiation between contusion and infarction: Contusions tend to have irregular margins, whereas infarcts typically present with well-defined borders [13].

•AVF: While non-traumatic AVFs often resolve spontaneously and can be managed conservatively, trauma-related AVFs are less likely to close spontaneously and, therefore, more frequently require embolization [14].

•Assessment of renal artery occlusion and dissection: While many patients with renal artery occlusion are managed conservatively, early revascularization should be considered when preserving renal function as a clinical priority.

•Hilar vascular injury: Complete transection of the renal artery may lead to retroperitoneal hematoma with active bleeding. In patients with renal hilar laceration, perirenal hematoma may be minimal or absent, necessitating careful evaluation [15].

•Devascularization with active bleeding: The 2018 AAST classification identifies devascularization with active bleeding as a distinct category, separate from non-hemorrhagic Grade IV injuries caused by thrombosis [15].

Management Strategies for Renal Trauma

The management of renal trauma can be broadly categorized into non-operative management (NOM), which includes conservative treatment and selective TAE, and operative management (OM). According to the World Society of Emergency Surgery (WSES)-AAST guidelines [9], OM is indicated in patients with hemodynamic instability, uncontrollable life-threatening hemorrhage with avulsion of the renal pedicle, or renal vein injury with non-self-limiting hemorrhage. Similarly, the European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines [16] recommend laparotomy for Grade V injuries associated with hemodynamic instability or a poor or only transient response to initial fluid resuscitation. In contrast, NOM is generally preferred when hemodynamic stability is maintained. In particular, injuries classified as AAST Grades I to III are typically managed non-operatively [17].

Recent studies [18] comparing TAE with surgery for high-grade renal trauma have demonstrated that TAE is associated with significantly lower mortality rates (adjusted OR: 4.93; p = 0.007) and complication rates in Grade V injuries (p = 0.008). Another comparative study [19] reported complication rates of 6.4% in the TAE group versus 17.2% in the surgical repair (SR) group (p < 0.001). For grade IV injuries, complication rates were 4.2% (TAE) versus 13.7% (SR), and for grade V, 12% (TAE) versus 44% (SR), suggesting that TAE is an effective, kidney-preserving treatment strategy. With advancements in TAE techniques, several recent studies have indicated that even grade IV and V injuries can be managed non-operatively, provided the patient is hemodynamically stable [20-24]. Notably, up to half of grade V blunt renal injuries―including “shattered kidneys” can be successfully treated with NOM, with renal salvage rates as high as 90% [25]. This trend highlights a growing emphasis on organ preservation. Multivariate analyses have also identified age over 55 years and motor vehicle collisions as significant risk factors for NOM failure [25].

In pediatric patients, approximately 94% of renal injuries result from blunt trauma [26]. Children are more susceptible to high-grade injuries than adults because of their relatively less perirenal fat, weaker abdominal musculature, and reduced bony thoracic protection [27]. Despite this increased vulnerability, NOM is often successful, even in severe cases classified as AAST grade IV or V [28]. A multicenter study reported an 86.4% success rate for NOM in pediatric patients with grade III to V injuries. NOM, including TAE, is strongly recommended for hemodynamically stable patients, even in the presence of persistent or delayed bleeding [27]. Although TAE has been reported to be safe and effective in some pediatric patients with renal trauma, its utilization remains rare. However, data from the National Trauma Data Bank indicate that the actual use of embolization in pediatric renal trauma remains limited. Among 4,506 pediatric patients with renal trauma, only 88 (approximately 2%) underwent embolization. Similarly, an institutional study reported a TAE utilization rate of just 0.9% among 1,216 pediatric patients. These findings underscore the need for further research to better define the role of TAE in managing pediatric renal trauma [26, 28]. Several factors may account for this limited use. First, NOM without embolization shows a high success rate in children, even in many high-grade injuries. Second, technical challenges, such as small vessel caliber and limited device compatibility, may discourage its routine use. Third, concerns about radiation exposure are more pronounced in pediatric patients due to their higher sensitivity and longer life expectancy. Finally, the lack of pediatric-specific evidence and limited clinical experience contribute to the low utilization of TAE in this population [26, 29].

Although universally accepted criteria for TAE have not been established, it is commonly performed when angiographic findings reveal active extravasation of contrast media, pseudoaneurysm, or AVF [16]. Recent studies suggest that TAE may also be both effective and safe in some hemodynamically unstable patients [31]. However, current international guidelines still recommend surgical intervention as the standard treatment for hemodynamically unstable patients with renal trauma [9, 16].

Renal venous bleeding is typically low-pressure and often self-limited due to the tamponade effect of the perirenal space. However, successful hemostasis has been reported using arterial embolization, which reduces venous outflow and indirectly controls venous bleeding [32, 33]. However, in cases of severe injuries involving the main renal vein and ongoing hemorrhage, surgical intervention remains the standard approach [9].

Even when extravasation is detected on CECT, approximately 32% of patients demonstrate no extravasation on subsequent catheter angiography. In these cases, the rebleeding rate without embolization has been reported at 8.3% [34], indicating that routine embolization is not required in patients with negative angiographic findings [9]. Current international guidelines, including those from the EAU and WSES-AAST, recommend selective embolization based on angiographic findings [9, 16].

Selection of Embolic Materials for TAE in Renal Trauma

The selection of embolic materials for TAE in renal trauma depends on the type of vascular injury, such as pseudoaneurysm, active extravasation, or AVF [9]. A wide range of embolic agents is available, including coils, n-butyl cyanoacrylate (NBCA), microparticles, vascular plugs, and gelatin sponge particles [31].

Given the end-arterial anatomy of the renal vasculature and the low risk of rebleeding through collateral circulation, microcoils are the most commonly used embolic agents for renal embolization [35-37]. In contrast, particulate embolic materials such as gelatin sponge particles should be avoided when treating AVFs because of the potential risk of pulmonary embolism [31, 38, 39].

NBCA is typically reserved for cases involving small target vessels (approximately 1.5 mm in diameter) or when coil deployment is not feasible [31]. In hemodynamically unstable patients requiring rapid hemostasis, NBCA is also well-suited for damage control IR because of its rapid and effective occlusion properties [40].

Clinical Outcomes of TAE in Renal Trauma

Several studies involving relatively large cohorts have reported that the overall clinical success rate of TAE for renal trauma ranges from 73% to 92.9% [41, 42]. One study reported grade-specific success rates of 94.2% for AAST Grade IV injuries and 85.3% for Grade V injuries, with Grade V identified as an independent risk factor for TAE failure (OR: 3.55, p = 0.02) [19]. Another study identified several independent predictors of TAE failure, including gross hematuria (OR: 3.16, 95% CI: 1.29-8.49; p = 0.015), hemodynamic instability (OR: 3.29, 95% CI: 1.37-8.22; p = 0.009), Grade V trauma (OR: 2.86, 95% CI: 1.06-7.72; p = 0.036), and urinary extravasation (OR: 3.49, 95% CI: 1.42-8.83; p = 0.007) [41]. Even when the initial TAE attempt fails, a second TAE procedure has demonstrated a high success rate of 80% to 100%, suggesting its consideration in cases of rebleeding [43, 44].

The overall complication rate after TAE has been reported to be approximately 25%. Documented adverse events include non-target embolization of normal arteries, access site bleeding, vascular dissection or thrombosis, contrast-induced nephropathy, post-embolization syndrome (e.g., back pain and fever), renal abscess, and coil migration [9, 25]. Regarding acute kidney injury (AKI), some studies have reported no significant difference in renal function between patients who underwent TAE and those who did not [45]. However, a large observational study involving 12,843 patients with blunt renal trauma reported an AKI incidence of 2.2% in the conservative management group, 8.7% in the TAE group, and 12% in the nephrectomy group. Both TAE (OR: 2.367; p = 0.001) and nephrectomy (OR: 2.745; p < 0.001) were identified as significant predictors of AKI [46]. These findings underscore the importance of performing TAE as selectively as possible to optimize renal function preservation [11].

Endovascular Treatment for Renal Artery Dissection and Occlusion

Traumatic renal artery dissection results from endothelial injury, which can lead to thrombus formation and subsequently cause renal embolism or infarction. In some cases, expansion of the false lumen or the development of an intramural hematoma may lead to acute arterial occlusion and renal ischemia. When the dissection causes severe stenosis―typically greater than 70%―chronic renal hypoperfusion may occur, increasing the risk of delayed-onset renovascular hypertension [47]. Invasive SR for renal artery dissection or occlusion is generally not recommended because of poor long-term outcomes [48, 49]. For main renal artery dissection that does not involve hemodynamic compromise and presents with non-critical stenosis (<70%), anticoagulation therapy is considered an appropriate management strategy [50, 51]. Plain balloon angioplasty is typically ineffective in these cases, as dissected arteries carry a high risk of elastic recoil [52].

Endovascular stent placement has been reported to be effective for managing traumatic renal artery dissection. Some cases have demonstrated long-term stent patency for up to 4 years, suggesting that stenting can help maintain blood vessel patency and renal function over time [50, 53]. Technical success rates of endovascular treatment range from 88.9% to 100%, while clinical success rates are approximately 75% [54]. However, when recanalization is attempted, the presence of arterial laceration or transection may lead to massive hemorrhage, requiring prompt embolization of the renal artery stump when necessary [48]. In patients with complete main renal artery occlusion, successful revascularization has been achieved even up to 12 hours post-injury. However, the optimal window for renal salvage is generally considered to be within 4 hours. Even with technically successful SR, preserved renal function is achieved in only 26% of patients beyond this time frame [55].

Although stent placement more than 3 hours after injury may help prevent renovascular hypertension, sufficient preservation of renal function is unlikely in most cases of complete occlusion [48, 56]. The degree of arterial stenosis or occlusion may influence the kidney's tolerance to ischemia. While the optimal ischemic time threshold remains uncertain, rapid intervention is essential to preserve renal function in patients with traumatic renal artery occlusion [56].

A systematic review reported that the incidence of urinary extravasation in high-grade renal injuries (AAST grade III-V) is approximately 29% (95% CI: 17%-42%), with an incidence of 51% (95% CI: 38%-64%) in Grade IV and V injuries specifically [57]. However, most cases resolve spontaneously, with reported spontaneous resolution rates ranging from 74% to 87% [58-60]. When extravasation does not resolve naturally, ureteral stent placement is commonly employed as a renal-preserving intervention [9, 61, 62]. One study reported that in patients with persistent extravasation and urinoma formation, urinary interventions such as ureteral stenting or nephrostomy were safely performed in approximately 70% of patients, facilitating successful completion of NOM [63]. The optimal timing of intervention has not been firmly established. Previous studies have reported that ureteral stents have been placed as early as within 24 hours and as late as 7 days after injury in cases of sustained extravasation [64-66]. Based on these findings, ureteral stent placement around 7 days after trauma appears to be a reasonable and evidence-based approach when extravasation does not resolve spontaneously [62, 63]. However, a multicenter study comparing early ureteral stent placement with observation for urinary extravasation found no significant differences in persistent extravasation rates (50.9% vs. 42.5%, p = 0.27), secondary nephrectomy (0% vs. 2.8%, p = 0.34), or trauma-related mortality (0% vs. 1.8%, p = 0.99). These findings suggest that the timing and necessity of intervention remain subjects of ongoing debate [64].

Percutaneous drainage plays an important role in preventing infection and abscess formation, and is sometimes combined with ureteral stenting to enhance therapeutic effectiveness [8]. Although the role of IR in treating urinomas is still largely supported by case reports, promising outcomes have been documented. In a study on urinary leaks following partial nephrectomy, IR procedures achieved success rates of 92%-100% [65]. In one series of 10 cases, embolization of the urinoma tract using a percutaneous approach with NBCA and coils yielded favorable outcomes [66]. Another report described the use of super-selective renal arterial embolization to defunctionalize isolated calyces, resulting in the cessation of urinary extravasation [67].

Additionally, percutaneous cryoablation has been reported as a treatment option for managing urinary extravasation in selected cases [68]. While these findings remain anecdotal, further research is warranted.
