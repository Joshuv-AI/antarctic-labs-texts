Janus bilayer coating of functional quaternized chitosan and borate bioactive glass for antibacterial protection and osseointegration
Authors: Revathi Alexander, Caixia Wan, Xiaohua Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Peri-implant infection and insufficient osseointegration remain leading causes of implant failure, yet existing surface strategies address these challenges sequentially rather than concurrently. A hierarchically organized bilayer coating was developed on titanium by integrating an electrospun quaternized chitosan and poly(vinyl alcohol) nanofibrous base with an electrosprayed overlayer of borate bioactive glass nanospheres encapsulated within a quaternized chitosan shell. The Si-free ternary 70B2O3–25CaO–5K2O composition, synthesized via a surfactant-directed sol-gel route, yields monodisperse spherical nanoparticles; shell encapsulation reduces cumulative boron release to 78.2% over 14 days and suppresses burst release to 0.33% at 2 h, sustaining ion delivery within the cytocompatible and osteogenically active range. Broad-spectrum bactericidal activity against a clinically representative polymicrobial peri-implant panel was achieved through synergistic contact killing and borate dissolution derived ionic perturbation. Among formulations evaluated, B40 comprising 40 wt% quaternized chitosan and 5 wt% bioactive glass simultaneously maximized infection control and osteogenic maturation in primary human mesenchymal stem cells, producing superior metabolic activity, cytoskeletal organization, collagen expression, and matrix mineralization. Surface cationic charge density and controlled borate ion flux are identified as coupled regulators of antibacterial and osteogenic responses, establishing a rational drug-free framework for multifunctional implant surface engineering.

Introduction

Titanium and its alloys remain the gold standard for load-bearing orthopedic and dental implants owing to their exceptional mechanical properties, corrosion resistance, and established biocompatibility [1]. Despite decades of clinical refinement, two interrelated biological failures continue to limit implant longevity: early bacterial colonization leading to biofilm-associated peri-implant infection, and insufficient osteoprogenitor cell engagement at the bone–implant interface, which delays osseointegration and increases the risk of aseptic loosening [2,3]. These failures are mechanistically coupled, as bacterial adhesion has been shown to competitively displace osteogenic cells from the implant surface [3], while poor osseointegration simultaneously compromises the biological seal against subsequent infection. Peri-implantitis affects approximately 22% of implant recipients, frequently necessitating implant removal and imposing substantial clinical and economic burden [4].

The clinical response to this dual challenge has historically been sequential, with perioperative antimicrobial prophylaxis administered prior to any osteogenic intervention, leaving the implant surface biologically undefended during the critical early integration period. Multifunctional coating strategies have been explored to close this window [3]. Metal ion-releasing systems incorporating silver, zinc, or copper have been found to achieve bactericidal thresholds at concentrations that simultaneously impair osteoblast and human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) viability, undermining the cellular machinery on which osseointegration depends [5,6]. Hydroxyapatite-based coatings have been reported to support bone bonding through osteoconduction but confer neither antimicrobial activity nor the soluble ionic environment required for rapid osteogenic commitment of resident progenitor cells [3]. Taken together, these limitations establish a need for coating architectures capable of delivering antibacterial protection and osteogenic stimulation concurrently, without cytotoxic compromise [2].

Electrohydrodynamic atomization methods, including electrospinning and electrospraying, represent powerful platforms for the fabrication of functional nanomaterials for biomedical applications [7,8]. These single-fluid processes exploit the interaction between working fluids and electrostatic energy to produce nanofibers and nanospheres with controlled morphology, composition, and surface properties. Electrospun nanofibrous coatings have attracted sustained interest as implant surface modifiers owing to their structural analogy with the native extracellular matrix and their demonstrated capacity to support progenitor cell attachment and lineage commitment [9,10]. Among available polymers, quaternized chitosan (QCS) is particularly well suited to this application, as its permanently cationic quaternary ammonium groups confer contact-active bactericidal function without requiring ion release or drug loading [11]. The high charge density that underlies this activity also renders direct electrospinning impractical, necessitating co-formulation with a carrier polymer. Poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) has been shown to serve this role effectively, providing the solution viscosity and intermolecular hydrogen-bonding interactions required for stable, bead-free fiber formation [12,13]. Although QCS/PVA nanofibers establish reliable antibacterial surface function, the absence of any osteoinductive capacity in this purely organic system necessitates incorporation of a bioactive inorganic phase to drive MSC osteogenic commitment beyond the baseline supported by fiber topography alone.

Bioactive glasses represent a well-characterized class of osteoinductive materials whose dissolution products have been shown to stimulate bone-forming cell activity through direct ionic signaling [14]. Silicate glasses, of which 45S5 is the archetype, have been extensively studied in this context, though their relatively slow network hydrolysis has been found to constrain the rate of ion delivery to surrounding tissues [15]. Borate bioactive glasses (BBGs), in which B2O3 replaces SiO2 as the primary network former, have been reported to undergo substantially faster hydrolytic degradation, generating a more sustained flux of soluble ions into the local biological environment [16] with released boron ions shown to promote osteogenic differentiation of MSCs through modulation of the local inflammatory microenvironment [17]. The same dissolution kinetics that confer this advantage, however, also present a principal limitation, as elevated boron concentrations have been found to compromise MSC viability, such that effective deployment of BBGs in biological systems requires controlled delivery rather than direct unconfined glass exposure [18].

This requirement for controlled delivery has not been adequately addressed by existing BBG coating strategies. The majority of reported compositions are multicomponent systems derived by substitution from established silicate formulations; the specific ternary 70B2O3–25CaO–5K2O system, incorporating K2O rather than the more commonly used Na2O as the sole network modifier in a Si-free composition, has not previously been reported [19,20]. Network modifier selection within such compositions carries additional functional significance, as the incorporation of K2O into borate glass networks has been shown to shift boron coordination from trigonal BO3 to tetrahedral BO4 through the borate anomaly [21], thereby modulating hydrolysis kinetics independently of overall glass stoichiometry and providing a structural mechanism for tuning ion release without altering bulk composition. Achieving morphological control over BBG particles presents a further challenge. At low modifier concentrations, borate networks are dominated by planar BO3 triangular units, which confer substantially lower network connectivity than the three-dimensional SiO4 tetrahedral framework of silicate analogues. This reduced connectivity produces poor intrinsic gelation during sol-gel processing and yields irregular particle geometries that compromise both electrospraying reproducibility and the predictability of dissolution kinetics [20,22,23]. Prevailing approaches to BBG–fiber integration have embedded the inorganic phase within electrospun matrices, an arrangement found to restrict ion exchange at the glass surface, demand higher glass loadings that progressively disrupt fiber morphology, and remove the bioactive phase from direct contact with the biological environment [24]. Deposition of unconfined particles onto fiber surfaces circumvents the exchange restriction but leaves dissolution entirely unregulated, introducing the burst release profile that renders elevated boron concentrations cytotoxic [8,25]. Surface deposition of inorganic particles within a polyelectrolyte binder has been proposed to impose diffusion-limited control over ion release while retaining the bioactive phase at the coating surface. However, this strategy remains restricted to injectable hydrogel formulations [26], leaving a critical gap in its application to electrospun fiber substrates. Furthermore, the approach has yet to be tested against the pronounced burst kinetics characteristic of borate glass compositions [23], nor has it been integrated with contact-active antibacterial layers that function independently of ion release.

To address these limitations, a Janus bilayer coating was fabricated on titanium substrates by integrating an electrospun PVA–QCS nanofibrous base with an electrosprayed overlayer of QCS-encapsulated BBG nanospheres. The asymmetric bilayer architecture, in which the nanofibrous base provides contact-active antibacterial function while the nanosphere overlayer delivers controlled osteoinductive ion release, is consistent with the Janus design principle of structurally and functionally distinct opposing surfaces [27]. The ternary 70B2O3–25CaO–5K2O (mol%) system synthesized via a surfactant-directed sol-gel route, and the subsequent deposition of these encapsulated nanoparticles onto an electrospun quaternized chitosan substrate, have not been previously reported in the literature. The QCS shell establishes a polyelectrolyte diffusion barrier at the glass surface, regulating ion flux within the cytocompatible and osteogenic range while reducing the alkaline transient associated with unconfined borate dissolution. The nanofibrous base confers contact-active broad-spectrum antibacterial activity independent of ion release, while BBG dissolution products provide synergistic suppression of peri-implant pathogens that resist contact killing alone. Osteogenic performance was assessed using primary human MSCs, which more accurately represent the progenitor cell population recruited to the implant surface in vivo than immortalized osteoblast lines used in most comparable studies.

Materials and methods

Materials

Commercially pure grade IV titanium sheets (CP Ti, UNS R50700, 10 × 10 × 1 mm) were used as substrates. Boric acid (H3BO3), calcium nitrate tetrahydrate (Ca(NO3)2⋅4H2O), potassium nitrate (KNO3), citric acid (0.3 g per batch), cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB, 1.8 g per batch), absolute ethanol, Tween-20, glutaraldehyde, genipin, and glycidyl trimethylammonium chloride (GTMAC) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich and used as received. Poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA, Mw ≈ 90,000 g mol−1) was dissolved as a 20 wt% aqueous solution. Chitosan (medium molecular weight; degree of deacetylation ≈ 85%) was solubilized at 2 g per 100 mL of 1% (v/v) aqueous acetic acid prior to quaternization with GTMAC. All solutions were freshly prepared before use.

Synthesis of borate bioactive glass nanoparticles

The 70B2O3–25CaO–5K2O (mol%) composition was selected based on its high borate network content, which drives rapid ionic dissolution. K2O was chosen as the network modifier in place of the more commonly used Na2O [20]; the larger ionic radius of K+ produces a distinct borate coordination environment through the borate anomaly at lower modifier concentrations, enabling finer modulation of network hydrolysis kinetics [20,28]. BBG nanoparticles were synthesized via a CTAB-directed sol-gel precipitation route. A 1:1 (v/v) mixed solvent of deionized water (150 mL) and absolute ethanol (150 mL) was stirred at 80 °C for 30 min. Precursor salts consisting of 4.32 g H3BO3, 2.95 g Ca (NO3)2⋅4H2O, and 0.51 g KNO3 were added sequentially at 30-min intervals. Citric acid (0.3 g) was introduced as a chelating agent to lower solution pH to approximately 3. Separately, 1.8 g CTAB dissolved in 100 mL deionized water was adjusted to pH ~11 with concentrated ammonia to serve as the structure-directing alkaline medium. The acidic precursor solution was added dropwise to the CTAB solution under vigorous stirring (500 rpm), aged overnight at room temperature, vacuum-filtered, washed sequentially with deionized water and ethanol, and calcined at 500 °C for 3 h.

Synthesis of quaternized chitosan

QCS was synthesized via reaction of chitosan with glycidyl trimethylammonium chloride (GTMAC) at a molar ratio of 3:1 (GTMAC to chitosan amino groups). 2 g of chitosan was dissolved in 100 mL of 1% (v/v) acetic acid under continuous stirring (500 rpm). GTMAC was then added dropwise, and the reaction mixture was maintained at 55 °C for 18 h. QCS was precipitated in ethanol, collected by vacuum filtration, and purified through redissolution in 2 wt% acetic acid followed by reprecipitation in ethanol. The resulting product was dialyzed against deionized water (MWCO: 3500 Da) for 72 h at room temperature, with the dialysate refreshed every 12 h, and subsequently lyophilized. The degree of quaternization (DQ) was determined by potentiometric titration of chloride counter-ions using AgNO3 [11,29].

Titanium surface preparation

Titanium sheets were cleaned by sequential ultrasonication in acetone, ethanol, and deionized water (15 min each) to remove organic and inorganic contaminants. Surface micro- and nanoscale roughness was generated by chemical etching in 4 vol% HNO3 and 0.75 vol% HF for 30 min, followed by thorough rinsing and drying under N2 gas.

Electrospinning and electrospraying

QCS was incorporated into a 20 wt% PVA solution at mass ratios of 20, 40, and 60 wt% relative to PVA, with QCS contents exceeding 60 wt % resulting in poor fiber continuity. The fiber-only coatings, designated E20, E40, and E60, were electrospun at 15 kV with a 15 cm tip-to-collector distance and a 0.5 mL h−1 feed rate onto a grounded rotating drum (600 rpm), while the bilayer coatings, designated B20, B40, and B60, were produced by electrospraying a bioactive overlayer onto the electrospun frameworks. BBG nanoparticles (5 wt%) and QCS (3 wt%) were dispersed in deionized water containing 0.1% (v/v) ethanol and 0.2 wt% Tween-20, stirred and ultrasonicated to ensure uniform distribution prior to and during electrospraying. Electrospraying was performed at 15 kV, a flow rate of 0.1 mL h−1, and a 15 cm tip-to-collector distance. All coatings were crosslinked by vapor-phase exposure to a 1:1 (v/v) glutaraldehyde/genipin mixture under vacuum at 50 °C for 12 h.

Physicochemical and structural characterization

Nanoparticle morphology and amorphous structure were examined by TEM and SAED; amorphous nature was further verified by XRD (Fig. S1). Cross-sectional morphology and coating thickness were evaluated by focused ion beam scanning electron microscopy (FIB-SEM). Particle size distribution, PdI, and zeta potential were determined by DLS, while elemental composition was characterized by EDX and ICP-MS. As ICP-MS detects dissolved cationic species rather than oxide phases, the measured elemental concentrations of B, Ca, and K were converted to their respective oxide equivalents (B2O3, CaO, K2O) for compositional reporting, consistent with the oxide-based stoichiometry of the target glass composition. N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms were measured at 77.35 K using an Autosorb 6100 FKM XR (Anton Paar) following degassing under vacuum at 100 °C for 12 h. Specific surface area was calculated using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method. Chemical characterization of the coating constituents and the bilayer coating was performed by attenuated total reflectance Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR). Spectra were acquired for quaternized chitosan (QCS), borate bioactive glass (BBG), and the fabricated bilayer coating over a wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1. A PVA reference spectrum was acquired separately to confirm peak assignments in the bilayer coating spectrum. Spectra were baseline-corrected and plotted with a vertical offset of 25% transmittance units between each spectrum for clarity of presentation. Fiber morphology and diameter (n = 30 fibers per sample, three images per group, ImageJ) were assessed by FE-SEM. TGA of the B-series bilayer coatings was performed under N2 atmosphere from 25 to 600 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 to quantify the BBG fraction incorporated in the coating. Cumulative release of B, Ca, and K from BBG nanopowder and electrosprayed coatings (5 mL PBS, pH 7.4, 37 °C) were quantified by ICP-MS over 14 days.

Antibacterial activity evaluation

Surface antibacterial activity was evaluated against Streptococcus mutans (SM), Staphylococcus aureus (SA), Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans (AA), and Porphyromonas gingivalis (PG). SM and SA were cultured in brain-heart infusion broth. AA was cultured under capnophilic conditions (5% CO2) on tryptic soy agar supplemented with 5% defibrinated sheep blood, hemin, and vitamin K1. PG was grown under strict anaerobiosis (Oxoid™ AnaeroGen™) on the same medium. Cultures were grown to mid-logarithmic phase and resuspended in PBS to 1 × 106 CFU mL−1. All samples were UV-sterilized (30 min per side) prior to static incubation at 37 °C. Adherent bacteria were recovered by vortex-assisted detachment, serially diluted, and plated for CFU enumeration. Colony forming units per plate were enumerated from triplicate independent experiments for each coating group and each bacterial strain, and results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3). Statistical comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s post-hoc test, with the uncoated titanium control as the reference group. Bacterial morphology was examined by FE-SEM following 2.5% glutaraldehyde fixation, graded ethanol dehydration, and critical point drying. Quantitative assessment of bacterial growth kinetics was performed by OD600 measurements over 24 h across all four strains on coated titanium substrates, with anaerobic and capnophilic conditions maintained for PG and AA respectively throughout the measurement period.

In vitro cell culture and osteogenic assays

hMSCs (ATCC PCS-500–012) were cultured in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO2. For short-term proliferation and morphological assessments (Days 1–5), cells were seeded at 5 × 103 cells cm−2 in 24-well plates onto UV-sterilized coated substrates, a density selected to permit individual cell morphology quantification while avoiding confluence-induced contact inhibition. For osteogenic differentiation assays (Days 7–14), cells were seeded at 1 × 104 cells cm−2 to support the paracrine signaling and cell-cell interactions required for osteogenic commitment, and the differentiation medium comprised basal α-MEM supplemented with 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, 50 μM ascorbic acid, and 100 nM dexamethasone. Metabolic activity was assessed by CCK-8 assay on Days 1, 3, and 5, expressed as a percentage of the Day 1 uncoated Ti control. Cytoskeletal morphology was evaluated by rhodamine-phalloidin/DAPI fluorescence staining; single cell spreading area was quantified by ImageJ from FE-SEM micrographs. ALP and ARS staining were performed on Days 7 and 14, respectively. 1% BSA blocking was applied uniformly to all surfaces, including the uncoated Ti control, prior to ALP and ARS staining to minimize nonspecific dye absorption by the nanofibrous coating matrix. ALP-positive and ARS-positive areas were quantified as a percentage of total image area using ImageJ. OCN protein expression was assessed by confocal immunofluorescence at Day 14, and mean fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ. Osteogenic gene expression (RUNX2, ALP, COL1A1, OPN, and OCN) was quantified by qRT-PCR at Day 14, normalized to GAPDH using the 2−ΔΔCt method and expressed as fold-change relative to the uncoated Ti control. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Sample sizes varied by assay: n = 3 for ALP, ARS, ion release, and gene expression; n = 6 for cell count per field; n = 20 for single cell spreading area; n = 30 for fiber diameter and polymer shell thickness measurements. Statistical comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test, with the uncoated Ti control as the reference group. CCK-8 metabolic activity data were analyzed by two-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s post-hoc test to account for the effects of coating group and time point. OCN mean fluorescence intensity was compared across all groups using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test. Significance thresholds: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Results and discussion

Characterization of BBG nanoparticles

TEM imaging revealed spherical nanoparticles with a mean diameter of 250 ± 20 nm, characterized by a dense electron-opaque periphery and lighter core, reflecting differential mass distribution within the amorphous borate network (Fig. 1A, B). SAED produced a single broad diffuse halo with no discrete Bragg reflections (Fig. 1C), and XRD showed the absence of crystalline phases (Fig. S1), collectively establishing the amorphous nature of the synthesized BBG. The amorphous network lowers the activation energy for hydrolytic attack relative to crystalline borate phases, accounting for the accelerated dissolution kinetics characteristic of sol–gel-derived BBGs [20].

EDX detected B, Ca, and K within the particles (Fig. 1D). ICP-MS quantified the composition as 21 wt% B, 16 wt% Ca, and 6 wt% K, equivalent to 69.2 wt% B2O3, 21.8 wt% CaO, and 7.4 wt% K2O (Fig. 1E). Oxide equivalents were calculated from measured elemental weight fractions assuming stoichiometric oxide compositions, with totals below 100 wt% reflecting rounding during elemental-to-oxide conversion. The nominal target composition of 70B2O3–25CaO–5K2O (mol%) corresponds to theoretical oxide weight fractions of 72.2 wt% B2O3, 20.8 wt% CaO, and 7.0 wt% K2O; measured values of 69.2 wt% B2O3, 21.8 wt% CaO, and 7.4 wt% K2O indicate close agreement with the target stoichiometry. The high B2O3 content drives rapid network hydrolysis and ionic dissolution, while K2O introduces non-bridging oxygens that modulate network connectivity and regulate degradation rate without promoting crystallization [20].

N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms exhibited a Type IV(a) profile with an H3-type hysteresis loop (Fig. S2A), and the specific surface area was 206 m2 g−1, among the higher values reported for sol–gel-derived BBG nanoparticles [23]. DLS yielded a Z-average diameter of 293.2 nm with a PdI of 0.016 (Fig. 2A), and zeta potential was 20.7 ± 10.8 mV (Fig. 2B). The positive zeta potential is tentatively attributed to surface borate group speciation under the measurement conditions and is consistent with the electrostatic driving force for QCS adsorption onto the BBG surface during electrospraying. The larger hydrodynamic diameter relative to the TEM-derived size reflects hydration layer and electrical double layer formation in aqueous suspension [30]. These colloidal properties collectively ensure reproducible electrospraying behavior, preventing aggregation-induced inhomogeneity during nanosphere deposition onto the fiber substrate.

Characterization of quaternized chitosan

FTIR spectra of native chitosan and QCS showed the appearance of a distinct absorption band at 1480 cm−1 in QCS, attributed to the symmetric methyl bending vibration of the N(CH3) 3 quaternary ammonium group introduced via GTMAC reaction (Fig. 4C). Both spectra retained characteristic polysaccharide backbone absorptions including O—H and N—H stretching at 3350 cm 1, C—H stretching at 2920 cm−1, amide I at 1650 cm−1, amide II at 1590 cm−1, and C–O–C glycosidic stretching at 1080 cm−1. Potentiometric AgNO3 titration yielded a degree of quaternization (DQ) of 32% [11,29].

The emergence of the N(CH3) 3 band confirms successful quaternization, while the retention of backbone absorptions at unchanged wavenumber positions indicates that the reaction proceeded selectively at the primary amine at C-2 rather than at the hydroxyl groups. DQ is directly proportional to the surface cationic charge density per polymer chain [29], and a value of 32% provides effective contact-active antibacterial function while preserving free amino and hydroxyl groups for hydrogen-bonding interactions with PVA during electrospinning [12,29].

ATR-FTIR spectra of QCS, BBG, and the bilayer coating are presented in Fig. S4. The bilayer coating spectrum retained characteristic absorption bands from all constituent materials, confirming successful incorporation of QCS, PVA, and BBG within the hierarchical architecture. A band at 1735 cm−1 attributable to C=O ester stretching confirmed retention of the PVA nanofibrous base layer. Critically, the QCS quaternary ammonium band shifted from 1479 cm−1 in free QCS to 1567 cm−1 in the coating, and the BBG B—O network vibration shifted from 1005 cm−1 to 1020 cm−1, with the B-O-B bending band shifting from 706 cm−1 to 714 cm−1. These wavenumber shifts indicate perturbation of the electronic environments of both the quaternary ammonium groups of QCS and the borate network of BBG, consistent with electrostatic complexation between the cationic QCS shell and the BBG nanoparticle surface during electrospraying. The broad absorption at 1020 cm−1 additionally reflects overlapping contributions from the C-O-C polysaccharide backbone of QCS and the shifted B—O network of BBG.

Bilayer coating architecture

FE-SEM imaging of the B-series coatings showed bead-free nanofibrous networks with discrete spherical BBG/QCS nanospheres distributed across the fiber surfaces (Fig. 3A). The primary QCS/PVA layer formed an isotropic nanofibrous network, with PVA providing sufficient chain entanglement to maintain fiber continuity. Fiber diameter decreased progressively with increasing QCS concentration, from 130 nm at B20 to 110 nm at B60 (Fig. 3B), falling within the 50–500 nm diameter range of native type I collagen fibrils in bone ECM [10]. FIB-SEM cross-sectional analysis revealed intact fiber morphology beneath the nanosphere overlayer with a uniform coating thickness of 25.0 ± 0.3 μm (Fig. 3C).

TEM analysis of the electrosprayed BBG/QCS nanospheres revealed a broad size distribution ranging from approximately 200 nm to 1 μm, reflecting the inherent droplet size distribution of the electrospraying process, with a uniformly dense electron-opaque core surrounded by a lighter polymeric shell (Fig. 3D). Shell thickness was consistent across the size range at 12.4 ± 4.9 nm (n = 30). Smaller nanospheres in the lower end of the size range exhibited core diameters consistent with encapsulation of individual BBG nanoparticles within the QCS shell, while larger nanospheres showed core diameters exceeding that of individual BBG nanoparticles, indicative of multiple nanoparticles compacted within a single polymeric matrix during electrospraying.

During electrospraying of the BBG/QCS suspension, charged droplets undergo rapid solvent evaporation under the applied electric field. The BBG nanoparticles, being denser and electrostatically stabilized within the droplet interior, concentrate at the core. Concurrently, the QCS chains migrate to the droplet periphery, driven by their amphiphilic character and surface charge density, and form the polymeric shell upon solidification. This proposed mechanism is consistent with the dense compacted core morphology confirmed by TEM. The progressive reduction in fiber diameter with increasing QCS concentration is attributable to the elevated charge density of quaternary ammonium groups, which increases solution conductivity and electrostatic repulsion within the viscoelastic jet, amplifying bending instabilities and elongational stretching during electrospinning [7,31], consistent with observations in cationic polyelectrolyte–PVA systems [12]. The resulting increase in fiber surface-to-volume ratio with increasing QCS concentration correspondingly augments the contact-active bactericidal surface area available for interaction with bacterial membranes [10]. The uniform shell thickness across nanospheres of varying core size suggests that QCS deposition during electrospraying is primarily governed by droplet surface charge rather than core particle size, producing a consistent diffusion-regulating barrier irrespective of the number of encapsulated BBG nanoparticles. The dense compacted core morphology, distinct from the lighter-core appearance of the raw BBG nanoparticles characterized in Section 3.1, is consistent with solvent evaporation-driven densification of the BBG nanoparticles within the QCS matrix during electrospraying.

Ion release and thermal analysis

Cumulative boron release from unconfined BBG reached 100% of total glass boron content by Day 14, with approximately 28% released within the first 2 h, indicative of pronounced burst kinetics (Fig. 4A(i)). In contrast, BBG/QCS nanospheres released only 78.2% of total boron over the same period, with 0.33% released at 2 h, confirming that the QCS shell effectively suppresses early burst release. The resulting sustained release profile maintained boron delivery within the cytocompatible and osteogenically active concentration range over 14 days [32,33]. Cumulative calcium release from unconfined BBG reached 88.2% by Day 14, while BBG/QCS nanospheres released 52.4% over the same period, with a progressive accumulation profile reflecting diffusion-limited transport through the QCS shell (Fig. 4A(ii)). The near-constant calcium release rate from unconfined BBG reflects rapid equilibration between dissolution and Ca—P phase formation on the exposed glass surface, rather than cessation of dissolution. PBS pH remained at 7.5 throughout the 14-day period for BBG/QCS nanospheres, while unconfined BBG elevated pH to 8.05, consistent with accelerated network hydrolysis and alkaline ion release from the exposed glass surface. The QCS shell therefore moderates not only ion flux but also the local pH environment, reducing the risk of alkaline-induced cytotoxicity.

Boron and calcium release from BBG/QCS nanospheres is consistent with a combined erosion-diffusion mechanism. The BBG core is proposed to undergo progressive hydrolytic network dissolution, while the nanoscale QCS shell is proposed to impose diffusion-limited control over the flux of dissolved ionic species into the surrounding medium. The suppressed burst release and progressive accumulation profiles observed for both boron and calcium support diffusion-controlled transport through the polyelectrolyte barrier.

Potassium release was not quantifiable due to the high background K+ concentration in PBS (793 μg mL−1 by ICP-MS), which precluded detection of glass-derived contributions above the background threshold. TGA of the B-series bilayer coating identified a principal decomposition event between 150 and 392 °C attributable to combustion of the organic polymer fraction, accounting for a mass loss of 29.8%. The thermally stable BBG phase contributed to a residual mass of 65.6% at 600 °C (Fig. 4B).

The marked suppression of boron burst release achieved by BBG/QCS nanospheres demonstrates that the 12.4 ± 4.9 nm QCS shell functions as an effective diffusion barrier, clearly distinguishing the intrinsic dissolution kinetics of the high-potency borate composition from its biological exposure profile without compositional modification. The attenuated calcium release from BBG/QCS nanospheres relative to unconfined BBG reflects rapid equilibration between dissolution and Ca–P phase formation on the exposed glass surface in phosphate-containing media [19,34]. The progressive Ca2+ accumulation from the electrosprayed nanospheres indicates that QCS-mediated diffusion control moderates this equilibration, sustaining Ca2+ delivery within the ionic range conducive to osteogenic signaling [35]. As K+ functions primarily as a network modifier in the 70B2O3–25CaO–5K2O system rather than an osteoinductive species, boron and calcium release were considered the primary indicators of BBG dissolution behavior. The BBG mass fraction confirmed by TGA is consistent with the electrospraying parameters and glass loading used, confirming effective incorporation of the inorganic phase within the bilayer coating.

Synergistic antibacterial activity against clinically relevant peri-implant pathogens

The uncoated Ti control exhibited confluent bacterial growth across all four strains (Fig. 5). Colony density on E-series surfaces decreased progressively with increasing QCS concentration, with E20 producing moderate reductions in S. mutans and S. aureus colony density and negligible effect on A. actinomycetemcomitans and P. gingivalis, while E40 and E60 showed progressively greater reductions against Gram-positive strains. Gram-positive strains were effectively eradicated at higher E-series concentrations, whereas A. actinomycetemcomitans and P. gingivalis exhibited sustained resistance across the entire E-series. Addition of the BBG overlayer in B-series coatings produced further reductions in colony density beyond E-series counterparts at equivalent QCS concentration. S. mutans and S. aureus plates were near colony-free across all B-series coatings, while A. actinomycetemcomitans and P. gingivalis showed substantial suppression in B40 and B60 relative to their E-series counterparts, though residual colonies remained visible.

FE-SEM imaging showed intact cell envelopes on uncoated Ti control surfaces across all four strains (Fig. 5). On E-series surfaces, membrane perturbation increased progressively with QCS concentration: E20 showed minimal morphological changes, while E40 and E60 displayed visible surface wrinkling, irregular cell outlines, and partial envelope collapse at fiber contact points. On B-series surfaces, adherent bacterial populations were markedly reduced relative to E-series counterparts, and residual organisms exhibited pronounced membrane disruption and cytoplasmic leakage, characterized by deflated and irregular cell morphology with extracellular debris at the cell periphery. On B-series surfaces, BBG/QCS nanospheres were clearly distinguishable from bacterial cells by their size and morphological regularity.

Representative CFU plate images confirmed the visual antibacterial hierarchy across all coating groups and bacterial strains (Fig. 6A). Quantitative CFU enumeration confirmed these observations (Fig. 6B). The uncoated titanium control supported mean colony counts of 325.0 ± 8.9, 125.0 ± 5.6, 82.0 ± 3.0, and 98.3 ± 3.5 CFU cm−2 for S. mutans, S. aureus, A. actinomycetemcomitans, and P. gingivalis respectively. All coating groups demonstrated statistically significant reductions relative to the uncoated titanium control (****p < 0.0001, one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s post-hoc test, n = 3). E-series coatings achieved progressive reductions with increasing QCS concentration, with E60 reducing S. mutans by 99.4%, S. aureus by 98.4%, A. actinomycetemcomitans by 97.6%, and P. gingivalis by 97.7% relative to the uncoated Ti control. B-series coatings exceeded E-series counterparts at all equivalent QCS concentrations. B40 reduced S. mutans by 98.9%, S. aureus by 99.2%, A. actinomycetemcomitans by 97.6%, and P. gingivalis by 97.9%. B60 achieved complete eradication of S. mutans and S. aureus (100% reduction), with no viable colonies recovered across all biological triplicates, indicating bacterial load below the detection limit of the spreadplate assay.

Quantitative OD600 growth assays revealed a clear divergence in antibacterial performance between E-series and B-series architectures (Fig. S3). E-series coatings achieved inhibition of 36.5–57.4% for S. aureus, 50.8–69.7% for S. mutans, 31.7–65.4% for P. gingivalis, and 35.5–57.4% for A. actinomycetemcomitans relative to the uncoated Ti control at 24 h. B-series coatings substantially exceeded these values, achieving 79.8–93.2% for S. aureus, 78.8–92.4% for S. mutans, 68.5–86.6% for P. gingivalis, and 65.1–87.9% for A. actinomycetemcomitans. The addition of the BBG overlayer provided an additional 21–43 percentage point increase in inhibition relative to fiber-only controls across all four strains. B40 and B60 coatings showed an absolute decline in OD600 values between 12 and 24 h across all four strains, indicative of a bactericidal rather than bacteriostatic mode of action.

The progressive dose-dependent activity of the E-series against Gram-positive strains is consistent with electrostatic adsorption of N (CH3)3+ groups onto anionic moieties of the bacterial cell envelope, inducing displacement of stabilizing divalent cations from membrane-binding sites and increasing membrane permeability [29,36,37]. The resistance of A. actinomycetemcomitans and P. gingivalis to E-series contact killing reflects the structural organization of the Gram-negative outer membrane, where the LPS layer presents a dense anionic barrier that attenuates polycationic QCS chain penetration [38]. The additional suppression of Gram-negative strains in B-series coatings, despite bulk pH remaining within the physiological range, indicates that the antibacterial enhancement is attributable to borate-mediated ionic perturbation rather than bulk alkaline shift. The combination of QCS contact killing and BBG dissolution-mediated ionic activity achieves broad-spectrum bactericidal performance against a clinically representative polymicrobial peri-implant panel that neither component achieves independently.

hMSCs cytocompatibility and proliferative response

CCK-8 metabolic activity showed no suppression relative to the uncoated Ti control across any coating group (Fig. 7A). On Day 1, metabolic activity was broadly comparable across all groups (106.1 ± 10.6% for control). By Day 3, B-series coatings exhibited higher metabolic activity than their E-series counterparts at equivalent QCS concentration, with B40 reaching 305.4 ± 6.4% and B60 reaching 278.7 ± 32.0%, an effect that reached statistical significance by Day 5 (p < 0.0001). B40 showed the highest metabolic activity on Day 5 at 469.7 ± 75.2%, approximately 1.9 times the Day 5 Ti control (245.1 ± 17.0%) and approximately 1.3-fold that of E40 (355.5 ± 10.1%).

The absence of metabolic suppression across all coating groups indicates that neither the QCS/PVA nanofibrous matrix nor the BBG ionic dissolution products at the concentrations delivered by the bilayer coating compromise hMSCs viability over the 5-day assessment period. The progressive enhancement in metabolic activity from Day 3 onwards in B-series coatings, exceeding that of E-series counterparts, is consistent with the established role of Ca2+ ions in promoting mitochondrial activity and cellular proliferation in MSCs [35], reflecting the additive contribution of BBG ionic dissolution to metabolic upregulation beyond the surface-mediated adhesive stimulus provided by the QCS nanofibers alone [39].

hMSCs morphology, spreading, and cytoskeletal architecture

FE-SEM imaging at Day 3 revealed surface-dependent differences in hMSCs spreading morphology (Fig. 7B). The uncoated Ti control exhibited restricted elongated spindle morphology with limited lamellipodial extensions. E-series surfaces promoted progressive cellular expansion from E20 to E60. B-series coatings induced a shift toward highly expanded polygonal phenotypes with flat lamellipodial sheets spanning multiple fiber junctions [10,39]. Single cell spreading area increased progressively with QCS concentration across E-series coatings relative to the uncoated Ti control (1333.19 ± 479.86 μm2), reaching statistical significance at E40 (1778.86 ± 178.52 μm2, p < 0.001) and E60 (1864.05 ± 183.01 μm2, p < 0.0001). B-series coatings substantially exceeded E-series values at all equivalent QCS concentrations, with B20 (2939.15 ± 376.90 μm2), B40 (3338.78 ± 307.36 μm2), and B60 (2550.30 ± 446.36 μm2) all significantly greater than the uncoated Ti control (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 7C).

Fluorescence staining at Day 3 showed sparse bipolar spindle morphologies with attenuated actin filaments on the uncoated Ti control (Fig. 8A). Stress fiber networks were evident on E20 cells, while E40 demonstrated denser cell packing with thicker actin cables and emerging cell-cell contacts. E60 showed elongated morphology consistent with E40, with lateral spreading remaining limited relative to B-series coatings. B-series coatings produced near-confluent fields with thick parallel actin bundles aligned across cell bodies and prominent cortical actin at cell-cell junctions. Quantitative cell counting yielded 147 ± 42 cells per field for the uncoated Ti control, 172–206 cells per field for E-series, and substantially higher densities for B-series: B20 at 251 ± 26, B40 at 317 ± 22 cells per field (2.2-fold over control, p < 0.0001), and B60 at 246 ± 50 cells per field (Fig. 8B).

Single cell spreading area increased progressively with QCS concentration across E-series coatings, consistent with moderate surface charge promoting favorable orientation of adsorbed adhesive proteins and maintaining integrin-accessible binding sites for cell attachment. The predominantly elongated morphology across E-series coatings, in contrast to the polygonal phenotype observed on B-series surfaces, suggests that fiber topography alone drives directional cell elongation along fiber axes, while BBG-derived Ca2+ is required to promote isotropic lamellipodial expansion and polygonal phenotype adoption [35]. The superior spreading area on B-series coatings relative to E-series counterparts at equivalent QCS concentration is consistent with the well-established role of Ca2+ in promoting focal adhesion assembly and lamellipodial stabilization in MSCs [35]. The decline from B40 to B60 is attributable to excessive cationic charge density imposing a kinetic barrier to cell adhesion, potentially through conformational changes in adsorbed serum proteins that restrict focal adhesion maturation [35,40,41].

Osteogenic differentiation, gene expression, and integrated biological response

At Day 7, ALP activity showed minimal staining on the uncoated Ti control (10.6 ± 4.6%; Fig. 9A). B-series coatings substantially exceeded their E-series counterparts at equivalent QCS concentrations (Fig. 9B), with B20 (76.2 ± 16.9%), B40 (94.5 ± 5.7%), and B60 (81.8 ± 12.5%) far exceeding E20 (21.5 ± 8.6%), E40 (38.5 ± 4.7%), and E60 (27.9 ± 6.6%), respectively.

ARS quantification at Day 14 confirmed the same hierarchy: E-series showed moderate mineralization (E20: 14.2 ± 2.3%, E40: 28.5 ± 1.2%, E60: 24.2 ± 5.2%), while B-series substantially exceeded these values (B20: 52.5 ± 7.3%, B40: 77.3 ± 4.8%, B60: 60.5 ± 3.8%), with B40 achieving maximal mineralization (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 9C, D).

OCN immunofluorescence at Day 14 was restricted to coated surfaces due to titanium opacity precluding confocal imaging of the uncoated control; immunofluorescence was retained to provide spatially resolved protein-level expression data, with qRT-PCR serving as the quantitative benchmark against the uncoated Ti control. E-series showed uniformly sparse fluorescence (E20: 8.8 ± 0.7 a.u., E40: 10.5 ± 1.3 a.u., E60: 9.5 ± 0.4 a.u.), while B-series showed substantially higher expression, with B40 achieving the highest value (29.8 ± 3.6 a.u., p < 0.0001), followed by B60 (21.1 ± 1.5 a.u.) and B20 (19.4 ± 2.2 a.u.) (Fig. 10A, B). OCN gene expression ranged from 6.40 to 7.60 in E-series, with B40 highest (31.50 ± 0.70), followed by B60 (25.10 ± 1.35) and B20 (17.07 ± 2.97) (Fig. 10C).

qRT-PCR at Day 14 revealed a consistent compositional hierarchy across all osteogenic transcripts. RUNX2 ranged from 3.10 to 4.50 in E-series and 6.88 to 10.50 in B-series, with B40 achieving the highest expression (10.50 ± 0.93), followed by B60 (8.43 ± 0.63) and B20 (6.88 ± 0.25) (Fig. 11A). ALP transcript levels followed the same ordering: B40 (60.13 ± 2.73) > B60 (46.30 ± 4.36) > B20 (36.73 ± 4.05) against E-series values of 9.30–11.17 (Fig. 11B). COL1A1 showed pronounced compositional dependence, ranging from 5.40 to 5.93 in E-series and 12.80 to 23.50 in B-series, with B40 (23.50 ± 2.81) approximately 4.3-fold above E40 (5.50 ± 0.66) (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 11C). OPN ranged from 4.50 to 5.00 in E-series and 10.50 to 16.00 in B-series, with B40 (16.00 ± 1.20), B60 (13.80 ± 2.75), and B20 (10.50 ± 0.82) maintaining the same compositional ordering (Fig. 11D).

The osteogenic transcriptional data establish a coherent compositional dependence across all five markers, with B40 achieving the highest expression in each case, followed by B60 and B20 in consistent rank order. This pattern confirms that intermediate cationic charge density in B40 generates the most productive combination of surface-mediated adhesive stimulus and BBG-derived ionic induction for terminal osteoblastic maturation [42,43]. Unlike the antibacterial response, which scales monotonically with QCS concentration, osteogenic maturation follows a peak-dependent relationship within the B-series, reaching its maximum at B40 and declining progressively at B60. The reduction from B40 to B60 across all markers is consistent with excessive cationic charge density impairing integrin-mediated adhesion signaling through conformational changes in adsorbed serum proteins, undermining the surface-mediated adhesive stimulus required to sustain the osteogenic commitment cascade [29,40,41]. The pronounced compositional sensitivity of COL1A1, reaching approximately 4.3-fold above E40 in B40, identifies it as the most discriminating molecular indicator of productive osteogenic maturation in this system. The spatial correlation between peak OCN expression and maximal ARS mineralization in B40 confirms BBG-derived ionic cues as the primary driver of late-stage osteoblastic commitment [33,44].

The integrated dataset establishes a consistent biological pattern governed by two interdependent surface parameters, namely the interfacial charge density determined by QCS concentration at a fixed DQ (32%), and the osteoinductive ionic microenvironment established by BBG dissolution. The antibacterial response scales monotonically with QCS concentration, with B60 demonstrating the highest efficacy. The hMSCs response is governed by a peak rather than a monotonic relationship, determined by the balance between surface-mediated adhesive stimulus and biochemical induction from BBG dissolution. At B20, reduced cationic charge density limits the surface-mediated adhesive stimulus required to sustain osteogenic progression despite the BBG ionic environment [39]. At B60, cationic charge density exceeds the threshold supporting productive osteogenic maturation, favoring a proliferative cellular state that limits terminal mineralization [29]. At B40, intermediate cationic charge density provides sufficient surface-mediated adhesive stimulus for osteogenic lineage commitment while regulated ion flux from BBG dissolution drives complete matrix mineralization. This peak response, achieved through modulation of QCS concentration at a fixed DQ, establishes surface-level cationic charge density as the primary regulator of biological outcome [29,39]. This distinction, monotonic for bacteria and peak-dependent for hMSCs, identifies B40 as the compositional point that simultaneously maximizes infection control and osteogenic maturation on titanium implant surfaces.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that a hierarchical Janus bilayer architecture, comprising an electrospun QCS/PVA nanofibrous base and an electrosprayed BBG/QCS nanosphere overlayer, provides a viable drug-free strategy for simultaneously addressing peri-implant infection and insufficient osseointegration on titanium implant surfaces. The monodisperse spherical BBG morphology achieved through surfactant-directed sol-gel synthesis enables structural regulation of dissolution kinetics without compositional modification, achieving sustained cytocompatible boron delivery through a nanoscale QCS diffusion barrier over 14 days. The asymmetric Janus architecture concentrates contact-active QCS bactericidal function at the fiber base while positioning the BBG nanosphere overlayer to deliver controlled osteoinductive ionic stimulation, a structural separation of function not previously reported for titanium implant coatings. Surface cationic charge density, governed by QCS concentration at a fixed degree of quaternization of 32%, functions as the primary design parameter regulating both biological responses, but does so through mechanistically distinct pathways: antibacterial activity scales monotonically with QCS concentration while osteogenic maturation follows a peak-dependent profile with a maximum at B40. This divergence identifies B40 as the coating configuration that simultaneously maximizes infection control and osteogenic maturation across a clinically representative polymicrobial peri-implant panel and primary human mesenchymal stem cells. Compared with previously reported multifunctional implant coatings that rely on antibiotic loading or cytotoxic metal ion release, the present system achieves broad-spectrum bactericidal activity and osteogenic maturation through purely physicochemical and ionic mechanisms, eliminating the risk of antibiotic resistance and cytotoxic threshold exceedance. These findings provide a rational framework for drug-free multifunctional implant coating design. Future work will investigate in vivo osseointegration and infection prevention in a rat femoral implant model, alongside mechanistic investigation of the osteogenic signaling pathways activated by the ionic dissolution products of the borate bioactive glass system at the protein level.
