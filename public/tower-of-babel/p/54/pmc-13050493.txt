Family Dysfunction, Interpersonal Risk Factors, and Suicidal Ideation Among Marginalized Adolescents Following Psychiatric Hospitalization
Authors: Allegra S Anderson, Katherine M Ingram, Leslie A Brick, Anastacia Y Kudinova, Michael F Armey, Nicole R Nugent
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:Suicide is the second leading cause of death among adolescents and young adults, with alarming increases in rates of suicide over the past two decades. Adolescents with marginalized identities, including LGBTQ and racial/ethnic minoritized youth, are at even greater risk for suicidality, yet remain underrepresented in suicide research. Given the psychosocial challenges associated with social marginalization, examining factors that may buffer or exacerbate risk in marginalized adolescents is crucial for informing intervention and prevention efforts.Method:The present study used structural equation modeling to examine longitudinal associations among thwarted belongingness (TB), perceived burdensomeness (PB), family dysfunction, and suicidal ideation (SI) in a sample of marginalized adolescents hospitalized for suicidal thoughts and behaviors (n = 100). Further, this study builds upon existing research by testing family dysfunction as a moderator of the associations between interpersonal risk factors and SI.Results:Adolescents who reported more TB and PB during hospitalization experienced more severe SI six months following psychiatric hospitalization. Notably, the association between TB and SI intensity was stronger among adolescents reporting higher levels of family dysfunction.Conclusion:Findings suggest that family dysfunction may exacerbate the impact of TB among marginalized adolescents following acute psychiatric care, emphasizing the need for family-focused interventions that bolster family communication, support, and adolescents’ sense of belonging.Keywords: Adolescent, belongingness, family, marginalized, suicide

INTRODUCTION

Suicide is the second leading cause of death among adolescents and young adults, with rates of suicide in the United States increasing by 37% between 2000 and 2022 (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC], 2025; Martínez-Alées et al., 2022). Adolescents with marginalized identities1 may be at even greater risk, with a strong body of research supporting higher rates of suicidality in lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, and/or questioning (LGBTQ) youth (Miranda-Mendizáabal et al., 2017; Perez-Brumer et al., 2017; Price-Feeney et al., 2020; VanBronkhorst et al., 2021). While racial and ethnic minoritized and/or marginalized youth have been historically underrepresented in suicide research (Opara et al., 2020; Polanco-Roman & Miranda, 2022; Véelez-Grau et al., 2023), prior research has demonstrated higher rates of suicidality in youth of color (Baiden et al., 2023), including increased rates of suicide attempts in Black and Hispanic adolescents (Ivey-Stephenson et al., 2020). Given the breadth of psychological and interpersonal challenges associated with social marginalization (Shepherd et al., 2023), understanding factors that may buffer or exacerbate risk for suicide in marginalized adolescents is a critical aim.

Suicidality in Marginalized Adolescents

Adolescence is a developmental period characterized by marked increases in suicidal thoughts and behaviors (STB) and suicide (Borowsky et al., 2001; Hua et al., 2024; Nugent et al., 2022). The prevalence of suicidal ideation (SI), including thoughts or desires to kill oneself, significantly increases in adolescence and is one of the most frequent psychiatric presentations in emergency departments and predictors of hospitalizations (Nock et al., 2013; Wolff et al., 2018). SI is often reported before suicide attempts, with the ideation-to-action transition typically occurring 1–2years after SI onset in treatment-seeking youth (Cha et al., 2018; Glenn et al., 2017; Loópez et al., 2022). Despite inpatient, partial hospitalization, and outpatient interventions, youth hospitalized for STB remain at elevated risk for suicidal behavior and rehospitalization (Fontanella et al., 2020; Nugent et al., 2022; Prinstein et al., 2008; Yen et al., 2013).

Adolescents with marginalized identities face higher suicide risk than their non-marginalized peers (Shepherd et al., 2023). U.S. national data from 1991 to 2019 indicate increases in SI for Black and American Indian/Alaska Native adolescents, with Black male youth showing the greatest increase in nonfatal suicide attempts compared to all other race and gender groups (Douglas et al., 2025; Xiao et al., 2024). Research has shown higher SI rates among multiracial adolescents (Wong et al., 2012), and greater depression and/or suicidal behavior among Latine and American Native/Pacific Islander youth relative to White peers (Bostwick et al., 2014). LGBTQ youth also show elevated risk for psychopathology and SI (Batejan et al., 2015; Fox et al., 2020; Hatchel et al., 2019).

These disparities may be understood through frameworks such as Minority Stress Theory (Meyer, 2003; Meyer et al., 2021) and the Weathering Hypothesis (Forde et al., 2019), which describe how interpersonal and structural discrimination burden one’s allostatic load, and result in painful thoughts and feelings about one’s worth, sense of belonging, and hope for the future. External expressions of prejudice can become internalized, such that adolescents experiencing social exclusion may develop expectations of rejection; internalized homophobia, transphobia, or racism; behavioral withdrawal; and subsequent exacerbation of depression and SI. This aligns with strong evidence linking minority stressors to psychopathology, distress, and suicidality among marginalized individuals (Baams et al., 2015; Hoy-Ellis, 2016; Kaufman et al., 2023).

Intersectionality Theory highlights how individuals with multiple marginalized identities experience interacting forms of oppression (e.g., heterosexism x cissexism x racism), creating distinct social and psychological risks for suicidality (Crenshaw, 2013b; Shepherd et al., 2023). For instance, sexual minority Latinas are more likely to report past-year suicide attempts compared to their White counterparts, whereas this difference has not been observed among sexual minority Latinos (Bostwick et al., 2014). Yet, research on marginalization has often focused on single identities (Shepherd et al., 2023), limiting understanding of risk and protective factors that are salient for individuals with varying marginalized identities that often intersect, overlap, and share the collective experience of being othered.

Interpersonal Risk Factors: Thwarted Belongingness and Perceived Burdensomeness

The psychological and systemic impacts of marginalization contribute to two of the central risk factors for suicidality outlined by the Interpersonal Theory of Suicide (Joiner, 2005; Van Orden et al., 2010): thwarted belongingness (TB) and perceived burdensomeness (PB). TB is characterized by feelings of social disconnection when one’s fundamental need for belonging is unmet (Kaufman et al., 2023; Van Orden et al., 2012), whereas PB refers to the belief that one is a burden on others (Joiner, 2005; Van Orden et al., 2010). Both constructs are theorized to play critical roles in the development, maintenance, and severity of SI (Joiner, 2005). Adolescents with marginalized identities may be especially vulnerable to elevated TB and PB, as both constructs are clearly linked with minority stressors like social isolation and rejection (Joiner, 2005; Kaufman et al., 2023; Meyer, 2003). For instance, TB is associated with suicidality among LGBTQ individuals and has been shown to moderate and/or mediate the relation between minority stressors and SI (Dunlop et al., 2022; Testa et al., 2017). Belongingness has also been identified as important in suicide prevention for youth with marginalized racial/ethnic identities (Véelez-Grau et al., 2023), with one recent study showing that TB predicts passive SI among Latine and Black adolescents (Vélez-Grau et al., 2025). Similarly, PB has shown robust associations with SI and may be particularly salient for marginalized adolescents who internalize experiences of rejection or family stress as evidence that their needs impose hardship on caregivers or loved ones (Buitron et al., 2016; Gulbas et al., 2019; Hill & Pettit, 2012; Vélez-Grau et al., 2025). Despite growing evidence, few studies have examined these associations in high-risk populations, such as adolescents recently hospitalized for STB, or in adolescents with multiple marginalized identities (Shepherd et al., 2023).

Family Dysfunction

Belongingness and PB are particularly salient within the family context, with extensive research demonstrating the significance of interpersonal relationships within the family domain for adolescents with marginalized identities (Vélez-Grau & Lindsey, 2022; Vélez-Grau et al., 2023; Zareian & Klonsky, 2020). Strong family functioning has been shown to protect against SI among Black, Latine, and LGTBQ youth, potentially by buffering feelings of TB (Eisenberg & Resnick, 2006; O’Donnell et al., 2004; Oakey-Frost et al., 2021; VanBronkhorst et al., 2021; Vélez-Grau et al., 2023). An instrumental factor influencing the perception of family support is family functioning, including the quality of communication, emotional responsiveness, problem-solving, and mutual involvement of the family system (Katz-Wise et al., 2018; Mansfield et al., 2015). While strong family functioning may reduce suicide risk, family dysfunction may exacerbate it (Pu et al., 2025). Prior research suggests that individual factors (e.g., TB and PB) may be most strongly associated with SI when socioecological risks in adolescents’ immediate environment (e.g., family dysfunction) are high (Douglas et al., 2025). Importantly, family dysfunction may also function as an upstream interpersonal context through which adolescents develop feelings of TB and PB (Joiner, 2005; Van Orden et al., 2010; Vélez-Grau et al., 2023, 2025). However, to our knowledge, these associations have not been tested longitudinally in high-risk samples of adolescents with multiple marginalized identities.

Present Study

The present study extends prior research by testing the longitudinal associations of salient risk and protective factors in a sample of marginalized adolescents hospitalized for STB. Specifically, we examine the direct associations between adolescents’ baseline TB, PB, and family dysfunction with their SI six months post-hospitalization. Further, we build upon existing research by testing family dysfunction as a moderator of the association between interpersonal risk factors and SI over time. We hypothesized that greater TB (Hypothesis 1), PB (Hypothesis 2), and family dysfunction (Hypothesis 3) would be associated with more intense and severe SI six months post-hospitalization. We also expected that family dysfunction would moderate the association between each interpersonal risk factor and SI, such that higher family dysfunction would strengthen the associations between TB (Hypothesis 4) and PB (Hypothesis 5) with suicidal ideation.

METHODS

Participants were recruited from the inpatient unit of a child psychiatric hospital in the northeastern United States as part of a broader study examining the associations among affect, social context, and suicidality (Nugent et al., 2022). Adolescents and their parents were approached during hospitalization and interested families completed informed consent and assent procedures. This study was approved by the hospital’s Institutional Review Board (IRB # 633181). During hospitalization, participants completed baseline self-report surveys and interviews and returned for an assessment six months following psychiatric hospitalization for STB. Participants were compensated for their time.

Participants

Inclusion criteria included psychiatric hospitalization for STB; past-month STB verified by clinical interview; age 13–18; sufficient English fluency to complete study procedures; consent of parent or legal guardian; and adolescent assent. Adolescents diagnosed with a psychotic disorder, developmental delay, autism spectrum disorder, or pervasive developmental disorder were excluded from the study. For the present study, adolescents endorsing at least one marginalized identity (i.e., racial, ethnic, gender, and/or sexual marginalized identity) were included in analyses (n = 100, 53% of participants who completed baseline procedures, N = 188).

Measures

Marginalized Identities

Marginalized identity status was based on adolescents’ self-reported ethnicity, race, gender identity, and sexual orientation. Participants were categorized as having a marginalized identity if they (1) identified as Latine, (2) endorsed a racial identity other than White (i.e., American Indian/Alaska Native, Asian, Black, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, or multiracial), (3) identified as a gender minority (i.e., transgender or non-cisgender), and/or (4) reported a non-heterosexual orientation, including gay or lesbian, bisexual, questioning, or selecting a response indicating that their sexual orientation was not listed. Thus, consistent with minority stress frameworks and prior research (Meyer, 2003; Meyer et al., 2021; Orellana et al., 2022) sexual minority status was operationalized as any indication outside of heterosexual norms rather than reliance on specific identity labels. Intersectionality cannot be reduced to an additive process of identity oppression, but rather should be considered in the context of interactive processes that can exacerbate overlapping systems of oppression (Moradi & Grzanka, 2017; Velez et al., 2019). Thus, this study examined salient risk and protective factors among a sample with a range of marginalized identities, including a dummy-coded covariate accounting for multiple marginalized identities within our models.

Interpersonal Risk Factors

Participants completed a subset of items from the Interpersonal Needs Questionnaire (INQ; Van Orden et al., 2012), a self-report measure of interpersonal needs fulfillment based upon the interpersonal theory of suicide (Joiner, 2005; Van Orden et al., 2010). The present study focused on two core interpersonal risk factors assessed by the INQ: TB and PB. TB was assessed using the 10-item TB subscale (e.g., “These days, I often feel like an outsider in social gatherings”), which captures feelings of social disconnection and unmet belonging needs. PB was assessed using the 8-item PB subscale (e.g., “These days, the people in my life would be better off if I were gone”), reflecting beliefs that one’s existence is a burden on others. Items from both subscales are rated on a 7-point Likert scale (Not true at all for me to Very true for me), with higher scores indicating greater levels of the respective interpersonal risk factor. Both subscales of the INQ are widely used in research on suicide risk (Hunt et al., 2020; Shepherd et al.,2023; Van Orden et al., 2008) and had strong internal consistency in the present sample (TB: ωt = .91; PB: ωt = .96).

Family Dysfunction

Family dysfunction was measured using the General Functioning subscale of the Family Assessment Device (FAD; Epstein et al., 1983), a widely used self-report measure of an individuals’ perception of their family functioning rated on a 4-point scale (Strongly Agree to Strongly Disagree). The General Functioning subscale includes 12 items measuring key aspects of family functioning including emotional support, problem-solving abilities, communication, acceptance, and family cohesion; with higher values indicating greater family dysfunction. Prior research suggests that mean scores of approximately 2.00 or higher on the General Functioning subscale reflect clinically significant family dysfunction (Epstein et al., 1983; Mansfield et al., 2015). The FAD has been used extensively to assess family functioning in families with a range of ethnic/racial backgrounds (e.g., Aarons et al., 2007; Cong et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2022) and showed strong internal consistency in the present sample (ωt = .92).

SI

Trained clinical interviewers administered the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS; Posner et al., 2011) at baseline and six months post-hospitalization to assess SI. Consistent with prior research examining SI in adolescents (e.g., Marraccini et al., 2022), two complementary summary indices were derived: SI severity and SI intensity. SI severity (range 0 – 5) reflects the type and intent of ideation and was calculated by summing endorsement (yes = 1, no = 0) of five ideation categories: wish to be dead; nonspecific active ideation; active ideation with method but no intent; active ideation with intent but no plan; and active ideation with both plan and intent. SI intensity (range 0 –25) captures qualitative features of ideation and was computed by summing ratings (0 – 5) across five domains: frequency, duration, controllability, deterrents, and reasons for ideation. A score of zero indicates no ideation, with higher scores reflecting greater intensity. The C-SSRS has well-established psychometric properties (Posner et al., 2011) and showed good internal consistency reliability in this sample at baseline (ωt = .96) and the 6-month follow-up (ωt = .97).

Data Analytic Approach

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.3 (R Core Team, 2025). Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to examine associations between baseline TB, PB, family dysfunction, their interactions, and SI at the 6-month follow-up. All constructs were modeled as manifest variables; no latent variables or measurement models were specified. Separate models predicted SI intensity and severity. Covariates included adolescents’ age and sex, given well-established developmental and sex-based differences in suicidal ideation during adolescence (CDC, 2025; Kaufman et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2019; Nock et al., 2013; Xiao et al., 2024), and baseline suicidal ideation to account for prior clinical severity (Glenn et al., 2017). In addition, all models included a dummy-coded covariate for multiple marginalized identity status (i.e., 0 = one marginalized identity, 1 = multiple marginalized identities) to account for cumulative exposure to social marginalization without reducing intersectionality to additive identity effects (Moradi & Grzanka, 2017; Velez et al., 2019). Predictor variables were mean-centered prior to conducting analyses involving interaction terms. For models including significant interaction terms, simple slope analyses were conducted to estimate the association between interpersonal risk factors and SI at low (−1 SD), mean, and high (+ 1 SD) levels of family dysfunction. In addition, Johnson-Neyman regions of significance were computed using unstandardized SEM parameter estimates to identify the range of family dysfunction values for which the association between variables was statistically significant.

Covariances were freely estimated between predictor variables and baseline SI to account for shared variance. Prior to model interpretation, multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIFs) derived from equivalent linear regression models; all VIF values were below conservative thresholds for multicollinearity (max VIF = 1.71) (Hair et al., 2019; Kutner et al., 2005). Models were estimated using the lavaan package in R (Rosseel, 2012), with SEM employed as an estimation framework to allow for full information maximum likelihood (FIML) estimation of missing data and simultaneous estimation of regression paths and covariances. Exogenous predictors were treated as “FIXED = TRUE.”

Missing Data

A small proportion of participants were missing baseline data (i.e., 3.77% for TB and PB [n = 4], 1.89% for family dysfunction [n = 2], .57% for baseline SI [n = 1]); 19 participants (17.9%) were missing data follow-up SI data due to attrition. Little’s MCAR test indicated data were missing completely at random (p = .74). Participants with missing data on exogenous variables were excluded from analyses (n = 6), and FIML was used for estimation of any remaining missing data, yielding an analytic sample of n = 100.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics for the analytic sample (n = 100) were as follows: 75% assigned female at birth, Mage = 15.28 years at baseline (SD = 1.46), 18% Hispanic or Latine ethnicity, 59% White, 22% more than one race, 15% Black or African American, 2% Asian, and 2% American Indian or Alaska Native race. The sample included adolescents with at least one marginalized identity, including self-identified gender (17%), sexual (73.6%), racial (40%), and ethnic minorities (18%). Sexual orientations among the sample included 27% heterosexual, 8% gay or lesbian, 35% bisexual, 12% “not sure”, and 18% “none of the above.” Of the analytic sample, 45 participants reported multiple marginalized identities. Participants reported significantly higher SI intensity (M = 14.91, SD = 5.37) and severity (M = 3.52, SD = 1.66) at baseline relative to six months following hospitalization for STB (Mintensity = 3.46, SD = 5.80, t(80) = 15.66, p < .001; Mseverity = .57, SD = 1.66; t(80) = 15.76, p < .001). At baseline, mean family dysfunction in the sample was 2.28 (SD = .54), a value exceeding commonly used thresholds for clinically meaningful family dysfunction on the FAD General Functioning subscale (Epstein et al., 1983; Mansfield et al., 2015). In partial support of Hypothesis 3, family dysfunction moderated the association between TB at baseline and SI intensity six months following hospitalization for STB (b = .21, p = .03). Specifically, at moderate (t(99) = 3.40, p = .005) and high (t(99) = 3.92, p = .002) levels of family dysfunction, TB showed a significant positive association with SI intensity at the 6-month follow-up (Figure 1). At low levels of family dysfunction, the association between TB and SI intensity was not significant, t(99) = 1.10, p = .29. Johnson-Neyman analyses further indicated that the association between TB and suicidal ideation intensity was statistically significant when family dysfunction was at or above approximately .41 units below the sample mean, corresponding to values near or above the clinically elevated range observed in the present sample (M = 2.28). In other words, for adolescents reporting strong family functioning, the association between TB and SI was no longer significant. These findings were specific for SI intensity (e.g., frequency and duration of suicidal thoughts) and not for SI severity (e.g., how active SI is, including presence of specific plan, intent to act, etc.). Post-hoc analyses

Given theoretical models emphasizing the family as a central interpersonal context shaping adolescents’ relational experiences (Vélez-Grau et al., 2023; Vélez-Grau & Lindsey, 2022; Zareian & Klonsky, 2020), post-hoc analyses were conducted to examine whether family dysfunction was associated with baseline levels of TB and PB. Separate models were estimated predicting TB and PB from family dysfunction, controlling for adolescents’ age, sex, and multiple marginalized identity status. Greater family dysfunction was significantly associated with higher levels of TB at baseline (b = 10.14, p < .001). Family dysfunction was also significantly associated with higher PB (b = 8.52, p < .001).

DISCUSSION

The current study examined the associations among marginalized adolescents’ interpersonal risk factors, family dysfunction, and SI six months following hospitalization for STB. Results showed that adolescents who reported greater TB and PB at baseline had greater SI at follow-up. Furthermore, associations between TB and SI intensity were stronger for adolescents reporting greater family dysfunction, emphasizing the interactive roles of family context and belongingness. Findings build upon prior research by testing the longitudinal associations of salient risk and protective factors in a sample of adolescents with a range of marginalized identities following psychiatric hospitalization.

Interpersonal Risk Factors and SI

Extensive research shows increased rates of SI among adolescents with marginalized racial identities (i.e., Black, American Indian/Alaska Native, multiracial; Wong et al. 2012; Xiao et al., 2024), and LGBTQ youth (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2020; Garofalo et al., 1999; Haas et al., 2011; Hatchel et al., 2019). Despite this, marginalized adolescents are often underrepresented in suicide research (Opara et al., 2020; Polanco-Roman & Miranda, 2022; Vélez-Grau et al., 2023), prompting calls for studies that incorporate belongingness and PB in suicide research for these groups (Vélez-Grau et al., 2023). Building upon research linking interpersonal risk factors and suicidality (Joiner, 2005; Van Orden et al., 2010, 2012), this study found that marginalized adolescents with greater TB and PB at baseline experienced more severe and intense SI six months following hospitalization for STB. This aligns with a growing body of research emphasizing TB and PB as key risk factors for marginalized youth (Gulbas et al., 2019; Hill & Pettit, 2012; Kaufman et al., 2023; Vélez-Grau et al., 2023, 2025; Vélez-Grau & Lindsey, 2022). Theoretical frameworks like the Weathering Hypothesis, Minority Stress, and Intersectionality theories (Crenshaw, 2013a, 2013b; Forde et al., 2019; Meyer, 2003; Meyer et al., 2021) suggest that marginalized adolescents face overlapping forms of oppression that become internalized and increase risk for SI. For instance, co-occurring minority or marginalization stress (e.g., social rejection), PB, and TB may contribute to expectations of rejection, behavioral withdrawal, and exacerbation of depressed mood and SI. This has been evidenced in prior studies showing that TB predicts passive SI among Black and Latine adolescents (Vélez-Grau et al., 2025), and moderates and/or mediates the association between minority stressors and SI among LGBTQ individuals (Baams et al., 2015; Dunlop et al., 2022; Kaufman et al., 2023; Testa et al., 2017). Findings are also consistent with prior research demonstrating robust associations between PB and SI in marginalized groups (Gulbas et al., 2019; Hill & Pettit, 2012). Taken together, addressing adolescents’ sense of belonging and PB may be a powerful strategy for reducing SI for marginalized youth.

Family Dysfunction and SI

Prior research emphasizes the protective role of family support against interpersonal risk factors, minority stress, and suicidality among Black, Latine, and LGBTQ youth (Eisenberg & Resnick, 2006; O’Donnell et al., 2004; Oakey-Frost et al., 2021; VanBronkhorst et al., 2021; Vélez-Grau et al., 2023). However, contrary to hypotheses, family dysfunction was not associated with adolescents’ SI over time in the present study. There are multiple interpretations of this finding. First, we used a well-established measure of family functioning (Epstein et al., 1983) that assessed communication, emotional support, problem-solving, acceptance, and family cohesion. While these factors have been linked to adolescent psychological wellbeing (Katz-Wise et al., 2018; Mansfield et al., 2015), a recent meta-analysis suggests that other facets of family dysfunction that were not examined in the present study (e.g., parental suicidal behavior) may be more predictive of adolescent suicide risk (Pu et al., 2025). Second, prior research shows mixed findings on the direct link between family functioning and adolescent SI (Zisk et al., 2019). For instance, Zisk et al. (2019) found no direct effect of family communication on SI severity in treatment-seeking adolescents, and prior research suggests that family functioning may influence SI indirectly through factors like emotion regulation, depression, and minority stress (López et al., 2022; Garcia Saiz et al., 2021). Thus, it is likely that the combination of family dysfunction with other risk factors is more influential for adolescent suicidality over time. In light of additional analyses demonstrating that family dysfunction was associated with TB and PB at baseline, these findings suggest that family dysfunction may shape adolescents’ interpersonal vulnerability indirectly by influencing core relational processes implicated in suicide risk (Joiner, 2005; Van Orden et al., 2010).

Interaction Between Family Dysfunction and TB

Consistent with this notion, our findings showed that family dysfunction moderated the association between TB and SI intensity at the 6-month follow-up. This supports prior research positing that individual factors (e.g., TB) are more strongly associated with SI when socioecological risk factors (e.g., family dysfunction) are high (Douglas et al., 2025). Notably, this effect was specific to SI intensity – reflecting the frequency and duration of SI – and did not extend to SI severity, which reflects more active SI (e.g., plans, intent). In other words, adolescents with greater TB and family dysfunction experienced more frequent and pervasive SI over time, but not more concrete suicidal planning. SI reports were also significantly lower at follow-up relative to baseline, suggesting a potential floor effect that could limit variability and constrain power to detect associations with SI severity. Notably, family dysfunction did not moderate the association between PB and SI, despite PB independently predicting SI at follow-up. This finding is consistent with prior research suggesting that TB, rather than PB, may be more sensitive to contextual and relational stressors when predicting SI over time (Vélez-Grau et al., 2023).

Clinical Implications

Our findings suggest that interventions targeting belongingness, PB, and family functioning may help reduce SI for marginalized adolescents. Interventions like Interpersonal Psychotherapy for Depressed Adolescents (IPT-A) (Mufson, 2004), which strengthen parent-adolescent relationships through improved problem-solving and communication, may enhance adolescents’ sense of belonging and acceptance (Vélez-Grau et al., 2025). Clinicians should utilize a critical consciousness in conceptualizing care, examining how adolescents’ identities influence the accessibility of belongingness and acceptance within their family systems (Vélez-Grau et al., 2023). This is particularly crucial for adolescents with multiple marginalized identities who may experience intersecting forms of oppression and social exclusion (Alvarez et al., 2022; Vélez-Grau et al., 2023). Finally, while not explicitly examined in this study, clinicians should assess broader socioecological factors – such as racism, discrimination, stigma, and socioeconomic stressors – that may heighten risk and inform culturally responsive care (Douglas et al., 2025; Vélez-Grau et al., 2023).

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Directions

The present study had several strengths, including its longitudinal design, diverse sample of adolescents with various marginalized identities, and examination of risk and protective factors in a high-risk sample recently hospitalized for STB. These strengths notwithstanding, several limitations can be addressed in future research. First, while the sample permitted the examination of risk and protective factors across marginalized identities, small subgroup sizes precluded our ability to make meaningful comparisons across specific marginalized identity groups. For instance, given the centrality of family context for Black and Latine adolescents (O’Donnell et al., 2004; Vélez-Grau et al., 2023), family dysfunction may have been particularly impactful for these groups. Future research should recruit larger and more diverse samples to inform targeted interventions. Second, sexual orientation was assessed using a fixed response option without free-text clarification, which limited more nuanced characterization of participants’ sexual identities. While this approach is consistent with prior research (e.g., Orellana et al., 2022), future studies would benefit from including open-ended response options to allow a more granular assessment of sexual orientation. Third, this study did not assess socioecological risk factors like minority or marginalization stress, perceived racism, or discrimination. Prior research suggests that exposure to stigma and barriers to care (e.g., lack of gender-affirming care) may heighten expectations of rejection, behavioral withdrawal, and suicide risk (Baams et al., 2015; Kaufman et al., 2023; Meyer, 2003). Future studies should examine how these socioecological factors interact with individual factors to influence suicide risk. Finally, reliance on self-report of family dysfunction and belongingness may have introduced shared method variance that inflated associations among variables (Kenny & Kashy, 1992). Replication using multi-informant, multi-method approaches is needed to determine the stability of these effects.

CONCLUSION

The present study examined the associations between marginalized adolescents’ interpersonal risk factors, family dysfunction, and SI six months following psychiatric hospitalization for STB. TB and PB emerged as key predictors of adolescents’ SI over time. Further, findings showed that family dysfunction may exacerbate the effects of TB for marginalized adolescents following acute psychiatric care, suggesting that interventions targeting belongingness and family functioning may reduce risk for SI in the high-risk months following psychiatric hospitalization.
