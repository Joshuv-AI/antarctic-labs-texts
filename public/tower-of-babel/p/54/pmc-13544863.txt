A Rare Case of Spinocerebellar Ataxia 50: The First Reported Case from the Indian Subcontinent with a Review of the Literature
Authors: Vijyeta Taparia, Tanushree Chawla, Chanchal Goyal, Vinay Goyal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spinocerebellar ataxia (SCA)-50 is a recently reported rare subtype. We describe the first case of SCA-50 from the Indian subcontinent. A 22-year-old man presented with progressive cerebellar ataxia over 2 years, along with oculomotor abnormalities and imaging findings indicating mild cerebellar atrophy. Genetic testing revealed an NPTX1 gene mutation, which correlates with SCA-50. Its unique pathogenesis distinguishes it from other SCA subtypes.

Introduction

Spinocerebellar ataxias (SCAs) are an inherited (autosomal dominant), progressive, neurodegenerative, and clinically heterogeneous group of disorders that mainly present as gait ataxia. To date, almost 50 distinct genetic SCAs have been identified, classified according to their genetic loci in order of identification.[1] SCA-50 is a recently described rare subtype. It was first described by Coutelie et al. in 2022.[2] We describe the first reported case of SCA-50 from the Indian subcontinent.

Case report

A 22-year-old right-handed man, born of a nonconsanguineous marriage, presented with a 2-year history of gradually progressive unsteadiness of gait, without sensory, motor, or bowel/bladder disturbances. There was no history suggestive of cranial nerve, cognitive, or behavioral deficits or abnormal movements/seizures. There was no history of drug or alcohol use, fever, weight loss, loss of appetite, trauma, or headache. Family history was not significant.

On examination, his Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score was 30/30, and speech was normal. Neuropsychological evaluation was normal. He had broken pursuits, hypometric saccades, and gaze-evoked nystagmus. The rest of the cranial nerves were normal. He had hyporeflexia in the lower limbs, with normal tone and power in all four limbs. Sensory examination was normal. Cerebellar examination revealed bilateral past-pointing with impaired tandem gait. The Scale for the Assessment and Rating of Ataxia (SARA) score was 6.

The patient was evaluated with a provisional diagnosis of chronic, gradually progressive cerebellar ataxia with ocular involvement, without motor, sensory, bowel/bladder, or cortical involvement. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain showed mild cerebellar atrophy [Figure 1]. Nerve conduction studies were normal. The patient was evaluated for treatable acquired causes of chronic cerebellar disorders; all relevant investigations were normal [Supplementary Table 1]. Postinfectious cerebellitis, paraneoplastic causes, and Whipple’s disease were considered unlikely based on the clinical history.

Basic investigations

After ruling out acquired causes, a heredodegenerative ataxia was considered. Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense variant in the NPTX1 gene at exon 3 (c.742C>T; p.Pro248Ser), suggesting SCA-50. Clinical phenotyping classified this as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS); however, in silico prediction parameters were used to assess its significance.

Through various software tools, used by in silico prediction, this variant was stated as damaging, suggesting its role of utmost significance in the development of SCA.

The patient is under follow-up and has shown steady progression, with an increase in the SARA score to 15 over 1 year.

Discussion

SCA-50 is an autosomal dominant neurologic disorder characterized predominantly by cerebellar ataxia and eye movement abnormalities, such as oculomotor apraxia, horizontal nystagmus, impaired smooth pursuits, diplopia, and broken saccades. The age of onset is predominantly between 34 and 71 years.[2] The clinical course is characterized by gradual progression, with chronic decline to a bedbound state over 15–20 years. Additional features include dysarthria, dysphagia, peripheral neuropathy, and other movement disorders such as myoclonus, tremors, chorea, dyskinesia, and cognitive impairment with executive dysfunction.[23] Brain imaging is usually consistent with cerebellar atrophy.

SCA-50 is caused by a mutation in the NPTX1 gene. The NPTX1 gene encodes neuronal pentraxin 1, an endoplasmic reticulum (ER) protein. The mutation leads to overexpression of pentraxin 1 protein, resulting in abnormal aggregated ER with hyperplasia and swollen cisternae, which activates the ER unfolded protein response through translocation of activating transcription factor 6 to the nucleus, ultimately leading to a decline in cellular viability. NPTX1 is predominantly expressed in the cerebellum and frontal cortex, including the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, which is involved in planning, preparation, and executive control of voluntary saccades, and the frontal eye fields, which are involved in intentional saccades.[4] Therefore, variation in this gene expression leads to predominant cerebellar and oculomotor involvement.

Coutelie et al. (2022)[2] reported 17 patients from 6 families who predominantly presented with cerebellar ataxia, along with variable ocular features and cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome. Family history suggested a pattern of autosomal dominant inheritance. A case reported by Schoggl[3] described an NPTX1 mutation in a 6-year-old with gradually progressive truncal and limb ataxia, with loss of speech at 21 months of age, followed by generalized seizures by 6 years of age. Another study by Helmchen et al.[4] reported a 58-year-old woman who developed severe oculomotor apraxia over 15 years, with a positive family history in her brother, father, and uncle. Functional MRI of the brain in our patient suggested reduced activity in the frontal eye fields bilaterally. Our case presented at a relatively younger age, suggesting that SCA can manifest across a wide age range, from early childhood to late adulthood. Our patient also had ocular signs similar to those reported previously, along with a VUS mutation in NPTX1. Although the variant was predicted to be damaging based on in silico analyses, further validation with functional studies and segregation analysis is required.

Family history was not significant; however, we were unable to test his parents with clinical exome sequencing due to financial constraints.

Conclusions

This is the first reported case of SCA-50 from the Indian subcontinent and also had a younger age of onset. Its distinct pathogenesis and involvement of the NPTX1 gene mutation leading to cerebellar ataxia make it unique and distinguishable from other SCA subtypes. Understanding these distinctive aspects of SCA-50, including its genetic basis and clinical presentation, is crucial for developing targeted diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.
