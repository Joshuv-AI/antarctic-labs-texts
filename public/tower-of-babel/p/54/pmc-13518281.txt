Elevation of Stearoyl‐Coenzyme A Desaturase and Monounsaturated Fatty Acids in Parkinson's Disease Serum
Authors: Finula I Isik, Russell Pickford, Michelle Chua, Simon JG Lewis, Nicolas Dzamko, Woojin Scott Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmerging evidence indicates that dysregulation of monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs), synthesized by the enzyme stearoyl‐coenzyme A desaturase (SCD), impacts on α‐synuclein pathology in the Parkinson's disease (PD) brain.ObjectiveThe objective of this study was to analyze SCD and MUFA‐enriched lipids in the periphery of patients with sporadic PD compared with healthy control subjects.MethodsSerum SCD protein was quantified using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay in patients with PD (n = 40) and control subjects (n = 41). Clinically, Parkinson's disease (PD) is a movement disorder that neuropathologically is characterized by misfolded α‐synuclein accumulation in Lewy bodies. In addition, emerging evidence indicates that this process is associated with dysregulated fatty acid metabolism. 1 As a lipid‐binding protein, α‐synuclein is highly sensitive to changes in the makeup of cellular membranes, which are composed of mainly phospholipids and sphingolipids. A growing body of evidence shows that increased monounsaturated fatty acid (MUFA) content in membrane lipids enhances α‐synuclein–membrane interactions, resulting in α‐synuclein misfolding and cytotoxicity. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 MUFAs are synthesized by the enzyme stearoyl‐coenzyme A desaturase (SCD), predominantly yielding palmitoleate (C16:1) and oleate (C18:1), which are incorporated into lipids. 6 Although SCD has been widely investigated in the context of metabolic disorders, growing interest has turned toward its involvement in neurodegenerative diseases, where lipid dysregulation is increasingly understood to play a critical role in neurodegenerative processes, including PD. 7 , 8

The pathogenic effects of MUFAs are attributable to the preferential affinity of α‐synuclein for MUFA‐containing lipids compared with those enriched in saturated fatty acids. 9 , 10 Increased MUFA incorporation into lipids induces physicochemical alterations in membrane properties, including enhanced membrane fluidity, which are proposed to facilitate α‐synuclein–lipid interactions and promote conformational changes in α‐synuclein monomers that predispose them to aggregation. 11 , 12 The observed strong association between MUFA‐enriched lipids and α‐synuclein levels supports the supposition that elevated MUFA content in lipids augments α‐synuclein toxicity, thereby potentially contributing to the pathogenesis of PD. Multiple lines of evidence indicate that inhibition of SCD, either pharmacologically or genetically, reduces α‐synuclein binding, aggregation, and toxicity in cell and animal models of PD, including human induced pluripotent stem cell–derived dopaminergic neurons. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 In animal models of PD, SCD inhibitors reduce fatty acid desaturation and the progression of motor deficits and restore dopaminergic integrity and neuronal survival, 4 , 21 thus increasing the interest in using SCD inhibitors to treat PD. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5

Despite the critical role of SCD in the central nervous system (CNS) and growing evidence that SCD inhibition may confer clinical benefit in PD, the status of SCD level and MUFA in the peripheral of patients with PD remains largely unexplored. To address this significant knowledge gap, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of SCD and MUFA profiles in PD and healthy control serum, with the goal of evaluating their potential as CNS‐relevant biomarkers of PD.

Subjects and Methods

Blood Serum

Fasted blood samples were obtained from participants diagnosed with PD (n = 40) and healthy control subjects (n = 41) following written informed consent. Participants were recruited from the PD research clinic at the Brain and Mind Centre, The University of Sydney. The cases were diagnosed according to clinically established criteria, 22 with no family history of PD. Healthy control subjects were age‐ and sex‐matched and had no neurological or psychiatric disorders or no first‐degree relatives diagnosed with PD. Blood was collected in Serum Separator Tube II Advanced tubes (BD Biosciences), incubated at room temperature for 30 minutes, gently inverted five times, and centrifuged at 1500g for 15 minutes at 21°C. Serum was then aliquoted into 100 μl volumes and stored at −80°C until use. The study was approved by the University of Sydney Human Research Ethics Committee (approval no. 2017/826).

Lipid Extraction and Liquid Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry

Lipids were extracted from serum using methanol and chloroform as previously described, 23 dried under nitrogen gas, reconstituted in 100 μl of isopropanol/methanol, and stored at −80°C. Lipid extracts (10 μl) were analyzed using a Q‐Exactive HF Mass Spectrometer coupled to a U3000 UPLC system (ThermoFisher Scientific) following our previously published methods. 23 , 24 Relative abundance of lipids was obtained from peak areas normalized to internal standards, and data were analyzed using LipidSearch software 4.2.29.

SCD Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay

Serum SCD was measured using an enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (OKEH07143; Aviva Systems Biology Corporation, San Diego, CA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. This kit targets the main SCD isoform SCD1, which is the dominant isoform expressed in most tissues, with no significant cross‐reactivity or interference with analogues in standard testing. Serum samples were diluted 1:2 and OD read at 450 nm using CLARIOstar.

Statistical Analysis

Group differences in SCD measurements between PD (n = 40) and healthy control subjects (n = 41) were evaluated using both descriptive and inferential approaches. The covariates sex, age, disease duration, Movement Disorder Society–Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale Part III, Montreal Cognitive Assessment, and levodopa equivalent daily dose were included in the analyses. For each group, we computed summary statistics (mean, median, standard deviation, and standard error). Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen's d, Hedges' g, and Cliff's delta to quantify the magnitude and distributional shift. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. For MUFA‐containing lipid classes, group means were calculated and compared using Welch's t test to account for unequal variances. Effect sizes were estimated using Hedges' g with small‐sample bias correction. To control for multiple comparisons across lipid classes, we adjusted P values using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Log2 fold changes (log2FCs; PD/Con) were computed to quantify relative differences, and percentage changes were derived for interpretability. Statistical significance was defined as FDR < 0.05. Data visualization included bar charts of group means for significant classes and a volcano plot (log2FC vs. −log₁₀P).

Results

SCD Is Elevated in PD Serum

We undertook a measurement of SCD protein in PD and control serum using ELISA. Patients with PD show substantially higher SCD levels than control subjects with a moderate‐large effect size and a highly significant group difference (Fig. 1A). Specifically, mean SCD is 1702 pg/ml in PD and 1158 pg/ml in control subjects; a Mann–Whitney U test indicates P = 2.2 × 10−4; and effect sizes are Cohen's d = 0.73 and Cliff's delta = 0.48, with a Hodges–Lehmann median difference = 544 pg/mL (PD > Con). The histogram shows two overlapping distributions of SCD, with PD curve and bars clearly shifted to the right, indicating generally elevated SCD levels in patients with PD compared with control subjects (Fig. 1B). The empirical cumulative distribution function plot provides a clear visualization of the cumulative probability of SCD levels in the two groups. The curve for PD lies consistently to the right of the control curve, indicating that patients with PD tend to have higher SCD levels across the entire range of values (Fig. 1C). The separation between curves throughout the distribution suggests that this difference is not confined to extreme values but reflects a systematic elevation in SCD among patients with PD.

Analysis of MUFA Content in Lipids in PD Serum

We next undertook a comprehensive analysis of MUFA content in lipids in PD and control serum using sophisticated liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry and LipidSearch software. Using Welch's t tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction (FDR < 0.05), 4 of 24 lipid classes exhibited statistically significant elevations in PD: methylphosphatidylcholine (MePC), phosphatidylcholine (PC), dihexosylceramide (Hex2Cer), and triglyceride (Fig. 2A). MePC showed the largest effect (mean PD = 63,165 vs. Con = 53,609; P = 2.6 × 10−7; log2FC = 0.237; Hedges' g = 1.25; FDR = 6 × 10−6), followed by PC (PD = 100,657 vs. Con = 87,261; P = 2.2 × 10−6; log2FC = 0.206; g = 1.13; FDR = 2.6 × 10−5). Hex2Cer and triglyceride were also elevated in PD by approximately 20% (log2FC = 0.266 and 0.251, respectively; FDR < 0.04). Volcano plot visualization highlighted these classes in the upper‐right quadrant, and bar charts confirmed consistent PD elevation across significant classes (Fig. 2B). Near‐significant trends were observed for acylcarnitine (+27.7%, FDR = 0.19) and lysophosphatidylglycerol (+15.3%, FDR = 0.18), although these did not survive multiple‐testing correction. The PC C16 desaturation index was 0.0136 for control subjects and 0.0153 for patients with PD (P = 0.0430). The PC C18 desaturation index was 3.6237 for control subjects and 4.2736 for patients with PD (P = 0.0397). The significant increases in both indices in PD further support increased SCD activity in PD. In terms of biomarker potential, MePC and PC MUFAs were most promising with area under the curve values of 0.8311 and 0.8183, respectively (Fig. 2C).

Discussion

Substantial evidence indicates that α‐synuclein interaction with membrane lipids represents an early pathogenic event that precedes α‐synuclein aggregate formation. 25 Cellular membranes are composed of phospholipids containing diverse fatty acids, including MUFAs, which are synthesized by SCD. Studies examining MUFAs in the PD brain have shown that increased levels of MUFAs promote α‐synuclein aggregation and toxicity. 6 , 16 However, the level of MUFAs in the blood of patients with PD remains poorly characterized. To address this significant knowledge gap, we performed a comprehensive analysis of MUFAs and SCD in the serum of patients with sporadic PD and healthy control subjects. We observed elevated SCD levels in PD, accompanied by increased MUFA content across multiple lipid classes. These findings suggest that alterations in MUFAs in the peripheral circulation of PD reflect corresponding changes occurring in the PD brain. In addition, our results align with a recent study examining the C16 and C18 fatty acid desaturase indices in plasma from individuals with PD and control subjects. 26 That study also supported the use of these desaturase indices as surrogate markers of SCD activity, potentially valuable for evaluating pharmacodynamic responses to SCD inhibition.

Specifically, we observed significant increases in MUFAs in PC classes (PC and MePC) with large effects. PC is the most abundant phospholipid in serum, where it serves as a key component of lipoproteins and plays vital roles in membrane structure and lipid metabolism and transport throughout the body. PC is also one of the most abundant phospholipids in the brain, where it serves as a major component of all cellular membranes. Increased levels of MUFA in membranes alter membrane fluidity and curvature that promote α‐synuclein binding, expand the pool of aggregation‐prone monomers, and destabilize tetrameric forms, facilitating α‐synuclein pathology. 27 , 28 In cell and animal models of GBA1‐associated PD, suppression of SCD activity restores membrane lipid balance, promotes α‐synuclein tetramerization, improves lysosomal function, and protects dopaminergic neurons from degeneration. 29

Another class of lipids with elevated MUFA content in PD serum was Hex2Cer. The enrichment of MUFA‐containing Hex2Cer species is consistent with strong evidence indicating that glycosphingolipid metabolism is perturbed in PD, a process increasingly linked to lysosomal dysfunction and α‐synuclein pathology. 30 Glycosphingolipids are key structural and signaling components of neuronal membranes and lysosomes, and their imbalance can profoundly influence protein aggregation, cellular stress responses, and inflammatory signaling. 31 Genetic, biochemical, and cellular studies have demonstrated that loss‐of‐function mutations in GBA1 lead to the accumulation of glucosylceramides and related glycosphingolipids. 32 Reduced GBA1 activity disrupts lysosomal lipid homeostasis and impairs autophagic flux, resulting in decreased clearance of α‐synuclein. 33

We also found moderate MUFA elevation in triglycerides in PD. When MUFAs dominate triglyceride pools, polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) are increasingly sequestered into neutral lipid droplets rather than incorporated into bilayers, resulting in stiffer, less dynamic membranes that compromise vesicular trafficking, receptor function, and synaptic signaling. 34 , 35 In the context of PD, where mitochondrial dysfunction and reactive oxygen species generation are prominent, such MUFA‐driven remodeling may exacerbate lipid peroxidation, impair mitochondrial membrane integrity, and accelerate dopaminergic neuron degeneration. 36 , 37 Collectively, these findings underscore that although MUFAs are essential for membrane flexibility, their disproportionate enrichment at the expense of PUFAs can destabilize neuronal lipid homeostasis and potentiate neurodegenerative cascades.

The therapeutic potential of SCD inhibition has been validated in multiple models of PD. In transgenic mice expressing human α‐synuclein, treatment with brain‐penetrant SCD inhibitors reduced α‐synuclein phosphorylation and aggregation, mitigated neurodegeneration, and improved motor performance. 3 One such compound, YTX‐7739, demonstrated favorable pharmacokinetics and effective CNS target engagement, supporting its advancement into early‐stage clinical trials for PD. 21 Although inhibition of SCD produces robust neuroprotective effects in preclinical studies, several challenges remain. Complete suppression of SCD can compromise the viability of specific neuronal populations, and systemic inhibition is known to disrupt peripheral lipid metabolism. These considerations suggest that partial inhibition or brain‐selective delivery strategies may be necessary to optimize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing adverse effects.

There are limitations to this study. First, the absence of a validation cohort limits the generalizability of the findings; therefore, future studies involving larger, independent cohorts are required for validation. In addition, the relatively small sample size and the incomplete accounting of all medications (eg, statin) and clinical parameters (eg, body mass index) in the analyses may have influenced the results.

In conclusion, our findings show a broad manifestation of MUFA dysregulation in the periphery of patients with PD that align with emerging evidence implicating CNS fatty acid dyshomeostasis and lipid pathway disruption in PD pathogenesis.
