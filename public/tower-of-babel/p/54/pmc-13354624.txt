Endophytic Aspergillus glaucus as a novel epothilone B producer: Antiproliferative activity and in silico analysis
Authors: Ashraf S. A. El-Sayed, Sara Refaat, May A. El-Sayed, Eman Fikry, Nora Tawfeek, Maher M. El-Domiaty, Azza M. El-Shafae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpothilones are among the most potent anticancer agents, particularly against drug-resistant tumors, owing to their high affinity for β-tubulin stabilization, inhibiting the microtubule disassembly, ultimately leading to cell cycle arrest. However, their limited natural availability has restricted broader clinical and industrial exploitation. Accordingly, this study aimed to screen novel fungal isolates with sustainable and high metabolic capacity for epothilone production. Aspergillus glaucus OR342865.1, an endophyte isolated from the palm tree Latania lontaroides, exhibited the highest epothilone productivity (240 µg/L) upon response surface methodology optimization. The chemical identity of the purified compound was resolved by different chromatographic and spectroscopic analyses, revealing a structural profile consistence with authentic epothilone B. The putative epothilone B displayed potent cytotoxic activity against HepG-2, MCF-7, and HCT-116 cell lines, with half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of 0.028, 0.034, and 0.13 µM, with selectivity indices 12.5, 10.3, and 2.7, respectively, relative to Vero cells. The anti-invasive assay demonstrates a significant suppression (35.5–40.2%) of migratory capacity in MCF-7 and HepG-2 cells following treatment, indicating antimetastatic potential. Cell cycle analysis revealed that epothilone treatment induced G1 phase arrest in HepG-2 cells, with a 55.03% increase compared to the control. The total and early apoptotic populations increased to 32.2% and 22.03%, respectively, while late apoptosis exhibited a 57.6-fold elevation relative to untreated cells. The biological productivity of A. glaucus for epothilone B was stably maintained for up to 6 months when preserved as a slope culture at 4 °C. Molecular docking analysis demonstrated that epothilone B exhibits coherent multitarget interactions with key oncogenic kinases, including SRC, EGFR, JAK2, PIK3CA, mTOR, and HSP90, in addition to binding the β-tubulin taxane site, highlighting its capacity to modulate multiple cancer-related signaling pathways. To our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating the high metabolic potential of A. glaucus for epothilone B production, positioning this fungus as a promising platform for sustainable industrial-scale biosynthesis of this clinically valuable anticancer agent.Key points• Aspergillus glaucus, an endophyte of Latania lontaroides, is a potential epothilone B producer.• The purified epothilone has plausible antiproliferative, apoptotic, and antimetastatic activities.• The epothilone had coherent multitarget interactions with key oncogenic kinases and β-tubulin taxane site.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-026-13954-3.

Introduction

Epothilones are natural macrolides that represent hybrid secondary metabolites derived from polyketide and nonribosomal peptide biosynthetic pathways, characterized by epoxide, thiazole, and ketone moieties (Gerth et al. 1996). They were initially isolated from the myxobacterium Sorangium cellulosum and exhibited potent anticancer activity against a wide range of solid tumors (Mühlradt and Sasse 1997). The anticancer efficacy of epothilones is primarily attributed to their high binding affinity for β-tubulin, which stabilizes microtubules and inhibits their depolymerization, thereby arresting cell division at the G2/M phase (Mühlradt and Sasse 1997). Epothilones and Taxol share a common molecular target, β-tubulin, interfering with its function during the cellular mitotic division (Cheng et al. 2008; Villegas et al. 2023). Nevertheless, epothilones possess several favorable biological properties compared with Taxol, including higher aqueous solubility, lower molecular weight, and reduced binding energy toward tubulin (Lee and Swain 2008). These physicochemical advantages contribute to the superior anticancer activity of epothilones, particularly against drug-resistant tumor phenotypes (Gerth et al. 1996). Microtubule-targeting agents are generally classified into two major categories: microtubule-stabilizing agents, such as epothilones and Taxol, and microtubule-destabilizing agents, including vinca alkaloids and colchicine (Pham et al. 2023; Ye et al. 2019; Zhao et al. 2011). Epothilones and Taxol bind to the same recognition site on microtubules, suppressing tumor cell proliferation by inducing microtubule polymerization and stabilization (Chandra 2012; Sohn and Okos 1998). Although epothilones are naturally produced by S. cellulosum, the extremely slow growth rate of this bacterium, together with low epothilone yields, significantly limits its feasibility as a commercial production platform and constrains large-scale production and clinical implementation (Penazzi et al. 2016; Ye et al. 2019). Recently, fungal species such as Aspergillus fumigatus (El-Sayed et al. 2021b, 2025) and Aspergillus niger (Refaat et al. 2024) have been reported to produce epothilone B with a chemical structure identical to that of the authentic compound originally isolated from S. cellulosum. The overall epothilone yield obtained from A. fumigatus and A. niger was approximately 6.2-fold higher than that reported for Burkholderia species (Li et al. 2013). Although the feasibility of fungal culture manipulation for epothilone production is promising, a progressive decline in epothilone productivity during fungal storage and repeated subculturing remains a major challenge. Accordingly, screening for novel fungal isolates exhibiting enhanced epothilone productivity and long-term biosynthetic stability has become a primary research objective. In this context, endophytic fungi inhabiting medicinal plants with established ethnopharmacological relevance are considered promising sources of structurally diverse and biologically active secondary metabolites (El-Sayed et al. 2017, 2022). Members of the family Arecaceae are well recognized for their ethnopharmacological importance and rich phytochemical diversity (Shukla and Dubey 2022). The palm tree Latania lontaroides is a representative species of the Arecaceae family; however, limited studies have investigated its bioactive metabolites or the diversity and biosynthetic potential of its associated endophytic fungi. Accordingly, the present study aimed to isolate and characterize endophytic fungi from L. lontaroides, evaluate their epothilone-producing capacity and biosynthetic stability, and elucidate the chemical identity and biological activity of the produced epothilone.

Materials and methods

Isolation of the fungal endophytes inhibiting L. lontaroides

L. lontaroides (family Arecaceae) was collected from El-Abd Garden, Giza, Egypt, in March/2022, taxonomically authenticated by plant taxonomist Therese Labib, and deposited as a voucher specimen (ZU-Ph-Cog-0516) at the Herbarium of the Pharmacognosy Department, Zagazig University (https://www.cogn.zu.edu.eg). The plant samples were transported to the laboratory, thoroughly rinsed with sterile distilled water, and processed for fungal endophyte isolation (El-Sayed et al. 2018b). Leaf samples were surface-sterilized sequentially with 70% (v/v) ethanol, then 2.5% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite for 2 min, then segmented into small sections and aseptically plated on potato dextrose agar (PDA, 200 g potato extract, 20 g glucose, and 20 g agar per liter) supplemented with ampicillin (1 μg/mL); the plates were incubated at 30 °C for 15 days (Abde-Fatah et al. 2022; El-Sayed et al. 2018a, 2020). The developed fungal colonies were subsequently purified and maintained as slope cultures PDA and Czapek-Dox medium (Raper and Fennel 1965) and identified based on their morphological characteristics by the universal taxonomic keys (Raper and Fennel 1965; Booth 1971; Domsch et al. 1980; Pitt 1979).

Antifungal activity–guided assay and screening for epothilone production

The antifungal activity of ethyl acetate (EA) extracts of the fungal endophytes was evaluated against Aspergillus flavus, as a clinically relevant fungal pathogen (El-Sayed et al. 2021b). The recovered fungal isolates were cultured in PD broth for 10 days at 28 °C, filtered; the filtrates were centrifuged at 5000 rpm; the resulting supernatants were extracted with EA (El-Sayed et al. 2021b), and their biological activity was assessed (Eldeghidy et al. 2023) with fluconazole as a positive control. All the experiments were performed in triplicates. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of the EA extract was determined by saturating sterile paper discs with the different concentrations (1, 5, 10, 25, 50, and 100 μg/ml), placing to the surface of 6-h-old PDA cultures of A. flavus. The cultures were incubated, and the diameters of the inhibition zones were measured and recorded.

The crude EA extracts were preliminarily fractionated using thin layer chromatography (TLC) (silica gel 60 F254, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) with methylene chloride:methanol (95:5, v/v) as the mobile phase (Bollag et al. 1995; Gerth et al. 1996). The TLC plates were visualized by UV at λ247 nm, and the fluorescent spots exhibiting identical Rf values to the authentic epothilone B (Cat# 0.152044–54−7) were considered as positive samples. The corresponding silica gel spots were scraped off, and epothilone was eluted using methylene chloride (Bollag et al. 1995; El-Sayed et al. 2021b; Gerth et al. 1996). The purity of extracted epothilone was assessed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (YOUNG IN Chromass Co., Anyang-si, Gyeonggi-do, Korea) equipped with a RP-C18 column (Cat.#959963-902, Agilent ZORBAX RR Eclipse Plus, Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). The isocratic phase was methanol:acetonitrile:water (25:35:40, v/v/v) at a flow rate 1 mL/min, for 25 min, at absorbance λ247 nm (Gerth et al. 1996; Lau et al. 2002; Molnár et al. 2000; Yue et al. 2018). Epothilone concentration was quantified compared to the retention times and peak areas of the authentic epothilone B (CAS# 152044-54−7, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) (Gerth et al. 1996).

Identification of the selected fungal isolates

The selected fungal isolate was molecularly identified based on the sequence of their internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region (Abdel-Fatah et al. 2021; El-Sayed et al. 2019, 2020, 2021b; Patel et al. 2016). The genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted using cetyltrimethylammonium bromide reagent (CTAB) (Ali et al. 2016) and used as a template for PCR with the primers ITS4 5′-GAAGTAAAAGTCGTAACAAGG-3′ and ITS5 5′-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3′ (El-Sayed et al. 2021a, b). The PCR reaction mixture contains gDNA, 10 pmol of each primer, 10 μL of 2 × PCR master mix (Cat.# 25–027, Boca Scientific Inc., Dedham, Massachusetts, USA), and sterile distilled water to 20 μL total volume; the amplification conditions were performed as previously described (El-Sayed et al. 2023, 2024a). The amplicons were resolved by 2% agarose gel in TBE buffer, compared to DNA ladder (Cat. # PG010-55DI, Nex-gene Ladder, Puregene, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The amplicons were purified and sequenced, and the obtained sequences were nonredundantly aligned with BLAST analysis on NCBI database. The phylogenetic relatedness was conducted by the multiple sequence alignment with ClustalW and neighbor-joining method with 500 bootstrap replications by MEGA X software (Tamura et al. 2007).

Chemical structure validation of the purified epothilone

The functional groups of the putative sample were characterized by Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy (Beckman Coulter Inc., Brea, CA, USA) (400–4000 cm−1) using KBr pellets. The UV-Vis spectra were recorded at wavelength range 200–600 nm with spectrophotometer (RIGOL, Suzhou, China).

The proton nuclear magnetic resonance (HNMR) analysis was conducted with using UltraShield Plus system (Bruker Biospin GmbH, Rheinstetten, Germany) at 400 MHz using CDCl3 as solvents. The chemical shifts and coupling constants were represented in ppm (δ-scale) and hertz (Hz), respectively.

The putative compound was further identified by liquid chromatography-electrospray ionization and tandem mass spectrometry (LC/ESI–MS/MS) (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA), by a Hypersil Gold C18 column and an LCQ Deca mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization source operating in a positive mode. The mobile phase was 0.1% formic acid (solvent A) and acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid (solvent B). A gradient elution was executed from 2 to 98% of solvent B, over 30 min at 0.2 mL/min flow rate. The target precursor ion was subjected to MS/MS fragmentation, and the resulting molecular fragments were interpreted comparing with reported data (El-Sayed et al. 2021b, 2025; Refaat et al. 2024).

Cytotoxicity and anti-invasive activity of the putative epothilone

The cytotoxicity of the EA extract of the selected fungal isolate and the putatively extracted epothilone was evaluated against human breast (MCF-7, ATCC HTB-22), colon (HCT-116, ATCC 116-CCL-247), and hepatocellular carcinoma (HepG-2, ATCC HB-8065), with Vero cells (ATCC CCL-81) as control cells, by the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay (Cory et al. 1991). The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) with 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)−1-piperazineethane-sulfonic acid (HEPES) buffer, GlutaMAX™ (Cat. # 35050061, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA USA), and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 103 cells per well, incubated at 37 °C in humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 for 12 h. Subsequently, the tested compound was added at various concentrations, and the plates were further incubated for 48 h under the same conditions. MTT reagent (25 μL) was added to each well, and the resulting formazan crystals were quantified colorimetrically at λ570 nm. The half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were calculated as the compound concentration causing 50% inhibition of cell viability relative to untreated controls (Cory et al. 1991). The selectivity index (SI) was calculated according to the equation SI = CC50 value (normal cells)/IC50 (tumor cells) (Yousefbeyk et al. 2022).

The anti-invasive activity of purified Aspergillus glaucus EFBL-SR-derived epothilone was evaluated against HepG-2 and MCF-7 cell lines (Gebäck et al. 2009; Jonkman et al. 2014). The cells were seeded into 24-well plate culture at a density of 5 × 106 cells/well and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C to allow the formation of a confluent monolayer. A straight-line scratch was made across the cell layer, followed by gentle washing to remove detached cells, and then, the wells were subsequently supplemented with fresh medium of IC25 concentration of epothilone, compared to 2% dimethyl sulfoxide containing medium as control. The plates were incubated under the same conditions, and the cell migration into the wounded area was monitored microscopically. The percentage of wound closure was assessed based on the gap area in treated samples relative to the control wells.

Apoptosis and cell cycle analyses of HepG-2 cells treated with the purified epothilone

The apoptotic response of the HepG-2 cells was evaluated using an Apoptosis Detection Kit (Cat.# K101-25, BioVision, Milpitas, CA, USA), which relies on externalization of phosphatidylserine (PS) during the apoptosis process. The externalized PS binds with Annexin V-FITC to form an Annexin-PS complex that was detected by flow cytometry (Martin et al. 1995). The HepG-2 cells were seeded into a 96-well plate (2 × 103 cells per well), treated with epothilone IC50 value, and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Cells were subsequently harvested and resuspended in 200 μL of 1 × Annexin-binding buffer, followed by Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) stains. The samples were incubated in the dark for 15 min at room temperature, and apoptotic cell populations were analyzed by flow cytometry at excitation λ488 nm and emission λ530 nm (Edgar 2004).

The effect of purified epothilone on cell cycle of HepG-2 cells was determined using a PI cell cycle analysis kit (Cat. # ab139418, Abcam Ltd., Cambridge, UK). The cells were seeded into 48-well plates and incubated for 12 h at 37 °C, followed by treatment with purified epothilone at the IC50 value and reincubated at the standard culture conditions. The cells were subsequently harvested by centrifugation, fixed in 70% (v/v) ethanol for 2 h at 4 °C, washed, and stained with PI for 30 min in dark. The cellular DNA content was analyzed by flow cytometry at excitation λ493 nm and emission λ636 nm, using FlowJo v10 software package (https://docs.flowjo.com/flowjo/) to assess the ratio of cells in G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases.

Bioprocessing of A. glaucus EFBL-SR nutritional conditions to maximize its yield of epothilone by the response surface methodology

The Plackett–Burman design was employed to screen the significant nutritional parameters affecting epothilone productivity by A. glaucus EFBL-SR (El-Sayed et al. 2021b, 2024a; Refaat et al. 2024). Nineteen variables of the nutritional factors were selected according to our previous study (Refaat et al. 2024), represented by high (+ 1) and low (−1) levels following the first ordered equation: Y = β0 + ΣβiXi.

The predicted yield of epothilone (Y), independent variables (Xi), linear coefficient (βi), and model of intercept (β0) were represented. The experiments were performed in triplicates, and the mean response values were used.

Epothilone productivity of A. glaucus EFBL-SR with storage and effect of plant extracts on triggering the epothilone yield

The biosynthetic stability of epothilone production by A. glaucus with the storage was evaluated. The initially isolated fungal strain was maintained as slope cultures at 4 °C for a period of 10 months, and its epothilone productivity was monitored monthly under the standard conditions as described above.

The influence of L. lontaroides leaf extracts of dichloromethane, EA, and ethanol on epothilone productivity by A. glaucus EFBL-SR was evaluated. Fresh leaves (10 g) of L. lontaroides were pulverized in 50 mL of each solvent, stored at 4 °C for 12 h, and filtered, and the filtrates were centrifuged at 5000 rpm, concentrated to 10 mL of final volume by rotary evaporator. Each plant extract was added to 4-day-old cultures of A. glaucus EFBL-SR of 6-month storage, followed by incubation for 15 days under standard fermentation conditions. Epothilone was subsequently extracted and quantified as described by Refaat et al. (2024). Blank media containing only plant extracts (without A. glaucus inocula) and control cultures of A. glaucus without plant extracts were included in parallel (El-Sayed et al. 2022).

Network pharmacological and molecular docking analyses of epothilone B

Cancer-associated molecular targets of epothilone B were identified in silico. The structure of epothilone B was retrieved from PubChem (Kim et al. 2025), and the experimentally validated protein targets associated with epothilone B were obtained from the DrugBank database (Knox et al. 2024). The potential human targets were predicted using SwissTargetPrediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch) (Daina et al. 2019). The retrieved protein entries were standardized and converted to their official gene symbols using UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org) (Zaru and Orchard 2023). Cancer relevance of the retrieved targets was evaluated using DisGeNET (https://www.disgenet.org) (Piñero et al. 2019). Each gene was queried individually, and its association evidence with neoplastic diseases was assessed based on curated datasets and high-confidence annotations. Genes demonstrating verified links to cancer pathology were classified as cancer-associated targets for subsequent analyses.

Construction of the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network

Following the identification of cancer-associated targets, a PPI network was constructed using the STRING database (version 12.0, https://string-db.org/) (Szklarczyk et al. 2023) with the Homo sapiens proteome and a confidence score threshold of 0.7. The interaction data generated by STRING was subsequently imported into Cytoscape software (ver. 3.10.2; NIGMS, Bethesda, Maryland, USA) (Shannon et al. 2003) for network visualization and topological evaluation. Topological parameters were calculated by the CytoNCA plugin of betweenness centrality (BC), closeness centrality (CC), and degree centrality (DC), which respectively quantify the intermediary role of nodes, their positional proximity within the network, and the number of direct interactions (Fikry et al. 2024; Tang et al. 2015). Hub nodes were identified through a two-step hierarchical filtering strategy. First, nodes with degree centrality values equal to or greater than twice the median were selected. Second, core targets were determined by retaining nodes with BC, CC, and DC values equal to or above the median of each metric. This approach enabled the identification of highly connected and topologically influential proteins that may serve as key regulatory nodes within the epothilone B-associated cancer network.

Molecular docking analysis of epothilone B with the core network targets and β-tubulin

The molecular docking study was conducted to characterize the potential binding of epothilone B to a panel of cancer-associated proteins identified through the prior network pharmacology analysis. Specifically, HSP90AA1, EGFR, PIK3CA, SRC, JAK2, mTOR, HSP90AB1, and PIK3CG were identified as core hub targets, while β-tubulin at the taxane-binding site was included as an experimentally validated target, given its established role in mediating the microtubule-stabilizing activity of epothilone B. Three-dimensional (3D) structures of all proteins were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (Burley et al. 2017) using PDB IDs 6GP8 (Tassone et al. 2018), 1M17 (Stamos et al. 2002), 7PG6 (Gong et al. 2023), 7NG7 (Temps et al. 2021), 6WTN (Chang et al. 2021), 4JSV (Yang et al. 2013), 3NMQ (Yun et al. 2011), 4WWO (Cushing et al. 2015), and 1JFF (Löwe et al. 2001). Protein preparation was performed in UCSF Chimera v1.17.3 (Fikry et al. 2023; Pettersen et al. 2004) following a unified workflow. All crystallographic water molecules, ions, buffer molecules, and nonessential heteroatoms were removed. Co-crystallized ligands were retained only during the binding site identification step and removed before docking. Multimeric structures were reduced to the catalytically relevant chain: The p85α regulatory subunit was removed from 7PG6, nonbinding chains were removed from 4JSV, and only the β-tubulin chain containing the taxane pocket was preserved from 1JFF. Hydrogen addition, charge assignment, and structural optimization were carried out in UCSF Chimera (Pettersen et al. 2004). The cleaned receptors were then exported to PDBQT format in AutoDockTools; the Gasteiger charges were applied and nonpolar hydrogens merged (Forli et al. 2016).

Epothilone B was obtained from PubChem in 3D Structure Data File (SDF) format, its stereochemistry verified, hydrogens added, and the structure energy-minimized and converted to PDBQT using OpenBabel 2.4.1 (O'Boyle et al. 2011). For each receptor, the docking grid was centered on the centroid of the co-crystallized ligand present in the original crystal structure (AMPCPP in 6GP8, erlotinib in 1M17, 1LT in 7PG6, the SRC inhibitor in 7NG7, ruxolitinib in 6WTN, the mTOR inhibitor in 4JSV, EC44 in 3NMQ, the PI3Kγ inhibitor in 4WWO, and paclitaxel in 1JFF). The exact grid coordinates are provided in Supplementary Table S1, and a unified cubic grid box of 25 × 25 × 25 Å was applied across all receptors. To validate the docking protocol and establish comparative binding benchmarks, the native ligands were re-docked into their respective receptor structures using the same grid parameters and scoring function prior to ligand docking experiments. Docking simulations were then performed using AutoDock Vina v1.1.2 (Trott and Olson 2010) with standardized settings (exhaustiveness 32, energy range 4 kcal/mol, 10 output poses), and the resulting conformations were ranked according to their predicted binding affinities.

Protein–ligand interactions were evaluated using Discovery Studio Visualizer v21.1.0.20298 (Biovia 2021) and UCSF Chimera (Pettersen et al. 2004). Docked complexes were assessed to confirm correct placement within the binding cavity, and two-dimensional interaction diagrams were generated to characterize hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, π-alkyl contacts, and van der Waals contributions. Representative docked conformations, along with accompanying 2D interaction diagrams, were prepared to illustrate the key protein–ligand interactions.

Fungal deposition

The ITS sequence of A. glaucus EFBL-SR was deposited into the GenBank with accession # OR342865 and preserved at the Enzymology and Fungal Biotechnology Lab, Faculty of Science, Zagazig University, with deposition # EFBL-SR025.

Statistical analysis

The screening and nutritional bioprocessing analyses were conducted in biological triplicates. The epothilone productivity was expressed by means ± SD. The F-test and statistical significance were determined by ANOVA, with Fisher’s least significant difference post hoc test.

Results

Isolation of the fungal endophytes of L. lontaroides and their antifungal activity

Nine endophytic fungal isolates were isolated from L. lontaroides leaves on PDA medium. Based on the fungal morphological characteristics, the isolates were identified as A. glaucus, A. fumigatus, Aspergillus candidus, A. niger, Aspergillus tamarii, Aspergillus oryzae, Alternaria alternata, Mucor fragilis, and Cladosporium cladosporioides (Table 1). The isolates were cultivated on PDB medium under standard conditions, after which the cultures were extracted with EA. The antifungal activity–guided assay of the fungal EA extracts was evaluated against A. flavus, as a model human fungal pathogen. Based on inhibition zone diameters (Fig. 1A), the EA extracts of A. glaucus, A. alternata, and A. fumigatus exhibited the highest inhibition of A. flavus, with inhibition zones of 23.4, 21.5, and 18.5 mm, respectively. Moderate antifungal activity was observed for the EA extracts of A. candidus and M. fragilis (15.3–15.5 mm), whereas lower inhibitory effects were recorded for A. niger, A. tamarii, and A. oryzae, with inhibition zones ranging from 10.1 to 12.5 mm (Table 1). Accordingly, the MIC values were determined for the most active extracts; the antifungal activity of these extracts was noticeably a concentration-dependent manner, confirming the bioactive potential of the tested fungal isolates. As shown in Fig. 1B, the MIC values of the EA extracts of A. glaucus, A. alternata, A. fumigatus, and M. fragilis for A. flavus were 17.6, 20.1, 23.4, and 25.3 μg/mL, respectively, compared to 15.8 μg/mL for fluconazole as the reference antifungal agent. Screening for epothilone production and preliminary purification

Based on the antifungal activity–guided assay, the most active fungal isolates (A. glaucus, A. alternata, A. fumigatus, and M. fragilis) were selected for the subsequent screening of epothilone production. The EA extracts of the four isolates were fractionated by TLC and compared to authentic epothilone B, as visualized by UV at λ254 nm, as described in “Materials and methods.” As shown in the TLC profile (Fig. 1C), the EA extracts of A. glaucus and A. alternata exhibited distinct spots with identical color and Rf values to the authentic epothilone, indicating the presence of putative epothilone. The highest epothilone yield was detected in A. glaucus (119.8 μg/L), followed by A. alternata (55.2 μg/L), as semiquantitatively determined by the TLC. The silica gel spots corresponding to the putative epothilone were scraped from the TLC plates, eluted with EA for epothilone extraction. The purity and concentration of the target compounds were further evaluated by HPLC analysis. As shown in the HPLC chromatograms (Fig. 1D), the TLC-purified compounds from A. glaucus and A. alternata exhibited identical retention times (3.4 min) to the authentic epothilone B under the same conditions. Based on the peak area analysis, the epothilone yields from A. glaucus and A. alternata were 105.3 and 52.7 μg/L, with purification efficiencies of 85% and 82%, respectively, as calculated by the ratio of the main epothilone peak area to the total chromatographic peak area.

Identification of the potent epothilone-producing fungi

Based on the antifungal activity and epothilone productivity, the isolate A. glaucus EFBL-SR was selected for the extensive morphological identification and molecular confirmation. The morphological characteristics of the isolate EFBL-SR were examined on PDA and Czapek–Dox agar media for 10 days at 30 °C, with daily macroscopic and microscopic inspections. The developing colonies initially exhibited a grayish to deep-green surface coloration, with a pale-yellow central zone, of pale yellow to light reverse side (Fig. 2B). The fungal colony was moderately deep, with sulfur-orange mycelia, a floccose texture, and sparse sporulation forming masses of pale-green conidia (Fig. 2C). Subglobose vesicles, uniseriate sterigmata, and the phialides cover the entire vesicle, these features typically consistent to those of A. glaucus (L.) Link (Chen et al. 2017; Clarke and Griffiths 1970; Raper and Fennel 1965). The identity of the fungal isolate was molecularly confirmed by ITS sequence analysis using gDNA as a PCR template. The PCR amplicon of ~ 650 bp was purified, sequenced, and nonredundantly BLAST searched on the NCBI database. The ITS sequence gave 99.8% similarity with zero E value with the sequences of A. glaucus and was deposited to the GenBank with accession # OR342865.1. From the phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 2D), two major clades of the ITS sequence of A. glaucus were resolved, namely, Clade I (Aspergillus montevidensis group) and Clade II (Aspergillus amstelodami group). The ITS sequence of A. glaucus EFBL-SR clustered within Clade II, showing 99.8% similarity with A. amstelodami (KX674617.1 and MK036407.1) and Aspergillus cristatus (KY828871.1 and KF923732.1). In contrast, approximately 99.1% similarity was observed with A. montevidensis (OW985234.1, PV277823.1, OU989271.1, and OW982449.1) within Clade I. Chemical structure elucidation of epothilone extracted from A. glaucus

The putative epothilone of A. glaucus was characterized using UV-Vis spectroscopy, FT-IR, and LC–MS/MS analyses. The UV-Vis spectrum exhibited a maximum absorption at λ247 nm, consistent with the previous UV spectrum of epothilone B (Fig. 3A) (El-Sayed et al. 2021b; Gerth et al. 1996; Refaat et al. 2024). The FT-IR spectrum displayed characteristic absorption bands corresponding to hydroxyl (O–H) stretching at 3263 cm−1 and carbonyl (C = O) stretching at 1694 cm−1. Aliphatic C-H stretching vibrations were observed at 2985 cm−1, while the absorption region between 1612 and 1437 cm−1 were attributed to the ester functional groups. The absorption bands at 1237 cm−1 and 1100 cm−1 refer to the presence of an epoxy ring and aromatic ring stretching, respectively (Fig. 3B). Stability of epothilone productivity by A. glaucus with storage and effect of different L. lontaroides extracts

The stability of epothilone productivity by A. glaucus was assessed during storage at 4 °C up to 10 months, compared to the zero “first” culture. From the results in Supplementary Fig. S1, the epothilone productivity by A. glaucus was obviously suppressed with the storage period, reaching to 49.41 and 16.58 μg/L, after 6 and 10 months, i.e., 2.5 and 7.3 reduction folds, respectively, compared to the initial yield (119.8 μg/L). The biological half-life time for epothilone productivity by A. glaucus was about 5 months of storage as slope culture at 4 °C. The impact of different L. lontaroides leaf extracts on restoring and inducing the epothilone productivity by A. glaucus was assessed using ethanolic, dichloromethane, and EA extracts. A 4-day-old culture of the sixth month-stored A. glaucus was amended with the extracts, incubated under standard conditions and their epothilone production was quantified. From the results in Supplementary Fig. S1, the epothilone productivity by A. glaucus has been partially restored by EA plant extract (70.42 μg/L), compared to the sixth month-stored culture (49.41 μg/L), i.e., 1.4 increment folds. However, the dichloromethane and ethanolic extracts had no obvious effect on restoring epothilone productivity by A. glaucus.

Nutritional optimization of A. glaucus to maximizing its epothilone yield by the response surface methodology

Epothilone production by A. glaucus was enhanced through nutritional optimization using response surface methodology. Given the critical role of medium composition in regulating microbial secondary metabolism, a systematic statistical approach was applied to evaluate the influence of nutritional and physiological factors on secondary biosynthesis (El-Sayed et al. 2021b; El-Sayed et al. 2019; Eldeghidy et al. 2023). From the screening profiles of the 19 nutritional, physiological factors, and growth elicitors influencing epothilone production by A. glaucus, the matrix of the actual and predicted yields based on the Plackett–Burman design is summarized in Table 2. The highest actual and predicted yields of epothilone by A. glaucus were 240.02 and 220.55 μg/L, respectively, and were reported at run #11, with residuals 19.47 μg/L, while the lowest yield of epothilone (29.22 μg/L) was detected at run #20. The statistical significance of the model was conformed from the F value 5.13 and a low noise level of 0.66%; moreover, the Prob > F value (< 0.05) further supports the statistical significance of the model in Supplementary Table S2. The predicted R-square value (0.297) showed a reasonable agreement with the adjusted R-squared value (0.566), indicating acceptable predictive capability. In addition, an Adeq. precision ratio of 8.66 demonstrated an adequate signal-to-noise ratio. The Plackett–Burman design exhibited a coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.78), reflecting a good fit of the linear regression model to the experimental data. Network pharmacological and molecular docking analyses of epothilone B

The curated drug–protein interactions retrieved from the DrugBank, with structure-based predicted targets generated using SwissTargetPrediction, yielded a combined set of 119 nonredundant human genes associated with epothilone B in Supplementary Table S1. Subsequent evaluation of these genes using DisGeNET identified 86 targets associated to malignant disease processes. These cancer-associated targets were subsequently selected for downstream PPI network analyses. The potential anticancer mechanism of epothilone B was investigated by subjecting its cancer-associated targets to protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis using the STRING database. A confidence cutoff of 0.7 was applied, and isolated nodes were removed, resulting in a curated PPI network composed of 74 nodes and 285 edges in Supplementary Fig. S3. Topological characterization of the network was subsequently performed in Cytoscape 3.10.2 using the CytoNCA plugin, which calculated key centrality metrics, including degree centrality (DC), betweenness centrality (BC), and closeness centrality (CC) (Table 3). Hub genes were identified through a two-tiered filtering strategy. In the first stage, nodes with DC values ≥ 10 (twice the network median) were retained, generating a condensed subnetwork of 23 nodes connected by 132 edges (Table 3). A second round of stringent filtering, based on median-derived thresholds (BC ≥ 175.32, CC ≥ 0.24, and DC ≥ 14), further refined the network to a core module consisting of 8 nodes and 24 edges (Supplementary Fig. S3). Ranking these nodes by DC values revealed HSP90AA1, EGFR, PIK3CA, SRC, JAK2, HSP90AB1, MTOR, and PIK3CG as the dominant hub genes (Table 4). Collectively, this systematic topological evaluation pinpointed pivotal molecular targets that may mediate the therapeutic actions of epothilone B in cancer, highlighting their central regulatory roles within the underlying PPI architecture. Epothilone B adopted stable conformations within all investigated active sites, forming combinations of hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts, π-interactions, and van der Waals contacts that collectively supported its capacity to engage multiple cancer-associated targets. Discussion

Epothilones are macrolides originally produced by myxobacterium S. cellulosum and are recognized for their potent anticancer activity to a wide range of tumor types (Bollag et al. 1995; Chou et al. 1998). Their mechanism of action is primarily attributed to a high affinity for β-tubulin microtubule arrays, where they inhibit microtubule disassembly and promote polymerization, ultimately inducing apoptosis and arresting cell division at the G2/M phase (Molnár et al. 2000). A major pharmaceutical advantage of epothilones over Taxol is their high water solubility, which eliminates the need for adjuvants in formulation and delivery (Forli 2014). In contrast, taxanes are only partially water soluble and require formulation with polysorbate 80 and Cremophor EL, excipients that are associated with severe hypersensitivity reactions and impaired cardiac function (Forli 2014; Rowinsky et al. 1993; Yared and Tkaczuk 2012). In addition to these favorable pharmacokinetic properties, epothilones exhibit strong activity against taxane-resistant tumor cells and do not provoke proinflammatory cytokine release, owing to the absence of endotoxin-associated components; thus, epothilones have been approved for the treatment of advanced/aggressively metastatic breast cancer (Rowinsky et al. 1993). Despite these advantages, the limited yield and availability of epothilones remain the major challenges. Commercial production from S. cellulosum is severely constrained by its extremely low productivity and slow growth rate, even after extensive attempts to enhance yield through nutritional optimization and heterologous expression of epothilone biosynthetic genes (Cao et al. 2011; El-Sayed et al. 2021b). Consequently, alternative biological sources have been explored, particularly endophytic fungi associated with medicinal plants. Previous studies have demonstrated the capacity of fungal endophytes such as A. fumigatus, isolated from Catharanthus roseus (El-Sayed et al. 2021b), and A. niger, isolated from Latania loddigesii (Refaat et al. 2024), to produce epothilone. However, low production yields and rapid attenuation of epothilone biosynthesis during storage and repeated subculturing remain persistent limitations (El-Sayed et al. 2021b; Refaat et al. 2024).

In the present study, a bioactivity-guided selection strategy was employed to identify a potent epothilone-producing endophytic fungus from L. lontaroides, a plant species with no previous reports on its biological activities or associated endophytic microbial communities. Initial screening focused on the antifungal activity of EA extracts, rather than purified metabolites, allowing prioritization of metabolically active isolates based on their overall bioactive potential. Among the recovered endophytes, A. glaucus exhibited the strongest antifungal activity against A. flavus, as reflected by the largest inhibition zone and the lowest MIC value. The rationale of using antifungal guided assay as a preliminary indicator for the cytotoxic activity is the similarity of the metabolic pathways for the rapid cell division and nucleic acid biosynthesis of the fungal cells and tumor cells (Kondaka et al. 2025). A. flavus is the second most prevalent opportunistic pathogen that causes both invasive and superficial infections in humans, exhibiting the highest levels of morbidity and mortality (Rudramurthy et al. 2019).

Subsequent chemical screening revealed that A. glaucus also possessed the highest epothilone production capacity among the antifungal-active isolates. TLC and HPLC analyses confirmed the presence of epothilone, justifying the selection of A. glaucus for further purification and structural elucidation. The isolate A. glaucus OR342865.1 was the highest epothilone producer 105.3 µg/L that was further enhanced through nutritional screening by the Plackett–Burman design. Since medium composition and factor interactions play a critical role in regulating fungal secondary metabolite biosynthesis (El-Sayed et al. 2021a, 2022; Eldeghidy et al. 2023), application of this design resulted in a twofold increase in epothilone yield, reaching 240.02 µg/L. The yield of the fungal epothilone B was slightly lower than that of S. cellulosum (50 mg/L (Gong et al. 2007) and 82.0 mg/L (Cao et al. 2011)). However, the feasibility of molecular manipulation and nutritional optimization of fungi to maximize their epothilone productivity reinforces their potential as a promising industrial platform for epothilone production. This optimized yield is comparable to that reported for A. fumigatus (60 µg/g) from C. roseus (El-Sayed et al. 2021b) and A. niger (266.9 µg/L) from L. loddigesii (Refaat et al. 2024), underscoring the production potential of A. glaucus. Similar response surface–based optimization strategies have been reported for improving the production of epothilone, camptothecin, and Taxol by fungi (El-Sayed et al. 2021a, b; Eldeghidy et al. 2023). A significant decline in productivity was observed by the sixth month, with epothilone yield decreasing to 49.41 µg/L, corresponding to a 2.4-fold reduction, relative to the initial culture. Notably, partial restoration of epothilone production was achieved upon supplementation with the EA extract of L. lontaroides, increasing yield by approximately 1.4-fold to 70.42 µg/L. This recovery suggests the presence of chemical signals within the plant extract that stimulate the epothilone biosynthesis. Similar attenuation and partial restoration patterns have been reported for A. fumigatus and A. niger, as well as for other fungal secondary metabolites such as polyketides and terpenoids during prolonged storage (Refaat et al. 2024).

Collectively, the results of HPLC, UV-Vis, FT-IR, 1HNMR, and LC–MS/MS analyses confirmed that the purified target compound from A. glaucus was epothilone B, based on its identical functional groups, structural features, molecular skeleton, proton distribution pattern, diagnostic molecular ion peak (m/z 508.302), and fragmentation pattern, which were consistent with those of authentic epothilone B (El-Sayed et al. 2021b; 2025; Refaat et al. 2024). The IC50 values of the purified epothilone were 0.028, 0.034, and 0.13 μM for HepG-2, MCF-7, and HCT-116 cells, selectivity indices of 12.5, 10.3, and 2.7, respectively, compared to Vero cells. These findings are consistent with previous reports describing the anticancer activity of epothilone produced by A. fumigatus against multiple cancer cell lines (El-Sayed et al. 2021b), further supporting the role of fungal-derived epothilone as a potent and selective anticancer agent. Invasive aspergillosis, predominantly caused by A. flavus and A. fumigatus, occurs more frequently than invasive candidiasis, particularly among immunocompromised patients (Badr et al. 2021; Pfaller et al. 2006). A. flavus has been recognized as a principal etiological agent of invasive fungal rhinosinusitis, otitis, and pulmonary infections in cancer patients and is often associated with marked resistance to commonly used antifungal therapies (Hedayati et al. 2007). In addition, A. flavus has been implicated in a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations, including chronic granulomatous invasive fungal sinusitis, cutaneous aspergillosis, keratitis, and osteomyelitis following traumatic inoculation (Badr et al. 2021; Pfaller et al. 2006). So, the dual antifungal and anticancer activity of A. glaucus epothilone raises the pharmacokinetic prospective of this compound, with particular relevance in immunocompromised cancer patients, which the invasive fungal infections frequently coexist with tumor progression. Accordingly, the combined antifungal and anticancer properties of A. glaucus-derived epothilone may represent a promising and cost-effective therapeutic strategy with potential clinical relevance (Refaat et al. 2024). Practically, A. glaucus was not reported as producer of highly regulated, potent mycotoxins as aflatoxins or ochratoxins, with its ability to produce a narrow range of mycotoxins (Greco et al. 2015). The target compound “epothilone” was purified and its purity was checked by TLC and HPLC prior to the cytotoxic analyses.

The anti-invasive activity of purified epothilone of A. glaucus was evaluated in MCF-7 and HepG-2 cells. Epothilone treatment significantly suppressed the wound closure in both cells compared to untreated controls. Specifically, wound closure was reduced by approximately 35.0 and 40.2% to MCF-7 and HepG-2 cells (p < 0.05). These findings indicate that epothilone effectively inhibits cell migration and regenerative capacity, supporting its potential role in suppressing tumor progression and metastatic behavior. The effect of A. glaucus epothilone on cell cycle progression and apoptosis was investigated in HepG-2 cells at its IC50 value. The epothilone treatment predominantly induced cell cycle–associated growth arrest at the G1 phase, as evidenced by the increased growth arrest percentage compared to control cells. This observation suggests that, under the applied experimental conditions, epothilone interfered primarily with early cell cycle progression rather than later mitotic phases. Concomitantly, epothilone treatment resulted in a marked induction of apoptotic cell death in HepG-2 cells, as confirmed by Annexin V-FITC/PI staining. Quantitative analysis revealed substantial increases in total apoptosis (13.3-fold), early apoptosis (24.5-fold), and late apoptosis (57.6-fold) relative to untreated control cells, indicating that apoptosis represents a major mechanism underlying the antiproliferative effect of epothilone in this model (Gan et al. 2011; Reichenbach and Höfle 2008). However, emerging evidence indicates that epothilone-mediated cell cycle modulation may vary depending on concentration, exposure time, production source, and cellular context. Epothilone B produced by fungal sources has previously been reported to induce G1 phase arrest in HepG-2 cells. Notably, epothilone B obtained from A. fumigatus under solid-state fermentation conditions was shown to exert pronounced antiproliferative activity in HepG-2 cells, accompanied by G1 cell cycle arrest and tubulin polymerization effects (El-Sayed et al. 2024b). Epothilone has been known to halt the cellular growth by inducing the tubulin polymerization, stabilizing the microtubule assembly, preventing the mitotic spindle, causing cell cycle arrest at G2/M phase (Mühlradt and Sasse 1997). However, epothilone B from A. glaucus inhibits the cell cycle at G1 phase that might have an indirect effect on cyclin-dependent kinase regulation, cyclin D/E expression, inhibiting the epidermal growth factor receptor EGFR, PI3-kinase, tyrosine-protein kinase SRC, tyrosine-protein kinase JAK2, mTOR (Schirripa et al. 2022). Collectively, these findings indicate that the effects of epothilone on cell cycle regulation depend on the experimental and cellular context and may involve different cell cycle checkpoints that act before the initiation of apoptosis.

The network pharmacological and molecular docking analyses of epothilone B were conducted to assess their predictive interactions with the different cellular enzymes, to elucidate their potential anticancer mechanisms. Evaluation of the combined target list in DisGeNET yielded 84 genes with a direct relation to cancer, as confirmed from the PPI network analysis by STRING revealing a highly interconnected structure, with several proteins exhibiting a strong centrality. The topological evaluation by CytoNCA refers to eight core targets with the highest influence within the network, namely, HSP90AA1, EGFR, PIK3CA, SRC, JAK2, MTOR, HSP90AB1, and PIK3CG. The central positioning of these nodes confirms that the activity of epothilone B could be for the interaction with the broader regulatory landscape that complements its well-established microtubule-stabilizing properties (Cheng et al. 2008; Villegas et al. 2023). Although tubulin represents the primary molecular binding site of epothilone B, it did not emerge among the topological hub genes within the PPI network. This observation is consistent with the fact that tubulin functions mainly as a structural cytoskeletal component with limited signaling connectivity, which inherently reduces its network centrality (Zocchi et al. 2023). Nevertheless, its absence from the core module does not diminish its mechanistic importance, since microtubule stabilization remains the direct cytotoxic action of epothilone B (Ezzo and Etienne-Manneville 2025; Jiménez et al. 2017), while the identified hub targets reflect broader regulatory nodes that are indirectly influenced within cancer-associated networks. The biological functions of these core targets align closely with key hallmarks of cancer. The HSP90 isoforms (HSP90AA1 and HSP90AB1) act as molecular chaperones that stabilize a wide array of oncogenic proteins, with a noticeable activity to support tumor survival, maintain proteostasis under cellular stress, and contribute to resistance to apoptosis (Albakova 2024; Basset et al. 2023; Neckers and Workman 2012; Xiang et al. 2025). EGFR plays a critical role in proliferative and survival signaling and is frequently overexpressed in several malignancies, driving an aggressive growth and metastatic progression (Aldonza et al. 2021; Ali and Wendt 2017; Seshacharyulu et al. 2012; Uribe et al. 2021). SRC functions as a nonreceptor tyrosine kinase that integrates signals from growth factors and adhesion receptors, promoting cytoskeletal remodeling, invasion. and metastatic dissemination (Chen et al. 2025; Huang et al. 2025; Lieu and Kopetz 2010; Zhang and Yu 2012). PIK3CA and PIK3CG are key catalytic components of the PI3K pathway that regulates cell proliferation, metabolism, and survival, and the dysregulation of this pathway contributes to the drug resistance of tumor cells (Li et al. 2024; Schmitz et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2024). MTOR acts as a central metabolic regulator that controls protein synthesis and cellular energy balance, allowing tumor cells to sustain rapid growth and adapt to nutrient or stress conditions (Marafie et al. 2024). From the PPI analysis, epothilone B may influence key signaling pathways that promote tumor development and progression. The emergence of these proteins as dominant hub nodes reinforces the systems-level profile of epothilone B and indicates that its therapeutic potential extends beyond microtubule stabilization to include the modulation of pivotal signaling hubs that maintain oncogenic network integrity. Docking analysis demonstrated that epothilone B possesses a coherent multitarget interaction profile, engaging the oncogenic kinase SRC, EGFR, JAK2, PIK3CA, PIK3CG, and mTOR, as well as the molecular chaperones HSP90AA1 and HSP90AB1, in addition to the β-tubulin taxane site. The predicted binding energies, which ranged from −6.6 to −9.3 kcal/mol, indicate that the compound maintains stable and energetically favorable interactions across structurally diverse protein families. This pattern reflects the versatility of epothilone B and supports its potential to influence multiple cancer-related pathways. The strongest affinities were observed for SRC, EGFR, and JAK2. These interactions involved dense hydrophobic contacts and π-stacking with aromatic residues within the ATP-binding pockets. Such features imply high structural complementarity and suggest that epothilone B may interfere with kinase-regulated processes that govern proliferation, cell motility, and adaptive resistance (Beyett et al. 2022; Hubbard 2018; Raji et al. 2024; Shaban et al. 2024). The notable interactions with mTOR, HSP90AA1, and HSP90AB1 further expand the mechanistic scope. The affinity toward mTOR also indicates potential interference with metabolic regulation and cell growth, which are essential components of cancer cell survival (Mafi et al. 2022; Marafie et al. 2024; Marques-Ramos and Cervantes 2023). The interaction with PIK3CG was comparatively weak, although the ligand still adopted a coherent binding orientation, which can be attributed to the smaller and more confined binding pocket characteristic of this isoform. Given the central role of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling axis in regulating cancer cell survival and metabolic adaptation (Glaviano et al. 2023; He et al. 2021; Jiang et al. 2025; Peng et al. 2022), even with limited engagement with these kinases may still contribute incrementally to the overall biological activity of epothilone B. Epothilone B adopted a stable conformation within the β-tubulin taxane pocket, engaging key residues through hydrophobic and π-alkyl contacts that hold the ligand firmly within the cavity. The overall interaction pattern supports effective occupancy of the binding site and is consistent with a functional microtubule-stabilizing interaction (Schmitt et al. 2024). Collectively, the findings indicate that epothilone B operates through a poly-pharmacological mechanism, engaging multiple structural and signaling proteins in a coordinated manner. This broad interaction profile is consistent with the compound’s potent antiproliferative and proapoptotic activities as reported in previous studies and highlights its therapeutic potential beyond microtubule stabilization. The overall trends support a dual-mechanism model in which epothilone B induces direct cytotoxicity through β-tubulin binding while simultaneously influencing key signaling nodes that regulate proliferation, survival, and metastatic behavior. The combined disruption of these structural and regulatory pathways provides a coherent explanation for its strong anticancer efficacy and suggests an expanded therapeutic breadth within complex tumor networks.

In conclusion, the isolate A. glaucus OR342865.1, an endophyte of L. lontaroides, was identified as a promising source for biologically active epothilone B. A bioactivity-guided selection strategy, followed by systematic nutritional optimization, resulted in a significant enhancement of epothilone B yield, highlighting the critical role of medium composition in regulating fungal secondary metabolite biosynthesis. Comprehensive chemical characterization confirmed the structural identity of the purified compound, while biological evaluation demonstrated markedly improved anticancer potency and selectivity compared to tested cell lines. Furthermore, integrated in vitro assays, network pharmacology, and molecular docking analyses support a dual-mechanism model in which epothilone B exerts direct cytotoxic effects through β-tubulin stabilization while simultaneously modulating key oncogenic signaling hubs involved in proliferation, survival, and metastatic behavior. Collectively, these findings reinforce the therapeutic relevance of endophytic fungi as alternative and economically viable platforms for epothilone B production and support further development of fungal-derived epothilone B as a multifunctional anticancer agent.
