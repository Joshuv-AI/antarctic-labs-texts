Prevalence and predictors for cisplatin-induced toxicities in Zimbabwean women with cervical cancer
Authors: Oppah Kuguyo, Alice Matimba, Mugove G Madziyire, Thulani Magwali, Collet Dandara, Charles FB Nhachi, Nomsa Tsikai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAim: To describe treatment-induced toxicities (TITs) and associated factors in Zimbabwean cancer patients receiving cisplatin.Methods: In total, 252 Zimbabwean women with cervical cancer, receiving cisplatin were followed up over 12 months for TITs and disease status.Results: Peripheral neuropathy (70%) and ototoxicity (53%) were most prevalent. Advanced disease (OR = 1.3; 95% CI = 1.1–1.5; p = 0.02), pain comedications (OR = 1.3; 95% CI = 1.1–1.5; p = 0.03), alcohol (OR = 2.8; 95% CI = 1.1–7.5; p = 0.04) and comorbidities (OR = 1.2; 95% CI = 1.1–1.4; p = 0.04) increased peripheral neuropathy and ototoxicity risk. Older age increased risk of disease progression (OR = 1.9; 95% CI = 1.4–3.0; p = 0.033).Conclusion: High peripheral neuropathy and ototoxicity prevalence were observed, which are not routinely monitored in Zimbabwe. There is a need for capacity building to incorporate comprehensive TIT testing and optimize cancer care in Zimbabwe.Keywords: : Africa, chemotherapy related toxicity, cisplatin-induced toxicity, deep vein thrombosis, ototoxicity, peripheral neuropathy, personalized medicine, renal injury, treatment-induced toxicities, Zimbabwe

Background

Cisplatin is one of the first-in-line treatments for cancers of the ovary, testes, esophagus, head and neck, lung, intestine, prostate, skin, bladder and cervix across the globe [1,2]. Cisplatin has a narrow therapeutic index, and so, is associated with high rates of treatment-induced toxicities (TITs) and wide-ranging response rates (between 20-85%) [3,4]. It was previously thought that high cumulative doses of cisplatin were solely responsible for TITs and varying cure rates that are observed between patients [5]. Consequently, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines recommended lowering cisplatin doses and co-administration of other chemotherapeutic agents or radiotherapy to achieve radical goals [6]. These advances have decreased, but not eliminated adverse outcomes of cisplatin such as TITs, potentiating the role of other driving factors.

Factors such as age, performance status, co-medications, co-morbidities and the presence of metastasis have been established as propagators for TITs and are key considerations in prescribing chemotherapy [4,7]. Moreover, racial and ethnic differences in susceptibility to TITs and other adverse outcomes of treatment have also been observed [8]. Particularly, individuals with African ancestral background are more likely to develop TITs and have poor survival rates compared with European, Asian and Hispanic ancestry populations administered with the same treatment and dosing regimens [9–11]. The role of ethnic diversity in response to treatment is multifactorial. Genetic diversity is illustrated to contribute most significantly to TIT susceptibility differences by ethnicity [8,12]. African ancestry is associated with admixture and heterogeneity, therefore, allelic variations in some pharmacology genes can alter drug bioavailability, thereby increasing the risk of TITs [13]. Other factors such as low socioeconomic status, and longer time between diagnosis and treatment as observed in African ancestry populations, have also been widely reported to contribute to the racial and ethnic differences in TIT development and treatment outcomes [14,15].

Even with the contribution of ethnicity to TITs, there are very limited studies analysing the prevalence and risk factors of TITs among African populations [3,16,17]. In Zimbabwe, only 3 known studies describe TITs experienced by cancer patients, reporting on hematological toxicity, renal injury, gastrointestinal toxicity and reproductive toxicities [18–20]. This leaves a host of other TITs under-reported in these populations, potentiating under-management. Most resource-limited settings are severely understaffed and incapacitated, as a result, there are a few TITs that are routinely managed [21].

This study aims to report on the prevalence of TITs experienced after cisplatin administration and describe risk factors associated with TITs using a Zimbabwean cervical cancer cohort as a model. Cervical cancer is a leading cancer and cause of cancer-related deaths (18%) in Zimbabwe, due to late diagnosis of patients and high comorbidity rates [22,23]. A recent study also highlighted treatment limitations in Zimbabwe, because of prolonged radiotherapy machine downtimes in the country, which collectively result in patients requiring aggressive treatment regimens with cisplatin, further exacerbating TITs [18,23]. The literature demonstrates that cervical cancer management using cisplatin and/or radiotherapy is associated with gastrointestinal, genitourinary and hemotologic toxicities [16–20]. We previously conducted a retrospective analysis of TITs observed in Zimbabwean cervical cancer patients that received treatment between the years 2012 and 2017 and found deep vein thrombosis, vesicovaginal fistula, emesis, renal injury and peripheral neuropathy [24]. However, this study was conducted retrospectively and reported high loss to follow-up of patients during treatment. This highlights a need for prospective follow-up of patients for TITs, to understand the context-specific burden of TITs and associated risks for cancer patients receiving cisplatin in a resource-limited setting.

Materials & methods

Ethical approval & informed consent

Ethical approval to conduct this research was obtained from institutional and national ethical review boards, namely, the University of Zimbabwe Faculty of Health and Medical Sciences and Parirenyatwa Group of Hospitals Joint Research Ethics Committee (412/16), and Medical Research Council of Zimbabwe (A/2153) respectively. All study participants provided consent to enrol in the study in writing.

Research design & setting

This prospective study followed participants over 12 months. Participants were identified and recruited at the Parirenyatwa Group of Hospitals Radiotherapy and Chemotherapy Centre (RTC), a central outpatient oncology centre in the public health sector, in Harare Zimbabwe.

Study cohort

Recruitment occurred between June 2016 and January 2019, as part of the biorepository for the pharmacogenomics of cervical cancer cohort [25]. The key eligibility criteria are summarized in Table 1. All individuals recruited provided written informed consent to participate in the study and met all the eligibility criteria. In total, 410 women were recruited as part of the biorepository for pharmacogenomics of cervical cancer cohort, and of these, 252 had complete data on TITs of interest in this study [25]. Therefore, this study reports on a total of 252 women, based on the availability of complete follow-up data.

Recruitment & data collection

Eligible participants were informed of the study verbally and provided consent in writing. Sociodemographic and behavioural data including age, weight, height, HIV status, comorbidities, alcohol consumption and tobacco smoking were collected from participants through face-to-face interviews. Clinical information such as disease stage, presence of metastatic disease, type of anti-cancer treatment administered, treatment dose and co-medications such as pain drugs were collected from clinical records. Participants were also followed up for TITs before anticancer therapy commencement and over 12 months. Secondary treatment outcomes were also collected at the 12-month time-point from clinical records.

Phenotype characterization

Treatment-induced toxicity follow-up

This study focused on eight TITs, namely deep vein thrombosis (DVT), renal injury, peripheral neuropathy, ototoxicity, radiation proctitis, vaginal stenosis, vesicovaginal fistula (VVF) and vestibulopathy. These TITs were of interest due to the limited available data on Zimbabwe in the literature. Five of these TITs – DVT, radiation proctitis, vaginal stenosis and VVF were abstracted from the medical records (Table 2) before treatment and 12 months after treatment commencement. Serum creatinine clearance, a marker for renal injury, was also abstracted from the clinical records before treatment, and at 3-, 6- and 12-month time points. The other three TITs – peripheral neuropathy, ototoxicity and vestibulopathy, are not routinely managed in primary care in Zimbabwe, and so were evaluated for lack of published data on cancer in Zimbabwean populations at pre-treatment, 3-, 6- and 12- month time points. All TIT evaluations in this study were performed at routine follow-up visits, before participants were administered a dose of cisplatin.

Renal injury

As per routine care in the RTC, renal function was measured using serum creatinine clearance assay. Serum creatinine values were abstracted from the participant's clinical records and applied to calculate the estimated glomerular filtration rates (eGFR) in ml/min/1.73 m2. A medical calculation tool, GlobalRPH (https://globalrph.com/), was applied to compute eGFR using the Cockcroft-Gault equation. Renal injury was defined as eGFR <44 ml/min//1.73 m2, as per the National Cancer Institute's Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) v4.03.

Ototoxicity

Hearing loss and physical audiometric assessments were used collectively to define ototoxicity. To assess hearing loss, the whispered speech test was used [26]. Audiometric evaluations included physical examination for external and internal ear inflammation; as well as patient-reported ear pain, tinnitus and vertigo. Grading was done using the CTCAE v4.03 definitions of mild (grade 1), moderate (grade 2), severe (grade 3) and lethal (grade 4).

Peripheral neuropathy

Peripheral neuropathy was evaluated by grading pain, tingling/numb sensations, sensory loss via monofilament sensitivity and vibration sensibility tests and loss of strength. Patient-reported neuropathic pain and burning/tingling sensations were recorded and graded using the CTCAE v4.03 guidelines. A Semmes Weinstein 5.07 (10 g) monofilament and a digital biothesiometer with a 128Hz tuning fork, were applied to detect sensory loss in the feet as recommended [27]. Lower limb strength was measured using the repeated chair stand test, which counts the number of completed chair stands over a 30-second period. A total count of >25 chair stands were considered normal, and less than 24 reflected loss of strength in women aged <60 years, while the threshold for >60 years was 12–17 chair stands as average [28]. The Total Neuropathy Score was employed to grade the degree of neuropathy per the CTCAE v4.03.

Vestibulopathy

The head impulse test was used to assess the ability to focus on a target. Vestibulocular complication was characterized by an eye making catch-up saccades after head movement in two of three tests.

Follow-up for secondary end-points

At the 12-month time point, secondary end-points such as disease status, in other words, disease-free survival vs. disease progression/relapse; vital status, in other words, alive vs. deceased were collected as treatment outcome data in this study. All secondary output data collected were abstracted from patient clinical files, as classified by specialist oncologists in the RTC treatment facility employing imaging (computerized topographical and ultrasound scanning), physical examination (external and internal palpation) and histological testing for abnormal cytology. The Response Evaluation Criteria for Solid Tumours (RECIST) v1.0 is routinely used in the RTC treatment facility to define response to treatment. Disease-free survival was defined as no evidence of palpable tumor, visible mass using imaging techniques and no abnormal cytology seen in the cervical biopsy tissue. Disease progression was defined as no evidence of tumor mass shrinkage 12 months after treatment had begun. Relapse was when complete response, namely, no evidence of palpable tumor, visible mass using imaging techniques and no abnormal cytology was observed after commencing anti-cancer therapy, however, a recurrence of the tumor was confirmed by physical examination and biopsy tests at the 12-month time point. Mortality was defined as death during the follow-up period.

Data analysis

STATA v12.0 (StataCorp, TX, USA) was used for data analysis. Demographical and clinical data were allocated into continuous data or categorical data. Continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (inter-quartile range), while categorical data were expressed as absolute or relative frequencies. The normality of data was examined using Shapiro-Wilk's test. To test the trend of TITs development, the Chi-squared test of homogeneity was performed, using Pearson's Chi-squared test as a test statistic. Univariate logistic regression was used to associate clinical factors with TITs and determine factors associated with risk of TITs. The relationship between TITs and treatment outcomes was analyzed using univariate and multivariate regression models. Covariates used in each multivariate regression analysis model showed significance in univariate analyzes based on odds ratios, 95% confidence intervals and p-values. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was used to define statistical significance.

Results

All participants were black Zimbabweans between 26 and 90 years of age. The median age of the group was 50 years (IQR = 43–61 years) (Table 3). Median BMI was 25.9 kg/m2 (IQR = 16–47). High HIV infection (45%) was recorded. Alcohol consumption (11.6%) was higher than tobacco smoking (5%). Most of the cohort (85%) had locally advanced cervical cancer (FIGO stage >IIB) that originated from the squamous cell epithelium (83%). Common treatments administered included cisplatin-based chemoradiotherapy (∼75%), followed by radiotherapy only (∼17%).

The mean cisplatin dose was 35 mg/m2, while radiotherapy was administered in fractionated doses of 6Gy x3; 6Gy x4 or 7Gy x3 for brachytherapy; and 46Gy in 23 fractions over 4 weeks of external beam radiotherapy. All treatments administered and dosing regimens were comparable by HIV status (p = 0.285) (Figure 1). About 42% of the cohort were receiving pain co-medications. Of the 252 participants, 60 (24%) were confirmed deceased and 192 (76%) were alive at the 12-month time-point. Prevalence of treatment-induced toxicities

The prevalence of at least grade 1 (mild) TITs at different time points is illustrated (Table 4). The most developed TIT at the 3-month time point was the loss of touch sensation (69.8%), followed by loss of vibration sensation (59.5%), vertigo (53%) and ear inflammation (13.5%). At the 6-month time point, loss of touch sensation (59.5%), loss of vibration sensation (58.3%) and vertigo (51.6%) were the most commonly occurring TITs. At the 12-month time point, peripheral neuropathy in the form of loss of touch (59.5%) and vibration sensation (54%) was also the most frequently observed TIT followed by vertigo (47%), radiation proctitis (15.9%), VVF (15.9%) and vaginal stenosis (5.6%). In this data, no cases of vestibulopathy were detected. At 3 months, external ear inflammation (p < 0.01), internal ear inflammation (p = 0.01), vertigo (p = 0.02), loss of touch and vibration sensations (p < 0.01), while at 6 months, vertigo (p < 0.01), loss of touch sensation (p < 0.01) and loss of vibration sensation (p < 0.01) significantly increased after cisplatin administration. Twelve months after cisplatin, loss of touch sensation (p < 0.01), vibration sensation (<0.01), vaginal stenosis (p = 0.01) and radiation proctitis (p < 0.01) significantly increased. Prevalence of other TITs did not significantly change when comparing the before and after treatment.

Risk factors in association with treatment-induced toxicities

Univariate logistic regression analyzes were used to assess the relationship between clinicopathological factors with the development of TITs at specific time-points (Table 5). Older age was a risk factor for renal injury at all time points (OR range = 1.1–2.1; 95% CI ranges 1.1–4.1; p < 0.01). Higher BMI was protective against renal injury at pre-treatment (OR = 0.6; 95% CI = 0.5–0.8; p < 0.01), 3 month (OR = 0.6; 95% CI = 0.2–0.8; p < 0.01) and 6-month time points (OR = 0.7; 95% CI = 0.5–0.9; p < 0.01). Larger tumor size was associated with protection against renal injury (OR = 0.6; 95% CI = 0.5–0.9; p = 0.01). Individuals who were receiving cisplatin therapies only were more likely to have renal injury at 3 months (OR = 1.4; 95% CI = 1.1–1.8; p = 0.02) and 6 months (OR = 1.6; 95% CI = 1.1–2.2; p = 0.01), compared with patients receiving chemoradiotherapy. Advanced disease stage (OR = 1.3; 95% CI = 1.1–1.5; p = 0.02) and pain comedications (OR = 1.3; 95% CI = 1.1–1.5; p = 0.03) were associated with loss of touch sensation. Alcohol consumption (OR = 2.8; 95% CI = 1.1–7.5; p = 0.04) was associated with chronic tinnitus. Comorbid conditions (OR = 1.2; 95% CI = 1.1–1.4; p = 0.04) were associated with vertigo at 6-month time-point. No factors were associated with DVT, radiation proctitis, vaginal stenosis and VVF. Vestibulopathy was excluded from further analyzes due to a prevalence of 0%.

Association of treatment-induced toxicities with secondary end points

Univariate regression association analysis of TITs with disease status and survival status was performed (Figure 2). An association between early onset (3 months) of kidney injury (OR = 1.4, 95% CI = 1.1–2.0, p = 0.022) and mortality was determined. Although not statistically significant (p > 0.05), a strong tendency for early onsetting loss of lower limb strength to determine prognosis was observed. The association of early onsetting loss of lower limb strength yielded odds ratios of 3.7 (95% CI = 0.6–21.2) and 3.0 (95% CI = 0.4–20.1) with relapse and mortality, respectively (p < 0.05). Late onset (6 months) of ear pain also indicated a strong tendency toward prognosis, although the relationship could not be clarified due to confidence interval thresholds and p > 0.05. Late onset of ear pain also showed a strong tendency to mortality (OR = 9.0; 95% CI = 0.9–92.1; p = 0.066) and mortality (OR = 9.8; 95% CI = 1.0–101.7; p = 0.055), however, this was not statistically significant. Chronic kidney injury (at 12 months) was associated with a 1.6-fold higher risk of relapse (95% CI = 1.0–2.5; p = 0.041). A statistically significant association between chronic loss of vibration sensation and disease progression (OR = 2.0; 95% CI = 1.1–3.5; p = 0.022) was determined.

Discussion

This study aimed to describe the prevalence and risk factors of TITs experienced by cervical cancer patients in Zimbabwe. The topical data found peripheral neuropathy as the most common TIT developed by cancer patients receiving cisplatin, consistent with previous reports [29–31]. Not many studies describe cisplatin-induced peripheral neuropathy in African cancer patients receiving cisplatin, however, a study conducted on Kenyan populations reported high frequency (84%), in support of the topical data [17]. In most African countries, peripheral neuropathy is not routinely monitored in cancer care yet African ancestry is a significant predisposing factor [32,33]. Our study, together with Ezzi and collaborators significantly highlights a need for further research on peripheral neuropathy in African populations, as well as policies that include peripheral neuropathy screening in primary cancer care. Peripheral neuropathy can have detrimental effects that range from emotional, psychosocial and physical disability, leading to poor quality of life. Therefore, early detection as a result of regular screening can result in patients receiving early interventions, to prevent the detrimental effects of undetected peripheral neuropathy.

In this study, comorbidities were correlated with an increased risk of chronic peripheral neuropathy. Although the specific comorbid conditions were not quantified in this analysis, common comorbidities in this cohort were hypertension and diabetes- both of which are known exacerbators of peripheral neuropathy. High BMI was also found to increase the risk of peripheral neuropathy in this study. High BMI has been previously established to exert additive risk in these nerve-damaging comorbidities in other words hypertension and diabetes, in further support of the topical data [34,35].

This study also reports on a prevalence of ototoxicity (>50%) that is comparable to the global average (60%) of ototoxicity after patients have received cisplatin [36,37]. Systemic cisplatin is transported into inner ear fluids and cochlear cells, where it kills sensorineural hair cells and causes hearing loss, ear pain, inflammation, vertigo and tinnitus [38,39]. This study is one of the few studies reporting on ototoxicity developed by cancer patients in developing countries, despite the threat that ototoxicity poses to the quality of life of cancer patients and survivors. This lack of ototoxicity data can translate to poor management and monitoring [40–42]. In Zimbabwe, cancer patients are not routinely managed for ototoxicity, similar to data from India and South Africa [43–45]. This is attributable to shortages of manpower in treatment facilities resulting in short consultation times; a lack of protocols and guidelines for ototoxicity evaluation in the health systems; as well as a shortage of practitioners with specialist audiology training in resource-limited settings

In this study, hearing loss was infrequently observed (2%). Rates of cisplatin-induced hearing loss in cancer can range between 1–90%, depending on the age of patients, disease site and the evaluation test [26,45]. The whispered speech test was used to evaluate hearing loss in this study, which focuses on detecting loss of pure tone audiometry. Although the whispered speech test has a limited range when compared with other audiometric evaluation methods such as audio scopes, studies in the UK and USA highlight that the sensitivity and specificity of the whisper speech test are adequate as initial screening tools for individuals who need further audiometric testing [26,36,45]. In future, it is important to confirm the current findings using audio scopes and determine the validity of current findings. When validated, our findings can be applied to develop protocols for a cheap, easy and quick hearing loss initial screening method for resource-limited settings such as Zimbabwe. This is an important step toward comprehensive assessment for cancer patients and improving their quality of life, a critical aspect of personalized medicine.

Alcohol consumption was found to be a significant risk factor for ototoxicity in the data presented here. This may be a consequence of the westernization of African countries including Zimbabwe, which has seen a surge in habits such as alcohol consumption in the younger population groups, compared with the older populations [44]. Alcohol deregulates the cellular homeostatic balance and induces cytosolic and nuclear modifications that promote hair cell death and cochleotoxicity [45,46]. In resource-limited settings, there may be shortages in resources and man-power to routinely monitor all cancer patients but a predictive algorithm incorporating cumulative risk factors such as age and alcohol as determined in this study, can be applied to identify individuals who can be prescribed for stringent ototoxicity monitoring- toward developing the predictive algorithm, a case-control study with a larger sample size can be used to obtain more data in future studies.

The data presented here show comparable frequencies of renal injury before and after treatment. This can be ascribed to the stringent monitoring of renal function between cisplatin administration in Zimbabwe, and subsequent patient rehydration after each cisplatin dose, as recommended by the National Comprehensive Cancer Care Network. The overall prevalence of renal injury in this study (16%) corresponds with studies from Tunisia (15%), Thailand (18%), South Africa (17%) and the global average (20%) [7,47–49]. A previous study on a cohort of Zimbabwean women with cervical cancer also recorded a significantly higher frequency of renal injury (41%), than what was found in the topical data [19]. Differences in prevalence observed can be a result of sample size differences because Manyau and collaborators analyzed 72 participants while the current cohort was made up of more than 250 participants.

Risk factors for renal injury found in this study included older age, high dose of cisplatin and co-administration of pain medication, resonating with the literature [50–52]. Of great interest in this data was the correlation of low BMI with renal injury. Some published data reports that low BMI corresponds with low serum albumin levels, translating to low excretion of cisplatin and renal injury [53–55]. However, contraindicative reports have also found that high BMI perpetuates inflammation, a risk factor for renal injury, and predisposes individuals to conditions such as diabetes and cardiovascular disease, both of which are conditions associated with declining renal function [56–58]. The relationship between BMI and renal injury is long debated and poorly understood, in different diseases and socioeconomic contexts. Therefore, more research is needed on a larger sampling to understand the impact of BMI on cisplatin-induced renal injury.

In this data, no cases of vestibulopathy were detected. In previous publications, the occurrence of vestibular malfunction ranged between 0 and 50% after cisplatin administration [59]. This wide range has been ascribed to methodological variations in assessing vestibulopathy. The current study used the head impulse test, which has been described as an adequate screening tool for insidious disequilibrium in the vestibulocular system. Alternatively, these wide-ranging frequencies can also be because there are no direct tests to measure vestibulocular malfunction, instead, inferences are used [59]. As such, there is not much that is known about vestibulopathy, and there are limited comprehensive evaluation methods. In other studies, it is suggested that the velocity of head rotation should also be quantified in addition to the head impulse test, to identify individuals that can be tested further. So, in future, multiple tests including the velocity of head rotation can be performed to obtain more data for a stronger inference of vestibulopathy.

HIV positivity in cancer increases the risk of drug-drug interactions which may decrease therapeutic efficacy, increase TITs and eventually mortality [60]. HIV has been associated with higher TIT frequency, more severe TITs, higher risk for hospitalization as a result of TITs and poor tolerance to anticancer therapy [61–63]. In the topical cohort, a high HIV infection rate (45%) was reported, however, HIV status was not associated with any TITs, and did not influence the outcome of cisplatin therapy. Similar to the current study, comparable tolerance of cisplatin-based chemoradiotherapy was observed despite HIV status, in data obtained from the AIDS Malignancy Consortium, a multi-centre clinical trial for HIV/AIDS across the Sub-Saharan Africa region [64]. Other studies have also published similar results to the topical data [61,65]. The lack of association observed between HIV and TITs in the present study can be because the patients receiving cancer care have restored immunomodulation, and normal CD4+-T-cell counts. The standard practice of care for cancer patients, including at the RTC warrants stringent monitoring for HIV markers such as viral load and CD4+-T-cell counts. Therefore, immunosuppressed individuals with low CD4+-T-cell counts or high viral loads are delayed treatment until immunomodulation is restored.

In studies where HIV positivity is associated with increased TIT risk, genetic variability in enzymes such as cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4) which play a role in the metabolism of both antiretroviral and anticancer therapies may contribute to these adverse outcomes. Some populations express an inherently diminished CYP3A4 enzymatic activity and so, concurrent administration of drugs that utilize the CYP3A4 pathway increases the risk of adverse drug reactions [66]. Although the role of genetics is not interrogated in this study, there is a need to conduct future studies to determine the role of host genetic markers in response to treatment.

In this study, older age was found to be an independent predictor for disease progression. Older age is associated with a diminished immune system, multiple morbidities that further complicate treatment and delayed diagnosis [67–70]. Zimbabwe utilizes the World Health Organisation's Global strategy for the elimination of cervical cancer guidelines, which motivates cervical cancer screening between 35 and 45 years of age [71]. However, our data and the literature suggest that there is a need to intensify cervical screening initiatives for elderly women to promote earlier detection even in advanced age. Implementing policies that consider a wider age range for screening is an important step toward improving the overall prognosis of cancer especially in resource-limited settings where there is a high disease burden which may complicate patient management.

International guidelines for cancer care also requires hematologic monitoring by analyzing the complete blood count before each treatment cycle [72]. However, the present study did not analyze the hemogram, posing a limitation on the study. Hemotoxicity data was excluded due to missing complete blood cell count data in the patient records. At the RTC, patient data including complete blood cell count are stored as hard paper copies, increasing the risk of misplacement. The RTC aims to migrate from physical patient records to electronic patient records, to prevent loss of such crucial data for research such as hemotoxicity [24]. In future, a study on the prevalence and risk factors of hemotoxicities would be of great significance to improve policies of cancer management in Zimbabwe.

Conclusion

Our study is the first to report on frequencies of treatment-induced toxicities such as peripheral neuropathy and ototoxicity in a Zimbabwean cancer cohort. This study highlights a need to integrate a wider range of TIT monitoring in primary care in Zimbabwe, including peripheral neuropathy and ototoxicity. The data presented here also illustrated that comorbid conditions, BMI, alcohol and age are significant predisposing factors for TITs. These factors can be incorporated into an algorithm that predicts the risk of adverse reactions to cisplatin-based therapies, which would be of great significance, especially in countries with limited resources. This study highlights fundamental phenotyping data that can be applied in future pharmacogenomics studies toward personalizing medicine in countries such as Zimbabwe where healthcare is expensive and an out-of-pocket expense for patients.
