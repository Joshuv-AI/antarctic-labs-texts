COMBINE EBIs: A novel COllaborative Method for Building INterventions from Existing Evidence-Based Interventions
Authors: Justin Knox, Kara Portier, Christopher Magana, Maggie Denning, Christopher M Ferraris, Emily Dove-Medows, Cho-Hee Shrader, Ohemaa Poku, Phil Kreniske, Robert Remien, Efrat Aharonovich, Jennifer C Elliott, Denis Nash, Kathryn Lancaster, Till Baernighausen, Kayo Fujimoto, Adam Carrico, John A Schneider, Alida Bouris, D Scott Batey, Sheree R Schwartz, Jose Bauermeister, Patrick S Sullivan, Joseph G Rosen, Gina Wingood, Milton Wainberg, Deborah Hasin, Elvin Geng, Patrick A Wilson, Stefan D Baral
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose:Combining evidence-based interventions (EBIs) is a discrete process from adapting EBIs, and specific guidance for how to combine EBIs could be helpful amidst the proliferation of frameworks that combine and stage EBIs and calls for services to be combined or bundled. To address this gap, we developed and applied the COllaborative Method for Building INterventions from Existing Evidence-Based Interventions (COMBINE-EBIs) approach, a five-step process for combining EBIs.Methods:The five steps of COMBINE-EBIs are: (1) Identify and Select EBIs, (2) Develop a Shared Conceptual Model, (3) Evaluate the Conceptual Model, (4) Create a Single Combined Protocol, and (5) Refine through Further Input.Results:We developed and applied the 5-step COMBINE-EBIs process to build a fully refined, pre-tested, combined multi-component intervention that leverages intrinsic social network support and mHealth technology to support people with HIV who drink heavily improve HIV care outcomes and reduce alcohol use.Conclusions:COMBINE-EBIs is a rigorous, systematic and efficient approach for building multi-component, multi-modal interventions to address multiple, co-occurring health behaviors simultaneously. COMBINE-EBIs is a resource efficient approach that could facilitate the creation of additional multi-component interventions to address complex, co-occurring health conditions synergistically. Future research should evaluate the feasibility and utility of COMBINE-EBIs, including where adaptations are needed to maximize utility.Keywords: Evidence based interventions, Combining interventions, Implementation Science

Introduction

Multi-component interventions that address a single health issue or multiple co-occurring health issues are needed because health issues are often complex and commonly co-occur. Addressing single health issues robustly and co-occurring health issues simultaneously confers benefits and aligns with a movement towards whole-person health [1]. Addressing complex or inter-related health issues operating at multiple social-ecological levels offers the possibility of developing interventions to address syndemic health issues [2]. Such multi-component interventions could be developed by combining multiple existing evidence-based interventions (EBIs). Existing EBIs may operate through differing modalities to address the same or multiple health issues simultaneously. This synergy presents an opportunity to improve specific health needs stemming from co-occurring health issues, improve efficiency, and contribute to implementation science by improving the adoption of feasible and acceptable EBIs into practice. Combining EBIs is a research efficient approach that leverages the efforts already invested in developing and evaluating existing EBIs; a critical advantage amidst limited public health resources.

Study designs have been developed to evaluate the delivery of multiple interventions simultaneously or in sequence like Multiphase Optimization Strategy (MOST) [3], Sequential Multiple Assignment Randomized Trials (SMART) [3], Learn as You Go (LAGOS) [4], and Just-in-Time Adaptive Interventions (JITAI) [5]. Such designs offer evaluation strategies for combining or staging multiple EBIs. However, they do not provide specific methods for how to combine EBIs, nor do they explicitly discuss considerations for designing EBIs operating across modalities or addressing multiple health issues simultaneously. Frameworks have also been developed to guide the identification of shared elements across EBIs for a given domain [6,7], which could then be used to develop interventions tailored to a specific population [6]. Modular interventions have also been developed in which various procedures and techniques common across EBIs for a range of potentially comorbid psychiatric conditions have been bundled within a single intervention, with the implementation strategy for that intervention tailored to individual needs [8]. Such modular approaches provide a great deal of flexibility to meet client/patient needs. Frameworks like ADAPT-ITT [9], and those proposed by the CDC ADAPT Team [10] and the PRISSMA Project [11], provide guidance on adapting interventions while preserving effectiveness for new populations/contexts, with some models combining multiple interventions as they adapted them to a new context [11]. Building off of these previous models, we posit that combining EBIs is a discrete process from adapting EBIs, and we propose a method to guide the combination of EBIs. We believe there is unique value to describing a theory-informed process that advances implementation science by articulating how and why combined interventions work, and using other models (e.g., Implementation Research Logic Model [IRLM] [12], Consolidated Framework for implementation Research 2.0 [CFIR] [13], implementation mapping [14], mechanism mapping [15]) to conceptualize and link determinants, strategies, mechanisms, and outcomes. The need for this methodological guidance is highlighted by increasing evaluations of combined EBIs [16-19], and calls for developing methods to combine or bundle services [20-22].

To address this gap, we developed the COllaborative Method for Building INterventions from Existing Evidence-Based Interventions (COMBINE-EBIs) approach, a five-step process for combining EBIs. COMBINE-EBIs can be used with previously mentioned models and other established multi-level intervention frameworks [14,23]. Other frameworks offer structured methods for designing interventions that engage individuals, caregivers, providers, and broader systems. Similarly, COMBINE-EBIs centers collaboration with potential users of the combined intervention and implementation partners (i.e., those delivering or supporting delivery of the intervention). Further, we consider relevant constructions from an implementation science determinants framework in each step of the COMBINE-EBIs process. This enhances rigor by encouraging systematic consideration of relevant implementation determinants. We used the updated CFIR for this perspective [13], although other frameworks could be used. The Framework for Reporting Adaptations and Modifications to Evidence-based interventions (FRAME) [24] and the Framework for documenting modifications to Implementation Strategies in healthcare (FRAME-IS) [25] provide methodologies for the documentation of modifications to EBIs and implementation strategies. Our approach also stresses the importance of documenting decisions made during the combining process, including justification for these decisions. We also provide a case study of applying COMBINE-EBIs to create an intervention from two existing EBIs that address HIV care and alcohol use by leveraging intrinsic social support and mobile health (mHealth) technology. We discuss this process and implications regarding implementation of the combined intervention. We aim to offer a practical, methodologically robust, and reproducible framework to develop multi-component interventions by leveraging existing EBIs that address the health needs of populations affected by complex, co-occurring health issues.

Steps and activities of the COllaborative Method for Building INterventions from Existing Evidence-Based Interventions (COMBINE-EBIs).

Step 1, Identify and Select EBIs.

The research literature should be searched, and discussed with peers and mentors, including potential users of the intervention and potential implementation partners, to identify and select two or more EBIs for combination. Building relationships and buy-in is critical in planning stages such as this and is prioritized in other community-engaged frameworks [26-28]. Engaging community members and implementation partners early and asking about their needs and capacities is critical. Researchers might establish a community advisory board to serve as a partner in this process and subsequent steps.

As EBIs are being identified, it could also be helpful to obtain source materials for each EBI from respective study teams and online sources. Source materials include study protocols, intervention manuals and activities, training resources, and fidelity monitoring resources. These materials could help with the EBI selection process. They also will be critical in subsequent steps, particularly as they are used more extensively in Step 4.

Considerations during the EBI selection process include: distinctiveness, cultural relevance (e.g., alignment and resonance with the needs and social context of potential users), and potential synergy (i.e., ability to amplify each other’s impacts). Given the goal of addressing distinct, co-occurring health outcomes, identified EBIs might use distinct modalities (e.g., social support, digital technology), and synergies are possible. EBIs could also be antagonistic, so selected EBIs should be distinct and compatible; that way the combined intervention can be delivered in a shared setting. Length is another consideration. Some conditions might be more amenable to a brief, single session intervention; other, more complex conditions might require more intensive intervention. The length of the combined intervention and sequencing of activities should be considered. Other preliminary considerations include the underlying theories and assumptions informing the EBIs; the nature of the behaviors that they target; the skills/personnel needed to deliver the combined intervention; and the intended users of the intervention. Questions to guide these decisions might include: Was the intervention designed for a specific population? Do the eligibility criteria overlap, or are they separate? Would adapting an EBI for a particular target population be necessary when combining it with another EBI, and what should be the new eligibility criteria for the combined intervention?

Delivery organizations specifically might consider several key questions to determine whether selected EBIs align with their organizational objectives and desired outcomes for a combined intervention. These include: What outcomes or behaviors does each EBI target, and do those align with programmatic goals? Was the intervention designed for a population that matches those being served? Do the eligibility criteria of selected EBIs overlap, or are they separate? Would adapting an EBI for a particular population be necessary when combining it with another EBI, and what should be the new eligibility criteria for the combined intervention? What theoretical frameworks, behavioral mechanisms, or core elements underlie each EBI, and are they complementary or potentially conflicting? What personnel, training, or qualifications are required to deliver each EBI effectively? For example, a school district exploring a combined intervention to support student mental health might consider pairing a social-emotional learning curriculum with a trauma-informed training. The school district would need to assess whether eligibility criteria align with their student population, whether teachers and mental health staff have the capacity to deliver both EBIs within existing roles, who would be responsible for training school personnel, and whether the underlying frameworks and core elements are complementary. Additional operational questions include: What are the training requirements and intensity (e.g., length of training, need for certification)? What are the direct and indirect costs of program delivery (e.g., materials, technology, staff time)? What data collection or technical infrastructure is needed for implementation or evaluation? Will engaging with the EBI’s original developers or administrators be necessary to ensure fidelity and understand core elements? As another example, a public health department seeking to address chronic disease in a specific population might look to combine a diabetes self-management program with a culturally tailored nutrition intervention. This would require evaluating how well the behavior change goals of the two EBIs align, whether the delivery formats are compatible (e.g., group vs. individual), and whether staff have the capacity and expertise to implement the combined EBI.

CFIR constructs to consider during this step include those from the Inner Setting domains to help ensure that selected EBIs are in line with programmatic goals, address relevant behaviors and/or outcomes, and are compatible with existing resources/capacities. Specifically, social capital, which refers to the value that comes from social networks and the relationships within them, plays a critical role. These relationships, which can be characterized by trust, shared vision, and information exchange, may exert a greater influence on implementation success than individual attributes alone [29,30]. Additionally, constructs from the Outer Setting domain are relevant to consider as this covers relevant external factors, such as population needs, relevance, and policy or funding environments. For example, assessing compatibility with organization priorities while also addressing community/stakeholder-identified needs can improve both feasibility and acceptability, respectively, of the combined intervention.

Step 2, Develop a Shared Conceptual Model.

A conceptual model is a visual or narrative representation that shows how and why a set of program components are expected to lead to specific outcomes. Researchers or practitioners should deconstruct the EBIs to understand how they might work together. EBIs should be characterized in terms of their components, including: constructs targeted by each intervention (e.g., self-efficacy, social support), mechanisms of influence through which change is expected to occur (e.g., skills-building, motivational interviewing), behavioral outcomes (e.g., increased physical activity, taking medication consistently), and clinical outcomes (e.g., lower blood pressure). Because the focus of the combined intervention will be creating synergy between the components of multiple EBIs, researchers or practitioners should also theorize how the distinct components are inter-related in the combined intervention and identify duplicative elements. Partners might begin by sketching a simple diagram or logic model [12] that maps the relationships between inputs, intervention components, targeted behaviors, and intended outcomes. In some cases, existing visualizations or logic models from the original EBIs can be adapted to reflect the combined intervention’s structure and theory of change. Several guiding questions can help shape the model: What are the core elements of each EBI, and how does each contribute to behavior change or clinical outcomes? Are there shared constructs or mechanisms across the EBIs that could be aligned? Are there redundant or conflicting elements that may need to be revised or removed? What is the hypothesized pathway from intervention delivery to the intended outcomes in the combined model? A relevant CFIR Process construct to consider during this step is stakeholder engagement to co-develop the model, which will help to ensure shared understanding of the combined intervention and desired outcomes. This collaborative approach strengthens buy-in; clarifies core elements and supports alignment between the combined intervention and the context(s) in which it will be implemented. It is best to do this as early as possible to ensure that the conceptual model reflects both the evidence base and the practical, lived realities of the settings and populations it aims to serve. This process should result in a final conceptual model that depicts how the distinct components of the combined EBIs interrelate and/or modify one another. Other relevant CFIR constructs emanate from the Innovation domain, specifically source, relative advantage, trialability, complexity, design, and cost.

Step 3, Evaluate the Conceptual Model.

Researchers or practitioners should formatively characterize the constructs from the shared conceptual model and explore how these constructs are inter-related. If possible, this should be done using data collected among potential users of the combined intervention. This might include secondary analysis of existing datasets to explore the relationships among constructs identified in the conceptual model. Primary data collection might be required to elicit specific feedback about potential compatibility, synergy, or redundancy for the EBIs identified in Step 1. Primary data collection, which might be more feasible in a community organization setting, also provides the opportunity to receive input about the potential adaptation of specific EBIs, which might be necessary if combining interventions alters any core elements of the EBIs. Such key aspects include their delivery in different settings (e.g., clinic- vs. community-based), format (e. g., in-person vs. virtual), length, structure, or target populations. Formative research can be conducted with potential users of the combined intervention and through engagement with implementation partners (e.g., case workers, administrators, clinicians) from Step 1. To evaluate the conceptual model with potential program leaders or participants in a feasible and minimally burdensome way, several qualitative approaches can be employed. For instance, practitioners can conduct small focus groups with community members to discuss the perceived relevance, acceptability, and clarity of the proposed program. These guided discussions are efficient for gathering a range of perspectives at once and identifying shared community norms or concerns [31]. For more granular, individual-level feedback, think-aloud interviews provide a powerful method for user-testing specific materials (e.g., brochures, websites, or forms). In this method, a participant verbalizes their thought process while interacting with a program element, revealing in real-time what is clear, confusing, or impactful [32]. To proactively identify implementation challenges, a program “pre--mortem” assessment is an invaluable and low-cost tool. This structured brainstorming exercise convenes stakeholders to imagine the program has already failed and asks them to work backward to identify the potential reasons why, allowing the team to address these weaknesses in the design phase [33]. These well-established procedures provide practical, actionable feedback and are a common element in many implementation science adaptation approaches (e.g., ADAPT-ITT). Relevant CFIR constructs to consider during this step include those from the Inner Setting domain, such as organizational culture, relational connections (i.e., social capital), implementation climate, and available resources. Constructs from the Characteristics of Individuals domain that might be relevant are knowledge, beliefs, and self-efficacy related to the combined intervention.

Step 4, Create a Single Combined Protocol.

Researchers or practitioners should next develop a cohesive intervention protocol, integrating the core elements of each EBI. First, source materials from existing EBIs should be obtained (e.g., standard operating procedures/manuals, training materials, fidelity rating sheets, evaluation tools, pamphlets, referral procedures). This process aims to understand and maintain the core elements of the existing EBIs (i.e., those fundamental components of an EBI’s design that should remain unchanged to ensure effectiveness) [10]. These core elements are often grounded in behavioral theory, which explain mechanisms and context. Researchers might consult original developers of the EBIs; review conceptual frameworks and theoretical foundations in their writings, including within internal documents; and review prior implementation data. Detailed lists or tables can be created to highlight similarities and differences across components of each EBI. As intervention components are combined, researchers should consider the appropriateness of different formats (e.g., in-person vs. virtual, 1:1 vs. group sessions) and activities (e.g., education, goal setting, self-monitoring), assessing whether combining components can be mutually reinforcing. Consideration should also be given to compatibility of interventions. For example, each intervention may be designed for the needs of a specific population, having its own distinct recruitment and screening procedures, eligibility criteria, timelines for intervention delivery and assessments, delivery settings, types of interventionists, and material requirements. All of these should be defined for the new combined intervention, examining and harmonizing the workflows and procedures from each EBI. Researchers should assess available material and human resources, while also exploring potential partnerships within the community to implement and evaluate the combined intervention. To ensure transparency, reproducibility, and fidelity, it is important to systematically document all decisions and justifications for modifications and exclusions. FRAME can be used to guide how to systematically document this process, such as what was modified, the nature and rationale for each modification, the level affected (e.g., content, delivery format), and who was involved in the decision-making [24]. This structured reporting can help ensure that adaptations are captured in a way that supports replicability and fidelity monitoring.

Findings from Steps 2 and 3 can be used to define the needs and preferences of potential users of the combined intervention and implementation partners. Researchers or practitioners should follow principles of Designing for Dissemination and Sustainability [34], which emphasize developing interventions that are accessible, relevant, and feasible to deliver within real-world settings. These principles include minimizing burden on participants and staff, aligning interventions with existing workflows and technologies, ensuring fit with organizational and cultural context, and incorporating stakeholder input throughout the design process. Applying these principles can help streamline the combined intervention by eliminating redundancies and merging overlapping content to avoid unnecessary duplication; however, fundamental components that need reinforcement and are critical for eliciting behavior change should be conserved. Researchers or practitioners should review intervention language, activities, and tools, adapting them, as needed, to ensure accessibility and cultural relevance. Cultural translation frameworks [35-37] may helpfully distinguish between superficial changes (e.g., language modifications) and deeper, culturally embedded factors (e.g., values, beliefs, and historical context). This work should be done with input from community members and leveraging frameworks to support cultural adaptation of EBIs [35, 38-40]. Relevant CFIR constructs to consider during this step include those from the Inner Setting domain, such as compatibility of the combined intervention with existing workflows, organizational priorities, and delivery structures, as well as the availability of necessary resources, such as staff, time, and available technology. Addressing these elements during protocol development ensures that the intervention can be implemented without overburdening existing systems and that it would be feasible given the capacity of the implementation setting.

Step 5, Refine through Further Input.

Following prototype development in Step 4, community members and implementation partners should be invited to refine the combined intervention. Those involved earlier in the process can be hired as consultants for this final iterative phase, leveraging these established connections to enable deeper integration of their insights. Community members and implementation partners will bring valuable perspectives and can fill gaps in the research team’s expertise (e.g., clinical expertise, experience with potential users, and/or lived experience). This refinement phase should explicitly focus on implementation objectives identified by Proctor et al., such as acceptability, feasibility, appropriateness, and adoption, to ensure the intervention is relevant, usable, and capable of integration into real world settings [41]. Community members can provide input to ensure that the combined intervention is culturally relevant, feasible, appropriately framed in tone and language, and optimized to meet their preferences and needs. These individuals should pre-test the intervention and provide feedback based on actual experience with the combined intervention. Consultants may engage in reviewing study materials, participating in structured interviews or think-aloud sessions, and pre-testing intervention components (e.g., mobile health tools). Their involvement can help identify and address practical or ethical considerations, such as confidentiality, stigma, or accessibility. Content and methodology experts who have designed or implemented the interventions can guide adjustments to the combined intervention and provide input on how the combined intervention could fit into existing workflows. The latter is key for supporting optimal appropriateness, fidelity, and sustainability [42-44]. Relevant CFIR Process constructs to consider during this step are: reflecting, evaluating, and ongoing stakeholder engagement to iteratively refine the intervention, which helps ensure the combined protocol remains relevant, acceptable, and feasible. As part of this step, researchers or practitioners can also use FRAME-IS to document changes made to implementation strategies themselves, such as modifications to delivery personnel, training protocols, setting, or mode of delivery [25]. FRAME-IS can help differentiate between planned and reactive adaptations, and can ensure that strategy-level changes are captured alongside intervention content modifications. Detailed records of all feedback received should be maintained, including justification for modifications to the combined intervention and resolution of potentially conflicting input. This ensures transparency and provides documentation for intervention development [24,25].

COMBINE-EBIs case study: SupportSync

This case study summarizes the development of a multi-component intervention addressing HIV care and alcohol use among people with HIV (PWH), leveraging intrinsic social support and digital technology. We developed and applied the 5-step COMBINE-EBIs approach to create a single multi-component intervention by combining two existing EBIs.

Step 1: Identify and Select EBIs.

There are over 1.2 million PWH in the US, and an additional 38.7 million worldwide [45,46]. Many PWH experience suboptimal health and HIV-related outcomes [47-53]. Improving health and HIV-related outcomes among PWH is critical for both their individual health and the prevention of HIV transmission [54, 55]. Moreover, alcohol use is prevalent among PWH and is associated with sub-optimal HIV-related outcomes [56]. Thus, interventions for PWH who drink heavily are needed.

Intervention 1: nGage

Interventions that activate, re-invigorate, and maintain naturally occurring social support networks can improve HIV care outcomes [57-61]. nGage is a brief, theory-driven (i.e., the Information Motivation Behavior [IMB] model and the Trans-theoretical Model of Behavior Change) [62-66] intervention improving HIV treatment engagement by harnessing existing social networks to provide social support and improve problem-solving regarding barriers to antiretroviral therapy (ART) adherence and HIV care continuity [67,68]. Participants in nGage (heretofore: index participant [IP]) complete a sociogram exercise to identify a support confidant (SC); the IP selects an SC that they confide in and can rely on for support to join them in the intervention [69]. A single-session dyadic intervention is then delivered by a trained interventionist. The session supports the IP and SC to develop behavioral skills to provide HIV-related social support [63,70], target IP motivational readiness (i.e., stage of change) [66] and self-efficacy for HIV care continuity and ART adherence, and jointly develop a care and support plan [71]. Following the in-person intervention session, brief booster sessions are delivered monthly via phone, text, or email to check-in with the IP and SC about their experiences receiving/providing social support, answer questions, and troubleshoot any challenges. A multi-site, randomized controlled trial (RCT) demonstrated that nGage effectively improves retention in HIV care and self-reported ART adherence among Black sexual minority cisgender men (SMM) and transgender women (TW) [67]. However, nGage does not specifically address alcohol use and could be strengthened by adding specific attention to alcohol use.

Intervention 2: HealthCall

mHealth is an important intervention modality that could be used in combination with a social network intervention, like nGage, to help PWH reduce their drinking. HealthCall is an mHealth intervention that serves as a “counselor-extender” by offering effective theory-based intervention components, externally-structured self-monitoring and personalized feedback, to help PWH who drink heavily reduce their alcohol use [72,73]. HealthCall begins with a brief counseling session where participants formulate drinking reduction goals and receive training on using the HealthCall app daily (~3 min) to self-monitor alcohol use and ART adherence over 60 days. At 30 and 60 days, counselors provide personalized feedback and positive reinforcement based on participant mHealth data during brief in-person visits. HealthCall has been shown to reduce alcohol use in PWH in multiple trials [74-80], with high levels of cultural acceptability and engagement [78].

Given the distinctiveness, compatibility, cultural relevance, and potential synergy of nGage and HealthCall, we combined these two EBIs to create a single, multi-component intervention called SupportSync.

Step 3: Evaluate the Conceptual Model.

We conducted mixed-methods research to evaluate the combined conceptual model developed in Step 2 using an explanatory, parallel mixed-methods design (QUAN=QUAL) [81-83]. We conducted secondary analyses of existing quantitative data and of primary data collected through in-depth interviews. As recommended, we used data collected among potential users of the combined intervention: Black SMM and TW (heretofore: sexual/gender minority individuals [SGM]). Each activity is summarized below, and results from the primary data collection are described in further detail elsewhere [84].

First, we identified existing data collected among Black SGM and conducted secondary analyses of that data to evaluate our conceptual model. Across multiple data sets, analyses, and manuscripts, we contributed various key findings to the literature. For example, we found positive associations between social factors (e.g., social norms, social cohesion, housing instability, age, neighborhood poverty, sleep, psychosocial stress) and alcohol use and HIV-related sex behaviors [85-90] and between drug use and HIV-related sex behaviors [87]. We also found that Black persons with an alcohol use disorder (AUD) were less likely to receive treatment compared to White persons with an AUD [91], and cannabis use, but not other types of substance use, explained racial differences in viral suppression [92]. We also evaluated a multi-component intervention that did not improve PrEP adherence among Black SGM [93], and in a subsequent analysis we showed that the IMB Model successfully predicted adherence across intervention arms [94]. Using another dataset of Black SGM [95], we found mixed associations between types of substance use and HIV transmission related behavior [96]. Lastly, findings from a HealthCall trial demonstrated the intervention supported drinking reductions [79] and improved ART adherence [80]. Collectively, these analyses deepened our understanding of the intervention constructs (e.g., social networks/support/context and mHealth), behavioral outcomes (e.g., HIV prevention and care behaviors, substance use), and clinical outcomes (e.g., viral suppression).

We also conducted two sets of in-depth interviews: one set included Black SGM (n = 16) and their identified SCs (n = 14), and the other set included implementation partners (n = 17) who had at least 3 years of experience working with Black SGM on issues related to HIV and/or substance use (e.g., outreach workers, clinicians, healthcare administrators). Interviews elicited the expertise of participants to assess the fit of HealthCall and nGage with each other and identified ways that the combined EBI could be tailored to meet the specific needs of Black SGM and implementation partners. Interviews began with a presentation on HealthCall and nGage, detailing the goals and components of each individual intervention—suggesting ways in which the interventions could be combined (e.g., sharing the mHealth tracking data with the SC). Participants were asked open-ended questions about their overall impressions; implementation facilitators/barriers; their experiences with HIV, alcohol use, and social support (or their experiences providing care related to these areas); mHealth use and preferences; the lives and needs of Black SGM; and potential ways to optimize the combined intervention. We used thematic analysis to analyze the interviews [97]. Themes that emerged from the feedback received across these groups included: emphasis on the importance of trust, honesty, and accountability within the relationship between Black SGM and their SCs, feasibility and potential impact of the intervention, the context of Black SGM lives, and suggestions on how to strengthen the intervention [84].

Step 4: Create a Single Combined Protocol.

We obtained source materials for each EBI from the nGage and HealthCall study teams and online sources. Source materials included study protocols, intervention manuals and activities, training resources, fidelity monitoring resources, and referral materials (e.g., information pamphlets). We organized these materials into a table that categorized key objectives, theory-based techniques, core elements, and target outcomes for each intervention. We also applied principles from Designing for Dissemination and Sustainability [34] with the aim of creating a combined intervention that was contextually appropriate, aligned with end-user preferences, and feasible to implement. Table 2 reflects how these considerations informed our decisions about session structure and integrating core elements. Redundancy

We consolidated information about ART and strategies to enhance HIV care engagement that were present in both interventions. To reduce participant burden, we condensed the two baseline intervention sessions from nGage with the baseline MI session from HealthCall into a single, comprehensive session. This decision aligned with the emphasis in HealthCall to minimize clinical burden (by leveraging mHealth technology) and Black SGM preferences that were expressed during the IDIs in Step 3.

Synergy

We offered Black SGM the option to have the mHealth ART and alcohol tracking data shared with their SC using personalized, bi-weekly progress reports. We also decided to include training for SCs in the baseline session on providing tracking data-informed positive reinforcement, synergizing this aspect of nGage with HealthCall’s text message-based positive reinforcement. This decision aligned with the feedback received from Black SGM on the importance of SCs in providing accountability during Step 3.

Harmonizing language

The tone used in each of the EBIs was distinct and required harmonization. For example, the alcohol education component from the HealthCall baseline session is based on the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism Clinician’s Guide and takes a prescriptive tone [98]. Participants might receive feedback that they were drinking at levels clinically indicated to reduce their alcohol use. nGage tries to assess participants’ underlying motivations to stay engaged in HIV care and orient them towards being able to achieve their goals. We embraced a goal-oriented (vs. prescriptive) tone for SupportSync, in line with nGage’s approach. This decision was supported by what we learned in Step 3 (and was reinforced during Step 5), where we received feedback that emphasized the importance of person-centeredness, harm reduction (i.e., creating approachable and realistic options for incremental reductions in drinking rather than abstinence only), and developing an adaptable intervention capable meet participants’ individualized needs and goals with nuance and agility.

Adaptations

The combined intervention included adaptations beyond the original versions of HealthCall and nGage. We provide a few examples of these adaptations, informed by the research conducted as part of Step 3. First, we expanded eligibility criteria to include non-binary and gender-expansive individuals assigned male at birth because interview participants emphasized the importance of including other SGM. Second, the mHealth tracking will be operationalized using REDCap and by sending survey links via daily text messages rather than a standalone app because interview participants prefer an intervention that worked within existing platforms that they already use (e.g., text messaging). Third, we added opportunities for participants to discuss mental health (a major deterrent to HIV care continuity) and alcohol use throughout the combined intervention based on participant input. Fourth, we decided to implement SupportSync ourselves (instead of training staff to deliver it in-person in clinical settings) using a fully virtual format because we were able to collect primary data in Step 3 and engage Black SGM and their SC’s in meaningful discussions about HIV care and alcohol use virtually. This also removes a key barrier observed in nGage, which was scheduling in-person appointments with multiple individuals. Fifth, we removed nGage’s sociogram activity and instead ask participants to invite a trusted support person to the interview using a simplified process during screening, streamlining intervention activities and eliminating the need for multiple baseline sessions.

After Step 4, we finalized the SupportSync protocol that we will deliver virtually (via Zoom). We applied guidance from FRAME and documented key adaptations decided upon during this step, including what was changed, why it was changed, who was involved in the change, and whether the changes were planned [24]. For example, merging baseline sessions and simplifying SC recruitment were planned adaptations to reduce burden and improve feasibility. Documenting these decisions enhances transparency. SupportSync begins with a 1.5-hour baseline session with an IP (a Black SGM) and a SC identified during screening and enrollment. The baseline session (grounded in the IMB and Transtheoretical Model of Change theories) provides education on HIV care, alcohol use, social support, and addressing barriers to social support. Afterwards, the IP and their SC collaboratively set goals related to ART adherence and alcohol use, identify potential barriers to achieving those goals, and develop strategies for providing each other social support [71]. The IP receives instructions on completing daily self-monitoring using text message-delivered weblinks to two items on alcohol use and one on ART use. IPs can also record information about other substance use, mood, and mental health. After baseline, there are two brief (15 min) follow-up virtual sessions with the IP (the SC is invited but not required to attend) at 30-days and 60-days post baseline session to assess progress, reinforce behavior changes, and address new challenges. We also completed an IRLM to specify the conceptual linkages between the core implementation science elements (determinants, strategies, mechanisms, and outcomes) of SupportSync ( Fig. 3) [12]. Additionally, we will evaluate SupportSync using a hybrid effectiveness-implementation type 1 design [99] to examine possible effect sizes, feasibility, acceptability, and factors influencing implementation of the combined intervention compared to a randomized time/attention-matched control condition. Additional details of this trial are provided in the Appendix.

Step 5: Refine through Further Input.

Lastly, we implemented multiple activities to elicit further input and refine the SupportSync protocol. We used Proctor et al.’s implementation objectives (e.g., acceptability, feasibility, appropriateness, and adoption) to guide how we gathered and applied feedback [41]. First, we organized a 1.5-hour workshop attended by 19 academic investigators from academic medical centers, clinical training and education centers, and state health departments. Academic investigators had expertise in a range of disciplines, including intervention development, implementation science, clinical trial design, biostatistics, epidemiology, psychology, and social work. Initially, we introduced the SupportSync protocol and elicited targeted feedback. Key sources of feedback included: leveraging or establishing a community advisory board to enhance community engagement, assessing medical mistrust among potential users, and exploring the feasibility and considerations of virtual implementation.

Second, we hired 9 consultants: 4 were Black SGM, and 5 were implementation partners with expertise in intervention development and implementation with mHealth and social support interventions. Consultants provided feedback on SupportSync in terms of Proctor’s implementation outcomes (i.e., acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, feasibility) [41] and articulated potential determinants (i.e., barriers and facilitators) of these outcomes. Each consultant spent 6–20 h completing activities, including: (a) reviewing SupportSync study materials and providing written feedback, (b) pre-testing the baseline session, (c) pre-testing the mHealth tracking tool, and (d) meeting with the study team to discuss feedback. Consultants raised key issues including stigma, medical mistrust, confidentiality, participant engagement, recruitment, intersectionality, drug use, and mental health. Consultants also evaluated the perceived safety and appropriateness of the study materials and procedures. We iteratively updated the SupportSync protocol based on this feedback and kept detailed records of suggestions and respective changes [24]. For example, input from Black SGM consultants led us to make an education component of SupportSync more conversational, adding questions to actively engage participants. This will create space for participants to share lived experiences related to HIV care, alcohol use, and mental health. We also added prompts throughout the intervention protocol to encourage deeper conversations tailored to each dyad. This aligns with broader feedback and with insights from pre-testing, indicating the need for enhanced flexibility in adapting to participants’ needs, pre-existing knowledge of the content, and levels of engagement. Based on both types of consultant feedback on the importance of tailoring, we expanded the mHealth component to include optional tracking of non-alcohol substance use and mental health, and we include data on these topics in personalized bi-weekly progress reports. Several modifications following consultant input reflected changes to our implementation strategies, which we tracked based on guidance provided by FRAME-IS [25]. These included enhancing interventionist support (e.g., updated fidelity monitoring tools, tips for adjusting delivery) and improving accessibility (e.g., literacy level, tech access). These changes were planned and aimed at improving feasibility, acceptability, and to help achieve equity in real-world settings.

The five step COMBINE-EBIs process resulted in a fully refined, pre-tested, combined multi-component intervention that leverages intrinsic social support and mHealth technology to support Black SGM achieve alcohol use reduction goals and improve HIV care outcomes.

Discussion

Developing and applying the five-step COMBINE-EBIs process revealed multiple salient, generalizable considerations. First, combining interventions likely involves working across multiple levels of the socio-ecological model, with potential for synergy [2]. Those using COMBINE-EBIs should consider how the interventions potentially interact (e.g., accumulation, amplification, facilitation, cascade, convergence), which will help to avoid creating a combined intervention that is scattered, redundant, or has competing effects [100]. An innovation of COMBINE-EBIs is that the model provides guidance throughout the process of combining EBIs beyond the initial selection process. Understanding local context is critical throughout this process [101], and the collaborative approach we propose includes multiple opportunities for community engagement throughout.

Regarding this synergy, our case study combined an EBI delivered in-person with an mHealth EBI. It provides an example of how digital technology can enhance real-world interactions and can be mutually reinforcing. Although digital interventions have great potential for simultaneous tailoring and scalability, in-person approaches have the benefit of fostering deeper connections. Using both modalities in a combined intervention harnesses both of these respective strengths [102]. With respect to SupportSync, previous iterations of nGage experienced challenges coordinating in-person meetings, prompting us to explore virtual delivery. However, this adaptation could present concerns related to confidentiality, privacy, and the potential loss of in-person social cues essential for effectiveness. These issues should be carefully considered when designing and evaluating the effectiveness and implementation of combined interventions following their development.

In developing SupportSync, we examined key CFIR domains at each step [13]. Adaptations made during the COMBINE-EBIs process also have implementation science implications. For example, as we shifted the delivery method from in-person to virtual, this redefines the Inner Setting domain from a physical clinic environment to a digital space, altering considerations related to organizational culture, resources, and workflow. Shifting to virtual delivery also alters the Intervention Characteristics, as digital interventions may require adaptations for accessibility, usability, and participant engagement. This shift also has implications for regarding determinants of implementation outcomes, including scale-up and sustainability. In low-resource settings, structural factors like internet, data, smartphone, and electricity access become more relevant [103]. It is important that implementation science evaluation plans accommodate these adaptations.

An additional consideration when implementing Steps 2 and 3 of COMBINE-EBIs involves mechanisms, which can serve as proximal outcomes for evaluating whether the combined intervention is working along theorized causal impact pathways. Understanding how mechanisms interact with contextual factors (i.e., determinants of implementation) can advance the model toward a broader “explanatory theory.” [15] Developing hypotheses about how EBI mechanisms interact with contextual factors can clarify whether combining intervention catalyzes synergy (one mechanisms is expected to magnify another’s effects) or multi-segmentation (the combination of mechanisms allows an intervention to reach across previously unseen segments of responders). This also has important implications for evaluating the effectiveness and implementation of the multi-component intervention.

Relatedly, another consideration is defining evidence and determining how it applies to the newly developed multi-component EBI. Specifically, we posit: does the combined intervention retain the evidence base of its component EBIs, or does it require re-evaluation as a novel intervention? Defining the evidence base for the combined intervention has important implications for future evaluations (research and funding opportunities) and practice (recommendations regarding public health impact, scale-up, and dissemination). Consideration of what constitutes evidence is included as part of the COMBINE-EBIs approach: maintaining core EBI elements, developing a shared conceptual model, and evaluating it (Steps 2 and 3). The methods used to establish initial EBI evidence should also inform decisions about the combined intervention’s evidence base. This ties into larger discussions about using a broader typology of evidence that more equitably weighs study designs beyond RCTs (e.g., quasi-experimental and hybrid study designs) [104] and that includes a balance of explicit (research) and tacit (lived experience) knowledge [105].

COMBINE-EBIs has certain limitations. First, collaboration and community engagement, while critical, also present challenges. Community collaboration requires resources and can be impeded by structural barriers. In addition, the priorities and timelines among different types of collaborators are often misaligned. We point to existing methods on community engagement and co-creation [26-28], and note additional methods of co-creation/collaboration that could be considered [106,107]. Relatedly, it can be challenging to harmonize input received through the COMBINE-EBIs approach, particularly when feedback conflicts or might be considered beyond the project’s scope. This raises concerns related to prioritization—determining which emerging needs should be incorporated while maintaining feasibility and alignment with each EBI’s core elements. Also, challenges related to intellectual property may arise when making adaptations to core components of licensed EBIs. Addressing these concerns is important to ensure legal compliance and ethical scholarship, especially in collaborative and translational contexts where questions of ownership may be particularly salient. It is important to consult institutional technology transfer office and policies to clarify ownership, licensing rights, and permissible uses of proprietary tools, data, or frameworks. Securing appropriate permissions and ensuring accurate attribution when necessary (e.g., use of third-party materials) is essential. As new products are developed, researchers are encouraged to consider open-access or Creative Commons licensing to help balance innovation with accessibility. Lastly, although the SupportSync case study demonstrates the feasibility of using the COMBINE-EBIs approach, further evaluation is needed to assess its applicability across diverse contexts.

Multi-component interventions that combine multiple existing EBIs to address complex, co-occurring health conditions offer promise for accelerating public health gains. COMBINE-EBIs is a rigorous, systematic approach for building multi-component, multi-modal interventions to address complex health issues or multiple, co-occurring health issues simultaneously. It builds upon the resources of prior work, is aligned with calls to use interventions with proven efficacy [108], and emphasizes community engagement. Using the COMBINE-EBIs approach could facilitate the creation of additional multi-component interventions to address complex, co-occurring health conditions synergistically. Future research should evaluate the feasibility and utility of COMBINE-EBIs, including any adaptations needed to maximize its utility. For example, implementation science methods [109], such as effectiveness-implementation hybrid designs [110], can be used to simultaneously evaluate the implementation of combined interventions in real world settings while assessing their effectiveness, with special attention to potential synergistic effects. Models such as the IRLM [12] (which we used in Step 4 of our case study) can help researchers and practitioners think through how the combined intervention will achieve its implementation and effectiveness outcomes, and identify potential adaptations that could maximize utility. Researchers and practitioners who apply COMBINE-EBIs could follow FRAME and FRAME-IS guidance and document adaptations that are made to the 5-step process and generalize whether those changes would be useful for others to follow as part of dissemination efforts. Applying the COMBINE-EBI approach has the potential to accelerate the creation of multi-component EBIs and put them into practice in a resource efficient way, which can help to improve population health across contexts.
