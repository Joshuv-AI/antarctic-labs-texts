Tick-associated microorganisms (Borrelia, Rickettsia, Hepatozoon, and Hemolivia) in Amblyomma varanense ticks parasitizing Asian water monitors (Varanus salvator): Insights from a hotspot in Ratchaburi Province, Western Thailand
Authors: Duriyang Narapakdeesakul, Su Yi Phyo Phyo Myint, Zaw Thet Aung, Witchuta Junsiri, Jitkamol Thanasak, Tanett Pongtheerat, Frederic Beugnet, Piyanan Taweethavonsawat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Asian water monitors (Varanus salvator) serve as significant hosts for ticks in Southeast Asia, yet the diversity of microorganisms potentially vectored or endosymbiotic within their ticks in Western Thailand remains underexplored. This study investigated the occurrence and genetic diversity of tick-associated microorganisms in ticks parasitizing this varanid species at the Khaozon Wildlife Breeding Center (KWBC), Ratchaburi Province. Morphological and molecular analyses confirmed that all 30 adult ticks collected were Amblyomma varanense (Arachnida: Ixodidae). PCR-based analysis coupled with DNA sequencing revealed that Rickettsia was the most prevalent microorganism detected (90.0%, 27/30), with gltA sequences genetically clustering within the spotted fever group (SFG) and closely related to the human pathogenic Rickettsia monacensis and Rickettsia tamurae. Utilizing the flaB gene, Borrelia spp. were identified in 76.66% (23/30) of ticks, forming lineages within the reptile-associated (REP) clade, distinct from Lyme borreliosis (LB) group. Furthermore, Hepatozoon sp. and Hemolivia sp. were each identified in 3.33% (1/30) of ticks; notably, this represents the first molecular record of Hemolivia sp. in A. varanense from a varanid host. Nevertheless, this discovery relies on a single genetic marker (18S rRNA gene). Our study revealed a diverse microbial community in varanid-associated A. varanense ticks at the KWBC. Despite the absence of human infections by reptile-associated Borrelia spp. and Rickettsia spp., the existence of these microorganisms underscores the critical need for ongoing One Health surveillance at this anthropogenic-forest interface.

Introduction

Tick-borne diseases represent a significant and escalating threat to public health and veterinary medicine worldwide. Climate change, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions, combined with rich biodiversity and intensifying human activities, offers optimal conditions for the survival and reproduction of diverse arthropod vectors, along with the transmission of their associated pathogens (Nepveu-Traversy et al., 2024; Byun et al., 2025; Gong et al., 2025). Since the turn of the millennium, research has prioritized ticks parasitizing domesticated or farmed animals due to the associated economic losses; however, there is a growing recognition of the significance of wildlife in the sylvatic cycles of infectious agents (Tsao et al., 2021; Defaye et al., 2022; Kazimírová et al., 2024). The rising incidence of human cases caused by tick-borne pathogens originating in wildlife has consequently attracted global public health concern and become a focal point of modern epidemiological research (Tsao et al., 2021; Kazimírová et al., 2024; Nepveu-Traversy et al., 2024; Gong et al., 2025). However, while research often focuses on mammalian hosts, reptiles are also epidemiologically relevant because they inhabit the anthropogenic-forest interface (Doherty et al., 2020), serving as significant reservoirs for diverse microorganisms (Mendoza-Roldan et al., 2021).

In Thailand, the Asian water monitor (Varanus salvator), one of the most widely distributed wildlife species in lowland freshwater and brackish wetlands (Auliya and Koch, 2020), has been recognized as a reservoir for various microorganisms (Thanasak et al., 2022; Junsiri et al., 2024, 2025). Our prior findings in the western and central regions indicated that this varanid harbored multiple unidentified species of Borrelia, Rickettsia, Anaplasma, and Hepatozoon (Junsiri et al., 2024, 2025); concurrently, several questions remain regarding the associated ticks and the diversity of tick-associated microorganisms (TAMs). In the southern region, Asian water monitors are heavily parasitized by hard ticks of the genus Amblyomma (Arachnida: Ixodidae), specifically Amblyomma varanense and Amblyomma helvolum (Kaenkan et al., 2020). Similarly, its closely related varanid sibling, the Bengal monitor (Varanus bengalensis), which inhabits the northeastern and southern regions, has frequently been parasitized by A. varanense and A. helvolum (Hirunkanokpun et al., 2025). Despite these ticks likely exhibiting a high host tropism for reptiles, cohabitation of animals and humans in anthropogenic areas may facilitate cross-host spillover of ticks, exemplified by the lizard tick Amblyomma limbatum, which has been reported to parasitize humans in South Australia (Norval et al., 2020). As varanids increasingly exist in human-dwelling areas, it is essential to investigate the presence of TAMs within their associated ticks to evaluate the potential, albeit still unsubstantiated, public health risks at this wildlife-human interface.

Regarding TAMs, recent studies have detected Borrelia sp. in varanid-associated Amblyomma ticks that phylogenetically cluster within the reptile-associated (REP) group, a lineage distinct from the relapsing fever (RF) and Lyme borreliosis (LB) groups (Kaenkan et al., 2020; Hirunkanokpun et al., 2025). Concurrently, two putatively novel rickettsial species, “Candidatus Rickettsia isanensis” and “Candidatus Rickettsia ranongensis”, have been proposed and assigned to the spotted fever group (SFG), alongside known species responsible for Mediterranean spotted fever (Rickettsia conorii and Rickettsia monacensis) and Rocky Mountain spotted fever (Rickettsia rickettsii) (Nepveu-Traversy et al., 2024; Hirunkanokpun et al., 2025). Despite these advancements, prior investigations have been restricted to the southern and northeastern provinces of Thailand. Thus, establishing a broader surveillance framework is essential to determine the actual geographical distribution of these agents. Furthermore, while reptile-associated Borrelia and Rickettsia have yet to be documented as infecting humans, the strains circulated in ticks parasitizing varanids in the country exhibit lineages that are related to human pathogenic species, signaling a potential public health threat that necessitates further exploration.

Besides Borrelia and Rickettsia, bacteria within the family Anaplasmataceae, particularly the genus Anaplasma, are increasingly acknowledged as components of the reptile-associated microbial community (Supriyono et al., 2019; Junsiri et al., 2025). Recent molecular surveys in Thailand have discovered a novel lineage of Anaplasma sp. present in Asian water monitors and A. varanense tick infesting Sumatran short-tailed python (Python curtus) (Hirunkanokpun et al., 2025; Junsiri et al., 2025). This lineage is related to other Anaplasma species detected in ticks from the Bengal monitor (Amblyomma gervaisi) and chimpanzee (Amblyomma tholloni), as well as “Candidatus Cryptoplasma californiense” in free-ranging Ixodes pacificus tick (Eshoo et al., 2015; Khan et al., 2022; Lacroux et al., 2023). Meanwhile, Anaplasma spp. circulating in Asian water monitors in Bogor, Indonesia, comprised two distinct lineages: one related to isolates from bird-associated ticks and the other to ruminants (Supriyono et al., 2019). Despite the established presence of highly genetically diverse Anaplasma species in varanids, the diversity of this agent in their associated ticks remains largely uncharacterized.

Members of the genera Hepatozoon and Hemolivia (Apicomplexa: Adeleorina) are intra-erythrocytic haemogregarines typically present in reptiles (Barta et al., 2012; Harris et al., 2011; Junsiri et al., 2024, 2025). While they are generally considered to have negligible zoonotic relevance, their inclusion in this study is necessitated by their role as significant ecological indicators of vector-host dynamics within the sylvatic cycle. Exploring these protozoans alongside bacteria provides a more holistic perspective on the microbial community maintained by varanid ticks. In the northeastern and eastern regions of Thailand, Hepatozoon spp. have been detected in A. varanense and A. helvolum ticks parasitizing colubrine (Fowlea piscator, Ptyas korros), elapid (Ophiophagus hannah), and python (Python bivittatus) snakes, with two distinct phylogenetic lineages observed (Sumrandee et al., 2015). Additionally, Amblyomma geoemydae tick infesting an elongated tortoise (Indotestudo elongata) in the southern region was found to harbor Hemolivia sp. (Ahantarig et al., 2019). Despite their occurrence in snake-associated ticks, these haemogregarines have not been documented to date in ticks parasitizing varanids.

This study aimed to bridge these knowledge gaps by employing a cross-sectional survey to investigate the occurrence and genetic diversity of bacterial (Borrelia, Rickettsia, Anaplasma) and protozoan (Hepatozoon, Hemolivia) microorganisms in ticks parasitizing Asian water monitors in Ratchaburi Province, Western Thailand. By characterizing these TAMs, this research attempts to elucidate the role of varanid ticks in the sustainment of the sylvatic transmission cycle and assess the potential implications for wildlife conservation and public health in the country.

Materials and methods

Sampling site and tick collection

Tick surveys were conducted at the Khaozon Wildlife Breeding Center (KWBC) (13°34′54.7″N, 99°29′17.5″E), a government facility in Ratchaburi Province, Western Thailand (Fig. 1), owing to its expectation as a hotspot for diverse microorganisms (Junsiri et al., 2025). It is located at an elevation of approximately 131–138 m above sea level, as assessed by Google Earth Pro v7.3.6.9345, on the foothills of the Tenasserim Hills, which delineate Thailand’s border with Myanmar. Additionally, KWBC is surrounded by mountains (elevation ranging from 171 to 355 m) and tropical lowland evergreen forests (Stibig et al., 2004), principally consisting of dipterocarp and deciduous trees, along with interspersed buildings, ponds, and wetlands.Fig. 1Google Earth Pro satellite-based maps showing the location of the Khaozon Wildlife Breeding Center (KWBC) in Ratchaburi Province, Western Thailand (A) and the surrounding environment and habitat (B).Fig. 1

Our prior investigations on Asian water monitors (V. salvator) (n = 16) captured in this area from November 2023 to May 2024 uncovered complicated species of microorganisms (Borrelia, Rickettsia, Anaplasma, and Hepatozoon) (Junsiri et al., 2025). Later, in August 2024, a total of 16 varanids were captured by staff; however, it is unknown whether they are from the same population as in the previous survey, to inspect them for ectoparasites as part of a zoonotic pathogen surveillance programme (Permit No. 0909.713/886). A total of 30 ticks, which were readily observable, were meticulously picked from animal body surfaces using forceps and thereafter preserved individually in 1.5-ml microcentrifuge tubes with 70% ethanol. Subsequent to tick collection, all varanids were released back to their original habitats. All tick samples were then delivered to the laboratory at the Faculty of Veterinary Science, Chulalongkorn University, for further investigation of TAMs under the biosafety regulations (Permit No. 2531043).

Morphological identification of ticks and DNA extraction

Ticks were identified morphologically for the genus, species, sex, and life stage using the taxonomic keys (Tanskul et al., 1983; Kwak, 2020). Subsequently, following the protocols outlined by Hirunkanokpun et al. (2025), ticks were decontaminated by progressively rinsing the surface three times with 10% sodium hypochlorite, 70% ethanol, and sterile distilled water, each for 1 min. The individual ticks were transversely sectioned and then homogenized with a scalpel blade. Genomic DNA (gDNA) was isolated from one-half of each tick body, encompassing the salivary gland and midgut, with a NucleoSpin® Tissue Kit (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions, except for the elution step. To obtain a high yield of gDNA, Buffer BE, which had been preheated to 70 °C, was reduced by 70% from 100 μl to 30 μl and thereafter added to the filtered column prior to the elution process. After these steps, the elution was repeated. gDNA of the first elution was used for screening of TAMs, and gDNA of the second elution was used for molecular identification of tick species.

Molecular identification of ticks and detection of tick-associated microorganisms

To molecularly identify tick species, conventional PCR (cPCR) was used to amplify the partial sequence of the mitochondrial 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene, utilizing universal primers (Black and Piesman, 1994) and thermal cycling conditions outlined in Supplementary Table S1. Detection of TAMs was conducted through nested or semi-nested PCRs (nPCR, snPCR) with universal primers intended to amplify Borrelia flagellin (flaB) gene (Stromdahl et al., 2003), Rickettsia citrate synthase (gltA) gene (Ishikura et al., 2003), Anaplasma 16S rRNA gene (Barlough et al., 1996; Parola et al., 2000), and haemogregarine 18S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene (Perkins and Keller, 2001; Ujvari et al., 2004). Amplification was conducted in a total volume of 25 μl of the reaction mixture, containing 12.5 μl of 2× GoTaq® Green Master Mix (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), 0.75 μl of each primer (10 pmol), 9 μl of nuclease-free water, and 2 μl of gDNA template. During the nPCR or snPCR amplifications, primary PCR products were diluted 5-fold using UltraPure Water (Canvax, Valladolid, Spain) and then utilized as a template in the secondary PCR. gDNA of Borrelia sp., Rickettsia sp., Anaplasma sp., and Hepatozoon sp. isolated from varanid blood samples by Junsiri et al. (2025) served as positive controls, whereas UltraPure Water (Canvax) was used as a negative control. All PCR reactions were performed in a MiniAmp™ Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystems™, Waltham, MA, USA).

PCR-positive bands were purified using a NucleoSpin® Gel and PCR Clean-up (Macherey-Nagel) following the manufacturer’s protocols and then sequenced bidirectionally through Sanger sequencing service by U2Bio Company Limited (https://www.u2bio.com), which was performed in an ABI 3730XL DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems™). In cases of observed mixed chromatograms, amplicons were subcloned into pGEM®-T Easy Vector Systems (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). The reaction mixture comprised 5 μl of 2× Rapid Ligation Buffer, 1 μl of pGEM®-T Easy Vector, 1 μl of T4 DNA Ligase, 1 μl of UltraPure Water (Canvax), and 2 μl of purified PCR products. The ligated vector was subsequently transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α competent cells, and DNA sequencing was performed on three recombinant plasmid clones using M13F(-20) (5′-GTA AAA CGA CGG CCA GT-3′) and M13R (5′-CAG GAA ACA GCT ATG AC-3′) primers provided by U2Bio Company.

Sequence processing and genetic analyses

Sequencing results were meticulously assessed for the existence of ambiguous chromatograms using BioEdit v7.2.5 (Hall, 1999). The nucleotide sequences newly generated herein (Supplementary Table S2) were compared to those in the GenBank® database employing the BLASTN search. Thereafter, sequences were aligned using the G-INS-i strategy in MAFFT v7 (Katoh et al., 2017) and analyzed to ascertain the number of nucleotide sequence type (ntST) via DnaSP v6.12.03 (Rozas et al., 2017). Furthermore, MEGA 11 v11.0.13 (Tamura et al., 2021) was used to calculate the genetic divergence, utilizing the uncorrected p-distance model, incorporating transitions + transversions substitutions at uniform rates.

The phylogenetic trees were constructed using the Bayesian inference (BI) method in BEAST v2.7.7 (Bouckaert et al., 2019). The best-fitting evolutionary models were selected using ModelFinder based on the lowest values of Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) (Posada and Buckley, 2004; Kalyaanamoorthy et al., 2017). Due to the considerable genetic diversity and potential intricate phylogenetic topologies previously observed in Borrelia flaB, Rickettsia gltA, and Hepatozoon 18S rDNA sequences (Cook et al., 2015; Supriyono et al., 2019; Koual et al., 2024; Junsiri et al., 2025), an optimised relaxed clock (Douglas et al., 2021) was utilized, along with a Yule model tree prior and Bactrian operator (Yang and Rodríguez, 2013). This approach allows individual branches of a phylogeny to have independent evolutionary substitution rates while also assuming that every species has an equal opportunity of experiencing a speciation event at any given time, which is advantageous for visualizing closely related or novel species. The BI analysis was performed in the subscribed CIPRES Science Gateway (https://www.phylo.org). The posterior probability support for each node was inferred from 100,000,000 MCMC steps, with sampling conducted every 10,000 generations from two independent chains. The first 10% of MCMC steps were removed as “burn-in”, and the phylogenetic reconstructions were visualized in FigTree v1.4.4 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree).

Results

Tick identification

All ticks (n = 30) collected from Asian water monitors at KWBC were in the adult stage, consisting of 15 males and 15 females (Supplementary Table S2). Ten of the female ticks were engorged with blood. All ticks were morphologically and molecularly identified as Amblyomma varanense, with the partial 16S rDNA sequences (403 bp) being identical across all isolates (designated as ntST1) (Supplementary Table S3). BLASTN search utilizing ntST1 revealed a 100% similarity and 100% query coverage with A. varanense RNG121 (GenBank: OR366536), which infested a Sumatran short-tailed python (Python curtus) in Ranong Province, Southern Thailand (Hirunkanokpun et al., 2025).

Molecular detection of tick-associated microorganisms in ticks

The PCR-based analyses showed that A. varanense ticks tested positive for Borrelia spp. (76.66%, 23/30), Rickettsia spp. (90.00%, 27/30), and haemogregarines (6.66%, 2/30). Anaplasma was not detected in any of the analyzed ticks. Only one tick (TickAm11) was PCR-negative for all aforementioned microorganisms. Through DNA sequencing and BLASTN search (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3), 23 Borrelia-positive and 27 Rickettsia-positive ticks were confirmed for their infections. Two samples of Borrelia (TickAm09 and TickAm23) and Rickettsia (TickAm22 and TickAm25) exhibited mixed chromatograms, implying the existence of multiple ntSTs, which necessitated subcloning. Additionally, two haemogregarine-positive ticks were confirmed: one was infected with Hepatozoon sp. and the other with Hemolivia sp. Notably, monoinfection was identified in 8 ticks (27.58%), whereas double and triple infections were detected in 20 (68.96%) and 2 (6.89%) ticks, respectively. Because the positivity rates of ticks harboring Borrelia spp. and Rickettsia spp. were extremely high, gDNA was therefore extracted from the remaining transversely sectioned bodies of each tick to verify the accuracy of these results and to ensure that no contamination occurred during the study, especially considering the nPCR approach. The bacteria and protozoan microorganisms were re-examined, and the number of positive samples remained the same as before.

Borrelia and Rickettsia

Twenty-five nucleotide sequences of Borrelia flaB were obtained (300 bp), with 9 ntSTs detected (Supplementary Table S3). Among these, four nSTs (ntST1, ntST4, ntST7, and ntST8) were identical to Borrelia sp. RT18S (GenBank: LC428377), Borrelia sp. RT4M (GenBank: LC428373), Borrelia sp. RT1S (GenBank: CP088943), and Borrelia sp. RT15S (GenBank: LC428376), respectively, derived from A. varanense ticks fed on Asian water monitors (V. salvator) in Bogor, West Java, Indonesia (Supriyono et al., 2019). Five other ntSTs (ntST2, ntST3, ntST5, ntST6, and ntST9) exhibited similarities of 99.00–99.67% with those Borrelia isolates (GenBank: MK462207, CP088943, LC428371, LC428372, and LC428373) detected in varanid A. varanense ticks in Bogor (Supriyono et al., 2019).

The BI analysis categorized our ntST1 to ntST9 in the Borrelia reptile-associated (REP) group (Fig. 3). The posterior probabilities supported the presence of five tentatively phylogenetic subclades (A-E) of the varanid-associated Borrelia lineage. Eight ntSTs (ntST1, ntST3 to ntST9) were classified as subclade A, together with those from A. varanense ticks (Supriyono et al., 2019). Only ntST2 clustered in subclade B with isolates from A. varanense and Amblyomma helvolum ticks parasitizing varanids in Thailand (Kaenkan et al., 2020). Four Borrelia isolates previously detected in Asian water monitors at KWBC (Junsiri et al., 2025) were phylogenetically positioned in subclade C and distinct from our isolates found in ticks herein. Meanwhile, subclades D and E comprised Borrelia found in Amblyomma calabyi and Amblyomma exornatum from varanids in Australia (Gofton et al., 2023) and an unknown location, respectively. Furthermore, the interspecific divergence between subclade A and subclades B, C, D, and E was 6.47%, 8.5%, 8.8%, and 14.73%, respectively. Subclade B exhibited divergences of 8.02%, 8.27%, and 13.5% when compared with subclades C, D, and E, respectively. Hepatozoon and Hemolivia

Discussion

In Thailand, findings of TAMs in varanid-associated ticks have been previously recorded in the southern (Pattani, Narathiwat, Satun) and northeastern (Sisaket) provinces (Kaenkan et al., 2020; Hirunkanokpun et al., 2025). Therefore, our study, conducted at KWBC in Ratchaburi Province, broadens the geographical range of TAMs in ticks parasitizing varanids in the western region, which has also been recognized as a hotspot for several vector-borne pathogens and wildlife microorganisms (Aung et al., 2022; Poofery et al., 2022; Narapakdeesakul et al., 2023, 2024). Additionally, the rain shadow effect in the area (Pinpipat et al., 2025), as surrounded by mountains (Fig. 1B), could preserve the precipitation and protect ticks from lethal dryness, while the tropical evergreen forest habitat may create suitable environmental conditions for the life cycles of ticks and TAMs. Our investigation was conducted at a singular time-frame (August 2024), and a longitudinal study remains crucial to clarify the seasonal dynamics of tick abundance and microbial prevalence driven by climatic or related environmental factors.

Furthermore, during the time-frame of investigation, no blood samples were obtained from varanid hosts. The genetic discrepancy between the TAMs, notably Borrelia, identified in the ticks and those previously detected in varanids (Junsiri et al., 2025) raises the hypothesis that these ticks are not exclusive to the Asian water monitors they infest, but may have a multifaceted interaction with other reptiles in the area. Given that A. varanense seems to be associated with multiple reptile hosts (Kaenkan et al., 2020; Hirunkanokpun et al., 2025), it is essential to identify the blood-meal sources. Unfortunately, ten engorged female ticks failed to amplify the vertebrate cox1 sequences using established universal primers developed by Alcaide et al. (2009) (Supplementary Table S4). This might be due to the prolonged storage of tick samples, leading to the degradation of DNA in the blood, or it may be necessary to develop primers specific to each reptilian group, particularly varanids, snakes, and tortoises. Additionally, due to time constraints and the difficulty of handling animals, staff collected those ticks that were easily visible to the naked eye, which were later identified as all adults. Since the other life stages (larvae and nymphs) were disregarded, it cannot be confidently claimed that A. varanense is the only tick species present on varanids in the area. Moreover, ticks were surveyed from 16 varanids; however, we did not record the quantity of ticks on each animal or specify whether the ticks derived from the same or different hosts. These also limit our ability to assess the actual tick density, host specificity, and TAM prevalence over the investigated timeframe.

This study identified only A. varanense ticks, all of which were genetically identical to an isolate infesting a Sumatran short-tailed python in Ranong Province (Hirunkanokpun et al., 2025). This is unsurprising considering that this tick species has been reported to parasitize multiple varanid and snake species (Kaenkan et al., 2020; Hirunkanokpun et al., 2025). In fact, Ratchaburi Province (west) and Ranong Province (south) are geographically connected by the Tenasserim Hills, perhaps facilitating distribution routes between these two provinces. Based on the BI analysis integrated with a speciation model (Fig. 2), it seems that isolates STN3 (GenBank: MK480199) and PTN177 (GenBank: MK480197), obtained from Varanus spp. in southern provinces (Kaenkan et al., 2020), established distinct clades from other A. varanense. Genetically, ntST1 had 96.77% and 96.27% similarities with STN3 and PTN177, respectively, while STN3 and PTN177 shared 96.76%. Although this observation relies on a single mitochondrial gene (16S rRNA) and is devoid of nuclear gene markers inquiry, genetic divergence of greater than 3% potentially represents a distinct species (Smit et al., 2024). It is hypothesized that A. varanense may constitute a complex group comprising several species or subspecies members. Further studies are essential to investigate insights into the evolutionary history, particularly at the genomic level, to elucidate the taxonomic status of these reptile-associated ticks.

Borrelia detection rate of 76.7% (23/30) observed in varanid ticks at KWBC is notably high when compared to earlier findings in Thai provinces: Narathiwat (20.0%, 1/5), Satun (18.4%, 7/38), Pattani (15.8%, 3/19), and Sisaket (2.4%, 2/83) (Kaenkan et al., 2020; Hirunkanokpun et al., 2025), but lower than those reported in Bogor, Indonesia (95.7%, 22/23) (Supriyono et al., 2019). Surprisingly, the majority of ntSTs (ntST1, ntST3 to ntST9) identified in this study shared a lineage with Borrelia strains from varanid-associated A. varanense ticks in Bogor (Supriyono et al., 2019), distinct from those isolates discovered in Asian water monitors at KWBC (Junsiri et al., 2025) (Fig. 3). This genetic congruence between Western Thailand and West Java raises intriguing questions regarding the transboundary distribution of these bacteria, particularly given that Bogor is situated in West Java, which may possess an island ecology. Despite the mainland’s connectivity to the islands during the Late Pliocene and Early Pleistocene epochs likely facilitated the historical dispersal of reptile communities (Berghuis et al., 2025), the rate of bacterial evolution is very rapid, particularly over millennia (Duchêne et al., 2016). The ancient connectivity alone may not explain this pattern. Contemporary factors, such as the cross-border migration of reservoirs and ticks or the anthropogenic trade of exotic reptiles (Pietzsch et al., 2006; Mihalca, 2015), likely contribute to the maintenance of these shared lineages. Furthermore, regarding genetic divergence and phylogenetic position, at least five tentative Borrelia subclades (A-E) may exist in the varanids and their associated ticks. Varanids exhibit a high degree of species diversity, and their microorganisms may have co-evolved and diversified alongside their hosts since ancient times (Brennan et al., 2021). Nonetheless, for species-level identification or the proposal of putatively novel species, multi-locus sequence typing, beyond a small fragment of flaB gene (300 bp), is essential.

Rickettsia sp. was confirmed in 90.0% (27/30) of our varanid ticks, a significantly higher rate than those reported in Sisaket, Thailand (14.8%, 12/81) (Hirunkanokpun et al., 2025) and Bogor, Indonesia (21.7%, 5/23) (Supriyono et al., 2019). The high prevalence in these ticks likely reflects that the KWBC is a hotspot for rickettsial circulation, and these agents may function as primary endosymbionts of A. varanense ticks at this location. Despite obtaining 30 gltA sequences, only three ntSTs were detected, reflecting the highly conserved nature of this genetic marker. The BI and genetic analyses strongly supported that our rickettsial isolates are conspecific with Uncultured Rickettsia sp. S5 (GenBank: KJ769648) from an Indian python-associated A. varanense tick in Johore, Peninsular Malaysia (Kho et al., 2015). This demonstrates the geographical distribution of this bacterium across the Thai and Malay Peninsulas. Our isolates are firmly positioned within the rickettsial SFG (Fig. 4). Importantly, they established a related lineage to Rickettsia monacensis and Rickettsia tamurae, both of which are recognized for their pathogenicity in humans (Jado et al., 2007; Imaoka et al., 2011; Kim et al., 2017). Despite the absence of documented infections in humans by reptile or varanid-associated tick rickettsiae, and the lack of characterization regarding their pathogenesis and virulence, the possibility of their transmission to humans should not be neglected, particularly considering that varanids regularly exist in human-dwelling areas (Auliya and Koch, 2020). While A. varanense ticks do not prefer humans and the bacteria are presently their endosymbionts, the narrowing gap between wildlife habitats and human settlements may create a spillover risk, as reported in South Australia (Norval et al., 2020). Furthermore, akin to Borrelia, these rickettsiae warrant additional multi-locus sequence typing beyond the use of gltA sequences (437 bp) alone employed in this study.

Despite the high infection rate of Hepatozoon spp. typically observed in varanids and reptile-associated ticks (Sumrandee et al., 2015; Junsiri et al., 2024, 2025), our study detected a parasite of this genus in only a single sample (3.3%, 1/30). This discrepancy may be ascribed to the kinetics of sporogony within the tick or the time of collection in relation to the tick’s feeding period. The BI analysis revealed the monophyly of our Hepatozoon ntST1 and isolates from the wolf snakes in Seychelles (Harris et al., 2011) (Fig. 5). This transoceanic genetic relatedness across isolates in Southeast Asia and East Africa suggests a complex evolutionary history, perhaps involving ancient dispersal events (Vidal et al., 2012) or the trading of reptiles across the Indian Ocean basin (Fuller et al., 2011). However, to elucidate the transmission route and origin of the microorganism, integrating genome-level data with molecular dating analysis is crucial for explaining the aforementioned hypothesis.

Besides Hepatozoon sp., Hemolivia sp. was also identified in one tick sample (3.3%, 1/30). This genus is typically associated with testudine, bufonid, and scincid hosts (Harris et al., 2013; Cook et al., 2015; Ahantarig et al., 2019). To date, only four ixodid tick species have been implicated in the transmission of Hemolivia spp.: Hyalomma aegyptium and Amblyomma geoemydae, which fed on tortoises (Harris et al., 2013; Ahantarig et al., 2019), Amblyomma dissimile on toads and iguanas (Cotes-Perdomo et al., 2018; Koual et al., 2024), and Amblyomma limbatum on shinglebacks (Smallridge and Paperna, 1997). To our knowledge, this is the first molecular record of Hemolivia in A. varanense tick associated with Asian water monitors. Our isolate (ntST2) was recovered as a phylogenetically distinct lineage from Hemolivia mariae, Hemolivia stellata, and Hemolivia sp. DJH-2013 (Fig. 5), perhaps indicating a potentially novel species in varanid ticks. With regard to Hepatozoon sp., this hypothesis should be explored using genome-level data rather than relying solely on the partial 18S rDNA sequence, which is reasonably conserved among haemogregarines and typically yields short branch lengths with minimal resolution among closely related species (Cook et al., 2015; Cotes-Perdomo et al., 2018; Koual et al., 2024). Regrettably, the GenBank® database (accessed on February 16, 2026) currently has only 18S rDNA reference sequences of Hemolivia. While this genetic marker is primarily employed for universal detection and identification, mitochondrial and apicoplast genes or genomes may provide stronger phylogenetic and evolutionary signals within apicomplexan parasites (Arisue and Hashimoto, 2015). Further studies should consider using these markers beyond the 18S rRNA gene to investigate Hemolivia spp. in both animals and vectors.

Conclusion

This study significantly expands the known geographical range of tick-associated microorganisms in Western Thailand, providing the first molecular evidence of Borrelia, Rickettsia, Hepatozoon, and Hemolivia in Amblyomma varanense ticks parasitizing Asian water monitors (Varanus salvator). From a One Health perspective, the presence of Borrelia and Rickettsia, despite the absence of human infections with reptile-associated strains, underscores the necessity of monitoring and managing reptile-associated ticks to mitigate potential spillover risks at the wildlife-human interface.
