Optimizing photon conversion routes in cinnamate derivatives
Authors: Michael Hymas, Jack Dalton, Ivan Romanov, Hans Sanders, Mario Barbatti, Josene M Toldo, Wybren Jan Buma, Vasilios G Stavros
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractE-Z photoisomerisation is a key relaxation process in biological chromophores, supporting processes such as vision and photoprotection. Here we use such pathways to optimise photon-to-heat conversion processes. To this purpose, we synthesise methyl cinnamate derivatives in which steric and electronic substitutions modulate excited-state decay. Femtosecond spectroscopy in solution and gas phase shows how para-methoxy substitution facilitates access to a conical intersection, while steric hindrance at the central double bond manoeuvres excited population towards a barrierless pathway back to the ground state, leading to derivatives isomerising on a timescale approaching that of cis-11-retinal, involved in vision. By associating substitution patterns with potential energy surface topography determined by multireference quantum chemical calculations, we identify how nonradiative decay can be accelerated in cinnamate scaffolds. These results advance mechanistic insights into cinnamate photophysics and pave the way for the rational design of efficient photon converters for photo-switching, heat generation, and light-filtering applications. Subject terms: Excited states, Quantum chemistry

Introduction

Structures capable of photo-isomerisation are ubiquitous in nature1. E-Z (or trans-cis) photo-isomerisation underpins diverse biological processes, including visual transduction2, photoprotection3–5, and phototaxis6. Often coupled to a protein scaffold, the rapid and predictable characteristics of photo-isomerisable biological molecules have inspired the design of biomimetic species via chemical synthesis, principally for use as photo-switches7. Research into the photo-isomerisation of molecules has advanced significantly in recent decades following the advent of ultrafast spectroscopy2,8. A striking example of natural optimisation is the E-Z isomerisation of cis-11-retinal, the primary event in vision. This takes approximately 200 fs to complete, with a conical intersection (CoIn) reached within 75 fs2, triggering the conformational change of rhodopsin9. Although vision is ultimately limited by slower downstream processes, this initial isomerisation step is exceptionally fast and central to its efficiency9.

Another photo-isomerisable moiety found in many species, particularly in plants, is the cinnamate backbone10. Functionalisation at the phenyl ring and at the carboxylate in this structure generates a wide range of derivatives, including sinapates, coumarates, and ferulates4,11–15, many of which act as UV filters. It is presumed that the structural speciation of these molecules is derived from an evolutionary response to their host’s environment16–18. Methyl cinnamate (MC) is the methyl ester of cinnamic acid and is widely found in plants and their fruits19. Analogues of MC, including para-hydroxy MC (p-HMC, otherwise known as methyl coumarate), have been studied as models for isomers in photoactive proteins, and as model plant photo-protectants themselves3,20. These studies have revealed systematic substitution effects on photoisomerisation21–23.

It has been previously observed that simple derivatisations of the structure of photo-isomers can sufficiently perturb their ground and/or excited potential energy surfaces (PESs) to significantly increase the rate of photo-isomerisation, particularly by reshaping CoIn topography22,24–28. In this study, we designed and synthesised derivatives of MC to investigate how substitution can be used to tailor internal conversion (IC) of electronically excited states along such photoisomerisation pathways. We use a dual-pronged approach in which we: firstly, introduce electron-donating substituents on the phenyl ring to modulate the electron density around the isomerisable double bond; and secondly, influence steric interactions to facilitate access to the CoIn. To this purpose, three derivatives of MC were synthesised with substitutions on the phenyl ring and at the central double bond (Fig. 1) and studied using a range of spectroscopic techniques.

Starting from the unsubstituted cinnamate, we first substituted the ortho positions with methoxy groups. These electron-donating substituents increase the electronic density on the benzene ring and, consequently, on the C=C bond, raising the HOMO energy and making the molecule more reactive towards the CoIn. Thus, we expected that this would decrease the energy barrier to CoIn, facilitating IC via twisting around the central double bond. Moreover, we also expected that the ortho substitution would cause unfavourable steric interactions, leading to a faster geometry relaxation of the initially excited population, driven by the twisting of the central C=C bond. In contrast to these expectations, we find, however, that this starting derivative, (E)-di-ortho-methoxy-MC (1), exhibits a relatively slow nonradiative deactivation, both in solution and in vacuo.

Motivated by previous findings on the effects of substitution at the para position of MC and its derivatives26, we then append an electron-donating para methoxy group to yield (E)-di-ortho, para-methoxy-MC (2), which succeeds in accelerating IC by several orders of magnitude as compared to 1. Subsequently, inspired by cis-11-retinal, introduction of a methyl group at the C=C double bond — to kick-start isomerisation by steric hindrance — produces methyl-(E)-di-ortho, para-methoxy-MC (3). Compound 3 exhibits the fastest nonradiative deactivation, almost an order of magnitude faster than 2, and on par with isomerisation lifetimes reported for cis-11-retinal2.

To rationalise the experimental results, we map the molecules’ PESs and CoIn topographies using quantum chemical calculations. Specifically, we used TD-DFT to compute UV-visible spectra in the gas and solvent phases, and extended multi-state complete active space second-order perturbation theory (XMS-CASPT2) as well as mixed-reference spin-flip TDDFT (MRSF-TDDFT) to access the PES and CoIn topographies. Such calculations enable us to identify key features of molecular and electronic structure and use these as a guiding principle for optimising their IC pathways.

Results and Discussion

We begin by presenting the experimental and theoretical results for UV-visible absorption and emission spectra. After that, we briefly introduce some key features of the calculated PESs, using different electronic structure methods, to guide the discussion of ensuing femtosecond spectroscopies. We then discuss the experimental results for the dynamics in the solvent and gas phase, followed by the computed reaction pathways. In this last section, we compare the key minimum energy CoIn geometries and energy barriers of molecules 1–3, examining the presence or absence of S₁ minima. Finally, we relate these PES features to the fs-TA lifetimes, thereby grounding the theory in experimental findings.

UV-visible absorption and emission spectra

UV-visible absorption spectra were obtained for molecules 1-3 (see synthesis information in the Supporting Information, SI Section A) in ethanol (see Fig. 1), acetonitrile, and 1,4-dioxane (see SI Section B, Figure S1) as appropriate model solvents with decreasing degrees of polarity. These measurements provide the baseline against which ultrafast dynamics will be interpreted. We observed the strongest, most redshifted transition for molecule 2 (in all solvents), followed by molecule 1, and finally by molecule 3.

Trends in energy and intensity of observed absorption band maxima for the three molecules were rationalised using TD-DFT (ωB97X-D/cc-pVDZ level) in vacuo and with implicit solvent (Table S2). Although blue-shifted from observed UV–visible absorption maxima (as is expected for gas vs. solution phase measurements), the trends in the calculated TD-DFT vertical excitation energy match those seen in the experiment. At this level of theory, the electronic band origin is assigned as a strongly absorbing S1(V(ππ*)) state in molecules 1-3.

Molecule 2 has a planar ground state geometry and exhibits the most effective conjugation pattern and the lowest S1 excitation energy due to the para-methoxy group’s stronger resonance effect and the absence of steric hindrance between ortho substituents and the central methyl group. In molecule 3, the methyl substituent at the central double bond introduces steric hindrance, reducing the planarity (53° from planarity) and degree of conjugation across the chromophore (Figure S8), yielding higher excitation energies and smaller oscillator strengths.

In molecule 1, the absence of the methyl substituent attached to the central double bond affords planarity in the ground state. In contrast, the absence of a methoxy substituent at the para position influences the conjugation and electronic transition properties due to the so-called para-substituent effect29. These two effects combined explain why the excitation energy of 1 has an intermediate value between molecules 2 and 3. Although it is also planar in the ground state, 1 exhibits a much smaller oscillator strength for its brightest state (S1, labelled V(ππ*)) in comparison to 2, but a much higher oscillator strength for the second bright state (S2, labelled V’(ππ*)) (see Table S2). This state ordering is given by TD-DFT calculations, but a state switch is observed with the multiconfigurational methods, as discussed later.

The decrease of conjugation extension caused either by the break of planarity (as in 3) or lack of para-substituent (as in 1) is also reflected in the charge transfer (CT) character of the molecules. Twisted molecules decrease the overlap between hole and electron, thereby decreasing the oscillator strength, and therefore exhibiting a more pronounced twisted intramolecular charge transfer (TICT) character30. Indeed, the CT character computed for the bright state (S1) of molecule 3 (q(CT) = 0.406, Figure S6) is higher than for the other two molecules. The magnitude and direction of the CT are also influenced by the nature of the substituents at the para-position. In general, electron-donating groups increase intramolecular CT while electron-withdrawing groups decrease it30,31. In molecule 2, the electron-donating para-methoxy group increases the electron density in the ring’s π-system30, which is propagated towards the central double bond, stabilising the S1 ← S0 transition and slightly increasing the TICT character of 2 (q(CT) = 0.391) when compared to 1 (q(CT) = 0.374).

Figure S7 illustrates the natural orbitals involved in the three lowest energy electronic transitions for 1-3 calculated with TD-DFT. The brightest state (V(ππ*)), consists primarily of a HOMO to LUMO excitation, with the HOMO delocalised over the entire π-system. A second ππ* state with a much smaller oscillator strength (V’(ππ*)) is associated with excitation from the HOMO–1 orbital localised on the benzene ring to the LUMO. Vertically, the next lowest excited singlet state according to TD-DFT is of nπ* character, predominantly involving excitation from a carbonyl lone pair (HOMO-2) to the LUMO. Typically, this corresponds to the S3 state but it can be adiabatically lower than the other two states depending on the substituent and the level of theory used in the calculation.

The inclusion of implicit solvent with well-separated dielectric constants (cyclohexane and ethanol) in the TD-DFT calculations demonstrates the persistence of the trend observed in the gas phase. For the three molecules, there is an S1 (ππ* character) bathochromic shift upon solvent addition (Table S2), resulting from the energetic stabilization caused by the solvent. S2 also has ππ* character (V’(ππ*)), and it is also stabilized by the inclusion of solvent (although to a smaller extent). S3 is an nπ* state, a forbidden transition with a negligible oscillator strength (i.e., optically dark), which is slightly destabilized in more polar solvents. The S1(ππ*) state exhibits a consistent, solvent-independent energetic stabilization across the series. This behaviour results from two competing contributions: electronic effects, whereby electron-donating para-substituents in 2 lower the ππ* transition energy relative to the unsubstituted cinnamate, and steric effects, whereby increased molecular twisting in 3 reduces conjugation and raises the excitation energy.

Steady-state fluorescence quantum yields for each molecule in ethanol were measured at < 1% in all cases. Nevertheless, time-resolved emission spectra (Figure S2) for 1 in ethanol, acetonitrile, and 1,4-dioxane gave lifetimes in agreement with those from fs-TA results (vide infra). These results indicate that relaxation proceeds mostly through nonradiative channels for 1, and entirely through these for 2 and 3, consistent with the ultrafast isomerisation pathways studied later.

Impact of electronic structure level on state ordering and calculated PESs

Although TDDFT can provide a reasonable description of the transitions at the Franck–Condon region, highly correlated methods are needed to further investigate the PES of these molecules. This is due to the possibility of heterolytic C=C bond breaking, which can give rise to a pyramidalised sp³ carbon, which carries the lone pair32. This is not adequately described by TD-DFT. Therefore, to investigate the relaxation pathways for the molecules in this study, we performed multiconfigurational calculations at the XMS-CASPT2 level. Additional calculations at the MRSF-TDDFT level were performed as a second way to check the ordering of the states (which differs between TD-DFT and XMS-CASPT2). Thus, the next sections discuss the results of PESs calculated with XMS-CASPT2, which are supported by the results from MRSF-TDDFT. Details of the calculations are provided in the Computational Methods.

Before moving to the experimental results, it is useful to note several similarities and differences in the results from the different electronic structure methods discussed in this work, and their impact on the PESs of 1-3.

The first concerns the ordering of the lower electronic singlet excited states calculated with the three methods (see Tables S2 and S3) for the vertical excitation from S0. While TD-DFT predicts the same energetic order for the three molecules, that is, V(ππ*) < V’(ππ*) < nπ*, XMS-CASPT2 and MRSF-TDDFT predict a different state order for molecules 1 and 3. For the latter molecule, the S1 state is still the bright V(ππ*) state, while the nπ* and V’(ππ*) state switch order. This has no overall impact on the dynamics since, after excitation, the V(ππ*) state is strongly and rapidly stabilized. However, for molecule 1, the two ππ* states lie very close in energy, and at both XMS-CASPT2 and MRSF-TDDFT levels, the bright state is now the S2(V(ππ*)) state. This switch in the state order has a pronounced impact on the relaxation pathway of molecule 1. This impact can be schematically seen in Fig. 2, and it is discussed in detail in the section “Mapping the PES”. Another difference between the methods is the absolute value of the oscillator strength (f): XMS-CASPT2 predicts an oscillator strength for the V(ππ*) state that is only about twice that of the V’(ππ*), whereas the other methods yield much larger differences.

Next, in the analysis of excited-state geometries and adiabatic energies, it was found that the XMS-CASPT2 and MRSF-TDDFT methods yield similar structures and state splittings (Tables S4–S8) (TD-DFT also provided similar geometries for the S1 state of the three molecules, but this is not relevant to the discussion). We can, therefore, highlight the following differences and similarities between them: (i) molecule 1: V’(ππ*), V(ππ*), and nπ* are nearly degenerate at the XMS-CASPT2 level with adiabatic energies of 3.97 eV, 3.91 eV, and 3.88 eV, respectively (Table S4); at the MRSF-TDDFT level, the states are nearly degenerate too: V’(ππ*, 4.04 eV), V(ππ*, 4.11 eV), and nπ*(4.10 eV) (Table S7); (ii) molecule 2: adiabatic state order is the same as for vertical excitation (the V(ππ*) state is always the S1 state) for all methods; (iii) molecule 3: no minimum was found when optimizing the S1(V(ππ*)) state. Thus, although MRSF energies are, in general, slightly higher, the adiabatic gaps remain comparable. These comparisons demonstrate that the overall trends and barriers are consistent across methods. We therefore interpret the experimental results (discussed in the following sections) in light of the XMS-CASPT2 results.

Femtosecond spectroscopies

To evaluate the dynamical properties of molecules 1-3, femtosecond transient electronic absorption spectroscopy (fs-TEAS) measurements were undertaken in solutions of ethanol, acetonitrile, and 1,4-dioxane, and combined with time-resolved ion-yield (TR-IY) and photoelectron (TR-PE) spectroscopy experiments in vacuo. These experiments reveal a clear acceleration of excited-state decay across the series, consistent with the substitution strategy and with the progressive removal of excited-state stabilising features, as discussed below.

Photo-dynamics in solution

Collected transient electronic absorption (TEA) spectra from fs-TEAS were analysed using a global fit33 to extract kinetic lifetimes (see Fig. 3 for TEA heatmaps and kinetic traces in ethanol, Figure S3 for evolution-associated decay spectra (EADS), Figure S4 for heatmaps in acetonitrile and 1,4-dioxane and Table S1 for lifetimes from all systems). Pump wavelengths for fs-TEAS were selected from band maxima in the respective UV-visible absorption spectrum for each system (Fig. 1 and Figure S1). In each successive case, as intended, we successfully designed each photo-isomer with progressively faster excited-state deactivation dynamics. The spectral signals and trends in extracted lifetimes for the excited-state processes were similar between solutions, so the discussion of features shall focus on molecules 1-3 in ethanol.

Lifetimes extracted from global fitting of 1, following photoexcitation at 300 nm, are summarised as follows: τ1 (98 fs) is assigned to rapid motion away from the Franck-Condon region of the photoexcited state (S2 (V)); τ2 (11.16 ps) reflects vibrational cooling in the excited state; τ3 (105.9 ps) reflects trapping in the S1 (V’) (discussed below) and slow nonradiative deactivation of this population via IC to the S0 (see Fig. 2a); τ4 (>1.8 ns) captures unrecovered excited-state signal and the absorption profile of generated photoproduct E isomer, in accordance with previous assignments24. In 2 (and noting the state switching in V(ππ*) and V’(ππ*) states for 1 versus 2 and 3), τ1 is lengthened (~450 fs), and τ2 is truncated (2.91 ps), before the long-lived (τ3 > 1.8 ns) isomer photoproduct is generated. The corresponding photophysical processes involved are a combination of rapid motion away from the Franck-Condon region, solvent rearrangement, and vibrational cooling in the S1(V) (τ1), propagation through a S1/S0 CoIn (τ2) before the long-lived (τ3) isomer product is generated (see Fig. 2b for summary). Similarly, τ1 (~110 fs) in 3 reflects the combined evolution of the wavepacket across the excited PES and IC through a CoIn, before the long-lived (τ2 > 1.8 ns) isomer photoproduct is generated, as shown in Fig. 2c.

From molecule 1 to 2, a ~ 36-fold reduction in the lifetime of excited-state depopulation is thus observed, effected only by the addition of the para-methoxy. As with electronic transition energies, dynamical features are interpreted in terms of perturbations to the electronic structure (vide infra). Although 3’s shortest extracted lifetime is less than our measured IRF (see Table S1 and Figure S5), and can therefore not be reported confidently, it is clearly far shorter (~27-fold) than in 2. As mentioned above, the methylated double bond disrupts conjugation across the molecule due to steric hindrance. As evidenced by the ultrafast results, this hindrance also accelerates photo-isomerisation, in agreement with the finding by Zhao et al.25. This may be due to a reduction in barrier height afforded by a twist around the isomerisable double bond. For the moment, we will defer further discussion of this aspect and return to it when we discuss the results of our quantum chemical calculations (see “Mapping the PESs” discussion below).

Photo-dynamics in vacuo

The fundamental spectroscopic properties and photo-dynamics of 1 and 2 were investigated in vacuo using resonance-enhanced two-photon ionisation (R2PI) spectroscopy (with nanosecond lasers), while 1, 2, and 3 were investigated as well through time-resolved ion-yield (TR-IY) and photoelectron (TR-PE) spectroscopy (with femtosecond lasers). These measurements confirm that the substitution-dependent lifetimes are intrinsic to the molecular scaffold, independent of solvent. In what follows, we focus on the femtosecond experiments (Fig. 3g–l), since the nanosecond results, discussed in SI Section D, are fully consistent with, and support the conclusions of, the femtosecond experiments.

Molecule 1 was photoexcited at its electronic band origin of 318 nm, as determined from R2PI (Figure S17), while 2 and 3 were photoexcited at 311 and 287 nm, respectively, based on their peak absorption in cyclohexane (see Figure S19 for R2PI of 2 and S20 for UV-visible absorption spectra). Subsequent photophysics were tracked with a 250 or 200 nm ionising probe. To obtain a quantitative understanding, the resulting transients were fit with an exponential decay fitting model (all dynamical processes starting at a pump−probe time delay Δt = 0) consisting of two forward (Δt > 0) exponential decays for 1 and one for 2 and 3, following that employed previously by the authors27.

Starting with the TR-IY results, Fig. 3g presents the fitted TR-IY transient of 1 with a 250 nm probe, yielding two forward lifetimes of τ1 = 2.81 ± 0.58 ps and τ2 > 1 ns. Since the ultrafast gas-phase measurements photoexcite with a broad energy distribution (see Figure S17) and thus populate two close-lying ππ* excited states, the S2 (V) and S1 (V’) states (calculated at multireference methods, see “Mapping the PESs” for further discussion), the first lifetime is assigned to electronic ground state (S0) repopulation from the S2 (V) state via successive CoIns. The second lifetime parallels the long (17 ns) lifetime obtained from ns TR-R2PI spectroscopy measurements (see Figure S18) and is assigned to trapped population in the S1 (V’) state, which is populated either via initial photoexcitation or through ultrafast IC from the initially populated S2 (V) state (see Fig. 2a). In the case of 2 and 3, fitting the TR-IY transients with a 250 nm probe in Fig. 3h, i produced a single lifetime of τ1 = 180 ± 75 fs and τ1 = 40 fs, respectively. Lifetime τ1 for 3 is less than half of the IRF at this wavelength (~100 fs; see Figure S21e) and can therefore not be reported confidently, although it is evidently shorter than that seen for 1 and 2. In all cases, the TR-IY transients of 1, 2, and 3 with a 200 nm probe produced approximately equivalent dynamics, indicating that the 250 nm probe provides sufficient energy to observe all excited-state dynamics (see Figure S22).

The results of the TR-PE experiments provide further insight into the potential energy changes underlying the deactivation of these molecules in vacuo. Figure 3j displays a false colour heatmap of the TR-PE electron kinetic energy (eKE) for 1 with a 250 nm probe, highlighting a constant eKE distribution between 0 and 7,500 cm−1, as a function of the time delay. At the same time, we notice that kinetic fitting of the TR-PE transient integrated over this eKE window revealed additional photo-dynamics arising from the dimeric cluster of 1, so these specific TR-PE spectra should be considered with some caution (see Figure S23 for further details). In contrast, TR-PE spectra for 2 and 3 show significant eKE changes with time. For 2 in Fig. 3k, two eKE features are apparent at ~10,000 and ~4000 cm−1 that, under initial inspection, indicate population transfer to a nearby state. However, considering the lack of close-lying electronic states from the calculations (vide infra), it is more probable that these two features stem from ionisation from the same electronic state for which the ionisation cross-section is geometry dependent (notice that the ~4000 cm−1 feature overlaps with reverse dynamics, making it appear to start from time zero). For 3 in Fig. 3l, a single eKE feature starting at ~12,500 cm−1 quickly decays alongside a dramatic shift in eKE, suggesting ultrafast motion down a steeply inclined PES (see Fig. 2c). The TR-PE transients produced from integrating over all eKE in 2 and 3 reproduce the lifetimes extracted from the TR-IY transients in Fig. 3h, i, respectively (see Figure S24).

In short, processes identified from collected gas-phase results reflect similar deactivation routes as those in solution. IC to the ground state is faster in the gas phase than in solution, possibly due to the lack of retardation from solvent viscosity34. Furthermore, although a one-to-one comparison of dynamical lifetimes between solution and gas phase is not possible, the excited-state deactivation lifetimes follow the same trend as in solution, indicating a fundamental structure–dynamics relationship.

Mapping the PESs

Quantum chemical calculations for each system were performed to provide further insight into the experimental results. These reproduce the observed trends and show how substitution reshapes the excited-state landscape. The PESs are discussed in light of the XMS-CASPT2 results, as introduced earlier. Additional checks were done using MRSF-TDDFT.

Initially, we focus on the similarities and differences between molecules 2 and 3, which exhibit ultrafast decay facilitated by easily accessible CoIns. Twisted CoIns with dihedral angles near 90° are expected for this class of molecules. Excitation drives bond-length alternation (BLA) along the π-system (including the phenyl ring and the allyl group), elongating the central allylic C=C bond and enabling subsequent torsional motion toward the twisted CoIn35. Two limiting cases can arise from this π-bond breaking32. The first arises from heterolytic bond breaking, giving rise to a structure with zwitterionic character and consequent pyramidalisation of one of the carbon atoms, which will carry the lone pair (acquiring sp³ character)35. The second case occurs when bond cleavage is homolytic, resulting in a diradical species and, consequently, no pyramidalisation is observed36. In our simulations, we searched for both limiting cases; however, geometry optimisations invariably converged to the second case, with very modest pyramidalisation. Schematic potential energy profiles for 2 and 3 are shown in Fig. 4. In both molecules, the lowest excited state is V(ππ*).

Excited states of molecules 2 and 3.

Potential energy profiles for molecules 2 (left) and 3 (right) calculated with XMS-CASPT2. S0, S1, and S2 states are represented by black, red, and blue lines, respectively.

Molecule 3 has a twisted ground-state geometry (see Figure S8) due to significant steric hindrance between the ortho-substituents and the central methyl group, introducing a 53° distortion from planarity. This structural twist reduces conjugation, leading to a smaller oscillator strength and lower extinction coefficient. No minimum on the S1 PES was located, and optimising this state, the brightest V(ππ*), drives the population directly towards a twisted CoIn located 3.71 eV above the S0 minimum. We thus conclude that the initial twisted geometry facilitates the torsional motion required to reach the CoIn. In addition, this CoIn is slightly peaked (Figure S9), suggesting efficient population transfer away from the CoIn. Figure S9 also shows the two vectors composing the branching plane: h is associated with the torsion around the C=C bond (isomerisation), while g describes the in-plane skeletal deformations. The peaked character is reflected in P and B, dimensionless parameters introduced by Fdez. Galván et al.37 and derived from the two vectors defining the CoIn branching space: the gradient difference vector and the nonadiabatic coupling vector38. P < 1 values indicate peaked intersections, which, accompanied by B < 1 (bifurcating pathway), are associated with strong funnelling of the nuclear wavepacket toward and away from the intersection. In contrast, P > 1 values indicate a sloped intersection, while B > 1 indicates a single path. In this case, the nuclear wavepacket is not strongly driven away from the crossing, which increases the probability of V(ππ*) ← S0 up-funnelling (i.e., population transfer back to the upper state), decreasing the photoisomerisation quantum yield. Together, these two factors explain the fastest excited-state relaxation in molecule 3. For all molecules, P and B values are provided in the associated plots of the CoIn (Figures S9, S10, and S11) as well as in Table S9.

For molecule 2, however, a V planar minimum is found 0.40 eV lower in energy than the vertical energy of V(ππ*) at the XMS-CASPT2 level (Table S5); at the MRSF-TDDFT level, this difference is 0.29 eV (Table S8). This planar geometry allows for strong conjugation, stabilising the V(ππ*) state and creating a relatively flat V(ππ*) PES. The optimisation of V’(ππ*) and nπ* (S3) states delivers adiabatic energies higher than the V(ππ*) minimum. The presence of the V(ππ*) planar geometry after the initial BLA creates a small barrier that must be overcome before arriving at the twisted CoIn. The two minimal energy CoIns optimised at SA5-CASSCF are shown in Figure S10 together with selected most relevant bond lengths and angles. The two CoIns (CoIn_a and CoIn_b) were located at 3.69 eV (shown in Fig. 4 and Figure S10) and 3.82 eV (Figure S10), respectively, above the S0 minimum.

At the MRSF-TDDFT level, the lowest-energy twisted CoIn was located 3.04 eV above the S0 minimum. CoIn_a and CoIn_b differ slightly in the torsional angle (C6–C7–C8–C9), degree of pyramidalization (H12–C7–C8–H29), and the C=O bond length (C9–O11). CoIn_a, being the most stable of the two, also includes the H12 atom more in-plane compared to CoIn_b. Linear interpolation in internal coordinates (LIIC) between the V planar minimum and each one of these CoIns shows an energy barrier of 0.15 eV (CoIn_a) and 0.29 eV (CoIn_b), shown in Fig. S14. At the top of the barrier, the oscillator strength is already significantly reduced (0.317) and quickly vanishes after it. Thus, the most stable CoIn identified also leads to the smallest energy barrier, at least across the region of the potential energy landscape explored. This small energy barrier can be easily overcome due to the excess energy conferred from excitation at the centre of the absorption band, corresponding to the V(ππ*) vertical transition.

Furthermore, characterisation of the CoIn topography for 2 (Fig. S10) indicates that CoIn_a is peaked and bifurcated. This is borne out by the P and B values (provided in Fig. S10 and Table S9), which define the branching space and are both smaller than unity37. Such CoIns are more amenable to population transfer to other electronic states. They are known for mediating ultrafast decay pathways (faster than sloped intersections) and give rise to photoproducts (in this case, the cis isomer). We also evaluated the CoIn energies at the MRSF-TDDFT level, and these results are consistent with those from CASSCF calculations.

The small-amplitude, long-lived transient signal (τ > 1.8 ns) observed in fs-TEAS for 2 and 3 is attributed to the Z-isomer photoproduct (i.e., a broad red-edge feature), confirming that E → Z photoisomerisation does occur but with a low yield. In line with the branching plane analysis, the minor photoproduct signal is consistent with an overall low isomerization quantum yield. The combined analysis of the PESs and CoIn topographies for 2 and 3 supports the experimental observations in both the gas and solution phases. Both 2 and 3 present peaked minimum-energy CoIns. The steric hindrance introduced by the methyl group attached to the central C=C bond plays a key role in breaking the planarity of molecule 3, thereby preventing the formation of a minimum on the V(ππ*) PES. In contrast, the presence of a small energy barrier between the V(ππ*) planar minimum and the CoIn in molecule 2 explains the slower excited-state dynamics when compared to molecule 3, as this minimum is absent in the latter (see Fig. 2b, c).

Molecule 1 exhibits distinct behaviour in both gas- and solution-phase experiments. Starting with the analysis of vertical transitions, we observe features that differ from molecules 2 and 3. Similar to molecule 2, molecule 1 also presents a planar ground state minimum, allowing a more extended conjugation of the π-system, which is, however, impacted by the absence of the methoxy group at the para position. The primary consequence of this group’s absence is the ordering of states: the V’(ππ*) state is pushed down in energy and becomes dramatically close to the V(ππ*) state. For molecule 2, at the Franck-Condon region, the S1 (V(ππ*)) and S2 (V’(ππ*)) states are separated by 0.28 eV (0.23 eV at MRSF-TDDFT level). In contrast, in 1 they are virtually degenerate (separated by 0.06 eV at XMS-CASPT2 and 0.09 eV at MRSF-TDDFT levels, see Tables S4 and S7) and their order is inverted: S1 has V’(ππ*) character while S2 is of V(ππ*) character. This makes the V’(ππ*) state more accessible in 1 than in 2. At the S0 equilibrium geometry, the nπ* state is the S3 state at both levels of theory. V’(ππ*) has a weaker absorption, implying that optical excitation predominantly populates the V(ππ*) state.

Upon optimisation of their geometries, the three lowest excited states (V(ππ*), V’(ππ*), and nπ*) of molecule 1 yield planar geometries. Tables S4 (XMS-CASPT2) and S7 (MRSF-TDDFT) present the relative (adiabatic) energies of the optimised electronic states. At the XMS-CASPT2 level, the adiabatic energies of the V(ππ*), V’(ππ*), and nπ* states are 3.91 eV, 3.97 eV, and 3.88 eV, respectively. Thus, the energy differences between them are on the order of the method’s uncertainty. We, therefore, also computed the PES for molecules 1 and 2 using MRSF-TDDFT. This method finds adiabatic energies for molecule 1 as 4.11 eV (V(ππ*) state), 4.04 eV (V’(ππ*) state), and 4.10 eV (nπ* state). Although the state ordering differs from that of XMS-CASPT2, this confirms that the three states are energetically very close (Fig. S12). Their geometries are also similar, differing only in the BLA pattern (Fig. S11).

With these results in hand, and considering the experimental results, we have identified two potential relaxation mechanisms of molecule 1 (see Fig. 2a). Both rely on the fact that an initial wavepacket is generated in the V(ππ*) state, since the transition to this state has the highest oscillator strength. The most likely mechanism involves bifurcation of this V(ππ*) population, some of which relaxes ballistically to the S0 (on a few ps timescale, τ1 in the gas-phase) through successive CoIns, and the remaining population propagates through an S2/S1 CoIn populating the V’(ππ*) state. Population trapping in the minimum of this state occurs before decay via an IC to the S0 state (associated with the ~100 ps lifetime observed in solution and ns lifetime observed in the gas-phase). Facile IC between the V and V’ states is the first step leading to the latter ‘slow’ decay pathway, and supported by the very small V(ππ*)-V’(ππ*) energy gap ( < 0.1 eV), oscillator strength redistribution between the two ππ* states, and character-swapping with geometry, which are typical signatures of strong vibronic coupling and Duschinsky mixing39 that drive nonadiabatic population transfer. The swapping of states is a common feature of a strong crossing and state mixing (adiabatic mixing), which ensures highly efficient population transfer. As the CoIns located are downhill, there is ample excess energy for the population to access them, in agreement with the fs-ps experimental decay.

Once on the V’(ππ*) PES, the molecule relaxes to a V’(ππ*) minimum energy geometry and can become trapped in this minimum before evolving along torsional modes that lead to the S0 CoIns. We computed the LIIC pathways connecting the V’(ππ*) minimum to both CoIns (at XMS-CASPT2 level). The path connecting this state to CoIn_a yields a 0.11 eV barrier, while the pathway connecting to CoIn_b yields 0.18 eV (see Fig. S13). The LIIC pathway also shows a strong mixing between V(ππ*) and V’(ππ*) states before the oscillator strength starts to vanish while the torsional angle increases.

Another possible, but disfavoured, deactivation mechanism is discussed in SI Section C (under “Alternative relaxation mechanism involving triplet states”). This pathway involves populating the nπ* state, followed by intersystem crossing (ISC), but can be ruled out based on our ns REMPI experiments. The combined experimental and computational results thus indicate that molecule 1 decays via bifurcation of the wavepacket after crossing the V(ππ*)/V’(ππ*) CoIn. Part of the population quickly reaches the V(ππ*)/S0 intersection (undergoing a ballistic deactivation), whereas the remaining population decays slowly (on a ps time scale) after being temporarily trapped in a shallow V’(ππ*) potential well (see Fig. 2a).

Conclusions

Through a combined experimental and computational strategy, we have shown that the molecular structure, exemplified by cinnamates 1, 2, and 3, can be precisely tuned to control their photochemical behaviour. By incorporating electron-donating groups on the phenyl ring, we modulate the electron density across the isomerisable double bond, while steric effects guide the system toward a conical intersection. These modifications enable controlled repopulation of the ground electronic state.

Molecule 1 exhibits a complex relaxation behaviour: near-degenerate V(ππ*) and V’(ππ*) states and strong state-mixing manifest in the bifurcation of the excited-state population, with both ultrafast and slower decay channels. Such slower decay channels are clearly detrimental to the overall performance of these systems. We have shown that the underlying process of population trapping can be countered by para-methoxy substitution, which separates the V(ππ*) and V’(ππ*) states and facilitates access to the V(ππ*)/S0 conical intersection. In molecule 2, we thus do not observe contributions from slower decay channels, but only ultrafast excited-state relaxation to the ground state. Calculations show, nevertheless, that ballistic motion of the wavepacket on the V(ππ*) potential energy surface is still impeded by the fact that this potential energy surface features a stable minimum leading to a barrier that must be overcome to access the V(ππ*)/S0 conical intersection with the ground state. This potential energy minimum has been removed by introducing steric hindrance on the C=C bond in molecule 3. As a result, the molecule becomes pre-twisted, lacks a stabilising excited-state minimum, and can undergo ballistic motion towards its V(ππ*)/S0 conical intersection on an ultrafast timescale, on par with cis-11-retinal.

Together, these results show how targeted substitution on a simple isomerisable backbone can systematically tune internal conversion rates from the nano/picosecond to the femtosecond regime. This mechanistic insight provides a basis for designing systems for which efficient internal conversion is crucial, such as photon converters, UV filters, and photo-switches.

Methods

Experimental Methods

UV-visible absorption spectra and emission

Steady-state optical experiments were undertaken with solutions in a 1 cm quartz cuvette. For emission measurements, each solution’s absorption was made to ~0.1. All solutions were wrapped in foil to exclude ambient light and maintained at 21 ± 1 °C between experiments.

Femtosecond spectroscopies

Details regarding the femtosecond transient electronic absorption spectroscopy (fs-TEAS), gas-phase time-resolved ion-yield (TR-IY) and time-resolved photoelectron (TR-PE) spectroscopy experiments’ setups are described in detail elsewhere27,40–42, but a summary is provided herein.

Starting with the fs-TEAS setup, a 1 mM solution of each solute-solvent system was circulated using a flow-through cell (Demountable Liquid Cell, Harrick Scientific Products Inc., Pleasantville, NY, USA) between two CaF2 windows (diameters of 1 mm and 2 mm for the front and back, respectively) spaced by 100 µm, defining the sample path length. A fundamental 3 W, 800 nm laser beam with a pulse repetition rate of 1 kHz and a pulse width of ~40 fs was generated by chirped pulse regenerative amplification (Spitfire XP, Spectra-Physics) from a Ti:sapphire oscillator seed (Tsunami, Spectra-Physics). This pulse train was split into three beams, two of which were utilised in the fs-TEAS experiment and the remaining was used in the gas-phase experiments (see below). One 1 W beam produced pump (photoexcitation) pulses via the use of an optical parametric amplifier (TOPAS-C, Light Conversion), which was subsequently reduced to a pulse repetition rate of 0.5 kHz using a chopper that blocked every other pulse. The second 1 W beam was divided into two beams of 0.95 W and 0.05 W, and the former discarded. The latter was focused into a translating CaF2 window to produce a ~ 310–740 nm white light supercontinuum probe pulse, kept at 54.7° (‘magic angle’) to quash artefacts from rotational dynamics. Pump-probe time delay (Δt) was varied by reflecting the probe beam off a gold retroreflector mounted onto a motorised delay stage. Both beams overlapped in the solution in the transparent flow-through cell, and the probe pulse was subsequently collimated and focused into a fibre-coupled spectrometer (AvaSpec-ULS1650F, Avantes). Spectral changes in optical density (OD) were calculated from ‘pump on’ (Iλpumped) and ‘pump off’ (Iλ0) intensities, by:

fs-TEAS scans at each time delay were repeated a minimum of 10 times for each system.

For the gas-phase TR-IY and TR-PE setups, the pump pulse was generated via the same optical parametric amplifier used in the fs-TEAS experiment. The remaining 1 W of the fundamental beam was used to generate the probe pulse, either through pumping a second optical parametric amplifier to generate 250 nm or through successive frequency conversion using a series of type I, type II, and type I β-barium borate crystals to produce 200 nm. The pump pulse was delayed with respect to the probe pulse using a hollow UV-enhanced aluminium retroreflector mounted on a motorised delay stage. The polarisation of the pump and probe were parallel with respect to one another and in the plane of the detectors. Laser powers were set to ensure single photon dynamics. The pump and probe were focused and overlapped into a cold molecular beam produced by seeding molecules 1, 2, or 3 heated to 160, 180, or 190 °C, respectively, into 1.5 bar He gas. The gaseous mixtures were expanded into vacuum via an Even-Lavie solenoid valve43,44 and were subsequently passed through a 2 mm diameter skimmer.

Computational Methods

Ground and excited-state geometries for 1, 2, and 3 were first optimised using density functional theory (DFT) and time-dependent DFT (TDDFT) (ωB97XD/cc-pVDZ), respectively. Vertical absorption and emission energies were computed with the aug-cc-pVDZ. Frequency calculations confirmed that the obtained geometries correspond to an actual minimum on the potential energy surface (PES). Starting from this S0 minimum calculated at the DFT level, we performed multiconfigurational calculations to construct potential energy profiles.

The charge-transfer (CT) analysis of the lowest electronic transitions was evaluated by computing the q(CT) number and natural transition orbitals (NTOs), at the ωB97XD/aug-cc-pVDZ level using the TheoDORE program45. q(CT) numbers range from 0 to 1, where values closer to 1 imply a state with greater CT character, while values closer to zero imply a localised excitation.

Optimisations of stationary points for 1, 2, and 3 were carried out using complete active space self-consistent field (CASSCF) level with the ANO-S-VDZP basis set. Energies were corrected using extended multi-state complete active space second-order perturbation theory (XMS-CASPT2) and the ANO-S-VDZP basis set46. For the three molecules, the active space consisted of 12 electrons in 11 orbitals consisting of 5π and 5π* orbitals and a nO from the carbonyl group. State averaging was performed over five states. CoIns were optimised at the same level of theory. IPEA shift of 0.00 and imaginary level shift of 0.1 a.u were used for XMS-CASPT2 calculations. Linear interpolation in internal coordinates (LIIC) between V(ππ*) minima and CoIns was performed at the XMS-CASPT2/ANO-S-VDZP level. All multiconfigurational calculations were carried out using the OpenMolcas program47.

Mixed-reference spin-flip TDDFT (MRSF-TDDFT) calculations were performed using the OpenQP v1.0 package48. The CAM-B3LYP functional49 was used together with the cc-pVDZ basis set. Ground and excited-state minima and minimum-energy CoIns were optimised at this level of theory.
