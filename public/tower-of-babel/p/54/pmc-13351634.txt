Long-term real-world outcomes following preoperative radiotherapy and conservative surgery for trunk and extremity soft tissue sarcomas: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Reinhardt Krcek, Nattinee Wattakiyanon, Anthony M. Griffin, Zhihui Amy Liu, Charles N. Catton, Siyer Roohani, David B. Shultz, Philip Wong, David G. Kirsch, Hiba M. Othman, Peter C. Ferguson, Kim Tsoi, Jay S. Wunder, Peter W.M. Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundRadiotherapy (RT) combined with surgery is standard of care for most soft tissue sarcoma (STS) of the extremity and trunk. Preoperative RT is often preferred because it enables a lower radiation dose and is associated with more favorable late toxicity profiles. We report real-world, long-term oncological, toxicity, and functional outcomes of patients treated with preoperative RT at a sarcoma reference center.MethodsWe did a retrospective analysis of data from our prospectively maintained database (data collected from April 1989 to November 2020) from a single sarcoma center in Canada. We included patients with histologically confirmed STS of extremity or trunk. Eligible patients received preoperative RT with either 2D/3D-Conformal Radiation Therapy (3D-CRT) or Intensity Modulated Radiation Therapy (IMRT), followed by surgery 4–6 weeks later. Local and oncological outcomes were assessed. Long-term toxicities were graded using the RTOG late toxicity scoring criteria. The Musculoskeletal Tumor Society Rating Scale (MSTS) and Toronto Extremity Salvage Score (TESS) evaluated functional outcomes. Further study endpoints included the occurrence of distant metastases (DM) and overall survival (OS), stratified by histology.FindingsAmong 1027 patients (370 3D-CRT, 657 IMRT), the median follow-up was 5.9 years (range: 0.5–24.6 years). Major wound complications occurred in 28% with 3D-CRT and in 21% with IMRT (p = 0.09). The 5-year cumulative rates of local recurrence (LR) and distant metastasis were 5.2% vs. 5.4% and 25.5% vs. 25.6%, with a 5-year overall survival of 79.3%. None of these outcomes differed significantly between 3D-CRT and IMRT. Grade ≥2 late toxicity occurred in 25% (30% in 3D-CRT and 22% in IMRT), including fibrosis (17%), skin toxicity (9%), joint stiffness (6%), and edema (9%). IMRT was associated with significantly lower rates of grade ≥2 skin toxicity (OR 0.28), subcutaneous fibrosis (OR 0.55), and joint stiffness (OR 0.49) compared to 3D-CRT. Functional outcomes were comparable between IMRT and 3D-CRT. Patients with grade ≥2 toxicities had lower functional scores (p < 0.001).InterpretationIn this large cohort with mature follow-up, long-term local control and function were excellent with low rates of high-grade toxicity. Modern RT techniques were associated with less late toxicity without compromising local control, but acute wound complications and distant metastases remain ongoing challenges.FundingNone.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Perioperative Radiotherapy (RT) has become a cornerstone in the modern management of Soft Tissue Sarcomas (STS). We searched PubMed until December 2025 using the search terms “soft tissue sarcoma”, “STS”, “preoperative radiotherapy”, “preoperative RT”, “perioperative radiotherapy”, “3D-CRT”, “IMRT”, “long-term toxicity”, “wound complications”, and “functional outcomes”. Only English articles were analyzed. Studies limited to only pediatric populations were excluded. A few prospective trials have confirmed the role of RT for STS, and after the SR-2 trial by O'Sullivan et al., in 2002, preoperative RT has been considered the recommended approach due to similar oncological outcomes compared with postoperative RT and fewer long-term side effects. However, this prospective trial and the other available trials are limited by relatively small sample sizes, heterogeneous RT techniques, and relatively short follow-up. Given the rarity of STS, direct comparative studies of different RT techniques are difficult to conduct, and robust long-term data evaluating toxicity, functional outcomes, and the impact of modern RT techniques such as Intensity Modulated Radiation Therapy (IMRT) or Volumetric Modulated Arc Therapy (VMAT) remain scarce.

Added value of this study

This study represents the largest reported single-center cohort of STS patients treated exclusively with conventionally fractionated preoperative RT followed by surgery, with more than 1000 patients captured over three decades of real-world experience. The prospectively maintained database and long follow-up provide a uniquely comprehensive assessment of oncological outcomes, late toxicities, and functional results. The study also offers detailed comparative data between conventional 2D/3D-Conformal RT (summarized as 3D-CRT) and modern VMAT/IMRT (summarized as IMRT), demonstrating similar rates of local control while showing lower rates of late side effects with IMRT. Moreover, this study adds information about long-term oncological outcomes including distant metastases and survival. This maturity of follow-up adds substantial value to the evidence base regarding preoperative RT for extremity and trunk STS.

Implications of all the available evidence

Existing evidence and our findings confirm that preoperative RT, particularly with the use of IMRT, followed by limb-sparing surgery provides excellent long-term local control and long-term functional outcomes for extremity and trunk STS. The results from this large cohort reinforce the robustness of this practice while highlighting acute wound complications and distant metastases as persistent clinical challenges. The observed reduction in selected late toxicities with IMRT supports wider adoption of modern RT techniques. While this study shows possible benefits of modern RT techniques with excellent local outcomes, further research should focus on improving other oncological outcomes, especially reducing the risk of distant failure.

Introduction

The combination of radiotherapy (RT) and limb-sparing surgery has become the cornerstone in the modern management of soft tissue sarcomas (STS) of the extremities and trunk. With this combined treatment, local control (LC) rates of about 90% are achievable in the primary setting.1,2

While the initial trial by Rosenberg utilized postoperative RT following limb-sparing surgery,3 evidence supports the use of preoperative RT for STS and is preferred over postoperative RT.4 Although it increases the risk of perioperative major wound complications (MWC), preoperative RT shows similar LC rates1 and typically requires smaller treatment volumes with lower radiation doses, which may lead to fewer long-term toxicities associated with better functional outcome.5,6 These late radiation toxicities, including fibrosis, joint stiffness, or edema together with surgical soft tissue loss typically affect the physical ability of STS patients.6

Image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)-based planning have further refined RT for STS. In Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) 0630, preoperative RT delivered with MRI-based target definition, reduced target volumes, and daily image guidance was associated with a significant reduction in long-term toxicity, with relevant late toxicity (RTOG grade ≥2) decreasing from 37% in historical controls to 10.5% at two years,2 with a 2 year local failure free survival rate of 94% and 65% distant failure free survival.

While IGRT with its reduced margins provides toxicity reduction, the outcomes for LC or overall survival (OS) are largely determined by tumor size, grade, depth and surgical margins.7, 8, 9 Although real-world long-term outcome data for extremity and truncal STS treated with surgery and RT have been reported previously for conventionally fractionated RT,10 large datasets focused specifically on preoperative RT with accompanying functional outcome data remain comparatively limited. On the other hand, data on hypofractionated preoperative RT are emerging for STS,11 with very good LC rates >90% and low toxicity with a median follow-up of 43 months12 - however, the published studies to date lack comparative data.13

This study analyzes the long-term outcomes of conventionally fractionated preoperative RT followed by limb-sparing surgery in patients with STS treated at a single reference sarcoma center between 1989 and 2020, with a focus on MWC, local recurrence (LR), treatment-related toxicities, functional outcomes, and overall oncological outcomes.

Methods

Study design and participants

We retrospectively reviewed data from a prospectively maintained STS database from our joint sarcoma center in Canada. Eligible patients were those with non-metastatic extremity or truncal STS treated at our institution with curative intent using conventionally fractionated preoperative RT followed by surgery. Patients were excluded if they had metastatic disease at presentation, did not receive conventionally fractionated preoperative RT (1.8–2 Gy per fraction), had preoperative chemotherapy, were treated with palliative intent, or did not undergo definitive surgical resection. This study included all eligible patients treated during the study period. Because of the retrospective design of the analysis, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed.

Clinical and treatment variables were extracted from the database, including age at diagnosis, gender, disease location, tumor size at the diagnosis, tumor depth, tumor grade, histology, MWC, RT doses and RT techniques. Histologic diagnoses were abstracted from the original pathology reports and classified according to the diagnostic terminology in use at the time of diagnosis. At time of original diagnosis, previous and current institutional policy has been to have pathology review by central sarcoma pathologists. No pathology re-review or retrospective reclassification according to current World Health Organization (WHO) criteria was performed for this study.

Management of STS

At our center, STS of the extremities and trunk were managed with a limb-preserving approach (where applicable) using surgery and pre- or postoperative external beam radiotherapy (EBRT) wherever feasible. Institutional policy was that all cases are jointly assessed by sarcoma surgeons and radiation oncologists and reviewed at a weekly multidisciplinary cancer conference (MCC) to guide management. Preoperative RT was generally the preferred approach if RT was indicated. Chemotherapy was generally not recommended for most histologies at our center, and practice is based on the data from the EORTC 62931 trial.14 Patients underwent regular follow-up with imaging, and any concerns were re-discussed at the MCC. Clinical and functional data were entered in the prospectively maintained database at every follow-up where available.

Radiotherapy planning

All eligible patients for this study received preoperative, conventionally fractionated RT with a total dose of 45–52 Gy over a period of 5–6 weeks, delivered in 1.8–2.0 Gy fractions. Treatment was administered using either 2D/3D conformal radiotherapy (summarized as 3D-CRT) or Intensity modulated Radiation Therapy/Volumetric Modulated Arc Therapy (summarized as IMRT), typically followed by surgery 4–6 weeks later.

All patients in the IMRT group were planned using a simulation computed tomography (CT), and a simulation CT was used for all 3D cases.

Planning was supported by MRI particularly in the IMRT cohort. The Clinical Target Volume (CTV) as per institutional standard of practice for both techniques extended 4 cm proximally and distally from the tissue at risk of subclinical spread-defined by the gross tumor volume (GTV) contoured on MRI if available- and 1.5 cm radially, limited by anatomic barriers to tumor spread, such as bone and fascia. Peritumoral edema, if visible, was included in the CTV. A planning target volume (PTV) expansion of 5 mm was used for IMRT treatments. In the 3D-CRT cohort, the PTV margin was 1.5 cm longitudinally and 1 cm in all other directions, in accordance with historical institutional practice.

Data analysis

This study focussed on MWC within 120 days as defined by the SR-2 trial,1 LR, functional outcomes, and treatment-related toxicity, stratified by treatment technology. Further study endpoints included the occurrence of distant metastases (DM) and overall survival (OS), stratified by histology.

The toxicities were assessed using the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC)/RTOG late toxicity scoring criteria encompassing late skin toxicity, subcutaneous fibrosis and joint stiffness.15 Edema was measured according to Stern's criteria.16

Assessment of functional outcome

The long-term functional assessments were evaluated using the Musculoskeletal Tumor Society Rating Scale 87 and 93 (MSTS)17 and the Toronto Extremity Salvage Score (TESS).18 The MSTS is a clinician-rated tool designed to assess functional outcome. MSTS 93 includes six domains that evaluate the factors pain, function, emotional acceptance, and three extremity specific measures. The MSTS 93 scored each on a scale from 0 to 5. The total score can range from 0 to 30 and is converted to a point scale from 0 to 100%. MSTS 87 includes seven categories, each worth 5 points, for a maximum total of 35 points. The TESS is a patient-reported measure of physical function, reflecting the patients' experiences in daily living activities, including self-care, mobility, and role functions. The TESS score ranges from 0 to 100. For both measures, a higher score indicates better functional ability. In this study, the last available values during the follow-up after surgery were reported.

Statistical analysis

Patients' baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were presented as mean and standard deviation, or median and range, while categorical variables were summarized with frequency and percentage. Comparisons of baseline characteristics between RT technique groups were descriptive.

Overall survival (OS, event death) was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared between groups using the log-rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazard models were employed to evaluate patient, disease, and treatment factors associations with OS.

Time to LR (event local failure) and distant metastasis (DM, event diagnosis of distant metastases) were analyzed using the cumulative incidence function, with death as a competing risk. Univariate comparisons between groups were performed using Gray's test. Multivariable cause-specific Cox proportional hazard models were used to assess the associations of patient, disease, and treatment factors with LR and DM. To compare different histologies, the seven most common histologies in our cohort-myxoid liposarcoma, myxofibrosarcoma, other liposarcomas, undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (UPS), malignant fibrous histiocytoma (MFH), leiomyosarcomas, and malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNST)- were compared with the other types of STS in our cohort. Major wound complications (MWC) and late toxicity outcomes were analyzed as binary endpoints using logistic regression, and results are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals.

Functional outcomes, measured by the TESS and MSTS87 and 93, were analyzed using multivariable linear regression models. Clinically relevant covariates were selected a priori for all multivariable analyses and included age, sex, tumor size, grade, depth, disease location, margin status, prior surgery, and histology, as appropriate.

Multivariable analyses were performed using a complete-case approach, and missing grade was excluded. For toxicity and functional outcomes, denominators reflect the number of patients with available assessments. Given the heterogeneous mechanisms and timing of missing data, formal imputation was not performed. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied, and as such, results should be interpreted with caution. R version 4.4.3 was used for all statistical analyses.

Ethics statement

The Research Ethics Board at University Health Network approved the study (REB# 18–5689). A waiver of consent was provided for this retrospective study.

Role of the funding source

There was no funding source for this study.

Results

Patient and tumor characteristics

Between April 1989 and November 2020, we identified 1152 patients with a histologically confirmed diagnosis of STS, and who received preoperative RT for their disease. We excluded patients who were initially diagnosed as metastatic (n = 44), were treated with preoperative chemotherapy (n = 44), treated for radiation-associated sarcoma, or patients who were treated with different RT schemes, mainly hypofractionation during the COVID-19 pandemic (n = 6 of 8). The most common histologies were myxoid liposarcoma (n = 146, 14%), myxofibrosarcoma (n = 134, 13%), other liposarcomas (n = 109, 11%, details provided in Supplement S1), undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (UPS, n = 106, 10%) and malignant fibrous histiocytoma (MFH, n = 104, 10%). While most of the patients had planned primary sarcoma surgery at our center (n = 791, 77%), the remaining 231 (23%) patients underwent prior unplanned surgery elsewhere. Thirty patients were treated for a local relapse, that was previously managed with surgery alone. Missing data for tumor size (n = 14, 1.4%) and grade (n = 45, 4.4%) were primarily attributable to previous surgery being performed at another center, where pathology reports were either unavailable or lacked documentation of these variables, and for histologies with no reportable grading.

Of the total, 370 (36%) received preoperative 3D-CRT and 657 (64%) received preoperative IMRT. In most cases, RT dose was 50 Gy in 25 fractions (n = 1001, 97.4%). Twelve patients received an additional postoperative boost after preoperative RT (n = 12, 1.2%), all treated with 3D-CRT (all RT schemes in Supplement S3).

The median follow-up after surgery was 5.9 years; 8.7 years (range 0.6–24.6 years) for 3D-CRT and 5 years (range: 0.5–16.1 years) for the IMRT cohort. In total, follow-up data were available for 594 patients (57.8%) at 5 years and for 219 patients (21.3%) at 10 years.

Major wound complications

A total of 236 MWC (24%, 236/998 documented) were observed following surgery, 21% in the IMRT group and 28% in the 3D-CRT group (p = 0.09). On MVA (Table 2), factors significantly associated with MWC included age (OR: 1.01 per year, p = 0.013), lower extremity location (OR: 3.44 vs. upper extremity, p < 0.001), trunk location (OR: 2.28 vs. upper extremity, p = 0.018), and tumor size per 5 cm increase (p = 0.007). No statistically significant associations were found for gender, grade, depth, margin status, histologies, or RT technique (all p > 0.05). In a subsite analysis restricted to lower-extremity tumors, adductor compartment involvement was not independently associated with MWC in either unadjusted (OR 0.82, 95% CI: 0.51–1.36, p = 0.44) or MVA (OR 0.91, 95% CI 0.55–1.54, p = 0.71). Local recurrences

In total, 58 LR (6%) were observed during follow-up. The 5 year LR rate was 5.2% (95% CI: 3.2–7.8%) for 3D-CRT and 5.4% (95% CI: 3.7–7.5%) for IMRT, with no statistically significant difference between both groups (Fig. 2A, p = 0.965, Gray's test). Factors which are associated with LR on MVA (Supplement S5) were grade 2 and grade 3 STS (HR = 5.07 and 5.31, p = 0.03 and 0.027) and positive margins (HR = 7.49, p < 0.001). Distant failure

In total, 288 patients (28%) experienced distant metastases during follow-up. At 5 years, the cumulative incidence of distant metastases was 25.5% (Supplement S6, 95% CI: 22.8–28.3%), with no significant difference between the RT techniques (p = 0.764). On MVA (Supplement S7), tumor size per 5 cm increase (HR 1.4, p < 0.001) and higher tumor grade (grade 2: HR 3.19; grade 3: HR 5.52; both p < 0.001) were independently associated with an increased risk of distant failure, and there was no difference between the RT technique (HR 0.93, p = 0.59). Among histological subtypes, leiomyosarcoma (LMS) showed a higher risk of developing DM (HR 1.62 compared to other sarcomas, p = 0.03). In contrast, myxoid Liposarcomas (HR 0.58, p = 0.018) and other Liposarcomas (HR: 0.32, p < 0.001) were associated with a lower risk of distant failure. These findings were confirmed in a sensitivity analysis using dichotomized tumor size (HR 2.46 for >5 cm, p < 0.001). In total 33 patients developed lymph node metastases during follow-up (3.2% of the cohort).

Overall survival

In total, 283 patients died during follow-up. The estimated 5-year OS was 79.3% (95% CI: 76.8–81.9%), with no significant difference between RT techniques (p = 0.178). On MVA, factors independently associated with worse OS included higher age (HR: 1.03 per year, p < 0.001), tumor size per 5 cm increase (HR: 1.40, p < 0.001), and grade 3 tumors (HR: 2.54, p < 0.001). Histology had an influence on OS (p < 0.001), Supplement S8. On MVA, among histological subtypes, other liposarcomas had the lowest HR of death (0.38, p < 0.001), Supplement S9. These findings were confirmed in a sensitivity analysis using dichotomized tumor size (HR 2.43 for >5 cm, p < 0.001). Of the 18 patients excluded for less than 6 months of follow-up, 11 died during this interval.

Late toxicity (RTOG grade ≥2) after preoperative radiotherapy.

A total of 187 patients experienced at least one RTOG grade ≥2 toxicity involving the skin, subcutaneous tissue, joints and edema- 78 patients (30%) in the 3D-CRT group and 109 patients (22%) in the IMRT group. Bone fractures occurred in 17 patients. Severe other toxicities (RTOG grade ≥3) were observed in 32 patients: 14 (5%) in the 3D-CRT group and 18 (4%) in the IMRT group.

On MVA for any grade ≥2 late toxicity, IMRT was associated with significantly lower odds compared to 3D-CRT (OR 0.61, 95% CI: 0.39–0.93, p = 0.023). Larger tumor size (OR 1.30 per 5 cm increase, 95% CI: 1.07–1.58, p = 0.007) and lower extremity location (OR 1.73 vs. upper extremity, 95% CI: 1.08–2.84, p = 0.025) were independently associated with increased toxicity risk. Myxoid liposarcoma histology was associated with significantly lower odds of grade ≥2 toxicity (OR 0.42, 95% CI: 0.21–0.79, p = 0.009), Supplement S10a.

IMRT was independently associated with significantly lower rates of grade ≥2 skin toxicity (OR 0.28, 95% CI: 0.14–0.53, p < 0.001), subcutaneous fibrosis (OR 0.55, 95% CI: 0.34–0.89, p = 0.015), and joint stiffness (OR 0.49, 95% CI: 0.25–0.96, p = 0.037) compared to 3D-CRT. For edema, no significant difference between techniques was observed (OR 1.31, p = 0.46). Larger tumor size was independently associated with higher rates of edema (OR 1.66 per 5 cm increase, 95% CI: 1.28–2.17, p < 0.001) and lower extremity location with higher rates of edema (OR 3.32 vs. upper extremity, 95% CI: 1.34–10.08, p = 0.018). Myxoid liposarcoma histology was associated with lower odds of subcutaneous fibrosis (OR 0.45, 95% CI: 0.20–0.95, p = 0.044). No other variables reached significance across these endpoints (Supplement S10b–e).

Functional outcome data were available for 671 patients (65.3%). No significant difference in TESS and MSTS scores was observed between 3D-CRT and IMRT, with mean TESS scores of 85.6 vs. 85.0 (p = 0.35), mean MSTS87 scores of 31.2 vs. 31.3 (p = 0.62) and MSTS93 scores of 88.4 vs. 89.6 (p = 0.92), Supplement S11a. On MVA, IMRT and 3D-CRT were associated with comparable functional outcomes across all three measures (TESS, MSTS87, MSTS93; all p > 0.05). Older age (all p ≤ 0.009) and larger tumor size (per 5 cm increase; all p ≤ 0.034) were independently associated with worse functional scores, whereas male sex was associated with better scores (all p ≤ 0.003). Lower extremity location predicted worse TESS (p < 0.001) and MSTS93 scores (p = 0.026), while superficial tumor depth was associated with better MSTS87 (p = 0.014) and MSTS93 scores (p = 0.017). Grade 3 disease was associated with worse MSTS87 scores (p = 0.043), and myxoid liposarcoma histology was associated with better MSTS87 scores (p = 0.028). Prior unplanned surgery, margin status, and other histologies were not consistently associated with functional outcomes. Detailed results of the MVA are shown in Supplement S11b–d.

Patients with late toxicity of RTOG grade ≥2 or edema according to Stern ≥2 had significantly lower functional scores than those with grade 0 or 1 toxicity (p < 0.001 for skin, subcutaneous, joint and edema). The statistical association also applied for fractures (TESS p = 0.019, MSTS87 p = 0.002 and MSTS93 p = 0.003).

Long term follow-up

A total of 219 patients had at least 10 years follow-up. Most of them were treated with 3D-CRT (n = 135) and the rest with IMRT (n = 84). The cohort comprised mostly male patients (58%), with deep-seated (80%), high-grade (47% G3), and lower extremity-located (66%) tumors. Median follow-up was 11.1 years, Supplement S12. The 15-year cumulative incidence of LR was 6.2% (95% CI: 2.0–10.5) for 3D-CRT and 8.3% (95% CI: 2.4–14.3) for IMRT, with no significant difference between techniques (Gray's test, p = 0.45, Supplement S13).

On MVA, LR likewise did not differ between 3D-CRT and IMRT (HR 1.60, p = 0.43), with positive surgical margin as the only independent predictor of LR (HR 9.91, p < 0.001).

Functional outcomes were likewise similar between techniques (Supplement S14a–c). On multivariable analysis, lower extremity location was associated with worse TESS scores (p = 0.025), while superficial tumor depth predicted better MSTS87 (p = 0.026) and MSTS93 (p = 0.006) scores. Older age independently correlated with worse MSTS87 and MSTS93, and grade 3 histology with worse MSTS87 (p = 0.019). With respect to late toxicity, no significant difference between 3D-CRT and IMRT was identified for any toxicity endpoint (Supplement S15a–e). However, for the skin toxicity endpoint specifically, larger tumor size was independently associated with grade ≥2 skin toxicity (OR 2.02 per 5 cm increase, p = 0.027, Supplement S15b).

Within this cohort, the 15-year DM rate was 16.1% for 3D-CRT and 20.2% for IMRT (p = 0.26), and the 15-year OS was 85.7% and 90.4%, respectively (p = 0.73).

Adjustment for treatment era

To address potential temporal heterogeneity over the inclusion period, we incorporated calendar era into an MVA, defining Era 1 (pre-IMRT) as 1989 to February 2005, Era 2 (transition/overlap era) as March 2005 to March 2007, and Era 3 (IMRT era) as April 2007 to 2020. In the adjusted analyses, era was not independently associated with LR, DM, OS, MWC, edema, joint stiffness, or functional outcomes. Later eras were associated with significantly lower rates of late toxicity, including any grade ≥2 toxicity in Era 3 versus Era 1 (OR 0.56, 95% CI: 0.35–0.90, p = 0.015), as well as lower skin and subcutaneous toxicity in Eras 2 and 3.

Overlap era IMRT/3D-CRT

In the overlap era sensitivity analysis (March 2005–March 2007), comprising 109 patients treated contemporaneously with either 3D-CRT (n = 58) or IMRT (n = 51), no significant differences between techniques were observed across any endpoint in multivariable analysis, including late toxicity, functional outcomes, LR (HR 1.17, p = 0.84), DM (HR 0.80, p = 0.57), OS (HR 0.58, p = 0.14), or MWC (OR 0.98, p = 0.96).

Discussion

This large single-center cohort study examined real-world outcomes of preoperative RT followed by surgery for STS of the extremities and trunk. Our data demonstrate that preoperative, conventionally fractionated RT provides excellent LC rates, functional and toxicity outcomes, and that acceptable rates of MWC after surgery may be achieved.

These results are particularly relevant in the context of the emerging trend toward individualized oncology care and hypofractionation in Radiation Oncology. Comparative phase 2 data for hypofractionation in STS are still pending, and our data may provide a robust benchmark for evaluating alternative fractionation strategies in the future.

We highlight that MWCs remain one of the biggest challenges when combining preoperative RT with surgery. In our cohort, the incidence rate of MWCs was substantial at 21% in the IMRT group, but lower than those reported in the RTOG-0630 trial, the phase 2 IMRT trial from the Princess Margaret Cancer Centre, and the preoperative arms of the SR-2 and IMRiS trials.1,2,19,20 Moreover, risk factors in our cohort differed. While lower extremity location is a well-established risk factor, besides smoking and adiposity,21 the associations with higher age and trunk location were not specifically reported in the previous studies, as well as increased tumor size. These discrepancies may be attributable to the larger sample size (including n = 315 patients with an STS >10 cm) which may have increased statistical power compared with the other cohorts, as well as to further advances in complex reconstruction techniques, such as the use of musculo-cutaneous flaps.

Besides MWC, another important aspect of the local outcome is LC, as previous literature showed poor outcomes for locally relapsing STS.22 Our long-term results, with a substantial proportion of patients having 5-year (n = 594, 57.8%) and 10-year follow-up (n = 219, 21.3%), show a 5-year LC rate of almost 95%. This is in line with the previously discussed randomized trials and a large retrospective analysis by Lansu21 et al. with almost 200 patients, with a shorter follow-up time. Our data also confirms the established LR risk factor of positive margins. Furthermore, higher tumor grade was associated with worse LC. Although prior studies have reported mixed results regarding the association between tumor grade and local control in STS,23,24 higher grade was associated with worse LC in our large, homogeneous cohort, adding further data to this ongoing debate.

Importantly, we did not observe a difference in LC between patients treated with 3D-CRT and the more conformal IMRT, suggesting that the general concerns regarding the risk of ‘target misses’ with highly conformal techniques such as IMRT25 do not apply to STS. Moreover, our data suggest there may be differences in LC across histologies, as shown in a previously published large, multi-institutional pooled database.26 However, we were not able to differentiate between individual histologies in our MVA, likely due to the very low number of LR and the large number of histologies in our cohort. On the other hand, our study has a relatively high proportion of myxoid liposarcoma (14%). Given the known radiosensitivity of this subtype and the favorable prospective results of dose de-escalated preoperative RT with 36 Gy in the DOREMY study,27 this histologic distribution may have contributed to the high local control observed in the present series, although its impact is likely limited given that this subtype represents a minority of the cohort.

The addition of RT to STS surgery aims to facilitate high rates of LC while enabling tissue conservation and ultimately limb preservation in the extremity3 but toxicity and functional outcome are vital considerations in overall management. It has been previously shown that preoperative RT has advantages compared to postoperative RT for such outcomes.6 In line with previous findings for preoperative RT,2 our study revealed that IMRT decreased long-term skin toxicity, subcutaneous fibrosis and joint toxicity in comparison to 3D-CRT, while on the other hand there was no overall impact on the functional outcome between the techniques. However, the comparison between 3D-CRT and IMRT should be interpreted cautiously, as treatment technique was closely linked to calendar period, resulting in differences in follow-up and baseline characteristics that cannot be fully accounted for in a retrospective post hoc analysis. Moreover, a sensitivity analysis restricted to the overlap period of both techniques (2005–2007), did not show significant differences between 3D-CRT and IMRT across any endpoint, although better toxicity outcomes were detected in the most recent era. While grade 2+ toxicities have been associated with worse functional outcome, toxicities were overall low, and it is not surprising that this did not translate into poor functional outcome for either group.

While conventional fractionation remains the standard for preoperative treatment of STS, previously mentioned hypofractionation approaches including moderate and ultra-hypofractionated schemes, are increasingly being explored.13 Two recent large, prospective datasets on moderate hypofractionation merit comparison, despite differences in certain aspects such as follow-up duration or exclusion of certain histologies. The first was a single-arm phase 2 HYPORT-STS study, which included 120 patients.28 This trial investigated a 3-week course of RT (at 2.85 Gy/fraction) and reported MWC in 31% of cases with a similar LC rate to our study. Initial reported grade 2+ late toxicity was apparently lower than in our patient cohort, at a median follow-up of 24 months. This difference might be attributable to the hypofractionation scheme, though the incidence remains comparable to that reported in other prospective trials of conventionally fractionated RT in STS. Subsequently, at a median time of 43 months, 4-year local recurrence-free survival of 93% and a favorable longer term toxicity profile was reported.12 However, in contrast to our study, chemotherapy was used in a substantial proportion of patients. The second trial recently reported a similar hypofractionation regimen with 23.1% rate of MWC,29 which is comparable to that observed in our cohort. The HYPORT-STS trial had a lower proportion of larger tumors (>10 cm), which could influence the risk of MWC, as also suggested by the trend seen in our cohort.

The largest ultra-hypofractionated study (phase 2) included 311 patients treated with 25 Gy in 5 fractions. While the acute wound complication rate was comparable with the other prospective conventionally fractionated trials, and the fracture rate was less than 1%, the 86% LC rate was at the lower end of acceptable range and may be attributable to the (relatively) lower biological dose used.30

Other ultra-hypofractionated studies with higher doses using 30–40 Gy in 5 fractions included relatively small cohorts with limited follow-up.31, 32, 33, 34 LC rates were >90% but the incidence of high-grade complications-including fractures, major vascular events, and amputations-was somewhat unexpected. Such severe complications, particularly treatment-related amputations, are exceedingly rare in modern cohorts treated with conventional doses using IMRT.

Regarding fractures specifically, they reported fracture rates of 4% (one patient, 2 months after surgery,31), 3.1%,32 4.5%33 and 2.7%.34 Given that radiation-induced fractures typically manifest at a mean time of 38 months,35 and that the median follow-up across these studies ranged from approximately 21 to 37 months in relatively small cohorts (n = 25–110), the true long-term incidence of such events may be underestimated. In our cohort, a fracture rate of 1.7% (17/1027) was observed at a substantially longer median follow-up, with a substantial proportion of patients followed beyond 10 years.

While hypofractionation studies published thus far show promise, the data are inadequate to adopt as standard of care without comparative studies to conventionally fractionated schedules. Therefore, the Connective Tissue Oncology Society states that hypofractionated preoperative RT should not yet be standard of care for STS of the extremities and trunk outside of clinical trials.13 Of note, while such trials are now planned and may soon start recruiting, mature long-term results are not expected for several years.

While our data reinforces the excellent results that may be achieved in local management of STS, it also underscores the high risk of distant metastasis in STS, which affected one quarter of our cohort. Effective systemic therapies in localized disease to date have been limited with little change in the last 30 years but, as recently shown by the promising phase 2 results of pembrolizumab in high-risk UPS and liposarcomas,36 new therapies are emerging. Ongoing efforts to build on these findings include additional immunotherapy trials currently in development.

Although this cohort represents the largest single sarcoma-center series of STS patients treated with preoperative RT, we were unable to demonstrate differences in LC across histological subtypes, likely due to the low number of local failures and multiple histologies, none of which consisted of a large proportion of the overall cohort. Moreover, an imbalance in prior surgery between the 3D-CRT and IMRT groups likely reflects temporal changes in referral patterns and centralization of sarcoma care over the long study period, including the introduction of the Provincial Sarcoma Services Plan of Ontario and should therefore be interpreted as an era-related baseline difference rather than a technique-specific effect. We were not able to access full dosimetric data from the cohort across treatment eras and this precluded formal dose-volume histogram analysis between techniques leading to an inability to correlate dose volume relationships to observed toxicities.

Moreover, missing data were limited for baseline covariates but more substantial for late toxicity and functional outcomes, which were unavailable for approximately one third of patients, reflecting variable follow-up duration, death before long-term assessment, transfer of care to outside institutions, incomplete clinician-reported toxicity documentation, and non-completion of patient-reported questionnaires. Because patients with severe progression, early death, or major morbidity may have been less likely to undergo long-term assessment, while clinically well patients may also have returned to local follow-up, the direction of bias is uncertain, and long-term toxicity and functional findings should be interpreted as applying to patients with available assessments.

These findings highlight the need for the collection and analysis of multicenter real-world data to better characterize individual subgroups and to enable more personalized recommendations regarding treatment strategies resulting in local control, oncological risks, and functional outcomes. Further, although the database was prospectively maintained, the nature of the analysis was a retrospective analysis, with the associated inherent biases. Given that multiple statistical tests were performed without formal multiplicity correction, secondary findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

In conclusion this study underscores the excellent local and long-term functional outcomes of conventionally fractionated preoperative RT in the management of STS and appears to demonstrate an advantage in long-term side effects with modern RT techniques such as IMRT. These data may provide a useful benchmark for future studies analyzing hypofractionation and combined treatment schemes, though cross-trial comparisons should be interpreted with caution.
