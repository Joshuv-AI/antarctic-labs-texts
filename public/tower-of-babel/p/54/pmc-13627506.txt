From Patient Selection to Surveillance: Artificial Intelligence Applications in Radioiodine Therapy – A Systematic Review
Authors: Maria Obrycka, Saeid Abdelrazek, Łukasz Żukowski, Janusz Myśliwiec, Piotr Szumowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Radioactive iodine therapy requires individualized decisions regarding patient selection, administered activity planning, imaging assessment, and follow-up. This systematic review summarizes current applications of artificial intelligence (AI) in the 131I treatment pathway and evaluates barriers limiting clinical implementation. Following PRISMA 2020 guidelines, PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Wiley databases were searched for original studies involving human patients treated with 131I for differentiated thyroid cancer or benign thyroid disease, where AI, deep learning (DL), or machine learning (ML) methods were utilized. Evidence was synthesized, and study quality was assessed using the NIH/NHLBI Study Quality Assessment Tool. Among 775 identified records, 24 studies met inclusion criteria. AI applications included pretreatment decision support, detection and characterization of iodine-avid disease, dosimetry and activity optimization, radiation-safety assessment, treatment response prediction, levothyroxine dose estimation, and recurrence-risk stratification. The available evidence was dominated by retrospective, single-center studies, primarily focused on differentiated thyroid cancer, with limited external validation. AI has the potential to improve standardization and personalization of 131I therapy, but heterogeneous methodologies and insufficient evidence of clinical utility currently restrict routine adoption. Prospective multicenter studies with harmonized endpoints and robust validation are required to facilitate clinical translation.

Introduction

Radioactive Iodine Therapy: Basic Principles

Radioactive iodine therapy (RAIT) represents one of the earliest and most successful examples of targeted radionuclide therapy in medicine, leveraging the unique ability of thyroid follicular cells to actively concentrate iodide via the sodium–iodide symporter (NIS) [1–5]. Since its first clinical application in the early 1940 s by Saul Hertz [6], RAIT has become a cornerstone in the management of both benign and malignant thyroid diseases [7–10]. Despite ongoing debate regarding the potential risk of secondary malignancies following RAIT, the available evidence remains heterogeneous and inconclusive [10–13]. While several large studies have not demonstrated a clear increase in overall cancer-related mortality or heritable genetic effects after therapeutic 131I exposure [14], recent meta-analyses have reported possible dose-dependent associations with specific secondary malignancies, including leukemia and salivary gland cancers [15–17]. Additionally, emerging evidence suggests that RAIT may be associated with clonal hematopoiesis, a potential precursor of hematologic malignancies, although the long-term clinical significance of this finding remains uncertain [18]. Despite RAIT being generally safe and well tolerated, with most adverse effects being mild and short-term (including radiation thyroiditis, post-therapy thyrotoxicosis, salivary gland dysfunction, transient bone marrow suppression, hypothyroidism, and Graves’ orbitopathy) [19, 20], current long-term evidence remains inconclusive, highlighting the need for continued long-term surveillance and individualized risk–benefit assessment, particularly given the high disease burden [9, 21–23].

RAIT Challenges

Activity Selection

One of the central challenges in RAIT is selecting the administered activity, as it directly determines the balance between therapeutic efficacy and radiation-related toxicity. However, no universal consensus exists regarding the optimal administered activity [24–28]. Instead, major societies typically recommend activity ranges tailored to clinical intent (e.g., remnant ablation, adjuvant therapy, or treatment of known metastatic disease), which contributes to meaningful variation in real-world practice [10, 29]. In differentiated thyroid cancer, activity selection is influenced by the method of TSH stimulation (thyroid hormone withdrawal vs rhTSH) [1]. Pre-therapy iodine status also significantly affects 131I biodistribution, and low-iodine diets are routinely used to maximize uptake in target thyroid tissue [30]. Whole-body radiation exposure depends largely on systemic iodine retention and clearance, which may be prolonged in conditions of renal impairment [31]. Similarly, lithium can increase absorbed dose to target tissue by prolonging 131I retention, but may also increase non-target radiation exposure, requiring careful dosimetric assessment [1, 32].

In benign disorders, the Marinelli formula is used to calculate approximate target absorbed dose, although it requires accurate volume and uptake data, and doesn’t explicitly account for clinical history such as antithyroid drug (ATD) use, which can change iodine kinetics [33]. Furthermore, the short-interval intraindividual variability in Graves’ uptake/turnover and phenomena such as stunning or self-stunning complicate dose prediction, such that “meticulous dosimetry” still may not achieve a precise individual thyroid dose [34]. Clinically, centers therefore use either standardized (fixed) activities, sometimes roughly adapted to thyroid size, or dosimetric calculations based on thyroid volume and biokinetics often using the Marinelli approach to approximate a target absorbed dose.

Radioactive Iodine and Imaging

Planar thyroid radionuclide imaging is limited by subjective interpretation, reader-dependent variability, tissue superposition, and limited quantification [35]. SPECT/CT improves lesion localization and quantitative assessment of 131I uptake, but requires hybrid equipment, increases cost and complexity, and adds CT-related radiation exposure [36]. Moreover, the lack of widely implemented automated thyroid segmentation and the continued reliance on manual image processing prolong analysis time and introduce substantial inter-operator and inter-center variability [37, 38]. Thyroid ultrasonography is widely accessible, safe, and relatively inexpensive with high spatial resolution, but it does not provide direct functional information on iodine uptake or organ kinetics [39]. Notably, radiology has increasingly addressed analogous challenges such as reader variability, time-intensive segmentation, and standardization of quantitative reporting through AI-enabled tools for automated detection, segmentation, and workflow triage, which have demonstrated improved efficiency and reproducibility in several imaging domains [40].

These advances suggest that nuclear medicine could similarly benefit from AI-driven solutions, particularly for automated segmentation, quantitative analysis, and CT-free attenuation correction in thyroid SPECT, thereby improving standardization, reproducibility, and clinical scalability of imaging in the context of RAIT.

Predicting RAIT Outcomes

Individual-level outcome prediction after RAIT is inherently complex in both benign thyroid disease and post-thyroidectomy differentiated thyroid cancer, because therapeutic response emerges from multiple interacting determinants that are rarely measured comprehensively before administration. Reported predictors of RAIT failure in benign hyperthyroidism include male sex, delayed RAIT, prior ATD use, higher fT4, very high 24-h uptake, and larger thyroid volume [41], but key modifiers (ATD timing/duration, iodine contamination after contrast) are often inconsistently measured or documented [42, 43]. In DTC, prognostication is further limited by heterogeneity in remnant or tumor burden and especially lesion-level iodine avidity, and higher prescribed activities do not reliably improve long-term outcomes [44]. In benign hyperthyroidism, clinically relevant outcomes such as remission, persistent hyperthyroidism, or post-therapy hypothyroidism may differ despite similar activity-selection strategies [45]. Reported success rates therefore vary considerably between studies due to differences in outcome definitions and follow-up duration, as thyroid function may continue to change for months after 131I therapy [46–48]. These findings highlight the need for standardized endpoints and follow-up periods when evaluating post-RAIT outcomes and support the development of integrated predictive models combining clinical exposure history, imaging, and biokinetics.

Artificial Intelligence in Nuclear Medicine

Methodologically, clinical AI applications can be categorized according to data modality and intended clinical function (Fig. 1). In medical imaging, DL models are widely used for classification, detection, and segmentation tasks, enabling automated quantitative analysis and imaging biomarker extraction [49, 50]. Complementary radiomics approaches use hand-crafted imaging features combined with classical ML algorithms to improve interpretability and integration with clinical data [51]. AI methods in medical research

The complexity and variability of radioactive iodine (131I) therapy make it a promising area for AI-supported clinical decision-making [49, 50]. AI methods may help address some obstacles by integrating multimodal data to support more precise and individualized therapeutic decisions [51, 52]. DL approaches could improve automated image analysis and quantitative assessment, while ML models may enhance prediction of treatment response, absorbed dose estimation, and risk stratification [52]. Importantly, AI may assist in addressing the shortage of nuclear medicine specialists by serving as a decision-support and educational tool for trainees, improving interpretation skills and clinical consistency [53–56]. Clinical implementation, however, requires high-quality, curated data, rigorous validation, calibration, interpretability, and clinically relevant performance metrics to ensure safety and generalizability across populations [56–60].

Materials and Methods

Contents of Review

This systematic review advances the field of artificial intelligence applications in RAIT by critically synthesizing evidence on AI-supported diagnostic assessment, dosimetry, treatment response prediction, and safety/logistics considerations, while addressing key methodological, clinical, and ethical challenges. Unlike previous reviews focused mainly on model performance, this work adopts a clinically oriented perspective linking AI developments with real-world nuclear medicine workflows and decision-making. The review provides a taxonomy of AI applications in RAIT, identifies major evidence gaps such as heterogeneous datasets and limited external validation, highlights AI as a decision-support tool, discusses ethical and governance considerations, and outlines priorities for future research, including an explainable AI and prospective clinically integrated validation studies.

Formalization of Research Questions

This systematic review aims to classify current AI approaches applied to RAIT, critically appraise their reported performance and clinical utility, identify evidence gaps, and outline priorities for future research to support safe, scalable, and ethically responsible translation into clinical nuclear medicine practice. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

We included studies involving adult patients treated with 131I for differentiated thyroid cancer or benign thyroid disorders (e.g., Graves’ disease, toxic adenoma, toxic multinodular goiter) that applied AI methods such as ML, DL, or CNN-based approaches to clinically relevant components of RAIT, including imaging, outcome prediction, dosimetry, radiation safety, or discharge planning. Non-human, theoretical, unrelated isotope studies, reviews, editorials, conference abstracts, protocols without results, and studies lacking measurable RAIT-related outcomes were excluded.

Data Extraction and Synthesizing the Studies

We applied a prespecified, standardized extraction template to capture study-level evidence consistently, including bibliographic context, clinical use case (imaging/segmentation, response prediction, dosimetry/activity planning, radiation safety/logistics), study design and cohorts, data modalities, and the full AI pipeline. We also extracted validation approaches, performance metrics, and author-reported limitations relevant to bias and generalizability. Two reviewers performed independent extraction with discrepancies resolved by consensus meetings. Synthesis was organized by application domain and used narrative and thematic analysis to identify methodological patterns, sources of heterogeneity, and translational barriers; given expected heterogeneity in populations, endpoints, and reporting, meta-analysis was not prioritized, with emphasis placed on rigor, clinical relevance, and reproducibility.

Results

The literature review identified 775 records in databases. Zotero automatically identified 214 duplicate records and 271 records were excluded based on their publication type (e.g. editorials, conference abstracts, letters), which were subsequently verified by reviewers and removed. A total of 290 papers were eligible for screening. 218 records were excluded manually by two reviewers in agreement based on titles and abstracts, 26 reports were not retrieved in full-text availability. Out of 46 remaining papers, 24 were chosen based on previously established PICO criteria and were included in this review paper (see Fig. 2). The studies included in the review were published between 2018 and 2025. Discrepancies regarding the selection of studies for inclusion in the review were resolved through consensus meetings, while unresolved cases were evaluated and excluded by a senior nuclear medicine specialist. Studies Characteristics

Across the 24 included studies, the most common design was retrospective single-center, sometimes based on synthetic data. Notably, 10 of the included studies were based on Chinese patient cohorts, 2 from Korea, 2 from Iran and 2 from the USA. Patient populations were typically female-predominant and middle-aged (most cohorts reporting mean ages in the 40 s–50 s), and the most frequent indication for RAIT was differentiated thyroid cancer, especially papillary thyroid carcinoma. Fewer studies addressed benign thyroid disease (e.g., Graves’/hyperthyroidism) or post-treatment management (e.g., hypothyroidism/levothyroxine dosing), as well as radiation-safety and dosimetry contexts. The most common AI approaches were supervised “tabular” machine learning models, most frequently using tree-based ensembles/boosting (random forest, gradient boosting/XGBoost/Extra Trees) plus SVM and logistic regression, while a smaller subset used DL on imaging (4 out of 24, mainly CNN/U-Net–type architectures for CT or whole-body scintigraphy tasks). A few studies emphasized interpretability/structure discovery via rule learning, clustering, or hybrid interpretable frameworks (3 out of 24 studies). Overall, studies’ aims clustered around decision support for predicting iodine avidity or non-avidity or occult metastases before therapy, forecasting treatment response/ablation success, recurrence or therapy failure, and enabling more personalized and efficient care through dosimetry or administered-activity planning, discharge and radiation-safety prediction, automated image classification, segmentation, and individualized levothyroxine dosing.

Quality Assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Study Quality Assessment Tool developed by the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI), National Institutes of Health (NIH). Each criterion was rated as “Yes” (criterion fulfilled; green), “No” (criterion not fulfilled; red), or “Cannot Determine/Not Reported” (insufficient information; yellow). For quantitative assessment, fulfilled criteria (“Yes”) were assigned 1 point, whereas criteria rated as “No” or “Cannot Determine/Not Reported” were assigned 0 points. The overall score was calculated as the percentage of fulfilled applicable criteria. Studies were categorized as having high, moderate, or low methodological quality based on the proportion of fulfilled NIH/NHLBI criteria: ≥ 75%, 50–74%, and < 50%, respectively. The assessment was performed independently by two reviewers, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer. Therapy Planning

Selecting Patients

Selecting appropriate candidates for RAIT in differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) is challenging because the clinical decision hinges on two distinct, but complementary, pre-therapy questions: whether a patient harbors clinically relevant metastatic disease that may be occult on conventional imaging yet detectable on 131I whole-body scanning, and whether identified metastases are likely to be 131I-avid (and therefore treatable) versus non-avid (131I-refractory), in which case additional 131I exposure may offer limited benefit [87, 88]. Su et al. (2025) study represents the first trend by creating an explainable, clinic-ready tabular ML by assembling a retrospective cohort of 131I-treated DTC patients and defining occult lung metastasis as CT-negative but 131I-WBS-positive disease [63]. They screened candidate predictors and retained 11 routine variables (demographics, staging/tumor features, LN metastasis count, TSH/Tg/TgAb, administered activity), trained six classifiers (SVM/DT/RF/LR/XGBoost/KNN) with a 7:3 train–test split, and used SHAP to interpret the best-performing LR model. Gao et al. (2024) [64] and Song et al. (2025) [65] reflect the second trend: CT-based deep learning as a surrogate for uptake phenotype, and the field’s move from single-step classifiers to workflow models. Gao used pretreatment non-contrast CT with avidity labels derived from post-RAIT SPECT, implementing a two-stage segmentation: classification cascade (SE V-Net then SE-Net). Song et al. built a multicenter pipeline that first detects nodules on pretreatment CT (Faster R-CNN/FPN) and then classifies uptake phenotype (ResNeSt50), trained and evaluated with cross-validation and independent internal/external testing using Rx-WBS as the reference label.

Beyond confirming the central role of Tg as a surrogate of disease burden, these AI studies highlight additional determinants relevant to selection, including demographics and basic clinicopathologic features. In Su et al. occult lung metastasis risk was independently associated with older age and male sex, alongside higher stage/invasion and thyroid biomarkers. In the CT-based uptake studies, age also differed by phenotype: Gao et al. reported older patients in the non-avid group (with no significant sex difference), and Song et al. similarly observed a slightly older non-avid group. Importantly, both imaging pipelines emphasize small nodule size as a key practical limiter for CT-driven triage, underscoring that the hardest lesions to detect are also the hardest to classify reliably. Together, they support a selection paradigm that aims to minimize futile exposure, streamline escalation to alternative strategies when appropriate, and move toward more personalized, uptake-informed treatment planning.

Protocols, Activity Selection, Dosimetry, and Biokinetics

Individualized 131I dosimetry can help maximize tumor dose while staying within safety limits (e.g., avoiding bone marrow toxicity and radiation pneumonitis), but “full” protocols are often time-consuming (multi-day imaging + repeated blood draws), which is a major barrier to routine use. Recent AI research in RAIT is converging on a common objective: enabling individualized dosimetry and activity selection despite practical constraints (limited sampling, limited imaging resources, and inter-physician variability). In Georgiou et al. (2024) [66], a feedforward deep-learning neural network (regression; two hidden layers) was trained to predict maximum permissible activity (MPA) from early 4/24/48-h measurements, achieving no significant difference versus the full MIRD protocol in an internal test set (p = 0.351), supporting protocol compression by omitting late time points. In Jalilifar et al. (2025) [67], multiple ML approaches were compared (including linear regression, SVR, decision tree), with a multilayer perceptron (MLP) performing best for predicting organ absorbed doses from planar imaging plus a single 45° oblique view, validated against SPECT/CT GATE Monte Carlo ground truth and further assessed in external validation; most organs showed < 15% differences, while spleen and lung regions retained larger negative biases, consistent with expected challenges in smaller/motion-affected structures. In Ciucci et al. (2024) [68], a two-stage ensemble was used to stabilize time-integrated activity (τ/AUC) under sparse/noisy sampling: ML1 (soft-voting logistic regression classifier) selected mono- vs bi-exponential kinetics, and ML2 (AdaBoost with gradient-boosting regressors) estimated parameters/τ. This approach improved robustness relative to conventional fitting with AICc/F-test selection (∼98% classification accuracy; τ errors were constrained to ± 25%), with the unexpected observation that ML frequently favored bi-exponential behavior in patient TACs while preserving high agreement in τ. Finally, Popovic et al. (2021) [69] applied supervised classification to postoperative planning, where an artificial neural network (ANN) best reproduced expert activity-category decisions (95.71%, κ = 0.96) and improved junior-physician concordance after ANN-assisted learning, highlighting utility as both decision support and a training/standardization tool. Overall, these studies suggest AI can support clinically actionable RAIT management by shortening dosimetry workflows, approximating high-fidelity dosimetry from accessible imaging, improving τ estimation with limited time points, and standardizing activity selection, although external validation remains variable and performance limitations cluster in anatomically or kinetically challenging cases.

Safety, Radiation Exposure and Outpatient Management

Radiation protection considerations after therapy extend beyond the treated individual to household contacts and also shape service delivery, because inpatient isolation capacity and discharge timing depend on residual activity and dose-rate constraints. Within this context, two included studies evaluated data-driven approaches to support release decisions and outpatient logistics.

Ebrahimi et al. (2018) [70] pursued individualized third-party dosimetry, training a feed-forward neural network on 52 thyroid cancer families (99 patient–relative pairs) using direct relative dose measurements (dosimeter worn 7–21 days) plus patient dose-rate kinetics (2–48 h) and standard release metrics. Despite generally low observed exposures (mean 0.45 mSv; 7 relatives > 1 mSv, none > 5 mSv), the network delivered an expected improvement over conventional patient-specific calculations and, more notably, an unexpectedly large gain in identifying higher-risk relatives (> 1 mSv: AUC 0.957 vs 0.633), highlighting a practical role for risk-stratified counseling and tailoring restrictions, especially in households with young children. Zhang et al. (2025) [71] addressed a complementary operational endpoint: 48-h discharge eligibility under a national residual-activity threshold (< 400 MBq). They fitted an exponential decay model to serial residual activity measurements obtained every 24-h at a distance of 1 m. This approach enabled estimation of the effective half-life. Subsequently, they trained an SVM model using readily available clinical and retention features. They reported strong discrimination (AUC 0.92; accuracy 88.04%) consistent with the mechanistic expectation that shorter effective half-life predicts earlier discharge, and identified sex and 24-h residual activity as influential predictors, supporting earlier planning of isolation duration while remaining aligned with safety limits.

Therapy Assessment

Imaging Analytics

Lin et al. (2021) [72] exemplify the earlier workflow-oriented paradigm by training an attention-augmented CNN (Dscint) for whole-body scintigraphy classification (normal, bone metastasis, arthritis, thyroid carcinoma) using physician report–derived labels and an internal 7:3 patient-separated split (10 repeated runs). Notably, performance proved highly augmentation-dependent, deeper architectures (e.g., Inception-v4) did not improve discrimination, and class-averaged metrics masked clinically relevant failure modes (lowest F1 in arthritis), collectively highlighting data scarcity and label/phenotype heterogeneity as key constraints for robust deployment. In contrast, Chantadisai et al. (2024) [73] represent the emerging decision-support paradigm by coupling automated ROI-based radiomics with regularized logistic regression to predict successful ablation at 6–12 months after standardized low-risk PTC treatment, using cross-validated model selection and a clinically anchored composite outcome. Expectedly, radiomics alone did not outperform Tg, but the combined model improved discrimination primarily via higher specificity, while an important practical limitation, segmentation failure at near-background uptake, clustered in very low Tg, favorable-outcome cases, implying that “non-informative” images may themselves signal low risk.

Both studies on imaging differ in endpoint (diagnostic categorization vs ablation success), modeling paradigm (end-to-end DL vs radiomics + clinical integration), and dominant technical constraints (data augmentation dependence and class-specific error patterns vs segmentation/feature-extraction feasibility and incremental gains from combining imaging with Tg). However, together they support the feasibility of AI to either standardize scan interpretation or enhance outcome stratification from 131I imaging, while also underscoring the need for stronger generalizability evidence beyond internal validation.

Early and Intermediate Treatment Outcomes in DTC/PTC

Over the past few years, AI research in the RAIT pathway has evolved from describing isolated prognostic factors to building multivariable predictive decision-support models from routine clinical and biochemical data. In a small single-center cohort, Lubin et al. (2021) [74] used a random forest model based on EMR features to predict first RAIT failure. The identified predictors were largely expected and included higher pre-RAIT Tg/TgAb levels and adverse pathology findings. The model also showed an atypical association between higher administered activity and therapy failure. This association was most consistent with confounding by indication. Importantly, the ML approach identified predictors that were not retained in classical logistic regression models, illustrating that nonlinear models can recover signals missed by traditional approaches. Sa et al. (2024) [75] extended this trend with larger retrospective datasets and an explicitly interpretable pipeline (LASSO feature selection, multi-algorithm benchmarking, SHAP explanations) to predict effective response to RAIT and biochemical remission under TSH suppression, achieving strong internal discrimination and reinforcing that Tg metrics dominate prediction while adding actionable contributions from uptake or scan surrogates (eg, remnant on Rx-WBS) and TSH suppression adequacy. Bülbül & Nak (2024) [76] similarly applied multiple classifiers with imbalance handling and cross-validation in papillary thyroid cancer, finding that while few single variables differentiated ER vs non-ER (notably TgAb), boosting models still achieved good AUC/accuracy, supporting the recurring pattern that ML can integrate many weak predictors into clinically useful stratification.

Post-Therapy Endocrine Management

Recent AI work around 131I is expanding beyond imaging or dosimetry into post-therapy endocrine management, where the clinically decisive problem is how quickly and safely patients reach their individualized TSH targets on levothyroxine or restore euthyroidism after post-therapy hypothyroidism. Liu et al. (2024) [77] exemplify a trend toward lean, clinic-ready models with prospective utility. Using random-forest–guided selection from 16 candidates, they retained six simple predictors (BSA, weight, hemoglobin, height, BMI, age) and found SVR to perform best. In a two-center prospective implementation, the model-predicted dose was initiated on day 2 after RAIT. This approach markedly increased early attainment of ATA risk-adapted TSH targets and shortened the time-to-target compared with historical empirical dosing. These findings demonstrate a clinically meaningful benefit of decision support, even though the per-dose step accuracy was only moderate. In contrast, Ngan et al. (2025) [78] reflect the parallel trend of high-dimensional EMR modeling in broader hypothyroidism populations (including post-RAIT cases), integrating comorbidities, drug–drug interactions, food/timing effects, etiology, and labs. An Extra Trees regressor achieved strong internal dose-prediction performance, with BMI and comorbidity burden emerging as dominant drivers, which is an expected finding biologically, but notable in quantifying that these factors may outweigh simple weight-based dosing.

Collectively, a key next research step would be development and external validation of models that jointly predict dose plus individualized monitoring cadence (i.e., when to remeasure thyroid function and revisit) to operationalize truly personalized post-RAIT endocrine follow-up.

Benign Hyperthyroidism Applications

Despite the predominance of AI research in differentiated thyroid cancers, optimizing RAIT management in non-malignant thyroid disorders remains clinically important, as hyperthyroidism is highly prevalent and can be successfully treated with 131I, motivating AI approaches that improve planning and follow-up [41, 42].

Duan et al. (2022) [79] retrospectively analyzed Graves’ disease patients receiving first RAIT and applied ML to predict hypothyroidism at 6 months using 138 baseline clinical and laboratory variables. Feature selection combined random-forest importance, partial least squares discriminant analysis VIP scores, and Mann–Whitney testing with stepwise refinement to derive a 7-variable model (age, thyroid mass, 24-h 131I uptake, AST, TRAb, TMA, neutrophil count) presented as a nomogram. At 6 months, 69.4% developed hypothyroidism, and model discrimination was moderate with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.72 in training and 0.74 in validation, supporting individualized post-treatment surveillance and earlier planning for thyroid hormone replacement. In a complementary application focused on enhancing accuracy and reliability in absorbed dose calculations, Wang et al. (2025) [80] developed an AI-based tool to automate thyroid gland segmentation on CT scans and use the resulting geometry to calculate patient-specific radiation doses in hyperthyroid patients treated with radioiodine. Their model (MSRA-UNet + +) outperformed the standard UNet + + approach in segmentation accuracy (Dice score 85.61% vs. 81.15%) and additionally generated uncertainty maps to flag ambiguous regions for clinician review. When AI-derived segmentations were used for dosimetry, the traditional Marinelli–Quimby formula overestimated absorbed dose by an average of 7.74% (up to 10.73%) compared to the more precise MIRD/Monte Carlo method, suggesting that geometry-aware dose calculation could meaningfully improve activity planning in benign thyroid radioiodine therapy.

Long-Term Follow-up: Recurrence Prediction and Risk Stratification

Investigations applying AI to predict outcomes after RAIT in DTC have largely focused on post-treatment recurrence risk stratification and on employing diverse methodological paradigms including supervised predictive modeling, symbolic rule induction, unsupervised cutoff discovery, and metaheuristic optimization. Supervised tabular ML in real-world institutional cohorts shows modest but credible gains: Wang et al. (2024) [81] gathered 29 potential input variables and trained LR/SVM/XGBoost/RF/NN with imbalance handling, LASSO feature selection, and CV/Bayesian tuning, achieving only moderate discrimination, yet outperforming ATA stratification and reinforcing expected dominant signals from postoperative Tg and nodal burden, while adding a potentially novel contribution from metabolic comorbidities. In parallel, Kim et al. (2023) [82] used inductive logic programming to generate interpretable IF–THEN rules driven largely by serial Tg, yielding high specificity but lower sensitivity and abstentions, consistent with a role in dynamic re-stratification rather than early prediction. Jang et al. (2023) [83] applied K-means clustering with repeated resampling to derive data-driven lymph-node cutoffs that separated recurrence-free survival (HR-based risk groups), illustrating an alternative “AI-as-cutpoint-discovery” paradigm without AUC-based classification. In contrast, studies using the public UCI datasets, such as the studies by Shrestha et al. (2025) on WOA–XGBoost [84] and Deif et al. (2025) on TIEO–XGBoost [85], report very high performance under cross-validation, which, while methodologically focused on optimizer novelty, appears less clinically persuasive given the absence of external validation and potential optimism. Triayudi et al. (2025) [86] most directly addressed generalizability by combining fuzzy co-clustering with cost-sensitive distillation to a calibrated student model and validating across TCGA/SEER, supporting feasibility of portable recurrence-risk models. Clinically, these approaches collectively suggest a pathway toward more individualized post-RAIT surveillance intensity; however, prospective multicenter evaluation with therapy-specific inputs remains the key gap.

Discussion

Categorization of AI Methods by Clinical Utility and Modeling Approach

Across the 24 included studies, artificial intelligence methodologies were applied across the full RAIT care continuum, encompassing pre-therapy assessment, treatment planning, radiation safety and logistical management, as well as post-therapy evaluation and long-term follow-up. This comprehensive distribution of applications suggests that AI may contribute to a clinical paradigm shift in RAIT by moving from isolated, task-specific analytical tools toward an integrated, end-to-end decision-support framework embedded within routine workflows across the entire patient pathway.

Furthermore, AI applications in RAIT can be systematically grouped by clinical tasks along the care pathway into: (i) pre-therapy decision support (e.g., triaging metastatic DTC such as predicting iodine avidity/non-avidity from pretreatment imaging or clinicopathologic variables), (ii) imaging interpretation and quantification (deep-learning classification/standardization of whole-body scintigraphy and post-therapy scan feature extraction), (iii) response and outcome prediction (ablation success, excellent/effective response, or early therapy failure using radiomics + clinical inputs or tabular ML), (iv) dosimetry and activity planning (NN/ML surrogates to reduce sampling burden, approximate Monte Carlo dosimetry from pragmatic planar protocols, and model biokinetics under sparse measurements), (v) radiation safety and logistics (predicting discharge eligibility and exposure to relatives/contacts), and (vi) downstream management and surveillance (levothyroxine dosing support and recurrence-risk stratification). Data Sources and Validation Strategies

In the included studies, AI models for RAIT drew on heterogeneous data streams that can be summarized into four recurrent input domains. First, nuclear medicine imaging (whole-body scintigraphy, post-therapy scans, and in some workflows planar/SPECT-derived measurements) was used both as a direct prediction substrate (e.g., DL-based scan classification) and as a source of quantitative imaging features (radiomics or uptake-derived metrics). Second, anatomic imaging, predominantly CT, served as the key modality for models targeting metastatic characterization and iodine avidity or non-avidity phenotyping. Third, most predictive models incorporated a broad set of clinical, demographic, and laboratory variables beyond thyroid-specific biomarkers, commonly including age, sex, anthropometrics (BMI, weight, body surface area), cardiovascular, metabolic markers (e.g., lipid profile such as LDL), renal and hepatic function surrogates, medication history, and comorbidity burden, alongside disease-specific parameters (TNM/ATA features, nodal burden, administered activity, TSH suppression, and Tg/TgAb dynamics). Fourth, dosimetry and biokinetic datasets (serial activity or dose-rate measurements, blood sampling, effective half-life, and time–activity curve parameters) were used in studies aiming to scale individualized activity planning or approximate Monte Carlo dosimetry under pragmatic sampling constraints. Overall, this heterogeneity of input data highlights that AI-driven RAIT models are inherently multimodal, integrating both functional and anatomical imaging.

Methodological and Clinical Trends

Several consistent methodological and clinical patterns emerge from the included studies. Clinically, the literature is strongly skewed toward DTC, particularly papillary thyroid cancer, with comparatively fewer models addressing benign thyroid diseases. The most frequently targeted endpoints were recurrence or structural disease progression, followed by treatment response–related outcomes (e.g., ablation success, excellent/effective response, or early therapy failure), reflecting the central role of RAIT in long-term oncologic surveillance rather than acute decision-making. In contrast, fewer studies focused on operational or safety-related endpoints, such as discharge eligibility or radiation exposure of contacts, despite their high practical relevance. Methodologically, there is a clear division by data type: deep learning dominates image-centric tasks (e.g., scan classification, lesion detection, uptake phenotyping), whereas tabular machine learning approaches (often tree-based ensembles or gradient boosting) prevail in recurrence prediction, endocrine dose estimation, and risk stratification. Importantly, a growing subset of studies emphasizes interpretability and clinical transparency, using rule-based systems, SHAP explanations, or distillation-based frameworks, signaling a shift away from purely black-box models toward decision-support tools. This provides evidence of a gradual transition from retrospective model development toward clinically actionable decision-support systems that can be integrated into routine RAIT workflows to support treatment personalization, improve consistency of dosing and follow-up strategies, and ultimately enhance patient safety and therapeutic outcomes. In the longer term, such tools may also help reduce the routine workload and administrative burden on clinical staff; however, they are intended to complement rather than replace specialist expertise in nuclear medicine.

Barriers to Implementation and Future Directions

Across the included studies, the main barriers to clinical implementation of AI in RAIT cluster into issues of generalizability, interpretability, workflow integration, and governance. Many models are trained on retrospective, single-center datasets with scanner-, protocol-, and population-specific characteristics, which increases the risk of dataset shift and performance degradation when transferred to new institutions or clinical settings. Moreover, the predominance of Chinese cohorts among the included studies may influence the representativeness of the findings, as thyroid disease epidemiology and management differ across geographic regions. China has historically experienced both iodine deficiency and iodine excess despite long-term universal salt iodization programs, resulting in regional variability in iodine exposure and thyroid disease prevalence [87]. Differences in dietary iodine intake, environmental exposure, TSH reference ranges, and treatment practices may affect the clinical characteristics of thyroid disorders, potentially influencing AI model development and predictive performance, therefore, more studies across diverse geographic populations are needed, and ideally, future AI models should be developed using heterogeneous multinational patient cohorts to improve external validity and reduce population-specific bias [30].

Furthermore, endpoint and label heterogeneity (e.g., varying definitions of ablation success, response categories, recurrence, or iodine avidity) limit reproducibility and make benchmarking difficult, while label quality can be constrained by imperfect reference standards and follow-up variability [88]. Moreover, 6–24 months evaluation windows used in many studies can introduce a time-window (follow-up duration) bias, because late events such as DTC recurrence or RAIT-induced hypothyroidism may be under-ascertained when outcomes are assessed within a short period of time [89, 90].

In addition, several approaches face practical workflow constraints, such as failure of segmentation or feature extraction when uptake is low, reduced sensitivity for small lesions, or reliance on dosimetry and biokinetic inputs that are not routinely collected, meaning that high-performing models may not be feasible at scale. Furthermore, implementation requires more than discrimination metrics: many studies provide limited reporting on calibration, uncertainty, and decision thresholds, and only rarely evaluate clinical impact, safety trade-offs, or how clinicians should act on model outputs.

Importantly, despite the fact that RAIT is widely used in benign thyroid disorders, most current AI research is concentrated on thyroid cancer, leaving a substantial opportunity gap. In benign disease, the clinical objective varies, but achieving and maintaining euthyroidism while minimizing post-therapy hypothyroidism creates a strong rationale for AI tools that can integrate high-dimensional clinical information to predict the patient-specific activity most likely to meet that goal. Because dosing in benign thyroid disease is frequently individualized and practice patterns vary, a robust, well-calibrated model that converges multimodal predictors into a transparent activity recommendation could meaningfully reduce trial-and-error dosing and support clinicians with consistent decision-making [91].

Although the educational role of AI in nuclear medicine was not directly evaluated in the reviewed studies, several papers compared the performance of AI predictors with that of human specialists. This suggests that future research may also explore AI-based educational and decision-support tools for training young physicians and improving clinical consistency [92]. Future directions could also address emerging applications of large language models (LLMs) in nuclear medicine, although current studies are still preliminary, LLMs could support research workflows and patient-facing communication tools (e.g., simplifying and translating RAIT instructions and improving risk–benefit communication), provided that rigorous validation and safety governance are established [93].

A further limitation is the use of the NIH/NHLBI Study Quality Assessment Tool rather than AI-specific frameworks such as PROBAST-AI or TRIPOD + AI [94, 95]. This decision was driven by the substantial methodological heterogeneity of the included studies, many of which were retrospective, exploratory, or focused on technical AI applications (e.g., segmentation, CT-free SPECT, dosimetry, and workflow optimization) rather than formal clinical prediction models. In addition, several studies lacked the detailed methodological reporting required for comprehensive AI-specific bias assessment. Therefore, a more general quality-assessment framework was considered more feasible for consistent cross-study evaluation in this broad systematic review [96]. With further maturation of the field, increasing availability of standardized reporting, and greater methodological sophistication of AI-based studies, future systematic reviews may consider the application of AI-specific risk-of-bias assessment frameworks whenever feasible and appropriate, to enable a more detailed evaluation of model development, validation, and potential sources of bias.

Ethical and governance considerations remain central to the safe integration of artificial intelligence into RAIT. Key issues include bias and fairness, particularly the risk of differential model performance across patient subgroups categorized by sex, age, body habitus, or disease phenotype, which may translate into inequities in clinical decision support. Data privacy and compliance with regulatory standards for sensitive health information are also essential, especially given the increasing use of multimodal datasets [97]. In addition, model drift over time (driven by changes in patient populations, imaging protocols, or treatment practices) poses challenges for maintaining long-term reliability and requires continuous monitoring and recalibration [98]. Importantly, because AI systems may influence decisions related to radiation exposure, administered activity, or treatment escalation, their use must be accompanied by transparent, auditable oversight mechanisms that ensure traceability of recommendations and preserve clinician accountability in final therapeutic decisions [97].

Alongside these technical challenges, ethical considerations remain central: clinicians and health systems must determine how much influence AI outputs should have, how to preserve clinical responsibility and informed consent, and how to prevent automation bias, especially in high-stakes contexts involving radiation exposure. More broadly, it is reasonable to ask whether, and under what safeguards, further AI development should be pursued. The strongest justification is when research is explicitly designed to improve patient outcomes, equity, and safety through transparent evaluation, prospective validation, and clear governance structures rather than pursuing performance metrics alone.

Conclusion

Overall, the reviewed studies suggest that AI should currently be positioned as a clinical decision-support tool rather than a replacement for nuclear medicine specialists. The greatest immediate clinical value of AI appears to lie in supporting repetitive, data-intensive, and multidimensional tasks, such as imaging interpretation, risk stratification, dosimetry estimation, treatment-response prediction, and long-term surveillance. In this context, AI may improve workflow efficiency, standardization, and personalization of RAIT, while still requiring physician oversight and final clinical interpretation.

At present, however, most available evidence remains retrospective and methodologically heterogeneous, with limited external validation and inconsistent endpoint definitions. Therefore, the field has not yet reached the stage of fully autonomous clinical implementation. Future progress will likely depend on the development of multicenter prospective studies, harmonized reporting standards, explainable AI frameworks, and robust validation across different populations and imaging protocols.

Future AI applications in nuclear medicine will probably become increasingly embedded into routine workflows, assisting not only in prediction and planning, but also in therapy optimization, safety management, and physician training. At the same time, broader implementation will require careful attention to interpretability, calibration, regulatory oversight, and clinical accountability to ensure safe and trustworthy use in real-world practice.
