Hypercholesterolemia enhances early dissemination and Borrelia burgdorferi burden in a mouse model
Authors: Julia González, Lauren Hinger, Nikhat Parveen, Alvaro Toledo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Lyme disease spirochete, Borrelia burgdorferi, requires cholesterol to grow. The spirochete acquires cholesterol from the host to form cholesterol glycolipids, which are then incorporated into the spirochete’s membrane. This study aimed to determine whether higher levels of serum cholesterol could facilitate the infection and contribute to the pathogenesis of Lyme disease. We investigated the effect of acute and chronic hypercholesterolemia on spirochetal infection in C3H/HeJ mice fed a high-fat diet (HF) compared to mice fed a control diet. The course of infection in mice was followed for 3 weeks (short-term effects) and 16 weeks post-infection (long-term effects) by measuring spirochete bioluminescence in vivo. At the endpoint, bacterial burden was measured in tissues by real-time PCR, and histology was performed to assess differences in the inflammatory response. Between days 10 and 14 post-infection, live imaging showed that mice on a HF diet presented a significantly higher spirochetal burden and greater dissemination than the controls. These differences were transient and restricted to the first two weeks of infection, without long-term effect observed. Histology showed no significant differences in inflammation between HF and control mice. However, qPCR showed that mice fed with HF diet had a higher B. burgdorferi burden in tissues, including heart, visceral, and subcutaneous fat. These findings revealed that high cholesterol levels resulting from a HF diet led to increases in spirochetal burden and dissemination early in the infection, suggesting that cholesterol may contribute to spirochete persistence and associated Lyme disease symptoms.Keywords: Lyme disease, Cholesterol, Hypercholesterolemia, Infection, Pathogenesis

Introduction

Cholesterol is a common sterol in vertebrate animals. It is important for the integrity and fluidity of eukaryotic membranes, and as a precursor of steroid hormones and bile. In conjunction with sphingolipids, cholesterol forms lipid rafts in eukaryotic cells, which are defined as tightly packed membrane microdomains enriched in proteins involved in signaling [1,2]. Bacteria exploit host lipids for different purposes, using them as an energy source, building blocks for membrane synthesis or camouflage [3]. Cholesterol is uncommon among prokaryotes since they cannot synthesize it and depend on a vertebrate host for its acquisition [4]; however, cholesterol presence has been reported in several species of Borrelia, Helicobacter, Mycoplasma, Ehrlichia, Anaplasma, and Brachyspira [5–9].

Cholesterol is an essential building block for the membranes of Borrelia burgdorferi. The inner and outer membranes have phospholipids, phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylglycerol [10–12], and three glycolipids, two of which have cholesterol: cholesteryl 6-O-acyl-β-d-galactopyranoside (ACGal) and cholesteryl-β-d-galacto-pyranoside (CGal) [13–16]. The third glycolipid in the spirochete’s membranes is mono-galactosyl-diacylglycerol (MGal) [17]. The lipid composition of the inner and outer membranes in B. burgdorferi is similar. The outer membrane is richer in cholesterol glycolipids, while the inner membrane has a higher relative content of the non-cholesterol glycolipid MGal [18]. In addition, phospholipids in the outer membrane are more saturated than those in the inner membrane [18]. Collectively, these differences affect the ability of the IM and OM to form membrane microdomains enriched in proteins associated with tick-host adaptation, transport, and signaling. This organism has a small genome and is auxotrophic for amino acids, fatty acids and cholesterol [4,10,19]. Borrelia burgdorferi uptakes cholesterol from host cells [20] and subsequently transports it to the cytosol, where it is glycosylated by the glycosyltransferase BB0572 [21] to form cholesterol glycolipids. Cholesterol glycolipids are exported and incorporated into the membranes, allowing the spirochete to grow and divide.

In a previous study, we showed that apolipoprotein E-deficient (apoE−) and low-density lipoprotein receptor-deficient (LDLR−) mice infected with B. burgdorferi had an increased number of spirochetes in the joints and inflamed ankles compared to infected wild-type (WT) mice [22]. LDLR- and apoE-mice have mutations on the low-density lipoprotein receptor and apolipoprotein E, resulting in increased levels of serum cholesterol. These mice have been used to study hyperlipidemia and atherosclerosis. The cholesterol levels in these two mouse strains were 20 times higher than in WT [22], limiting their value in unveiling the impact of hypercholesterolemia specifically on Lyme disease pathogenesis that would be relevant to human infection.

In this study, our goal was to determine whether serum hypercholesterolemia in WT mice could affect the dissemination and yield of bacteria in vivo and exacerbate the pathogenesis. To this end, we fed two groups of C3H/HeJ mice with high-fat (HF) and control diets and infected them with a B. burgdorferi strain that possesses a Borrelia codon-optimized luciferase gene [23]. We used bioluminescence imaging to follow the course of infection in HF diet and control groups. In addition, we measured the bacterial yield in organs and assessed, through histology, whether the inflammatory response was exacerbated in the experimental group.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval and animal welfare.

All mouse work was performed under approved IACUC ID999900485 at Rutgers University. Mice were euthanized with CO2 at different times depending on the experimental group.

Experimental design.

Two models were developed in this study: acute hypercholesterolemia (Groups 1 and 2; Fig. 1A) and chronic hypercholesterolemia (Groups 3 and 4; Fig. 2A). All mouse groups included a HF group fed with a 60 % cal. fat diet (Research Diets, New Brunswick, NJ) and a Control (C) group fed 10 % cal. fat diet (Research Diets) (Figs. 1A and 2A), and each of these diet groups (HF and C) consisted of four male and four female C3H/HeJ mice. Borrelia burgdorferi infection was followed up in both models for 3 weeks (Groups 1 and 3) and 16 weeks (Groups 2 and 4) to determine the short-term and long-term effects. At the end of the experiments, the body composition was calculated for each mouse before euthanasia.

Bacteria inoculation.

For the infection of mice, B. burgdorferi N40 (bbe02::Bbluc-aadA mutant) [23] was grown at 33 °C in BSK-II medium supplemented with 6 % (vol/vol) rabbit serum (Sigma) in the presence of 80 μg/ml of streptomycin. For the acute hypercholesterolemic model, eight-week-old mice were inoculated intradermally in the lower dorsal area with 106 spirochetes after being fed a HF or control diet for 3 weeks, while for the chronic hypercholesterolemic model, twenty-week-old mice were inoculated intradermally in the lower dorsal area with 106 spirochetes after being fed a HF or control diet for 15 weeks.

Imaging.

The spirochetal burden and dissemination were assessed by bioluminescent images taken during the course of infection. Mice were injected intraperitoneally for in vivo imaging with 1.5 mg of D-luciferin in 100 μl PBS. Bioluminescent images were taken using a IVIS Lumina X5 (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA) as previously described [23] at 4, 10, 14 and 21 days post-infection (dpi) for the short-term infection in the acute (Group 1) and 4, 10, 12, 14, 21 days post-infection for the short term infection in the chronic (Group 3) hypercholesterolemic models. The long-term infection in the acute (Group 2) and chronic (Group 4) hypercholesterolemic models were measured weekly after week 4 until no signal was detected. Captured images were analyzed using Live Image Software (PerkinElmer, MA). Body composition was analyzed using an Albira PET/CT Imaging System (Bruker, Billerica, MA) to determine the Fat/Muscle/Bone percentage before euthanizing the mice.

Blood collection.

Roughly 100 μl of blood was collected using a lateral tail vein blood procedure one day before infection to measure cholesterol levels in serum, and 1 ml–1.5 ml of blood was collected by cardiac puncture immediately after euthanizing mice with CO2 to measure specific IgG responses to B. burgdorferi.

Serology.

B. burgdorferi-specific IgG antibodies for Groups 1 and 3 were measured by ELISA as previously described [22]. Serum cholesterol was determined using a cholesterol quantitation Kit (Sigma) following the manufacturer’s instructions and read with an accuSkan GO (Fisherbrand) at 570 nm.

Quantitative PCR of B. burgdorferiin Mouse Tissues.

Total DNA was isolated from mouse joints, ears, spleen, heart, and visceral and subcutaneous fat using the DNeasy kit (Qiagen), following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentrations were measured using a NanoDrop ND-1000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA), and all samples were then diluted in nuclease-free water (Molecular Biologicals International, Inc., Irvine, CA) to 10 ng/μL. Quantitative PCR was performed using a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (ThermoFisher) with flaB and mouse nidogen primers ([24]; Toledo et al., 2012), as previously described [22]. The average quantity of Borrelia flaB copies was normalized by the average quantity of mouse nidogen copies [24].

Histology.

Knees and hearts from mice were collected in buffered 10 % formalin (Azer Scientific, Morgantown, PA). Samples were processed and H&E-stained at the Research Pathology at Rutgers University. The section quality for the joint and heart samples was qualitatively assessed. Arteritis and bursitis were scored on a scale from 0 (no inflammation) to 4 (severe inflammation). The samples were coded and double-blinded before being evaluated by the pathologist.

Statistics.

Serum cholesterol levels and differences between high-fat and control groups, in terms of body composition and serology results, were compared using t-tests. Moreover, we evaluated the intensity of bioluminescent images by calculating the sum of photons/s within the regions emitted by luciferase in each image, and we also assessed the dissemination of B. burgdorferi by calculating the interior area (number of pixels) for each region. Images were analyzed using the Bruker molecular imaging software MI SE (version 7.1.3.20550). We compared the mean intensity and dissemination area between the diet groups (control vs high fat) using non-parametric Mann-Whitney tests and unpaired t-tests with Welch’s correction for the normally distributed data. For all tests, the level of statistical significance was set at a p-value of 0.05. All statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 User Guide (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

Results

In vivo imaging of bioluminescent B. burgdorferi in mice.

Groups 1 and 3 captured the dissemination and spirochetal burden in the early infection stage (up to 3 weeks) in the acute and chronic hypercholesterolemia models, respectively. On the other hand, Groups 2 and 4 followed the long-term infection dynamics of acute and chronic hypercholesterolemia models, respectively, from week 3 until the bioluminescence signal was no longer detected (Figs. 1 and 2).

In acute hypercholesterolemia model at short term (Group 1), we observed by 10 dpi a significant higher spirochetal burden in the mice fed on HF diet than in the control group (p-value <0.05) (Fig. 1B and C for mice imaging and 1F for boxplot fluorescence intensity.), which indicates that spirochetes multiply faster in mice with high serum cholesterol. In addition, in mice fed HF diet B. burgdorferi disseminated faster, with a significant larger area affected by 10 dpi (p-value = 0.001) (Fig. 1B, C and 1G). However, these differences in the infection dynamic were transient, as there were no differences between the HF and control groups in spirochetal burden or dissemination at 14 dpi and 21 dpi.

Interestingly, we observed a similar infection dynamic trend in the chronic hypercholesterolemia model at short term (Group 3). Mice fed on HF diet showed a higher spirochetal burden at 12 dpi (p-value <0.001) and 14 dpi (p-value <0.05) (Fig. 2B and C and 2F for quantitation of bioluminescence), demonstrating the consistency of the results obtained in the acute model (Group 1). Moreover, spirochetes disseminated better in mice fed a HF diet compared to controls at 12 dpi (p-value <0.001) and 14 dpi (p-value <0.01) (Fig. 2B, C and 2G). These differences in burden and dissemination were also transient in the chronic model, suggesting that elevated serum cholesterol is sufficient to promote the spirochetal infection in the early stages.

There were no differences between sexes in spirochetal burden or dissemination in Group 1 (short-term effect on acute model). However, we observed differences between sexes in the controls of Group 3 (short-term effect on chronic model) regarding spirochetal burden at 10 dpi (p-value <0.05) and dissemination at 10 dpi (p-value <0.05) and 12 dpi (p-value <0.05). While the male group had more extensive dissemination at 10 dpi (male = 1001.8 ± 8.7e+01 vs female = 445.2 ± 140.6), the opposite occurred at 12 dpi (male = 56.6 ± 1e+01 vs female = 353.5 ± 77.0), suggesting that differences could be due to a small difference in the infection dynamics rather than differences in actual burden. No differences between sexes were observed in the mice fed a HF diet in Group 3.

At long-term, the results on infection dynamics of acute (Group 2) and chronic (Group 4) hypercholesterolemia models, showed no differences between HF and control mice in dissemination or spirochetal burden detected by bioluminescence (Panels D, E, H, I of Fig. 1 for acute model, and Fig. 2 for chronic model), confirming that high cholesterol levels have a major impact early in the infection. No differences between sexes were detected in these groups.

Serum cholesterol levels.

Serum cholesterol levels were measured right before infecting mice with B. burgdorferi at 8 weeks (acute model) and 20 weeks (chronic model). Cholesterol levels were significantly elevated in mice fed a HF diet compared to controls (t-test; p-value <0.001) (Fig. 3). We did not find differences between males and females within the same group (t-test; p-value >0.05) (Table 1S).

Level of statistical significance for t-test: p-value <0.05 (*); p-value <0.01 (**); p-value <0.001 (***).

Body composition analysis.

The body composition of each mouse was analyzed right before euthanasia, including muscle, bone, and fat tissue.

In the acute hypercholesterolemia model, at short-term (Group 1) males fed on HF had a significant increase in body fat percentage (t-test; p-value <0.05), while there were no significant differences in females (Fig. 4A). However, in the long-term (Group 2), males and females fed on HF diet had a significant increase in the percentage of body fat compared to controls (male t-test, p-value <0.01; female t-test, p-value <0.001) (Fig. 4B).

Body composition analysis of males and females between control (C) and high fat (HF) diet groups, showing the percentage of fat based on actual body weight (BW) for (A) Group 1, (B) Group 2, (C) Group 3 and (D) Group 4. Level of statistical significance for t-test: p-value <0.05 (*); p-value <0.01 (**); p-value <0.001 (***).

Similarly, in the chronic hypercholesterolemia model, we observed that short-term (Group 3), males and females fed on an HF diet had a significant increase in the percentage of body fat (male t-test, p-value <0.01; female t-test, p-value <0.001) (Fig. 4C). Interestingly, the long-term (Group 4), only males fed on an HF diet showed an increased percentage of body fat (t-test, p-value <0.05) (Fig. 4D). In none of the groups, whether males or females were fed an HF diet, did they show differences (t-test, p-value >0.05) in bone or muscle composition compared to the controls (Fig. S1).

Borrelia specific IgG response.

We measured IgG antibodies three weeks after infection to determine whether the HF diet had altered the antibody response against the spirochete. No differences were observed between mice fed on HF diet and controls (Fig. 5).

Detection of spirochetal burden in tissues by qPCR.

Although no differences were found in spirochetal burden by bioluminescence at the end of the experiments for any of the groups, this approach lacks sensitivity compared to molecular methods such as quantitative PCR. In acute hypercholesterolemia model at short-term (Group 1), we found no differences by qPCR between mice fed on an HF diet and controls in any tissue sample (Fig. 6; these mice were fed for 15 weeks). However, we observed differences between mice fed on an HF diet and controls in the other groups that were fed on a diet for a longer time: 24, 19 and 32 weeks (Group 2, 3 and 4, respectively) (Fig. 6).

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis of spirochete burdens in different mouse tissues: Heart, visceral fat (VF), subcutaneous fat (SF), ear, spleen and knee. Group 1 (A) Group 2 (B), Group 3 (C) and Group (D). Level of statistical significance for t-test: p-value <0.05 (*); p-value <0.01 (**); p-value <0.001 (***).).

In the acute hypercholesterolemia model at long term (Group 2), a significant increase in the number of Borrelia was found in the visceral fat (p-value <0.001; t-test), spleen (p-value <0.01; t-test) and knee tissue (p-value< 0.01; t-test) of mice that fed on a HF diet. Similarly, in the chronic model at long term (Group 4), we found a higher load of spirochetes in the visceral fat (p-value <0.05; t-test), heart (p-value <0.01; t-test), subcutaneous fat (p-value <0.01; t-test) and ear tissue (p-value <0.01; t-test) of mice fed on a HF diet. At short term, we only observed differences between mice fed on an HF diet and controls in the chronic hypercholesterolemia model (Group 3), with a higher number of Borrelia found in the heart (p-value <0.001; t-test) and the knee tissue of HF mice (p-value <0.01; t-test).

Histology.

Histology results did not show significant differences in the inflammatory response between the control and HF groups in the heart and joints (Fig. S2).

Discussion

The spirochete B. burgdorferi, requires cholesterol to grow and recruits it from the vertebrate host [20]. Thus, one fundamental question in Lyme disease pathogenesis is whether cholesterol levels in the host facilitate infection and exacerbate Lyme disease pathogenesis. Our previous study using apoE (apoE−) and LDLR (LDLR−) deficient mice showed an increased severity of the infection with higher numbers of spirochetes in the joints and gross arthritis compared with WT mice, suggesting that hypercholesterolemia affects the severity of Lyme disease [22]. These mouse models (LDLR− and APOE−) are helpful in determining the interaction between cholesterol, spirochetes and host immune responses, particularly in the context of apolipoproteins-mediated inflammation [25]. However, the levels of serum cholesterol in these mouse models are 15–20 times higher than those in WT mice fed on an HF diet, which have physiological cholesterol levels closer to those found in humans with hypercholesterolemia and, therefore, represent better the physiological cholesterol levels that B. burgdorferi would encounter in hypercholesterolemic patients. In this study, we showed that high cholesterol levels in C3H/HeJ enhance early dissemination and burden of B. burgdorferi in tissues, suggesting that elevated cholesterol may facilitate the establishment of the infection in patients with hypercholesterolemia.

Regardless of whether mice were fed an HF diet for 8 weeks or 20 weeks before infection, elevated cholesterol levels might be sufficient to support large spirochetal numbers and enable an early dissemination in the infection, while differences in age at the time of infection or obesity are not critical. Interestingly, the differences in spirochetal burden between HF and control mice were transient. After the second week of infection, no further differences were detected using in vivo imaging; thus, high cholesterol levels could not protect the spirochete from clearance by the immune system. It is important to note that by qPCR, we detected differences in B. burgdorferi burden in some tissues, suggesting that hypercholesterolemia could facilitate the persistence of the spirochete in the host. There were no differences between sexes in spirochetal burden or dissemination in HF mice.

In line with the results of our study using mouse models (LDLR− and APOE−) [22], a longitudinal health system cohort study found that hypercholesterolemia in patients is associated with a higher risk and severity of Lyme disease [26]. This cohort study also found that cholesterol genetic scores and rare LoF variants in CD36 and LDLR were associated with Lyme disease risk [26]. It has been reported that the innate immune response is suppressed in diet-induced obese mice [27], which could enable infection and partially contribute to our observations. In addition, obesity increases the risk of type 2 diabetes, primarily through inflammation that originates in expanded, stressed adipose tissue [28]. This chronic inflammatory state alters the behavior of immune cells by enhancing the activity of pro-inflammatory macrophages and skewing T-cell responses toward a Th1-dominant profile [29]. As a result, immune cells become functionally impaired, compromising their ability to respond to infections [30].

Another potential explanation for the increased risk of Lyme disease in patients with high cholesterol is that a lower infectious dose may be sufficient to establish an infection, as the spirochete encounters a favorable nutritional environment, facilitating division and dissemination. The number of spirochetes transmitted through tick feeding is time-dependent, and therefore, the longer the ticks feed, the better the chances that the spirochete will infect the host. Previous studies have shown that a single Ixodes scapularis nymph requires at least 48 h to infect a mouse [31–34]. Therefore, it is plausible that cholesterol levels in the host affect the transmission dynamics, decreasing the tick’s attachment time to establish the infection in the vertebrate host and, consequently, exposing patients with hypercholesterolemia to a higher risk of acquiring Lyme disease.

Although our results clearly showed that hypercholesterolemia plays a significant role early in the infection, its long-term impact remains unclear. We did not find differences in humoral responses between WT mice fed on HF and control diets, regardless of whether they were on a long-term or short-term HF diet before infection. These results are similar to those previously found in WT mice [22]; however, inflammation drastically differed from those observed in LDLR- and APOE-mice [22]. Although the mechanisms leading to exacerbated inflammation in APOE− and LDLR are unclear, it has been hypothesized that it could be mediated by apoB-100 reactive T cells, which may have a limited or anti-inflammatory effect in most cases. Furthermore, they may become pro-inflammatory and pathogenic under certain conditions (e.g., individuals with elevated LDL levels) [35], [52]. It is possible that exacerbated inflammation in these mouse models could be driven by a complex interplay between host immune responses, cholesterol, and B. burgdorferi, which could be responsible for some of the persistent symptoms that certain patients experience after treatment.

The finding of B. burgdorferi in visceral and subcutaneous fat may simply be a consequence of being in an organ that can provide easy access to the lipid nutrients that this organism requires. However, there are well-known links between the adipose tissue and the immune response (for review [36]). These links could also result in enhanced local immune responses, or, as has been suggested for other bacteria, that adipose tissue could represent a protective niche. In other bacteria, residence in adipose tissue has been interpreted as increasing their capacity to persist. Mycobacterium tuberculosis can enter adipocytes and survive in a non-replicating state that is insensitive to major anti-mycobacterial drugs [37,38]. Two species of pathogenic Rickettsiae have been found in adipose tissue at late stages of infection. Rickettsia prowazekii was detected in adipose tissue, but not in other organs, several months after recovery from the primary infection, suggesting a role for this tissue as a reservoir for persistent infections of this bacterium, the cause of Brill-Zinsser disease, the relapsing form of epidemic typhus [39]. Coxiella burnetii, the agent of Q fever, was detected in adipose tissues at late stages of mouse infection [40]. The spirochete Leptospira interrogans colonizes the inner walls of the blood vessels in adipose tissue in infected hamsters [41]. The residence of B. burgdorferi in visceral and subcutaneous tissue represents a new finding that may have implications for its persistence.

Lastly, one aspect that remains understudied is the effect of cholesterol on the pathogenicity of B. burgdorferi, and whether this could contribute to the severity of Lyme disease and the exacerbated inflammation reported in previous studies [22,26]. In Helicobacter pylori, a gastric pathogen that incorporates cholesterol from the host [8], cholesterol plays a role in virulence. The enzyme cholesterol-α-glucosyltransferase converts cholesterol in the host’s cell membrane into cholesteryl glucosides [42], promoting immune evasion, adhesion and antibiotic resistance [43–46]. Interestingly, cholesterol-lowering agents such as statins decrease inflammation and inhibit virulence factors, resulting in attenuated infectivity [47–49], which is important for a disease that is associated with dyslipidemias (increased LDL and cholesterol) [50]. In a Lyme disease mouse model, statins have been shown to reduce spirochetal burden and modulate immune responses [51]. Cholesterol-lowering agents, such as statins, may reduce host cholesterol availability, thereby limiting the incorporation of cholesterol into the membrane of B. burgdorferi. This disruption could impair growth, membrane integrity and potentially diminish the bacterium’s survival or virulence.

The spirochete encounters two very different milieus during its life cycle: the tick vector and the mammalian host. It is well established that the spirochete tightly controls the expression of many lipoproteins to adapt from the tick vector to the mammalian host and vice versa. Moreover, the expression of lipoproteins during infection is also tightly controlled; however, these changes are not yet fully understood. The spirochete responds to environmental cues, such as pH and temperature, but it may also respond to other cues. Cholesterol may contribute to changes in gene expression, which in turn may facilitate the progression and dissemination of disease. It is important to note that Borrelia, like H. pylori, also has a cholesterol-α-glucosyltransferase that converts cholesterol in the host’s cell membrane into cholesteryl glucosides.

Conclusions

This study provides evidence that B. burgdorferi can exploit elevated cholesterol levels in mice, increasing the spirochete burden and facilitating its dissemination during the early stages. The increased numbers of B. burgdorferi in organs and tissues in the hypercholesterolemic models compared to the controls also suggest that cholesterol may play a role in the persistence of the spirochete and, therefore, contribute to the symptoms associated with post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome.
