The association between anthropometric indices and ischemic heart disease: a large-scale cross-sectional study on the Iranian population
Authors: Amin Nakhostin-Ansari, Erfan Razavi, Shakiba Seifi, Mohammad Ahmadi, Amir Human Hoveidaei, Mahdi Nalini, Abdollah Gandomkar, Fatemeh Malekzadeh, Hossein Poustchi, Mohammad Reza Fattahi, Amir Anushiravani, Reza Malekzadeh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate the superiority of anthropometric indices compared to others for predicting ischemic heart disease (IHD) or cardiometabolic risk factors. This study was a cross-sectional analysis of the Pars Cohort Study data. In total, 9229 Valashahr inhabitants aged 40–75 were included in the analysis. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) analyses was used to compare the predictive accuracy of four anthropometric measures, including body mass index, waist to height ratio (WHtR), waist to hip ratio (WHR), and waist circumference (WC). IHD prevalence was 10.4% in our sample. The prevalence of diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension, dyslipidemia, and metabolic syndrome was 12.7%, 29.2%, 58.4%, and 22.3%, respectively. All anthropometric indices had poor to good accuracy in predicting IHD risk factors, with AUCs ranging between 0.580 and 0.818. WHR was the most accurate measure for predicting IHD in both genders. All indexes had a better accuracy for predicting DM, dyslipidemia, and metabolic syndrome (MetS) in males than in females. To conclude, anthropometric measures, especially WC and WHtR, are recommended for predicting metabolic syndrome in primary prevention settings. These simple indices could help physicians find those who need further evaluation for MetS.Subject terms: Cardiology, Medical research

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases are among the leading causes of death worldwide every year. Ischemic heart disease (IHD) is the most important cardiovascular disease and the first cause of disability and death, with higher disability-adjusted life year (DALY) and mortality rates than other cardiovascular diseases1. About 126 million people, almost 1.72% of the world’s population, are affected by IHD. It is expected that by 2030, this number will increase from 1655 to 1845 per 100,000 people1. A study showed that 80% of all IHD deaths occur in low and middle-income countries2. However, regardless of income, IHD remains the main cause of death in all countries3. In older adults, the highest rate of IHD mortality is in North Africa, Europe, Central Asia, the Middle East, and South Asia4. The incidence of IHD has increased in Iran over the past years5, and since 1960, cardiovascular diseases have been the main cause of death6.

Determining IHD risk factors is the cornerstone of primary prevention7. Several well-known risk factors for IHD include8, diabetes9–11, high serum triglyceride level12, high serum cholesterol level13, old age14, smoking, metabolic syndrome (MetS)15, and central obesity16,17. Obesity is one of the main risk factors for IHD, as the prevalence of obesity has increased due to a more sedentary lifestyle and the consumption of fast foods18. There are anthropometric characteristics that describe obesity, such as body mass index (BMI), waist to hip ratio (WHR), waist to height ratio (WHtR), and waist circumference (WC), which can predict the incidence of cardiometabolic diseases19–23 A cohort study found a relationship between the WHR and chronic heart diseases among middle-aged men in France24. Ineke van Dis et al., in a cohort study survey of adults in Poland, found that the prevalence of fatal cardiovascular diseases (CVD) is twice as much as non-fatal CVD in patients with obesity25. Among all anthropometric characteristics, Sarah Choi et al. found that WHR and WC have stronger relationships with the prevalence of lipid-associated cardiovascular disease26. Additionally, many studies have shown the relationship between anthropometric measures and IHD, but evidence for this in different geographic regions is insufficient. Some studies even suggest that race can be a confounding factor, which requires more studies on this subject27,28.

As we mentioned, IHD has increased worldwide over the past years and is the number one leading cause of disability and death. One of the important risk factors for IHD is obesity, and focused studies on features that measure obesity show different results in different geographic regions and races. So, we conducted a cross-sectional study in Iran to evaluate the association between IHD and anthropometric measures in a sample of Iranian adults.

Methods

Study design and settings

The Pars Cohort Study (PCS) data was used to conduct the present cross-sectional study. The details and methodology of PCS have been described previously29. PCS is a prospective cohort study conducted from 2012 to 2014 in Valashahr, a southern region in Fars Province. The project’s major objective was to determine the epidemiology, prevalence, and risk factors of non-communicable diseases in the area. Accordingly, 9721 Valashahr inhabitants aged 40 to 75, representing nearly the entire region’s population, were invited to participate in this study. After being informed about the study process by phone calls, they had an appointment with the staff to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria were unwillingness to participate in the study or being a temporary resident in Valashahr. Subsequently, 9264 participants were enrolled in the study.

Data collection

Data collection was performed in three stages, including a structured questionnaire applied for an interview, limited physical examination at the end of the interview, and laboratory tests on the various samples obtained from participants. In the first stage, the demographic and basic characteristics, such as age, gender, and past medical history, including hypertension, diabetes, and IHD, were recorded. Additionally, the history of smoking throughout life was recorded.

In physical examination, anthropometric indices such as body weight, height, WC, and hip circumference were measured. Measurements for WC were taken at the midpoint between the inferior border of ribs and the iliac crest on a horizontal plane, using soft tape measures. The broadest point around the buttocks was used to assess hip circumference. Two measures to the closest 0.5 cm were taken for each waist and hip circumference. If the inter-measurement variation exceeded 2 cm, a third measurement was performed. The average of the two closest measurements was calculated and reported. WHR was calculated by dividing the mean WC by the mean hip circumference. Male participants with a WC ranging from 94 to 101.9 cm and females ranging from 80 to 87.9 cm were categorized as overweight, while males with WC ≥ 102 cm and females with WC ≥ 88 cm were considered obese. Moreover, men with a WHR ranging from 0.90 to 0.99 and women ranging from 0.80 to 0.84 were categorized as overweight, while men with a WHR ≥ 1.00 and women with a WHR ≥ 0.85 were considered obese. Other participants with fewer amounts for each WC or WHR were classified as normal30,31. Furthermore, WHtR was calculated by dividing WC by height. The cut-off value for higher risk indication was regarded as 0.532. The Body mass index BMI was computed by dividing the weight in kilograms by the square of the height in meters. According to World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations, participants were divided into four groups based on their BMI; underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5 ≤ BMI < 25 kg/m2), overweight (25 ≤ BMI < 30 kg/m2), and obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2)30.

Participants’ blood pressure was taken twice from each arm in a sitting position following 5 min of rest. Hypertensive patients were defined as those with a history of hypertension or those with systolic blood pressure (SBP) greater than 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) greater than 90 mmHg in the physical examination of the PCS.

Blood samples were drawn from all participants to measure blood biochemical parameters deemed as cardiometabolic risk factors. These biochemical markers are total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), triglyceride, and fasting blood sugar (FBS). Diabetes mellitus (DM) was defined as FBS ≥ 126 mg/dl (7 mmol/L) or self-reporting as a known case of DM. Dyslipidemia among participants of the study was defined as TC > 200 mg/dl (5.2 mmol/L), or LDL > 130 mg/dl (3.4 mmol/L), or HDL < 35 mg/dl (0.9 mmol/L), or triglycerides > 150 mg/dl (1.7 mmol/L).

The presence of MetS was determined according to the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP ATP III) definition with at least three out of five following criteria: (1) WC greater than 40 inches in men and 35 inches in women, (2) systolic blood pressure > 130 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure > 85 mmHg, (3) fasting triglyceride level ≥ 150 mg/dl, (4) HDL level less than 40 mg/dl in men and less than 50 mg/dl in women, and (5) FBS ≥ 100 mg/dl.

The main outcome of the present study was self-reported IHD. Participants were considered to have IHD if they stated they had a history of angina, infarction, or heart failure acclaimed by a physician. Rheumatic heart diseases were not counted as IHD.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were expressed as frequency (percentage). The chi-square test was used to compare the groups regarding the categorical variables. The accuracy of anthropometric indices, including WC, WHtR, WHR, and BMI, for the prediction of DM, hypertension, dyslipidemia, MetS, and IHD was evaluated by calculation of the area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for both males and females. We performed a subgroup analysis to enhance the accuracy of the analyses considering gender-specific differences in anthropometric measures. AUC values falling within 0.9–1 were classified as excellent, 0.8–0.9 as good, 0.7–0.8 as fair, 0.6–0.7 as poor, and 0.5–0.6 as indicating a lack of predictive capacity33. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS (statistical package for the social sciences), version 21. A P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical approval

The Digestive Disease Research Institute’s and Shiraz University of Medical Sciences institutional review board approved the project (IR.TUMS.MEDICINE.REC.1400.562).

Consent to participate

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All methods were performed according to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Thirty-five participants whose IHD status was missing were excluded from the analysis, and the remaining 9229 participants (4974 female and 4255 male) were included in this study. The mean age of the participants was 52.65 ± 9.68 years. The prevalence of IHD was 963(10.4%) in our sample. Among all participants, 1171 (12.7%) had DM, 2698 (29.2%) had HTN, 5386 (58.4%) had dyslipidemia, and 2059 (22.3%) had MetS. Also, 1886 (20.4%) individuals had a history of smoking.

Prevlances of DM, hypertension, dyslipidemia, MetS, and IHD were significantly different across participants with different BMI, WC, WHR, and WHtR (p < 0.001). Post-hoc analysis indicated that the prevalence of IHD in underweight people wasn’t significantly different from those with normal BMI, overweight participants, and obese participants (p > 0.05). Additionally, the prevalence of IHD wasn’t significantly different between overweight and obese individuals based on both BMI and WC (p > 0.05). Otherwise, there were significant differences between participants across different anthropometric measures groups concerning the prevalence of DM, HTN, dyslipidemia, MetS, and IHD (p < 0.05).

Discussion

For over two decades now, IHD has maintained its status as the leading global cause of mortality34. Various risk factors have been related to coronary events, some are modifiable. Control of risk factors is more efficient and economically plausible than hospital-based care and has shifted our approach in recent years toward early detection and risk factor modification rather than secondary or tertiary prevention35. Anthropometric measures might be easy-to-use and practical tools for screening cardiometabolic risk factors at the first line of the healthcare system in the public health setting. This study has been conducted to investigate this and evaluate the superiority of any anthropometric measure to others for predicting cardiometabolic risk factors and IHD. The present research findings suggest that the predictive value of anthropometric measures for individual cardiometabolic risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia is deemed inadequate. On the other hand, when it comes to a composite of cardiometabolic risk factors in MetS, there was a fair to good prediction accuracy for women and men, respectively. WC, followed by WHtR, seemed to be better predictors for MetS than BMI or WHR. Furthermore, we found that the idea of using anthropometry for the prediction or screening of IHD was too ambitious and is not recommended based on the results of our study.

The prevalence of IHD and risk factors in Valashahr is relatively high. Several studies have produced estimates of IHD and cardiometabolic risk factors worldwide, with considerable variances in different countries and regions. A cross-sectional survey of individuals from Ravansar, an area in Kermanshah province in Iran, reported the prevalence of HTN to be 15.5% and BMI ≥ 25, almost 43%, which was markedly lower than 29.2% and 55% in our study36. Another study from the north of Iran estimated the prevalence of MetS as 71.5%, which is considerably higher than 22.3% in the present study37. DM prevalence in the PERSIAN Guilan Cohort Study population was two-fold compared to Pars Cohort participants38. These variations could be partly attributed to the age range of individuals included in studies, the criteria for defining MetS, and the population’s lifestyle in different regions.

The results of the current study failed to show the ability of anthropometric measures to discriminate the presence of distinct cardiometabolic risk factors with satisfactory accuracy. This is in line with the findings of previous studies. In their studies, Mahdavi-Roshan et al.38. and Wang et al.39 evaluated the correlation between anthropometric indices and cardiometabolic risk factors. A significant correlation was found for most indexes, though the association strength was weak and almost negligible in all conditions. An investigation of 44,048 Chinese participants from the PURE Cohort Study revealed the poor predictive capacity of anthropometric measures for dyslipidemia, abnormal blood pressure, and hyperglycemia by evaluating the AUC values adjusted for sociodemographic, lifestyle, and medical history characteristics40. A possible explanation might be that many factors other than obesity contribute to the presence of DM, HTN, and dyslipidemia. Hence, poor predictive capacities are not unexpected. Nonetheless, some studies found fair predictive value in specific conditions. For instance, WHtR revealed fair strength for DM and HTN prediction in women in a study from Turkey41. Another survey of USA adults manifested fair predictive ability of WC and WHtR for impaired fasting glucose in women42. These findings were clearly limited to a specific index, gender, and risk factor, and the pattern might vary across different populations. To conclude, the use of anthropometric measures for screening or predicting each cardiometabolic risk factor is not recommended in the literature, which is consistent with the result of the present study.

Our results revealed that central obesity indices such as WC and WHtR were better predictors for MetS in both men and women. This is consistent with a previous study conducted in the north of Iran37. Likewise, Khunti et al.43 reported significantly more predictive capacity for WC than BMI and WHR for MetS prediction. A possible explanation for the superiority of centripetal obesity measures might be self-correlation. One component of MetS criteria is central obesity identified by WC, and it was not too unexpected that those anthropometric measures contain this index, such as WC itself and WHtR predict MetS better than BMI. This can raise doubts about whether excluding WC from MetS criteria would yield different results. Several studies have redefined the composite of cardiometabolic risk factors by excluding WC from the MetS criteria. Can et al. in their study, demonstrated that WC, compared with BMI, was a significantly better predictor for non-adipose components of MetS41. Two other studies also reported larger AUC values for WC predicting non-adipose components of MetS compared to BMI, though this difference was insignificant44,45. We did not find it necessary to exclude abdominal obesity from MetS criteria in our study because the current study aimed to detect the optimal surrogate for all risk factors. Our approach was more practical rather than theoretical. Nonetheless, self-correlation might not be the only explanation for the findings of the studies mentioned above. The higher metabolic and inflammatory activity of visceral fat compared to other region fat deposits could explain central obesity measures’ superiority in predicting the composite of cardiometabolic risk factors32. While most previous studies and even a systematic review favor using central obesity measures, several studies have inconsistent results. Hamzeh et al. reported the superiority of BMI over WC and WHR for MetS prediction36. A community-based survey of the Chinese population revealed a similar result. The reason for these discrepancies is unclear, but it might have something to do with ethnicity and lifestyle. Overall, our study and evidence from the literature mainly highlight the importance of body fat distribution and recommend using WC or WHtR to predict MetS.

We found that the optimal index to discriminate the presence of IHD in both men and women was WHR, among others, though due to very poor strength, the use of it is not recommended for screening in routine clinical practice. This is inconsistent with the suggestions of a previously published systematic review on this topic. However, there might be an explanation. The systematic review conducted by Darbandi et al. estimated the pooled AUC values for WHR, the optimal predictor, to be 0.69 (95% CI 0.66–0.73) in men and 0.71 (95% CI 0.68–0.73) in women46. Although the prediction accuracy reported in this study seems to be higher than our estimation in the current study, the AUC values included values under 0.7. This has led us to deduce that anthropometric measures are inappropriate for IHD prediction, while the authors of the mentioned study concluded otherwise. We believe that a realistic approach for IHD prediction is still applying multifactorial models that have been developed in longitudinal or prospective studies, and the use of only one risk factor, such as obesity-related indices, for IHD prediction, though tempting, is over expectation.

With its large sample size, this study attempted to provide evidence of the accuracy of anthropometric parameters for predicting cardiometabolic risk factors and IHD. However, there are several limitations to this study. First, the participants of this study were middle-aged people from a specific region in the south of Iran, which precludes the generalization of the results to other age groups or regions. Second, the cross-sectional design of the present study made us unable to evaluate and compare parameters for prospective risk prediction of outcomes. Third, some factors may have partly confounded the results of our study. For instance, dyslipidemia treatment or adopting a healthy lifestyle might have influenced the lipid profile or other cardiometabolic risk factors. Fourth, the status of IHD in participants was based on self-reported data, and we did not have access to participants’ medical history documents. This is an important limitation, especially considering that 49% of participants in the PCS were illiterate29. Nonetheless, the current study, with all its limitations, may still have important implementation for early detection of cardiometabolic risk factors to provide opportunities for lifestyle changes and control of risk factors before the occurrence of ischemic coronary events.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study suggested that anthropometric measures should not be used for the prediction of distinct cardiometabolic risk factors and IHD. However, these obesity-related indices could be applied for screening and early detection of MetS as a composite of cardiometabolic risk factors. The optimal anthropometric parameters to predict MetS are WC followed by WHtR, which reflects the centripetal fat distribution. Those with elevated levels of WC or WHtR should be further evaluated for other cardiometabolic risk factors. These findings can improve targeted interventions for risk factor modification to prevent IHD.
