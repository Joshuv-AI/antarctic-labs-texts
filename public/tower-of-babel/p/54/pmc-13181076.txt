Incidence of autism spectrum disorder in children in Kazakhstan and risk factors associated with all-cause hospitalizations
Authors: Kamilla Mussina, Dmitriy Syssoyev, Abduzhappar Gaipov, Dimitri Poddighe, Dinara Almabayeva, Dinara Galiyeva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe aimed to describe incidence and period prevalence of Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) and examined risk factors associated with all-cause hospitalizations in children registered in the Unified National Electronic Healthcare System (UNEHS) in 2014–2021 in Kazakhstan. Cohort comprised of patients less than 18 years old with ASD defined according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) as codes: F84.0-F84.1, F84.5, F84.8-F84.9, registered in the UNEHS from 2014 to 2021. The study included 9,993 children with ASD, of whom 5,912 (59.2%) experienced at least one hospitalization during follow-up. The incidence proportion of ASD increased from 12.8 to 26.6 per 100,000 children between 2014 and 2021, while period prevalence rose from 7.2 to 148.2 per 100,000 during the same period. ASD incidence was substantially higher among males than females. In adjusted Cox models, gastrointestinal disorders (HR 3.86, 95% CI 3.47–4.28), hydrocephalus (HR 1.50, 95% CI 1.29–1.74) and anemia (HR 1.37, 95% CI 1.23–1.51) were associated with increased hospitalization risk. Children aged 11–17 years had higher hospitalization risk compared with those aged 6–10 years (HR 1.35, 95% CI 1.22–1.50), whereas ADHD was associated with a lower risk of hospitalization (HR 0.64, 95% CI 0.49–0.85). Several comorbidities, particularly gastrointestinal and neurological disorders, were strongly associated with hospitalization risk. Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a lifelong neurodevelopmental condition marked by impairments in social communication and interaction, along with restricted and repetitive sensory–motor behaviors1. Symptoms typically present in early childhood and commonly co-occur with conditions such as intellectual disability, attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), gastrointestinal disorders, and epilepsy2.

Most epidemiological studies report a steadily increasing global prevalence of ASD. Data from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention indicate that the prevalence of childhood ASD rose from 16.8 per 1000 children in 2014 to 27.6 per 1000 children in 20203. In Europe, the prevalence of childhood ASD was estimated at 1.15% in 2016, while in Asia it reached 3.9% during the same period1. In contrast, official data from the Ministry of Healthcare of the Republic of Kazakhstan reported a substantially lower ASD prevalence of 2.6 per 100,000 children in 20184.

Worldwide estimates of ASD incidence vary considerably, ranging from 4.3 to 5.5 per 10,000 children in Australia and 4.5 per 10,000 in North America to 8.3 per 10,000 in the United Kingdom, with the highest reported incidence observed in China (27.2 per 10,000)5. In Canada, the crude incidence of childhood ASD increased markedly from 0.16% in 2004 to 0.39% in 20156.

Geographic heterogeneity in ASD prevalence and incidence likely reflects differences in sociocultural contexts, healthcare access, diagnostic practices, data availability, and research capacity. Globally, ASD is diagnosed in more children each year than pediatric diabetes mellitus or childhood cancer6.

Rates of psychiatric hospitalization among children with ASD, as well as hospitalization risk associated with clinical and demographic factors, have increased over time7. ASD is consistently more prevalent in boys than in girls worldwide, with a ratio of 2.7 to 8.3 boys to 1 girl5. Hospitalization risk decreases with increasing age, indicating a lower likelihood of admission among older children5. Hospitalized children with ASD are also more likely to have comorbid conditions, including attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder, gastrointestinal disorders, depression, intellectual disability, epilepsy, and other neurological disorders, compared with non-hospitalized children5.

Although some epidemiological data on pediatric ASD in Kazakhstan are available, primarily reporting prevalence estimates and absolute case numbers, evidence on risk factors for all-cause hospitalization among children with ASD remains limited4, 8. Kazakhstan has well established Unified National Electronic Healthcare System (UNEHS), implemented in 2014, integrates nationwide inpatient and outpatient electronic medical records across all healthcare institutions. Leveraging these comprehensive population-level data offers a unique opportunity to generate robust epidemiological evidence on pediatric ASD in Kazakhstan.

Therefore, this study aimed to describe incidence and prevalence of ASD and examined factors associated with all-cause hospitalizations in children registered in the UNEHS in 2014–2021 in Kazakhstan.

Methods

Study source

The data on pediatric ASD in Kazakhstan was retrieved from the UNEHS. The UNEHS consists of inpatient and outpatient electronic registries, which includes individual patient data on socio-demographics and clinical data based on International Classification of Diseases 10 (ICD-10) codes. Detailed description of UNEHS database is available from previous study9.

Study design and population

Children less than 18 years old who were diagnosed with ASD between 2014 and 2021 were selected for the study cohort. The following variables were extracted from the registry: patient’s demographics (date of birth, gender, age, and residency), date of diagnosis, ICD-10 codes for ASD as the first diagnosis (F84.0-F84.1, F84.5, F84.8-F84.9), comorbidities (gastrointestinal (GI) disorders, anemia, epilepsy, hydrocephalus, cerebral palsy, ADHD) and anonymized population registry number (RPN). RPN is a unique patient identification number which can be used within the healthcare records system. Inclusion criteria: 0–17 years children at ASD diagnosis, first recorded or hospitalized ASD cases between January 1, 2014 and December 31, 2021. Exclusion criteria: implausible dates (e.g., hospitalization before birth), no follow-up information after ASD diagnosis, duplicate records.

Exposure ascertainment (comorbidities)

Comorbidities were identified using ICD-10 codes recorded in UNEHS. To address potential ascertainment bias and reverse causality, we conducted sensitivity analyses restricting comorbidities to diagnoses recorded prior to the first hospitalization event, ensuring appropriate temporality.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome was first hospitalization after ASD diagnosis, defined as any inpatient admission recorded in UNEHS.

Data management

The data management procedures are presented in the flow chart on Fig. 1. Initial data consisted of 50,606,395 patient records from inpatient electronic registry of hospitalized patients (n = 13,841,689) and outpatient electronic registry of dispensary patients (n = 12,604,505). After removing duplicates and adult records, the final cohort consisted of 9,993 pediatric patients with the diagnosis of ASD.

Flow chart on pediatric ASD patients registered in UNEHS for 2014–2021.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata MP version 16.1. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations, whereas non-normally distributed variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages. Normality was assessed graphically using histograms. Group comparisons were performed using two-sided t tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables, and Pearson’s chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, as appropriate. Two-sided p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Follow-up extended from the date of ASD diagnosis to the first all-cause hospitalization or the end of follow-up (31 December 2021). Incidence and period prevalence proportions were calculated per 100,000 population, using annual counts of incident or prevalent cases divided by the corresponding average population aged 0–17 years. Hospitalization rates were expressed per 100 person-years (PY). Population denominators were obtained from the Statistics Committee of the Ministry of National Economy of the Republic of Kazakhstan which covers the UNEHS11. For the period prevalence, cases diagnosed before the period and still alive/registered were taken as numerator.

Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. For variables violating this assumption, time-dependent effects were modeled using interactions with time in the Cox regression model. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. Multivariable models were adjusted for age, sex and comorbidities, including gastrointestinal disorders, anemia, epilepsy, hydrocephalus, cerebral palsy, and ADHD. To address potential ascertainment bias and reverse causality, sensitivity analyses were conducted restricting comorbidities to diagnoses recorded prior to the first hospitalization event, ensuring appropriate temporality.

Ethical approval

All experimental protocols were approved by Nazarbayev University Institutional Research Ethics Committee. The study was performed according to both international local ethics guidelines and regulations as well as declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Among 9,993 children with ASD, 5,912 (59.2%) were hospitalized during follow-up. Hospitalized children were slightly older at diagnosis (6.1 ± 2.7 vs. 5.8 ± 2.0 years, p < 0.001) and had marginally longer median follow-up (3.1 (IQR 1.2–4.8) vs. 2.9 (1.4-5.0) years, p < 0.001). Age distribution differed significantly between groups, with adolescents (11–17 years) more frequently hospitalized (p < 0.001). Males predominated overall (76.9%), with a higher proportion among non-hospitalized children (p < 0.001). Urban residence was more common among hospitalized children (p < 0.001). All examined comorbidities, including GI disorders, anemia, epilepsy, cerebral palsy, hydrocephalus, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, were significantly more prevalent in hospitalized children (all p < 0.001), with the largest differences observed for neurological conditions and GI disorders (Table 1).

Discussion

This is a first study that describes incidence and period-prevalence of ASD in pediatric population and studied risk factors associated with all-cause hospitalizations in Kazakhstan, in general, in Central Asia using a large-scale administrative health data. This study investigated increased incidence and period prevalence of pediatric ASD in Kazakhstan from 2014 to 2021. Moreover, prevalence of ASD was higher among boys compared to girls. It was determined that older age, having co-occurring disorders such as gastrointestinal disorders, hydrocephalus and anemia, were associated with significantly higher hospitalization risk.

According to the results of our study, incidence proportion of pediatric ASD increased from 12.8 to 26.6 per 100 000 from 2014 to 2021. Age and sex-specific ASD incidence was higher for 3 to 4 times for males compared to females for the whole study period. Whereas, period prevalence significantly raised from 7.2 to 148.2 per 100 000 from 2014 to 2021. This is a relatively low rate compared with developed countries, because the rate of autism is rapidly increasing globally each year12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17. According to the reports of the Center for Disease Control announced in 2021, the highest prevalence of pediatric ASD is reported in the US, which is more than 700 per 100 000 population12. The lowest prevalence rate is estimated in European countries. For instance, France had the lowest ASD rates of 69.3 per 10,000 people or 1 in 144 people and Portugal had 70.5 per 10,000 or 1 in 142 in 201912. However, tracking and comparing rates of ASD globally is challenging since there are no specific, uniform criteria for assessing and diagnosing ASD. It cannot be confirmed by blood analysis or brain tomography. This disorder can be diagnosed by expert’s observations and some assessment tests that differ in each country by their standards. Differences in study designs and protocols may influence the prevalence of ASD estimates, limiting the comparability between countries. The increased incidence and prevalence of pediatric ASD in our country can be explained by improved diagnostic tools, increased awareness of population, better screening programs and changes in diagnostic criteria.

In our study, prevalence of ASD was higher among boys compared to girls. Gender is considered as a prominent factor affecting ASD prevalence. In many studies, it was reported that ASD is 4 to 5 times more common for boys rather than girls1, 7. This can be explained by showing fewer unusual behaviors among girls. Several studies have shown that girls with ASD have better speech behavior and show less abnormalities in social communications than boys, therefore girls might be misdiagnosed or lately diagnosed by ASD1.

The age of diagnosis is another factor affecting ASD prevalence. In our research, the mean age at diagnosis was 6.0 ± 2.4 years and the majority of pediatric ASD patients were children of 0–5 years old. Various studies represent that the age of diagnosis varied from 3 to 10 years between 1990 and 2012, the mean age is reported to be around 5 years (60 months). Moreover, severe forms of ASD can be diagnosed in the preschool years1. There are several factors that might influence age at diagnosis such as type of ASD diagnosis, gender and additional comorbidities. The World Health Organization (2014) states that timely detection and management of ASD in a primary care is a significant part of healthcare system3. Thus, age at diagnosis is a significant risk factor for the development of ASD, since early detection of the disorder leads to early treatment and improvement of later language and cognitive abilities3.

Children with ASD or other developmental disabilities may be at greater risk for psychiatric hospitalization. This can also be observed in our study, where more than half of pediatric ASD patients were hospitalized, 5,912 (59.2%) children. The majority of hospitalizations were due to psychiatric cause (62%). Several studies estimated that children with ASD are 6 times more likely to be psychiatrically hospitalized rather than children without ASD7, 18. Inpatient treatment cannot be used as a treatment of core symptoms of ASD, however it might improve some aggressive behavior and decreases cases of self-injury, as well as leads to the heath improvements of patients with other co-occurring mental health disorders. Since examining risk factors associated with hospitalization might identify potential points of intervention that could decrease the need for children with ASD to be hospitalized, in this research, we examined factors associated with all-cause hospitalizations. It was determined that older age (11–16 years) is significantly associated with higher risk of all-cause hospitalizations. This coincides with the results of the study done in University of Pennsylvania School of Medicine7.

The substantial increase in ASD incidence and hospitalization rates observed over the study period could be explained from different perspectives. Firstly, COVID-19-related healthcare system disruptions may have influenced both service access and documentation patterns. Pandemic-era restructuring of care delivery, delayed outpatient evaluations and shifts in admission criteria could transiently increase hospitalization rates or distort person-time denominators. Consequently, the observed increases likely represent a composite of improved detection, administrative evolution and healthcare system restructuring rather than solely a biologic or epidemiologic expansion of ASD. Moreover, these increases likely reflect changes in healthcare systems and surveillance practices rather than a true escalation in underlying disease frequency alone. Expanded access to developmental screening services, heightened awareness among clinicians and caregivers, and broader diagnostic recognition may have progressively improved case identification. Simultaneously, the continued development and stabilization of the national electronic health registry may have enhanced data completeness, leading to more comprehensive capture of diagnoses and hospital admissions in later years. Administrative and policy-related factors may have further influenced observed trends. Greater standardization of diagnostic coding, evolving reimbursement structures and modifications in clinical documentation requirements could increase recorded ASD diagnoses and psychiatric admissions independent of changes in clinical severity. In addition, shifts in referral patterns or hospitalization thresholds may have altered healthcare utilization over time.

Hospitalized pediatric ASD patients are more likely to be diagnosed with other co-occurring disorders such as GI disorders, epilepsy, cerebral palsy, ADHD, depression and mental retardation10. In our study, gastrointestinal disorders, hydrocephalus and anemia are significantly associated with higher risk of all-cause hospitalizations. This is also consistent with several studies that noted higher rates of medical comorbidities in pediatric ASD patients than non-ASD patients2, 10, 19. For example, in the study by Burns et al. (2023), found that the most commonly observed category of comorbidities among hospitalized pediatric ASD patients was neurological comorbidities, with an overall rate of 11.8%.

Our findings show that the prevalence of GI disorders, and anemia is high in our study population. Other studies have also shown that these childhood diseases are common in children with ASD2, 20. There are several factors that make it more difficult for children with ASD to be diagnosed with GI conditions, including repetitive or atypical behavior and limited language skills. Sensory and behavioral symptoms observed in many children with ASD may mask the child’s ability to report physical symptoms such as abdominal pain or itching. Therefore, our study suggests that physicians working in the fields of gastrointestinal disorders should be aware of the diagnostic features of ASD, have a high index of suspicion for ASD, and have a low threshold for assessing ASD or referring patients are tested for ASD2, 20.

There were several limitations in our study. First, since data collection in our database started from 2014, the data for previous years were not available, therefore there might be underestimation of prevalence rate. Instead, we were able to perform period prevalence rate for the period between 2014 and 2021. Second, since this is a secondary data it might be influenced by the measurement accuracy. There was a lack of information on clinical data, therapies that were used among hospitalized patients and criterias how ASD is diagnosed. Moreover, heredity, parental age, medical conditions during pregnancy (e.g., hypothyroidism), complications of pregnancy (e.g., premature birth, infections, medications used during pregnancy) information was missing which might improve the quality of the study analysis.

Residency information was missing primarily among outpatient records, likely due to differences in data capture between inpatient and outpatient sources. While complete-case sensitivity analyses showed no substantial impact on effect estimates, this missingness highlights a potential limitation in interpreting urban versus rural comparisons, particularly when data are not missing completely at random.

Another limitation of our study is that we considered only the first hospitalization per child and did not analyze recurrent hospitalizations. Consequently, our findings on hospitalization rates may underestimate total healthcare utilization among children with ASD. Additionally, same-day admissions, transfers, and emergency room visits were not captured due to database constraints. Future studies using methods for recurrent events could provide a more comprehensive assessment of hospitalization burden in this population.

Moreover, comorbidity ascertainment is likely biased toward hospitalized children, as hospital encounters may preferentially capture and code comorbid conditions. This raises the possibility of reverse causality or information bias, where comorbidities are recorded because a child is hospitalized rather than truly preceding the hospitalization. To address this, sensitivity analyses restricting comorbidities to diagnoses recorded before the first hospitalization were conducted; hazard ratios were attenuated but remained statistically significant. Nevertheless, this potential bias should be considered when interpreting the observed associations between comorbidities and hospitalization risk.

Third, the observation nature of the study precludes from making any causal conclusions. However, this a first study in Kazakhstan that described incidence and prevalence rates as well as the risk factors associated with all-cause hospitalizations among pediatric ASD cohort in Kazakhstan.

Conclusion

This is a first study that describes incidence and period-prevalence of ASD in children and studied risk factors associated with all-cause hospitalizations in Kazakhstan using a large-scale administrative health data. The results showed an increase in the incidence and period prevalence rates of pediatric ASD cohort during the study period. Older age and comorbidities such as GI disorders, anemia and hydrocephalus are significantly associated with all-cause hospitalizations. Further studies on identifying other associations of clinical data, maternal and neonatal as well as environmental factors associated with pediatric ASD needs to be investigated. With more information available, it will be better able to understand and evaluate the needs of children with ASD for more effective interventions in order to improve the quality of life of children with ASD in Kazakhstan.
