Erdheim‐Chester disease associated with myeloid neoplasm: Clinical features, molecular landscape, and treatment outcomes
Authors: Ambroise Le Pogam, Matthias Papo, Pierre Hirsch, Fleur Cohen‐Aubart, Jean‐François Emile, François Delhommeau, Pierre Duffau, Jérôme Razanamahery, Florence Nguyen‐Khac, Christopher Nunes Gomes, Felicien Triboulet, Jeanne de La Rochefoucauld, Chloé McAvoy, Estibaliz Lazaro, Mehdi Hage‐Sleiman, Damien Roos‐Weil, François Chasset, Vincent Jachiet, Arsène Mekinian, Olivier Fain, Zahir Amoura, Jérôme Hadjadj, Julien Haroche
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractErdheim‐Chester disease (ECD) is frequently associated with clonal hematopoiesis and myeloid neoplasms (MN), but clinical phenotype and response to kinase inhibitors (KI) in this setting remain unclear. We analyzed 67 patients with ECD associated with MN (ECD‐MN) from a French national cohort and assessed ECD treatment response, MN progression, and leukemic transformation. Outcomes were compared with those of 348 patients with ECD without MN. ECD‐MN were characterized by low blast counts and favorable MN prognostic scores. Compared with ECD patients without MN, those with MN were older (median 65 vs. 59 years, P < 0.0001) and had more frequent cardiac (54% vs. 37%, P < 0.01), pulmonary (48% vs. 31%, P < 0.01), gastrointestinal (34% vs. 11%, P < 0.0001), and lymph node involvement (31% vs. 8%, P < 0.0001). KI therapy led to higher ECD response rates at 6 and 12 months compared with pegylated‐interferon α (Peg‐IFN) (96% vs. 58%, P = 0.02). MN progression and leukemic transformation at 6 months occurred, respectively, in 25% and 3% of KI‐treated patients versus 25% and 18% of those treated with Peg‐IFN. After a median follow‐up of 25 (12–38) months on KI, the mutational landscape remained unchanged. Median overall survival was shorter in patients with MN (76 vs. 163 months, P < 0.001). In conclusion, ECD associated with MN represents a distinct clinical entity, marked by broader organ involvement and poorer prognosis. KI therapy provides a superior response without increasing the risk of MN progression or leukemic transformation, supporting its use as a frontline treatment in this population.

INTRODUCTION

Erdheim‐Chester disease (ECD) is a rare adult‐onset multisystem histiocytosis characterized by infiltration of tissues by histiocytes driven by activating mutations in the MAPK (Mitogen‐Activated Protein Kinase) pathway, most frequently BRAF V600E. 1 , 2 ECD involves a broad spectrum of manifestations and frequently overlaps with other histiocytoses in their clinical and/or histopathological presentation, including Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) or Rosai‐Dorfman‐Destombes disease (RDD), resulting in mixed histiocytosis (MH). 3 Targeted kinase inhibitor (KI) such as BRAF and MEK inhibitors (BRAFi and MEKi respectively), are now considered as standard‐of‐care in severe and/or refractory cases. 4 Unlike LCH, which occurs in children and young adults, ECD mainly occurs in middle‐aged patients, with a median age of 56 years (range 29–86 years) at diagnosis. 5 Up to 40% of ECD patients present with clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP), and 10% develop overt myeloid neoplasm (MN) 1 most commonly chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML), myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS), myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN), or overlap syndromes. CHIP and MN associated with ECD appear to exhibit a specific somatic mutational landscape and have a strong prognostic impact, especially when the MAP kinase pathway is affected by mutations in KRAS or NRAS. 1 , 6 , 7 , 8 Although the frequent co‐occurrence of ECD and MN suggests a common clonal hematopoietic origin for both diseases, genomic divergence between tissue‐infiltrating histiocytes and myeloid progenitors has been reported, supporting a model of parallel but independent clonal evolution possibly leading to dissociated responses to KI. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Importantly, the safety and efficacy of KI therapy in patients with concomitant MN remain uncertain. Concerns include potential for differential treatment response, incomplete targeting of myeloid progenitors, and paradoxical progression of JAK2‐ or RAS‐mutated clones under BRAF inhibition. 12 , 13 , 14 Moreover, baseline prognostic scores and risk of leukemic transformation in ECD‐MN are poorly characterized. The aim of this study was to characterize the clinical and molecular features of ECD‐MN and to evaluate the outcomes of KI therapy, including histiocytosis response, safety, hematologic progression, and overall survival.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

Multicenter retrospective study including all adults (≥18 years) with biopsy‐proven ECD or MH associated with or without myeloid neoplasm, referred between May 1990 and May 2024 to the Internal Medicine Departments of Pitié‐Salpêtrière or Saint‐Antoine Hospitals (Paris, France), either as inpatients or during multidisciplinary meetings. All cases underwent centralized pathology review. ECD and MH were diagnosed according to international guidelines. 15 MN were diagnosed and classified according to the 2022 World Health Organization criteria. 16 Patients with lymphoid malignancy were excluded. Patients provided written informed consent (clinical trial registration NCT04437381 [Molecular Targets for the Treatment of Histiocytosis HISTIO‐TARGET]).

Data collection

Medical records were searched to retrieve clinical parameters (sex, age at diagnosis, neoplastic and cardio‐vascular history, and detailed organ involvement), biological parameters (blood count and C‐reactive protein [CRP] at diagnosis and at 6 and 12 months after each treatment line and bone marrow aspiration analysis at diagnosis), histological proof of histiocytosis, as well as treatments received and outcomes. The MN mutational profile was obtained by targeted deep next‐generation sequencing (NGS) analysis performed at a centralized reference laboratory on bone marrow of the exonic regions of 44 genes (details of analyzed genes in Supporting Information S1: Table S1) at MN diagnosis and during treatment. Libraries were obtained from bone marrow DNA using Sophia GeneticsTM custom panel. Sequencing was performed using a MiSeq sequencer (IlluminaTM). Alignments and variant calling were performed using a dedicated pipeline using Sophia DDM software (Sophia Genetics). Coverage was over ×500 in more than 90% of target regions. The minimal threshold of variant allele frequency (VAF) to detect a mutation was 1%. Nontruncating‐variants of uncertain significance (VUS) with VAF above 40% were excluded from the analysis (considered as germline variations or rare polymorphisms of uncertain pathogenicity). Prognosis of hematological malignancies at diagnosis was assessed by IPPS‐R (International Prognostic Scoring System–Revised) and IPPS‐M (International Prognostic Scoring System–Molecular) score for MDS, and CPSS‐M (Chronic Myelomonocytic Leukemia Prognostic Scoring System–Molecular) score for CMML. 17 , 18

Outcomes

Primary outcome for histiocytosis response was evaluated by fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography and computed tomography (FDG‐PET‐CT) at 6 and 12 months, assessed by the positron emission tomography response criteria in solid tumors (PERCIST criteria). 3 , 4 Complete response (CR) was defined by complete resolution of pathologic 18F‐FDG uptake. Partial response (PR) was defined by ≥30% reduction in 18F‐FDG uptake of target lesions. Stable disease (SD) was defined by failure to complete the above criteria for CR or PR. Overall response rate (OR) was defined by the addition of CR and PR rates. Secondary outcome was CRP level at 6 months, with CRP < 5 mg/L assessed as CR, CRP ≥ 5 mg/L but <50% of initial CRP assessed as PR, CRP ≥ 5 mg/L and >50% of initial CRP assessed as SD. We used a composite histiocytosis response criteria defined as the less favorable response (SD, PR, or CR) between CRP response and FDG‐PET‐CT response at 12 months. Overall survival as well as occurrence of adverse events (AEs), including cytopenia, were assessed for all therapeutic regimens. Side effects and their severity were defined according to Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE v6.0). Hematological response was assessed by peripheral blood count and bone marrow aspiration at 6 months if available. MN progression was defined according to blast count or acute myeloid leukemia (AML) transformation in accordance with the 2022 WHO classification. 16

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as median and range, and categorical variables as numbers and percentages. Differences between patient groups were tested with the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test or parametric t‐test for continuous data, and with Fisher's exact test or χ 2 test for categorical data. Mutation VAF evolution on KI was analyzed with a nonparametric Wilcoxon rank test. Survival analyses at last‐follow‐up were performed using Kaplan−Meier curves and the log‐rank test. Statistical analysis was performed using Prism 9® software.

RESULTS

Patients

Among 421 patients with ECD or MH, six had a concomitant lymphoid neoplasm and were excluded from the analysis, and 67 (16%) had MN and were included in the study (Figure 1). Among the 348 patients without MN, CHIP defined as recurrent myeloid mutations was detected in 77 (18%) patients. Thus, a total of 144 (34%) patients had myeloid mutations with or without MN in the whole histiocytosis cohort.

Characteristics of ECD in patients with associated MN

Patients with ECD‐MN were compared to 348 ECD without MN (with or without CHIP) (Table 1). Patients with ECD‐MN were significantly older (68 vs. 59 years; P < 0.0001) and more frequently male (84% vs. 71%; P < 0.05) compared to those without MN. They had significantly more frequent cardiac involvement (54% vs. 37%, P = 0.01; particularly right atrial and pericardial), pulmonary involvement (48% vs. 31%, P = 0.01; mainly pleural), renal and perirenal lesions (84% vs. 64%, P = 0.004), lymphadenopathy (31% vs. 8%, P = 0.0001), and hepatodigestive manifestations (34% vs. 11%, P = 0.0001; mainly hepatic hilum infiltration, mesenteric panniculitis, or cholangitis), compared to patients without MN. There was no significant difference in the proportion of MH or in the prevalence of BRAF/MAP2K1 mutations between ECD‐MN and ECD patients.

Hematological characteristics

Among the 67 ECD‐MN, 34 (51%) had CMML, 18 (27%) had MDS, and 15 (22%) had MPN. ECD was diagnosed prior to MN in 67% of cases. Characteristics of MN are described in Table 2. ECD‐MN had a median age of 68 (42–88) years at MN diagnosis, and 84% were male. Anemia (hemoglobin <13 g/dL for male and <12 g/dL for female) was present in 53% of patients with a mean hemoglobin level of 11.3 g/dL. Monocytosis (monocyte count >1 G/L and/or >10% of leukocytes) was present in 36 (54%) of cases, with a mean monocyte count of 1.4 × 109/L (15.6% of leukocytes). The mean monocyte count in myeloid neoplasms other than CMML was 0.7 × 109/L (9.6%) (data not shown). All patients with CMML had CMML‐1 except one with CMML‐2; 31 (91%) were myelodysplastic, and three were proliferative. Median CPSS‐M prognosis score was 1, suggesting a good prognosis. Among patients with MDS, 12 (66%) had low‐blast MDS with a median IPSS‐R score of 2 and a median IPSS‐M score of −1, suggesting a good prognosis. Among patients with MPN, eight (53%) had myelofibrosis (MF), seven (40%) essential thrombocythemia (ET), and one (7%) polycythemia vera (PV). Among the 67 patients, 12 (18%) had chromosomal abnormalities, and the most frequent abnormalities were chromosomal deletions (Table 2). TET2 mutations predominated in both ECD with MN (n = 32, 47%) and ECD with CHIP (n = 28, 36%) groups (Figure 2); and the mean number of mutations per patient was respectively 2.1 (range 1–6) and 1.7 (range 1–5) (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1). The following most prevalent mutations in ECD‐MN were KRAS (7%) and ASXL1 (7%), whereas those in ECD with CHIP were DNMT3A (20%) and ASXL1 (9%) (Figure 2B and Supporting Information S1: Figure S2A‐B). Mutations in SRSF2, TP53, KRAS, and NRAS were significantly more prevalent in the ECD‐MN group than in the ECD‐CHIP group (respectively 18 vs. 5%, P = 0.02, 10% vs. 3%, P = 0.05, 15% vs. 3%, P = 0.01, 15% vs. 4%, P = 0.04). Conversely, DNMT3A mutations were more frequent in ECD‐CHIP than in ECD‐MN (26% vs. 7.5%, P = 0.004). Furthermore, there was a significant enrichment in mutations involving signaling pathways in ECD‐MN compared to ECD‐CHIP (7.5% vs. 4%, P = 0.003), including NRAS, KRAS, CBL, NF1, PTPN11, ETNK1, SH2B3, and CSF3R (Figure 3). Mutational landscape was similar across CMML and MDS, with a predominance of TET2 (33% for both), ASXL1 (respectively 7% and 10%), SRSF2 (respectively 10% and 5%), and KRAS (10% and 7%) mutations (Supporting Information S1: Figure S2C‐D). In contrast, MPN patients had a predominance of TET2 (28%), JAK2 (20%), NF1 (10%), CBL (8%), and CALR (8%) mutations (Supporting Information S1: Figure S2E), and a lower frequency of NRAS mutations (7% in CMML, 5% in MDS, and 2.5% in MPN). Of the 67 patients, 12 presented with chromosomal abnormalities. Detailed mutational data are available in Supporting Information S1: Table S2.

Treatment outcome

A total of 104 histiocytosis treatment lines were administered to 61 (91%) patients with ECD‐MN. These included 16 BRAFi lines (vemurafenib: n = 15, dabrafenib: n = 1), 27 MEKi lines (cobimetinib: n = 23, trametinib: n = 2, binimetinib: n = 2), eight combined BRAFi + MEKi regimens, and 30 pegylated‐interferon α lines (Peg‐IFN) (including 17 treated exclusively with Peg‐IFN who had never received any other therapeutic lines). Peg‐IFN was always prescribed as first‐line therapy, whereas MEKi, BRAFi, and combined BRAFi + MEKi were used as first‐line treatment in 48%, 44% and 25% of cases, respectively (Table 3). Among the 45 (67%) patients whose diagnosis of ECD preceded the diagnosis of MN, 24 (53%), five (11%), seven (16%), and two (4%) were receiving IFN, BRAFi, MEKi, or combined BRAFi+MEKi therapy at MN diagnosis, respectively, while seven (16%) patients were not receiving any treatment prior to the diagnosis of MN. Other treatment strategies included targeted therapies (IL‐1 receptor inhibitors [n = 4], JAK inhibitors [n = 4], anti‐TNFα monoclonal antibodies [n = 3]), hypomethylating agents (azacitidine, n = 6), vinblastine (n = 1), cladribine (n = 4), and one allogeneic stem cell transplantation. Histiocytosis manifestations were the treatment indication in the majority of the cohort (90%), while MN was the leading indication for treatment in seven (10%) patients (six treated with azacitidine and one with allogeneic stem cell transplantation). Thirty‐six (90%) patients on KI reached OR (11 (28%) CR and 25 (62%) PR) at FDG‐PET‐CT evaluation at 6 months, whereas only eight (38%) patients on peg‐IFN reached PR and none reached CR (P = 0.002). OR obtention rate according to the composite histiocytosis criteria was significantly higher with KI (BRAFi, MEKi, BRAFi + MEKi) than with Peg‐IFN therapy (96% [CR in 34%, PR in 62%] vs. 58% [CR in 23%, PR in 35%], respectively, P = 0.02). CR was more frequently achieved with MEKi than with BRAFi, both in terms of CRP response rates at 6 months (45% vs. 23%), FDG‐PET‐CT response rates at 6 months (35% vs. 8%), and composite response at 12 months (42% vs. 23%) (Table 3 and Figure 4). Hematological progression or AML transformation at 6 months occurred in 12/41 (29%) patients treated with KI and 11/28 (39%) patients treated with Peg‐IFN therapy (P = 0.2). Baseline mutational landscape was similar between patients presenting hematological progression or AML transformation and the rest of the ECD‐MN cohort (Supporting Information S1: Figure S3). In the subgroup of ECD‐MN with NRAS, KRAS, and/or JAK2 mutations (six patients including two NRAS, two KRAS, two JAK2, and two NRAS and KRAS) on BRAFi, no progression or transformation of MN was observed (Supporting Information S1: Table S3). Blood counts (hemoglobin, platelets, monocytes, leukocytes) remained similar at 6 months across all regimens (Supporting Information S1: Figure S4). Among six patients treated with azacitidine, two achieved hematological response, recorded as hematological SD. One patient was treated with allogeneic stem cell transplantation as a fourth‐line therapy due to AML transformation and exhibited partial histiocytosis response at both 6 and 12 months.

Mutational landscape evolution

Follow‐up data on myeloid mutational landscape were available for 23 patients, including 20 treated with KI (BRAFi n = 5, MEKi n = 10, or BRAFi + MEKi n = 5), two with azacitidine, and one with Peg‐IFN. Median time from treatment initiation to genetic evaluation was 25 (12–38) months for patients treated with KI (31 months with MEKi, 22 months with BRAFi, and 16 months in cases of dual therapy). Among the most prevalent mutations (TET2, ASXL1, KRAS, NRAS, and CBL), VAF remained stable with KI, except for a slight but significant increase in SRSF2 mutation number (from seven to nine mutations) and VAF (from a mean of 20% to 25%) (Supporting Information S1: Figure S5) without hematological progression or histiocytosis worsening after a median follow‐up of 29 (9–38) months. On KI, three KRAS mutations (one with BRAFi + MEKi, one with MEKi, and one with BRAFi) and two NRAS mutations (one with BRAFi and two with MEKi + BRAFi) emerged during follow‐up treatment without hematological progression or histiocytosis worsening after a median follow‐up of 28 (23–35) months for KRAS and 34 (12–45) months for NRAS. Conversely, one NRAS mutation disappeared under MEKi. Detailed mutational landscape evolution is available in Supporting Information S1: Table S4.

Safety

AEs are detailed in Supporting Information S1: Table S5. Among patients treated with MEKi, 55% experienced AEs, including cytopenia, folliculitis, rhabdomyolysis, severe infection, and erythroderma (each 7%–11%). Serious AEs (SAEs) occurred in 33%, leading to discontinuation in 26%, including one treatment‐related death due to urinary sepsis. Three patients were successfully switched to another MEKi without recurrence. Two patients developed T‐cell lymphoma during MEKi, one of whom died from angioimmunoblastic T‐cell lymphoma (AITL), and another from gastrointestinal T‐cell lymphoma NOS, without clonal evolution. Among BRAFi‐treated patients, 75% experienced AEs (mainly cytopenia, cutaneous neoplasms, photosensitivity, hepatitis, erythroderma, pancreatitis, granulomatosis, and rhabdomyolysis). SAEs occurred in 50% and led to discontinuation in 44%, including one death from heart failure. With combined MEKi/BRAFi therapy, 88% had AEs (notably cytopenia, photosensitivity, folliculitis, hepatitis, rhabdomyolysis, and severe infection), with SAEs in 38% and discontinuation in 25%. Twelve Peg‐IFN‐treated patients (40%) reported AEs, mainly asthenia (33%). SAEs occurred in 13%, all leading to discontinuation, but without treatment‐related deaths. SAEs leading to discontinuation were more frequent with KIs than Peg‐IFN (45% vs. 13%, P = 0.07), and more frequent with BRAFi than MEKi (50% vs. 33%, P = 0.03).

Outcome

Median overall survival was significantly shorter in patients with ECD‐MN compared to those without MN (76 vs. 163 months, P < 0.0001) (Figure 5). After a median follow‐up of 44 months (23–90), median survival was 70 months in patients with CMML, 68 months in patients with MDS, and 116 months in patients with MPN without a significant difference between MN subtypes. Among ECD‐MN, median overall survival was 96 months with Peg‐IFN (n = 17), 70 months with MEKi (n = 24), 73 months with BRAF + MEKi dual therapy (n = 6), 61 months with azacitidine (n = 6), and 12 months in untreated patients (n = 6) (Supporting Information S1: Table S6). Overall survival was similar between patients treated with Peg‐IFN and those treated with KI (P = 0.86). Under KI, death was caused by ECD in 4/10 (40%), and by MN in 5/10 (50%).

DISCUSSION

We performed a multicenter retrospective study highlighting clinical, mutational, and therapeutic characteristics of a large cohort of ECD patients with MN. Compared with ECD patients without MN, ECD‐MN patients were older and experienced more frequent cardiac, interstitial, or pleural lung involvement, perirenal involvement, lymph node enlargement, and a broad spectrum of hepatic and digestive manifestations. As previously reported, 1 MN consisted of CMML (51%), MDS (27%), and/or MPN (22%) with an increased frequency compared to the age‐matched population. 19 MN displayed favorable CPSS and IPSS prognostic scores at diagnosis, with few proliferative CMML cases and low bone marrow or peripheral blast counts. However, overall survival was shorter in ECD‐MN compared to ECD patients without MN (76 vs. 163 months), without a significant difference across MN subtypes.

In comparison with ECD‐CHIP patients, the mutational landscape in ECD‐MN revealed a predominance of TET2, ASXL1, and SRSF2 mutations, as well as mutations in signaling pathway genes (i.e., KRAS, NRAS, and JAK2), which are commonly encountered in CMML and MDS. In ECD‐CHIP patients, classical TET2 and DNMT3A mutations were the most frequent and often isolated, while DNMT3A mutations were less common or consistently observed in association with other mutations among ECD‐MN. Loss of DNMT3A mutation may suggest clonal replacement by other mutations, such as TET2, SRSF2, KRAS, or NRAS in ECD‐MN. This supports a potential role for these emerging clones in the development of myeloid neoplasms and in shaping the phenotypic profile of ECD‐MN, consistent with the hypothesis of a common hematopoietic progenitor for both MN and histiocytosis. 20 , 21 Treatment was tailored to the histiocytosis manifestations in the majority of ECD‐MN. Conversely, treatment was rarely driven by underlying MN, with only six patients treated with azacitidine and one patient with allogeneic stem cell transplantation. At 6 months, OR was significantly higher with KI compared to Peg‐IFN (respectively 80% vs. 38%). These findings are consistent with the largest published ECD cohort without MN, which reported a 77% response rate with KI and 29% with Peg‐IFN. 22 At 6 and 12 months, CR was more frequently achieved with MEKi than with BRAFi or Peg‐IFN, and Peg‐IFN consistently failed to induce CR at 6 months. However, CRP levels significantly decreased in patients receiving Peg‐IFN, likely reflecting its preferential use in patients with less severe disease and its predominantly immunomodulatory or anti‐inflammatory effect targeting downstream inflammation rather than clonal histiocyte burden, which is consistent with the absence of CR on FDG‐PET‐CT. Importantly, this treatment allocation constitutes an inherent selection bias that must be acknowledged when comparing Peg‐IFN with KI, as Peg‐IFN was exclusively used as first‐line therapy, whereas MEKi and BRAFi were used as first‐line therapy in only 48% and 44% of cases, respectively, and more often in later lines in patients with more refractory or long‐standing disease, potentially contributing to higher rates of AEs and less favorable prognosis despite a better metabolic response.

Interestingly, the mutational landscape did not significantly change during KI treatment. Notably, there was no significant VAF increase of RAS variants under BRAFi. Indeed, it was previously reported that in RAF wild‐type cells, RAF inhibitors paradoxically activate the RAS/MEK/ERK signaling pathway. 12 This paradoxical activation may lead to the occurrence of sarcoidosis, 13 as well as cutaneous 14 or hematologic neoplasms, 23 especially in cases of RAS but also JAK2 mutations. 6 These events may be prevented by downstream blockade of the molecular cascade. Preferential use of MEKi in cutaneous cancers has demonstrated both efficacy and a favorable tolerance profile. 24 Of note, we observed a slight but significant increase in the number and VAF of SRSF2 mutations without any impact on hematological or clinical outcomes, within the available follow‐up period. Furthermore, progression of MN or AML transformation occurred less frequently on KI than on Peg‐IFN, with a similar follow‐up duration between the two regimens. No progression or transformation of AML was observed in patients with NRAS and/or KRAS mutations treated with BRAFi. Baseline mutational landscape was similar between patients experiencing AML transformation or MN progression and the rest of the cohort, suggesting that baseline mutational landscape may have limited predictive value for progression risk assessment under treatment. Notably, ECD with CHIP, including those harboring KRAS or NRAS mutations, did not progress to MN during follow‐up, although systematic CHIP screening was not standard practice in the absence of cytopenias, and VAF monitoring was unavailable, preventing precise documentation of potential progression from CHIP to MN. Prospective monitoring of myeloid mutational profiles over time will be crucial to determine whether changes in VAF or emergence of mutations are KI‐induced or reflect the natural evolution of clonal hematopoiesis. KI‐related AEs were numerous (67% AEs) and often severe (39% SAEs), leading to temporary or definitive treatment discontinuation in 31% of cases. These AEs were mainly cytopenias, for which KI causality is uncertain due to underlying MN, skin reactions (folliculitis, severe allergy, photosensitivity), and rhabdomyolysis on MEKi. Incidence of treatment‐related AEs in the current study is consistent with data from the LOVE Study cohort, 4 with fewer skin reactions possibly due to a reporting bias (in our cohort, respectively, 25% and 19% on BRAFi and MEKi vs. in the LOVE Study, 65% and 60% on BRAFi and MEKi, respectively). Following SAEs, several patients successfully switched from one MEKi to another and did not experience new AEs.

Two patients treated with MEKi developed T‐cell lymphomas, one categorized as NOS and the other as angioimmunoblastic T‐cell lymphoma. So far, there is no published data reporting the emergence of lymphomas related to MEKi treatment. This report prompts inquiries into the potential causative role of the drug, and raises questions as to the possibility of a transdifferentiation mechanism involving histiocytic cells, 21 or a shared clonal hematopoietic origin between histiocytosis and lymphoma. Unfortunately, genomic data regarding lymphomas were not available, limiting hypothetical mechanism insights. Overall, our results suggest that AE incidence and severity were similar to previously described cohorts of histiocytosis without MN, supporting the safety and efficacy of BRAFi, MEKi, and dual therapy in this population.

This study has several limitations. Its small sample size, due to ECD‐MN rarity, limits statistical power and does not allow subgroup comparisons, particularly in terms of therapeutic response and survival. Also, MEKi were often preferred over BRAFi based on clinician preference due to existing data supporting a risk of clonal disease progression on BRAFi. 12 , 13 , 14 , 23 , 25 Therefore, progression rates on BRAFi in our cohort may be artificially low. Moreover, due to the retrospective nature of data collection, follow‐up and hematological reassessments were not standardized, leading to heterogeneous and sometimes missing data. Nevertheless, it represents the largest known cohort specifically focused on ECD‐MN, 1 , 6 yielding previously unreported insights into the clinical, mutational, and therapeutic characteristics of this population, along with reassuring data on KI‐related MN progression risk.

In conclusion, patients with ECD‐MN display a distinct clinical phenotype, characterized by mild hematologic involvement but reduced overall survival. KI showed higher clinical efficacy than Peg‐IFN without evidence of promoting molecular or hematological progression or transformation. These findings support the use of KI as a safe and effective therapeutic option in ECD‐MN.
