Orthodontic Treatment of Anterior Open Bite Caused by a Car Accident
Authors: Naoto Hirose, Makoto Yanoshita, Sayuri Nishiyama, Naoki Kubo, Daiki Kita, Eri Tsuboi, Kotaro Tanimoto
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This article outlines the orthodontic treatment of a 21-year-old female patient with an open bite and temporomandibular joint disorders (TMDs) that developed after a severe car accident. The treatment plan utilized temporary anchorage devices (TADs) for upper molar intrusion to correct the open bite without resorting to orthognathic surgery. Over a period of 3 years, the treatment achieved a stable occlusion, normalized molar relationships, and improved esthetics. Despite the persistent of TMD symptoms, the patient experienced enhanced jaw movement and a reduction in abnormal chewing cycles. The combination of orthodontic treatment with TADs and splint therapy contributed to successfully restoring both esthetic and functional aspects. This case underscores the efficacy of TADs in addressing trauma-induced malocclusions, highlighting the potential for minimal intervention to achieve comprehensive treatment outcomes.

Introduction

Trauma to the craniofacial region, including traffic accidents frequently gives rise to malocclusions or occlusal discrepancies. While minor occlusal changes can be managed through prosthodontic interventions, the presence of substantial malocclusions necessitates orthodontic treatment.[12]

Open bite is one of the most challenging malocclusions to treat. Either conventional orthodontic treatment only with brackets and wires alone or that combined with orthognathic surgery is generally applied; however, it is considered hard to acquire ideal occlusion after orthodontic treatments and keep long-term stability in the retention period.[3] This report presents an orthodontic case of open bite with temporomandibular joint disorders (TMDs) caused by facial trauma on a car accident was presented in this report. This report aims to introduce a way to treat an open bite case with temporary anchorage devices (TADs) without orthognathic surgery and shows some tips to improve malocclusion and oral functions, including TMJ functions.

Case Report

The patient was a 21-year and 6-month-old female at the initial visit. She got involved in a car accident 4 months before an initial visit to our clinic and had injured severely on the whole body besides the maxillofacial regions. After the car accident, she underwent several reconstructive surgeries of the maxilla and orbital bone at Hiroshima University Hospital. Fortunately, there was minimal impact on the teeth and alveolar bone; however, due to the trauma to the TMJ, the overall occlusion was distorted, resulting in an open bite. It presented a large overjet of 5.0 mm and overbite of −0.5 mm, and Angle Class II molar relationship on both sides. It also showed arch length discrepancies (ALDs) of −1.5 mm on the maxillary arch and 0 mm on the mandibular arch, which caused mild crowding on the upper dentition [Figure 1a].

Treatment plan

The treatment plan involves no need for premolar extractions due to minimal ALD and proper anteroposterior positioning and inclination of the upper and lower incisors. However, extraction of the impacted two upper third molars is recommended to assist in controlling the position of the first and second upper molars. TADs (ISA orthodontic implants, BIODENT, Tokyo) will be placed in the center of the palate to facilitate impaction and distal movement of the upper molars, encouraging the mandible to move in a counterclockwise direction and correct the open bite.

Treatment progress

The treatment was performed with multibracket appliances and a TAD whose diameter was 2.0 mm, and thread length was 6.0 mm and implanted in the middle of the palate [Figure 3a]. Next, a 0.018ʺ × 0.025ʺ slot multibracket system was placed on both arches. Leveling and alignment were treated on maxillomandibular dentition with the use of 0.014ʺ and 0.016ʺ nickel–titanium wires. After completion of the initial alignment, the wires were being exchanged into thicker-sized ones stepwise. Then, it was finally replaced with 0.016ʺ ×0.022ʺ stainless steel archwires. Anterior open bite and Class II molar relationships were simultaneously corrected by intrusion and distalization of maxillary molars using TADs and tied elastics [Figure 3b-d]. When the orthodontic objectives had been achieved 3 years after starting the treatment, all of the brackets and apparatuses were removed. A stabilization splint was chosen in the maxilla both as an orthodontic retainer and for preventing TMJ damage caused by bruxism during nighttime. For the mandible, a Begg-type retainer was used.

Treatment results

The patient’s orthodontic concerns were fully resolved after a 3-year orthodontic treatment. The posttreatment facial photographs showed good facial proportions. The intraoral photographs presented the Class I molar relationship on both sides as a result of molar distalization with TADs. Proper overjet (3.0 mm) and overbite (1.5 mm) were also achieved. A functional occlusion with stable posterior support and adequate anterior guidance was established [Figure 4a].

(a) Posttreatment extraoral and intraoral photographs of the patient. (b) Posttreatment panoramic, lateral cephalometric radiograph, and cephalometric tracing

The comparison of cephalometric analysis between pretreatment and posttreatment results indicates that the maxillary incisors were slightly retroclined, and the mandible rotated slightly counterclockwise and moved forward, improving the Class II skeletal pattern. The posttreatment panoramic X-ray images showed that the maxillary and mandibular dentition had parallel roots with no apparent root or bone resorption [Figure 4b].

Clicking sounds persisted in both TMJs; however, no pains existed upon completion of treatment. In addition, the unstable open–close path of the mouth improved. MR images revealed persistent irreducible anterior disc displacement on both sides, as well as bilateral joint effusion detected as high intensity in the upper joint cavity surrounding the discs; however, no deformity of the mandibular condyles was observed [Figure 5]. We evaluated pre- and posttreatment jaw movements using the Gnatho-Hexagraph III JM-3000® (GC, Tokyo, Japan) [Figure 6]. The range of jaw movements during mastication had widened after the treatment. In addition, the error index (EI), calculated as a ratio of abnormal chewing cycles to total chewing strokes using the formula: EI (%) = number of abnormal chewing/total chewing × strokes 100 (Nakata et al., 2007), improved by 20% after the treatment from 50% at the start of the treatment.

Discussion

Open bite, where develops depending on skeletal and dental causes, is one of the difficult malocclusions to treat in orthodontics. Furthermore, there are some difficulties in maintaining the occlusion even in the retention phase.[4] There are several methods of treating open bite with multibracket appliances. First, the treatments are divided into two patterns, with or without orthognathic surgery.[5] In the cases with mild open bite, less overbite is corrected by extrusion of incisors with up and down elastics hooked on both incisors in a conventional manner.[6] On the other hand, for severe cases, molar intrusion by use of TADs is known to be more effective for the correction of open bite.[7] The usage of up and down elastics during orthodontic treatment is prone to cause extrusion of incisors and often ends in gingival recessions. TADs are not only very useful for the correction of Class II cases to distalize upper molars[89] but also for the correction of anterior open bite due to the vertical molar intrusion. By intruding the molars, occlusal interferences in the molar region are eliminated, consequently inducing an automatic counterclockwise rotation of the mandible, leading to improvement in the open bite.[10] It is considered that these attributes are from anteroposterior allowable range of condyle position in the articular fossa. The tolerance could contribute to the correction of Class II malocclusion if it works in an effective way.[11] In this case, an average of 2 mm molar distalization and 2.5 mm molar intrusion was achieved on both sides. The improvement of overjet, overbite, and skeletal ClassII pattern were due to the combination of molar distalization and intrusion, as well as the lingual inclination of the anterior teeth [Figure 7]. The combination of TADs implanted in the palate and TPA is highly effective for molar intrusion, leading to open bite correction. Generally, molar intrusion from TADs implanted on the palatal side tends to cause lingual inclination of the molars. In this case, TPA was used to prevent the unexpected molar inclination. Except for cases where oral habits have been successfully controlled, open bite tends to relapse during the retention period in general.[312] Solid retention with appropriate retainers, such as invisible retainer, should be needed to prevent vertical relapse after finishing orthodontic treatment.[13]

In this case, the abnormal change of occlusion is presumably attributed to the trauma to the TMJ, which caused the displacement of the mandibular condyle within the mandibular fossa. Fortunately, TMJ symptoms such as pains did not worsen during treatment. Furthermore, no progression of TMJ disorder was observed in the MR images taken at the end of treatment. The consistent use of splints during orthodontic treatment in patients with TMDs can also be effective. Keeping the TMJ at rest with a splint may prevent the onset of TMJ symptoms due to the rapid changes in occlusion caused by orthodontic treatment. This is particularly effective when there is nocturnal bruxism. In general, it is difficult to use splints during tooth movement in mutibracket treatment, but in this case, crowding in the lower dentition was not severe, and the straightening of the mandibular dentition was achieved relatively in the early treatment period, allowing for the long-term use of a splint in the mandible. Furthermore, a stabilization splint is also beneficial in determining the unstable jaw position of TMJ disorder patients. After using the stabilization splint, the jaw position often stabilizes in a muscularly balanced position, which can be useful in setting treatment goals for orthodontic therapy. In this case, during the latter half of the treatment, the patient was instructed to wear a dental splint on the lower dentition to prevent abnormal force from bruxism at night. The click sounds and the lack of smoothness of the jaw movement were improved when the retention period started [Figure 6]. It has been still unclear which was more effective, stable occlusion acquired by orthodontic treatment or dental splint therapy itself;[14] however, there is a possibility that both methods were synergistically effective.

In this case, by carefully addressing TMJ symptoms and the instability of occlusion, successful utilization of TADs enabled achieving both esthetic and functional restoration of occlusion. Furthermore, reconstructing occlusion through orthodontic treatment allowed us to avoid restorative interventions, thereby achieving minimal intervention as well.

Conclusion

The use of TADs allowed correction of the open bite without the need for tooth extraction and orthognathic surgery. The stable occlusion achieved through orthodontic treatment not only improves esthetics but also oral function, including TMJ.
