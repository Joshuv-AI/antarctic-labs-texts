Social Determinants of Cardiovascular Health in the Irish Traveller at Individual, Interpersonal, Community, and Societal Levels
Authors: Bandar Alhaidari, Frances Meagher, Michael J Daly
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSocial determinants, such as economic, cultural, and racial factors, are significant contributors to health disparities among patients with cardiovascular disease, particularly in disadvantaged populations. This report outlines the strategies taken at a single institution to educate medical students about the social determinants that affect the health care needs of the Irish Traveller community.Key words: case-based learning, Irish Traveller, marginalized communities, social determinants of health

An understanding of the social determinants of health is fundamental to medical practice, as it promotes holistic patient care,1 addresses health disparities,2 fosters collaboration across disciplines, and enhances public health strategies.3 The Irish Traveller ethnocultural group have a unique cultural identity within Ireland and a history of marginalization that directly affects health outcomes.4 Discrimination, racism, and social stigma are significant barriers to health care access for them, exacerbating health inequalities. Training health care providers to recognize these biases and act upon the social determinants of health may lead to more effective, culturally sensitive health care and improve health outcomes in marginalized communities worldwide.

At the Royal College of Surgeons in Ireland University of Medicine and Health Sciences, we use case-based learning (CBL) activities to foster holistic, patient-centered care. In our cardiovascular module, delivered in the first-year of a 5-year undergraduate degree program, students go on a 6-year narrative journey with John, a fictional man from the Irish Traveller community who has cardiovascular disease. Each of our 8 CBL sessions use script-based narratives and student-led role-play to inform facilitated small-group discussions. As a part of one CBL session, we asked each small group (n = 30; 12 students per group) to submit a written response to the following prompt: “Compare and contrast the Published Figure [A] with the Pavee Point Community Quilt [B]. Discuss how the factors identified by both might apply to the Traveller Community and other marginalized groups.” Both published figure [A]5 and the Pavee Point Community Quilt [B]6 are shown in Figure 1. The Pavee Point Community Quilt was developed and produced by the Pavee Point Primary Health Care for Travellers Project.6

Qualitative analysis of responses to our prompt identified 4 themes: 1) there are limitations in standardized health communication; 2) community-centered design enhances cultural relevance in health communication; 3) there is a mismatch between health communication and structural barriers; and 4) visual tools require balancing accessibility and clarity.

Limitations in Standardized Health Communication

Student groups noted that although published figure [A]was structured, multilevel, and clearly organized, its effort to be universally applicable resulted is a loss of cultural specificity. They felt the figure's strength lay in its systematic design, linking individual, interpersonal, and societal factors; however, many groups reflected that this approach risked neglecting the distinct needs of communities such as the Irish Travellers. As one group wrote “This is where [A]'s strengths lie, there is a clear connection made between the individual, interpersonal, and community/societal levels.” Another group stated that “[A] aims to encompass the specific issues of as many different communities as possible, and so ends up less specific to the needs of the Traveller population.” Students felt that the effort toward generalizability may limit [A]'s influence on communities who face unique barriers specific to their culture.

Groups contrasted figure [A] with the Pavee Point Community Quilt [B], stating that although both identified key barriers, [B] foregrounded the experiences of Traveller communities: “Both identify key health risks, but [B] addresses the [Traveller] community specifically while [A] addresses universal discriminants.” One group remarked on the absence of monetary symbols in [B], interpreting this to reflect a different set of communal values: “Interestingly, there is no symbol on [B] representing money—reflecting that financial gain is not central to Traveller values, where trade, mutual care, and community carry more weight than income.” This contrast reveals the cultural disconnect that can exist between traditional health care systems and marginalized communities.

Community-Centered Design Enhances Cultural Relevance in Health Communication

Many groups suggested that [B]'s community origin gave it greater relevance to the Traveller community when compared with [A]. Although [A] was recognized to be informative and clinically structured, [B] was felt to be more engaging to the Traveller community owing to it being grounded in a shared experience. One group noted, “[B] is tailored to an audience that fostered a deep sense of community versus [A] which is more individualized,” reflecting the idea that collectiveness can enhance receptivity. Another group added, “[B] was made by [the wife of John in our CBL case] and other Traveller women—people who the community trust and relate to. It's built from a shared experience, for shared benefit.” These comments recognize that trust, familiarity, and a shared experience can increase the effectiveness of health communication in marginalized groups such as Irish Travellers.

Moreover, the cultural alignment of [B] was viewed as empowering: “[B] offers a more culturally relevant empowerment for the Traveller community to adhere to, as it resonates with them better, rather than the very clinical interface of [A]. The emotional appeal of [B] could provide better engagement,” one group reflected. The contrast was described as one between a “grassroots and community-driven” tool and a more “academic and clinical” resource, each exploring the social determinants of cardiovascular health through different lenses.

Mismatch Between Health Communication and Structural Barriers

Student groups noted that health communication tools often contain unaddressed assumptions about communities' access to care and the structural realities they may face. Figure [A], although comprehensive and well suited for general application, was perceived as implicitly assuming stable access to health care services and an ability to engage with them. In contrast, [B] was felt to foreground questions on access. One group stated, “[A] seems to assume prior access to health care and how to utilize it, whereas [B] informs on how to supply yourself with the basic health care to survive.” Several groups stated that [B]'s focus on social determinants aligned more strongly with communities that may be facing systemic exclusion. One comment remarked that “[B] applies to the Traveller Community and other marginalized groups, such as Canada's Indigenous peoples” in its attention to how structural barriers shape both health outcomes and engagement with care. Another group framed it plainly: “[A] focuses on adherence—the assumption that if patients are given enough information and support, they will comply. [B] focuses on access—asking whether communities like Travellers are even in a position to engage with the health care system at all.” The responses within the student groups suggest that when health communication presumes access and readiness to act, it risks excluding those for whom such presumptions do not hold. Students emphasized that effective, inclusive tools require both cultural relevance and structural awareness.

Visual Tools Require Balancing Accessibility and Clarity

Students consistently noted the potential of visual communications to bridge literacy gaps and engage diverse groups. The Pavee Point Community Quilt [B] was praised for its pictorial format, described as more inclusive for communities with limited and/or informal education. One group stated that [B] could “be used to teach varying age groups since pictures are a more universal form of communication,” and another group remarked that “the visual symbolism of [B] made the health message more accessible to Travellers, especially given the historically limited access to formal education and [low levels of] medical trust.” At the same time, students acknowledged potential trade-offs, such as how an absence of text can lead to ambiguity: “there are zero words,” one group noted, “leaving a lot of interpretation up to the individual,” thereby identifying the potential for misinterpretation unless symbols are explicitly explained. This insight was particularly evident in the case of John: “He cannot read [A]. He doesn't have a phone. He doesn't fully understand his diagnosis or the plan for recovery. He is emotionally overwhelmed and unsure how to engage—not because he doesn't care, but because the model wasn't made with him in mind. [A] gives him knowledge, but it's [B] that offers him belonging.”

Discussion

Overall, our students agreed that an understanding of the social determinants of health is essential in providing holistic medical care. By the end of the CBL program, they had developed a more nuanced view of patient health, recognizing that health outcomes are influenced by factors beyond biological or clinical parameters, such as socioeconomic status, education, and living conditions1; consequently, this led to a more empathic approach, as they appreciated the challenges their patients may face in accessing care.

As we know, social determinants are significant contributors to health disparities,5 with economic and social factors exacerbating cardiovascular disease risks, particularly in disadvantaged populations.2 Educating medical students about these disparities enables them to tailor their clinical approaches to address inequities and advocate for better health care policies. Embedding an appreciation for social determinants within medical curricula cultivates a generation of physicians who are not only aware of individual health factors but also actively engage in addressing the systemic issues affecting cardiovascular health. This transformative learning prepares students to become advocates for equitable health care, ultimately improving health outcomes in diverse populations.
