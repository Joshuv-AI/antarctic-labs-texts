Acute Aortic Syndromes
Authors: Michel Pompeu Sá
Published: 2026

Acute aortic syndromes (AASs) are medical emergencies encompassing life-threatening conditions such as type A (TAAD) and type B aortic dissections, intramural hematoma, and penetrating aortic ulcers. The management of AAS presents unique challenges; beyond timely diagnosis and postoperative care, it requires a high degree of surgical expertise to manage complex aortic anatomy and repair fragile dissected tissues. These difficulties are further aggravated by the presence of potentially fatal complications such as malperfusion syndrome, cardiac tamponade, and stroke. Considering these aspects, AASs are entities that warrant specialization, centralization, and collaboration (Figure 1).

Acute Aortic Syndromes: A Call for Specialization, Centralization, and Collaboration

Recently, a joint guideline1 published by the Society of Thoracic Surgeons and the European Association for Cardiothoracic Surgery recognized the aorta as an organ in its own right due to its unique function and critical importance.

In JACC: Case Reports, we highlight aortic cases with multiple presentations, assessments, and strategies adopted for treatment. Advancements in endovascular therapies, especially in complex aortic procedures, underscore the need for specialized training. At the same time, the ever-growing application of endovascular techniques led to a lack of enough development of skills to the practice of traditional open aortic surgery when they are needed. In this scenario, aortic fellowship programs can offer high-volume exposure to diversified cases, research, mentorship, and opportunities for professional network.

Furthermore, as the field of cardiovascular surgery advances, a particular shift toward centralized and specialized care with the aim of optimizing patient-relevant outcomes has been observed. This centralization of expertise permeates throughout facets of cardiovascular surgery, and multidisciplinary aortic centers of excellence located in metropolitan areas currently exist throughout the United States and Europe, which has led to a centralization of the surgical exposure of the trainees.

This process of centralization seems to be beneficial for patients. A study demonstrated lower mortality at high-volume hospitals for overall repair of aortic dissections.2 However, despite significant advances in perioperative care, surgical techniques, and prosthetic devices, TAAD repair remains associated with a high mortality rate, more than 20% in many centers.3, 4, 5 As endovascular technology advances6, 7, 8 in the setting of AAS and practice patterns consequently change, analyses should focus on understanding the balance between procedural volume, mortality, and access to training and care for thoracic aortic dissections.

In this scenario, the introduction of an on-call aortic specialist has been associated with lower in-hospital mortality and accompanied by a practice-pattern shift toward more extensive repairs,9 with the latter being associated with better long-term survival and lower risk of need for reoperations in patients with TAAD.10 Indeed, in patients with TAAD, aortic surgeons perform a significantly higher number of aortic root procedures, aortic arch interventions, and cross-clamp times and cardiopulmonary bypass times are significantly shorter in comparison with nonaortic surgeons,11 which compounds into better outcomes.

The 2022 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines suggest that a collaborative aortic team should comprise cardiac surgeons, vascular surgeons, aortic endovascular specialists, imaging experts, cardiovascular anesthesiologists, and an intensive care unit familiar with the nuanced care of patients with AASs.12 Therefore, interdisciplinary collaboration becomes of paramount importance when dealing with AAS.

By highlighting integrated assessment and multiple therapies, JACC: Case Reports can turn complex cases into clear lessons for cardiovascular surgeons and aortic specialists who treat a critical population of patients who demand unique clinical and policy considerations—with unrepaired acute TAAD conferring a 1%–2% per hour risk of rupture and mortality. The commitment of this journal is clear: to share case-based knowledge that translates, case by case, into better care for aortic patients, especially in cases for which evidence is sparse and guidance limited.
