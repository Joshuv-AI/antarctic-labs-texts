Background

Alcohol use associates with several legal-, health-, and behavior-related negative consequences. While binge and/or heavy alcohol use are particularly relevant to negative consequence, binge alcohol use [1, 2] and heavy alcohol use [3, 4] remain prevalent in the United States. Young adulthood is a critical period for the onset and exacerbation of alcohol use and young adults engage in binge alcohol use at a higher prevalence than other age groups [5]. As such, investigating factors that prospectively associate with future alcohol misuse in young adults is critical to reduce negative consequences of alcohol use.

For the purposes of our study, we use the definition of “alcohol sensitivity” as an individual’s subjective response to acute alcohol effects that could indicate an individual’s future risk for alcohol misuse. Laboratory alcohol challenges administering standardized dose(s) of alcohol versus a placebo (or alternatively, No Alcohol) beverage are the gold standard for measuring acute alcohol-related effects, including subjective effects considered to be indicative of alcohol sensitivity: “positive” effects (e.g., stimulation, alcohol reward, affect enhancement), and “negative” effects (e.g., sedation) [6, 7]. Seminal work by King et al. have consistently demonstrated that greater alcohol sensitivity to stimulation and reward and lower alcohol sensitivity to sedation are prospectively associated with naturalistic alcohol misuse patterns (including binge drinking) and increased risk of developing alcohol use disorder (AUD) symptoms [8, 9]. However, prior prospective alcohol sensitivity research has not accounted for social factors and there is a need for alcohol sensitivity studies to address social context [10].

Several laboratory studies have demonstrated that the acute effects of alcohol can be enhanced under social conditions (e.g., two or more individuals participating in the same lab room) compared to solitary conditions (e.g., a single individual participating alone), which we label here as “social facilitation of alcohol effects”. Kirkpatrick and de Wit reported that dyads comprised of two strangers experienced greater stimulation and decreased negative alcohol effects compared to participants who consumed alcohol alone [11]. Doty and de Wit found that alcohol produced more “positive” subjective effects (e.g., “high” and “drug liking”) in participants who were tested in social groups compared with those who were tested alone [12]. Taken together, prior laboratory studies using a between-subject design, demonstrate that social context can increase the subjective effects of alcohol [11, 12]. However, it is possible that individuals vary in their sensitivity to social facilitation of alcohol effects, which would require within-subject investigations.

A recent ecological momentary assessment (EMA) study demonstrated that during social alcohol use (vs. solitary alcohol use) participants reported greater positive subjective alcohol effects (e.g., increased alcohol stimulation and reward) and decreased sedation [13]. Another study found that adolescents with greater retrospectively reported social facilitation of alcohol effects prospectively associated with greater alcohol use and binge alcohol use [14]. Alcohol consumption in young adults often occurs in social settings [15–17] and alcohol use of friends associates with individual alcohol use in young adults [18]. Thus, investigating how sensitivity to social facilitation of alcohol prospectively associates with alcohol use and alcohol consequences may be particularly relevant in young adults.

This trial protocol describes an ongoing prospective alcohol administration study (Peers and Alcohol Longitudinal Study, PALS) in young adults who binge drink and drink with friends (ages 21–29; N = 250). This study underwent peer review at the Center for Scientific Review Addiction Risks and Mechanisms Study Section (ARM) during the funding process. Using the gold standard laboratory alcohol challenge, we will characterize the phenomenon of social facilitation of alcohol effects to investigate predisposing factors to – and mechanisms of – social facilitation of alcohol effects. Based on prior studies demonstrating that extraversion and social alcohol expectancies may associate with greater social reward and positive alcohol effects [19–22], we propose these as predisposing factors to social facilitation alcohol effects. Based on prior research demonstrating that alcohol consumed in social settings increases affiliative behavior and emotional contagion (i.e., individual’s affect can alter the affect of others in a coordinated manner; 11, 23), we propose increased agreeableness, emotional responsiveness, and emotional contagion as mechanisms of social facilitation of alcohol effects. Upon completion of the laboratory protocols, the same participants then complete 28-days of EMA, which capture social context and proximate alcohol use outcomes, and 6- and 12-month follow ups, assessing long-term alcohol use outcomes. EMA and longitudinal assessments allow us to investigate the role of social facilitation of alcohol effects measured in the laboratory on future alcohol use and consequences. The combined EMA/longitudinal follow-up allow for granular assessment (including social context) of real-time alcohol use uncontaminated by retrospective recall (EMA) and the ability to measure alcohol use patterns up to one year later (follow-up).

Methods

Study aims

Aim 1

Examine the magnitude, predispositional factors, and mechanisms of social facilitation of alcohol effects.

Aim 2

Investigate social facilitation of alcohol effects on proximal alcohol outcomes.

Aim 3

Investigate social facilitation of alcohol effects on long-term alcohol outcomes.

Participants

Participants are recruited using social media advertisements, flyers, and referrals from friends. Similar recruitment strategies have been used in prior studies to reach target sample size. Inclusion criteria for the study requires that participants: (1) are 21–29 years old; (2) report regular alcohol use with at least one past 30-day heavy drinking episode (as defined by the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism [NIAAA]: 5 + drinks [male] or 4 + drinks [female] in a day [24]); (3) have a body mass index (BMI) between 18 and 30 (to limit the volume of alcohol administered [11]); (4) own an iOS or Android smart device compatible with LifeData (https://www.lifedatacorp.com/); (5) are fluent in English; and (6) have a same-sex platonic friend that also meets eligibility requirements and is willing to participate. Exclusion criteria are: (1) pregnancy, breastfeeding, or intent to get pregnant in the next 60 days (females); (2) medical conditions or medication use contraindicated for alcohol administration; and (3) seeking treatment for alcohol use or wanting to quit or cutdown alcohol use in the next 60 days.

Baseline session

At the start of the baseline session, participants provide informed consent with study staff and provide a legal form of identification (e.g., driver’s license, passport) to verify their identity and age. Once consented and enrolled, participants complete height and weight measurement using Tanita WB-3000 Digital Scale (for BMI measurement), a urine screen for drugs (e.g., amphetamines, cocaine, benzodiazepines, and opioids) and pregnancy (females only), and a brief unstructured interview with study staff regarding their alcohol history. Participants then complete baseline measures using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at USC [25]. Participants can be found ineligible based on screening during the baseline session. Participants complete the baseline session procedures separately and then, if eligible, dyads are scheduled for their laboratory sessions. Laboratory sessions are randomized between dyads in blocks of 24 possible session orders (to ensure a counterbalanced design within each block); each randomized block is duplicated so that female and male dyads complete sessions in similar orders.

Laboratory sessions

Participants attend four randomized, counter-balanced laboratory sessions as a dyad and transportation to/from all sessions is provided by the study. Sessions vary by alcohol use (0.0 g/kg alcohol vs. 0.8 g/kg alcohol) and social context (alone vs. with friend). Each laboratory session is spaced at least 48 h apart. For laboratory sessions, participants fast starting 3 h before the session to standardize alcohol consumption. Participants arrive to the laboratory between 8:00 am and 12:00 pm (with ideal arrival time being 10:00 as shown in Fig. 2, yet allowing for a wider arrival window for feasibility). Upon arrival, they complete breath alcohol readings (BrAC) to confirm alcohol abstinence and a urine test to confirm drug abstinence (e.g., amphetamines, cocaine, benzodiazepines, and opioids) and non-pregnancy (females). At this time, participants are taken to either the same room (with friend) or separate rooms (alone) depending on session condition, and all study tasks occur in that room. Participants then complete a pre-drink behavioral intoxication test (i.e., Finger-to-Nose Test, One Leg Stand Test). Behavioral intoxication tests are scored with an ideal “sober” response as 0 points (touching nose with finger while staring at a single point ahead, standing on one leg for 30 s) and points for signs of impairment, or failure to complete the task. Participants are then served a weight-adjusted breakfast and complete pre-drink assessments (Ax1). Following the pre-drink assessment, participants consume standardized beverages (described below). During drink administration, participants complete two brief assessments of self-reported alcohol effects 10 and 20 min after the first drink is served. Assessments including self-reported measures and BrAC readings are then repeated 30, 60, 90, 120, 180, and 240 min following drink administration start (Ax2-7). In the friend condition only, participants complete a standardized conversation task (described below) 30 min after drink administration (Ax2) around the estimated peak BrAC. After the last assessment (Ax7), participants repeat the behavioral intoxication check and BrAC reading. Participants are discharged when their behavioral intoxication test score is within 1 point of their pre-drink assessment and their BrAC is less than 0.02%. Participants who do not meet these discharge requirements at the final assessment (Ax7) are provided with a meal and comfortable space to wait until they meet discharge requirements. All participants are provided transportation home following each laboratory session.

Standardized beverages

The 0.8-g/kg alcohol dose is prepared in a 16% solution by volume with alcohol and cranberry juice. The no-alcohol beverage consists of cranberry juice only. Participants are informed of the alcohol content of each beverage. For males, alcoholic beverages are prepared in a volume of 450 ml/70 kg and divided into equal thirds (i.e., 150 ml/70 kg for each third) for administration at 10-min intervals over 30 min. Alcohol doses for females are 15% less to adjust for sex differences in total body water. These calculations are based on several prior studies [11, 26, 27] to convert to approximated 3–4 standard drinks throughout the 30-minute administration period (varying by weight and sex). This dose is expected to raise peak BrAC to 0.08% (i.e., the CA legal limit for driving) between 30 and 60 min post drink administration, with BrAC decreasing after the 60-min time point and has been a well-tolerated dose for this population (i.e., young adults with a history of recent binge alcohol use).

Standardized conversation task (friend session only)

In the friend session, participants complete a 10-minute standardized conversation task. Using procedures adapted from Aron, Melinat [28], dyads engage in alternating responses to a series of questions. One dyad member leads the discussion for 5 min and then the other dyad member leads for 5 min. The conversation task is video- and audio-recorded and participants provide consent to be recorded during this task at each social session.

Ecological momentary assessments (EMA)

At the final laboratory session, participants begin their 28-day period of EMA using the LifeData app (https://www.lifedatacorp.com/), which is compatible with iOS and Android phones. Each day for the 28-day period, participants receive a notification-initiated WAKE survey in the morning (response window open from 6:00 am-2:00 pm), a DAY survey (distributed randomly from 2:00 pm-9:00 pm, open for 60 min), and a NIGHT survey (response window open from 9:00 pm-12:00 am). Participants are asked to complete a user-initiated survey in the app immediately before drinking their first drink of the day (PRE-ALC) and receive timed follow-up notification-initiated surveys 60 and 120 min later (POST-ALC).

Six- and twelve-month follow-ups

Participants are sent a REDCap link to complete a follow up survey at six and twelve months following the first laboratory session.

Primary outcomes

Alcohol-related reward and subjective effects (Laboratory sessions)

The Drug Effects Questionnaire (DEQ) is used to assess the extent to which they experienced the drug effects (i.e., “feel drug,” “feel high,” “like drug,” “dislike drug,” “want more”) on a visual analog scale from not at all to extremely [29–31]. “Like drug” (liking) and “want more” (wanting) will serve as primary measures of alcohol reward.

Acute alcohol subjective responses is assessed using the stimulation and sedation subscales of the Biphasic Alcohol Effects Scale (BAES), a 14-item survey that instructs individuals to rate the extent to which drinking alcohol has produced related subjective effects (0 = not at all to 10 = extremely) [32].

Primary social facilitation of alcohol effects scores will be the BAES stimulation and sedation scales, as well as DEQ liking and wanting. We chose these as primary measures, as individual differences in all four (measured under solitary conditions) have been shown to associate with future development of AUDS [8, 9].

Alcohol use (EMA)

At the WAKE prompt, participants answer, “Yesterday, how many alcohol drinks did you consume? (Note: One alcoholic drink is equivalent to a 5 oz glass of wine, 1 beer, 1 shot, etc.)” [33]. Quantity of alcohol use is the number of drinks reported at WAKE. Number of drinks reported at WAKE will be recoded to create two binary variables alcohol use (alcohol use [1 + drinks], no alcohol [0]) and binge alcohol use (binge [female: 4 + drinks; male: 5 + drinks], no binge (female: <4 drinks; male < 5 drinks]).

Alcohol consequences (EMA)

At the random surveys and each follow up survey, participants are asked if they experienced any of 10 negative and positive alcohol consequences “since the last prompt”. If participants endorsed alcohol use the prior day at the WAKE prompt, they complete the same negative and positive alcohol consequences “when drinking yesterday”. At the WAKE prompt following drinking days, participants complete two additional questions (yes/no) about whether they forgot what happened and if they have a hangover. Items were selected from [34]. Positive alcohol consequences will be the sum of the reported positive alcohol consequences and negative alcohol consequences will be the sum of reported negative alcohol consequences.

Alcohol use (Follow-ups)

At the 6- and 12-month follow-ups participants complete a timeline follow-back (TLFB) assessing past 30 day alcohol use. The TLFB is considered a reliable measure of recent alcohol use [35]. On days where alcohol use was reported, follow up branching is used to assess number of standard drinks. Quantity of alcohol use is the number of drinks reported for each day. Number of drinks reported for each day will be recoded to create two binary variables alcohol use (alcohol use [1 + drinks], no alcohol [0]) and binge alcohol use (binge [female: 4 + drinks; male: 5 + drinks], no binge (female: <4 drinks; male < 5 drinks]).

Alcohol consequences (Follow-ups)

At the 6- and 12-month follow-ups, participants complete the validated Brief Young Adult Alcohol Consequences Questionnaire (YAACQ) measures past three-month alcohol-related consequences [36, 37] and the positive drinking consequences questionnaire [38]. To be able to compare across positive drinking consequences and negative alcohol consequences, responses are based on a frequency scale from the positive drinking consequences questionnaire [38]. Alcohol consequences will be the sum of all reported alcohol consequences.

Statistical power and sample size justification

Effect size estimates for determining power for laboratory studies are based on laboratory studies indicating that drinking in the presence of other drinkers versus drinking alone produces significant social facilitation of alcohol effects (Cohen’s d = 0.34–0.89; 11) and alcohol increases stimulation in platonic friend dyads (B = 0.26; SE = 0.09; [39]), and an EMA study finding an interaction between alcohol episode and social context in predicting positive mood (B = 0.27, SE = 0.12; [40]). Considering the small sample sizes of previous studies, if we base our effect size on a more conservative estimate (i.e., Cohen’s d = 0.20), N = 200 would provide 0.80 power. Using the APIMPower Program to compute power for proposed APIM (N = 100 dyads), we will be sufficiently powered to detect minimum beta=0.192 to achieve 1-β = 0.803, assuming r=.25 between actor and partner variables and 0.02 of correlation of the errors [41].

Estimated effect sizes for analyses related to social facilitation of alcohol effects associations with alcohol use (Aims 2 & 3) are based on prior studies showing sensitivity to alcohol response in the laboratory exerts a small to large effect on prospective alcohol use [42] and an EMA study showing a small effect of social context on alcohol use [13]. Based on our pilot data and prior studies, we expect a large interclass correlation (ICC) for alcohol use, and medium interclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for quantity of alcohol use [22, 43] and alcohol consequences [43]. We conservatively estimated compliance as 75% based off of our prior EMA studies [44, 45]. Thus, we expect participants will provide approximately 21 (75%) total EMA days (L2 = 200, L1 = 4200 EMA days), which should provide adequate power [46]. Alcohol consequences models will utilize EMA days with alcohol use. Based on inclusion criteria and 75% EMA compliance, we expect participants will provide approximately 9 (75%) total alcohol use days (L2 = 200, L1 = 1800 alcohol days). For the most complex smallest n analyses proposed, we would be powered to detect medium to large effects [47].

Data analysis and statistical plan

We will minimize missing data and attrition by following recommendations for EMA studies [48, 49] and techniques that have been successful in obtaining appropriate retention and compliance in our prior studies with similar designs [11, 14, 44, 45]. Preliminary analyses of EMA and baseline data will assess EMA compliance and patterns of missing data (i.e., MAR). The proposed multilevel modeling and structural equation model techniques will use full-information maximum likelihood (FIML) estimation.

All hypotheses will be tested in a series of multilevel models (MLM; nested as appropriate based on data structure per hypothesis). The use of normal and generalized multilevel models (e.g., logistic, hurdle, or zero-inflated models) will be dictated by the distribution of the outcome.

For Aim 1 we will develop a series of MLM to account for 2 × 2 within-subject experimental conditions (level-1, L1) nested within individuals (level-2, L2) on our primary alcohol sensitivity outcomes (i.e., stimulation, sedation, liking, wanting). To test for mechanisms we will develop an actor partner model for indistinguishable dyads to assess actor and partner effects on alcohol sensitivity outcomes.

For Aims 2 & 3 our primary predictor will be social facilitation of alcohol effects. We will calculate social facilitation of alcohol effects from the Axpeak timepoint from each laboratory session [6, 8, 9, 42]. For each predictor (i.e., BAES-stimulation, BAES-sedation, DEQ-Like, DEQ-Want), peak social facilitation of alcohol effects (L2) will be calculated as: Alcohol Social Session minus Alcohol Alone Session, and analyses will control for related peak values of No Alcohol Social Session minus No Alcohol Alone Session. For Aim 2, a series of MLM will be developed to predict alcohol use outcomes on a given day, accounting for nested data structure (L1: repeated measures; L2: individual) from person-mean centered social facilitation of alcohol effects. To test whether the associations of social facilitation of alcohol effects with alcohol use are amplified in social situations, we will expand models by adding within-person social context (alone vs. 1 + other person: L1), and their cross-level interaction.

Aim 3 will be tested with MLM containing social facilitation of alcohol effects scores and each alcohol outcome (alcohol quantity, alcohol use, binge alcohol use, alcohol-related consequences) over the past 30-days at each timepoint (6 months, 12 months). Each outcome will be predicted from social facilitation of alcohol effects.

Ethical considerations

This protocol has approval from the University of Southern California Institutional Review Board (UP-24-00590) and amendments to the protocol will be submitted and approved by the University of Southern California Institutional Review Board. Because the proposed project is a relatively low risk study and is not blinded, RDP and MGK will be responsible for data safety monitoring. The primary ethical consideration for the study is the administration of alcohol to participants. We followed the guidelines for conducting alcohol research provided by NIAAA to mitigate the risk to participants resulting from acute alcohol administration [50], including remaining in the laboratory until their BrAC was below 0.02% and they were able to pass a behavioral intoxication test. We excluded participants with medical conditions or medications counterindicated for alcohol use and/or if participants are in treatment or plan to seek treatment for their alcohol use. Our inclusion criteria also required participants to engage in current heavy drinking episodes. This requirement ensured that participants did not receive an alcohol dose in the laboratory that exceeded their usual drinking levels. Study personnel monitored BrAC via a breathalyzer throughout the experimental sessions. Participants are provided transportation to/from all experimental sessions. Additionally, participants may experience discomfort answering interviews or self-report questionnaires and/or from being videotaped during laboratory experimental sessions. Interviewers are skilled in talking about sensitive information with subjects and participants are informed that video recordings of the experimental sessions will be created and stored on a secure server. Participants will be reminded that their participation in each of the study procedures is voluntary and that their videos will be confidential and that all coded data resulting from the sessions will be de-identified.

Discussion

This study addresses important questions related to how social facilitation of alcohol effects influence proximate and long-term alcohol use in young adults. Importantly, this study combines two lines of research (i.e., social influences on alcohol effects, alcohol sensitivity) noted as influencing risk for alcohol misuse in young adults. For this study, we focused on dyadic friends as our social context for the laboratory alcohol administration because friends are a common social context for young adults [15, 51]. We considered investigating social facilitation of alcohol effects using larger group sizes. Previous research using triads has shown robust alcohol effects on increases of social behavior [52] and affiliative behaviors among strangers [23]. However, we decided to use dyads for several reasons. The authors have demonstrated social facilitation of alcohol effects in stranger dyads [11] and limiting the social sessions to dyads increases the feasibility of scheduling. While we believe that sensitivity to social facilitation of alcohol effects is an important novel risk factor, broadly, and is not specific to the friend context, the results of this study are only able to confirm social facilitation of alcohol effects in platonic friends. However, broader social contexts will be collected during the EMA and we will be able to investigate how different social contexts interact with social facilitation alcohol effects to influence alcohol use in the real world.

For this study, we will utilize a no alcohol control beverage (i.e., no deception about the content of the beverage), which are a valid and common control condition in alcohol administration studies [40, 53]. This approach does not allow us to investigate non-pharmacological factors (e.g., expectancy effects) that may contribute to social facilitation of alcohol effects. Placebo manipulations can lead to unanticipated compensatory effects [54], which are not the primary focus on the study aims. While we considered including a placebo condition (in addition to the no alcohol condition) to disentangle pharmacological and expectancy/dosage-set effects, we ultimately decided that this was beyond the scope of the study aims and benefits would be offset by the additional participant burden (i.e., 2 additional laboratory sessions).

Given the large sample size, multi-method approach, and longitudinal assessment, we believe this study will importantly extend the prior literature by providing a comprehensive social-contextual model for understanding social related alcohol use as a risk factor for future alcohol misuse and alcohol use disorders. This study will extend prior research in several ways. We will examine potential between-person and between-dyad factors that may predispose individuals and dyads to greater social facilitation of alcohol effects, which could serve to identify whose alcohol misuse may occur in social settings. This could guide targeting of social-related alcohol interventions and/or social alcohol expectancies (e.g., expectancy challenge interventions) to those with increased social facilitation of alcohol effects. Investigation of mechanisms and naturalistic time-varying social context will provide critical information in terms of understanding how and when social contexts enhance the pharmacological effects of alcohol and contribute to future risk of AUD. This could potentially indicate the benefit of testing the efficacy of behavioral and/or pharmacological interventions – such as those geared toward reducing rewarding acute alcohol effects – in social contexts. As social alcohol use is highly prevalent in young adults, including college and non-college samples, this study would contribute to the ongoing development of a social-contextual framework of how alcohol use in social settings may associate with future alcohol misuse and AUD symptoms.
