Evaluating the human–artificial intelligence interaction in optical diagnosis of early colorectal carcinoma through endoscopist responses to simulated artificial intelligence: a multinational online study

ABSTRACT
Background Suboptimal human–artificial intelligence (AI) interaction is a potential roadblock for implementation of AI in clinical practice. We aimed to evaluate the interaction between AI and endoscopists in the optical diagnosis of colorectal carcinoma (CRC). Methods International endoscopists were invited to diagnose colorectal lesions online. After a pretest of 15 videos, the diagnosis could be adjusted in the subsequent 35 videos after receiving a simulated AI diagnosis. Histology was the gold standard. Endoscopists’ diagnoses before and after AI were compared using the signal detection theory. Inappropriate response to AI was defined as decreased diagnostic performance by the endoscopist after AI. Results In 2730 diagnoses, the 78 participating endoscopists reached diagnostic accuracies of 71.4 % before and 73.7 % after AI ( P < 0.001). Before AI, endoscopists missed only a few true CRCs (mean hit rate 0.86), at the cost of a mean false alarm rate of 0.35. Inappropriate response to AI was observed in 28.2 % of endoscopists. After AI, most endoscopists (41.0 %) shifted toward a more conservative attitude, tending to diagnose a lesion as non-CRC. Conclusions Inappropriate response to AI is an issue in human–AI interaction for the optical diagnosis of CRC. Before using AI, endoscopists should become familiar with their own optical diagnostic performance, and that of the AI system, to possibly improve interaction.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) applications in gastrointestinal endoscopy are developing rapidly. AI has the potential to improve characterization of colorectal lesions. However, implementation in daily clinical practice is not yet common. On the one hand, this is probably a consequence of disappointing results regarding the added value of AI assistance 1 . On the other hand, suboptimal interaction between AI and its users is hypothesized to be a roadblock for implementation 2 . The role of human–AI interaction has been highlighted as a key challenge in integrating AI into clinical endoscopy practice 2 .

The stand-alone diagnostic performance of AI could be prone to bias 3 . Without proper understanding of how AI makes decisions, humans could show inappropriate responses to AI, potentially causing AI to not live up to its full potential 4 5 .

Previous research has shown that both substandard (under)trust and excessive (over)trust are relevant issues in the use of AI for the diagnosis of diminutive colorectal polyps as non-neoplastic or neoplastic 6 . However, using AI to improve the endoscopic recognition of colorectal carcinoma (CRC) is a relatively novel application, and human–AI interaction in this context has not yet been evaluated 7 . The interaction may also differ because the consequences of a misdiagnosis are substantially more serious than for non-neoplastic or neoplastic colorectal polyps.

The aim of this study was to evaluate endoscopists’ responses to AI in the optical diagnosis of CRC in an online test with simulated AI diagnoses.

Methods

Study design

Between September 2024 and December 2024, endoscopists were invited to participate in an online test involving the optical diagnosis of colorectal lesions. The test was open to international participation by any endoscopist performing colonoscopies. Participating endoscopists were recruited through the Dutch T1 Colorectal Cancer Working Group, COMET T1 CRC study consortium, the Fujifilm Blue Light Imaging expert group, and UEG My Connect. Additionally, snowball sampling was applied. All participating endoscopists consented to taking part in the study. The study is part of the COMET T1 CRC project, which was approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of Maastricht University Medical Center (UMC) + (METC 2022–3436) and Leiden UMC (N23.026), and by the Medical Research Ethics Committees United (W23.035).

Online test design

Video 1 Example video from the online test, showing a rectosigmoid lesion in high-definition white-light and narrow-band imaging; histopathology result was tubulovillous adenoma with high grade dysplasia.

The online test started with questions on the background information of the endoscopist such as their years of endoscopy experience (a full list of questions is available in Part 1 s of the online-only Supplementary material). Thereafter, 50 cases of colorectal lesions ≥ 10 mm were shown (27 % CRC; Fig. 1 s ), each containing the lesion location and an endoscopy video in high-definition white-light and narrow-band imaging (lesion characteristics in Table 1 s ; example in Video 1 ). Returning to the previous case was not possible after proceeding to the next case. For each case, endoscopists were asked whether they thought the lesion in the video did or did not contain CRC.

All endoscopists received the cases in the same order, determined by one-time randomization. Without disclosing this to the endoscopists, the first 15 cases were used as a pretest to assess the optical diagnosis performance of each endoscopist. The next 35 cases were all shown a second time immediately after the endoscopist provided their initial diagnosis. During this second showing, the endoscopist also received an AI diagnosis, displayed as “Artificial intelligence diagnosis: colorectal carcinoma present/absent in this lesion.” The endoscopist then provided their optical diagnosis again, offering the opportunity to choose a different diagnosis after viewing the AI diagnosis. Endoscopists were able to rewatch or pause the video when providing their initial diagnosis; therefore, a learning effect from the second showing with the AI diagnosis was not expected. Endoscopists were not informed that the displayed AI results were simulated and set to a sensitivity of 90 % and specificity of 70 %, in order to objectively view the interaction between endoscopists and AI. Additionally, a potential learning effect from AI was disabled, as the endoscopists did not know whether the diagnosis was correct or not. More information on the data selection process and rationale for the simulated AI performance can be found in Part 2 s in the Supplementary material.

Outcomes

Endoscopists’ optical diagnosis results before and after receiving the AI diagnosis were analyzed using the signal detection theory. This is a statistical method to describe the decision-making process of humans when discriminating between the presence of a signal or noise 8 . For this study, accurately diagnosing the presence of CRC in a video means detecting the signal as such and thus a “hit,” while inaccurately diagnosing the presence of CRC in a video means judging the noise as “signal” and thus a “false alarm.” The d-prime value is the ability of the endoscopist to separate CRC (signals) from colorectal lesions without CRC (noise). A higher d-prime value represents better optical diagnostic performance. The “criterion” is a measure of response bias: the tendency to diagnose the presence of CRC. A low criterion value means that the endoscopist is liberal and has a tendency to diagnose CRC, leading to more hits but also more false alarms 9 . A high criterion value means that the endoscopist is conservative and has a tendency to diagnose non-CRC, leading to fewer hits but also fewer false alarms.

Inappropriate response to AI was defined as cases in which the endoscopist changed their diagnosis in the direction of AI when the endoscopist was initially correct and simulated AI was incorrect. These cases led to a decrease in d-prime (negative delta d-prime).

Statistical analysis

The change in diagnostic accuracy before and after AI was tested using the McNemar test. Mean (SD) hit rate, false alarm rate, d-prime, and criterion were calculated for each endoscopist for their diagnoses before and after receiving the AI diagnosis. In addition, these values were calculated for the simulated AI. Delta d-prime was calculated as d-prime after AI minus d-prime before AI. Delta criterion was calculated as criterion after AI minus criterion before AI. The correlation between the endoscopists’ years of endoscopy experience and d-prime before AI, delta d-prime, and delta criterion was assessed using Pearson correlation. A two-sided P value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA) and Matlab.

Results

Endoscopist characteristics

In total, 78 endoscopists participated in the online test. The endoscopists had a mean of 9.6 years of endoscopy experience. Other endoscopist characteristics are shown in Table 2 s .

Diagnostic performance of endoscopists before and after AI

Before receiving the AI diagnosis, 71.4 % of the 2730 diagnoses for the presence or absence of CRC were correct. After AI, the number of correct diagnoses increased to 73.7 % ( P < 0.001). Association between years of endoscopy experience and interaction with AI

The d-prime value before AI was negatively correlated with years of endoscopy experience, but this correlation was not statistically significant (Pearson’s correlation –0.064, P = 0.58) ( Fig. 2 s ). Delta d-prime was positively correlated with the endoscopists’ years of endoscopy experience, although this was not statistically significant (Pearson’s correlation 0.217, P = 0.06) ( Fig. 3a ). Delta criterion was negatively correlated with the endoscopists’ years of endoscopy experience, but this was also not statistically significant (Pearson’s correlation –0.075, P = 0.52) ( Fig. 3b ).

Discussion

In this multicenter study with a large group of participating endoscopists, we showed that using simulated AI outcomes improved the overall diagnostic performance of 50 % of the endoscopists. Inappropriate response to AI was observed in 28.2 % of endoscopists. After AI, most endoscopists shifted toward a more conservative attitude (41.0 %).

In order to work safely with AI, the response to the AI output needs to be appropriate 10 . A previous study evaluating the human–AI interaction for the endoscopic diagnosis of diminutive colorectal polyps as non-neoplastic or neoplastic showed an inappropriate response to AI in 18.6 % of cases 6 . Our study used analysis according to the signal detection theory and showed higher levels of inappropriate AI response (28.2 %). Given the greater consequences of a CRC misdiagnosis, endoscopists might be more inclined to change their diagnosis to match that of the AI diagnosis due to automation bias, which is the tendency to cognitively detach oneself from the task if it is performed by an AI algorithm 11 . However, the current study cannot determine whether changes to diagnoses to match those of AI were due to automation bias. The increased false alarm rate after AI by 20.5 % of the endoscopists could be explained by endoscopists with any level of expertise preferring to avoid false negatives 12 . However, the observation of a positive delta criterion by 41.0 % of endoscopists shows that most endoscopists tended toward more diagnoses of non-CRC lesions after receiving the AI diagnosis.

The current study did not find a significant correlation between years of endoscopy experience, d-prime before AI, delta d-prime, or delta criterion, indicating that endoscopy experience did not necessarily influence the interaction with AI. This is an interesting result, as it could be expected that more experienced endoscopists have higher optical diagnosis levels and could evaluate the AI outcomes more critically. This supports previous research indicating that years of endoscopy experience does not predict expertise in optical diagnosis of diminutive colorectal polyps 13 .

Several limitations of this study should be mentioned. First, the endoscopists’ optical diagnoses were collected using an online test. This could result in endoscopists’ decision-making processes not fully reflecting clinical practice, because their diagnoses did not have any consequences and their own ability to adequately visualize a colorectal lesion had no influence. Additionally, the lesions in the test set were selected by the researchers, which could impact the generalizability of results.

Second, the AI model in this study was simulated and cannot be applied in clinical practice. However, this made it possible to create the optimal setting to investigate human–AI interaction with AI performance levels similar to those that could be achievable and beneficial in clinical practice. The incorrect AI diagnoses were selected by the researchers and could introduce a selection effect to specific failure modes that endoscopists encounter.

Finally, a relatively small test set was used, which was not determined through sample size calculation. To obtain reliable hit rates, a higher CRC prevalence compared with real-world prevalence was necessary. This may affect the human–AI interaction. Using larger test sets might improve the ability of humans to estimate the diagnostic performance level of AI 14 . The test set size did enable the use of videos of colorectal lesions instead of still images, complying with clinical practice, unlike many previous studies. Additionally, 50 test cases were manageable, enabling a large group of 78 international endoscopists to participate, far more than the number of participants (21, 23, or 18) in previous studies 6 12 15 .

Increasing awareness of AI performance could create more appropriate AI responses 16 . When interpreting an AI diagnosis, a sensible starting point could be to look at the differences between endoscopists’ and AI’s optical diagnosis performance based on how often they agree or disagree and which diagnosis is correct 12 . In this study, the endoscopists were not informed about the diagnostic performance of the AI system, in order to objectively evaluate the human–AI interaction. However, in clinical practice, endoscopists should ensure they understand the algorithm that they use and are familiar with its diagnostic performance, which should preferably be obtained from multicenter external validation studies. Familiarity with specific AI algorithms is thought to be essential for AI acceptance 10 . Future research could indicate whether specialized training in an algorithm before implementation could be beneficial.

In conclusion, this study with a large group of international endoscopists showed that inappropriate response to AI is an issue in human–AI interaction for the optical diagnosis of CRCs. Overall, the change in diagnosis after receiving the simulated AI diagnosis did lead to a small gain in diagnostic accuracy of 2.3 %. Before using AI in clinical practice, endoscopists should become familiar with the pitfalls and strengths of both their own optical diagnosis performance and the AI performance in order to improve interaction. Future research should focus on ways to improve the human–AI interaction, preferably in real-time endoscopy practice.
