Histopathological Outcomes of Myoma Uteri Surgery Over a 10-Year Period: A Retrospective Evaluation of Sarcoma, STUMP, and Benign Variants
Authors: Ferit Özmaskan, Reyhan Gündüz, Uğur Çalışır, Mehmetcan Kaydal, Abdurrahman Sengi, Mehmet Nuri Duran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMyoma uteri has a high incidence, particularly during the reproductive years, and represents the most common indication for hysterectomy. In this retrospective study, we aimed to investigate the incidence of uterine sarcoma, smooth muscle tumor of unknown malignant potential (STUMP), and benign myoma variants in the histopathological examination of patients undergoing surgery for myoma uteri and to contribute to the existing literature.MethodsBetween January 2013 and January 2023, 504 patients who underwent hysterectomy or myomectomy for myoma uteri at our hospital were included in this study. Demographic and clinical findings of the patients, accompanying chronic diseases, previous operation history, presenting complaints, cytology (Pap smear) results, myoma size as determined by ultrasonography, endometrial sampling results, blood tests, operation type, final pathology results, and presence of malignancy were retrospectively analyzed from our database. In 39 patients with STUMP, age, marital status, body mass index (BMI), gravidity, parity, abortion history, comorbidities, surgical history, Human Papilloma Virus (HPV) status, fibroid size, and endometrial biopsy results were analyzed individually.ResultsThe STUMP detection rate was 7.7% (n = 39), which may be related to regional demographic characteristics and the tertiary referral nature of our institution. No statistically significant associations were identified between histopathological outcomes and the following variables: patient age, menopausal status, tumor size, number of myomas, surgical approach, and preoperative imaging findings.ConclusionAlthough most uterine leiomyomas are benign, only a small number of tumors with potential clinical significance, such as STUMP, can be detected. Since this situation cannot be predicted in the preoperative evaluation, that is, demographic and clinical data, pathological evaluation should be performed in patients diagnosed with leiomyoma. Surgical planning should be performed carefully, especially in patients with a desire for fertility, and STUMP cases should be followed closely.

Introduction

Myoma uteri is the most common tumor encountered in women of childbearing age. Other names of myoma uteri include fibromyomas, myofibromas, leiomyomas, leiomyofibromas, fibroleiomyomas, fibromas, and fibroids.1 Myomas are benign tumors that arise from the smooth muscle cells of the uterus.2 They rarely tend to be malignant in nature. Although they are usually asymptomatic, they may cause various symptoms, depending on their size and location. The common symptoms include pelvic pain, vaginal bleeding, infertility, and frequent urination.3 This condition negatively affects the quality of life of many women, and is considered an important health problem in society. Its prevalence ranges from 30% to 50%, and typically regresses spontaneously after menopause.4–6 Diagnosis is made incidentally in most patients and confirmed by history, drug use, physical examination, and if necessary, transabdominal ultrasonography (USG) and pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).7

Surgery is the primary treatment option for fibroids. There are two surgical methods. These surgical methods, known as myomectomy and hysterectomy, are preferred on a patient–patient basis. Myomectomy is the surgical removal of detected fibroids, and is especially preferred in young patients in the reproductive period who desire fertility after surgery. Depending on the location and size of fibroids, surgical intervention can be performed laparoscopically or laparotomically. Hysterectomy is a surgical procedure in which the entire uterus is surgically excised. Laparoscopic or laparotomic hysterectomy may be preferred, especially in perimenopausal and postmenopausal patients who do not desire children and have additional indications. In addition, hysteroscopic myomectomy can be performed in cases of submucosal and pedunculated submucosal myoma uteri measuring < 5 cm.8

Despite advances in histopathological classification, smooth muscle tumors of uncertain malignant potential (STUMP) remain a significant clinical challenge due to their unpredictable biological behavior. These tumors do not have well-defined criteria for malignant transformation, and their clinical course may vary widely, ranging from benign behavior to late recurrence or distant metastasis. As a result, accurate prognostic assessment is difficult, and optimal postoperative management and follow-up strategies remain controversial. This persistent clinical and prognostic uncertainty underscores the importance of careful histopathological evaluation and contributes to the ongoing debate regarding the most appropriate clinical approach to STUMP.

Preoperative differentiation between benign leiomyomas and malignant or borderline uterine smooth muscle tumors remains challenging. Conventional imaging modalities, including ultrasonography and magnetic resonance imaging, may provide information regarding tumor size and location; however, they lack sufficient specificity to reliably distinguish STUMP or sarcoma from benign leiomyomas. Similarly, preoperative clinical and laboratory findings are often nonspecific. As a result, definitive diagnosis can usually only be established through postoperative histopathological examination, which highlights the limitations of preoperative assessment and underscores the rationale for the present study. Human papillomavirus (HPV) testing and endometrial biopsy are commonly used in the preoperative evaluation of patients presenting with abnormal uterine bleeding or suspected uterine pathology. Although these assessments are not diagnostic for STUMP, they are frequently performed to exclude coexisting cervical or endometrial malignancies. Therefore, evaluating HPV status and endometrial biopsy findings in our cohort aimed to explore whether these routinely used preoperative tools showed any association with histopathological outcomes.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the detection rates of uterine sarcoma, Smooth Muscle Tumor of Uncertain Malignant Potential (STUMP) and benign myoma variants when final histopathological examinations were evaluated after surgery in patients admitted to our tertiary care hospital serving the Southeastern Anatolia Region over the last ten years and diagnosed with uterine myoma.

Methods

Our study employed a retrospective cross-sectional design at the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Faculty of Medicine, Dicle University. The data and histopathological results of 504 patients who underwent hysterectomy or myomectomy using abdominal, vaginal, hysteroscopic, or laparoscopic methods for myoma uteri between January 2013 and January 2023 were reviewed from the hospital records (Figure 1). Patients diagnosed with myoma uteri but not operated on (patients who were recommended medical treatment or follow-up), those whose records could not be accessed from the hospital database, and those with missing data were not included in the study.

The demographic characteristics of the patients included age, marital status, Body Mass Index (BMI) gravidity, parity, history of abortion, menstrual status, history of menopause, previous operations, reasons for presentation, cytology Pap-smear results, ultrasonographic findings (status of uterus and adnexa, size of existing fibroids), endometrial sampling results, preoperative complete blood count and biochemical parameters, surgical methods performed, final pathology findings, presence of malignancy, and the need for additional surgery.

Endometrial sampling was performed under local anesthesia using a Carmen injector in patients with abnormal uterine bleeding before surgery, and the obtained materials were sent for histopathological examination. Research assistants and faculty members in our clinic performed the ultrasonographic evaluations and surgical procedures.

The histopathological diagnosis of smooth muscle tumors of uncertain malignant potential (STUMP) was established according to accepted criteria based on a combination of morphologic features, including cellular atypia, mitotic index, and the presence or absence of tumor cell necrosis. Tumors that did not meet the definitive diagnostic criteria for benign leiomyoma or leiomyosarcoma were classified as STUMP. All histopathological evaluations were performed using postoperative surgical specimens. All histopathological examinations were reviewed by experienced pathologists in our institution. In cases with diagnostic uncertainty, the final diagnosis was reached through consensus following re-evaluation of the histopathological findings.

Data were analyzed using SPSS 24.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics are presented as numbers and percentages for categorical variables and as mean ± standard deviation or median (25th-75th percentile) for continuous variables. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Pearson chi-square test, and the normal distribution of continuous variables was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. The Mann–Whitney U-test was used for pairwise comparisons between groups. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

Myoma uteri is one of the most common causes of presentation in gynecology outpatient clinics. Patients with STUMP may present with various nonspecific symptoms such as irregular uterine bleeding, pelvic discomfort, or a feeling of fullness in the lower abdomen. In some instances, these tumors are incidentally discovered as uterine masses during imaging studies or routine gynecologic examinations. Nevertheless, a considerable proportion of STUMPs remain asymptomatic and are detected during investigations of other unrelated medical conditions. Although the malignancy rate is low, surgical and histopathological examinations are mandatory for diagnosis because of the lack of reliable diagnostic tests during the preoperative period.

Recent studies have reported that the median age at STUMP diagnosis ranges from 41 to 48 years.9–11 In contrast, the mean age of patients in our study was significantly lower (35.5 years). This difference likely reflects the demographic and reproductive characteristics of our region, where fertility rates are higher and women tend to enter their reproductive years at an earlier age. In such populations, asymptomatic uterine masses may be detected incidentally during cesarean sections or other obstetric surgeries performed for fertility-related reasons. Furthermore, as our hospital serves as a tertiary referral center, we often receive younger symptomatic patients referred for surgical management. Taken together, these factors suggest that the younger mean age in our cohort does not contradict the existing literature but rather represents the unique demographic and clinical profile of the population we serve, where STUMP is more likely to be diagnosed at a younger age. Similarly, the mean BMI was 26.1 ± 5.5 kg/m2, with an average gravidity of 2.1 and parity of 1.0. These results suggest that the population included in our study was younger and had lower BMI than those reported in the literature.

Although the histological incidence of leiomyomas in the general population is up–70–80%, the rate of cases with clinical symptoms is between 20–30%.12 Various studies have reported that the most common reason for presentation is abnormal uterine bleeding. In our study, 41.7% of the patients presented with abdominal pain, 29.4% with vaginal bleeding, and 29% with infertility. These findings indicate that our patient group consisted of symptomatic patients, rather than an asymptomatic population. Data on the dimensions of uterine fibroids have shown considerable variability in the literature. Raman et al13 reported a mean fibroid diameter of 5.5 ± 2.6 cm, whereas Cianci et al14 have reported an average diameter of 7 cm. In our series, the mean myoma diameter was 8.7 ± 3.9 cm, which is slightly higher than most previously reported values and may reflect referral patterns and more advanced disease at presentation in our tertiary care setting. Fibroid size remains an important factor in surgical decision-making.

HPV and endometrial biopsy findings were evaluated, and the results were negative in 97.7% of the HPV samples. Benign pathology was reported in all biopsied patients. STUMP was not observed in HPV-positive cases and there was no significant difference between the presence of STUMP and HPV-negative cases. Similarly, there was no significant difference in the frequency of STUMP between groups with and without endometrial biopsy. These findings are consistent with those in the literature; it has been reported in different studies that smear and biopsy results are mostly benign. It has been reported that malignancy is detected at rates ranging between 0.2–1.8% even in cases with benign diagnoses.15,16 In our study, no malignancy was detected; however, the STUMP rate was 7.7%. Our findings indicated that the malignancy rate in our cohort was higher than that reported in most published studies. This difference likely reflects the patient population characteristics and referral patterns specific to our tertiary care institution. In addition, regional demographic characteristics and the tertiary referral nature of our institution may have contributed to differences in age distribution and the relatively high STUMP rate observed in our cohort.

When the relationship between the presence of STUMP and clinical and demographic characteristics was evaluated, no significant differences were found between the patients with and without STUMP in terms of age, BMI, marital status, obstetric history, fibroid size, HPV status, and endometrial biopsy results. This finding is compatible with those of similar studies in the literature. Previous studies have also failed to show a significant relationship between the presence of STUMP and demographic or clinical parameters. Taken together, these findings suggest that commonly used demographic, clinical, and preoperative parameters have limited value in predicting STUMP. This underscores the need for cautious surgical decision-making and supports the continued reliance on postoperative histopathological evaluation rather than preoperative risk stratification alone.

Clinical Implications

The findings of this study highlight the clinical importance of careful surgical planning and postoperative follow-up in patients operated on for uterine leiomyomas. Given the inability to reliably distinguish STUMP from benign leiomyomas in the preoperative period, histopathological evaluation remains essential for all surgically treated cases. In particular, fertility-preserving surgical approaches should be considered cautiously, and patients diagnosed with STUMP should be informed about the potential need for close and long-term follow-up.

Conclusion

Although most uterine leiomyomas are benign and are often managed without complications, a small subset may demonstrate unpredictable biological behavior. In particular, tumors such as STUMP cannot be reliably distinguished from benign leiomyomas during the preoperative period. Therefore, thorough histopathological evaluation remains essential for all surgically treated patients diagnosed with leiomyoma. Based on the integration of our findings with existing literature, surgical decision-making should be individualized, especially in women who wish to preserve fertility. Furthermore, patients diagnosed with STUMP should be informed about the importance of close and long-term follow-up, as early detection of recurrence or progression may have a significant impact on subsequent management.
