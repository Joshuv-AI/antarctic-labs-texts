Testis-sparing surgery for Leydig cell tumor in children: long-term results of a multicentric retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jia Wei, Zixia Li, Gengyu Du, ChunLei Yang, Ning Li, Min Yang, Daxing Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeydig cell tumors (LCTs) of the testis are rare in children. Although testis-sparing surgery (TSS) is increasingly accepted as the preferred treatment, large-scale studies with long-term follow-up remain limited. This study compared the clinical characteristics of LCTs with those of testicular teratomas and endodermal sinus tumors and evaluated the long-term outcomes of TSS in children to enhance urologists’ understanding of this condition.MethodsPatients diagnosed with LCTs between January 2015 and December 2024 at three tertiary medical centers were enrolled, along with equal numbers of patients with testicular endodermal sinus tumors and testicular teratomas as control groups. Clinical data were collected and analyzed using SPSS 22.0 software.ResultsThis study enrolled 14 patients with testicular LCTs, 14 with testicular endodermal sinus tumors, and 14 with testicular teratomas. Patients with LCTs exhibited a significantly older age at presentation (P = 0.003). Precocious puberty was more prevalent in LCT patients (P < 0.001), and elevated serum testosterone levels were identified in 9 of 14 patients (64.3%). Scrotal ultrasound revealed hypoechoic masses with peripheral rim hypervascularization in LCT-affected testes, features absent in the other two groups (P < 0.001). All LCT patients underwent TSS with microscopically negative margins, and no patient experienced recurrence during follow-up (median: 42 months; range: 15–61 months). Furthermore, affected testes reached volumes comparable to those of the contralateral healthy testis within 3–4 years postoperatively.ConclusionThis study compares pediatric LCTs with testicular teratomas and endodermal sinus tumors to help urologists diagnose LCTs earlier and reduce misdiagnosis. The findings support TTS as an effective treatment for pediatric LCTs that preserves testicular function. Long-term follow-up using scrotal sonography and endocrine evaluation is supportive for managing pediatric LCTs.Keywords: Leydig cell tumors, Pediatric, Testis-sparing surgery, Long-term followup

Introduction

Testicular Leydig cell tumors (LCTs) are a subtype of testicular sex cord-stromal tumors. Although LCTs are extremely rare, accounting for only 1% to 3% of all testicular tumors, they represent the most common form of interstitial testicular neoplasm [1]. LCTs exhibit a bimodal age distribution, occurring most frequently in boys aged 5 to 10 years and in adults aged 30 to 60 years [2]. In pediatric populations, LCTs account for 3% to 6% of testicular tumors, with the majority being benign. In contrast, 10% to 20% of LCTs in adults are malignant [3]. These hormonal active tumors produce androgens such as testosterone and, occasionally, estrogen. In boys, LCTs frequently lead to precocious puberty, manifesting as advanced bone age and premature development of external genitalia and pubic hair [4]. In some cases, LCTs are discovered incidentally during ultrasonography or upon detection of a palpable testicular mass [5].

Due to their benign characteristics, LCTs in pediatric populations generally have an excellent prognosis [6]. Consequently, early diagnosis and prompt intervention are essential for optimal outcomes. Traditionally, radical orchiectomy has been the preferred treatment for pediatric testicular LCTs. However, testis-sparing surgery (TSS) is increasingly being adopted as an alternative treatment modality for LCTs in children. Despite this trend, few large case series with long-term follow-up data exist to evaluate the feasibility and safety of TSS for pediatric LCTs [6].

In this study, we compared the clinical and imaging characteristics of LCTs with those of testicular teratoma and endodermal sinus tumors and analyzed the long-term outcomes of TSS for pediatric LCTs. We hypothesized that TSS is a safe and effective treatment approach for pediatric LCTs.

Patients and methods

This research was approved by the Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University (Approval No. 2022-IRB-175) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki for clinical studies. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their parents.

The study enrolled all patients under 18 years of age who were pathologically diagnosed with LCTs and admitted to the Department of Urology at Children’s Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang Univeristy, the Department of Urology, Tongji Medical College, Wuhan Children's Hospital, Huazhong University of Science and Technology and the Department of Pediatric Surgery, Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology between January 2015 and December 2024. These three institutions are tertiary, research-oriented medical centers in eastern and central China. Additionally, clinical data were collected from patients who were diagnosed with either testicular endodermal sinus tumor or testicular teratoma and were admitted to the Department of Urology at the three medical center mentioned above between January 2015 and December 2024, serving as control groups. The number of patients in the control group was identical to that in the LCT group, with eligible medical records randomly selected via computer-generated random numbers.

The collected clinical data included patient characteristics, medical history, laboratory results, imaging findings, surgical procedures, pathological diagnoses, and long-term follow-up records. Tanner staging was used to evaluate external sexual characteristics [7]. Bone age was assessed by radiography of the patient’s hand and wrist. Precocious puberty was determined based on assessment of the patient’s Tanner stage, bone age, and sex hormone levels. Exclusion criteria included: (1) testicular tumors associated with any syndrome (e.g., Klinefelter syndrome, disorders of sex development); (2) pathological diagnosis of Sertoli-Leydig cell tumor or Leydig cell hyperplasia; and (3) age over 18 years.

The follow-up protocol required patients to undergo physical examination, scrotal ultrasonography, and sex hormone testing in outpatient clinics at 2 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and annually postoperatively.

Statistical analyses

Based on final pathological results, patients were divided into three groups: (1) testicular LCTs, (2) testicular endodermal sinus tumors, and (3) testicular teratomas. The Kruskal-Wallis test and Fisher’s exact test were used to analyze differences among the three groups for non-normally distributed continuous variables, while the chi-square test was used for categorical variables. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with a P-value of < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 14 patients were identified, with ages at diagnosis ranging from 1 to 10.7 years (median age: 8.5 years). Pediatric LCTs typically occur during prepubescence. Tumors were located in the right testis in eight cases, the left testis in four cases, and bilaterally in two cases. Common presenting symptoms of LCTs included precocious puberty, testicular enlargement, palpable mass, and incidental findings on ultrasound examination (Table 1). Precocious puberty, a characteristic clinical manifestation, was observed in five cases and was characterized by premature development of pubic hair and penile enlargement. Six other patients presented with a palpable scrotal nodule.

Patient characteristics

Patients with LCTs had a significantly older age at onset (median [Q1, Q3]: 102 months [70.25, 120]) compared to those in the endodermal sinus tumor group (21.50 months [12.25, 35.75]) and the teratoma group (41.50 months [14.25, 122.75]). Approximately 35.71% (5/14) of LCT patients presented with precocious puberty, whereas none of the patients in the endodermal sinus tumor or teratoma groups reported this as their primary symptom. All children with endodermal sinus tumors presented with a palpable scrotal nodule as their chief complaint, compared to 50% (7/14) of LCT patients and 57.14% (8/14)of teratoma patients. Only 14.29% (2/14) of LCT patients presented with incidental findings on physical examination, while 42.86% (6/14) of teratoma patients had this as their primary presentation. Clinical manifestations of endodermal sinus tumors were predominantly characterized by palpable scrotal nodules. The most common presenting complaint in children with LCTs was precocious puberty, whereas children with teratomas typically presented with either a palpable scrotal nodule or incidental findings on physical examination. A statistically significant difference in chief complaints was observed among the three groups (P < 0.001).

Laboratory features

Serum hormone levels, including follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), and testosterone, were evaluated in all participants. Elevated serum testosterone levels were detected in 64.28% (9/14) of LCT patients, of whom five (35.71%) exhibited clinical signs of precocious puberty accompanied by suppressed FSH and LH levels. No LCT patients demonstrated abnormal levels of alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) or beta-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-HCG).

Tumor marker profiles differed significantly among the three groups (Table 2). AFP levels were markedly elevated in 100% (14/14) of patients with endodermal sinus tumors, compared to 0% (0/14) in LCT patients and 14.28% (2/14) in teratoma patients (P < 0.01). Conversely, sex hormone elevation was observed in 64.28% (9/14) of LCT patients, 14.3% (2/14) of endodermal sinus tumor patients, and 7.14% (1/14) of teratoma patients (P < 0.01). NSE levels were normal in 100% of LCT patients (14/14), 71.43% (10/14) of endodermal sinus tumor patients, and 57.14% (8/14) of teratoma patients (P = 0.02). CEA levels remained within normal limits in 100% of LCT patients (14/14), 100% of teratoma patients (14/14), and 85.71% (12/14) of endodermal sinus tumor patients, with no significant difference among groups (P = 0.317).

Radiologic characteristics

All patients underwent scrotal ultrasonography, including conventional B-mode and color Doppler imaging. On grayscale ultrasound, LCT lesions predominantly appeared as hypoechoic masses in 85.71% (12/14) of cases (Table 1; Fig. 1A, D). In contrast, endodermal sinus tumors demonstrated varied echogenicity: 50% (7/14) were heterogeneous (Fig. 2A), 28.57% (4/14) were hyperechoic, and 21.43% (3/14) were isoechoic. Teratomas similarly showed heterogeneous ultrasound characteristics: 35.71% (5/14) were hypoechoic (Fig. 2C), 42.86% (6/14) were heterogeneous, and 21.43% (3/14) were isoechoic. The distribution of echogenicity patterns differed significantly among the three groups (P < 0.01).

Color Doppler ultrasonography revealed distinct vascular patterns among tumor types (Table 2). Peripheral rim hypervascularization was identified in 57.14% (8/14) of LCT patients (Fig. 1B, E), a characteristic feature not observed in teratomas and only one case with endodermal sinus tumor(7.14%,[1/14]). Conversely, central increased blood flow was significantly more common in endodermal sinus tumors (85.71% [12/14]) compared to LCT (7.14% [1/14]) and teratoma groups (7.14% [1/14]) (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2B, D). Normal blood flow patterns were observed in 92.86% (13/14) of teratoma cases.

All patients underwent chest radiography and abdominal and inguinal ultrasonography, with results within normal limits. Advanced bone age was identified in 35.71% (5/14) of LCT cases evaluated. Patients with suspected endodermal sinus tumors underwent contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography (CT) for tumor staging, which identified retroperitoneal lymph node metastasis in 42.85% (6/14) of cases. Two LCT patients underwent contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen and pelvis according to institutional protocol at one of the participating centers. CT imaging demonstrated cystic low-density areas with high-density calcifications within the testicular masses, exhibiting heterogeneous mixed attenuation (Fig. 3). Contrast-enhanced CT effectively delineated characteristic features of tumor subtypes: fat, calcification, and cystic components in mature teratomas (Fig. 2E-F), and heterogeneous enhancement patterns in endodermal sinus tumors (Fig. 2G-H). Enlarged inguinal lymph nodes were occasionally observed in association with endodermal sinus tumors.

Surgical procedure

Testis-sparing surgery (TSS) was performed on all LCT patients (14/14) and teratoma patients (14/14). Intraoperative frozen section examination was conducted in all cases. Neither radical inguinal orchiectomy nor retroperitoneal lymph node dissection was required for these patients. In contrast, all patients with endodermal sinus tumors (14/14) underwent radical inguinal orchiectomy, with 42.85% (6/14) also requiring retroperitoneal lymph node dissection.

Pathologic characteristics

Histopathological examination of all LCT specimens revealed benign tumors with negative surgical margins (14/14). Microscopic examination demonstrated widespread Leydig cells surrounded by an extensive capillary network. The tumor cells exhibited a polygonal morphology with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, distinct cell boundaries, and occasional lipid-laden foamy cells (Fig. 4A-B). No mitotic figures or necrosis were identified. Immunohistochemical staining for inhibin and vimentin was performed in 57.1% (8/14) of cases, demonstrating positive expression in all tested specimens (Fig. 4C-F). According to European Association of Urology Guidelines on Testicular Cancer staging criteria, all LCT cases were classified as stage IA [8].

Follow-up

Complete follow-up data were available for 13 of 14 patients with LCTs (92.9%), with one patient lost to follow-up at 3 months postoperatively. The median follow-up duration was 42 months (range: 15–61 months). During the follow-up period, no patients developed local recurrence or distant metastasis, yielding a disease-free survival rate of 100%. Serial ultrasound examinations demonstrated progressive testicular growth in the operated testis. Testicular volumes achieved symmetry with the contralateral testis within 3–4 years postoperatively in all evaluable cases (Fig. 1C, F). Endocrine outcomes were favorable, with normalization of serum sex hormone levels observed in 76.9% (10/13) of patients with available follow-up data. Among the five patients who initially presented with precocious puberty, complete clinical regression or stabilization of pubertal symptoms occurred in all cases (100%, 5/5) within 6–12 months following TSS.

Follow-up data were available for 12 of 14 teratoma patients (85.7%), with two cases lost to follow-up at 3 months postoperatively. The median follow-up duration was 31 months (range: 5–57 months). No local recurrence or distant metastasis was observed during the follow-up period. Complete follow-up data were obtained for all endodermal sinus tumor patients (14/14, 100%), with a median follow-up duration of 35 months (range: 8-76months). During the fellow-up, no local recurrence or distant metastasis occurred.

Discussion

Leydig cells and Sertoli cells constitute the principal cellular components of sex cord-stromal tumors and perform distinct endocrine and supportive functions within the testicular microenvironment. Leydig cells, residing in the testicular interstitium, synthesize testosterone under luteinizing hormone (LH) stimulation—a process fundamental to the development of male secondary sexual characteristics and maintenance of spermatogenesis.

This multicentric retrospective cohort study analyzed the clinical, biochemical, and radiologic characteristics of 14 pediatric patients with histologically confirmed LCTs and compared these findings with the control groups comprising patients with endodermal sinus tumors and teratomas. Our results demonstrated that precocious puberty, manifesting as premature penile enlargement and pubic hair development, constitutes the pathognomonic clinical presentation in 35.7% (5/14) of pediatric LCT cases. This endocrine manifestation results from autonomous androgen hypersecretion by neoplastic Leydig cells. Nevertheless, half of the LCT patients presented to the clinic due to detection of a testicular mass. Notably, 21.4% (3/14) of patients maintained testosterone levels within age-appropriate reference ranges and were diagnosed incidentally during evaluation for testicular enlargement or routine ultrasonographic screening [9], underscoring the heterogeneity of clinical presentations.

The pathogenesis of pediatric testicular LCTs remains incompletely elucidated. Current evidence suggests that dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, resulting in chronic LH hypersecretion, may induce excessive Leydig cell proliferation and neoplastic transformation. Established risk factors include cryptorchidism, Klinefelter syndrome, SRY-positive 46,XX testicular disorder of sexual development [10–12], and germline DICER1 mutation [13]. Clinicians should maintain heightened vigilance for LCTs when managing patients with these predisposing conditions.

The rarity of LCTs contributes to frequent diagnostic uncertainty on imaging studies, with common misdiagnoses including teratomas, endodermal sinus tumors, and vascular malformations. The differential diagnosis in prepubertal boys presenting with testicular masses requires systematic consideration of age-specific tumor epidemiology and biomarkers. Our comparative analysis revealed distinct clinicopathologic profiles among the three tumor types examined. Endodermal sinus tumors demonstrated a predilection for younger patients (median age: 21.50 months), invariably presenting with markedly elevated serum AFP levels (> 10 ng/mL in 100% of cases) and testicular enlargement. Conversely, LCTs exhibited peak incidence between 5 and 10 years of age (median: 102 months, P = 0.003) with characteristic sex hormone elevation in 64.29% of cases and absence of AFP elevation. Teratomas typically manifested as painless testicular enlargement or incidental ultrasonographic findings without biochemical abnormalities.

Distinguishing LCT from Leydig cell hyperplasia (LCH) presents a diagnostic challenge, as both conditions may manifest with precocious puberty and testicular masses. While clinical and radiological features overlap considerably, definitive differentiation requires comprehensive histopathological evaluation and immunohistochemical analysis, including assessment of inhibin and vimentin expression [14]. However, atypical presentations of LCTs, such as sarcomatous or microcystic pattern, may pose diagnostic challenges.

Ultrasound is a valuable initial screening tool for testicular tumors. Consistent with previous studies, LCT lesions commonly present as homogeneous, hypoechoic, spherical masses on ultrasound, often with peripheral rim hypervascularization on color Doppler imaging [15]. Tumor margins may be smooth or lobulated; although lobulated margins correlate with tumor size in LCTs, they do not independently indicate malignancy. Variability in ultrasound-based diagnosis has been documented, with previous research showing that 11 of 13 borderline LCT cases were misdiagnosed as seminomas, endodermal sinus tumors, lymphomas, or adrenal rest tumors [16]. Endodermal sinus tumors can present as hyperechoic, heterogeneous, or isoechoic, often with increased blood flow, whereas most teratomas demonstrate normal blood flow on Doppler ultrasound. Recent studies have reported the use of contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) in evaluating LCTs and other malignant testicular tumors [17, 18]. As ultrasound technology advances, more sophisticated ultrasound techniques will likely aid in diagnosing pediatirc LCTs.

The Leydig Cell Tumor Severity Score (LeSS) is a pathological tool used to distinguish benign from malignant tumors, incorporating six factors: mitosis, tumor size, necrosis, infiltrative growth pattern, vascular invasion, and risk classification [19]. Additionally, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) or next-generation sequencing (NGS) can detect MDM2 and CDK4 amplification to aid in differential diagnosis [19].

In pediatric oncology surgery, radical orchiectomy is no longer considered essential for all testicular conditions. Instead, there is growing emphasis on preserving the native testis whenever feasible through testis-sparing surgery (TSS). This approach aims to maintain testicular physiological function, including hormone production and spermatogenesis, while minimizing potential long-term impacts on the patient’s quality of life, such as hormonal imbalances and fertility issues. The shift towards TTS is based on improved understanding of the natural history of pediatric testicular diseases, as well as advancements in surgical techniques and intraoperative diagnostic methods (e.g.,intraoperative frozen section examination), which allow for precise tumor removal while preserving healthy testicular tissue. The previous study has demonstrated that intraoperative frozen section examination achieves high accuracy in differentiating between malignant and benign lesions in testicular mass [20]. Most pediatric LCTs are now identified at early stages. Given their predominantly benign nature in children, TSS is widely adopted with intraoperative frozen sections examiniation. Overall survival rates following TSS parallel those of radical inguinal orchiectomy [21]. In our study, all of 14 LCT patients underwent TSS with microscopically negative margins, and no patient suffered a relapse during the follow-up (median: 42 months, range 15–61 months). Furthermore, affected testes grew to volumes comparable to the contralateral healthy testis within 3–4 years postoperatively, suggesting uggesting effective preservation of fertility in these pediatric patients. Previous research also reported the similar results in a cohort of 9 LCT cases, however, 2 patients underwent a subsequent radical orchidectomy due to positive surgical margins in pathological examination [6]. Although pathological sections of remain 4 patients after TSS also indicated positive tumor margins, they still opted for conservative observation. During the follow-up period ranging from 4 to 48 months, no tumor recurrence was observed [6]. EAU-ESPU pediatric urology guidelines on testicular tumors in prepubertal boys recommend TSS for a primary approach in tumors with favorable preoperative ultrasound findings [22]. However, a multicenter retrospective study indicated that TSS does not compromise relapse-free survival over long-term follow-up [23]. Patients undergoing TSS should receive routine testicular ultrasounds every 3–6 months for at least three years postoperatively. In malignant cases, metastasis typically involves retroperitoneal lymph nodes (60%), lungs (38%), liver (29%), and bones (10%) within two years post-surgery [24].

Nevertheless, a significant percentage of LCT patients were under clinical surveillance without specific operative intervention [25]. Active surveillance is considered a safe management strategy for small LCTs, with emphasis on monitoring for testicular failure and tumor recurrence through clinical and radiologic evaluations [26].

Prompt diagnosis, appropriate surgical management, and comprehensive hormonal assessments are essential for optimizing LCT patient outcomes and long-term health. In this study, the affected testes demonstrated significant recovery following TSS, with testicular volume reaching equivalence to that of the unaffected testes within 3 to 4 years, as evidenced by sonographic follow-ups. A multidisciplinary approach involving pediatric urologists, oncologists, endocrinologists, and fertility specialists is critical to providing comprehensive care for pediatric patients with LCT.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, due to the low incidence of pediatric LCTs, the cohort size was relatively small (n = 14), which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Second, the number of medical records contributed by the three participating centers was uneven, potentially introducing selection bias. Third, loss to follow-up occurred in both the LCT and testicular teratoma groups, which might have affected the completeness of outcome data and led to potential bias in the analysis. Additionally, as a retrospective study, this work has inherent limitations, which could impact the validity of the results. Future prospective, multi-center studies with larger and more balanced cohorts are warranted to confirm the conclusions of the present study.

Conclusions

By comparing LCTs with testicular teratomas and endodermal sinus tumors, this study elaborates on the clinical, laboratory, and imaging characteristics of LCTs. It is anticipated that these findings will assist urologists in the early diagnosis of LCTs and reduce the incidence of misdiagnosis. Additionally, this study contributes to the expanding body of evidence supporting that TSS is an effective therapeutic approach for pediatric LCTs while preserving testicular function. Long-term follow-up with scrotal sonography and endocrine evaluation is recommended to support the management of pediatric LCTs.
