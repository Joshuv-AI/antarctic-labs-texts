Myocardial Krüppel‐Like Factor 5 Abundance Holds Prognostic Value for Myocardial Recovery of Patients With Heart Failure Following Mechanical Unloading and Circulatory Support
Authors: Georgios Siokatas, Rana Hamouche, Eleni Maneta, Craig Selzman, Konstantinos E. Hatzistergos, Stavros G. Drakos, Konstantinos Drosatos
Published: 2024

Heart failure (HF) constitutes a global health challenge, wielding its impact as one of the leading causes of mortality. Implantation of left ventricular assist device (LVAD) has emerged as a pivotal intervention in the management of advanced HF and serves either as a temporary bridge to cardiac transplant or as a destination therapy. Recent clinical studies (INTERMACS [Interagency Registry for Mechanically Assisted Circulatory Support], 1 RESTAGE‐HF [Remission from Stage D Heart Failure] 2 ) have remarkably shown reversal of cardiac remodeling and recovery of heart function in a subset of patients with HF and an LVAD. In several cases the clinical improvement meets the criteria for LVAD removal. We have shown that these patients (responders) have a distinct transcriptomic and proteomic signature when compared with the nonresponders. 3 Moreover, our previous research in humans with ischemic HF and mouse models of myocardial ischemia showed that the expression of the transcriptional factor Krüppel‐like factor (KLF)5 is induced in cardiomyocytes and stimulates biosynthesis of ceramides, 4 which constitute a major component of cardiac lipotoxicity. Various studies have established a toxic role of KLF5 for different cell types of the heart, including cardiomyocytes, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and immune cells. 5 We showed that pharmacologic and genetic inhibition of cardiomyocyte KLF5 in mice with ischemia attenuated cardiac remodeling and improved heart function and survival. 4 Thus, KLF5 suppression emerged as a novel therapeutic strategy for treating ischemic cardiomyopathy. 4 To expand our understanding about the involvement of KLF5 in HF progression, we explored whether higher myocardial KLF5 abundance affects myocardial recovery of patients with HF who are subjected to LVAD implantation.

To address this, we enrolled consecutive patients with advanced chronic HF requiring circulatory support with durable continuous‐flow LVAD as bridge‐to‐transplant or destination therapy, at one of the institutions comprising the Utah Transplantation Affiliated Hospitals Cardiac Transplant program. The study was approved by the institutional review board, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients (University of Utah Institutional Review Board 30 622). The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Echocardiographic examinations were performed at the echocardiography laboratories of the participating institutions and stored digitally. The echocardiograms were performed within 2 weeks preceding LVAD implantation and, serially at 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months post‐LVAD implantation. Patients were categorized into 3 groups based on the change in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and left ventricular end‐diastolic diameter within 1 year of LVAD unloading, as follows (Figure [A]): 1.Responders: patients with post‐LVAD LVEF ≥40% and LV end‐diastolic diameter <6.0 cm.2.Partial responders: patients with post‐LVAD LVEF <40% and ΔLVEF (post‐LVAD LVEF – pre‐LVAD LVEF) ≥5%.3.Nonresponders: remaining patients who did not meet the criteria to be classified in any of the previous groups.

Clinical and laboratory data for all patients were collected before and closest to LVAD implantation. Myocardial tissue was prospectively collected from the LV apical core at LVAD implantation, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and subsequently stored in −80°C. Control tissue was acquired from nonfailing donors whose hearts were rejected for transplant for noncardiac reasons such as infection, size mismatch, etc.

Total RNA was purified from the LV tissue and reverse transcribed to cDNA. Quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction was performed for the assessment of human KLF5 mRNA abundance, which was normalized to human RNA polymerase II subunit A (POLR2A) mRNA abundance. Our analysis identified that KLF5 expression levels in responders and partial responders were not significantly different from those in healthy donors (Figure [B]). On the other hand, the nonresponders had significantly higher KLF5 mRNA levels compared with all other groups (Figure [B]). When we compared patients with ischemic cardiomyopathy (ICM) and nonischemic cardiomyopathy (NICM) (Figure [C]), and men with women (Figure [D]), we did not observe significant differences within each group. The difference in myocardial KLF5 mRNA levels between responders and nonresponders was also reflected on the myocardial protein KLF5 content (Figure [E,F]) as shown in samples that were randomly selected across the entire distribution spectrum of KLF5 mRNA abundance (red symbols, Figure [B]). Specifically, protein analysis revealed a 2.3‐fold increase in KLF5 protein levels in the nonresponders group compared with responders. To explore if higher myocardial expression coincides with higher transcription factor activity of KLF5, we assessed mRNA levels of NADPH oxidase 4, which is a direct target of KLF5. 5 As expected, any differences between the responders and the partial responders groups were not statistically significant, but the nonresponders group exhibited significantly elevated NADPH oxidase 4 expression compared with the other 2 groups (Figure [G]). Similarly to KLF5 NADPH oxidase 4 mRNA levels did not show statistically significant differences between patients with ICM and NICM within each group (Figure [H]).

Our new findings corroborate with our previous observations in patients with ICM, 4 suggesting that cardiac KLF5 upregulation may play a role in HF progression. Furthermore, we show that higher myocardial KLF5 mRNA and protein levels before LVAD implantation is indicative of lower probability for myocardial recovery. Therefore, measurement of myocardial KLF5 mRNA or protein levels at the pre‐LVAD stage compared with healthy reference values may be used, in combination with additional prognostic markers of “no response to LVAD,” for stratification of the “high‐KLF5‐expressing” patients to increased risk groups who will be prioritized for heart transplant.
