Diffusion tensor magnetic resonance imaging of the heart: Now feasible on your neighborhood scanner
Authors: David E Sosnovik, Daniel B Ennis
Published: 2024

The recent interest in diffusion-based cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) arises largely from the potential of the technique to provide information about myocardial microstructural remodeling that is not available by other magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques and does not require the use of a contrast agent. Diffusion-based CMR is sensitive to the self-diffusion of water molecules in the myocardium but can be corrupted by the rapid bulk motion of the heart, if not properly accounted for. Pioneering studies showed that the primary direction of diffusion in the heart, also known as the primary eigenvector, reflected the orientation of its cardiomyocytes and could be measured in human subjects [1], [2]. These studies also showed that the secondary eigenvector of diffusion reflected the orientation of the sheetlets in the heart and that this changed substantially from diastole to systole [2].

Advances in MRI technology over the last decade have allowed cardiac diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) to be pursued by a growing number of centers and two principal approaches have emerged. The first utilizes a diffusion-encoded stimulated echo acquisition mode (STEAM) sequence [3], [4], and the second a diffusion-encoded spin echo sequence [5], [6]. The STEAM approach involves a long mixing time and can be performed on systems with standard gradients. The spin echo approach requires the diffusion-encoding gradients to be motion compensated and is optimally performed on systems with high gradient strengths (∼80 mT/m) [7], [8]. Many centers are exploring the importance of protocol parameters (number of diffusion-encoding directions, averages, and resolution), pulse sequences (spin echo and stimulated echo), diffusion-encoding method (b-value, directions, and motion compensation), artifact correction (distortion, fat, and de-noising), timing (late systole vs late diastole), hardware (gradient performance and main-field strength), reconstruction methods, and more.

The move to higher field strengths in the brain offers major gains with few disadvantages. The situation in the heart, however, is more nuanced, and in many centers, CMR is still performed at 1.5T. As Scott et al. point out in their paper [9], 79% of MRI scanners in the United Kingdom are 1.5T scanners and this is likely to be similar or even higher in most countries. Cardiac DTI is constrained by a low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) since the echo times (TE) used are often longer than the T2 of the myocardium and the diffusion-encoding gradients also result in substantial diffusion-related signal loss. While the initial descriptions of cardiac DTI in humans were performed at 1.5T, the prevailing thinking over the last 15 years has been that the gain in SNR at 3T would be of major benefit with such an SNR-constrained technique [4], [7].

In their paper, Scott et al. compared the spin echo and STEAM approaches using similarly equipped 1.5T (Gmax = 45 mT/m, Smax = 200 T/m/s) and 3.0T (Gmax = 60 mT/m, Smax = 200 T/m/s) MRI scanners in a group of 20 healthy subjects [9]. The goal of this work was to evaluate the quality of diffusion tensor cardiovascular magnetic resonance (DT-CMR) obtainable at 1.5T. This investigation is of interest because 1.5T systems are more widely available and are generally more widely used for CMR. Several reports of DT-CMR at 1.5T have suggested that the method is feasible despite the inherently lower SNR at 1.5T [5], [9], [10], but to the best of our knowledge, this is the first direct comparison of both stimulated and spin echo at 1.5T and 3T.

The authors did carefully attempted to control the large number of MRI protocol parameters. This includes a fixed set of diffusion-encoding directions (6), averages, trigger delays (small variations), breath-holds (10), breath-hold duration (18 R-R intervals), and spatial resolution (2.8 × 2.8 × 8.0 mm). There are, however, some protocol differences that other investigators may want to consider. For example, b-values of 150 and 450 s/mm2 were used for the motion-compensated spin echo (MCSE) studies, but the STEAM 3T protocol used 150 and 600 s/mm2. More subtly, the TE for the MCSE approach was shorter at 3T (TE = 62 ms) compared to 1.5T (74 ms) owing to the better gradient performance available for the 3T system. We acknowledge that it is very difficult to control all possible parameters.

It is important to note that the motion-compensation strategy used in the spin echo images refers to the design of diffusion-encoding gradients to null the first and second moments (M1 and M2) of motion (velocity and acceleration) [5], [6]. This should not be confused with motion-compensation techniques designed to address the physical translation of the heart, which cannot be used for DTI of the heart [11]. Of note, recent work suggests that M3 (velocity, acceleration, and jerk) compensated sequences may be of value, and in other contexts (e.g.,1500 mT/m gradients) M1 spin echo has been successfully used [12]. Other motion-compensated gradient waveforms based on convex optimization have also been used [13]. However, in this editorial, we use the term MCSE to refer to M1- and M2-compensated diffusion-encoded spin echo.

The authors use several common DTI-derived metrics to assess the quality of their data [9]. This includes mean diffusivity (MD), fractional anisotropy (FA), helix angle (HA), and sheetlet angle. Estimates of MD and FA were higher and lower, respectively, with MCSE than STEAM with little variation between field strength or cardiac phase. This phenomenon is well understood and is based on differences in the nature of the diffusion encoding with the two approaches. The authors show that DT-CMR was feasible at 1.5T and 3.0T using either breath-hold STEAM or MCSE when imaging was timed to peak-systole, but as shown by others MCSE acquired during late diastole remains unreliable for reasons that are not well understood [13], [14], [15]. Subjective image quality evaluations suggested that the STEAM approach improved at 3.0T, but no significant difference was seen for MCSE between field strengths. For the scientist/clinician new to cardiac DTI, which approach should one then use? Clearly, if one is interested in performing DTI of the heart in both systole and diastole then STEAM imaging on a 3T scanner would be preferable. The added value of obtaining both systolic and diastolic images is that it allows the motion of the cardiac sheetlets between diastole and systole to be compared [16]. The cardiomyocytes in the heart are arranged in small groups (sheetlets), which undergo a radial reorientation in their alignment from diastole to systole. The absolute value of the secondary eigenvector of the diffusion tensor, also termed E2A, reflects the angle of the myocardial sheetlets and is perturbed in a variety of cardiomyopathies [10], [16].

For systolic DT-CMR a substantial body or prior work indicates that both STEAM and MCSE work well. Many of the MCSE studies at 3T have been performed with gradients approaching 80 mT/m [7], [8], [14]. However, with some tradeoffs between spatial resolution and/or b-value, 3T scanners with standard gradient systems can also be used [15]. In their paper, Scott et al. show that MCSE can also be successfully performed on 1.5T scanners with standard gradient systems (45 mT/m) [9]. This lowers a large entry barrier for those without access to advanced 3T scanners and is, therefore, a welcome and important piece of work. Scott et al. reported higher HA gradient values with less variation with MCSE methods compared to STEAM methods. Conversely, the MCSE method seemed less sensitive to measuring changes in E2A [9]. More work is needed to validate in vivo estimates of all of these measures, but cardiac tissue from living subjects remains very difficult to procure.

The advantages of the STEAM approach, and instability of MCSE in diastole, have been discussed above. MCSE of the heart is systole, however, is being increasingly performed, and has several very appealing properties:

•The higher SNR of the MCSE sequence (vs STEAM) allows DTI images with higher spatial resolution to be acquired. While the acquired in-plane resolution of STEAM is typically 2.8 mm [9], [16], the acquired in-plane resolution of MCSE images has ranged from 1.6–2.5 mm [7], [8], [14]. Interestingly, in the study by Scott et al., the HA slope with MCSE was higher than with STEAM, even at the identical spatial resolution [9]. This may be a consequence of the differences in mixing times.

The study by Scott et al. shows that cardiac DTI can be performed without a major loss of quality on standard 1.5T scanners. This will lower the entry barrier for those who do not have access to advanced 3T scanners. However, the paper by Scott et al. also highlights some of the remaining barriers and challenges for more widespread use of cardiac DTI. It took 10 breath-holds (each lasting 18 heartbeats) and an average of 12 min to acquire STEAM data for just one short-axis slice in either systole or diastole [9]. Free-breathing MCSE with retrospective respiratory gating is more efficient, allowing the entire heart to be imaged in 15–20 min, but as noted above it is not suited to dynamic sheetlet imaging. Techniques will need to be developed to speed up the acquisition of the data and image the heart at all cardiac phases. In addition, the analysis of cardiac DTI datasets remains complex and time-consuming, as illustrated in the paper by Scott et al [9]. In their analysis, pipeline-corrupted images were identified and removed manually (1%–5% of images depending on exact conditions). Thereafter, non-rigid co-registration of the data was performed, which can be quite time-consuming, and after the creation of the HA maps, images without the “expected pattern” were subjectively identified and removed (in future studies it might be desirable to replace this subjective assessment with a quantitative metric based on HA range or slope). Finally, areas of the heart with negative eigenvalues and transmural HA profiles with positive slopes or low R2 values were also eliminated from further analysis. Machine-learning solutions will likely mitigate or solve many of these challenges, but for now an “analysis barrier” prevents many interested in cardiac DTI from using the technique.

Scott et al. are to be commended for carefully comparing STEAM and MCSE at 1.5T and 3T [9]. One limitation of their study that merits discussion, however, is the low spatial resolution used. As the authors acknowledge, the in-plane resolution they used falls out of the range used in most MCSE studies [7], [8], [14], and we cannot be sure whether similar results would be obtained with the higher resolutions typically used with MCSE. The TE of MCSE acquisitions is already long due to the time needed for the diffusion-encoding gradients, particularly on scanners with standard gradient systems. Increasing the TE to further increase the spatial resolution could become problematic even at 1.5T. It is probably fair to conclude, therefore, that while Scott et al. have shown that MCSE can work at 1.5T, the approach may not work at higher spatial resolutions.

Overall, the study by Scott et al. demonstrates the capability of obtaining DT-CMR data on a variety of systems and highlights some of the strengths and limitations of the reported approaches. As the field advances, it will be important to define reporting standards so that all key parameters are summarized and to continuously make data available to help define sources of measurement bias. While advanced high-field scanners will continue to provide important opportunities for innovation, Scott et al. have shown that cardiac DTI can be reliably performed on standard clinical scanners. While much remains to be done, their study brings cardiac DTI a step closer to widespread utility and, ultimately, clinical translation.
