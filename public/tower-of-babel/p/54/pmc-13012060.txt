DNA methylation in invertebrate genomes and cell lineage plasticity
Authors: Laurel S. Hiebert, Soojin V. Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The extent of DNA methylation varies widely across animal genomes, from almost undetectable levels in some taxa to high, pervasive methylation in others. Although DNA methylation has been linked to gene regulation, genome defense, and cellular memory, the evolutionary forces shaping its diversity across taxa remain unresolved. Using phylogenetic comparative analysis of 175 invertebrate species spanning 14 phyla, we show that species with greater regenerative capacity exhibit higher genome-wide CpG methylation, independent of phylogenetic relatedness. This relationship suggests that higher genomic DNA methylation is associated with traits indicative of greater somatic lineage renewal and cell lineage plasticity. We propose that, under such conditions, variation in DNA methylation among dividing cells may influence how cell lineages persist, expand, or are replaced within tissues. Thus, we hypothesize that cross-species variation in DNA methylation may reflect differences in somatic lineage renewal and and opportunities for within-body selection.

Variable Patterns of DNA Methylation Across Animals

DNA methylation refers to the addition of a methyl group (CH3) to a nucleotide base. In animal genomes, the most prevalent form of DNA methylation is cytosine methylation, generating 5-methylcytosine (5mC). This process typically occurs at cytosines followed by guanines (CpG dinucleotides). DNA methylation is implicated in development, genome stability, and the maintenance of cell identity (1–3). Yet across metazoans, some species have heavily methylated genomes, while others appear to lack methylation almost entirely; a striking diversity that remains poorly understood (4, 5).

Animal methylomes have traditionally been grouped into three categories: global, mosaic, and nonmethylated (3, 6–8). Global methylomes, like those of vertebrates, exhibit widespread CpG methylation. Mosaic methylomes, common in many invertebrates, show patchy methylation that is often enriched in gene bodies. In some genomes, DNA methylation is undetectable or nearly absent, as seen in Caenorhabditis elegans and dipteran insects (9–11).

However, growing comparative studies challenge the notion of three discrete categories, instead revealing more complex and evolutionarily labile patterns. For instance, the sponge Amphimedon queenslandica exhibits extensive DNA methylation, approaching vertebrate-like levels (12). In a survey of over 500 species, which included 45 invertebrates, genome methylation levels ranged from 2% in the prawn Penaeus to over 80% in the clam worm Alitta succinea (13). Annelid genomes alone span the full range, from unmethylated to highly methylated (13–15). These findings indicate that DNA methylation may change even over short evolutionary timescales (16, 17).

Cellular Memory and Other Proposed Functions of DNA Methylation

DNA methylation is often interpreted as having originated as a genome-defense mechanism (18, 19). In some prokaryotes, DNA methylation marks host DNA and helps restriction-modification systems discriminate self from foreign DNA, leading to the cleavage of unprotected incoming DNA (18–20). In many eukaryotes, including plants and fungi, methylation suppresses transposable elements (TEs), and its genomic extent often correlates with TE content (21–23). Observations from early-diverging animals and their unicellular relatives suggest that methylation may have played an early genome-defense role in ancestral metazoans by silencing TEs and viral genes (23, 24). However, in many invertebrate species, active TE silencing via DNA methylation is absent or minimal (12, 25–27).

The “cellular memory” hypothesis proposes that DNA methylation helps stabilize transcriptional states during development by providing a mitotically heritable record of regulatory state that supports the maintenance of cellular identity (1, 28, 29). This idea echoes Waddington’s original concept of an “epigenetic landscape,” in which developmental trajectories are stabilized through regulatory mechanisms that lock in cell fate (30). Theorists such as Jablonka and Lamb have expanded on this view, emphasizing epigenetic inheritance systems as mechanisms that can help preserve cellular identity across generations of cells (31, 32). Consistent with this framework, aberrant methylation patterns are associated with developmental disorders, cancer, and neurological diseases (33, 34).

Species such as C. elegans and Drosophila develop and differentiate without detectable DNA methylation (9, 10). Under the cellular memory hypothesis, their lack of methylation reflects limited somatic turnover, in which maintaining a stable epigenetic inheritance system offers little advantage. In contrast, longer-lived species tend to retain DNA methylation, possibly reflecting selection for cell identity preservation through ongoing tissue renewal (18, 31, 35). In this vein, DNA methylation is reduced or lost over time in lineages with early cell fate determination and minimal adult cell division, because of the costs associated with maintaining the methylation machinery and possibly the mutagenic potential of methylated cytosines (35, 36). While this framework offers a compelling explanation for interspecies differences in methylation, it is unclear whether tissues with higher turnover consistently depend more on DNA methylation-based memory. Functional tests, such as removing DNA methylation in high-turnover cells and assessing the stability of their identity, remain rare across animal lineages (37, 38).

DNA methylation has also been linked to phenotypic plasticity. DNA methylation and other epigenetic mechanisms may modulate gene expression in response to environmental cues, enabling the same genome to produce different phenotypes in different environments. For example, in honeybees, larval diet plays a critical role in caste determination (39), with diet-induced DNA methylation differences associated with the divergence of queen and worker phenotypes (40, 41). This case illustrates how environmentally responsive methylation can shape phenotypic diversity within species, but whether such plasticity could account for the broad cross-species variation in methylation remains uncertain (42, 43).

DNA Methylation and Cell Lineage Plasticity

The cellular memory hypothesis suggests that DNA methylation preserves transcriptional states through mitotic divisions, thereby maintaining cell identity across successive cell cycles (1). This model is particularly applicable to organisms in which adult tissues are renewed by lineage-restricted progenitors that are already committed to specific fates and could faithfully transmit epigenetic memory.

By contrast, highly regenerative animals do not always rely exclusively on committed progenitors. Instead, some regenerative species regenerate complex structures from a pool of pluripotent or totipotent cells with broad developmental potential, or, in some cases, through dedifferentiation (re-entry into the cell cycle by mature cells), transdifferentiation (direct conversion between differentiated types), or other forms of identity switching (44–46). In these systems, DNA methylation marks may need to be repeatedly reconfigured as cells change identity, making it less likely that DNA methylation serves primarily as a static record of fixed cell types. Regeneration thus challenges the interpretation of DNA methylation as a stable record of cellular identity because cell states are repeatedly redefined rather than inherited. If methylation marks are preserved through these transitions, they could retain residual regulatory patterns that bias or constrain reprogramming; if they are broadly erased, the loss of differential methylation patterns could blur distinctions among emerging lineages.

Among invertebrate animals, species capable of extensive regeneration often exhibit high levels of CpG methylation, whereas taxa with limited or no regeneration often show little or no methylation. This pattern has become clearer as genome-wide methylomes have been generated from an expanding diversity of taxa. For example, sponges, which can regenerate entire bodies from dissociated cells, exhibit high genomic methylation (47). In contrast, nematodes, rotifers, and tardigrades often show relatively limited postembryonic somatic cell proliferation and little or no regenerative capacity, and likewise lack genomic methylation, despite their capacity for physiological plasticity during dormancy (48–52). Annelids show frequent independent shifts in regenerative capacity. Dimorphilus gyrociliatus, a diminutive annelid that lacks regeneration, shows negligible methylation (0.16%), whereas its relative Capitella teleta, capable of posterior regeneration, displays moderate methylation levels (17.1%) (15, 53, 54).

Based on these observations and given the limitations of the cellular memory hypothesis, we posit a potential connection between DNA methylation and cell lineage plasticity. Because DNA methylation states are heritable through mitosis, variation in DNA methylation can both provide a substrate for heritable differences among cell lineages (and thus within-body selection) and influence which lineages persist and expand within tissues. In this model, genomic DNA methylation may reflect how cell lineage stability and renewal are modulated.

Comparative Test of the Cell Lineage Plasticity Model using Regenerative Capacity

To evaluate a key implication of the cell lineage plasticity model in a phylogenetic framework, we examined whether regenerative capacity, used here as a proxy for somatic lineage plasticity, is associated with genomic methylation. Under this model, species with greater regenerative capacity, where proliferation and (in many taxa) dedifferentiation and fate transitions occur, should exhibit higher genomic CpG methylation, whereas the cellular memory hypothesis does not uniquely predict an association with regenerative capacity per se. We compiled DNA methylation and regeneration capacity across 175 invertebrate species spanning 14 phyla, including new WGBS data from Pisaster ochraceus and Aplysia californica (Dataset S1). Given variation in tissue source, life stage, and methodology across studies, cross-species comparisons likely include measurement heterogeneity, but the dataset nonetheless spans a broad cross-section of invertebrate diversity, capturing major evolutionary transitions in both body organization and regenerative capacity.

Each species was assigned to one of three regeneration categories based on the extent and cellular basis of tissue replacement (Fig. 1 and Dataset S2): None or very limited regeneration, Tissue/organ regeneration, or Whole-body regeneration. None or very limited regeneration includes species where regeneration is restricted to wound healing or single-cell repair, without replacement of multiple differentiated cell types (e.g., eutelic taxa such as many nematodes). Tissue/organ regeneration includes species capable of patterned regeneration of multicellular structures such as appendages or posterior body regions, through reactivation of local progenitors or limited dedifferentiation (e.g., limb regeneration in certain arthropods, tail regeneration in cephalochordates). Whole-body regeneration includes species able to reconstruct a complete individual from a body fragment, such that at least two portions produced by mid-body bisection can regenerate the entire organism (e.g., regeneration from tissue fragments in some planarians and colonial tunicates). Regeneration was assessed across larval and adult stages, and species were classified by their highest observed capacity. When data for a given species were unavailable, regenerative capacity was inferred from closely related taxa.

Mapping these traits onto the phylogeny highlights both the taxonomic breadth of our dataset and the recurrent association between regeneration and genomic methylation across the animal tree (Fig. 1). High methylation levels occur in diverse lineages with strong regenerative potential, including sponges and echinoderms. In contrast, extremely low or undetectable methylation is observed in eutelic or nonregenerative taxa such as nematodes, tardigrades, rotifers, and myxozoans. Intermediate methylation often characterizes lineages with partial regenerative ability, including mollusks and certain arthropods. This phylogenetic pattern indicates that the relationship between methylation and regeneration is not confined to a single lineage but observed repeatedly across animals. This pattern provides the comparative foundation for our subsequent analyses, which test whether the qualitative association visible across the tree persists when phylogenetic relatedness is considered.

We used phylogenetic generalized least squares [PGLS; nlme package (55)] to test whether regeneration category is associated with genome-wide DNA methylation while accounting for phylogenetic relatedness. Regeneration category was modeled as a three-level categorical factor, and differences among levels were evaluated with pairwise contrasts. Genomic DNA methylation differed significantly among regeneration categories after accounting for phylogeny (LRT, χ2 = 32.6, P = 8.4 × 10−8, Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Table S3). Species capable of tissue/organ or whole-body regeneration had higher methylation than those with none/limited regeneration (both P < 0.001), whereas tissue/organ and whole-body regenerators did not differ (P = 0.92; SI Appendix, Table S4).

These results indicate that regenerative capacity, whether partial or whole-body, is associated with higher genomic methylation, even after accounting for shared ancestry. Because regeneration often entails repeated rounds of somatic proliferation and, in many taxa, cell fate transitions or dedifferentiation, such systems may generate more opportunities for heritable divergence among proliferating cell lineages. In this context, genomic methylation could influence how such lineage variation is stabilized, constrained, or differentially expanded within tissues, consistent with the predictions of the cell lineage plasticity model. We note that this comparative association does not establish a direct causal relationship. Notably, some taxa with robust regeneration lack detectable CpG DNA methylation. The planarian Schmidtea mediterranea and the trematode Schistosoma mansoni both possess pluripotent stem cells and show extensive regeneration yet lack detectable CpG methylation. These species may rely on alternative epigenetic mechanisms, such as N6-methyladenine (6mA) or histone modifications, which have been implicated in the regulation of regeneration and stem cell function in these taxa (56–61). Overall, these patterns are consistent with the cell lineage plasticity framework, even when CpG methylation itself is lost (61).

Lineage Plasticity Across Tissues and Environments

The positive association between genomic methylation and regenerative capacity across species suggests that analogous relationships may occur across tissues within organisms and across environmental contexts. Here we outline how the same framework generates testable predictions at finer scales, namely variation in methylation with regenerative plasticity across tissues and shifts in lineage contribution across environments.

In invertebrate deuterostomes such as echinoderms and cephalochordates, regeneration often proceeds through local dedifferentiated cells rather than the activation of a common pool of pluripotent stem cells (62). Each tissue may thus regenerate via distinct progenitor populations differing in developmental potential and potentially in methylation state. For example, in the sea cucumber Apostichopus japonicus, DNA methylation is relatively uniform among regenerating internal organs but distinct in the body wall (63), suggestive of tissue-specific differences in progenitor identity and plasticity. In several protostomes, cross-tissue DNA methylation appears more uniform, consistent with regeneration mediated by a shared pool of pluripotent or totipotent stem cells (13, 15). Future tissue-specific methylome data will allow direct tests of these predictions.

Environmental change can shift which epigenetically distinct cell lineages expand, persist, or are eliminated during development, regeneration, or remodeling. In honeybees, larval diet influences queen–worker differentiation, in part through DNA methylation-associated transcriptional programs (40, 41). In horned beetles (Onthophagus spp.), nutritional conditions alter DNA methylation and histone deacetylation, biasing the relative growth of alternative morphs (64–66). In these cases, methylation may influence developmental outcomes not necessarily by reprogramming fate directly but by biasing the relative expansion or persistence of alternative cell lineages.

Somatic Evolution and Heritable Epigenetic Variation Among Somatic Lineages

High regenerative capacity and extensive cell lineage plasticity provide opportunities for differential persistence and expansion among somatic lineages (67–69). When many cells remain proliferative or capable of fate change, heritable variation in growth, differentiation, or survival can lead to differential lineage expansion within tissues. Such somatic selection can contribute to development, homeostasis, and aging, and may be especially pronounced in species that continuously regenerate or remodel tissues (70–73).

While somatic evolution is often discussed in terms of genetic mutation, epigenetic changes occur far more frequently and can similarly influence cellular behavior (38, 74, 75). Epimutations can arise on timescales comparable to cell turnover, generating heritable variation among cell lineages during routine tissue renewal that may be exposed to selection within tissues. DNA methylation may therefore contribute to heritable variation among cells, shaping how variants persist, interact, and spread within tissues (76–78).

Because DNA methylation can alter transcriptional programs relevant to cell-cycle control, metabolism, and stress responses, variation in methylation could bias the relative persistence and proliferation of competing cell lineages within tissues (79, 80). Evidence from cancer biology illustrates this principle: clonal expansions often arise from cells with altered methylation landscapes that confer proliferative advantage (81–83). Consistent with our comparative results, methylation may similarly shape within-body selection among competing lineages in taxa with extensive somatic plasticity (84, 85).

Conclusions and Future Directions

Our comparative analyses demonstrate that genomic DNA methylation levels in invertebrates are significantly associated with regenerative capacity, independent of phylogenetic relatedness, consistent with a link between DNA methylation and somatic plasticity. We propose that DNA methylation provides a heritable substrate for variation among somatic lineages and can influence which lineages persist and expand within tissues (86).

Future work should test whether and how DNA methylation causally relates to cell lineage dynamics during development, regeneration, and tissue maintenance. Integrating single-cell methylome profiling, lineage tracing, and targeted epigenetic perturbations could clarify whether variation in DNA methylation confers competitive advantages among cells or, instead, reflects underlying differences in cell state or fate potential. Tissue-resolved methylomes paired with tissue-specific regenerative phenotypes across taxa may illuminate how epigenetic architecture varies across regenerative strategies. Comparative studies of alternative epigenetic marks, such as 6mA and histone modifications, will also be important for assessing whether distinct epigenetic systems could fulfill similar roles in taxa that lack CpG methylation.

More broadly, our findings highlight the value of comparative epigenomics for informing the functional roles of epigenetic marks. Such approaches may help reveal if and how within-body heritable variation relates to cellular cooperation and conflict.

Materials and Methods

Determination of Genomic CpG Methylation Levels for Pisaster ochraceus and Aplysia californica.

Animals were collected from Santa Barbara, CA. For Pisaster ochraceus, tube foot tissue was sampled; for Aplysia californica, foot tissue was sampled. Specimens were dissected, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and genomic DNA was extracted using a salting-out protocol (87) with an addition of 3M ammonium acetate to precipitate the DNA. DNA was purified using the Zymo DNA Clean & Concentrator-5 kit (Zymo Research, cat. no. D4004). The DNA was then fragmented using a Covaris M220 Focused-Ultrasonicator with a target of 200 bp, and size range was confirmed using the 2200 TapeStation (Agilent) and Genomic DNA ScreenTape (Agilent). After size selection, libraries were prepared with the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, NEB, cat. no. E7645S). Bisulfite conversion was performed using the EpiJET Bisulfite Conversion Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with unmethylated lambda phage DNA (NEB, cat. no. N3013S) included as a conversion control. Bisulfite-converted libraries were PCR amplified using Q5U Hot Start High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (NEB cat. no. M0515S) under the manufacturer’s recommended conditions, except for an annealing temperature of 68 °C for 8 cycles. The amplified libraries were cleaned up with Agencourt AMPure XP Beads (Beckman Coulter, cat. no. A63880).

Sequencing was performed using Illumina paired-end 150 bp reads, generating over 150 million read pairs per sample. The A. californica sample was sequenced by Psomagen on a HiSeq X platform, and the P. ochraceus sample by Novogene on a NovaSeq X Plus. Mean coverage exceeded 20× for both samples. Raw reads were quality-checked and trimmed using Trim Galore v0.6.10 (Krueger; GitHub: https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore), then aligned to the respective reference genomes (P. ochraceus: GCA_010994315.2; A. californica: GCF_000002075.1 assembly AplCal3.0, Broad Institute) using Bismark v0.24.0 (88, 89). To assess bisulfite conversion efficiency, reads were also mapped to the unmethylated lambda phage genome. Bisulfite conversion efficiency was over 99% for both samples. Methylation calls for all cytosine contexts were deduplicated and extracted. Global CpG methylation percentage was averaged to obtain a single genome-wide methylation estimate per species.

Data Compilation, Phylogenetic Reconstruction, and Assessment of Phylogenetic Signal.

We compiled genome-wide CpG methylation data for 175 invertebrate species, including two newly sequenced (P. ochraceus and A. californica) and 173 from published sources (Dataset S1). When multiple studies or tissue types were available for a single species, values were averaged.

Each species was assigned to one of three regeneration capacity categories based on a survey of the primary literature and reviews (Dataset S2):1.None/limited: no regeneration beyond wound healing or replacement of isolated cell types (e.g., nematodes, tardigrades).2.Tissue/organ: regeneration of multicellular structures such as appendages or tail but not full body reconstitution (e.g., arthropods with limb regeneration, amphioxus tail regeneration, mantle regeneration in mollusks).3.Whole-body: regeneration of an entire individual from at least two separate body fragments following bisection or fission (e.g., Hydra, colonial tunicates, some planarians, some annelids).

This framework reflects increasing degrees of regenerative potential and cell lineage plasticity across taxa. In some cases, the regeneration category was inferred from closely related species when direct data were unavailable, and the highest level of regenerative capacity across all life stages (including larval) was used for classification.

A taxonomy-based full phylogeny was also generated using PhyloT (NCBI Taxonomy, http://phylot.biobyte.de) and visualized with iTOL v7 (90) for illustration of methylation and regeneration data across all 175 species (Fig. 1). A time-calibrated phylogenetic tree was constructed using divergence times from TimeTree (91), providing resolved relationships for 119 of the 175 species. The tree was processed using the R packages ape (92) and phytools (93) and ultrametricized by non-negative least squares (NNLS). Zero and near-zero branch lengths (<10−6) were replaced with 10−4 to ensure numerical stability.

Raw methylation values were right-skewed; we applied a Tukey power transformation (λ ≈ 0.35) implemented with rcompanion (94). Normality was evaluated using Shapiro–Wilk and Anderson–Darling tests [nortest; (95)] (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 and Table S1).

Phylogenetic signal was quantified using Pagel’s λ, implemented in phytools, revealing a strong phylogenetic component in methylation levels (λ = 0.88, P < 10−22; SI Appendix, Fig. S2 and Table S2).

Phylogenetic Comparative Models.

We used phylogenetic generalized least squares (PGLS) models implemented in the nlme package (55) to test whether regeneration capacity correlates with genome-wide CpG methylation levels while accounting for phylogenetic relatedness. The dependent variable was the Tukey-transformed methylation level, and the predictor was the regeneration capacity as a categorical variable (None/limited, Tissue/organ, Whole-body). A Pagel’s λ correlation structure (corPagel, form = ~Species) was fitted from the 119-species ultrametric tree.

Model parameters were estimated by maximum likelihood. Significance was assessed by likelihood-ratio tests comparing nested ML-fitted PGLS models (anova in nlme). Estimated marginal means and pairwise contrasts were computed with emmeans (96) (SI Appendix, Table S4). Assumptions were checked via residual and influence diagnostics (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 and Table S5).

To test robustness to incomplete taxon sampling, we repeated the analysis using an expanded 175-taxon grafted tree (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 and Tables S3 and S6). Species absent from the calibrated backbone were added using a taxonomy-guided grafting approach implemented in R. For each species, hierarchical taxonomic ranks (genus–family–order–class–phylum) were retrieved from GBIF via the rgbif (97) function name_backbone(). Missing taxa were sequentially attached to the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) node of the lowest shared rank in the calibrated tree, determined using phytools and ape. When no MRCA was available, species were inserted as sisters to the closest matching tip based on shared higher-level ranks.

For each internal node corresponding to a recognized taxonomic clade, a Yule (pure-birth) speciation rate (λ) was estimated using diversitree (make.yule, find.mle) (98), and branch lengths for grafted species were scaled to 1/λ of the corresponding clade. If no rate estimate was available, a default of 0.01 was used. After all species were added, the composite tree was ultrametricized using non-negative least squares (force.ultrametric, method = “nnls”) to ensure equal root-to-tip distances. This yielded a 175-taxon tree, which produced results consistent with the calibrated 119-species analysis (ΔAIC = 3.7; χ² = 32.6; P < 10−7; λ = 0.87).

Analyses were performed in R 4.5.2 using ape v5.8.1, phytools v2.5.2, nlme v3.1.168, diversitree v0.10.1, rgbif v3.8.4, rcompanion v2.5.1, emmeans v2.0.0, and nortest v1.0-4.
