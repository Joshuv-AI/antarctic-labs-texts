Effects of isolated gastrocnemius tightness on foot posture, strength, function, and balance in children aged 7–16: a case-control study
Authors: Fatma Eren-Zengin, Deniz Tuncer, Hakan Senaran, Gokcer Uzer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIsolated gastrocnemius tightness (IGT) has been linked to foot conditions, but its clinical relevance in children remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate foot posture, muscle strength, functional performance, and postural control in children with IGT.MethodsFourteen children diagnosed with IGT, defined as having passive ankle dorsiflexion < 10° with the knee extended and ≥ 10° with the knee flexed according to the Silfverskiold test, and 14 age-matched children without IGT were included in the study. Foot posture was assessed using the Foot Posture Index (FPI-6), while lower extremity muscle strength was measured with a MicroFET2 handheld dynamometer. Functional performance was evaluated through single-leg and double-leg horizontal jump tests, and postural control was analyzed using the Biodex Balance System (BBS).ResultsChildren with IGT exhibited significantly poorer foot posture and ankle mobility compared to healthy controls, with very large effect sizes in foot posture index (FPI-6; d = 3.59 right, 3.31 left) and dorsiflexion range (e.g., active dorsiflexion in knee extension: 0.21° vs. 19.14°, d = 3.59). Muscle strength was also lower, particularly in the iliopsoas (right: 8.16 vs. 10.76 kgf, d = -1.24) and gluteus maximus (left: 8.71 vs. 11.42 kgf, d = -1.30). In functional assessments, the IGT group performed worse in horizontal jump tests, with reduced distances in both double-leg (126.10 vs. 142.75 cm, d = -0.50) and single-leg jumps (103.82 vs. 136.00 cm, d = -0.98). BBS data showed impaired postural stability (e.g., overall stability index: 0.76 vs. 0.43, d = 0.90) and lower limits of stability scores, especially in backward (37.79 vs. 66.00, d = -1.27) and diagonal directions (e.g., backward/left: 43.07 vs. 63.50, d = -1.08). Furthermore, sway scores were higher in the IGT group during the sensory organization test, particularly under eyes open/firm surface (0.89 vs. 0.52, d = 1.12) and foam conditions, indicating balance deficits.ConclusionsChildren with IGT exhibit notable impairments in foot posture, muscle strength, functional performance, and postural control compared to their healthy peers. These findings underscore the need for further research to confirm the observed associations in children with IGT and may help guide early identification efforts and the development of future intervention strategies aimed at improving functional outcomes.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT06363136 Registration date: 04.09.2024.

Introduction

Limited dorsiflexion, typically caused by tightness in the gastrocnemius-soleus complex, affects normal gait and can lead to compensatory movements that increase stress on the lower limb. Over time, such biomechanical changes may contribute to pain and structural problems [1, 2].

Isolated gastrocnemius tightness (IGT) is one form of equinus that specifically involves the gastrocnemius muscle [2, 3]. IGT in children may lead to several observable gait and postural deviations, including toe walking, premature heel rise during midstance, and flexible pes planovalgus. These manifestations are often attributed to limited ankle dorsiflexion, which alters the normal rocker mechanism and shifts loading patterns toward the midfoot. Prolonged compensatory pronation and increased ligamentous strain may contribute to medial arch collapse, while persistent pronated posture may, in turn, cause adaptive shortening of the gastrocnemius–soleus complex, reinforcing a cycle of dysfunction [4–6].

The IGT is based on clinical evaluation using the Silfverskiold test. In this test, the equinus deformity present with the knee extended disappears when the knee is flexed [7]. There is no consensus on the threshold at which the gastrocnemius is considered to be tight in children. While Liyanarachi et al. [8] suggest that IGT should not be interpreted as pathological in children, Sussman [9] emphasizes the challenges in establishing consistent diagnostic thresholds for IGT and the importance of considering age-specific norms. This debate highlights the ongoing need for standardized measures and clearer understanding of gastrocnemius tightness in pediatric populations.

Decreased ankle dorsiflexion, often observed in IGT, and flatfoot, can significantly alter gait patterns in children. This limitation in dorsiflexion typically results in compensatory mechanisms, such as increased knee and hip flexion during the stance phase of gait [10, 11]. Children who toe-walk present with substantial alterations in the gastrocnemius muscle functional properties, which appear well adapted to the characteristic demands of equinus gait [12]. In children with flatfoot, reduced ankle power during push-off is commonly observed, affecting gait efficiency and increasing energy expenditure. Associations between gait characteristics and clinical measures like pain symptoms and quality of life were explored [13].

IGT may cause limited ankle dorsiflexion and flat foot. Therefore, the literature was reviewed broadly; however, pediatric studies examining these conditions from a functional and biomechanical perspective remain limited. A recent comparative study showed that children with idiopathic toe-walking demonstrated decreased postural control and reduced lower extremity muscle endurance compared to typically developing peers [14]. In a large-scale pediatric study including 79 children with pes planus and 105 with neutral foot posture, no significant differences were found in 17 balance parameters. However, children with pes planus tended to show slightly poorer postural control, suggesting that early clinical interventions such as physiotherapy may help improve balance in this population [15].

Although studies directly focusing on IGT in healthy children are relatively scarce [8], available research indicates that ankle dorsiflexion tends to decrease with age in this population [16–18], suggesting a potential age-related pattern in gastrocnemius flexibility. A study conducted on schoolchildren aged 5–15 years in Norway highlighted that ankle dorsiflexion decreases with increasing age in this population, emphasizing the importance of age-appropriate norms. When the ankle dorsiflexion threshold is considered ≤ 5°, the majority (approximately 55%) of their study population was found to have gastrocnemius tightness. The authors concluded that IGT should not necessarily be interpreted as a pathological finding [8]. However, it remains unclear whether gastrocnemius muscle tightness is a normal finding in children, as the frequency of recurrent leg pain in this population has also been reported [19].

Dorsiflexion is generally higher during childhood and tends to decrease with age. Therefore, dorsiflexion limitations observed in children—particularly when restricted below 10°—are concerning, as they may indicate an early risk for worsening restriction over time. Literature suggests that severe dorsiflexion limitations in adulthood are associated with pathological foot deformities, which can negatively affect gait and functional performance. Consequently, dorsiflexion restrictions present in childhood may predispose individuals to biomechanical dysfunction and secondary musculoskeletal problems later in life.

Although IGT has been widely studied in adults and associated with conditions such plantar fasciitis, metatarsalgia, plantar ulcers, Achilles tendinopathy, pes planus, posterior tibial tendon dysfunction, and hallux valgus in adults [20–22], to our knowledge, no studies have directly investigated the clinical presentation and functional impact of IGT in children. This study aims to address this gap by evaluating the effects of IGT on foot posture, muscle strength, functional performance, and postural control in a pediatric sample. It is hypothesized that children with IGT will exhibit impaired foot posture, reduced ankle dorsiflexion-related muscle strength, decreased jump performance, and impaired postural control compared to their peers without IGT, due to the biomechanical consequences of limited gastrocnemius extensibility.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional and observational study was approved by the Bezmialem Vakif University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee on 14.06.2023 (Decision no: 111176) and registered on the ClinicalTrials.gov website (number: NCT06363136).

Sample size

The sample size was calculated by the G-Power 3.1 (Universitat Kiel, Germany) [23]. Based on Kim et al.‘s study [24] with flexible pes planus individuals, an anterior/posterior sway index, and a power of 95%, the sample size for each group was calculated to be 14 (α = 0.05, d = 1.31).

Participants

Fourteen children (2 girls, 12 boys) with IGT followed at the Orthopedics and Traumatology Department of Bezmialem Vakif University and an age-matched group of 14 children (5 girls, 9 boys) without IGT were recruited in the study.

IGT was diagnosed using the Silfverskiold test [7]. Passive ankle dorsiflexion was measured with a universal goniometer in two positions: with the knee extended in supine and with the knee flexed in prone. The diagnostic threshold was defined as < 10° of dorsiflexion in knee extension and at least a 10° increase in dorsiflexion when the knee was flexed [3]. The inclusion criteria were: being diagnosed as bilateral IGT, being between the ages of 7–16 years old, and having normal body mass index. The exclusion criteria were having high femoral anteversion, internal tibial torsion, metatarsus adductus, hypermobility (Beighton score > 4), presence of leg length discrepancy, presence of any neurological, rheumatological, musculoskeletal, metabolic, and/or connective tissue disorders. having a history of deformity or surgery related to the vertebral column or lower extremities, presence of cognitive, intellectual, and/or severe psychiatric disorders, and participation in any exercise program or sports activity in the past six months. For the control group, the same criteria were applied, except that a negative bilateral Silfverskiold test and the absence of pes planovalgus were required for inclusion.

Study procedure

The study conducted at the Pediatric and Cardiac Physiotherapy and Rehabilitation Education and Research Laboratory of Bezmialem Vakif University Faculty of Health Sciences, Department of Physiotherapy and Rehabilitation and the protocol followed of the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines.

Parents of all participating children were informed about the study’s objectives, duration, and procedures. An informed consent form was signed by each parents.

Children and their families who agreed to participate were given an appointment with the scheduled date and time for the evaluation. Following the completion of a sociodemographic questionnaire, clinical assessments were conducted for eligible children on the same day. All tests were performed barefoot. Prior to data collection, all participants were familiarized with the testing procedures through verbal instructions, demonstrations, and practice trials to ensure comprehension and reduce performance-related anxiety.

After recording the gender, age, body weight, height, body mass index (BMI), and dominant side of all children, clinical assessments were conducted. These included bilateral active/passive ankle dorsiflexion range of motion measurements, lower extremity muscle tightness evaluations (Silfverskiold test, Thomas test, Duncan-Ely test, popliteal angle test), sit-and-reach test, bilateral weight-bearing lunge test, Feiss line test (longitudinal arch angle), and navicular drop test.

The ankle dorsiflexion range was measured actively and passively in two positions: supine with the knee extended and prone with the knee flexed. A goniometer was used, with the stationary arm placed along the fibula shaft and the moving arm tracking the 5th metatarsal.

Lower extremity muscle tightness evaluations were conducted as follows: the Silfverskiold test was used to assess gastrocnemius tightness, the Thomas test for hip flexor tightness, the Duncan-Ely test for rectus femoris flexibility, and the popliteal angle test for hamstring tightness.

Outcome measures

Foot posture

Children’s foot postures were assessed using the Foot Posture Index (FPI-6), a practical method for evaluating foot alignment in multiple planes. The FPI-6 has demonstrated good validity and diagnostic accuracy for determining pediatric flexible flatfoot [25]. The test scores range from − 12 to + 12, based on six analyses scored between − 2 and + 2. The assessment was performed while the child stood comfortably, arms at their sides, facing forward. Positive scores indicated pronation, and negative scores indicated supination [26].

Lower extremity muscle strength

Lower extremity muscle strength was measured using a MicroFET2 handheld dynamometer (MicroFET2 force gauge, Hoggan Health Industries, Salt Lake City, Utah, USA), a method that has been shown to be reliable and valid for use in pediatric populations, including children as young as two years old [27, 28]. The strength of bilateral hip flexor-extensor, abductor, knee flexor-extensor, and ankle dorsiflexor-plantar flexor muscles were assessed. The tests were performed using the positions required for the muscle testing method described by Lovett, without compensatory movements. To ensure maximal effort during the muscle strength testing, verbal encouragement was provided throughout the assessment. The values resulting from isometric contraction were recorded as kilogram force. Measurements were repeated three times for five seconds bilaterally, and the mean value was recorded. A 30-second rest was taken between repetitions for the same muscle.

Functional performance

Functional performance was assessed using both double-leg and single-leg horizontal jump tests. The tests were conducted using the Eurofit test battery (European Council’s 1988). These tests have demonstrated good validity and diagnostic accuracy for assessing lower extremity functional performance in children [29, 30]. The dominant leg was defined as the preferred leg for kicking a ball for single-leg horizontal jump test. The children positioned at the designated line with feet shoulder-width apart for the double-leg jump or standing on one leg for the single-leg jump. For both tests, the child was instructed to bend their knees and explosively jump forward as far as possible, using their arms for momentum. The distance was measured from the start line to the back of the heel. Each jump was performed twice and the best result was recorded in centimeters as the score. A 30-second rest was provided between tests.

Postural control

The Biodex Balance System (Biodex, Inc., Shirley, New York, USA) is a valid and reliable instrument for evaluating of postural stability and balance [31]. All children completed the postural stability test assessing static balance, the limits of stability test assessing dynamic balance, and a modified clinical test for sensory interaction and balance (mCTSIB) assessing postural oscillations in four different conditions (eyes open/closed, firm/foam surface) on this platform.

During testing, the child was first asked to center the cursor on the screen comfortably. At that point, the foot position on the platform was recorded using the system’s coordinate grid. The same coordinates were carefully maintained in all three tests to ensure consistency in foot placement across assessments.

Postural stability test; evaluates the child’s ability to maintain their center of balance on a stable platform and records deviations from the center. The overall, anterior/posterior, and medial/lateral stability indices are calculated. A higher score indicates greater postural instability. Three measurements of 20 s each with 10-second breaks between them were taken.

Stability limits test; measures a child’s capacity to shift and control their center of mass within a defined range, assessing balance and postural stability across various directions. The test conducted on a static platform. Directional control (overall, forward, backward, left, right, forward/left, forward/right, backward/left, backward/right) is assessed and expressed as a percentage. Higher scores reflect better postural control (100% = excellent control).

mCTSIB assesses how different sensory inputs contribute to balance and the child’s ability to compensate when one or more sensory inputs are removed. The test was performed on a static platform with eyes open and closed, both on firm and foam surfaces and the sway index for each position was recorded. Four 30-second measurements with 10-second breaks were taken for each condition.

Statistical analyses

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 26, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics (mean ± standard deviation, number, and percentage) were provided for discrete and continuous variables. The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to assess the normality of data distribution. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. For between-group comparisons, independent t-tests (for normally distributed variables) and Mann-Whitney U tests (for non-normally distributed variables) were used. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were calculated to assess the magnitude of differences, with values ranging from small (0.2) to large (0.8 or above).

Results

This study included 14 children with bilateral IGT group and 14 healthy controls. In the IGT group, there were 2 girls (14.3%) and 12 boys (85.7%), while in the control group, there were 5 girls (35.7%) and 9 boys (64.3%). The mean values for age (years), height (cm), body weight (kg), BMI (kg/m²), and dominant lower extremity side are presented in Table 1. There were no statistically significant differences between the groups in these parameters (p > 0.05).

Muscle flexibility

The IGT group demonstrated significantly greater muscle tightness compared to the control group in the gastrocnemius (assessed by the bilateral Silfverskiold test, p < 0.001) and iliopsoas muscles (assessed by the Thomas test, p = 0.008). In contrast, there were no significant between-group differences in rectus femoris flexibility (Duncan-Ely test, p > 0.05), hamstring flexibility (popliteal angle test, p > 0.05), or sit and reach test (p > 0.05).

Ankle dorsiflexion range of motion and weight-bearing lunge test

Children in the IGT group exhibited significantly reduced ankle dorsiflexion compared to the control group, as evidenced by active and passive goniometric measurements in both knee extension and flexion positions in a non-weight bearing setting (p < 0.001). Similarly, the IGT group showed significantly lower dorsiflexion values in the bilateral weight-bearing lunge test for both the right and left sides (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Foot posture

The IGT group demonstrated significantly lower ankle dorsiflexion range of motion, reduced performance in the weight-bearing lunge test, and more pronated foot posture compared to the control group (p < 0.01). The effect sizes for these group differences ranged from large to very large (Cohen’s d = 1.11–3.59), indicating substantial clinical relevance (Table 2).

Lower extremity muscle strength

Statistically significant differences in muscle strength were observed between the IGT and control groups. The IGT group showed reduced iliopsoas (p = 0.017) and gluteus maximus (p = 0.002) strength, with medium to large effect sizes (Cohen’s d = − 0.88 to − 1.30). However, no significant differences were found for the strength of other muscles (p > 0.05) (Table 3) (Fig. 1).

Functional performance

There was a statistically significant difference between the IGT and control groups in both the double-leg horizontal jump test (p = 0.008) and the single-leg horizontal jump test (p = 0.012), with the IGT group demonstrating poorer performance. The effect size was medium for the double-leg horizontal jump test (Cohen’s d = − 0.50) and large for the single-leg horizontal jump test (Cohen’s d = − 0.98) (Table 4).

Postural control

There were significant differences between the IGT and control groups in postural stability parameters - reflecting static balance (overall stability index: p = 0.023, Cohen’s d = 0.90, large effect; anteroposterior stability index: p = 0.029, d = 0.89, large effect), as well as in limits of stability measures - reflecting dynamic balance (overall: p = 0.014, d = − 1.00, large effect; backward: p = 0.002, d = − 1.27, very large effect; forward/left: p = 0.043, d = − 0.80, large effect; forward/right: p = 0.010, d = − 1.04, very large effect; backward/left: p = 0.009, d = − 1.08, very large effect), with the IGT group demonstrating poorer balance performance. Similarly, in the mCTSIB, the IGT group exhibited greater postural sway indicated by higher sway index scores under under eyes open on firm surface (p = 0.007, d = 1.12, very large effect), eyes open on foam surface (p = 0.027, d = 0.89, large effect), and in the composite score (p = 0.025, d = 0.90, large effect) (Table 5) (Fig. 2a, b and c).

Discussion

This study was conducted to examine the foot posture, lower extremity muscle strength, functional performance, and postural control of children with IGT and to compare them with their healthy peers. In the literature, it was found only one study evaluating gastrocnemius muscle tightness in children [8]; however, no studies have been encountered that assess foot posture, muscle strength, functional performance, and postural control in children with IGT. Although normative data are available for a healthy population and ankle dorsiflexion measurements in patients with foot and ankle disorders, there is no consensus on when the gastrocnemius should be considered tightened. Some authors focus on ankle dorsiflexion with the knee extended, while others emphasize the difference in ankle dorsiflexion measured with the knee extended and flexed. However, comparing the results of these studies is challenging because some of the measurements use invalid methods, and the tests are not standardized [32–35]. Ankle dorsiflexion was assessed in two conditions: without weight-bearing and during bilateral weight-bearing using the lunge test. Significant differences were found between the IGT and control groups, with the IGT group showing reduced dorsiflexion compared to healthy peers.

IGT is defined in various ways in the literature. Barouk [36] suggested an increase of at least 13° in ankle dorsiflexion with the knee flexed compared to the negative or neutral ankle dorsiflexion when the knee is extended as the threshold for isolated tightness, while DiGiovanni et al. [3] considered angles of 5° and 10° indicative of gastrocnemius tightness. These conclusions were based on expert opinions. Biomechanical studies show that at least 10° of ankle dorsiflexion is needed during the stance phase of gait to allow tibial advancement [32–35]. In the current study, a Silfverskiold test was considered positive, and IGT was identified when passive dorsiflexion with the knee extended was < 10° and increased by ≥ 10° with the knee flexed [3]. It is essential to lock the talonavicular joint and relax the anterior muscles to avoid inaccurate results [3, 36].

Foot posture

The relationship between equinus and flatfoot deformity remains a topic of debate, particularly regarding whether equinus is a cause or a consequence of flatfoot [4–6, 37]. Two main theories attempt to explain the relationship between IGT - a common cause of functional ankle equinus - and flatfoot deformity. The first suggests that limited ankle dorsiflexion due to IGT leads to compensatory mechanisms, primarily within the foot. This results in prolonged subtalar joint pronation and an unlocked midtarsal joint, increasing midfoot mobility and ligamentous strain, which may contribute to the development of flexible flatfoot. Additionally, the altered biomechanics reduce the supinatory function of the Achilles tendon, further exacerbating foot collapse [37]. Alternatively, it has been proposed that flatfoot itself may contribute to the development of equinus. Excessive pronation and medial column instability increase midfoot dorsiflexion and flatten the arch, resulting in a plantarflexed talus and everted calcaneus, which over time cause adaptive shortening of the triceps surae complex, including the gastrocnemius muscle [5]. As a result, the foot remains more flexible than required during propulsion, limiting its lever function and contributing to a flatter foot posture. In turn, this altered foot mechanics and premature propulsion may impair jump performance by reducing force transmission and altering ground reaction force vectors. Moreover, compensations such as eversion of the tarsal joints to accommodate for restricted ankle dorsiflexion may exacerbate foot instability and hinder functional performance. Tightness in gastrocnemius muscle restricts ankle dorsiflexion when the knee is extended, causing a mechanical imbalance that leads to subtalar joint pronation and the collapse of the medial arch. These changes can further exacerbate flatfoot deformity due to increased stress on the plantar fascia and tibialis posterior tendon [5]. The literature has supported the clinical relevance of gastrocnemius tightness in pediatric flatfoot. Ford et al. categorized pediatric flatfoot into three subtypes, emphasizing that flexible flatfoot with Achilles tightness constitutes a distinct and clinically significant subgroup [38], affecting approximately 27% of cases and often presenting with midfoot pain and functional limitations [39, 40]. These findings align with our results, highlighting the biomechanical burden of IGT on foot posture and function in children. Unlike simple flexible flatfoot, this type is more likely to cause long-term discomfort and disability if left untreated. Importantly, symptoms such as abnormal gait, poor balance, and motor dysfunction associated with gastrocnemius or Achilles tightness may persist into adulthood [21, 41].

However, Liyanarachchi et al. [8] found no relationship between gastrocnemius tightness and pes planus using footprint analysis methods. This difference may be due to the sensitivity of the methods and measurement approaches used. In the present study, the comparison between the IGT and control groups also more clearly highlights the impact of gastrocnemius tightness on pes planus. Although Liyanarachchi et al. found no link between equinus and foot posture, the severity of equinus differs between samples. Their study showed an 18° dorsiflexion difference, indicating a clearer gastrocnemius tightness. In contrast, our participants had a smaller (~ 10°) difference with lower dorsiflexion in both positions, suggesting a more severe equinus pattern, which may explain the flexible flatfoot observed in our sample.

Lower extremity muscle strength

A review reported that injuries associated with pronated feet are linked to weakness in the trunk and hip muscles [42]. Prolonged foot pronation may lead to functional shortening of the limb, increased internal rotation of the lower limb, and anterior pelvic rotation, placing strain on the iliopsoas, piriformis, and gluteal muscles [43]. A study conducted in adults reported a reduction in isokinetic concentric strength of the hip flexors, extensors, internal and external rotators in patients with pes planus. The present study yielded similar results to those in the literature [44]. In our study, a significantly greater number of children in the IGT group exhibited a positive Thomas test - indicating iliopsoas tightness - compared to the control group. Additionally, measurements using a manual muscle dynamometer revealed that bilateral iliopsoas and bilateral gluteus maximus muscle strength were significantly weaker in the IGT group compared to healthy peers. This proximal compensation mechanism aligns with Janda’s theory of lower crossed syndrome, which describes how tight iliopsoas and weak gluteal muscles can result from postural adaptations to biomechanical constraints.

In the context of IGT, insufficient ankle dorsiflexion may promote anterior pelvic tilt and increased reliance on the hip flexors, ultimately contributing to gluteus maximus inhibition and altered lumbopelvic stability [45, 46]. These findings suggest that muscle imbalances in the lower extremity may contribute to biomechanical and postural changes in children. Tightness in the hip flexors in children with IGT, indicated by a positive Thomas test, can reduce hip extension and cause anterior pelvic tilt, similar to children with cerebral palsy [47]. In a study examining muscle strength and arch height, no significant difference was found between ankle muscle strength and pes planus in young adult women aged 18–24 years [48]. Similarly, a study with 49 participants (23 women, 26 men, aged 18–45 years) with pronated feet reported no significant differences in tibialis posterior, tibialis anterior, and peroneal muscle strength [49].

The selective weakness of the iliopsoas and gluteus maximus may be attributed to compensatory postural adaptations resulting from restricted dorsiflexion, such as anterior pelvic tilt and altered lumbopelvic mechanics, as described in the lower crossed syndrome. In contrast, the strength of other muscles may have been preserved through compensatory activation or reduced mechanical load, especially in a young population with adaptive neuromuscular plasticity. These findings support the notion that IGT may selectively impact proximal hip stabilizers while sparing distal or biarticular muscles depending on the degree of mechanical demand and compensation strategies.

Functional performance

In functional performance evaluation, parameters such as power, strength, and flexibility were assessed using single-leg and double-leg horizontal jump tests. While some studies in young adults [50, 51] reported no relationship between foot posture or arch height and performance, the resultsof the current suggest that restricted ankle dorsiflexion and a higher tendency for pes planus in children with IGT contributed to poorer jump performance. A study on 218 children who underwent performance tests reported no disadvantages in sports performance due to flat feet [52]. While flat feet al.one may not impair sports performance, the combined effect of gastrocnemius tightness and dorsiflexion restriction may contribute to functional limitations. This is supported by a review stating that ankle dorsiflexion range of motion influences sports performance and injury risk [53].

Postural control

The Biodex system was used to assess static and dynamic balance as well as postural sway, and found significant differences, with the control group performing better. Previous studies have shown that flat feet are linked to balance disorders [54–56]. The current study suggests that children with IGT not only have flat feet but also experience reduced dorsiflexion compared to their healthy peers. Other research in children has reported that limited dorsiflexion can affect balance and may predispose individuals to ankle injuries [57, 58]. Additionally, foot type can influence postural control strategies, as the feet provide both a base of support and somatosensory input [55, 59]. Gastrocnemius tightness may limit ankle dorsiflexion, affecting forward and backward movement. However, it is important to consider that balance impairments could also reflect an underlying neuromuscular deficit, such as impaired proprioception or central nervous system processing, which requires further exploration.

There are several limitations to this study. First, although the standardization of the assessment methods used in the study has been ensured, measurement differences may exist between different researchers. Second, due to the distinct clinical features of children with IGT—such as toe-walking and early heel-rise—blinding of the assessors was not feasible, which may introduce potential assessment bias. Third, while the literature supports that gastrocnemius muscle tightness can cause pain in children, pain was not assessed in this study. Fourth, the literature emphasizes the important role of intrinsic foot muscle activation in foot posture and stabilizing the foot, but intrinsic foot muscle strength was not evaluated in this study. Finally, the relatively small sample size and recruitment from a single clinical center limit the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

This study is the first to assess foot posture, muscle strength, functional performance, and postural control in children with IGT. It was found significant differences between the IGT and control groups in ankle joint range of motion, foot posture, muscle strength, functional performance, and postural control. These results highlight the need for comprehensive clinical assessments, including tools such as the FPI-6 for evaluating static foot alignment, the Biodex Balance System for assessing postural control, and handheld dynamometry for measuring muscle strength, to facilitate early identification and monitoring in children with IGT. Accordingly, targeted physiotherapy interventions focusing on improving muscle flexibility, foot muscle strength, and postural control may enhance functional outcomes in this population.
