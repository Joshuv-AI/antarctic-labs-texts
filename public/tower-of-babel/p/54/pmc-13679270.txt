Partner presence in labor: does it influence maternal anxiety and childbirth experience beyond pain?
Authors: Arif Onur Atay, Samican Ozmen, Mucahit Furkan Balci, Ozgen Nahya Ozdogar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the association between partner presence during labor and maternal state anxiety, birth satisfaction, and pain perception in women undergoing spontaneous labor.MethodsThis prospective comparative observational study included women with singleton term pregnancies, spontaneous active labor, and vaginal delivery. Women with labor induction, epidural analgesia, additional obstetric pathology, systemic disease, or known psychiatric illness were excluded. Maternal anxiety was assessed using the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory Form TX-1 (STAI TX-1) during active labor, pain intensity using a visual analog scale (VAS) during the active phase, and birth satisfaction using the Birth Satisfaction Scale-Revised (BSS-R) within the first 24 h postpartum.ResultsOf the 168 women, 68 labored with partner presence and 100 without. Women accompanied by their partner had significantly lower STAI TX-1 scores and higher BSS-R scores than those without partner accompaniment (both p < 0.001), whereas VAS pain scores did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.428). Birth satisfaction was inversely correlated with anxiety (Spearman’s rho=-0.373, p < 0.001) and weakly positively correlated with pain (Spearman’s rho = 0.198, p = 0.010). In multivariable analyses, partner presence remained independently associated with lower anxiety (unstandardized regression coefficient [B]=-5.24, 95% CI -7.14 to -3.34, p < 0.001) and higher birth satisfaction (B = 2.57, 95% CI 1.24 to 3.90, p < 0.001).ConclusionPartner presence during labor was associated with lower maternal anxiety and higher birth satisfaction, without a significant difference in pain intensity. These findings suggest that the beneficial contribution of partner accompaniment may be more closely related to psychological and experiential factors than to pain reduction alone during the childbirth process.

Introduction

Childbirth involves complex obstetric and physiological mechanisms, but it is not merely a physical event; it is also an intense psychological experience [1, 2]. The peripartum period encompasses antenatal preparation, the active phase of labor, and the postpartum period. The active phase of labor is one of the most critical and intense parts of this process, during which women may experience some of the most powerful and memorable aspects of childbirth [1, 3]. The subjective experiences formed during labor may leave long-lasting impressions and may influence postpartum well-being and preferences regarding future childbirth, including the choice of delivery mode [1–3]. Consequently, in the context of rising cesarean section rates globally, optimizing and improving the vaginal labor experience has become increasingly important in maternal healthcare, as more positive childbirth experiences may encourage women to consider vaginal birth in future pregnancies and may contribute to broader efforts to reduce avoidable cesarean deliveries [2, 4, 5].

Although childbirth is primarily an experience undergone by the woman herself, continuous support during labor may still play an important role [6]. To help women cope with this demanding period, labor companionship has been increasingly advocated and may include ongoing physical, emotional, and informational support provided by a chosen support person [6, 7]. A substantial body of literature has examined continuous support during labor and has generally linked it to improved maternal outcomes and a more positive childbirth experience [6]. Previous studies have explored several dimensions of labor companionship, including its associations with the overall birth experience, pain perception, maternal anxiety, emotional support, and birth satisfaction [2, 8]. However, the focus should also be narrowed to the specific context of partner support. Most available studies have evaluated companionship in general (often including doulas, healthcare professionals, or female relatives) whereas evidence specifically addressing partner presence remains more limited [2, 6]. Accordingly, the role of partner support, particularly in relation to maternal anxiety and birth satisfaction, has not yet been fully clarified [3, 8].

Although continuous labor companionship is increasingly advocated as part of respectful and woman-centered maternity care, partner presence during labor is not a routine or uniformly implemented component of intrapartum care in all healthcare systems, institutions, or cultural settings. In Türkiye, partner presence during labor may depend on hospital infrastructure, local policies, availability of private labor-delivery-postpartum rooms, and maternal preference. National mother-friendly maternity care initiatives have emphasized privacy-based single-person birth units, freedom of movement, and the possibility of an appropriate companion during childbirth [9]. Nevertheless, partner presence during labor remains variable in routine practice, which makes it important to evaluate its association with maternal psychological and experiential outcomes in this setting.

The childbirth experience and maternal birth satisfaction are multidimensional outcomes that cannot be explained solely by the intensity of physical pain [10]. Maternal anxiety, fear, emotional support, and the subjective appraisal of labor are also important components shaping the overall childbirth experience [1, 8, 10]. Although the general influence of partner presence during labor has been investigated, the relationship of partner presence with pain perception, maternal anxiety, and birth satisfaction has not been fully clarified when these outcomes are considered together [3]. In addition, empirical data from relatively homogeneous labor populations, such as women undergoing spontaneous, non-induced, epidural-free labor, remain limited in the current literature [1, 6]. Evaluating partner presence in such a population may help better characterize its association with maternal psychological and experiential outcomes while minimizing the confounding effects of pharmacological and obstetric interventions.

Although the general benefits of labor companionship and partner presence have been widely studied, some aspects of their relationship with maternal psychological and sensory outcomes may still warrant further characterization in the literature [4, 8]. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the association between partner presence during labor and maternal state anxiety, birth satisfaction, and pain perception in women undergoing spontaneous labor.

Methods

Study design and setting

This prospective comparative observational study was conducted in the delivery unit of a secondary care public hospital in Izmir, Türkiye, between October 2025 and March 2026. The annual delivery volume of the hospital is approximately 1,000 births. Partner presence in the delivery room was permitted according to maternal preference but it was not mandatory, systematically assigned, or specifically encouraged as part of the study protocol. The manuscript was prepared in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for observational studies. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Ege University (approval number: 25-10.1T/51).

Participants

The final analysis included women who met the eligibility criteria, delivered vaginally, and had complete study data. Eligible women who were admitted to the delivery unit with spontaneous active labor during the study period were approached consecutively by a member of the study team after the initial obstetric evaluation. Women were informed about the study at admission to the delivery unit, and those who agreed to participate provided written informed consent before administration of the study instruments. Participation was entirely voluntary, and refusal to participate did not affect obstetric care.

Eligibility was limited to women aged ≥ 18 years with singleton pregnancies, gestational age ≥ 37 weeks, and spontaneous active labor. Both nulliparous and parous women were eligible for inclusion, and parity was recorded as the number of previous births. Only women with complete study data were included in the final analysis. Women whose labor ended in cesarean delivery were not included in the final analysis, as the study was restricted to vaginal births.

Exclusion criteria included induction of labor, epidural analgesia, multiple pregnancy, fetal anomaly, and additional obstetric or maternal conditions that could affect the childbirth experience, including gestational diabetes mellitus, gestational hypertensive disorders including preeclampsia, systemic disease, and known psychiatric illness. Epidural analgesia was excluded because neuraxial analgesia directly modifies labor pain perception and could confound the assessment of the relationship between partner presence, maternal anxiety, birth satisfaction, and pain intensity.

Participants were categorized according to whether their partner was present during labor. For the purpose of this study, partner was defined as the woman’s husband/spouse. Partner presence was defined as the presence of the husband/spouse in the delivery room during active labor and was recorded as a binary variable on the study form by the attending delivery-unit staff or a member of the study team. The exact duration, continuity, and specific supportive behaviors of partner presence were not systematically measured. Partner absence referred specifically to the absence of the woman’s partner in the delivery room during labor and did not indicate absence of professional intrapartum care. All participants received standard care from the obstetric and midwifery team. The decision regarding partner presence was made voluntarily by the women themselves; no participant was encouraged, required, or restricted with respect to partner accompaniment. The reasons for the absence of partner accompaniment were not systematically recorded.

Data collection and study measures

Demographic and obstetric data, including maternal age, gravida, parity, gestational age at delivery, and birth weight, were recorded. Maternal state anxiety was assessed using the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory Form TX-1 (STAI TX-1) [11, 12], which was administered during the active phase of labor, defined as cervical dilatation of 6 cm or more in the presence of regular uterine contractions. The STAI TX-1 was completed during active labor in a setting kept as private as feasible within routine delivery-unit conditions; however, in women accompanied by their partner, the partner was not systematically asked to leave the room during questionnaire completion. Pain intensity was evaluated using a 0–10 visual analog scale (VAS), where 0 indicated no pain and 10 indicated the worst imaginable pain. The VAS was administered by a member of the study team during the active phase of labor, at the time when uterine contractions were perceived as most intense. Birth satisfaction was assessed using the Birth Satisfaction Scale-Revised (BSS-R) [13, 14], which was administered within the first 24 h after delivery.

The STAI TX-1, VAS, and BSS-R were administered by members of the study team who were trained in the study protocol. These instruments were participant-reported measures, and responses were provided by the participants themselves. Because partner presence was directly observable in the delivery room, the assessors were aware of whether each participant was in the partner-present or partner-absent group.

Outcome measures

The co-primary outcomes of the study were maternal state anxiety and birth satisfaction. Maternal state anxiety was assessed using the STAI TX-1, and birth satisfaction was evaluated using the BSS-R total score. Pain intensity, measured by VAS, was considered a secondary outcome.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi version 2.6.45.0. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots. Because continuous variables did not consistently meet normality assumptions, they were summarized as median (range) or median (interquartile range), as appropriate, and non-parametric methods were used consistently for unadjusted group comparisons. Baseline demographic and obstetric characteristics, as well as STAI TX-1, BSS-R, and VAS scores, were compared between women with and without partner presence during labor using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Effect sizes for between-group comparisons were reported as rank-biserial correlations. Correlations among birth satisfaction, state anxiety, and pain scores were evaluated using Spearman correlation analysis. Multivariable linear regression analyses were performed to identify factors independently associated with maternal state anxiety and birth satisfaction scores. In these models, partner presence, age, parity, birth weight, gestational age, and pain score were entered as predictors. To assess whether the association between partner presence and study outcomes differed by parity status, interaction terms between partner presence and parity group were added to the multivariable models. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed because this prospective observational study was conducted over a predefined study period. All eligible women admitted with spontaneous active labor during the study period who provided consent and had complete data were included. Based on the final group sizes, a sensitivity power analysis indicated that the study could detect a standardized between-group difference of approximately 0.49 in the co-primary outcomes with 80% power at a two-sided Bonferroni-adjusted alpha level of 0.025. To account for the two co-primary outcomes, a Bonferroni-adjusted two-sided significance threshold of p < 0.025 was used for interpretation of the primary outcome analyses. For secondary and exploratory analyses, a two-sided p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

During the study period, 393 women admitted to the delivery unit were assessed for eligibility. At the initial eligibility assessment, 175 women were excluded because they did not meet the study criteria: induction of labor (n = 76), absence of spontaneous active labor (n = 46), age < 18 years (n = 3), gestational age < 37 weeks (n = 19), multiple pregnancy (n = 1), gestational diabetes mellitus (n = 14), gestational hypertensive disorders including preeclampsia (n = 6), maternal systemic disease (n = 4), known psychiatric illness (n = 3), and epidural analgesia (n = 3). Among the remaining 218 women who were potentially eligible at admission, 34 were not enrolled because rapid labor progression precluded informed consent and administration of the study instruments. Of the 184 enrolled women, 5 were excluded because labor resulted in cesarean delivery after admission among otherwise eligible women, and 11 were excluded because of incomplete study data. Therefore, 168 women with complete study data were included in the final analysis, of whom 100 labored without partner accompaniment and 68 with partner accompaniment. All included women had vaginal deliveries, and no operative vaginal deliveries with vacuum or forceps occurred in the final study population. No statistically significant differences were observed between the partner-present and partner-absent groups in the recorded baseline demographic and obstetric variables, including maternal age, gravida, parity, parity status, gestational age at delivery, and birth weight (Table 1).

Women accompanied by their partner during labor had significantly higher birth satisfaction scores and lower state anxiety scores than those without partner accompaniment. The median BSS-R total score was 27 (range, 20–38) in the partner-present group and 24 (range, 15–40) in the partner-absent group (p < 0.001; rank-biserial correlation = 0.417). The median STAI Form TX-1 score was 26 (range, 18–43) in women with partner presence and 34 (range, 23–50) in those without partner presence (p < 0.001; rank-biserial correlation = -0.493). In contrast, pain scores did not differ significantly between the groups, with a median VAS score of 7 (range, 3–10) in both groups (p = 0.428; rank-biserial correlation = 0.071) (Table 2). In multivariable linear regression analyses adjusted for age, parity, birth weight, gestational age, and pain score, partner presence during labor remained independently associated with both lower state anxiety and higher birth satisfaction. Partner presence was associated with a significant reduction in STAI Form TX-1 scores (B = -5.24, 95% CI: -7.14 to -3.34, p < 0.001) and a significant increase in birth satisfaction scores (B = 2.57, 95% CI: 1.24 to 3.90, p < 0.001). Pain score was independently associated with both outcomes, showing an inverse association with state anxiety (B = -0.61, 95% CI: -1.16 to -0.05, p = 0.033) and a positive association with birth satisfaction (B = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.02 to 0.81, p = 0.038). Higher parity was also independently associated with higher birth satisfaction scores (B = 1.22, 95% CI: 0.28 to 2.16, p = 0.011), whereas age, birth weight, and gestational age were not significantly associated with either outcome. The models explained 21.8% of the variance in state anxiety scores and 16.3% of the variance in birth satisfaction scores (Table 4). In exploratory interaction analyses, no statistically significant interaction by parity was found for either state anxiety (B = -0.06, p = 0.979) or birth satisfaction (B = -2.23, p = 0.128).

Discussion

The principal findings of this study were that partner presence during labor was independently associated with lower maternal state anxiety and higher birth satisfaction, whereas pain intensity did not differ between the groups. These associations remained statistically significant after adjustment for recorded maternal and obstetric covariates; however, residual confounding cannot be excluded, particularly because psychosocial, relational, and contextual factors were not measured. Moreover, birth satisfaction was inversely correlated with maternal anxiety, while no significant correlation was found between anxiety and pain intensity. Taken together, these findings suggest that the contribution of partner presence to childbirth experience is more closely linked to psychological and experiential dimensions than to pain perception alone.

These results are broadly consistent with the literature on continuous labor support, which has generally linked companionship during childbirth with a more positive birth experience and better maternal psychological outcomes [6, 15]. More specifically, our findings are also in line with reports suggesting that support provided by a husband or partner may be associated with lower maternal anxiety and greater satisfaction during labor [2, 16]. However, the literature is not entirely uniform. Some studies have reported less consistent or non-significant effects of familiar companion or partner support on maternal psychological outcomes and birth satisfaction [4, 6], while qualitative research has shown that partners themselves may feel anxious, overwhelmed, or insufficiently prepared during labor, which may limit the support they are able to provide [17]. Such differences across studies may reflect variation in labor settings, participant characteristics, outcome measures, assessment timing, and the extent to which partners were prepared for their supportive role [16, 18]. In addition, although some previous reports have suggested that companionship may have a greater psychological impact in first-time mothers [18], no statistically significant interaction by parity was observed in our study. However, this exploratory analysis should be interpreted cautiously because the study was not specifically powered to assess interaction effects; therefore, possible effect modification by parity cannot be excluded.

Interestingly, although partner presence was associated with significantly lower maternal state anxiety and higher birth satisfaction, pain intensity did not differ significantly between the accompanied and unaccompanied groups. This suggests that the beneficial association of partner companionship with childbirth experience is not primarily explained by pain reduction and that partner presence should not be conceptualized as a direct analgesic or pain-relieving intervention. Instead, spousal support may be more closely related to buffering maternal anxiety and enhancing the overall psychological experience during labor. Furthermore, the absence of a significant correlation between maternal anxiety and pain intensity reinforces the notion that experiencing lower pain does not necessarily correspond to a better or less distressing childbirth experience [1, 4]. The weak positive correlation observed between pain intensity and birth satisfaction should be interpreted cautiously; it does not imply that pain is inherently beneficial or that greater physical pain directly improves childbirth satisfaction. Rather, because labor pain largely reflects uterine contractile activity and the physiologic progression of childbirth, it may not always function as a purely negative determinant of the birth experience. In some women, a more intense labor experience may still be appraised positively when the process is experienced as supported, meaningful, and manageable [1, 10]. Consequently, these findings suggest that birth satisfaction is a multidimensional construct reflecting not only sensory pain intensity but also emotional support, coping capacity, perceived safety, and the subjective appraisal of the labor environment. Ultimately, partner presence appears to influence the childbirth experience predominantly through psychological and experiential pathways rather than through pain reduction alone.

These findings suggest that childbirth satisfaction is a multidimensional construct that cannot be explained solely by physical pain, fear, or anxiety. Rather, emotional support, perceived safety, coping capacity, the sense of being accompanied, and the overall subjective appraisal of labor may all contribute to how childbirth is experienced and remembered [4]. In this context, partner presence may represent more than the physical presence of another person in the delivery room, and may instead reflect a relationally meaningful form of support during labor [4]. Accordingly, the present findings support a broader understanding of childbirth as not only a physiological and obstetric event, but also a psychological and interpersonal experience.

The strengths of the present study include its prospective design, the assessment of maternal anxiety, birth satisfaction, and pain intensity within the same study population, and the relatively homogeneous sample restricted to singleton term pregnancies with spontaneous onset of labor, vaginal delivery, and no induction or epidural analgesia. In addition, the multivariable models allowed adjustment for several relevant maternal and obstetric covariates. Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered. First, the study was conducted at a single center with a moderate sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Partner presence was not assigned randomly but was based on maternal preference and real-world circumstances. The reasons why some women labored without partner accompaniment were not systematically recorded; therefore, unmeasured social, relational, logistical, or psychosocial factors may have influenced both the likelihood of being accompanied and the level of anxiety during labor. In addition, only state anxiety during active labor was assessed, and no pre-labor or trait anxiety measurement was available. Therefore, the study cannot determine whether lower anxiety among women with partner presence reflected a true intrapartum effect of accompaniment or pre-existing differences in anxiety between the groups. Although partner presence was recorded as a binary variable during active labor, the exact duration, continuity, intermittency, and specific supportive behaviors of the husband/spouse were not systematically assessed. Therefore, the study could not distinguish between mere partner presence and continuous or effective labor support. Although participants were admitted to the delivery unit in active labor rather than during latent-phase hospitalization, the interval from admission to delivery was not systematically recorded. Therefore, we could not assess whether the duration of intrapartum hospitalization influenced maternal anxiety or birth satisfaction. All principal outcomes were based on self-reported measures, and birth satisfaction was assessed within the first 24 h postpartum; therefore, responses may have been influenced by the immediate emotional context of childbirth. In addition, because partner presence was directly observable, assessors were aware of group status, and the STAI TX-1 may have been completed in the partner’s presence in some cases. This may have introduced response-context or social-desirability bias, potentially in the direction of lower reported anxiety among women accompanied by their partner. Finally, because of the observational design, the findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal.

In conclusion, partner presence during labor was associated with lower maternal anxiety and higher birth satisfaction, highlighting the potential relevance of partner accompaniment in maternal childbirth experience. Notably, pain intensity did not differ according to partner presence and was not significantly correlated with maternal anxiety, whereas birth satisfaction showed a weak positive correlation with pain intensity. Together, these findings suggest that a more satisfactory childbirth experience is not simply equivalent to less pain, and that the beneficial contribution of partner presence appears to be more closely related to reduced anxiety and a more positive psychological experience of labor than to changes in pain intensity. Future multicenter studies should include pre-labor and trait anxiety assessments, more detailed measures of the duration and quality of partner support, and postpartum follow-up to better clarify the causal and longitudinal relevance of partner accompaniment during labor.
