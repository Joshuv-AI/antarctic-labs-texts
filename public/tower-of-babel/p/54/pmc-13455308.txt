Survival and predictors of mortality among TB–HIV co-infected children attending ART clinics in public hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Abrham Tesfaye Habteyes, Tebabere Moltot, Enguday Demeke Gebeyaw, Awraris Hailu Bilchut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe double burden of tuberculosis (TB) and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) remains a major global health challenge in the twenty-first century. Children (ages 0–15 years) are particularly vulnerable to TB–HIV co-infection, exhibiting higher morbidity and mortality rates than other age groups, with the burden especially pronounced in developing countries. Approximately 75% of global TB–HIV co-infections occur in Sub-Saharan Africa. This study aimed to assess the five-year survival status and identify predictors of mortality among TB–HIV co-infected children attending antiretroviral therapy (ART) clinics in public hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.MethodsA facility-based retrospective cohort study was conducted in pediatric ART centers in Addis Ababa. A total of 241 TB–HIV co-infected children followed between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2023, were included, with five-year survival outcomes recorded. Kaplan–Meier survival curves and log-rank tests were used to assess differences in survival among predictor variables. The independent effect of each predictor on time to death following TB diagnosis was estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression, with results expressed as adjusted hazard ratios (aHR) at a 5% significance level. To minimize overfitting due to the limited number of events, the final multivariable model included a parsimonious set of clinically and biologically relevant predictors.ResultsOf the 241 children included, 35 (14.5%) died, yielding a crude mortality rate of 3.63 per 100 child-years (95% CI: 2.61–5.07) over 962.3 child-years of follow-up. The estimated cumulative five-year survival rate after TB–HIV co-infection was 83.31% (95% CI: 77.51–87.73). Significant predictors of mortality included a CD4 count below the threshold level (aHR: 2.84; 95% CI: 1.38–5.83), a history of opportunistic infections (aHR: 5.04; 95% CI: 2.01–12.69), and fair/poor ART adherence (aHR: 5.69; 95% CI: 2.73–11.90).ConclusionThe five-year survival rate of TB–HIV co-infected children in this study was consistent with reports from other low- and middle-income countries. Mortality was significantly associated with low CD4 counts, a history of opportunistic infections, and suboptimal ART adherence. These findings highlight the need for early identification of immunosuppressed children, and targeted interventions are essential to improve child survival.Keywords: Tuberculosis, HIV, Children, Survival, Mortality predictors

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) co-infection remains a major global health priority [1]. The interaction between TB and HIV is both bidirectional and synergistic [2–4]–HIV accelerates the progression of latent TB infection to active disease, while TB hastens the progression of HIV infection to its advanced stages [5]. The double burden of TB and HIV represents one of the most significant global health challenges of the twenty-first century [6]. Moreover, HIV remains the primary reason for the failure to achieve TB control targets in many settings [1].

Children are highly affected by TB–HIV co-infection, a major contributor to child mortality globally [7]. Diagnosing childhood TB is difficult due to challenges in isolating Mycobacterium tuberculosis [8], leading to its neglect and underestimation as a cause of death [9]. Globally, in 2023, an estimated 191,000 children (< 15 years) died from TB, including about 25,000 co-infected with HIV [10, 11]. In 2024, nearly 390,000 children in high-burden countries were diagnosed, with only 4.4% HIV-positive [12]. Over 80–90% of pediatric TB cases and most TB–HIV deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries, especially sub-Saharan Africa, South-East Asia, and the Western Pacific [13].

TB–HIV co-infection remains a significant public health challenge among children in sub-Saharan Africa, which contributes substantially to childhood morbidity and mortality [14]. In Kenya, among more than 23,000 children receiving TB treatment, the prevalence of TB–HIV co-infection was reported to be 28% [15]. In Tanzania, 12.3% of co-infected children died during follow-up [16]. Similarly, a study conducted in the Congo reported a markedly higher mortality rate of 47.73% among TB-HIV co-infected children [17].

The survival rate of TB–HIV co-infected children varies across countries and studies. In Myanmar, the five-year survival rate was 82.0% [18], while a multi-country study reported survival probabilities of 85.0% at 2 months and 80.7% at 6 months [19]. In Ethiopia, the five-year survival probability was 85.8% in Gondar [20], and in Tigray, survival declined from 94.0% at 2 months to 82.9% at 12 months [21]. TB-HIV co-infection mortality rates in African countries range from 10.5% to 17.5%, including Tanzania (17.5%) [22], Ethiopia (14%) [23], Uganda (10.47%) [24], South Africa (13.8%) [25], and Democratic Republic of Congo (13%) [26].

Integrated programs have been implemented to prevent, diagnose, and treat TB among people living with HIV. However, TB remains a major global and national health concern, requiring strong action to achieve the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and Stop TB strategy targets [27]. TB continues to be the leading cause of mortality among children living with HIV, particularly in low-income countries such as Ethiopia [28, 29]. Although Ethiopia is working to reduce TB and HIV infections in line with the SDGs [30], the World Health Organization (WHO) still lists the country among the top 30 TB–HIV burden nations, with death rates among TB–HIV patients ranging from 1.4% to 2.8% [7].

Global and sub-Saharan child mortality data show that TB–HIV co-infection remains a major public health concern, requiring further investigation of its predictors. However, evidence on survival among children with TB–HIV co-infection in sub-Saharan Africa is limited [31]. Although some studies [20, 21] have evaluated survival among TB–HIV co-infected children in Ethiopia, evidence from Addis Ababa remains limited. As the city hosts most referral facilities and all tertiary hospitals, it represents an important setting for assessing treatment outcomes among TB–HIV co-infected children. Therefore, this study aimed to fill this gap by determining the survival rate and identifying predictors of mortality among TB–HIV co-infected children attending antiretroviral therapy (ART) clinics in public hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.

Methods

Study area and period

The study was conducted in Addis Ababa, the capital and largest city of Ethiopia. The city has twelve public hospitals, of which six are administered by the Addis Ababa Health Bureau and five by the Federal Ministry of Health. In addition, one hospital (Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital) is affiliated with a university [32]. This study was carried out in five randomly selected public hospitals: Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital, Zewditu Memorial Hospital, Ras Desta Damtew Memorial Hospital, St. Paul’s Hospital Millennium Medical College, and St. Peter Specialized Hospital. The study included TB–HIV co-infected patients who were enrolled between January 2019 and December 2023.

Study design

A five-year retrospective cohort study was conducted among children newly diagnosed with TB–HIV co-infection and enrolled in ART at public hospitals in Addis Ababa between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2023. Each participant was followed for up to five years from the date of enrolment and was censored at the end of the follow-up period.

Population

The source population comprised all TB–HIV co-infected children under 15 years of age who were ever enrolled in pediatric ART clinics at public hospitals in Addis Ababa. The study population included all TB–HIV co-infected children under 15 years of age from the selected hospitals who were registered in HIV care between January 2019 and December 2023.

Eligible criteria

All TB–HIV co-infected children under 15 years of age who were diagnosed and followed up for outcome at the selected public hospital pediatric ART clinics during the study period were included. Children with missing outcome data and those referred to other health facilities were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination

The sample size was determined based on both outcome and predictor variables using the Epi Info version 7 statistical software package [33]. The calculation was based on a mortality proportion of 17.2% and key predictors - treatment failure, CD4 count, and cotrimoxazole preventive therapy (CPT) identified in a previous study conducted in northwest Ethiopia [34]. Assumptions included a 95% confidence level (Zα/2), a 5% margin of error (d), 80% statistical power (Zβ), and a 1:1 ratio of exposed to unexposed participants. Because the sample size calculated using predictor variables was smaller than that calculated using the outcome variable, the larger sample size was selected to minimize sampling error. After adding a 10% contingency for incomplete records, the final sample size was determined to be 241 participants.

Sampling technique and procedure

A multistage sampling technique was employed in this study. First, five hospitals were randomly selected from the twelve public hospitals in Addis Ababa. Next, TB–HIV co-infected children who received ART between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2023, were identified from ART logbooks in the selected hospitals. The number of eligible patients identified in each hospital was 85 at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital, 57 at Zewditu Memorial Hospital, 29 at Ras Desta Damtew Memorial Hospital, 126 at St. Paul’s Hospital Millennium Medical College, and 167 at St. Peter Specialized Hospital. The total sample size was then proportionally allocated to each hospital based on the number of eligible patients. Finally, the required number of patient records from each hospital was selected using a simple random sampling technique.

Study variables

Dependent variable

The survival status of TB–HIV co-infected children was dichotomized as “0” for censored and “1” for death.

Independent variables

Independent variables included sociodemographic, clinical, and follow-up characteristics of both the child and caregiver. Sociodemographic characteristics comprised age, sex, residence, and caregiver–child relationship. Clinical characteristics included CD4 cell count, baseline anemia status, WHO clinical stage, presence of opportunistic infections (OIs), site of tuberculosis (TB), timing of TB diagnosis, TB drug resistance status, and previous history of TB (TB episodes occurring before enrollment). Follow-up characteristics included ART regimen, treatment adherence, cotrimoxazole preventive therapy (CPT), and isoniazid preventive therapy (IPT). Variables such as CD4 count, OIs history, and ART adherence were measured at baseline and treated as fixed covariates in the analysis due to lack of consistent longitudinal measurements.

Standard and operational definitions

Event: the occurrence of death from the first confirmed diagnosis of TB–HIV co-infection to the end of the study. In this study, death was treated as all-cause mortality during follow-up. TB-related deaths could not always be reliably distinguished from deaths due to other causes; therefore, all deaths among TB–HIV co-infected children were included. While this approach may introduce some misclassification and captures the overall mortality burden.

Censored: participants who survived until the end of the study or who were lost to follow-up or transferred out. They were treated as right-censored at their last known follow-up date and consistent with standard survival analysis procedures.

ART adherence: classified as Good (missed ≤ 3 doses per month), Fair (missed 4–9 doses per month), and Poor (missed > 9 doses per month) according to the calculated percentage of ART drug taken from the total monthly dosage [35]. These measures were derived from clinical records.

Anemia in children was defined according to WHO criteria as a hemoglobin concentration below 11.0 g/dL, 11.5 g/dL, and 12.0 g/dL for children aged 6–59 months, 5–11 years and 12–14 years, respectively [36].

Opportunistic infections such as Herpes zoster, Bacterial pneumonia, Oral thrush, Candidiasis, Diarrhea (chronic/acute), Pneumocystis jirovecii Pneumonia, Cryptococcal Meningitis, and conditions other than infections like cancers such as Kaposi sarcoma. Taken from the national HIV care/ART follow-up form. Tuberculosis was excluded from this definition because all participants were enrolled based on TB–HIV co-infection.

Stunting defined as a height-for-age Z-score below − 2 standard deviations from the WHO Child Growth Standards median [37].

The CD4 threshold level defined based on the WHO guideline. The child below those cutoff CD4 counts is considered to have severe immunodeficiency: CD4 count 1500 cells/µL for children < 12 months, CD4 count 750 cells/µL for children 12–35 months, CD4 count 350 cells/µL for children 36–59 months, and CD4 count 200 cells/µL for children 5–14 years [38].

Treatment Failure- considered if the patient has either clinical, immunologic, or virologic failure [35]:

Clinical treatment failure: Was the detection of new or recurrent WHO clinical stage of III or IV.

Clinical treatment failure: Was the detection of new or recurrent WHO clinical stage of III or IV.

Immunologic treatment failure: Was a drop in CD4 level, after the initial immune recovery, following ART initiation to values at or below the age-related CD4 threshold for treatment initiation or 30% drop in the CD4% from the highest value after ART initiation.

Immunologic treatment failure: Was a drop in CD4 level, after the initial immune recovery, following ART initiation to values at or below the age-related CD4 threshold for treatment initiation or 30% drop in the CD4% from the highest value after ART initiation.

Virologic failure: A persistent viral load exceeding 5000 copies/ml in a child who is fully adherent to treatment and who was on ART for at least 24 weeks.

Data collection tools and procedures

A structured data abstraction form was adapted from previous related studies [15, 39–43] and from the national ART entry and follow-up forms used in ART clinics, in accordance with the study objectives. Patients’ survival status was obtained from medical records, and survival time was calculated in months from the date of TB–HIV co-infection diagnosis to death, censoring, or the end of the study period. Both baseline and follow-up records were reviewed prior to data extraction. The medical records of eligible TB–HIV co-infected children were then systematically reviewed, and relevant data were collected by five ART-trained nurses working in the respective ART units.

Data quality control

Data quality was ensured through the use of standardized data abstraction tools and continuous supervision throughout the data collection process. The content validity of the checklist was evaluated by senior experts, and a pre-test was conducted on 5% of the study sample at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital. Language clarity, tool appropriateness, completion time, and necessary revisions were assessed based on the pre-test findings. Data collectors and supervisors received intensive training on record review procedures prior to data collection. During data collection, supervisors closely monitored data collectors and reviewed completed checklists daily for completeness and accuracy. Spot checks and random back-checks were also conducted to ensure consistency across study sites, and any identified inconsistencies were resolved through clarification and feedback sessions.

Data processing and analysis

Data were coded, cleaned, entered, and edited using EPI-Data version 3.1 [44] and subsequently transferred to STATA version 16 for statistical analysis [45]. Descriptive statistics were computed, and the incidence density rate (IDR) was calculated for the entire study period. Kaplan–Meier survival curves and log-rank tests were used to assess differences in survival across covariate categories, and a life table was generated for the study population.

To identify predictors of mortality, a bivariable Cox proportional hazards model was first performed to evaluate the independent effect of each predictor on the outcome. Variables with a p-value ≤ 0.25 in bivariable analysis were considered as candidates for the multivariable model. This more permissive cutoff was applied to avoid excluding variables that might become significant after adjustment, as bivariable tests may have low power to detect important predictors or confounders [46, 47]. To minimize overfitting due to the limited number of events, the final multivariable model included a parsimonious set of clinically and biologically relevant predictors. The events-per-variable ratio was approximately 8.75 (35 events and 4 covariates), which is slightly below the recommended threshold.

Variables with a p-value < 0.05 in the multivariable analysis were considered statistically significant, with results reported as hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Isoniazid preventive therapy (IPT) was excluded from the multivariable Cox regression because it was initiated after completion of TB treatment. Including IPT as a baseline exposure could introduce immortal time bias; therefore, it was omitted from the primary survival analysis. A sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding participants lost to follow-up to assess the impact of alternative censoring assumptions on the findings.

Potential multicollinearity among predictors was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with all variables in the final model showing VIF values between 1 and 5, indicating no significant multicollinearity. Model assumptions were checked using the Schoenfeld residual test (all variables had p-values > 0.1) and the global test (χ² = 7.79, p = 0.0997). Goodness-of-fit of the Cox proportional hazards model was evaluated by plotting the Cox-Snell residual against Nelson-Aalen cumulative hazard, and the result indicated that the model adequately fit the data (Fig. 1). Missing data were assessed for all study variables prior to analysis. The extent of missing observations was quantified for key predictors. Records with missing outcome information were excluded. Because the proportion of missing data among predictor variables was small (< 5% for all variables), complete-case analysis was applied. Therefore, imputation methods were not applied. Sensitivity analysis comparing complete and incomplete cases was performed to assess potential bias.

Results

Between January 2019 and December 2023, a total of 241 TB–HIV co-infected children were enrolled from five public hospitals and followed for up to five years from their enrollment date. During the follow-up period, 35 children died, while 206 were censored. The mean age of the children at the time of diagnosis was 7.58 years (SD ± 3.52), and 132 (54.8%) were female. Fifty children (20.7%) were orphaned, and 86 (35.7%) resided in rural areas. Among the primary caregivers, 70% were aged 25–34 years (Table 1).

Survival status of TB–HIV co-infected children

During 962.3 child-years of follow-up, the overall mortality rate was 3.63 deaths per 100 child-years (95% CI: 2.61–5.07). The median follow-up time could not be estimated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method, as the follow-up distribution did not reach 50% during the study period. The cumulative mortality proportion over five-years for the 35 children who died was 14.5% (95% CI: 10.6–19.6).

Of the total participants, 206 (85.5%) were censored during follow-up. Among these, 162 (67.2%) were alive at the end of the study, 18 (7.5%) were lost to follow-up, and 26 (10.8%) were transferred to other institutions; all censored participants were treated as right-censored at their last known follow-up date. To assess the potential impact of informative censoring, a sensitivity analysis was conducted excluding participants lost to follow-up. The results were consistent with the primary analysis, indicating that the findings were robust to alternative censoring assumptions.

Overall survival function of TB–HIV co-infected children

In this study, 241 TB–HIV co-infected children were followed for up to five years. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis estimated the overall survival rate following TB–HIV co-infection diagnosis to be 83.31% (95% CI: 77.51–87.73%) at 60 months of follow-up (Fig. 2). The estimated survival rates at 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 months were 93.25%, 88.87%, 85.87%, and 83.31%, and 83.31%, respectively (Table 4). The median survival time could not be determined, as the follow-up distribution did not reach 50% during the study period.

Predictors of TB–HIV co-infected children’s mortality

In bivariable analysis, twelve candidate variables were assessed for their association with mortality, including child’s residency, hemoglobin level, CD4 count, history of OIs, height-for-age, CPT, ART adherence, history of TB, site of TB infection, TB drug resistance, treatment failure, and HIV stage. Of these, seven variables were statistically significant.

Given the limited number of deaths (35 events) and to minimize the risk of overfitting, the final multivariable Cox regression model was restricted to a parsimonious set of clinically and biologically relevant predictors. The final model included CD4 count, history of OIs, ART adherence, and TB drug resistance. This approach ensured stable estimates while retaining the most clinically relevant predictors of mortality. Missing data were minimal: CD4 count was missing for 7 participants (2.9%), HIV stage for 5 participants (2.1%), TB drug resistance for 11 participants (4.6%), and ART adherence for 3 participants (1.3%).

TB–HIV co-infected children with a CD4 count below the threshold level were 2.84 times more likely to die than those with a CD4 count above the threshold [aHR: 2.84; 95% CI: 1.38–5.83]. Children with a history of OIs had a 5.04-fold higher risk of death compared to those without such a history [aHR: 5.04; 95% CI: 2.01–12.69]. Additionally, children with fair or poor ART adherence were 5.69 times more likely to die than those with good adherence [aHR: 5.69; 95% CI: 2.73–11.90] (Table 5).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of children with TB–HIV co-infection in Addis Ababa, we found that the overall five-year survival rate was 83.3%, which is comparable to survival rates reported in pediatric TB–HIV cohorts from other low- and middle-income countries. Cox proportional hazards analysis identified several independent predictors of mortality, including low CD4 count, a history of OIs, and poor ART adherence. These findings underscore the continuing vulnerability of immunocompromised children and the importance of timely clinical monitoring, early identification of treatment failure, and targeted interventions to improve survival outcomes in high TB–HIV burden settings.

At the end of follow-up, the overall mortality proportion among the 35 TB–HIV co-infected children who died was 14.5% over the five-year study period. This finding is consistent with a study in Nigeria (14%) [48]. However, the mortality rate was higher than that of studies from England and Ireland (5%) [49], six African countries (10%) [50], South Africa (12%) [51], Uganda (10.6%) [52], Ghana (8.4%) [41], and a global systematic review (11.4%) [1]. However, it was lower than those reported from India (36.5%) [53], Cameroon (22.7%) [54], Congo (47.4%) [17], Malawi (20.2%) [39], and Southern Ethiopia (17.15%) [55]. The finding was comparable with studies in Gondar (14.02%) [20] and Tigray (15%) [21].

The observed variations in mortality rates across studies may be attributed to several factors. Differences in patient characteristics and disease severity may contribute a role; studies that included a higher proportion of children with advanced HIV disease, severe immunosuppression, or extrapulmonary and MDR TB are likely to report higher mortality. Additionally, differences in health system capacity and quality of care may explain the lower mortality observed in high-income settings such as England and Ireland, where early diagnosis, stronger health infrastructure, and more consistent access to optimized ART and TB treatment are available. Moreover, study setting and facility level may influence outcomes; studies conducted in tertiary referral hospitals often manage more complicated and late-presenting cases, which may result in higher mortality compared to primary or mixed-level health facilities. Finally, methodological differences, including study design, sample size, inclusion criteria, and length of follow-up, may also affect mortality estimates and limit comparability across studies.

In this five-year retrospective follow-up study, the overall mortality incidence rate among TB–HIV co-infected children was 3.63 per 100 child-years of observation. This rate is slightly higher than those reported in Nigeria (1.4 per 100 child-years) [56]) and Southern Ethiopia (2.78 per 100 child-years) [57]. However, it is consistent with findings from other Ethiopian regions, including Northwest Ethiopia (3.7 per 100 child-years) [34] and Gondar (3.27 per 100 child-years) [20].

The observed higher mortality rate in the current study compared with some studies from Nigeria and Southern Ethiopia may be explained by several context-specific factors. One possible explanation is the late presentation of TB–HIV co-infected children with advanced HIV disease, which is known to increase the risk of poor treatment outcomes and death [34]. In addition, delays in diagnosis and initiation of appropriate ART and anti-TB treatment may have contributed to the higher mortality in our study. Differences in health service accessibility and quality of integrated TB–HIV care may also account for the variation, as children managed in settings with stronger early detection and follow-up systems tend to have better survival outcomes. Furthermore, differences in study populations and sample sizes across studies may have influenced the observed incidence rates and limited direct comparability.

In the present study, the overall cumulative survival probabilities at 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 months were 93.25%, 88.87%, 85.87%, 83.31%, and 83.31%, respectively. These rates were lower than those reported in Gondar, which were 91.2%, 88.6%, and 85.8% at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively [20], and slightly lower than Southern Ethiopia, which reported 96%, 92%, and 89% at the same time points [57]. Conversely, the survival probabilities were higher than those reported in Tigray, where the 12-month survival was 82.9% [21], and comparable to a study in Thailand, which reported 96.1%, 94%, and 87.7% at 1, 2, and 3 years [28].

These differences in survival may be explained by variations in access to rapid diagnostic tests and TB–HIV screening. In developed countries, earlier diagnosis and timely treatment contribute to better outcomes. In resource-limited settings such as Ethiopia, gaps in healthcare provider knowledge and skills for screening, diagnosing, and managing TB–HIV co-infection further restrict patient care [1, 34]. Even when providers are trained, resource constraints often hinder effective management. Additional factors influencing survival differences include methodological variations across studies and differences in sample characteristics of the children.

In the present study, children’s CD4 count was identified as an independent predictor of mortality. Children with CD4 counts below the threshold level were 2.84 times more likely to die compared with those above the threshold. This finding is consistent with various studies [34, 58]. The association between low CD4 counts and increased mortality is explained by HIV-related immunosuppression [59, 60]. CD4 T lymphocytes are essential for cell-mediated immunity and for controlling Mycobacterium tuberculosis through macrophage activation and granuloma formation [59]. Severe CD4 depletion compromises the containment of TB infection, leading to disseminated disease, increased susceptibility to opportunistic infections, and poorer clinical outcomes [59]. In children, whose immune systems are still developing, this immunosuppression further accelerates disease progression, increasing the risk of mortality [61].

A history of opportunistic infections was also a strong predictor of mortality. TB–HIV co-infected children with a history of OIs were 5.04 times more likely to die compared with those without such a history. This finding aligns with studies from both developed and developing countries [39, 40, 56]. The increased risk may be due to the greater degree of immunocompromise in TB–HIV co-infected children, which predisposes them to a wide range of OIs and rapid clinical deterioration [14]. Opportunistic infections can also contribute to malnutrition through decreased appetite, gastrointestinal complications, or increased metabolic demands, further exacerbating morbidity and mortality in this population [56].

Consistent with prior research, ART adherence was another key predictor of mortality. Children with fair or poor ART adherence were 5.69 times more likely to die compared with those with good adherence, a finding in line with studies conducted in different regions of Ethiopia [20, 40, 55, 57]. Poor adherence is associated with suboptimal treatment outcomes, delayed TB culture conversion, and higher mortality, whereas consistent ART improves immune function and survival among TB–HIV co-infected children [62]. These findings underscore the critical importance of ensuring optimal ART adherence to improve outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Practical and policy implications

The study findings have important implications for clinical practice and pediatric TB–HIV care programs. Children with low CD4 counts and a history of opportunistic infections are at the highest risk of mortality, highlighting the need for routine immunologic monitoring and early identification of high-risk children to guide timely interventions. Strengthening ART adherence support through counselling, follow-up, and caregiver engagement is critical to improving survival. Integrating these strategies into pediatric HIV care guidelines and national TB–HIV programs can directly reduce mortality and improve survival outcomes among TB–HIV co-infected children.

The study was conducted in nearly half of the public hospitals in Addis Ababa, which enhances its external validity and provides valuable evidence on TB–HIV co-infected children in this high-burden urban setting. However, because the study was limited to urban health facilities in Addis Ababa; where access to HIV care, diagnostic services, and treatment follow-up is relatively better than in rural areas, the observed survival outcomes are expected to be better in this urban setting compared to rural setting. Furthermore, differences in healthcare infrastructure, patient follow-up, and the availability of treatment services between urban and rural areas may limit the generalizability of these findings to the broader population in Ethiopia. Therefore, caution should be exercised when extrapolating these results to national-level estimates. Future multi-center studies including both urban and rural populations are needed to provide more representative national estimates.

Study limitation

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective cohort design relied on routinely collected medical records, which may contain incomplete or missing data, limiting the availability of some important variables. Although a complete-case analysis was applied due to low levels of missing data, exclusion of incomplete observations (10.8% overall) may have introduced some degree of selection bias. However, comparison of baseline characteristics between complete and incomplete cases did not show meaningful differences, suggesting that the impact on estimates is likely limited. Second, survival bias may have occurred, as children who presented late or were lost to follow-up early might differ systematically from those retained.

Third, the number of outcome events was relatively small (35 events), increasing the risk of overfitting in the Cox regression model and resulting in wide confidence intervals for certain predictors. The events-per-variable ratio was approximately 8.75, which is slightly below the commonly recommended threshold of 10–15 for optimal model reliability. As a result, the confidence intervals for some predictors were relatively wide, indicating a degree of uncertainty in the effect estimates. The wide confidence intervals may be attributed to our study sample size, which was not very large. To minimize overfitting, the final model was restricted to a limited number of clinically relevant variables.

Fourth, unmeasured confounders, such as socioeconomic factors, including caregivers’ occupation, educational level, and access to nutritional support, could influence survival outcomes but were not captured in the dataset. Fifth, cause-specific or relative survival was not assessed due to a lack of data on specific causes of mortality, which might overestimate the TB–HIV co-infection associated death rate. This study could not reliably distinguish TB-related deaths from non-TB deaths in the medical records; therefore, all-cause mortality was used as the outcome. This may introduce some misclassification bias.

Besides the above-mentioned limitations, there are others. In this study, patients lost to follow-up were treated as censored observations. However, in resource-limited settings, loss to follow-up may be informative and could be associated with unrecorded mortality. Additionally, loss to follow-up and transfers were treated as censoring events, which may introduce informative censoring bias despite sensitivity analysis. Although sensitivity analysis excluding these individuals did not materially change the findings, the assumption of non-informative censoring underlying the Cox proportional hazards model may not be fully satisfied. Alternative approaches, such as competing risks models (Fine–Gray sub-distribution hazards model) or cumulative incidence functions, could provide more accurate estimates in the presence of informative censoring and should be considered in future research.

Variables such as CD4 count, opportunistic infection history, and ART adherence may change over time; however, they were treated as baseline-fixed covariates in the Cox proportional hazards model. This may lead to misclassification of exposure status over the follow-up period and limit the ability to draw causal inferences. Additionally, measurement of ART adherence was based on routine clinical records, which may be subject to reporting bias and could underestimate true non-adherence. This misclassification may have influenced the observed associations.

The sample size was calculated based on the expected proportion of mortality rather than using formal survival analysis methods, such as log-rank test power calculations. However, the number of observed events was sufficient to support the Cox regression analysis and provide meaningful results. Lastly, the effect of IPT on mortality could not be reliably evaluated because IPT initiation occurred after TB treatment completion, introducing potential immortal time bias. Future studies incorporating time-varying covariates would better capture the dynamic nature of these factors.

Conclusion

The five-year survival rate among TB–HIV co-infected children was 83.3%, with mortality significantly associated with low CD4 count, history of opportunistic infections, and fair/poor ART adherence. Clinically, these findings highlight the need for early identification of immunosuppressed children, targeted interventions for high-risk groups, and strengthened pediatric HIV care services. Policy-wise, integrating routine CD4 monitoring, infection surveillance, and ART adherence support into national TB–HIV programs is essential to improve child survival. Future research should focus on prospective studies with larger sample sizes to validate these predictors and evaluate the effectiveness of interventions in pediatric TB–HIV populations in high-burden settings.
