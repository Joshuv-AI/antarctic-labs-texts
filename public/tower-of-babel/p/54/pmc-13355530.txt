Host-selective PMA-PCR enhances bacterial diversity recovery and lowers detection limits in plant microbiome profiling
Authors: Anatolijs Bervjačonoks, Antonija Rimša, Ivars Anspoks, Ance Roga, Megija Luņģe, Dita Gudrā, Anitra Zīle, Elza Kaktiņa, Jevgenija Ņečajeva, Davids Fridmanis, Anete Borodušķe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCo-amplification of host DNA remains a significant obstacle in plant microbiome profiling, as universal taxonomic markers, such as the 16S rRNA gene, are also present in plant organellar genomes. While strategies like blocking primers can enrich bacterial reads, they rely on prior sequence knowledge and may introduce bias. We developed a sequence-independent approach that suppresses host DNA amplification by combining physical pretreatments that selectively compromise plant cell integrity with propidium monoazide (PMA) treatment, which binds exposed DNA and prevents its PCR amplification. We observed that various pretreatments—including cryopreservation and mechanical disruption enhanced plant cell susceptibility to PMA without affecting bacterial cells. The applied approach resulted in significantly increased proportions of bacterial reads and improved detection of bacterial diversity in downstream NGS. Compared to sequence-dependent strategies such as blocking primers, this method achieved comparable or superior performance in reducing host DNA interference—particularly in low-biomass samples. Our results establish cryopreservation-enhanced PMA-PCR as a robust, sequence-independent method for high-resolution plant microbiome profiling.

Introduction

Co-amplification of host DNA, particularly chloroplast and mitochondrial 16S rRNA gene sequences, remains a major technical limitation in amplicon-based profiling of plant-associated microbiomes (Beckers et al. 2016, Lefèvre et al. 2020). This issue is especially pronounced in studies of tissues with low microbial biomass—such as flowers, fruits, and seeds—where host-derived reads can dominate sequencing datasets, severely compromising the resolution, sensitivity, and accuracy of microbial community analyses (Arenz et al. 2015, Ikenaga et al. 2016).

This challenge extends beyond basic microbiome characterization. In plant disease diagnostics, where early detection of pathogenic taxa is critical, the high background of host DNA can obscure low-abundance pathogens, leading to false negatives and reduced diagnostic efficacy (Tsoktouridis et al. 2014).

Several molecular strategies have been developed to mitigate host DNA interference, primarily by exploiting sequence divergences between bacterial and plant organellar rRNA genes. Among the most widely used are blocking primers, which bind plant-specific sequences and incorporate modifications that inhibit elongation during PCR, thereby selectively suppressing host amplification (Arenz et al. 2015). Similarly, PNA (peptide nucleic acid) and LNA (locked nucleic acid) clamps act as competitive inhibitors, binding with high affinity to chloroplast and mitochondrial 16S rRNA gene regions and preventing amplification (Lundberg et al. 2013, Ikenaga et al. 2016). Primer redesign strategies that favor bacterial amplification while discriminating against plant organellar sequences have also been employed with varying success (Arenz et al. 2015).

While these techniques can be highly effective, they rely on prior sequence information and may exhibit reduced efficiency in poorly characterized plant hosts. Furthermore, they risk unintended exclusion of bacterial taxa with organellar-like 16S rRNA gene sequences, potentially biasing community composition profiles (Abellan-Schneyder et al. 2021).

To overcome these limitations, sequence-independent approaches have been explored. Among them, density gradient centrifugation has shown promise for physically separating microbial cells from plant material. However, such methods are technically demanding and not easily scalable (Beckers et al. 2016).

During the investigation of the viability of complex endophytic microbial communities inhabiting the wild tree Sambucus nigra, we applied the optimized propidium monoazide PMA-PCR technique to distinguish between DNA from live and dead bacterial cells. PMA–PCR discriminates between viable and dead cells by allowing PMA to selectively penetrate cells with compromised membranes, where it covalently binds to DNA upon light activation, thereby preventing its amplification during PCR and enabling quantification of DNA originating solely from viable cells (Nocker et al. 2007). We serendipitously observed that after certain pretreatments that compromise plant cell integrity, followed by PMA treatment the host DNA amplification was reduced. Unexpectedly, analysis of next-generation sequencing data revealed that PMA-treated samples consistently showed a higher proportion of bacterial reads and a significantly greater number of bacterial taxa detected.

This observation led to the hypothesis that the harsh blending process that we used as a disintegration method for preparing S.nigra fruit slurry, primarily disrupted plant cells—likely due to their increased sensitivity to shear stress and larger cell size—resulting in increased exposure of plant DNA to PMA. This differential susceptibility suggests that controlled physical or enzymatic pretreatments could enhance the selective suppression of host DNA while preserving microbial DNA integrity. Building on this observation, we systematically evaluated a range of pretreatment strategies to enhance membrane permeability between plant and microbial cells, thereby improving the selective action of PMA. Several strategies may facilitate such selective permeability, including cryopreservation in glycerol, which disrupts plant vacuoles through ice crystal formation; ultrasonic treatment, which generates localized shear stress; and enzymatic degradation of cell wall components such as cellulose or pectin, which can increase plant membrane permeability. Building on these considerations, the present study aimed to evaluate how different pretreatment conditions influence the selective action of PMA and their effect on the recovery of bacterial community profiles from S. nigra samples.

Our findings demonstrate that optimized pretreatment combined with PMA-PCR significantly improves microbial signal recovery and diversity assessment in complex plant-derived samples. This method enhances the detection of low-abundance taxa and reduces host-derived sequence noise without requiring prior sequence knowledge. As such, it presents a robust, and broadly applicable strategy for host DNA suppression in plant microbiome research, with potential to improve both fundamental research and diagnostic applications.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

To investigate the use of propidium monoazide (PMA) for selectively suppressing plant DNA in plant-associated microbiome studies, samples were prepared using pre-cultured plant cells and a standardized mock bacterial community. Seven bacterial strains used were previously isolated from Sambucus nigra (Supplementary material 1).

The bacterial strains were cultured in overnight cultures at 28°C with shaking at 190 r/m for 16 h using LB Broth Low Salt (pH 7.2). Seven bacterial strains were mixed in equal optical density (OD) ratios to create a mock bacterial community.

Plant cells were obtained from sterile Sambucus nigra suspension cultures, development of the cultures is described elsewhere (Borodušķe et al. 2022).

PMA treatment and assessment of potential inhibitory effects of the sample matrix

One milliliter of PBS containing 100 μM PMA (final concentration in the sample) was added to the samples combining 300 mg freshly harvested plant cells from axenic S. nigra cell suspension cultures and bacterial biomass from 1 ml of seven-strain mock community (OD₆₂₀ = 0.5; equal volume ratios). Both the plant and bacterial material were pelleted biomass prior to addition, with no growth medium present at the time of PMA treatment. In the case of testing optimal PMA concentration, PMA has been tested at the following concentrations—10, 50, 70, 100, 150, and 200 μM (final concentration in the sample). Samples were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 5 min followed by an exposure to blue light (470 nm) for 30 min using a LED strip under gentle agitation (50 r/m) in a shaker incubator. After photocatalytic activation, samples were centrifuged at 500 x g, and the supernatant was removed. The resulting biomass was frozen at –20°C and stored for subsequent DNA extraction.

PMA-PCR has been reported to be sensitive to inhibitory compounds commonly present in complex environmental samples and therefore often requires case-specific optimization. To determine whether the S. nigra-derived matrix affected PMA DNA blocking performance, an extract was prepared by disintegrating S. nigra cells in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) using bead beating. The resulting homogenate was filter-sterilized and added to the samples at defined concentrations prior the PMA treatment. Quantification cycle (Cq) values were measured across a gradient of live-cell DNA proportions to assess potential inhibitory effects associated with the S. nigra matrix.

Sample pre-treatment

In total, three pretreatment methods were tested on control samples combining 300 mg freshly harvested plant cells from axenic S. nigra cell suspension cultures and bacterial biomass from 1 ml of seven-strain mock community (OD₆₂₀ = 0.5; equal volume ratios).

For the freezing pre-treatment, 500 µl of sterile 12% (v/v) glycerol were added and samples were frozen at –20°C or –80°C for a week. Following freezing, samples were rapidly thawed at 36°C to minimize deleterious effects on bacterial cells, centrifuged at 100 x g, and the supernatant was discarded to eliminate residual glycerol.

For the ultrasonic pre-treatment samples were processed in an ultrasonic bath (Sonorex 60 Bandelin, 20 kHz frequency) for three different time intervals: 1, 5, and 20 min.

For the enzymatic pre-treatment, 1.5%, 2.5%, or 3.5% cellulase (Onozuka R10, Duchefa C8001) in combination with 0.75% macerozyme R-10 (Duchefa, M8002) concentrations were used to digest plant cell walls. The enzyme solution consisted of 10 mM MES, 10 mM KCl, 0.4 M mannitol, with the selected cellulase concentration and 0.75% macerozyme at pH 5.7. The solution was heated at 55°C for 10 min, cooled at room temperature, and supplemented with CaCl₂ (5 mM final concentration) and 0.1% (w/v) bovine serum albumin. The enzyme solution was filter sterilized with 0.2 µm filter. Samples containing 300 mg plant cell biomass and bacterial mock community were treated with 1 ml of the enzyme solution, subjected to vacuum infiltration for 10 min, and were incubated on an orbital shaker at room temperature for 2, 6, or 18 h to facilitate enzymatic action on the plant cell walls.

After each pre-treatment, each sample was subjected to PMA treatment as described above in section 2.2.

Assessment of PMA method sensitivity using serial dilutions

To assess the sensitivity of the PMA method, serial dilutions of the bacterial culture were prepared from the original mock community with an optical density (OD₆₂₀) of 1.0, corresponding to ∼10⁸–10⁹ cells. The following dilutions were subsequently tested: OD₆₂₀ 1 : 1 000 (∼10⁵–10⁶ cells), OD₆₂₀ 1 : 1 000 000 (∼10²–10³ cells), and OD₆₂₀ 1 : 1 000 000 000 (∼10⁰–10¹ cells).

Plant cell amount remained constant at 300 mg across all samples, and PMA treatment, blue light exposure, and incubation parameters were standardized to allow for accurate assessment of PMA’s ability to block DNA amplification at varying bacterial concentrations.

DNA extraction

DNA was extracted using a method described by (Tsoktouridis et al. 2014). In brief, biomass was homogenized using 4 mm stainless steel beads. Cells were lysed using CTAB buffer with PVPP and β-mercaptoethanol (0.2%) added to remove PCR inhibitors. DNA was isolated from the homogenized samples using chloroform/phenol extraction and precipitation with isopropanol.

DNA concentration was estimated using the Quant-iT™ PicoGreen™ dsDNA Assay Kit using a Tecan Microplate Reader (Infinite M200 PRO).

Quantitative PCR

To optimize pretreatment conditions for selective suppression of host-derived amplicons, we performed quantitative qPCR targeting the 16S rRNA gene. qPCR was selected as a rapid and cost-effective screening method to compare relative differences in amplicon yield across treatment conditions. Because universal 16S primers amplify both bacterial and plant organellar 16S sequences in mixed samples, qPCR results were used only to assess relative changes in total 16S amplification between pretreatments, not to determine absolute bacterial or host DNA abundance. Conditions that produced the greatest reduction in total 16S qPCR signal while preserving bacterial amplification were taken forward for validation by 16S amplicon sequencing.

Following DNA extraction, the samples were standardized to 10 ng µl-1. A quantitative PCR reaction was performed using a Thermo Fisher QuantStudio 3 instrument, with a reaction mixture containing 0.25 µM primers (785F (Klindworth et al. 2013) and 1100R) targeting 16S rRNA gene variable regions V5 and V6 (Beckers et al. 2016), SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher), and nuclease-free water. PCR conditions included denaturation at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, 57°C for 30 s and 72°C for 30 s (data acquisition) followed by dissociation curve step. Data was collected and analysed in Design & Analysis software 2.8.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Blocking primer (Arenz et al. 2015) and degenerate primer (Tsoktouridis et al. 2014) methods were tested in parallel. qPCR was performed with a mixture of blocking primers (for chloroplast and mitochondrial DNA) and universal bacterial primers. All primer sequences are available in Supplementary 2.

Assessing the effect of PMA-qPCR on the number of detected bacterial reads in 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing of wild Sambucus nigra samples

Immature fruits of S. nigra were harvested, surface-sterilized, and homogenized in phosphate-buffered saline using a laboratory blender. The resulting homogenate was subjected to PMA treatment as described above. Following treatment, samples were centrifuged at 13 200 x g for 3 min to pellet the biomass. The obtained biomass was stored at –80°C until DNA extraction.

DNA was extracted as described above and subsequently used for amplification of the 16S rRNA gene (V5–V6 region) and NGS analysis. The number of bacterial reads obtained from PMA-treated and untreated samples was compared to evaluate the impact of PMA-qPCR treatment on bacterial community detection in wild S. nigra samples.

Next-generation sequencing

Primers were designed for PCR amplification of the 16S rRNA V5−V6 region specific to the bacterial domain, following the 16S Metagenomic Sequencing Library Preparation protocol for Illumina MiSeq (Illumina Inc., USA). Briefly, 4 ng of DNA was amplified using V5 (785F) and V6 (1100R) primers with the Phusion U Multiplex PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) under the following thermal cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 98°C for 30 s; 35 cycles of 98°C for 10 s, 67°C for 15 s, and 72°C for 15 s; and a final elongation at 72°C for 7 min. PCR product yield was assessed by 1.2% agarose gel electrophoresis and purified using the NucleoMag NGS Clean-Up and Size Select kit (Macherey-Nagel GmbH, Germany). The concentration of purified PCR products was measured with the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit on a Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). In the second PCR step, 4 ng of purified V5-V6 amplicon was used for index attachment, employing i7 and i5 index primers from a custom-ordered Nextera XT Index Kit (Illumina Inc., USA) supplied by Metabion International AG (Germany). The same Phusion U Multiplex PCR Master Mix and thermal cycling protocol were used. The indexed 16S rRNA amplicons were pooled and purified using NucleoMag magnetic beads. Amplicon quality and yield were assessed with the Agilent High Sensitivity DNA kit on an Agilent 2100 BioAnalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA), and with the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit on Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer. Prior to sequencing, all samples were diluted to 10 pM and pooled. Paired-end sequencing was performed using 500 cycles with the MiSeq Reagent Kit v2 on an Illumina MiSeq platform, targeting a minimum of 20 000 reads per sample.

Sequence analysis and data analysis

Sequence reads were demultiplexed using Illumina MiSeq Reporter Software (Illumina Inc., USA), followed by quality filtering and trimming using fastp tool. Primer sequences were removed from the 5′ and 3′ ends of reads (-f 30, -F 30, -5 20, -3 20), along with quality filtering (-q 20) and minimum length filtering (-l 40), using the –correction option. All quality-approved sequences were imported into QIIME2 v.2024.2 (Bolyen et al. 2019) for further analysis. The DADA2 (Callahan et al. 2016) plugin was used to merge paired-end reads and perform quality control; phiX reads and chimeric sequences were filtered using the pooled consensus method. The resulting feature table and representative sequences were used for de novo clustering with the vsearch (Rognes et al. 2016) plugin at a 97% sequence identity threshold. Multiple sequence alignment was performed using MAFFT (Katoh et al. 2013), and phylogenetic trees were constructed with FastTree2 (Price et al. 2010). Taxonomic assignment was carried out using a prefitted sklearn-based classifier, with reference to the Silva v.138.1 database at 99% identity (Glöckner et al. 2017), trained via a naïve Bayes classifier (Pedregosa et al. 2011). No-template controls were processed in the same manner as sample sequences. However, after DADA2 filtering and chimera removal, no sequences remained in the controls; therefore, they were excluded from downstream analysis.

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.6.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Data were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and for homogeneity of variances using the Brown–Forsythe test. When the assumption of equal variances was not met, Welch’s correction was applied. Differences among treatment groups were evaluated using one-way Welch’s ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test (Fig. 1b and c), lognormal ANOVA with Dunnett’s test (Fig. 3a), or one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test (Figs. 3b and 4a). Pairwise comparisons between two independent groups were performed using an unpaired two-tailed t-test (Fig. 3d). For Fig. 5b, two-way ANOVA was applied to assess the effect of treatment, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was determined at α = 0.05. Results are presented as mean ± SD, with significance levels indicated as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001. Groups sharing the same letter were not significantly different, whereas those marked with different letters differed significantly.

Results

PMA selectively inhibits PCR amplification of dead cell DNA in Sambucus nigra cell suspensions

To evaluate potential matrix effects, S. nigra extract was added to the reaction mix, and Cq values were measured across a gradient of live cell DNA proportions. No significant effect of the S. nigra matrix on Cq values was detected (Two-way ANOVA, matrix effect: F₁,₁₆ = 0.8057, p = 0.3827; live/dead DNA ratio effect: F₃,₁₆ = 70.73, P < 0.0001), indicating that matrix components did not inhibit PMA-PCR under the tested conditions (Fig. 1a).

Because PMA-PCR efficiency is affected by both PMA concentration and the effectiveness of light activation, we next tested a range of PMA concentrations (Fig. 1b) and biomass loads (Fig. 1c). PMA concentration had a significant impact on qPCR performance (Welch’s ANOVA, W (7, 6.760) = 147.2, P < 0.0001). Cq values generally increased with higher PMA concentrations, consistent with more efficient exclusion of non-viable cell DNA. However, this increase plateaued at 100 µM PMA, with no significant difference in Cq values between 100 µM and 200 µM treatments, suggesting that 100 µM is sufficient for effective discrimination while maintaining cost-efficiency.

Serial dilution of biomass improved PMA-PCR performance, likely due to enhanced light penetration necessary for photoactivation of PMA. However, further reduction of biomass below 3 mg/ml did not yield additional improvements in Cq values. Based on these results, all subsequent experiments were performed using 100 µM PMA and a biomass concentration of 3 mg ml-1.

Enhanced resolution of endophytic bacterial communities in Sambucus nigra fruits using PMA-PCR prior to amplicon sequencing

PMA-PCR based analysis of bacterial communities from immature fruit samples of wild S. nigra revealed that PMA-treated samples consistently showed a higher proportion of bacterial reads (Mann–Whitney U test, two-tailed, P = 0.0012) and a significantly greater number of bacterial taxa detected (Mann–Whitney U test, two-tailed, P = 0.0022) (Fig. 2).

This observation led to the hypothesis that the harsh blending process that we used as a disintegration method for preparing S.nigra fruit slurry, primarily disrupted plant cells—likely due to their increased sensitivity to shear stress and larger cell size—resulting in increased exposure of plant DNA to PMA. Consequently, this may have led to the selective suppression of plant DNA amplification, while DNA from intact endophytic bacteria remained largely unaffected.

Pretreatment with cellulase and freezing enhances suppression of plant DNA in PMA-PCR

To test the hypothesis that selective disruption of plant cells can enhance PMA-PCR–mediated suppression of host plant DNA without compromising bacterial viability, we evaluated three pretreatment methods: enzymatic digestion of plant cell walls with cellulase/pectinase, ultrasonic disruption, and cryopreservation.

For enzymatic pretreatment, in vitro cell suspensions of S. nigra were spiked with a defined bacterial mock community and subjected to cellulase/pectinase treatment at two enzyme concentrations and three incubation durations. The goal was to increase plant cell fragility, leading to the selective disruption of plant cells, rendering plant DNA more accessible to PMA. Higher enzyme concentrations and longer incubation times led to a greater increase in Cq values, suggesting more effective suppression of plant DNA amplification (Fig. 3a). Inclusion of a no-PMA control in this specific experimental setup would have further strengthened attribution of observed effects to PMA treatment. However, this experiment was primarily designed to compare different pretreatment strategies in combination with PMA-PCR, and therefore a no-PMA control was not included in this comparison. Notably, no-PMA controls were included in parallel experiments involving ultrasound and freezing pretreatments, where PMA alone did not substantially affect Cq values relative to untreated controls, suggesting a limited standalone effect under the tested conditions.

We also tested cryopreservation as a pretreatment, possibly allowing for differential disruption of plant cells. Sambucus nigra cell suspensions spiked with the bacterial mock community were frozen in 12% glycerol at either −20°C or −80°C for one week. Following thawing and PMA treatment, both freezing conditions significantly increased Cq values in 16S rRNA gene amplification, indicating enhanced accessibility of plant DNA to PMA binding (Fig. 3b).

To verify that freezing did not compromise bacterial cell viability, bacterial cultures and S. nigra cell suspensions were separately frozen, then subjected to PMA treatment. While a slight increase in Cq values was observed in bacterial samples—suggesting minor viability loss—this effect was not statistically significant. In contrast, plant-derived DNA amplification was significantly suppressed post-freezing (Fig. 3d).

We also tested sonication as a differential lysis method, hypothesizing that plant cells—due to their large vacuoles—might be more susceptible to disruption than bacterial cells. However, under the tested conditions, sonication did not selectively affect plant cells, and no significant improvement in suppression of plant DNA amplification was observed (Fig. 3c).

Cryopreservation combined with PMA-PCR significantly improves microbial DNA detection limits and performs comparably to blocking primers in suppressing host plant DNA

Observations of increased Cq values following PMA treatment combined with cryopreservation, together with a significant effect of the treatment on sterile plant biomass but no significant effect on bacteria-only samples, suggest that PMA-PCR coupled with cryotreatment may enhance the resolution of 16S rRNA gene-based next-generation sequencing of plant-derived bacterial communities. To evaluate whether the observed increase in Cq values primarily resulted from suppression of plant DNA and translated into improved detection of bacterial diversity, homogenized biomass of immature fruits of wild S. nigra was subjected to cryopretreatment or left untreated, followed by PMA-PCR. The resulting 16S rRNA gene V5–V6 amplicons were analyzed using next-generation sequencing. The results demonstrated that freezing pretreatment combined with PMA-PCR significantly increased the number of detected bacterial genera, indicating enhanced sensitivity in microbial community profiling (Lefèvre et al. 2020) (Fig. 4a). At the phylum level, PMA-PCR enabled detection of 10 additional bacterial phyla that were not observed in control samples, while the combination of PMA-PCR and cryopretreatment revealed two additional low-abundance phyla—Cyanobacteria and Hydrogenedentes—in the analyzed environmental DNA samples (Fig. 4b).

We compared the effectiveness of the PMA-PCR method with two other approaches commonly used to reduce host plant DNA coamplification: blocking primers (Lefèvre et al. 2020) and degenerate primers (Tsoktouridis et al. 2014). Degenerate primers, designed to selectively amplify bacterial DNA, resulted in significantly higher Cq values than those obtained from pure bacterial samples. This suggests suboptimal amplification of our bacterial community, likely due to primer mismatches or reduced efficiency (Klindworth et al. 2013, Beckers et al. 2016).

In contrast, PMA-PCR produced Cq values comparable to those obtained using blocking primers, indicating that both methods offer similar levels of suppression of host plant DNA while maintaining amplification of DNA from diverse bacterial taxa (Fig. 5a).

To evaluate whether this similarity extended to microbial community profiling, we subjected amplicons from both methods to NGS. At high bacterial DNA proportions, coamplification of plant DNA had minimal impact on bacterial read recovery. However, as bacterial DNA proportions decreased (0.1% and 0.01%), plant DNA increasingly outcompeted bacterial DNA during amplification, leading to a sharp drop in the number of bacterial reads recovered (Fig. 5b).

Notably, when samples were pretreated by cryopreservation and subjected to PMA-PCR, the number of bacterial reads improved significantly, especially at low bacterial DNA concentrations. At 0.1% bacterial DNA, PMA-PCR outperformed the blocking primer method, yielding a significantly higher number of bacterial reads (Chai et al. 2020). These results indicate that cryopreservation combined with PMA-PCR enhances detection sensitivity and microbial resolution in samples with low bacterial biomass, offering a robust alternative to blocking primers for minimizing plant DNA interference.

To evaluate whether the increased sensitivity observed in qPCR data translated into improved detection of low-abundance bacterial taxa that may otherwise be masked by co-amplification of host plant DNA, amplicons were analyzed using next-generation sequencing (Fig. 6). The results demonstrated that both treatments improved bacterial community taxonomic resolution by maintaining proportions of bacterial taxa more similar to original mock community over several orders of magnitude bacterial DNA dilutions than in control samples. However, it also has to be noted that in samples containing only 0.01% bacterial DNA, low-abundance Pantoea sp. was detected using the blocking primer approach, while it was not recovered in PMA-treated or control samples (Fig. 6d). Principal coordinates analysis based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarities of 16S rRNA gene V5–V6 amplicon data showed that samples treated with PMA-PCR and blocking primers clustered more closely with the original mock community, particularly at higher bacterial DNA dilutions. This indicates that both approaches reduce compositional distortion in plant-derived samples with low bacterial biomass.

Bray–Curtis-based PERMANOVA indicated a significant effect of treatment on community composition (pseudo-F12,33 = 7.81, R² = 0.74, P = 0.001). However, significant differences in multivariate dispersion were also detected (PERMDISP, F = 6.18, P < 0.001), indicating heterogeneity in within-group variability. Therefore, pairwise PERMANOVA comparisons were additionally performed to further resolve treatment effects. Pairwise PERMANOVA analysis showed that both methods partially preserved the original community structure at low bacterial DNA proportions. For PMA-PCR-treated samples containing 0.1% bacterial DNA, no significant difference was observed relative to the original mock community (pseudo-F = 3.09, R² = 0.38, P = 0.08). Similarly, blocking primer-treated samples containing 1% bacterial DNA showed no significant difference compared to the mock community (pseudo-F = 2.85, R² = 0.32, P = 0.064).

Overall, these results indicate that both PMA-PCR and blocking primer approaches improve the detection and representation of low-abundance taxa, providing a more accurate reconstruction of plant-associated bacterial community structure under conditions of high host DNA background.

Discussion

Classical taxonomic markers such as 16S rRNA gene are widely used in plant microbiome studies. (Yilmaz et al. 2014, Beckers et al. 2016) However, their application is often hindered by co-amplification of host mitochondrial and chloroplast DNA, which significantly reduces the efficiency of bacterial DNA amplification and, consequently, impairs accurate assessment of microbial diversity (Arenz et al. 2015, Lefèvre et al. 2020).

Several techniques have been developed and implemented to mitigate host DNA co-amplification. These approaches typically exploit small sequence differences between bacterial and plant organelle DNA to selectively block plant DNA or preferentially amplify bacterial targets (Arenz et al. 2015, Ikenaga et al. 2016). However, such sequence-based strategies carry inherent limitations: they may inadvertently exclude bacterial taxa with sequences similar to host DNA, and they require prior knowledge of the plant genome to ensure effective blocking (Ikenaga et al. 2016, Lefèvre et al. 2020).

Co-amplification of host DNA also remains a major bottleneck for the broader implementation of NGS-based diagnostics in plant microbial pathology. While NGS theoretically offers comprehensive detection of all microorganisms present in a plant sample, host DNA contamination reduces sensitivity, especially for low-abundance pathogens—posing a challenge for phytosanitary diagnostics (Zeng et al. 2016, Chai et al. 2020).

To address this, a method that enables species-independent, selective suppression of plant DNA, without compromising bacterial DNA amplification, would significantly improve both microbiome analyses and diagnostic sensitivity (Elizaquível et al. 2014, Gensberger et al. 2014, Papanicolas et al. 2019).

While investigating microbial viability in S. nigra samples stored long-term in glycerol solution at −80°C using PMA-PCR, we unexpectedly observed a significant increase in bacterial read counts and number of detected bacterial genera in PMA-treated samples compared to untreated controls. This observation led us to hypothesize that cryopreservation disproportionately damages plant cells, making them more permeable to PMA, while bacterial cells remain relatively unaffected—thus enabling selective suppression of host DNA (Elizaquível et al. 2014, Zeng et al. 2016). Given that plant cells are notoriously difficult to cryopreserve (Nausch and Buyel 2021), whereas bacterial cells typically retain high viability in glycerol-based storage, we explored cryopreservation as a selective pretreatment.

To test this hypothesis, we evaluated three pretreatment methods aimed at increasing plant cell membrane permeability while minimizing effects on bacterial cells: (1) cryopreservation, (2) enzymatic digestion of plant cell walls, and (3) sonication. Among these, enzymatic treatment and cryopreservation yielded the most effective suppression of host DNA, as indicated by significantly increased Cq values in plant-specific qPCR assays after PMA treatment.

When comparing the two most efficient pretreatment methods, enzymatic treatment requires extended incubation, raising concerns about potential bacterial growth during the process, which could bias taxonomic composition. In contrast, cryopreservation with a cryoprotectant such as glycerol offers a rapid, and practical approach that avoids incubation-associated biases (Papanicolas et al. 2019). Our findings suggest that cryopreservation effectively distinguishes plant and bacterial cells based on PMA permeability. Although we observed a slight, non-significant increase in Cq values for bacterial DNA post-treatment, further studies are needed to assess whether specific bacterial taxa are more sensitive to cryopreservation (Wang et al. 2021). Although the inclusion of plant-specific markers would further strengthen this observation, we prioritized conditions that reflect typical plant microbiome workflows, as the primary aim of this study was to evaluate the effectiveness of PMA treatment in improving the resolution of plant-associated microbial communities.

We further compared the cryopreservation-enhanced PMA-PCR approach to the widely used blocking primer method for suppressing plant DNA co-amplification. While both methods performed similarly in samples with a high proportion of bacterial DNA, blocking primers were significantly less effective in samples with lower bacterial DNA abundance—where host DNA amplification dominated and nearly masked bacterial signals (Arenz et al. 2015, Lefèvre et al. 2020). In contrast, PMA-PCR maintained significantly higher bacterial read counts under these challenging conditions. PMA-PCR demonstrated a significantly improved limit of detection in samples containing as little as 0.1% bacterial DNA.

We also evaluated the effect of PMA-PCR on bacterial community composition and structure using 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing of wild Sambucus nigra-derived material. Application of PMA-PCR in combination with cryopretreatment significantly improved the taxonomic resolution of endophytic bacterial communities. While PMA treatment alone enabled the detection of representatives from 10 additional bacterial phyla that were previously masked by host plant DNA co-amplification in control samples, its combination with cryopretreatment further increased resolution by enabling the detection of taxa belonging to two additional low-abundance phyla, thereby substantially altering the inferred bacterial diversity and community composition in immature fruits of S. nigra.

In addition, analyses of mock community samples with low bacterial DNA proportions demonstrated that PMA-PCR improves the reliability of bacterial community profiling under conditions of strong plant DNA background and limited microbial biomass. The approach applied in this study was designed to enhance the recovery and detection of bacterial sequences in plant-associated samples, rather than to directly assess bacterial viability. Potential methodological biases cannot be fully excluded and should be considered in future investigations. Further work using phylogenetically diverse mock communities will be important to assess potential taxon-specific biases associated with PMA-PCR and to better define its applicability across different microbial groups.

Overall, these results indicate that PMA-PCR provides increased robustness in preserving bacterial community structure under conditions of both high host DNA background and low bacterial DNA abundance. In particular, PMA-PCR maintained a community composition statistically comparable to the original mock community at lower bacterial DNA proportions than those required for the blocking primer approach, supporting its suitability for low-biomass, plant-derived microbial samples. These findings suggest that cryopreservation-enhanced PMA-PCR provides a powerful, sequence-independent tool for improving the resolution of plant microbiome studies (Zeng et al. 2016, Chai et al. 2020).
