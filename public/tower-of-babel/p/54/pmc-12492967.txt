Disruption of histone acetylation homeostasis reveals multilayered chromatin regulation for transcriptional resiliency
Authors: Vrinda Venu, Eric M. Small, Cullen Roth, Samantha H. Adikari, Anna Hendrika Cornelia Vlot, Kyle A. Sullivan, Chanaka Roshan Abeyratne, Daniel Jacobson, Shawn R. Starkenburg, Karissa Y. Sanbonmatsu, Christina R. Steadman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpigenetic modifications, nucleosome occupancy, and three-dimensional chromatin architecture collectively create a multi-layered, highly interactive regulatory system for controlling genomic functionality. Dysregulation of epigenetic processes leads to a plethora of abnormalities including disease states. Therapies focused on epigenetic modulation can alter gene expression to correct dysfunction, though the perpetuation of these states and the relationships among chromatin regulatory layers is not well understood.ResultsHere, we investigated global and local chromatin structural and functional responses after acute histone deacetylase inhibitor treatment (suberoylanilide hydroxamic acid) in lung cancer cells across time. Treatment substantially increased global histone acetylation resulting in a pervasive but not distinctive signature. The spread of acetylation did not significantly impact global chromatin accessibility, and nucleosome remodeling largely occurred at finer scales in functionally relevant genomic regions. Indeed, both H3K4 trimethylation, a mark of active transcription, and gene expression changes were altered in a controlled locus-specific manner, suggesting aberrant acetylation indirectly leads to balanced and bidirectional gene expression profiles from tighter regulation of other chromatin features. HDACi treatment induced (13%) genomic rearrangement in chromatin compartmentalization and moderate weakening of topologically associating domains.ConclusionsContinuous wavelet analysis of these features demonstrates that scale-dependent, locus-specific factors influence the relationship between chromatin architecture and functional output, suggesting that regulation of transcription and nucleosome remodeling is not entirely (nor linearly) dependent upon large scale compartment exchange. Structural and functional responses are most pronounced early after treatment with partial persistence of differential local chromatin features and expression later in time; this highlights the plasticity of chromatin regulation, which may have implications for the efficacy of epigenetic treatments. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of multi-layered regulation of transcription: in resilient systems, disruption of one chromatin feature does not distort the regulation of other features in supporting a transcriptional program that allows for survival.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13072-025-00631-4.

Background

Epigenetic processes constitute a series of regulatory mechanisms that cells employ to integrate and process external stimuli, ultimately resulting in the support and control of genomic function without altering the underlying sequence. This fundamental layer of regulation is manifested as chemical modification of chromatin components (histones and DNA), which are thought to alter accessibility of transcriptional machinery, thereby imparting significant influence on gene expression and ultimately, phenotype. Dysregulation of integral epigenetic processes leads to a plethora of abnormalities, ranging from developmental aberrations to disease states such as cancer. As such, direct targeting of modifications using epigenetic therapies focuses on controlling gene expression to correct dysregulation without persistence [5, 37, 57], though in many cases, the level of permanency or genome-wide effects has not been assessed. Epigenetic therapies utilize drugs that inhibit the activity or binding of chromatin remodeling enzymes that are responsible for imparting, removing, or remodeling epigenetic modifications or chromatin features [25]. In particular, histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACi) block enzymes that remove histone acetylation, resulting in either substantially increased acetylation or retention of acetylation that would have otherwise been removed. For its part, acetylation is thought to preferentially associate (or induce) open chromatin regions, thereby allowing for transcription factor binding and eventual gene expression, though direct evidence is lacking for causality. HDACi therapies are currently used in the clinical setting to treat a variety of diseases ranging from cancer [31] to neurobiological disorders [9]. The efficacy of HDACi treatment has been widely explored, particularly in relation to the progression of cancer, where treatment reduces tumor cell growth while concurrently increasing the expression of tumor suppressor genes and decreasing the expression of oncogenes [18, 19, 46]. Interestingly, reduced (hypo)acetylation of histones resulting from epigenetic therapies that inhibit histone acetyltransferase enzymes also produces similar anti-tumor functional outcomes [22]. Potentially, perturbation of acetylation homeostasis triggers a non-proliferative transcriptional program during treatment. Despite these observations, how bidirectional gene expression occurs in response to these global changes in acetylation is not well understood. Deeper knowledge of these processes is warranted given the presumed role of acetylation, its correlation to actively transcribed genes in non-disease states, the use of HDACi in clinical settings, and the persistence of drug resistance even for efficacious treatments.

The intricate interplay between epigenetic modifications and gene expression potentially impacts—or is mediated by—genomic structures like compartmentalization for regulation of entire genomic loci. In short, the functionality of the genome is intimately intertwined with its structural features and organization in three-dimensional (3D) space, including chromatin contact frequency, distribution, loop formation, and phase separation [15, 21, 36]. Indeed, the impact of environmental perturbations, including those that contribute to the development and progression of disease states, is mediated by dynamic chromatin remodeling in addition to epigenetic changes. These local features are potentially connected to larger global chromatin structures [18, 54]. The development and utilization of genome-wide chromatin capture technologies, such as Hi-C, delineate chromosomal rearrangements, annotation of genomic events, and provide global snapshots of 3D spatial configurations of chromatin inside the nucleus. These analyses, combined with chromatin accessibility from ATAC-seq assays and epigenetic modification measurements from ChIP-seq experiments can provide visualization of the entire chromatin landscape at various resolutions, ultimately allowing for deeper knowledge of the dynamic interactions between epigenetic modifications and chromatin structure–function relationships [22, 50]. Thus, a clear understanding of chromatin responses—including large (Hi-C) and small scale (ATAC-seq) structural features, along with epigenetic modifications—when acetylation homeostasis is perturbed by an epigenetic therapy, will help elucidate how functional outcomes occur and potentially persist.

Here, we present a comprehensive, genome-wide investigation to ascertain the impact of HDACi treatment on chromatin architecture, accessibility, epigenomics, and genome function. Chromatin responses were assessed in human lung carcinoma cells (A549) after treatment with suberoylanilide hydroxamic acid (SAHA) at two timepoints post treatment (24 and 48 h post treatment). SAHA is the first FDA-approved drug specifically targeted to HDAC enzymes, with non-specific inhibition of multiple HDACs including HDAC1, HDAC2, HDAC3, and HDAC6 [6, 31]. This experimental design serves as a framework for investigating chromatin 3D structure–function dynamics over time, with the 24-h time point providing information on cellular responses from direct interaction with the drug, while the 48-h time point demonstrates the retention of HDACi effects post treatment. This is the first report to describe the impact of SAHA on chromatin features in a paired manner, including histone modifications, accessibility, and contact changes. This detailed investigation of chromatin structure–function dynamics illuminates the complexity and intricate nature of global and local chromatin responses to an acute epigenomic perturbation and its contribution to transcriptional regulation.

We find concurrent alterations in gene expression, chromatin accessibility, and 3D chromatin architecture to various extents, in response to HDACi drug treatment. Importantly, the global increase in acetylation in response to SAHA does not result in a genome-wide increase in chromatin accessibility nor the opening of higher-order chromatin architecture; rather, regulation of local chromatin features supports bidirectional gene expression changes. Differences in compartmentalization and chromatin contacts were observed but their influence on expression is not synchronized in time. Connections between chromatin features and downregulated genes were more prominent, with reduced accessibility and reduced levels of histone modifications supporting transcription, suggesting a shift toward non-permissive chromatin states. Thus, the response to a substantial stressor that imbalances the homeostasis of histone acetylation involves reducing the connection between acetylation and gene expression, potentially as a survival strategy, resulting in tighter regulation of histone methylation. Further, while changes were more prominent 24 h post treatment, some alterations in chromatin features persist 48 h post treatment, suggesting potential long-term impacts of treatment on genome structure and function.

Results

Multi-omics characterization of chromatin response to SAHA treatment

Human lung carcinoma (A549) cells treated with the histone deacetylase inhibitor suberoylanilide hydroxamic acid (SAHA) and without (control) were monitored by ChIP-seq, ATAC-seq, Hi-C and RNA-seq to identify genome-wide epigenomic, chromatin architecture, accessibility, and transcriptional responses. The appropriate dose and duration of suberoylanilide hydroxamic acid (SAHA) treatment was determined from a pilot dose–response study (Figure S1). A concentration of 10 µM SAHA was chosen for subsequent experiments as this treatment considerably altered acetylation on lysine 27 of histone 3 (H3K27ac) while limiting acute cell death (Figure S1A). At both 24- and 48-h post treatment (hpt), intact cells had marginally increased cell size and rounded cell morphology as identified via bright field and fluorescence microscopy (Figure S1B).

By 48 hpt, a small degree of cell death was observed; however, only viable adherent cells were retained for multi-omics analysis. Time course experiments were conducted in triplicate; control (mock-treated) and treated (10 µM SAHA) A549 cells were collected at 24 and 48 hpt. Bulk, genome-wide assays for control and treated paired cells included ChIP-seq (H3ac, H3K27ac, H3K4me3) and RNA-seq, each with three biological replicates, and Hi-C with two biological replicates (Fig. 1A). ATAC-seq samples from three biological replicates per condition were generated from a separate repeated experiment. HDAC inhibitor treatment elicits strong time-dependent responses in chromatin modifications and chromatin structure

Treatment with 10 µM SAHA induced substantial changes in histone modification occupancy and chromatin structure compared to mock-treated (control) cells at each timepoint as highlighted by Principal Component Analysis (Fig. 1B). High replicate concordance was observed in all datasets (Figures S2-S5), suggesting a conserved response across independent biological treatments, as well as low technical error. Specifically, SAHA treatment induced strong treatment-specific changes in H3K27 acetylation occupancy (PC1 explaining 99% sample variance) and H3ac pan-acetyl occupancy (H3 lysines 9, 14, 18, 23, and 27) (PC1 explaining 98% sample variance). These outcomes were expected given the direct inhibition of class I and II HDACs by SAHA, which are involved in H3K27 homeostasis [7, 10]. These findings support that 10 µM SAHA treatment is sufficient to induce genome-wide perturbations in acetylation. Moreover, chromatin associated responses at 24 and 48 hpt, with less direct links to histone acetylation, were markedly different between control and treated cultures including chromatin accessibility, gene expression, H3K4 trimethylation, and to some degree, 3D chromatin contacts (Fig. 1B). Interestingly, while histone acetylation remains substantially altered in treated cells at 48 hpt, the magnitude of separation observed between control and treated cultures in the PCA plot of chromatin structure and functional responses (histone methylation and gene expression) decreases at 48 hpt. This suggests a rebound toward control culture characteristics, though an incomplete renormalization insinuates residual effects of treatment. This indicates not only a stronger effect size at 24 hpt compared to 48 hpt, potentially due to compensatory effects or heterogeneity in viability, but also the potential resiliency of some cell populations to acute acetylation stress.

Robust regulation of the chromatin landscape under perturbed acetylation homeostasis

Histone deacetylase inhibitors, such as SAHA, interfere with enzymes that remove lysine acetylation in histone and non-histone proteins. The ChIP-seq profile of histone 3 lysine 27 acetylation, which functions to promote gene expression, was markedly altered post SAHA treatment at both 24 and 48 hpt (Fig. 2A, curve comparison shown in Figure S6 A, B). At 24 hpt, cells display strong disruption of the modification, including spreading of the signal outside of control peak regions. This results in an overall decrease in peak signal (amplitude) relative to background signal in regions distal to called peaks (Fig. 2A). Additionally, peak annotation analysis indicates that new H3K27ac peaks are found post SAHA treatment, predominately in intronic regions, while peaks in canonical promoter regions are diminished or lost (Figure S7). In tandem, representative genome-browser view tracks (Fig. 2B) show a clear spreading of H3K27ac, reinforcing that SAHA treatment disrupts H3K27ac regulation. Why ‘flattening’ of the H3K27ac peak amplitude is observed at 24 and 48 hpt remains unclear (Fig. 2B). However, in support of this pattern of signal weakening at peaks (lower amplitude, therefore less acetylation), we find decreased expression of genes with histone acetyltransferase (HAT) function (Table S1). The reduced HAT gene expression in response to HDACi treatment is potentially a compensatory response resulting from increased global acetylation. Alternatively, given the relativistic nature of the ChIP-seq method and the inability to quantify true peak amplitude of this method (sometimes overcome by spike-in controls, with varying degrees of adoption in the field), it is possible that peak amplitude remains the same or increased but appears lower as the total ChIP signal has spread throughout the genome. Despite this caveat, the data clearly show that SAHA treatment alters H3K27ac occupancy, with profound genome-wide spreading of H3K27ac to non-canonical regions [24].

Despite the observations of global and loci-specific perturbations of histone acetylation homeostasis, the H3K4 trimethylation profile (HeK4me3), an epigenetic modification associated with active transcription (Fig. 2C, D), and chromatin accessibility profile (Fig. 2E, F) remain robustly regulated. Globally, we observe only a mild broadening of H3K4me3, at both 24 hpt and 48 hpt (Fig. 2C, curve comparison in Figure S6 C, D). Unexpectedly, there is essentially no global change in chromatin accessibility despite SAHA-induced dysregulation of histone acetylation, which has long been associated with active transcription and permissive chromatin remodeling (Fig. 2E, curve comparison in Figure S6 E, F). Overall, these results suggest a decoupling of global histone acetylation from other global regulatory mechanisms following SAHA treatment.

HDACi treatment induces locus-specific regulation of chromatin accessibility and H3K4 trimethylation

The lack of major variation in global chromatin accessibility warranted further investigation. When considering individual open chromatin regions (OCRs), their accessibility is increased (pink dots) and decreased (teal dots) due to HDACi treatment (adj p-value < 0.05 and log twofold change > 0) (Fig. 3A, Figure S8). HDAC inhibition induces more open regions with larger effects (fold change greater than two) (19,684 in Fig. 3A) suggesting a dramatic opening of certain regions. However, there are still 13,415 regions (teal dots, Fig. 3A) with decreased accessibility due to treatment. Collectively, these significantly differentially accessible regions (both open and closed regardless of fold change) due to treatment constitute approximately 29% and 19% of the ~ 342,398 total genomic OCRs at 24 and 48 hpt respectively, and all genomic OCRs cover approximately 5.45% of the genome. The differentially accessible OCRs are primarily associated with genic regions (~ 65%) (gene body ± 1 kb, according to NCBI RefSeq), though 28.5% are localized to intergenic regions, and 6.5% are annotated as ‘cell type specific intergenic’ enhancers. Unexpectedly, these results suggest that a specific well-regulated pattern of chromatin accessibility is maintained, even when histone acetylation homeostasis is globally disrupted, with only a small bias toward increased OCRs at loci resolution. Similarly, at 24 and 48 hpt respectively (Fig. 3B), 64% and 46% of H3K4me3 peaks display significantly increased and decreased H3K4me3 occupancy. However, unlike chromatin accessibility, SAHA treatment induces a fivefold increase in peaks with H3K4me3 peaks (Fig. 3B, pink colored dots and numbers). Transcription remains well-regulated in the face of dysregulated acetylation homeostasis

To better understand the functional implications of globally disrupted chromatin acetylation and loci specific changes in chromatin accessibility and H3K4me3, we turned to differential gene expression analysis. Similar to observations of changes in chromatin accessibility and H3K4me3 occupancy, 25% and 18% of genes are differentially expressed (regardless of fold change) at 24 and 48 hpt respectively (Fig. 3C). While the observed bidirectional differential gene expression has previously been reported in response to HDACi treatment [19, 49], we find substantially more upregulated genes (five times) than downregulated genes (Fig. 3C, pink colored dots and numbers), both with a fold change greater than two. Further, the genes upregulated due to treatment are largely inactive (or expressed at low level) under control conditions (median value of normalized transcript count: 0.94), whereas genes downregulated due to treatment are highly expressed genes in control cultures (median value of normalized transcript count: 111.73) (Fig. 3D, E). Gene ontology pathway analysis uncovers an overrepresentation of genes associated with stress responses and transcription-related pathways amongst upregulated genes, while downregulated genes are involved in pathways associated with mitosis progression (Supplementary File 1). We also performed a transcription factor (TF) motif analysis and observed no specific up or downregulation of TF in response to HDACi treatment (Figure S9). Similar to chromatin accessibility and H3K4me3 occupancy, this bidirectional pattern of differential gene expression (with high concordance amongst replicates) indicates an intact functional regulatory program, despite global disruption of histone acetylation homeostasis.

The chromatin response is specific to the directionality of gene expression changes

Next, we investigated the chromatin features associated with differential gene expression. Irrespective of expression status, gene promoters in cells treated with SAHA show significant spreading of acetylation across gene bodies (Figure S10 A). This is congruent with the signal “flattening” (lower amplitude) genome-wide that we observe after treatment, particularly at genomic regions that were “peaky” with higher amplitude in control cultures. Consequently, genes significantly upregulated (padj < 0.05 and log2 fold change > 2) or downregulated (padj < 0.05 and log2 fold change < −2) at 24 hpt possess relatively lower H3K27ac occupancy at promoters, even when spreading is accounted for; this effect is more pronounced at downregulated genes (Fig. 4A, B). Even when normalizing to H3 levels (in both control and treated cultures), the pattern remains the same, reinforcing our chromatin accessibility findings, and suggesting that alterations in H3K27ac occupancy are not predominately driven by chromatin remodeling (Figure S11). Overall, dysregulation of acetylation, particularly expansion into gene bodies, has limited impact on differential gene expression. For genes with H3K4 trimethylation, we observe a significant increase in occupancy around the TSS of upregulated genes but no major changes in trimethylation at the TSS of downregulated genes (Fig. 4C, D). Conversely, chromatin accessibility is significantly reduced at promoters and across gene bodies of downregulated genes without notable change in accessibility at upregulated genes (Fig. 4E, F). This is an interesting pattern, as downregulation of gene expression associates with a pronounced decrease in H3K27ac and accessibility, but gene upregulation does not require an active increase in these features. On the other hand, H3K4me3 is increased at the TSS of upregulated genes without notable reduction at downregulated genes. The chromatin responses to HDACi vary across the gene; however, a clustering analysis shows that feature changes (as the average profile) are generic patterns across multiple genes rather than driven by a handful of genes (Figure S12). This general profile for both up and downregulated genes and the chromatin features is shown in Fig. 4G.

In agreement with the observed accessibility profile, only ~ 23% of upregulated gene promoters possess differential OCRs with increased accessibility at 24 hpt. Potentially, if a promoter is otherwise accessible (e.g., nucleosome depleted), then to increase gene expression, traditional nucleosome remodeling (histone displacement) may not be necessary. Conversely, ~ 60% of downregulated gene promoters possess differential OCRs with decreased accessibility, suggesting a stronger association between gene down regulation, accessibility, and chromatin remodeling. Further, ~ 36% of upregulated gene promoters had increased H3K4me3, while ~ 60% of downregulated gene promoters had decreased H3K4me3. Thus, while these differences in the gene averaged profile plot are not apparent (Fig. 4C, D), a large percentage of downregulated genes lose H3K4me3. Altogether, controlled chromatin changes are specific to directionality of change in gene expression in a manner that supports their expression pattern. Further, even though histone acetylation is an important epigenetic modification, active addition of H3K27ac is not necessary for triggering gene expression. Increases in chromatin accessibility, which were expected to be driven by increases in acetylation, therefore do not directly underlie transcription activation for a large number of genes in response to SAHA treatment. H3K4me3 along with accessibility changes may truly drive permissive chromatin for transcription, particularly given its known roles in both assembly of the transcriptional preinitiation complex [29] and supporting transcriptional elongation [52, 53]. While the elucidating the precise role of H3K4me3 is still an active area of research (specifically whether it induces transcription or is deposited during the process), its presence is a key indicator of actively transcribed genomic regions. Thus, H3K4me3 remains an important signal for identifying active genes, especially when traditional RNA-seq methods are insufficient for distinguishing between newly synthesized and stable, stored mRNAs.

Finally, gene expression and chromatin changes persist to some degree after treatment. Differential gene expression fold changes at 24 hpt moderately correlate with fold changes at 48 hpt (Spearmann’s ρ = 0.64). A similar pattern occurs for chromatin accessibility, where changes at 24 hpt moderately correlate (ρ = 0.61) with those at 48 hpt, suggesting some persistence. Chromatin modifications H3K27ac and H3K4me3 have low correlation (ρ = 0.27 and ρ = 0.17, respectively) between the timepoints (Figure S13; all correlations are statistically significant with p-value < 0.001). As such, early changes in gene expression and accessibility are largely retained even after 48 hpt, while histone modifications (H3K27ac and H3K4me3) are relatively more dynamic despite the retention of functional change.

HDACi treatment induces moderate rearrangement of 3D chromatin architecture

Simplistically, epigenetic modifications are thought to control gene expression through alterations of chromatin structure, where marks like acetylation modify nucleosome rearrangements ultimately impacting chromatin compartmentalization. Given our interest in the relationship between epigenomic features and chromatin architecture, we sought to determine the impact of SAHA on genome organization through Hi-C analysis. Hi-C data provide information on the frequency and distribution of three-dimensional contacts among chromatin regions. While SAHA induces substantial changes in epigenome profiles and gene expression, treatment does not impact higher order chromatin organization to the same extent, at least at the time points we investigated. At 24 hpt, we find fewer short-range (~ 200 kb) and more long-range (~ 3 MB) chromatin contacts in response to HDACi (Fig. 5A). At broader scales, the chromatin is organized into active (A) and inactive (B) spatial compartments by segregating genomic elements for transcription within A compartments [20]. We define chromatin compartments at 100 kb resolution using eigenvalues computed from Hi-C data and accessibility information from ATAC-seq data to assign A versus B compartments (described in Methods, Compartment Analysis). Other studies have demonstrated that compartment exchange serves to illustrate the rearrangement of genomic structural features, which can vary up to 60% among cell types and in response to perturbations [47]. In response to HDACi, we find moderate genome-wide chromatin compartment exchange (Fig. 5B). At 24 hpt, ~ 13.6% of the genome (100 kb regions) exchanges chromatin compartment identity with the majority (~ 72%) switching from the inactive (B) to the active (A) chromatin compartment, suggesting a moderate alteration of chromatin organization. At 48 hpt, ~ 8.1% of the genome (100 kb bins) switch compartment identity indicating a partial reversal of treatment-induced compartment exchange. Importantly, we find minimal time-dependent shifts in compartment status and high reproducibility among replicates from control experiments (Figure S14), which contrasts sharply with those observed for treated cultures (Figure S15). For genes with differential expression and OCRs with differential accessibility, ~ 10% and ~ 11% (respectively) overlap with an exchanged chromatin compartment (A to B or B to A). Thus, while HDACi induces rearrangement of genome structure, regulation of transcription and nucleosome remodeling is likely not entirely (nor linearly) dependent upon large-scale compartment exchange. Rather, several scale-dependent, locus-specific factors may influence the relationship between broad-scale chromatin compartment exchange and fine-scale functional expression changes.

Chromosome and locus-specific association between compartment exchange and fine-scale functional features

To further investigate relationships among broad-scale structural features and fine-scale functional responses, we performed continuous wavelet transformation (CWT) of B-to-A compartment switching, gene upregulation, and gene density in response to HDACi treatment. To allow for a general comparison, we focused this analysis on a set of representative chromosomes (1, 17, 18, and 19) with particular characteristics, including variations in gene density. For reference, chromosome 1 is the largest (248.4 Mb) human chromosome with moderate gene density (22.6 genes/Mb). Chromosome 17 is small (84.3 Mb) and the second most gene-dense chromosome in the human genome (36.2 genes/Mb). Chromosome 18 is also small (80.5 Mb) but is quite gene-poor (15.5 genes/Mb), and chromosome 19 is even smaller (61.7 Mb) but is the most gene-rich chromosome in the human genome (46.9 genes/Mb). Aside from average differences in gene density across whole chromosomes, we also considered differences in the localization of gene-dense and gene-poor regions along these chromosomes (Figure S16).

Overall, analysis of CWTs for B-to-A compartment-switching and gene upregulation revealed chromosome-specific, location-specific, and scale-dependent signatures and correlations (Fig. 6, Figure S16). These chromosome-specific patterns are found for both B-to-A compartment transition CWTs (Fig. 6A, D, Figure S16 A, D) and gene upregulation CWTs (Fig. 6B, E, Figure S16 B, E). Here, B-to-A compartment switching at broader scales, where the signal is aggregated along wider stretches of the chromosome, is comparable for chromosomes 1, 17, and 19 with higher signal density on the p-arm of the chromosome compared to the q-arm of the chromosome (Fig. 6A, D, Figure S16 A, D). Conversely, on chromosome 18, the B-to-A compartment switching signal density spans the centromere and covers a large portion of the q-arm. As the wavelet becomes more contracted at finer scales (i.e., lower values), the signal is aggregated over smaller regions of the chromosome, and differences between chromosomes become more pronounced (Fig. 6A, D, Figure S16 A, D). For gene upregulation, differences in CWT heatmaps across chromosomes are very evident even at broader scales (Fig. 6B, E, Figure S16 B, E). These variations can only be explained in part by gene density patterns across chromosomes (Figure S17): gene upregulation and gene density CWT heatmaps show strong concordance in chromosomes 1 and 19, which are relatively gene dense. Genome-wide weakening of topologically associating domains with pronounced contact reduction at downregulated genes

Next, we investigated the dynamics of fine-scale chromatin organizational features, including topologically associating domains (TADs) and chromatin loops [35, 43]. Topologically associating domains (TADs) are genomic regions that spatially associate more than expected by chance [11] (example shown in Fig. 7A). Cis interactions within TADs (intra-TAD interactions) in control and treated cells at each time point were compared. There is an overall weakening of intra-TAD interactions at 24 hpt, demonstrated as negative values in (log2) fold change for contact frequency (Fig. 7B). Interestingly, these weakened TADs regain their strength at 48 hpt (an increase in fold change), highlighting the dynamic nature of TAD organization (Fig. 7B, Figure S18). The fold change among intra-TAD contacts among control cells is minimal when comparing across the time points (Fig. 7B), while there is an increase in contact frequency between 24 and 48 hpt in treated cells (Fig. 7B). Further, while intra-TAD contact frequency is reduced due to treatment, TAD boundaries are largely conserved at both timepoints (marginally different at 24 hpt) as we find similar insulation score profiles (Figure S19). Further, to investigate the influence of intra-TAD contact reduction on gene expression, we assessed the change in contact frequency around differentially regulated genes. There is a significant weakening irrespective of the direction of expression change (Fig. 7D, E). The (log2) fold change in frequency for the majority of differentially expressed genes is below zero (Fig. 7D). There are higher interactions at and around gene bodies suggesting that they are mostly found within TADs (shown as the observed/expected contact frequency heat map, Fig. 7E). The shades of violet indicate higher than expected contact frequency (Fig. 7E) in contrast to our observations of fewer than expected contacts outside TADs when TADs are aggregated genome wide (Figure S18, regions in shaded of orange). Curiously, the magnitude of contact weakening is stronger at downregulated genes (Fig. 7D) as they possess higher contact frequency in control conditions (Fig. 7E, top right panel). This suggests that either a subset of genes is more affected or is slower to recover from global TAD weakening that occurred after SAHA treatment.

Finally, we identified chromatin loops as regions of increased contact frequency between loop anchor loci in the Hi-C heatmap [45]. There are ~ 14,000 fine-scale loops per replicate, with only a small number statistically different due the HDACi treatment (Figure S20). This could be due to the heterogeneity across cells leading to weaker signals or due to the transient nature of functional chromatin loops. We combined replicates and identified several loops with considerable fold changes in interaction frequency (as exemplified in Figure S21 A) suggesting dynamic loop reorganization. To further distinguish functional loops, we made a subset of loops called ‘promoter loops’, where at least one of the loop anchor intervals overlaps with a transcription start site. Promoter loops and contacts are thought to play a major role in regulating gene expression [10, 48]. We assessed the promoters of differentially expressed genes: there is a weak, negligible correlation between promoter contact frequency and gene expression genome wide (Figure S21 B). This correlation weakens further when we assess promoters that interact with potentially active enhancers (Figure S21 C). Annotated enhancers for A549 cells [16] were deemed ‘active’ if they possess signals of accessibility including H3K27ac and H3K4me3 (Figure S22, described in methods section ‘Enhancer Annotation’). This suggests that at the genome-wide level, the minor loop contact frequency change at 24 hpt is not predictive of the differential gene expression. While differential chromatin loops associated with differential gene expression are not present, there is pronounced reduction of contacts at downregulated genes (Fig. 7D), indicating reorganization of previously transcriptionally active regions to less permissive states.

Discussion

In this study, we present a comprehensive genome-wide investigation of chromatin structure–function changes following a genome-wide epigenomic perturbation. In general, HDAC inhibitors, and more specifically SAHA, elicit altered gene regulation resulting in curbed tumor growth, allowing cells to survive and adapt [19]. Given the extensive characterization of SAHA’s impact on gene expression, [6, 19, 49, 55], we employed this system (SAHA in A549 cancer cells) to unravel the nature and persistence of locus-specific chromatin remodeling associated with genome function. Comparison of SAHA treated cultures with time-matched A549 controls allowed us to disentangle our observations without the influence of inherent chromosomal instability in the cancer cell line used for this study. Leveraging recent advancements in multi-omic experimental and computational techniques, we assessed how alterations in chromatin, particularly histone modifications, accessibility, and higher-order genome architecture, are associated with altered gene expression. Using this relevant model, we find that perturbation to global histone acetylation has persistent impacts on cellular function even when aberrant chromatin landscapes are abated. In response to disruption of acetylation homeostasis, the modification is largely decoupled from function, and finer control of other chromatin features supports the balance of gene expression. These findings highlight the power of interconnected yet independent multi-layered chromatin regulation in cellular resiliency.

As expected for an HDAC inhibitor treatment, histone acetylation levels are disrupted genome wide. After detailed investigation of the H3K27ac modification, the acetylation profile appears to broaden with less prominence at gene promoters and enhancers after SAHA treatment. In support of this observation, several key lysine acetyltransferase (HAT) enzyme genes were downregulated (Table S1), corroborating previous reports of the same phenomenon [19]. However, we observed increased histone acetylation across gene bodies, suggesting that reallocation of HAT enzymes already present may reduce acetylation at targeted regions. Thus, SAHA not only impacts the ability to remove acetylation—via direct inhibition of histone deacetylase enzymes—but also impacts the mechanism for active, targeted histone acetylation. This is likely a compensatory feedback mechanism to reduce the onslaught of aberrant hyperacetylation that occurs with HDAC inhibition. While we find that the strongest changes in acetylation are elicited 24 h post treatment, there is a rebound effect as cells attempt to recover. Concurrently, other chromatin features (H3K4me3, accessibility, 3D architecture) and gene expression also exhibit global deviations from control conditions that are more pronounced at 24 h after treatment than 48 h. Notably, the complete recovery to control levels of any chromatin feature does not occur by 48 h, suggesting some semi-permanent changes in response to treatment. However, it is possible that some of the surviving population of cells at 48 hpt did not interact with SAHA or are simply daughter cells where epigenetic modifications were not propagated. These genome wide observations are countered by our fine-scale investigations showing more restrained regulation in all features other than acetylation, with locus specific changes post treatment.

Although the field generally finds that histone acetylation is associated with open chromatin regions [3], our understanding of the relationship between modifications and associated nucleosome occupancy is still evolving [33]. In this study, increased acetylation genome-wide did not constitute a matched increase in chromatin accessibility, rather, accessibility on a genome-wide level was mostly unscathed by SAHA treatment. Thus, perturbation in the acetylation equilibrium is not sufficient to completely override the balance of chromatin accessibility dynamics. Furthermore, of the small number of OCRs impacted by SAHA, there are nearly equal numbers with increasing or decreasing accessibility, though regions with prominent changes were more likely to be open than closed. Previous studies also report minimal bidirectional changes in chromatin accessibility with increased [4] or decreased [22] global acetylation levels. While it seems that global acetylation changes do not directly correlate with global accessibility changes, the reduced expression of histone acetyltransferase enzymes or the relocation of said enzymes to gene bodies may be responsible for the reduced active acetylation of promoters, thereby affecting the accessibility of several loci. The majority of differentially accessible regions (71.5%) were associated with either genic regions or annotated enhancers highlighting that nucleosome remodeling largely occurred at functionally relevant genomic regions in response to treatment. These findings suggest that chromatin accessibility may be a pervasive feature that is more strongly connected to other features such as chromatin architecture and gene expression regulation than acetylation.

SAHA treatment induced bidirectional gene expression changes with more genes upregulated than downregulated after treatment. Interestingly, the majority of upregulated genes were inactive or lowly expressed under control conditions, while the majority of the downregulated genes were highly expressed under control conditions. Expression levels were eventually balanced after treatment, suggesting that residual cells undergo transcriptional reprogramming and potential decoupling from acetylation in response to aberrant epigenetic regulation. Transcriptional reprogramming is thought to involve redistribution of transcription factors and involvement of chromatin remodelers [51, 58]. While we cannot delineate the order of events, our results demonstrate that epigenetic and chromatin regulatory mechanisms are involved in the observed transcriptional changes (at the fine scale), and to a greater degree with downregulated genes. Specifically, in the majority of the downregulated genes, we observed reduced accessibility, H3K4me3, and H3K27ac, indicative of a shift toward non-permissive chromatin states. For upregulated genes, a small but significant increase in H3K4me3 at their promoters, without large increases in accessibility or acetylation, was sufficient to increase expression. Potentially, the chromatin landscape was permissive enough without additional modifications for transcription initiation. Recent studies have shown that H3K4me3 plays a role in transcriptional elongation and successful production of full-length transcripts [52, 53]. These findings further support the notion that gene expression can be independent of acetylation under certain conditions, such as stress responses.

The decoupling of chromatin architecture from epigenetic modifications and accessibility may underlie responses to HDACi treatment as well. The global chromatin architecture delineated from Hi-C assessments was the least impacted by SAHA treatment compared to other chromatin features. While a significant but small portion of the genome (~ 13%) exchanged compartments, only ~ 10% of differentially expressed genes and ~ 11% of OCRs were associated with compartment exchange. Thus, in this system, SAHA treatment induces a disconnect between transcriptional regulation, epigenetic modifications, chromatin accessibility and large-scale compartments during stress responses. While this assessment suggests weak associations at the global genome-wide level, our continuous wavelet transformation analysis demonstrates that concordance between chromatin architecture, gene upregulation, and gene density is specific to each chromosome, particular to genomic location, and dependent upon the scale of analysis. Indeed, differences in concordance were observed in different chromosomal arms and in chromosomes with more gene density; the concordance between modalities was also highly context dependent as well. Thus, discerning architectural changes at the genome-wide level may miss strong differential responses that occur on specific chromosomes, and even on specific regions of those chromosomes. To delineate the relationship between higher-ordered structure and genomic function, analysis on a per chromosome basis may be warranted. The frequency of inter-chromosomal interactions and positioning of the chromosome in nucleus (toward nuclear periphery vs internal) may impact chromosome-specific responses as well.

As we have previously demonstrated [50], fine-scale chromatin interactions play a prominent role in regulating cellular responses to external cues. Further, other studies have demonstrated that chromatin loops facilitate interactions among gene promoters and enhancers or silencers, thereby directly impacting gene expression [10, 48]. In our current study, we did not detect significant loop changes involving promoters concordant with the differential gene expression. There are several potential explanations for this observation. First, there may be a faster turnover of 3D chromatin contacts relative to gene expression. We may have not captured the entirety of chromatin reorganization associated with functional changes at 24 h post treatment, particularly given that the number of differential loops is even further reduced at 48 h post treatment. Despite the lack of significant differential loop interactions, there is a weakening of intra-TAD contacts, suggesting loss of structural integrity or mixing of otherwise insulated domains. We and others [50, 52, 53] have observed this phenomenon in response to various stressors, including viral infection. Curiously, in this study, we note that, concurrent with the rebound phenomena observed in other chromatin features, TADs also slowly regain strength by 48 h post treatment, suggesting a lack of persistence in changes to chromatin organization. Further, even though we do not detect differences in looping, the chromatin contacts are substantially weakened at downregulated genes after treatment; these genes were previously highly expressed. As such, reduced contacts may indicate a disruption of active transcription hubs—defined as the congregation of active promoters and enhancers in 3D proximity—at the sub-TAD level, thereby contributing to the weakening we found [59].

In all measured chromatin features (H3K27ac, H3K4me3, accessibility, and 3D contacts), the signal strength was lower at upregulated genes compared to downregulated genes. Further, the magnitude of change in the signal was larger at down regulated genes. This could be reflection of the magnitude of expression change since downregulated genes sharply reduced their transcription from a highly active state. Alternatively, there may be a difference in locus-specific chromatin dynamics, as suggested by the continuous wavelet analysis. When dynamic chromatin remodeling occurs rapidly, not all cells will present the same signals at the same time [28]. Thus, the investigation of dynamic (versus stable) features will have representation from fewer cells and therefore a weaker overall signal than a stable feature from the same collection of cells. The observed global TAD weakening could be a genome-wide response to global HDAC inhibition; however, regions with faster turnover of chromatin remodeling mechanisms may have adapted allowing for permissive gene transcription. Conversely, genomic regions with slower chromatin turnover possibly present a less permissive state. These findings are particularly of interest as perturbation in steady-state histone acetylation equilibria in either direction induces similar effects to those observed here [22]. The locus specific nature of these chromatin responses requires further investigation to identify the causality and whether this phenomenon is due to transcriptional activity or is a property of the locus. Given that stable chromatin features are more likely to occur in larger cell populations than dynamic features, single cell characterization of cellular heterogeneity would enable categorization of genomic regions based on their chromatin dynamics. A clear understanding of these mechanisms leading to region-specific outcomes will benefit the design of epigenetic therapeutics with minimized off-target effects.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates the effectiveness of multi-layered regulation of transcription: in resilient systems, disruption of one chromatin feature does not distort the regulation of other features in supporting a transcriptional program that allows for survival. Indeed, the traditional view that acetylation preferentially associates (or induces) open chromatin regions may not apply under these stress conditions. Further, the continuous wavelet analysis demonstrates that the impacts of SAHA on genome structure are highly dependent upon the chromosome, the location in the chromosome (p vs q arm), and the locus. This finding in particular suggests a needed focus on chromosome-specific changes for future multi-omics and multiscale chromatin dynamics analyses.

Methods

Cell culture

A549 cells, isolated from the lung tissue of a 58-year-old Caucasian male with lung cancer, were obtained from ATCC (Cat. #CCL-185). Cells were maintained in Complete Medium consisting of Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 4 mM L-Glutamine (ATCC, Cat. #30–2002) and 10% (v/v) Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS, ATCC, Cat. #30-2020). Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and 95% relative humidity. Multiple vials were cryopreserved at passage #2 in liquid nitrogen vapor until needed for cell culture experiments. For cryopreservation, cells were resuspended to a density of 106 cells per mL in ice-cold Freezing Medium consisting of DMEM, 20% (v/v) FBS, and 5% (v/v) dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO, ATCC, Cat. #4-X).

For cell culture experiments, A549 cells were propagated for at least two additional passages prior to seeding for HDACi drug treatments (described below). Cells were washed once in 1X PBS (Gibco, Cat. # 10010049), detached using 1X TrypLE Express Enzyme (Thermo Scientific, Cat. #12604021), resuspended into Complete Medium, and counted using the Scepter 3.0 Handheld Cell Counter and 60 µm Counter Sensors (Millipore, Cat. #PHCC360KIT). Cells were seeded for HDACi drug treatments at passage #7–8 into replicate tissue culture flasks (surface area = 175 cm2) in three biological replicates using 5 × 106 cells per flask. Seeded flasks were incubated for 72 h at 37 °C, yielding a confluent monolayer (~ 10 × 106 cells per flask) at the time of drug treatment.

HDACi drug treatments and cell harvest

Cell-seeding medium was removed after 72 h from replicate tissue culture flasks and replaced with Complete Medium containing 10 µM of the HDACi drug suberoylanilide hydroxamic acid (SAHA) or containing an equivalent volume of the drug vehicle only, DMSO (Pierce, Cat. #85190). Mock-treated (control) and drug-treated culture flasks were incubated in parallel for 24 or 48 h at 37 °C. During cell harvest (at 24 and 48 h post-treatment), drug-treated and mock-treated culture flasks for each biological replicate were processed in parallel for Hi-C, ChIP-seq, and RNA-seq analyses (described below). Cells were washed once in 1X PBS, detached using 1X TrypLE Express Enzyme for 20 min, then resuspended in Complete Medium to create a uniform single-cell suspension. Cell suspensions were held at room temperature and counted using the Scepter 3.0 Handheld Cell Counter and 60 µm Counter Sensors, then immediately divided as needed for downstream processing.

Chromatin conformation and epigenomics assays and library preparation

Hi-C: For Hi-C analysis, 1 × 106 cells per aliquot were processed in triplicate 24 and 48 h after treatment as previously described [50]. Briefly, cells were crosslinked with 37% (w/v) formaldehyde containing 10–15% (v/v) methanol (Sigma, Cat. #252549), treated with 200 mM glycine (Sigma, Cat. #G8790) and snap frozen for storage. As previously described, frozen crosslinked cells were processed into Hi-C libraries per the Arima Hi-C Kit manual (Cat. #A160134), the Arima-Hi-C Library Preparation user guide (Cat. # A160141), and the Accel-NGS® 2S Plus DNA Library Kit (IDT, Cat. #10009877). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 (PE 2 × 150 bp) at the LANL Genome Center.

ChIP-seq: For ChIP-seq, 10 × 106 cells per aliquot were processed in duplicate 24 and 48 h after treatment. Counted cells were resuspended in 1X PBS and crosslinked for 5 min using Pierce™ 16% (w/v) methanol-free formaldehyde (ThermoScientific, Cat. #28906) at a final concentration of 1% (v/v). Formaldehyde was quenched with 125 mM glycine. Crosslinked cells were pelleted and washed twice in ice-cold 1X PBS (Gibco, Cat. #10010049). Chromatin was isolated and fragmented using the Diagenode Chromatin EasyShear Kit Ultra Low SDS (Cat. #C01020010) following the manufacturer’s protocol and Diagenode Bioruptor Pico (Cat. #B01080010) (30 s on and 30 s off settings for 17 cycles) to generate ~ 350 bp sized fragments. Fragmented chromatin was stored at − 80 °C until use. Frozen chromatin was thawed on ice, and the volume was brought to 500 µl by adding IP dilution buffer (16.7 mM Tris–HCl, 0.01% SDS, 1.1% Triton X 100, 1.2 mM EDTA, 167 mM NaCl) supplemented with 1X Halt™ Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Thermo Scientific, Cat. #78430). Fragmented chromatin was precleared with 15 µl Pierce Protein A/G beads (Thermo Scientific, Cat. #88803) at 4 °C for 1 h with rotation; 50 µl of the precleared chromatin was saved as input and stored at − 80 °C until use. The following antibodies were used for ChIP experiments: H3ac (pan-acetyl) (Active Motif, Cat. #39139), H3K4me3 (Active Motif, Cat. #39060), and H3K27ac (Abcam, Cat. #ab4729). ChIP antibodies (4–5 µg) were prebound to 30 µl Protein A/G beads in 500 µl RIPA buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, 0.1% SDS, 1% Triton X 100, 1 mM EDTA, 140 mM NaCl, 0.1% Na deoxycholate) incubated for 2–4 h at 4 °C with rotation. Conjugated beads were pulled down and washed 3X with RIPA buffer. Antibody-conjugated beads were added to precleared fragmented chromatin and incubated o/n at 4 °C with rotation. Antibody-bound precleared fragmented chromatin was incubated with 30 µl Protein A/G beads for 2 h at 4 °C with rotation. Complexes were pulled down, unbound lysate was removed, and beads were washed as follows for 10 min each at 4 °C with rotation: RIPA; 2X RIPA 500 (RIPA buffer with 500 mM NaCl); LiCl buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, 250 mM LiCl, 0.1% SDS, 1% NP40, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% Na deoxycholate); and TE buffer (10 mM Tris pH 8, 1 mM EDTA). Chromatin was incubated in elution buffer (1% SDS, 0.1 M NaHCO3) at 65 °C for 10 min, incubated at RT for 10 min, and incubated o/n at 65 °C. The input DNA was thawed and its volume brought to 300 µL with elution buffer. Both chromatin and input samples were treated with 40 µg/ml RNaseA (Qiagen, Cat. #19101) at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by protein digestion with 80 µg/ml Proteinase K (ThermoScientific, Cat. #25530049). Samples were incubated with NaCl (100 mM final concentration) for 1 h at 65 °C in an Eppendorf thermomixer at 300 rpm. The DNA was purified using the Qiagen PCR cleanup kit (Qiagen, Cat. #28104), quantified via Qubit, and concentrated with Ampure beads (Beckman, Cat. #A63881) to remove any PCR inhibitors and meet library prep volume requirements. Libraries were generated via the Tecan Ovation V2 Ultra Low Kit with unique dual indexes (Tecan, Cat. #9149-A01) and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 (PE 2 × 150 bp) at the LANL Genome Center.

RNA-seq: For RNA-seq, 2 × 105 cells were cryopreserved in Freezing Medium into duplicate 1 mL aliquots 24 and 48 h after treatment. RNA was extracted using the RNeasy UCP Micro Kit protocol kit (Qiagen, Cat. #73934) as previously described [50]. Aliquots were thawed in treatment pairs (mock-treated and SAHA-treated) in a 37 °C water-bath, transferred to ice-cold Complete Medium, then centrifuged at 1000 rcf for 2 min. Cells were washed in 10 mL ice-cold 1X HBSS (Gibco, Cat. #14025092) and lysed in 350 µL ice-cold RULT Buffer containing 1% (v/v) b-mercaptoethanol (Sigma, Cat. #63689). RNA extractions, performed immediately after cell lysis, and included an on-column DNase I treatment step for 15 min to deplete DNA. RNA was eluted in 20 µL RNase-free water and total RNA was quantified with the Qubit RNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, Cat. #Q32855). RNA quality was measured using the Bioanalyzer RNA 6000 Assay (Agilent, Cat. #5067-1511). Ribosomal RNA was depleted from 500 ng total RNA using the Ribo-Zero Plus rRNA Depletion Kit (Illumina, Cat. #20037135). Sequencing libraries were generated using the Illumina NextSeq 500 High Output Kit v2.5 (Cat. #20024908) and eluted in DNA Elution Buffer (Zymo Research, Cat. #D3004-4-10). Library concentration and average size was verified using the TapeStation DNA 5000 Assay Reagents and Screen Tapes (Agilent, Cat. #5067-5589 and #5067-5588). Libraries were normalized and pooled based on the TapeStation concentrations, then pooled libraries were quantified using the Illumina/Universal Library Quantification Kit (KAPA Biosystems, Cat. #KK4824). Libraries were sequenced on an entire lane of NextSeq 500 HO flow cell to generate paired-end 151 bp reads (300 cycles).

ATAC-seq: In a separate experiment, A549 cells were cultured, seeded into flasks, and treated with 10 µM SAHA, and harvested exactly as described above. Approximately 2 × 105 cells were cryopreserved in triplicate 1 mL aliquots using ice-cold Freezing Medium 24 and 48 h after treatment as previously described [44]. Aliquots were subsequently thawed into Complete Medium, counted to achieve ~ 80,000 cells per sample, and processed for ATAC-seq analysis using the Active Motif ATAC-seq Kit manual (Cat. #53150) as previously described [44]. Briefly, cells were lysed and tagmented DNA was prepared and purified,DNA libraries were prepared via PCR using primers provided in the kit. Libraries were purified and additional size-selected using AmpureXP beads (Beckman, Cat. #A63880 per the manufacturer's protocol to retain fragments between 150 and 600 bp. Libraries were quantified using a high-sensitivity Qubit dsDNA Quantification Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, Cat. #Q32851 and a KAPA Library Quantification Kit (Roche, Cat. #KR0405. Libraries were sequenced via the Illumina NextSeq 2000 sequencer (LANL Genome Center in paired-end mode (PE151 using P3 chemistry.

Histone extraction and quantification

Cell lysis and subsequent histone extractions were performed using the Abcam commercial Histone Extraction Kit (Abcam, Cat. #ab113476) per the manufacturer’s instructions. At 24 and 48 h post treatment, 5 × 106 cells per culture (SAHA-treated and mock-treated) were lysed in 500 µL ice-cold 1X ‘Pre-Lysis Buffer’ and immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for long-term storage. Total histone proteins were purified from whole cell lysates and quantified using a Qubit Protein Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Cat. #Q33211) and the Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer. The Fluorometer was calibrated prior to each assay using the Qubit Protein Standards (provided in kit). Qubit Protein Assay results were corroborated using Bradford 1X Dye Reagent (BioRad, Cat. #5000205) and a dilution series Calf Thymus Histone H3 Standard (Roche, Cat. #11034758001) in distilled water. Samples and standards were incubated with 1X Dye (1:1) for 5 min, then absorbance was read immediately using a spectrophotometer at 595 nm. A commercial H3K27 Acetylation ELISA Kit (Active Motif, Cat. #53116) was used to quantify histone H3 acetylation induced by SAHA treatment on the extracted histones. Total input protein for each ELISA was normalized across all histone extracts to achieve 2 µg histone protein per well. Histone H3 protein standard (provided in kit) was used in a dilution series was used per plate; all conditions were assayed in triplicate. Absorbance was read using a spectrophotometer at 450 nm within 5 min of adding the Stop Solution.

Microscopy

Approximately one million cells from each A549 culture harvested 24 and 48 h post treatment were seeded into 6-well tissue culture plates with Complete Medium. Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and 95% relative humidity for 24 h. Cells were washed once in 1X HBSS, crosslinked with 4% (w/v) formaldehyde solution in PBS (Invitrogen, Cat. #FB002) for 15 min, and then washed twice with 1X HBSS. For nuclear staining, cells were incubated with DAPI Solution (Invitrogen, Cat. #62248) at 1 µg/ml in PBS for 10 min and washed once in 1X HBSS. Transcriptomics analysis

We generated 12 RNA-seq libraries and sequenced to approximately 17 million read pairs per library. Raw read quality control was performed using FastQC [2] and SeqMonk [1] to verify library quality and degree of PCR duplication,all metrics are within the expected range. The human T2T reference genome and annotations were retrieved from NCBI. Annotations were converted to gtf format using the AGAT ([23] package function (agat_convert_sp_gff2gtf.pl. A splice junction aware reference index was generated for the combined genome and annotation files using STAR [12] with the arguments (–runMode genomeGenerate –sjdbOverhang 149). Pre-alignment read trimming was not performed in favor of soft-clipping/quality filtering by the STAR aligner. Reads were aligned to the combined genome using STAR. Duplicate reads were marked but retained in downstream analysis per DESeq2 guidelines. Sense strand reads aligning to gene features were counted using htseq-count [40] with the arguments (–stranded = reverse). Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 [32] matrix design (~ time + treatment). For visualization, replicate alignment files were merged using samtools. bigWigs were then generated using the bamCoverage function from the deepTools [42] package with the arguments (–effectiveGenomeSize 3117292070 –normalizeUsing BPM –filterRNAstrand {forward/reverse/NULL} –binSize 1).

ATAC-seq analysis

ATAC-seq libraries were sequenced as described above and analysis was performed as previously described [44, 50]. Twelve ATAC-seq libraries were sequenced to a median depth of 57 million read pairs per library. Briefly, raw reads were trimmed and filtered to remove Nextra adaptors and reads with repetitive sequences using Fastp [8]. Bases with low quality scores (q < 15) were removed. Processed reads were aligned to the telomere-to-telomere (T2T) human reference genome, version 2 [38] via BWA [30]. Duplicate sequenced pairs and mitochondrial reads were marked and removed from analysis via Samblaster [14]. Samples were further filtered using samtools with the following flags: -F 4 -F 256 -F 512 -F 1024 -F 2048 -q 30. Samples were then passed to MACS2 in BAMPE mode. Loci displaying significant enrichment of paired-end reads were identified as peaks using MACS2 [17]. Replicate correlation was assessed using deepTools [42] -plotCorrelation function on SPMR normalized MACS2 output big wig files. Open chromatin regions (OCRs) were defined by MACS2-called peaks followed the BEDtools [41] merge function. OCR differential accessibility analysis was performed using the DESeq2 [32] matrix design (~ time + treatment).

ChIP-seq analysis

Raw read processing, mapping, and read filtration post mapping were performed for all ChIP and input reads as described for ATAC-seq data. Each ChIP-seq library was sequenced to approximately 23 million read pairs. For all ChIP experiments, high quality reads were analyzed using MACS2 to identify peaks with read enrichment. In contrast to ATAC-seq peak calling, corresponding treatment and time matched input control was used with each of the ChIP-seq data.

Chromatin contact map generation

Hi-C libraries were sequenced as described above and analysis was performed as previously described [50]. Eight Hi-C libraries were sequenced to have at least 600 million (150 bp) paired reads per library. Briefly, paired Hi-C reads were analyzed via Juicer Hi-C pipeline [13] and aligned to the T2T reference genome [38]. Restriction sites were identified, and Hi-C (.hic) files were generated using Juicer default settings with a mapping quality threshold of ‘q = 30’. We detected at least 700 million valid Hi-C contacts per condition. Replicate Hi-C maps were merged via Juicer (“mega” function).

Replicate correlation analysis

For ChIP-seq, ATAC-seq, and RNA-seq datasets, replicate correlation was assessed using deepTools [42] -plotCorrelation function. SPMR normalized MACS2 output bigwig files were used as input for ChIP-seq and ATAC-seq datasets. For RNA-seq datasets, BPM normalized bigwig files generated by deepTools bamCompare function (parameters described above) were used. For Hi-C datasets, chromosome wise estimation of spectral statistic [56] was utilized to measure reproducibility between Hi-C replicates. For each data set, all the following analyses were performed on separated replicates unless otherwise mentioned, however, given high replicate concordance (Figures S2-S5, biological replicates were merged for visualization.

Principal component analysis

For ChIP-seq, ATAC-seq, and RNA-seq datasets, principal component analysis was performed using the variance stabilization transformation package followed by plotPCA function from DESeq2. Raw reads from all individual samples across consensus peaks (i.e., merged intervals of MACS called peaks) per feature was used an input for ChIP-seq and ATAC-seq datasets. For RNA-seq datasets, read count per gene across all samples was used as input. The PCA for Hi-C datasets was conducted in Python using the FAN-C package at 50 kb resolution using –strategy fold change.

Differential peak calling and gene expression

For ATAC-seq, H3K27ac, and H3K4me3 ChIP-seq datasets, differential peaks were called using DEseq2 with ~ time + treatment matrix design. Similarly, differentially expressed genes were also identified using DESeq2. Raw transcript counts normalized by DESeq2 with the mean of ratios approach obtained from DESeq2 result table are plotted as absolute expression in Fig. 3D, E.

Global and local profiling of histone modifications and chromatin accessibility

To determine the global levels of feature profile distribution in control vs treated samples (Fig. 2), the deepTools –computeMatrix function with –binsize 10 kb was used. Input-normalized read coverage (log2 fold change) around midpoints of corresponding consensus peaks (i.e., merged intervals of MACS called peaks) were calculated for H3K27ac and H3K4me3 ChIP datasets. Samtools merge and index functions were used to merge replicate bam files. The deepTools bamcompare function was used for input normalization. For ATAC-seq datasets, RPKM-normalized read coverage around OCR midpoints in 10 bp sliding windows were calculated. RPKM normalized read coverage in 100 bp sliding windows were computed using bamCoverage function from deeptools. For visualization, plotHeatmap and plotProfile functions from deepTools without kmeans clustering were used. For statistical comparison of treated versus control signal profiles (shown in Figure S6), input normalized ChIP-seq signal and RPKM normalized ATAC-seq signal profiles were computed for each biological replicate separately. A custom R script was then used to compute replicate mean and standard deviation per 10 bp sliding windows around consensus peaks for control and treated conditions separately. A two-tailed Student t-test was employed in a window-wise manner to compute statistical significance (p-value) in treated versus control signal differences. The obtained p-value across the peak center ± 3 kb region is plotted separately.

To plot the feature profile across all (Figure S9), up, and downregulated genes (Fig. 4) ± 3 kb region, input normalized replicate-wise ChIP-seq signal track obtained as mentioned above was used. Whereas SPMR normalized replicate-wise signal track obtained as MACS2 output was used for ATAC-seq. Signal value from each individual replicate is plotted and a trend curve (solid black line for treated and dotted black line for control) was plotted by employing smoothing with method:loess and smoothing span 0.01). Statistical significance in signal value difference between treated and control conditions is computed in a window-wise manner by employing two-tailed Student t-test as mentioned above. P-value distribution is plotted as a scatterplot and overlaid with a smooth curve using method ‘gam’ to display the trend.

UCSC track visualizations

For UCSC track visualizations shown in Figs. 2B, D, F and 4G, input normalized, replicate-wise signal tracks are shown for all ChIP-seq data sets. Negative values are not displayed. SPMR normalized signal tracks are shown for ATAC-seq data sets and TPM normalized strand-specific signal tracks are shown for RNA-seq datasets. Smoothing over windows of 3 is applied for ChIP-seq and RNA-seq datasets. No smoothing is employed for ATAC-seq data. Replicates are overlaid by stacking. CAT/Liftoff gene tracks for T2T CHM13 assembly from UCSC browser are displayed as gene models.

Gene enrichment analysis

For gene enrichment analysis, significant differentially expressed genes (with p-value < 0.05 and log2fold change > 2 or < − 2) identified from RNA-seq analysis (described in Methods section ‘Transcriptome analysis’) were used. These gene lists were input to PANTHER [34] with options (Background = Homo sapiens and Statistic = Binomial) for identifying enriched gene ontology terms.

Distance decay analysis of chromatin contacts

Decay frequencies in contacts across genomic distances was performed using the FAN-C package [27] in Python. Decay profiles for each chromosome at 10 kb resolution were calculated and combined for visualization and analysis.

Loop identification

HiCCUPs [13] within Juicer was used to identify chromatin loops at 5, 10, and 25 kb resolution across replicated and merged Hi-C maps. Hi-C contact counts were gathered for each replicate across the merged list of loops. From these counts, differential loops were determined via DESeq2 [32], where comparisons among treatment (mock-treated and HDACi treatment) and time (24 and 48 h) were performed with time-matched controls per condition.

Compartment analysis

Compartment assignment scores at 100 kb resolution were estimated via Juicer (eigenvalue function). These scores combined with ATAC-seq profiles were used to label active (A) and inactive (B) compartments using the convention that the active A compartment has more ATAC-seq peaks. Compartment scores were reoriented (i.e., multiplying scores per chromosome by negative one) if the 95th percentile of counts of ATAC-seq peaks were labeled as compartment B in a majority of ATAC-seq libraries. To identify compartment exchange due to SAHA treatment, we selected 250 kb bins assigned to the same compartments in both Hi-C biological replicates. The replicate average compartment score was determined for each bin only considering bins in agreement between replicates. The changes from positive (mock-treated) to negative (HDACi treated) bin score value was assigned as A to B exchange; similarly, changes from negative (mock-treated) to positive (HDACi treated) bin score value were assigned as B to A exchange. Bins that remained positive or negative were assigned as ‘no exchange’ A or B compartments, respectively.

Enhancer annotation

Cell type (A549) specific enhancer annotation was obtained from EnhancerAtlas 2.0 (http://www.enhanceratlas.org/). UCSC LiftOver tool was then used to convert enhancer coordinates in hg19 to T2T assembly. Potentially active enhancers were annotated by employing deepTools computeMatrix to compute control and treated chromatin accessibility, H3K27ac, and H3K4me3 input normalized signal across all scaled enhancers ± 1 kb region. Further, enhancer clustering was performed on the resultant matrix with -kmeans 5. The enhancers that clustered together due to signals of higher accessibility, H3K27ac, and H3K4me3 in either control or treated samples were annotated as putatively active enhancers in the samples under study. A total of 12,598 out of 49,781 annotated enhancers were deemed active.

Data preparation for continuous wavelet transformation

Continuous wavelet transformation was performed for gene density, B-to-A compartment switching, and gene upregulation in response to HDACi treatment. The B-to-A compartment switching signal is a binary indicator of whether B-to-A switching (a change from closed to open chromatin compartments) occurred in any given non-overlapping 100 kb genomic bin. Gene density within each 100 kb bin was computed by counting the number of genes, consisting of pseudogenes, non-coding RNAs, and protein-coding genes as reported in the T2T reference genome. The mean log2 fold change values of all significantly upregulated genes (FDR < 0.05) within each 100 kb bin were taken. For both gene density and log2 fold change, values for genes that overlap the boundary between two bins are counted in both bins.

TAD strength analysis

In replicate merged Hi-C maps, Topologically Associated Domains (TADs) were annotated using the arrowhead function from the Juicer tools [13]. Knight-Ruiz balanced [26] Hi-C maps at 10 kb were used as input and arrowhead function was evoked by adding the “-r 10,000”, “-k KR” and “-m 5000” parameters. TAD strength analysis was performed on a combined list of unique TADs identified from control and treated cells per timepoint. Aggregate contact frequency (log2 observed/control, at 10 kb resolution) within these combined scaled TADs ± same-sized region was plotted separately for control and treated cells using the fanc aggregate function from the FAN-C package [27]. Further, -tad strength and -tads flags were included while calling fanc aggregate function to quantify strength of each TAD in replicate merged control and treated samples separately. To find the contact strength at differentially expressed genes, fanc aggregate function was employed on a bed file of all significant (adjusted p-value < 0.05) upregulated (log2 fold change > 0) and downregulated (log2 fold change < 0) genes with -tad strength and -tads flags. HiC map file at 10 kb resolution was used as input.

TAD boundary insulation score analysis

TAD insulation scores across replicate merged control and treated HiC maps were computed using the fanc insulation function from the FAN-C package. HiC map files at 5 kb resolution were used as input and insulation score was computed in 500 kb sliding windows across the genome. Based on this insulation score, we then annotated intervals with insulation score minima as TAD boundaries using FAN-C boundaries function. Timepoint-specific control and treated TAD boundary coordinates were then combined and the insulation score profiles at and around (± 500 kb) the midpoints of TAD boundaries were plotted using the deepTools [42] plotProfile function.
