Layer-by-layer assembly of bioactive nanofilms for in situ gene transfection of a LAMA3 mutant on hydrogenated TiO₂ nanotubes: in vitro evaluation of epithelial adhesion and hemidesmosome formation
Authors: Caiyun Wang, Ran Lu, Xu Cao, Yanting Mu, Su Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReliable peri-implant epithelial sealing is paramount for the prevention of peri-implantitis. Remarkably, both hemidesmosomes (HDs) and the internal basal lamina, which notably includes laminin332—essential for transmucosal epithelial sealing—are significantly diminished compared to their natural tooth counterparts. In situ gene therapy has emerged as a promising approach for the spatial regulation of HD formation.MethodsIn this study, we aim to develop a bioactive coating with an exceptional capacity for the in situ transfection of a recombinant adenovirus carrying a mutant of the laminin α3 chain gene (Ad-mLAMA3), thereby improving transmucosal epithelial sealing. Dopamine (DA), graphene oxide (GO), and type Ⅳ collagen (COL-Ⅳ) multilayers—(DA/GO/COL-Ⅳ)10, designated DGC—were assembled layer by layer on hydrogenated TiO2 nanotubes. The resulting construct was termed H‑DGC. We characterized H-DGC and evaluated its effect on HD formation in vitro following integration with Ad-mLAMA3.ResultsThe DGC nanofilms were successfully established on hydrogenated TiO2 nanotubes, enhancing in situ transfection efficiency. The integration of H-DGC with Ad-mLAMA3 promoted epithelial cell adhesion and mature HD formation by upregulating the expression of laminin α3 and integrin β4.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that H-DGC can be engineered as a bio-instructive and in situ interface, significantly enhancing epithelial cell adhesion and mature HD formation, and offering a strategy to prevent peri-implantitis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09250-1.Keywords: Gene delivery, Titanium implants, Soft tissue sealing, Peri-implantitis, In situ transfection, Nanofilms

Background

Titanium and its alloys have been widely utilized as biomedical materials for dental implants to treat dentition defects. Despite their success, the occurrence of peri-implant mucositis is alarmingly high, affecting up to 43% of patients, while peri-implantitis affects 22%, as highlighted in a systematic review [1]. These conditions contribute to substantial healthcare costs and significant patient distress [2, 3]. Moreover, a complete protocol for managing peri-implantitis remains elusive [4, 5]. Recent insights have underscored the importance of peri-implant soft-tissue attachment in enhancing implant health. Consequently, engineering bioactive titanium surfaces for the transmucosal section through surface property modification and bioactive coating application is critical for improving transmucosal soft tissue sealing and preventing peri-implantitis [6, 7].

Transmucosal soft tissue sealing involves the junctional epithelium, which adheres to transmucosal biomaterials via cell-matrix adhesive structures known as hemidesmosomes (HDs) and the internal basal lamina (IBL) [8, 9]. However, the peri-implant epithelium exhibits compromised functional sealing, characterized by a significant reduction in HDs and a discontinuous IBL compared to natural teeth [10, 11]. Enhancing HDs and IBL formation at the epithelium-titanium interface represents a viable strategy for augmenting soft tissue sealing to thwart peri-implantitis. Laminin332, an α3β3γ2 heterotrimer, plays a pivotal role in the epithelial IBL, interacting with specific transmembrane receptors, such as integrin α6β4 and integrin α3β1, to influence cell migration, adhesion, and HD formation [12, 13].

Prior studies have shown that immobilizing laminin332 or functional laminin-derived peptides on titanium surfaces fosters HD assembly and transmucosal epithelial sealing [14, 15]. Fischer et al. [16] explored a multifunctional implant surface synergistically modified with an antimicrobial peptide, GL13K, and a laminin-derived peptide, LamLG3, demonstrating that these engineered surfaces not only exhibit anti-biofilm properties but also enhance HD formation and keratinocyte mechanical adhesion. Liu et al. [17] developed chimeric peptides that reinforced epithelial sealing around titanium implants in an animal model. Panayotov et al. [18] used bioengineered peptides to improve the dental epithelial junction on implants, demonstrating enhanced hemidesmosome formation and basement membrane assembly in vitro and suggesting the potential for in vivo application.

The application of recombinant proteins or peptides in clinical therapeutics is currently constrained by factors such as cost, immunogenicity, and limited bioavailability. Gene therapy has emerged as a pivotal method for producing therapeutic target proteins. It has been identified that the expression of the α3 chain is a limiting factor for the assembly of the laminin332 heterotrimer [19, 20]. Furthermore, studies have shown that cell behaviors such as adhesion, migration, and HD assembly are primarily influenced by the C-terminal globular domain (G domain) of the laminin α3 chain [21, 22]. Notably, the formation of mature HDs and stable cell adhesion depends on the proteolytic truncation of the G4 and G5 subdomains in laminin α3 [23, 24], whereas the presence of the G4–G5 domain favors integrin α3β1-mediated cell migration rather than stable adhesion [21]. Direct overexpression of the mutant LAMA3 bypasses the rate-limiting step of endogenous proteolytic cleavage, ensuring immediate production of the functionally active protein and enhancing the predictability and efficiency of in situ gene delivery. Consequently, modifying titanium surfaces with a mutant LAMA3, specifically lacking the G4–G5 subdomains (mLAMA3), is promising for enhancing HD formation and transmucosal epithelial sealing. Achieving this goal necessitates precise spatial control over the in-situ loading, release, and delivery of genes at the epithelium-titanium interface.

The constrained utilization of gene therapeutics in oral implantology is largely attributed to the formidable task of seamlessly integrating effective in situ gene delivery mechanisms with biomaterials. Consequently, there exists an urgent need for the development of innovative substrate-mediated gene delivery systems that not only are effective at immobilizing genes but also possess superior biocompatibility. These systems hold the potential to significantly advance the field by overcoming current limitations and enhancing the therapeutic outcomes of dental implant procedures.

Yang et al. [25] explored the impact of LAMC2, the gene encoding the laminin γ2 chain, on cell adhesion and HD formation. They developed a chitosan/(hyaluronic acid/liposome-DNA) multilayer coating for titanium surfaces to deliver LAMC2, enhancing both transfection efficiency and HD-related gene expression in cells. Additionally, the release of plasmid DNA (pDNA) encoding a segment of the C-terminal globular domain of the laminin α3 chain (LAMA3) improved epithelial cell adhesion and biological sealing at transmucosal sites in vivo [26]. This chitosan/collagen/pDNA coating was applied to TiO2 nanotubes (TNTs) using cathodic electrophoretic deposition. However, a notable limitation of these studies lies in the exposure of the therapeutic gene to the modification process, which poses a potential threat to the vitality and efficacy of the gene. Zhang et al. [27] constructed a multilayer coating via layer-by-layer (LBL) assembly to immobilize the recombinant adenoviral vector LAMA3 (Ad-LAMA3), demonstrating that the Ad-LAMA3 coating enhanced epithelial cell adhesion and facilitated re-epithelialization of peri-implant soft tissue in an animal model. The multilayer coating did not directly improve the transfection efficiency, but utilized antibody-antigen-specific binding for Ad-LAMA3 loading. Despite some in vitro studies demonstrating the role of laminin332 and hemidesmosomes in epithelial adhesion, there remains a significant gap in translating these findings into clinical applications.

In this study, we aim to develop a bioactive coating with an exceptional capacity for in situ gene transfection to improve transmucosal epithelial sealing. Hydrogenated TNTs were employed as the foundational substrate for the subsequent application of bioactive coatings due to their nanostructures and abundant reactive hydroxyl groups (-OH) [28]. To enhance gene immobilization, dopamine (DA) and graphene oxide (GO) were strategically used as versatile adhesive agents because of their exceptional bioconjugation capabilities [29, 30]. Additionally, type Ⅳ collagen (COL-IV), a biomimetic natural extracellular matrix (ECM) component, was incorporated to provide a more bioactive microenvironment conducive to cellular interactions [31]. In this study, the LBL assembly technique was utilized to create versatile multilayer nanofilms on hydrogenated TNTs comprising DA, GO, and COL-IV. Subsequently, recombinant adenovirus vectors carrying the mutant LAMA3, devoid of the G4–G5 subdomains (Ad-mLAMA3), were immobilized on these nanofilms. The biological responses, including transfection efficiency, cell adhesion, and HD formation, of epithelial cells cultured on these modified surfaces were thoroughly evaluated.

Methods

Preparation of hydrogenated TNTs

Machined pure titanium sheets (dimensions 10 × 10 × 0.2 mm and 40 × 40 × 0.2 mm; purity 99.99%; sourced from Cuibolin Nonferrous Metal Industry Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) underwent a rigorous cleaning process using an ultrasonic method with acetone (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), absolute ethyl alcohol (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.), and deionized water, each for 5 min. These sheets served as the control group and were denoted as T. After cleaning, the titanium sheets were subjected to anodic oxidation and hydrogenation as previously described [28]. Briefly, T sheets were oxidized at 50 V for 15 min within an ethylene glycol electrolyte that comprised 0.5 wt% ammonium fluoride (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.) and 10 vol% deionized water. This step was followed by annealing at 500 ℃ for 2 h. The annealed samples were then placed in quartz tubes under a hydrogen pressure of 0.95 × 105 Pa and thermally treated at 500 ℃ for 4 h to produce hydrogenated TNTs, referred to as the H group.

Preparation of (DA/GO/COL-Ⅳ)10 nanofilms (DGC) on hydrogenated TNTs

Multilayer DGC nanofilms were fabricated using an LBL self-assembly technique on the H group sheets based on previous works [32, 33]. DA hydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich, Merck, Germany) was dissolved in 10 mM Tris buffer to prepare a DA/Tris solution (2.0 mg/mL, pH 8.5). COL-Ⅳ (Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in acetic acid buffer (pH 4.5) to prepare a 50 µg/mL COL-Ⅳ solution. The H sheets were initially immersed in a DA/Tris solution at room temperature for 5 min. Following this immersion, the sheets were washed thrice with deionized water and then dipped into a 0.2 mg/mL GO suspension (XFNANO, Nanjing, China; lateral size 50–200 nm), followed by another cleaning cycle. Next, the sheets were coated with COL-IV by submerging them in a 50 µg/mL COL-Ⅳ/acetic acid buffer solution (pH 4.5) for 5 min. The sheets were washed three times with deionized water to remove unbound COL-IV. (DA/GO/COL-Ⅳ)10 nanofilms on hydrogenated TNTs, denoted as H-DGC, were obtained by repeating this process ten times. All samples intended for in vitro experiments were sterilized under ultraviolet light for 20 min on both sides. The preparation process of the bio-instructive interface on hydrogenated TiO2 nanotubes is shown in Fig. S1.

Characterization of samples

The surfaces of the samples were characterized using various techniques. Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM; S4800, Hitachi, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was employed for surface observation, applying low vacuum conditions and an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. An atomic force microscope (AFM; Dimension ICON, Bruker, Germany) was utilized to analyze surface roughness. The scan range was 10 μm × 10 μm, and the scan rate was 0.8 Hz. Topographical analysis was performed using NanoScope analysis software v1.8 on the basis of the scan data. Water contact angles (CAs) were measured using a video-based optical system (OCA20; DataPhysics Instruments, Esslingen, Germany). 2 µL deionized water was dropped on the surface of each sample, and the CA value was measured after 5 s. Additionally, a nanoscratch test (TI 980, Bruker) was conducted under standard laboratory conditions (room temperature of 25 ℃ and a relative humidity of 40%) to assess the bonding strength of the samples, with the scratch length of 250 μm and the scratch velocity of 8.33 μm/s at 0-100 mN. A diamond conical indenter with a radius of 5 μm and an angle of 60° at the apex was used. Confocal Raman microscopy (LabRAM HR evolution, Horiba Scientific, Japan) was performed to determine Raman spectra within the 1000 to 3000 cm⁻¹ range. Surface elemental compositions were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; ESCALAB Xi+, Thermo Scientific, USA) equipped with an Al Kα source (1486.68 eV). The peak fitting of high-resolution C1s and N1s spectra was carried out using XPS Peak (version 4.1) software.

Construction of the recombinant adenoviral vectors

In this study, gene transfection was performed using adenoviral vectors that carried the genes of specific interest. The adenoviral vectors used in this study are replication-deficient (E1/E3 deleted), minimizing the risk of viral replication and off-target effects. These vectors were subsequently introduced into cells to transfect the gene, enabling the expression of the desired target protein. Recombinant adenoviruses overexpressing mLAMA3 (Ad-mLAMA3) and adenovirus-mediated mCherry (Ad-mCherry) were constructed by HANBIO (Shanghai, China). The reference sequence of LAMA3 (NM_000227.6) was retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene). A transcript variant lacking the terminal G4 and G5 subdomains of the C-terminal G-domain was used in this study. The size of this LAMA3 variant was 4098 bp. The designed sequences of primers (Forward: 5’-CTGTGACCGGCGCCTACTCTGGTACCGCCACCATGCCTCCAGCAGT-3’ and Reverse: 5’-TATCTTATCTAGAAGCTTAGGCTCGAGTTATTTGTCGTCATCATCCT-3’) were employed for the amplification of the identified variant using polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The titers of Ad-mLAMA3 and Ad-mCherry were 1.58 × 1010 and 3.16 × 1010 plaque-forming units per milliliter (PFU/ml), respectively.

Assessment of laminin α3 overexpression in Ad-mLAMA3 transfected cells

Human gingival epithelial cells (HGECs; hTERT TIGKs, CRL-3397; ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were cultured in complete DermaLife™ Basal Medium (LM-0004; Lifeline Cell Technology, USA) supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 ℃ in a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

To assess laminin α3 overexpression of Ad-mLAMA3 transfected cells, HGECs were seeded in six-well plates (5 × 105 cells/well) and cultured for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were transfected with Ad-mLAMA3 or Ad-mCherry at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100, which was selected based on preliminary optimization experiments (Fig. S2), while a complete medium was added to the blank control. After 16 h of culturing, the medium in all groups was replaced with a normal complete medium. The cells were then cultured for 48 h before being collected for Western blot analysis.

Western blot analysis was used to assess the laminin α3 overexpression ability of Ad‑mLAMA3 transfected cells. Protein samples were obtained using a cell lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Beijing, China) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime Biotechnology). Twenty-five micrograms of protein were separated on 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gels (Biosharp, Hefei, China) and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (PVDF; Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA). After blocking with 5% (w/v) skimmed milk for 2 h at room temperature, the primary antibodies, anti-laminin α3 rabbit antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and anti-glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase mouse antibody (GAPDH; Jackson ImmunoResearch Inc., West Grove, PA, USA), were incubated with the PVDF membranes overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with the corresponding secondary immunoglobulin G-horseradish peroxidase antibodies (IgG-HRP; Jackson ImmunoResearch Inc., West Grove, PA, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, the membranes were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA), and the gray values of each band were analyzed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). The Western blot experiment was performed with three independent biological replicates. Data analysis was conducted blinded to group allocation.

Transfection efficiency

To determine the transfection efficiency of Ad-mCherry in cells, H-DGC samples were coated with Ad-mCherry (H-DGC-AdmCherry) at varying titers of 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, or 2.0 × 108 PFU/ml in 24-well plates and incubated at 37 ℃ for 4 h. Consistent with suggestions in the literature [34], vector loading was conducted under sterile conditions in a biosafety cabinet to preserve vector bioactivity, thereby eliminating the need for terminal sterilization of the vector-loaded surface. Following three washes with PBS, HGECs were seeded onto the H-DGC-AdmCherry samples at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and transfected for three days. Subsequently, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with 4-6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Beyotime Biotechnology) for 5 min. When cells were successfully transfected with Ad-mCherry vectors and expressed the mCherry fluorescent protein, they became observable under a fluorescence microscope (BX51; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The number of mCherry-positive cells in three randomly selected fluorescent images from three samples in each group was counted using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health). The percentage of mCherry-positive cells was used to quantify transfection efficiency.

To assess the transfection efficiency of Ad-mCherry on different surfaces, T, H, and H-DGC samples were incubated with Ad-mCherry at a titer of 1.0 × 108 PFU/ml. Subsequently, HGECs were seeded onto these surfaces, and transfection efficiency was evaluated following this procedure.

Response of HGECs to samples

Cell adhesion

The surfaces of H-DGC samples were coated with Ad-mCherry and Ad-mLAMA3 (1.0 × 108 PFU/ml) at 37 ℃ for 4 h and named as H-DGC-AdmCherry and H-DGC-AdmLAMA3, respectively.

HGECs were cultured on T, H, H-DGC, H-DGC-AdmCherry surfaces, and H-DGC- AdmLAMA3 at a density of 3 × 105cells/well in 24-well plates. After incubation for 0.5, 2, and 4 h, cell adhesion was assessed using 3-(4,5-Dimethyl-2-thiazolyl)-2,5-diphenyl-2 H-tetrazolium bromide (MTT; Beyotime Biotechnology), following the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, the medium in each well was replaced with 300 µl complete medium containing 10% MTT solution (5 mg/ml) at each time point. After incubating at 37 °C for 4 h, MTT was reduced to formazan pigment by viable cells. Then, 350 µl dimethyl sulfoxide was added to dissolve formazan pigment and transferred to 96-well plates (100 µl/well). Optical density was determined using a microplate spectrophotometer (SpectraMax Paradigm, Molecular Devices, CA, USA) at a wavelength of 570 nm.

Cell proliferation

For the cell proliferation assay, HGECs were cultured on the surfaces of T, H, H-DGC, H-DGC-AdmCherry, and H-DGC-AdmLAMA3 at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well for 1, 3, or 5 days. At each time point, cell proliferation was assessed using the MTT assay (Beyotime Biotechnology) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunofluorescence

To evaluate the expression of HD-related proteins at the cell-material interfaces, specific immunostaining for integrin β4 and laminin α3 was conducted. HGECs were seeded onto plates of T, H, H-DGC, H-DGC-AdmCherry, and H-DGC-AdmLAMA3 at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well and incubated for three days. Following three washes with PBS, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Subsequently, the cells were blocked with 10% goat serum (Beyotime Biotechnology) for 30 min and incubated with primary rabbit antibodies targeting integrin β4 (1:250 dilution; Abcam) or laminin α3 (1:100 dilution; Abcam) overnight at 4°C. After washing, the samples were incubated with secondary anti-rabbit DyLight 488-conjugated IgG (1:400 dilution; Abcam) in the dark for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (Beyotime Biotechnology) for 5 min. Fluorescent images were captured using a fluorescence microscope and supporting camera (BX51; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM)

TEM assessment was conducted to observe the ultrastructural localization of HDs in cells. HGECs were seeded onto the surfaces of all groups at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well in six-well plates. After culturing for three days, the cells on the samples were scraped off with cell scrapers and collected in centrifuge tubes. The samples were then centrifuged and washed twice with cold PBS.

Next, the cells were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde overnight at 4 °C. Following fixation, the HGECs were post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 h at room temperature and washed thrice with PBS. Subsequently, the cells were stained with 2% uranyl acetate for 1 h and gradually dehydrated in a graded series of ethanol solutions. The samples were then infiltrated and embedded in Spurr’s resin. Ultrathin sections were obtained and stained with uranyl acetate and alkaline lead citrate for TEM analysis (JEM-1400; Japan Electron Optics Laboratory, Japan).

Quantitative Real-time PCR (RT-PCR)

HGECs were seeded on T, H, H-DGC, H-DGC-AdmCherry, and H-DGC-AdmLAMA3 at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well in six-well plates. After three days of incubation, total RNA was extracted from HGECs using the TRIzol Reagent kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), followed by reverse transcription into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent kit (TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan). The expression levels of HDs-related and adhesion-related genes, specifically LAMA3, integrin α6 (ITGA6), integrin β4 (ITGB4), integrin α3 (ITGA3), integrin β1 (ITGB1), and plectin (PLEC), were assessed using a RT-PCR reagent (CWBio, Beijing, China). All Ct values were normalized to those of GAPDH, serving as an internal control. The relative expression of genes was determined using the 2(−ΔΔCt) method. The RT-PCR experiment was performed with three independent biological replicates. Technical replicates were performed in triplicate for each biological replicate. Data analysis was conducted blinded to group allocation. Primer sequences (Shenggong, Shanghai, China) for the target genes are provided in Table 1.

Western blot analysis

HGECs were seeded on T, H, H-DGC, H-DGC-AdmCherry, and H-DGC-AdmLAMA3 at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well in six-well plates. After three days of incubation, protein samples were obtained and Western blot analysis was carried out following the procedure described in Sect. 2.5. The Western blot experiment was performed with four independent biological replicates. Data analysis was conducted blinded to group allocation.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent biological replicates. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 19; SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. Differences in multiple comparisons were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with subsequent least significance difference (LSD) post hoc test or the Kruskal-Wallis test. Results were deemed statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Characterization of samples

The surface morphologies of T, H, and H-DGC were examined using FE-SEM. In Fig. 1A, the surfaces of machined pure titanium sheets (T) appeared relatively smooth, whereas homogeneously distributed titanium dioxide nanotube arrays were evident on the surfaces of the H group. Following coating with 10 layers of DGC nanofilm, the nanotubes on the H-DGC surface were uniformly covered. Folds and ruffles (indicated by red arrows) were observed on the DGC nanofilms due to irregular self-assembled GO slices. Additionally, the DGC nanofilms exhibited three-dimensional fibrous networks with nanostructures such as nanofibrils and flocculent nanograsses (highlighted by green arrows) in the inset.

Two-dimensional (2D) topographical images of the samples analyzed by AFM are presented in Fig. 1B, and 3D images and corresponding z-profiles of the samples are presented in Fig. S3. Nanotubes on the H surface and the DGC coatings on the H-DGC surface were observed. Surface profile roughness (Ra, the arithmetic average of the profile height from the mean line) and three-dimensional surface area roughness (Sa, the arithmetic mean height of a surface) are shown in Fig. 1C. The Ra value of T was 7.0 ± 0.6 nm. The Ra value of the H surfaces (Ra = 86.6 ± 0.9 nm) was significantly higher than that of T (p < 0.05), whereas the DGC nanofilms on the H surfaces led to a decrease in Ra (46.1 ± 2.4 nm, p < 0.05 compared to the Ra value of the H group). In addition to the Ra value, the Sa of the T, H and H-DGC samples were measured, which were found to be 11.1 ± 1.2 nm, 84.1 ± 2.2 nm and 46.5 ± 1.3 nm, respectively. Notably, the similarity observed between the Ra and Sa values within each group suggested a relatively homogeneous surface topography across all samples.

Fig. 1D displays the average water contact angles (CA) of the different surfaces, which differed significantly (p < 0.05). The CA of the T group was 86.1 ± 2.1º. The hydrogenated TNTs (H group) featured superhydrophilic surfaces with a CA of 4.5 ± 0.5º, while DGC nanofilms increased the CA to 46.6 ± 1.9º.

Microtribological properties of the H and H-DGC are depicted in Fig. 2A. The interface fracture of the samples was indicated by the transition point of the nanoscratch curve from smooth to large fluctuations, where the lateral force represented the critical load. The critical load in the H group was approximately 43 mN, whereas that in the H-DGC group was approximately 57 mN.

B Raman spectra of T, H, and H-DGC. C XPS survey spectra of T, H, and H-DGC. D High-resolution XPS spectra of N1s for H-DGC. High-resolution XPS spectra of C1s for (E) H and (F) H-DGC

The Raman spectra of the samples are illustrated in Fig. 2B. Characteristic peaks of GO, including the D peak around 1,350 cm− 1 and the G peak at approximately 1,580 cm− 1, were detected in the H-DGC group, indicating the deposition of GO on the nanofilms by the LBL process. The D: G peak intensity ratio (Iᴅ/Iɢ) was determined to be 0.93, indicating moderate disorder arising from defects and oxygen-containing functional groups. These groups serve as crosslinking points to enhance binding with other components, thereby contributing to multilayer stability.

XPS was conducted to further analyze the elemental composition of the surfaces (Fig. 2C). Ti2p signals appeared on the T and H surfaces but disappeared completely in the H-DGC group due to coverage by the DGC nanofilms. The main elements on the H-DGC surface were C, O, and N, suggesting the presence of DA, GO, and COL-Ⅳ. As displayed in Fig. 2D, the high-resolution N1s spectrum of H-DGC resolved into three peaks at about 399.4, 400.3, and 401.8 eV assigned to aromatic N, C-NH, and protonated N (C-NH3+), respectively. The nitrogen content observed on the H-DGC surface can be attributed to various sources. The aromatic N peak was likely derived from the 5,6-dihydroxyindole (DHI) during PDA formation, as well as the product of the Schiff base reaction between COL-Ⅳ and DA under alkaline conditions [35]. Additionally, the possible formation of C-NH3+ could be explained by the spontaneous proton transfer to the amines [36, 37] present in COL-Ⅳ molecules and PDA. The high-resolution C1s spectrum of the H sample (Fig. 2E) was fitted with three peaks, assigned to C–C/C = C/CHx (284.8 eV), C-O (286.6 eV), and O-C = O (289.0 eV), indicating the presence of residual surface contaminants. Upon DGC coating, the intensities of the C-O and O-C = O peaks increased, and two new typical peaks emerged in the C1s spectrum (Fig. 2F), corresponding to C-N (285.5 eV) and C = O (287.9 eV). The C-N bonds were present in both PDA and COL-Ⅳ, while the C = O peak was attributed to carboxyl and epoxy functional groups on the GO surface, as well as the benzoquinonyl of DHI [38].

This finding suggests that Ad-mLAMA3 was effectively constructed as anticipated. Transfection efficiency

To optimize gene transfection efficiency in HGECs, H-DGC samples were coated with varying titers of Ad-mCherry. As depicted in Fig. 3C, mCherry expression was observed in all groups, indicating successful transfection of HGECs with Ad-mCherry on the H-DGC surface. Quantitative analysis revealed that transfection efficiency was approximately 90% at titers of 1.0 × 108 and 2.0 × 108 PFU/ml (Fig. 3D), significantly higher than at titers of 0.1 × 108 and 0.5 × 108 PFU/ml. To minimize virus consumption and enhance biological safety, 1.0 × 108 PFU/ml was selected as the optimal titer for subsequent experiments.

The transfection efficiency of HGECs on Ad-mCherry-coated T, H, and H-DGC is presented in Fig. 3E. Transfection efficiency of Ad-mCherry-coated H-DGC was markedly higher than that of the other groups (p < 0.05), indicating that H-DGC serves as an excellent platform for gene vector loading and in situ transfection.

Discussion

The current investigation aimed to evaluate the physicochemical properties of multilayer DGC nanofilms on hydrogenated TNTs and the biological impact of Ad-mLAMA3 immobilized on this modified surface in regulating mature HD formation to enhance transmucosal epithelial sealing. Our findings demonstrate that H-DGC nanofilms were effectively engineered to load Ad-mLAMA3, resulting in high transfection efficiency and subsequent upregulation of mature HD formation.

Recent studies have explored various nanotubular and novel coatings to improve peri‑implant soft tissue sealing. Liu et al. [39] reported a Pt nanorod‑embedded TiO₂ nanotube platform via atomic layer deposition, which combined mild photothermal antibacterial activity with enhanced fibroblast adhesion, migration, and expression of focal adhesion‑related genes. Deng et al. [40] demonstrated that highly ordered TiO₂ nanotube arrays fabricated by anodization enhanced human gingival fibroblast adhesion, spreading, and migration, with upregulation of integrin β1/β4/α6 and fibronectin expression. Xing et al. [41] developed vertical graphene coatings on titanium, which exhibited inherent antibacterial activity and promoted soft tissue sealing. Zhou et al. [42] constructed a controlled CO‑releasing coating that achieved switchable antibacterial and immunomodulatory functions, enhancing soft tissue integration by inducing M2 macrophage polarization and promoting fibroblast‑macrophage crosstalk. Compared to these approaches, our DGC‑AdmLAMA3 platform offers a distinct advantage: in situ gene transfection enables sustained endogenous production of the functional mLAMA3 protein, which directly promotes hemidesmosome formation and stable epithelial adhesion, rather than relying solely on surface‑bound peptides, ions, or transient drug release.

In our study, the superhydrophilic behavior of hydrogenated TiO₂ nanotubes (CA ≈ 4.5°) is attributed to the synergistic effects of surface nanostructuring and increased hydroxyl (-OH) content after hydrogenation treatment. While hollow nanotubes can potentially trap air, leading to a Cassie–Baxter hydrophobic state, the high surface energy and abundant hydrophilic -OH groups on hydrogenated TNTs promote complete wetting (Wenzel state), overcoming air entrapment [43]. This superhydrophilic property facilitates protein adsorption, as previously reported [28]. Furthermore, hydrogenated TNTs exhibit promising bioactivity in modulating cell adhesion, macrophage polarization, and osteogenic differentiation [44–46]. However, soft tissue integration around hydrogenated TNTs remained limited in vivo [47]. To concurrently achieve more versatile and bioactive titanium surfaces with high affinity, binding, and biological agents, including DA, GO, and COL-Ⅳ (multilayer DGC nanofilms) were introduced to the hydrogenated TNTs.

Our characterization outcomes revealed the successful construction of DGC nanofilms on the H surfaces. The DGC multilayers exhibited fibrous networks with nanostructures such as nanofibrils and flocculent nanograsses, likely attributed to the presence of COL-Ⅳ and polydopamine (PDA) formed in the LBL process. Previous studies have shown that biomimetic DA molecules can self-polymerize to form PDA with more reactive catechol and amino groups when the pH exceeds 8.5. These reactive groups allow them to bridge tightly to other agents [48, 49]. We hypothesized that the PDA molecules penetrated the GO and COL-Ⅳ layers as bridges [50] to stabilize the multilayer nanofilms. This hypothesis is supported by several lines of evidence: (1) XPS analysis revealed aromatic N peaks (from 5,6-dihydroxyindole, DHI) and C-N bonds characteristic of PDA formation (Fig. 2D–F); (2) the presence of C = O peaks corresponding to benzoquinonyl groups indicates oxidative crosslinking [38]; (3) the Schiff base reaction between PDA and COL-Ⅳ under alkaline conditions likely contributes to interlayer adhesion [35]; and (4) the increased critical load in nanoscratch tests (57 mN for H-DGC vs. 43 mN for H) suggests enhanced mechanical stability due to PDA-mediated crosslinking. Direct validation using techniques such as QCM-D or FTIR will be pursued in future studies.

In addition, GO is essential for the successful construction and performance of the DGC multilayer films. GO’s high specific surface area and numerous functional groups (hydroxyl, epoxy, carboxyl groups, etc.) provide binding sites [30, 51, 52] for PDA and COL-IV immobilization. Additionally, the nanoscratch experiment displayed the micromechanical stability of the DGC nanofilms (Fig. 2A), which might be due to the high mechanical strength of GO [53]. While nanoscratch testing under standard laboratory conditions in our study provides a reproducible measure of intrinsic coating-substrate adhesion, the mechanical behavior of hydrated polymer-based coatings may differ due to water uptake and swelling [54]. Nevertheless, our complementary stability assessment in PBS (14-day sustained release) indirectly supports the wet-state stability of the DGC nanofilms [55]. Thus, this study applied GO to further load COL-Ⅳ, and we speculated that the high specific surface area and biochemical and bioconjugation abilities of GO could be another critical factor for the stability of the multilayer DGC nanofilms.

In our study, multilayer DGC nanofilms exhibited enhanced bioactivity in HGECs. When DA self-polymerizes to PDA during the modification process, H-DGC may acquire mussel-derived adhesive properties [48], facilitating epithelial cell adhesion. Additionally, GO can modulate the extracellular matrix (ECM)–integrin clustering signaling pathway, thereby enhancing cell adhesion [56, 57]. Moreover, as a crucial component of basement membranes (BMs), COL-Ⅳ can integrate with laminins to provide anchorages for epithelial cells [58, 59]. Park et al. [60] investigated the effect of different ECM components on the epithelial cell-titanium interaction and verified that COL-Ⅳ provided the most excellent foundation for cell attachment compared with other ECM components. Other studies also confirmed that COL-Ⅳ could promote cell adhesion, colonization, and growth [61–63]. The three-dimensional fibrous networks of the DGC nanofilms may serve as the supporting matrix for IBL and HDs. Consequently, our findings indicated that H-DGC increased adhesive cell numbers during the early stages and promoted the expression of integrin β4 and laminin α3, which could facilitate HDs and IBL formation.

Furthermore, the multilayer DGC nanofilms were engineered to immobilize Ad-mLAMA3 for specific in situ gene transfection. Dopamine and graphene have been widely utilized for gene and drug delivery. For instance, a previous study used PDA to stabilize the miRNA delivery system in glaucoma therapy [64]. Qin et al. [65] took advantage of the secondary reactive ability of PDA to coat poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) microspheres encapsulated BMP-2 gene on the polyetheretherketone, achieving improved bioactivity and osteogenic ability. Jaleel et al. [66] developed a GO-based polymer core as a therapeutic pDNA vector to improve transfection efficiency. Despite the superhydrophilicity of the H group (CA ≈ 4.5°), which typically promotes protein adsorption, the transfection efficiency on H-DGC was significantly higher (Fig. 3E). This can be attributed to the DGC nanofilms providing abundant functional groups (catechol, amine, carboxyl) for stable adenoviral vector immobilization, the high specific surface area of graphene oxide enhancing vector loading, and the 3D fibrous network protecting viral vectors from denaturation while facilitating controlled presentation to cells. Thus, surface chemistry and topography, rather than wettability alone, dictate vector retention and transfection efficiency. Consequently, H-DGC nanofilms could lower gene doses and enhance biosecurity by improving in situ gene loading and transfection efficiency. We acknowledge that direct characterization and delivery kinetics of vector-loaded surfaces would further strengthen the study, and this represents an important direction for future investigation to evaluate the stability and bioactivity of vector-loaded surfaces.

Our study utilized a mutant form of laminin α3 lacking the G4-G5 subdomains (mLAMA3) as a specific therapeutic gene targeting epithelial cells at the epithelium-titanium interface. Our results demonstrated that mLAMA3 did not affect the overexpression of the laminin α3 chain, as shown in Fig. 3A and B. We observed that H-DGC-AdmLAMA3 significantly increased the gene and protein expressions of laminin α3 and integrin β4 compared to other groups. Furthermore, the expression of plectin, an intramembrane protein related to HDs [67, 68], was upregulated in the H-DGC-AdmLAMA3 group. We acknowledge that knockdown or inhibition experiments would provide stronger causal evidence for the role of laminin α3 in promoting hemidesmosome formation. Suppression of laminin α3 expression would be expected to reduce integrin β4 clustering, impair hemidesmosome assembly, and decrease epithelial cell adhesion, as previously demonstrated in laminin α3‑null keratinocytes [23, 69]. Future studies employing siRNA or CRISPR‑Cas9‑mediated LAMA3 knockdown in HGECs are warranted to further validate the functional role of mLAMA3 overexpression in promoting hemidesmosome formation.

Previous studies have reported that the intact laminin α3 chain, comprising G1-G5 repeats, tends to bind to integrin α3β1, promoting ECM-controlled cell migration [70, 71]. Conversely, proteolytic processing of laminin α3, releasing the G4 and G5 domains, results in an integrin α6β4-favored laminin332 ligand. This favors HD nucleation, stable integration into the basement membrane, and downregulation of cell motility [24, 71, 72]. The Ad-mLAMA3 used in our study likely increased the expression of the mutant laminin332, lacking G4 and G5 domains of laminin α3, which integrated with clustered integrin α6β4, as demonstrated in Fig. 5A. Subsequently, the cytoplasmic domain of integrin β4 could bind to the cytoskeletal cross-linker plectin, facilitating the formation of mature HDs, as shown in Fig. 5C.

Several studies have successfully enhanced peri-implant epithelial sealing by directly immobilizing laminin332-derived peptides or full-length laminin332 on titanium surfaces [17, 18]. While these conventional peptide- or protein-based strategies are effective, they present certain limitations that our in situ gene transfection approach aims to address. First, protein coatings provide a finite supply and passive delivery of bioactive molecules, whereas gene transfection enables sustained endogenous production of therapeutic proteins. Second, our platform enables spatially controlled transfection specifically of cells contacting the implant surface, minimizing off-target effects. Third, by delivering the pre-processed mutant mLAMA3, we bypass the rate-limiting proteolytic cleavage required for wild-type laminin332 activation, ensuring immediate production of the functionally active form. Collectively, these advantages position in situ gene transfection as a complementary and potentially superior strategy for achieving durable epithelial sealing.

We hypothesized that epithelial cells anchored firmly to the surface of H-DGC-AdmLAMA3 with increased mature HDs and organization of the IBL-like structure might inhibit cell migration and enhance epithelial sealing. However, further studies are needed to evaluate the colocalization of plectin, laminin α3, and integrin β4 to confirm the structure of HDs. Moreover, we observed that cell proliferation was enhanced only transiently on H-DGC-AdmLAMA3 surfaces, while adhesion and hemidesmosome formation were significantly improved. Sustained epithelial proliferation has been associated with apical downgrowth of the junctional epithelium, compromising soft tissue sealing [9]. Our findings suggest that H-DGC-AdmLAMA3 promotes a differentiated, adhesion-competent phenotype, consistent with reports linking hemidesmosome formation to cell cycle arrest and differentiation [10, 73]. Long-term in vivo studies are required to confirm sealing durability.

We also observed that the gene expression of integrin α3 and integrin β1 in the H-DGC-AdmLAMA3 and H-DGC groups was increased compared to that in the T group (p < 0.05), although to a lesser extent than integrin β4. Integrin α3β1 has been reported to regulate both cell adhesion and migration. Furthermore, integrin α3β1-regulated cell migration is inhibited when integrin α6β4 is stimulated, complementing integrin’s ability for stable cell anchorage via HDs. Additionally, integrin α3β1 may control BM assembly and organization [74], and keratinocytes lacking α3 integrin could not deposit or remodel a suitable laminin332 matrix [75]. Interestingly, the clustering of integrin α3β1 induced by laminin332 could upregulate laminin α3 proteolysis and decrease cell migration, suggesting a feedback mechanism [76]. We speculate that the increased expression of integrin α3 and integrin β1 in the H-DGC-AdmLAMA3 and H-DGC groups might suppress cell migration and promote the deposition of laminin332, thereby facilitating the formation of mature HDs and IBL.

While our study demonstrates the bioactivity of mLAMA3 in promoting hemidesmosome formation, direct comparisons with wild-type LAMA3 and clinically established surfaces such as sandblasted, large-grit, acid-etched (SLA) or anodized titanium implants were not performed. Such comparisons will be essential in future studies to benchmark the relative performance of this gene-modified coating. Moreover, given the potential detrimental impact of epithelial cell apical migration on connective tissue attachment, future investigations will involve constructing a co-culture of epithelial cells and fibroblasts to assess the effect of H-DGC-AdmLAMA3 on soft tissue sealing. Despite their replication-deficient design (E1/E3 deleted), localized application, and immobilization on the implant surface, the adenoviral vectors used in this study may still elicit host immune responses in vivo. Future studies should evaluate immunogenicity, biosafety, and biodistribution prior to clinical translation. In addition, the durability of epithelial sealing, long-term stability of the DGC nanofilms, and sustained transgene expression remain to be determined in long-term in vivo studies. Additionally, the mechanism underlying the assembly and secretion process of the mutant LAMA3-introduced recombinant laminin332 heterotrimer, deficient in the G4–G5 subdomains, and its subsequent promotion of HDs formation remains unclear.

Conclusions

In this study, DGC nanofilms were fabricated successfully on hydrogenated TNTs. The H-DGC surface exhibited favorable physicochemical properties, including nanostructured topography, moderate hydrophilicity, and mechanical stability. When combined with Ad-mLAMA3, this modified bio-instructive system can significantly upregulate the expression of laminin α3, plectin, and integrins in epithelial cells, thus directly modulating cell adhesion and mature HD formation in vitro. These results demonstrated that Ad-mLAMA3 loaded on H-DGC may improve epithelial–implant interactions. This work establishes a gene-activated coating platform that combines nanofilm technology with localized adenoviral transfection, offering a novel strategy for enhancing soft tissue integration in dental implants and broader applications in implantable medical devices. Future studies should include co-culture systems incorporating fibroblasts to simulate transmucosal tissue architecture, bacterial challenge assays to assess infection resistance, and in vivo evaluation in peri-implantitis models. Additionally, long-term coating stability, transgene expression durability, functional barrier assessments, and safety assessments must be addressed.
