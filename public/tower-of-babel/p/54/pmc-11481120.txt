Cryptosporidiosis in individuals with inflammatory bowel disease: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Belinda Liu, Alexander Schnider, Megan DeArmond, David B Banach, Brad A Haubrich
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCryptosporidiosis is a leading cause of moderate-to-severe diarrhoea globally, and, while it is often self-limited, in immunocompromised individuals, the infection can be associated with significant morbidity and mortality. Diagnosis might be missed or delayed in patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) due to similar presentation, and these patients may also be on immunosuppressive therapies, increasing their risk of infection. Additionally, gastrointestinal infection and dysbiosis may be a risk factor for IBD. Diagnosis, presentation and treatment of cryptosporidiosis in individuals with IBD, as well as any epidemiologic correlations between the two diseases, will be investigated.Methods and analysisMEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, Dissertations and Theses Global and grey literature will be searched. Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews was used for the protocol and will be for the review. Two reviewers will independently screen studies and extract data. The evidence and presentation of the results will be analysed with input from the review team. Studies of cryptosporidiosis in patients with IBD will be included. Paediatric, adolescent and adult studies in all patient environments will be included. Cases in which Crohn’s disease does not affect the intestine and cases in which cryptosporidial infection is not in the intestine will be excluded.Ethics and disseminationPublished clinical literature will be systematically reviewed, and this work does not directly involve patients. Consequently, ethical review by an institutional review board is not required. Data will be presented at academic conferences, and a culminating report will be published in a peer-reviewed journal.Open Science Framework registrationhttps://osf.io/j47mb.

Introduction

Cryptosporidiosis is an infection of the gastrointestinal tract caused by protozoan Cryptosporidium spp, with the majority of human cases caused by C. hominis and C. parvum. As an obligate parasite, Cryptosporidium is transmitted to humans through the ingestion of contaminated food and water, and through the fecal–oral pathway.13 Cryptosporidium is an intracellular parasite and a member of the eukaryotic phylum Apicomplexa; however, it lacks more enzymes for biochemical building blocks than other apicomplexans, so it relies more heavily on host nutrients.46 Infection by C. hominis is anthroponotic, while infection by C. parvum and others is generally zoonotic; though C. parvum has anthroponotic subtypes, with genomic evidence of recent introgression leading to anthroponosis.7 The life cycle of Cryptosporidium is complex. Infection can occur with ingestion of as few as 10 oocysts, and incubation time in the host spans about 1–2 weeks. Microbial development, with both asexual and sexual phases, results in invasion of neighbouring epithelial cells and in release of thick-walled oocysts, thus perpetuating the infectious cycle.2 4 8

Cryptosporidiosis is found globally, but it is more common in developing countries, which report the highest number of cases. Cryptosporidium is a common aetiological agent of both moderate-to-severe and less-severe diarrhoea in children, second to rotavirus.9 10 There are very few studies exploring the prevalence of cryptosporidiosis in travellers to developing nations. Many developing countries have no surveillance system for Cryptosporidium cases, making it challenging to monitor outbreaks and cases numbers.1 It is estimated that Cryptosporidium can be attributed to 20% and 9% of diarrhoea cases in children in developing and developed countries, respectively.4 Risk factors for cryptosporidiosis and related mortality are comprised of poor hygiene and sanitation,12 4 912 dehydration13 and immunodeficiency. Increased risk of infection and morbidity is well documented for patients with HIV/AIDS,3 14 including systematic reviews,1517 and cryptosporidiosis in patients with cancer has recently been reviewed.18 Immunodeficiency following solid organ transplantation (SOT) is also associated with increased risk.19 20

The small intestine is typically the site of infection by C. hominis and C. parvum,1 2 while C. muris has been shown to infect gastric mucosa instead of intestinal.21 Cryptosporidial pulmonary cases have also been reported.2 17 22 Clinical presentation can include diarrhoea, acute gastroenteritis, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting and fever.1 4 For many immunocompetent individuals, infection may be clinically undetectable, and symptomatic cases are usually self-limited with a short period of intestinal irritation and distress.3 In those who are immunocompromised, infection is much more severe and can be life-threatening due to dehydration and malabsorption.2414 23 Cryptosporidiosis may contribute to the carcinogenesis and tumour development.4 18

Diagnosis of cryptosporidiosis is most commonly performed by detection of oocysts by brightfield or fluorescent microscopy; however, this method requires instrumentation and technicians, and low sensitivity requires several days of sampling and testing. Immunoassays and PCR are also used, with varying sensitivities and specificities for Cryptosporidium sp. Recent advances in lateral flow techniques have allowed for point-of-care testing.2 4 24

Currently, there are no vaccines for cryptosporidiosis. The antiprotozoal prodrug nitazoxanide,25 available in tablets and oral suspension, is the only medication for cryptosporidial infection approved by the US Food and Drug Administration, the European Medicines Agency and Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency.1 26 Nitazoxanide shows limited efficacy in children under 1 year of age and in patients experiencing immunodeficiency, but several combination therapies have been reported to be successful, including with fluoroquinolone in patients with SOT and with azithromycin or spiramycin and paromomycin in patients with AIDS or SOT. Symptoms can be managed by fluid and electrolyte replacement and antimotility agents.26

One of the most commonly identified gastrointestinal diseases is inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), which includes ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD). IBD is characterised by inflammation in the gastrointestinal tract: UC is primarily isolated in the bowel, and CD can cause inflammation anywhere in the gastrointestinal tract. IBD has an immune-mediated aetiology, complex with inheritable and environmental risk, though its pathogenesis is not fully understood.2729 There is a higher genetic risk for CD than UC; nevertheless, familial aggregation occurs in both.2832 Over 240 susceptibility genes have been noted, including some associated with autophagy, detection of microbes, regulation of epithelial barrier function and regulation of innate and adaptive immune responses.29 32 33 Of these, about 70% of these are shared with other complex autoimmune diseases.28 Comorbidity of other immune-mediated diseases is associated with severity of IBD.27 Environmental factors for IBD consist of smoking, diet, stress and medication use (particularly antibiotics).283437 These factors can influence intestinal microbiota, and dysbiosis is positively correlated with IBD.128 29 34 35 3841 These factors are associated with Westernization, and IBD incidence is particularly increasing in newly industrialised nations.35 42 Similarly, history of digestive infections disrupting intestinal microbiome may affect immune regulation and IBD progression.28 38

Adolescents and young adults (18–35 years) are the age groups most frequently diagnosed with IBD, but IBD also occurs in paediatric cases and in older adults.35 IBD manifests with persistent and recurrent diarrhoea, often with blood or mucus. Sometimes this is accompanied by abdominal pain, and physical examination may reveal tenderness, distension or rigidity. Perianal skin changes, fissures or abscesses occur in 20%–80% of cases. Several extraintestinal presentations are documented. Diagnosis includes ruling out other diseases, abdominal and rectal examination, endoscopy with biopsy, and serum and stool biochemical tests.43

There is no cure for IBD at this time, but there are many treatment options for patients aimed at improving quality of life and slowing progression of the disease. For mild-to-moderate IBD, aminosalicylates, which reduce signalling at the epithelial barrier, are preferred. Corticosteroids can be used to reduce inflammation, and, for more severe cases, biologic and small molecule immunomodulators are used.43 Patients with additional immune-mediated disease may require more of this treatment.27

Both cryptosporidiosis and IBD affect the gastrointestinal tract and cause symptoms of diarrhoea and abdominal pain, which may suggest why cryptosporidiosis infection can be misdiagnosed to be an IBD flare.44 A few studies have suggested that cryptosporidiosis can exacerbate IBD symptoms4547 and have suggested oocyst testing in cases of sudden magnification of IBD symptoms.46 47 In addition, increased incidence of IBD has been noted after the 2010 waterborne cryptosporidiosis outbreak in Sweden.48 49

Recent systematic reviews on risk factors and epidemiology of cryptosporidiosis11 and on cryptosporidiosis in patients with HIV infection15 16 and cancer18 underscore the importance of this disease. Further, the growing global burden of IBD has prompted recent systematic reviews on epidemiology and risk factors.39 42 The roles of several bacterial infections on the pathogenesis of IBD have also been recently reviewed.40 41 However, to our knowledge, despite the high potential for exacerbated symptoms and missed diagnosis of cryptosporidiosis, the known relationship of microbiota with IBD progression, and the immunosuppressive state of many patients treated with IBD, there has not been a structured approach to reviewing the relationship between cryptosporidiosis and IBD. A scoping review is the most appropriate method because there is a very little literature exploring these comorbidities.

The objective of this scoping review is to begin to delineate relationships that may exist between cryptosporidiosis and IBD by assessing the extent of the related literature. We hope to fill the knowledge gap related to the comorbidity or sequential morbidity, including risk factors and epidemiology, clinical presentation, diagnostics, treatment and outcomes.

Study

This protocol was registered with Open Science Framework on 16 January 2024 (https://osf.io/j47mb). This study follows JBI manual and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses extension for scoping review (PRISMA-ScR) guidance. Full search and screening will commence in or about October 2024 and conclude by October 2025.

Inclusion criteria

Participants

This review will consider reports of patients with IBD. Both UC and CD cases will be included, with the latter limited to CD of the lower digestive tract and excluding cases exclusively affecting an individual’s oesophagus and/or stomach. Paediatric, adolescent and adult clinical cases will all be considered. Reports in which diagnosis of IBD was made before, during or after cryptosporidial infection will be included. Patients treated with immunosuppressive therapy and patients not on immunosuppressive therapy will both be included, but will be denoted in data extraction.

Concept

This scoping review will look at individuals who have cryptosporidiosis and IBD. Infections that are exclusively extragastrointestinal will be excluded. Infections by Cryptosporidium spp, including C. hominis, C. parvum, C. muris and others, will be included and delineated in the data extraction and analysis. The diagnosis, presentation and treatment of cryptosporidiosis will be investigated. Presence or absence of bacterial enteric pathogens will be included, but will be denoted in data extraction. Cases in which no enteric bacterial infection was present will be denoted, including positive bacteria agents and separated from cases in which no enteric bacterial infection was present. Cases in which a diagnosis of cryptosporidiosis or positive test for Cryptosporidium spp, will not be included. Data extraction will also account for reports in which bacterial infection was not tested and/or not reported.

Context

This scoping review will examine studies on cryptosporidiosis and IBD. Both inpatient and outpatient cases in any setting will be considered. No limitations by geographic or cultural settings will be made. No specific age criteria will be used as a requirement for inclusion. Diagnosis of cryptosporidiosis can occur before or after diagnosis of IBD. No language restrictions will be applied; most titles and abstracts are translated to English. Non-English studies that meet inclusion criteria will be translated using DeepL Translator 2023 (DeepL, Cologne, Germany). If DeepL Translator is not able to recognise the language, we will use Google Translate (Alphabet).

Types of sources

This scoping review will consider both experimental and quasiexperimental study designs including randomised controlled trials, non-randomised controlled trials, before and after studies and interrupted time-series studies. In addition, analytical observational studies including prospective and retrospective cohort studies, case–control studies and analytical cross-sectional studies will be considered for inclusion. This review will also consider descriptive observational study designs including case series, individual case reports and descriptive cross-sectional studies for inclusion.

If relevant qualitative studies are found that connect to the review question, any relevant data will be extracted. In addition, systematic reviews that meet the inclusion criteria, along with their referenced studies, will also be considered. This scoping review will include opinion papers if they are related to our review criteria or comment on included studies.

Search strategy

Preliminary searches were conducted in PROSPERO, PubMed, Embase, JBI Title registration and the journal JBI Evidence Synthesis for other protocols or reviews on this topic. None were found on the topic. The search strategy will aim to locate both published and unpublished studies. An initial limited search of MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase (Elsevier) and Google Scholar was undertaken to identify articles on the topic. Key articles were identified by the research team (BAH and BL). Yale MeSH Analyzer (2015–2023 Cushing/Whitney Medical Library, Yale University, Connecticut, USA) was used to help identify relevant MeSH terms and author keywords. With further refining and testing, text words contained in the titles and abstracts of relevant articles, and the index terms used to describe the articles were used to develop a full search strategy for MEDLINE (PubMed) (see online supplemental appendix 1). The search strategy, including all identified keywords and index terms, will be adapted for each included database and/or information source. The reference list of all included sources of evidence will be screened for additional studies, including systematic reviews related to the research question. The MEDLINE (PubMed) search strategy documented in online supplemental appendix 1 was peer reviewed by a health sciences librarian using Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies guidelines.50

The resources to be searched include MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase (Elsevier), Cochrane Library (Wiley) and CINAHL (EBSCO). The following resources will be searched for grey literature and unpublished literature: Dissertations and Theses Global (ProQuest), Google Scholar (Alphabet), ClinicalTrials.gov (NLM) and International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (WHO).

Study/source of evidence selection

Following the search, all identified citations will be collated and uploaded into Covidence 2023 (Veritas Health Innovations, Melbourne, Australia) and duplicates removed on that platform. Following a pilot test, titles and abstracts will then be screened by two or more independent reviewers for assessment against the inclusion criteria for the review using Covidence software. Potentially relevant sources will be retrieved in full and their citation details imported into Covidence. Any records that cannot be retrieved or translated into English will be documented in the text and the PRISMA flow diagram. The full text of selected citations will be assessed in detail against the inclusion criteria by two or more independent reviewers. Reasons for exclusion of sources of evidence at full text that do not meet the inclusion criteria will be recorded and reported in the scoping review. Any disagreements that arise between the reviewers at each stage of the selection process will be resolved through discussion, or with an additional reviewer/s. References of included full text records will be screened. Reviewers will do forward and backward citation chasing manually. If needed, software such as Citation Chaser (2022 Evidence Synthesis Hackathon), will be implemented. The results of the search and the study inclusion process will be reported in full in the final scoping review and presented in a PRISMA-ScR flow diagram.51

Data extraction

Data will be extracted from papers included in the scoping review by two or more independent reviewers using a data extraction tool developed by the reviewers. The data extracted will include specific details about the participants, concept, context, study methods and key findings relevant to the review question/s.

A draft extraction form is provided (see online supplemental appendix 2). The draft data extraction tool will be modified and revised as necessary during the process of extracting data from each included evidence source. Modifications will be detailed in the scoping review. Any disagreements that arise between the reviewers will be resolved through discussion, or with an additional reviewer/s. If necessary, authors of papers will be contacted to request missing or additional data, where required.

Data analysis and presentation

The data will be presented graphically or in diagrammatic or tabular form depending on what information is extracted. A narrative summary will accompany any tabulated or charted results and will describe how the results relate to the review objective and questions.

Ethics and dissemination

This study is a review of published literature and will not include patients. Institutional review board was therefore not required. Findings will be published as a scoping review in a peer-reviewed journal and at academic conferences on conclusion of the study.
