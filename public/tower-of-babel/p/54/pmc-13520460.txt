Perioperative biomarkers and graft function in living donor kidney transplantation: focus on postoperative prognostic nutritional index
Authors: Mustafa Anıl Turhan, Javid Ahmadov, Burak Kutlu, Furkan Atakan Akın, Seda Akyol, İhsan Ergün, Acar Tüzüner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly identification of factors associated with renal allograft function remains important in kidney transplantation. Nutritional, inflammatory, and hemodynamic markers may provide complementary information on post-transplant recovery, but their prognostic value in living donor kidney transplantation remains unclear. We evaluated the associations of perioperative Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI), Hemoglobin–Albumin–Lymphocyte–Platelet (HALP) score, Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR), and Renal Resistive Index (RRI) with early and medium-term graft function after living donor kidney transplantation.MethodsThis retrospective single-center study included 74 adult living donor kidney transplant recipients transplanted between February 2024 and June 2025. NLR, PNI, and HALP were assessed preoperatively and on postoperative day 30 (POD30), and RRI was measured on postoperative day 1 (POD1). Graft function was evaluated by estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) at POD30 and postoperative month 6 (POM6). Univariable and multivariable linear regression analyses were performed. Bootstrap resampling was used to assess the internal stability of parsimonious multivariable models, and ROC analysis was performed for POD30 PNI in relation to impaired POM6 graft function.ResultsAt POD30, graft function was independently associated with recipient age, donor preoperative eGFR, and donor/recipient body surface area ratio, whereas POD1 RRI was not independently associated. At POM6, recipient age, donor preoperative eGFR, and POD30 PNI remained independently associated with graft function. Bootstrap resampling generally supported the stability of the main associations. POD30 PNI significantly discriminated impaired POM6 graft function, defined as eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² (AUC 0.725, 95% CI 0.598–0.851, p = 0.002), with an optimal cut-off of 53.0.ConclusionsIn living donor kidney transplantation, early graft function appears to be influenced mainly by recipient- and donor-related factors, whereas medium-term graft function is additionally associated with postoperative nutritional recovery reflected by POD30 PNI. Background

Kidney transplantation remains the most effective therapeutic strategy for end-stage renal disease, offering marked improvements in both survival and quality of life [1]. However, early identification of factors influencing graft function after transplantation is critical for ensuring long-term success. Kidney transplant recipients are vulnerable to acute and chronic rejection episodes, immunosuppressive drug-related toxicities, and various complications, all of which may adversely affect graft survival [2]. Therefore, monitoring early postoperative clinical and biochemical parameters as predictive markers of graft function has become essential for both evaluating graft performance and informing timely therapeutic strategies [3].

The prevalence of malnutrition among kidney transplant recipients has been reported to range from 25% to 35%, and several studies have demonstrated that poor nutritional status is associated with both graft outcomes and overall patient survival [4, 5]. These findings highlight malnutrition as a clinically relevant parameter that should be carefully considered in the management of kidney transplant recipients. In recent years, several easily calculable composite indices reflecting systemic inflammation and nutritional status have been integrated into clinical practice. Among these, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI), and hemoglobin–albumin–lymphocyte–platelet (HALP) score have gained attention for their prognostic value in oncology, and more recently, they have increasingly been investigated in the field of transplantation as well [6–11].

Another important parameter, the renal resistive index (RRI), is a non-invasive Doppler ultrasonographic measure used after kidney transplantation to assess graft hemodynamics, and it has been described in the literature as a potential predictor of graft dysfunction [12–14]. Although several studies have demonstrated the association between RRI and early graft function, the literature remains limited regarding its combined evaluation with systemic inflammatory and nutritional biomarkers such as PNI and HALP [13].

To date, there are no comprehensive studies in the literature that simultaneously evaluate the impact of biomarkers such as PNI, HALP, NLR, and RRI on early and medium-term graft function after kidney transplantation. A combined assessment of these indices may contribute to a more accurate and holistic prediction of graft prognosis.

This study aims to evaluate the predictive value of pre- and postoperative NLR, PNI, and HALP scores, along with postoperative day-1 (POD1) RRI, in relation to early (day 30) and medium-term (month 6) estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) in living donor kidney transplant recipients. These biomarkers may offer practical tools for graft monitoring and support individualized post-transplant management strategies.

Materials and methods

This retrospective observational study included 82 patients who underwent living donor kidney transplantation at the Ankara Memorial Hospital Transplantation Center between February 2024 and June 2025. This study was reviewed and approved by the Uskudar University Non-Interventional Research Ethics Committee (Approval no: 61351342/020–59). Owing to its retrospective design, the requirement for informed consent to participate was waived by the Ethics Committee, in accordance with national regulations. All research procedures were conducted in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Patients under the age of 18, those with missing postoperative day-30 (POD30) eGFR data, and one patient who died in the early postoperative period due to renal artery anastomotic leakage and bleeding were excluded from the study. After applying these predefined exclusion criteria, 74 patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). All included patients completed 6-month (POM6) follow-up, and no patients were lost due to graft loss or death during this period.

Flow diagram of patient selection

Demographic characteristics (age, sex, body mass index [BMI], body surface area [BSA]), immunological profiles [number of HLA mismatches (HLA-MM), panel reactive antibody (PRA) status, and presence of donor-specific antibodies (DSA)], desensitization and induction therapy protocols, surgical details, and hospitalization duration were thoroughly reviewed. In addition, donor characteristics, including age, sex, BMI, and preoperative eGFR, were also recorded. Recipient and donor BSA values were calculated using the Mosteller formula: BSA (m²) = √([height (cm) × weight (kg)] / 3600). Donor–recipient size mismatch was assessed using the donor/recipient BSA ratio, calculated by dividing donor BSA by recipient BSA [15]. Laboratory parameters were obtained at multiple time points: preoperative (on the day of admission) and POD1, day 7, POD30, and POM6. Serum creatinine levels were recorded at each of these time points. eGFR was calculated using the 2009 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) formula, which is widely validated and commonly used in kidney transplant populations. Protocol biopsy was not routinely performed in our center; instead, allograft biopsy was performed only when clinically indicated on the basis of graft dysfunction, laboratory abnormalities, or clinical suspicion. All rejection episodes recorded in this study were biopsy-confirmed and classified according to Banff criteria. Two recipients required early postoperative dialysis; both had antibody-mediated rejection (ABMR), recovered after treatment, and were therefore retained in the analysis.

All recipients underwent comprehensive immunological evaluation including human leukocyte antigen (HLA) typing, lymphocyte crossmatch, flow cytometry crossmatch, and panel reactive antibody (PRA) screening using the Luminex platform. In PRA-positive patients, single-antigen bead assays were used to identify donor-specific antibodies (DSAs). Induction therapy was administered based on immunological risk stratification, including HLA mismatch burden, donor type (related vs. unrelated), and immunological profile (PRA/DSA status). Recipients with ≤ 3 HLA mismatches from related donors did not receive induction therapy, whereas those with > 3 mismatches or from unrelated donors received anti-T lymphocyte globulin (ATLG)-based induction therapy in combination with pulse corticosteroids. In addition, ATLG was administered in selected cases for the treatment of biopsy-confirmed acute rejection during the early postoperative period. Pulse steroid therapy consisted of intravenous methylprednisolone, followed by gradual tapering with oral corticosteroids. Maintenance immunosuppression consisted of tacrolimus, an antimetabolite (mycophenolate mofetil or mycophenolic acid), and corticosteroids, administered according to the institutional transplant protocol. Desensitization was performed in patients with positive PRA and/or DSA. Desensitization was performed when indicated using a standardized protocol consisting of five sessions of plasmapheresis combined with intravenous immunoglobulin and rituximab. All patients received standard prophylaxis, including trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for 6 months, valganciclovir for 100 days, and fluconazole for 6 months. Post-transplant follow-up and immunosuppressive management were conducted within a standardized institutional transplant protocol, with a broadly similar maintenance immunosuppressive regimen across the cohort. Tacrolimus dosing was applied according to this standardized protocol, with target trough levels of approximately 10 ng/mL during the first three months and 6–7 ng/mL up to 6 months thereafter. Corticosteroid therapy was initiated with oral prednisolone at discharge (40 mg/day) and gradually tapered weekly to maintenance dose of 5 mg/day.

All transplant surgeries were performed by the same experienced surgical team. Donor nephrectomies were performed using a laparoscopic approach in all cases. On POD1, Doppler ultrasonography of the renal allograft was routinely performed. All measurements were obtained between 09:00 and 12:00 to minimize circadian variability. To evaluate parenchymal perfusion, RRI measurements were obtained from the segmental and interlobar arteries at the upper, middle, and lower poles of the transplanted kidney. Measurements were performed with an insonation angle of ≤ 60°, with appropriate pulse repetition frequency (PRF) and gain settings. Three measurements were obtained at each location, and the mean value was recorded. Additionally, RRI measurements were obtained from the proximal, middle, and distal (anastomotic) segments of the renal artery, and mean values were calculated for each segment. All ultrasound examinations were performed using the same device by a single experienced radiologist to minimize interobserver variability. Examinations were performed under standardized clinical conditions in the early postoperative period. Patients were not in a strict fasting state, as oral intake had already been initiated. Measurements were obtained in the morning hours, generally following administration of calcineurin inhibitors. Fluid management was guided by urine output and clinical parameters to maintain stable hemodynamic conditions.

To evaluate the preoperative and postoperative inflammatory status, NLR was calculated at three points: preoperatively, on POD1, and POD30. Additionally, PNI and HALP scores, which reflect both nutritional and inflammatory status, were calculated preoperatively and on POD30. Preoperative values were used to reflect baseline status, POD1 RRI to capture the immediate postoperative hemodynamic milieu, and POD1/POD30 NLR together with POD30 nutritional indices to reflect the early systemic inflammatory and recovery response after transplantation. All patients received nutritional counseling from the same dedicated nutrition team following hospital discharge.

The postoperative eGFR threshold of 60 mL/min/1.73 m² was selected according to the KDIGO 2024 classification [16]. Accordingly, eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m² was defined as preserved graft function, whereas eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² indicated impaired graft function. In line with commonly used clinical and transplant literature thresholds, hypoalbuminemia was defined as serum albumin < 35 g/L [17, 18].

Statistical analysis

The distribution of continuous variables was assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while those not normally distributed were reported as median with interquartile range (IQR) and minimum–maximum values. For group comparisons, the Student’s t-test was applied to normally distributed variables, whereas the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, and analyzed using either the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Correlations between continuous variables were evaluated using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients, depending on the distributional characteristics. Variables with a p-value < 0.20 in univariable analysis, along with clinically relevant covariates, were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model. To avoid overfitting and multicollinearity, only selected variables were retained in the final model. Among the variables independently associated with POM6 eGFR, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed only for POD30 PNI to explore its discriminative ability for impaired 6-month graft function. To assess the internal stability of the parsimonious multivariable models, bootstrap resampling was performed using the simple sampling method with 1000 resamples. Bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) 95% confidence intervals were calculated for regression coefficients. A fixed random seed was used to ensure reproducibility. Diagnostic performance metrics, including area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity, were reported. Internal uncertainty of the ROC-derived AUC, optimal threshold, sensitivity, and specificity was explored in R using 2000 stratified bootstrap resamples. Subgroup analyses were additionally performed to explore potential effect modification across clinically relevant patient categories, using dummy variable coding and interaction terms within regression models. Sensitivity analyses were performed by adding HLA mismatch burden, PRA status, DSA status, and induction therapy to the parsimonious multivariable models in a second block. Model fit was evaluated with R² and adjusted R². For subgroup analyses, patients were stratified into three transplant groups: (1) preemptive kidney transplant recipients (no prior dialysis), (2) dialysis-dependent first transplant recipients (receiving their first kidney transplant after hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis), and (3) retransplant recipients (patients undergoing a second or subsequent kidney transplant). The dialysis-dependent first transplant group was used as the reference category in regression analyses. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A post-hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1 software. With a sample size of 74 and α = 0.05, the study had approximately 80% power to detect a moderate correlation (ρ ≈ 0.32) in univariable analyses. For multivariable linear regression models including 5–6 predictors, the study was powered to detect moderate effect sizes (f² ≈ 0.15), indicating that smaller associations may not have been reliably detected. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline demographic and perioperative characteristics of the 74 living donor kidney transplant recipients are summarized in Tables 1 and 2. The mean recipient age was 42.0 ± 13.1 years, 64.9% were male, and 48.6% underwent preemptive transplantation. Among the dialysis-dependent recipients, 6 were on peritoneal dialysis and the remainder were on hemodialysis before transplantation; the median pretransplant dialysis duration was 15.0 months (IQR, 25.0). Mean POD1 RRI was 0.63 ± 0.07. Median POD30 eGFR was 72.5 mL/min/1.73 m², and mean POM6 eGFR was 71.3 ± 22.8 mL/min/1.73 m². Acute rejection occurred in 14 recipients (18.9%); the median interval from transplantation to biopsy-confirmed rejection was 8.5 days (range, 2–47), indicating a predominantly early postoperative occurrence. Among the 14 biopsy-confirmed acute rejection episodes, 9 were classified as ABMR and 5 as T cell–mediated rejection (TCMR). Among the TCMR cases, 3 were Banff 1 A, 1 was Banff 2 A, and 1 was Banff 2B.

For POM6 eGFR (Table 5B), independent associations were observed for recipient age, donor preoperative eGFR, and POD30 PNI, while donor/recipient BSA ratio and acute rejection were not retained as significant predictors. In sensitivity analyses, the addition of clinically relevant immunological variables did not significantly improve model performance for either POD30 or POM6 eGFR, and the main associations remained materially unchanged (Supplementary Table S4).

POD30 PNI did not differ significantly across major clinical and immunological subgroups, including preemptive transplantation, acute rejection, retransplantation, desensitization, PRA status, and induction therapy (Table 6). In addition, no significant difference in POD30 PNI was observed between patients who received ATLG and those who did not (p = 0.332), including one additional patient who received ATLG for rejection during the early postoperative period prior to POD30 assessment. These findings support the robustness of the association between POD30 PNI and POM6 eGFR, suggesting that it is not merely explained by baseline subgroup differences. However, exploratory interaction analysis suggested that the association between POD30 PNI and POM6 eGFR may differ across transplant categories, with the strongest estimated effect observed in retransplant recipients (Supplementary Table S5).

Discussion

In this single-center cohort of living donor kidney transplant recipients, we evaluated perioperative inflammatory (NLR), nutritional (PNI, HALP), and hemodynamic (RRI) markers as readily available indicators of early post-transplant recovery. The main finding was that the determinants of graft function appeared to differ over time. At POD30, higher eGFR was independently associated with younger recipient age and donor-related factors, particularly higher donor preoperative eGFR and a higher donor/recipient BSA ratio. At POM6, younger recipient age, higher donor preoperative eGFR, and higher POD30 PNI remained independently associated with better medium-term graft function. This association likely reflects early postoperative recovery dynamics and should be interpreted with caution in terms of causality. In contrast, HALP, NLR, and POD1 RRI were not independently associated with graft function after adjustment.

Among the evaluated biomarkers, POD30 PNI showed the most consistent association with medium-term graft performance. Higher POD30 PNI values were independently associated with higher POM6 eGFR, and this association remained stable after bootstrap validation. These findings are broadly consistent with previous reports highlighting the prognostic relevance of postoperative nutritional status after kidney transplantation [11, 19]. However, this association should be interpreted cautiously. Because POD30 PNI is derived from albumin and lymphocyte count, it may reflect postoperative inflammation, immunosuppressive exposure, infection, and graft recovery itself, and thus likely captures early postoperative recovery dynamics rather than a direct causal effect. This pattern may also reflect differences in postoperative “recovery capacity,” referring to the ability to mount an effective nutritional and inflammatory response following surgical stress and immunosuppression, which may be more informative than baseline status alone.

In our cohort, POD30 PNI did not differ significantly across major clinical and immunological subgroups, suggesting that its association with POM6 eGFR was not simply driven by imbalance in these factors. Nevertheless, reverse causality cannot be excluded, as recipients with better early graft recovery may already have had more favorable albumin and lymphocyte levels by POD30. Because serial measurements between discharge and POD30 were not available, we cannot determine whether POD30 PNI is truly predictive of later graft function or primarily reflects uneventful early postoperative recovery. This pattern may also reflect overall postoperative recovery capacity, whereby recipients with more favorable nutritional and inflammatory recovery by POD30 may have greater physiological resilience during the early post-transplant period, although the underlying mechanisms remain speculative.

This interpretation is also supported by the absence of a significant association between preoperative PNI and either POD30 or POM6 eGFR. Similar to the findings of Yadigar et al. [20], pretransplant nutritional or inflammatory indices may have limited prognostic value in this setting. One possible explanation is that end-stage kidney disease is itself a chronic inflammatory and catabolic condition, which may reduce the ability of baseline composite scores to discriminate risk once transplantation restores metabolic and physiological balance. In our cohort, preoperative hypoalbuminemia was likewise not associated with graft function at POD30 or POM6. This difference may be related to the outcome measure and follow-up period, since Anderson et al. reported that preoperative hypoalbuminemia was independently associated with overall graft loss after kidney transplantation, although no difference was observed in 1-year creatinine [18]. Overall, these findings suggest that low pretransplant albumin may be more relevant to long-term transplant outcomes than to short- or medium-term creatinine-based graft function.

In contrast to PNI, HALP was not independently associated with either early or medium-term graft function in our cohort. Although HALP incorporates a broader range of hematologic parameters than PNI, these additional components may be more susceptible to perioperative influences such as blood loss, hemodilution, early shifts in erythropoiesis, platelet variability, and medication-related effects. Similarly, NLR was not independently associated with graft outcomes. These findings are in keeping with earlier studies showing that composite inflammation- and nutrition-based indices such as PNI and HALP may be associated with clinical outcomes, although their relative usefulness appears to vary across disease settings [8, 9, 20–23]. While these markers have been widely studied in oncology, their role in transplantation remains less clearly defined [11, 20]. In our cohort, the results suggest that perioperative inflammatory and nutritional markers do not all carry the same prognostic relevance, and that indices reflecting postoperative nutritional recovery may provide more clinically meaningful information than isolated inflammatory changes. At the same time, the lack of an independent association for HALP should not be interpreted as evidence of biological irrelevance, as the analysis may have been underpowered to detect modest effects. Still, the prognostic role of HALP remains uncertain and should be evaluated further in larger prospective multicenter studies before it can be considered for routine clinical use.

One of the more unexpected findings of our study was the association between preemptive transplantation and lower eGFR, particularly at POD30, with attenuation by POM6. A relatively higher eGFR was also observed among retransplant recipients in our analysis. These findings should be interpreted cautiously and should not be taken to indicate an intrinsic disadvantage of preemptive transplantation or a consistent functional advantage of retransplantation. Several factors may help explain this pattern. Although preemptive transplantation is generally linked to better long-term patient and graft outcomes [24], early postoperative creatinine kinetics may not be comparable across all recipient groups. In one perioperative study, non-preemptive recipients showed a greater decline in serum creatinine during the first postoperative month, but this difference was no longer present during longer follow-up [25]. Early creatinine-based eGFR may also be influenced by muscle mass, volume status, and residual native kidney function. In addition, residual confounding related to donor characteristics, vascular anatomy, operative complexity, or recipient selection may have contributed to this pattern. Notably, preemptive recipients in our cohort had a lower donor/recipient BSA ratio, whereas donor BSA and donor preoperative eGFR were otherwise similar between groups, suggesting that size matching or lower effective nephron dose may have partly contributed to the lower early eGFR observed in this subgroup. Overall, these findings are more likely to reflect the combined influence of baseline and perioperative factors than a direct effect of transplant category itself. This interpretation is further supported by the attenuation of the association by POM6, while the broader literature generally favors preemptive transplantation in terms of long-term outcomes [25–28].

Donor-related factors also seemed to have an important influence on graft function in our cohort. Donor preoperative eGFR was independently associated with both POD30 and POM6 eGFR, while donor/recipient BSA ratio was independently associated with POD30 eGFR. These findings support the idea that donor renal reserve and size matching may contribute to post-transplant renal performance [15, 29–31]. Recipient age remained inversely associated with graft function at both time points, whereas BMI appeared to be more relevant in the early postoperative period, suggesting that anthropometric and metabolic factors may have their greatest impact shortly after transplantation [29–33]. Together, these findings suggest that donor-related characteristics and recipient anthropometric factors may be important determinants of short- and medium-term graft function in this setting.

Acute rejection also warrants careful consideration. In our cohort, biopsy-confirmed rejection occurred early, with a median onset of 8.5 days after transplantation, meaning that most episodes preceded POD30 PNI measurement. Even so, acute rejection was not independently associated with POM6 eGFR in the final multivariable model. Several factors may account for this. Given the limited number of rejection episodes, the analysis may have been underpowered to detect moderate associations, and rejection severity and treatment response may have varied substantially. It is also possible that part of the effect of early rejection was indirectly captured by POD30 biochemical markers. In addition, modeling rejection as a binary variable may have reduced our ability to reflect differences in timing, cumulative burden, and treatment intensity.

Our findings regarding RRI should be interpreted as non-significant rather than unfavorable. In our cohort, POD1 RRI showed a weak inverse association with POD30 graft function in univariable analysis, but this association did not persist after adjustment for either POD30 or POM6 eGFR, and bootstrap analysis did not support a robust independent effect. This result is not entirely surprising. RRI is shaped not only by intrarenal resistance but also by perioperative hemodynamic conditions, fluid status, vascular tone, interstitial edema, and systemic vascular compliance, all of which may limit its specificity for predicting subsequent graft function in the immediate postoperative period [14, 34]. Previous studies have likewise shown considerable heterogeneity in the reported thresholds, timing, and prognostic utility of RRI after transplantation [35]. It is possible that serial RRI measurements obtained during the early postoperative course are more informative than a single POD1 value [36]. In this context, our results do not argue against the clinical use of RRI but rather suggest that a single POD1 measurement may offer limited independent prognostic value beyond donor and recipient characteristics.

Our findings suggest that the factors associated with graft function differed across follow-up periods. In the early postoperative phase, recipient- and donor-related variables—particularly recipient age, donor preoperative eGFR, and donor/recipient BSA ratio—appeared to play a greater role, while preemptive transplantation was also retained in the POD30 model. By contrast, at POM6, recipient age, donor preoperative eGFR, and POD30 PNI remained independently associated with graft function. RRI, HALP, and NLR were not independent predictors at either stage. Retransplant recipients represent a clinically distinct group and may differ from primary transplant recipients in both baseline risk and post-transplant course [37]. In our exploratory interaction analysis, the association between POD30 PNI and POM6 eGFR appeared to vary according to transplant category. However, this finding should be interpreted with substantial caution because the number of retransplant recipients was small and subgroup analyses in modest-sized cohorts are inherently unstable. In addition, prior immunosuppressive exposure before retransplantation was not recorded in a sufficiently standardized manner and may have contributed to residual confounding. Similarly, although POD30 PNI significantly discriminated impaired POM6 graft function defined as eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m², our main findings are based on continuous eGFR analyses, and the ROC-derived threshold should be regarded as exploratory. Overall, these results suggest that POD30 PNI may serve as a complementary marker of medium-term graft recovery, but larger prospective studies are needed before subgroup-specific interpretation or threshold-based clinical use can be supported.

From a clinical perspective, our findings suggest that POD30 PNI may provide simple but useful complementary information about postoperative recovery after living donor kidney transplantation. In our cohort, POD30 PNI did not differ significantly across the main clinical, immunological, or baseline nutritional subgroups, supporting the idea that its association with POM6 graft function was not confined to a single patient subgroup. If externally validated in larger prospective cohorts, a POD30 PNI below 53.0 may help identify recipients warranting closer nutritional evaluation; however, given the wide bootstrap confidence interval for the optimal cut-off (45.25–56.25), this threshold should not be used for individual patient decision-making without such validation. From a practical perspective, POD30 PNI may therefore be viewed as a simple adjunctive marker of recovery trajectory and supportive monitoring parameter rather than a stand-alone prognostic marker.

The findings of the present study are also consistent with observations from other major surgical disciplines. In hepatobiliary, pancreatic, and colorectal surgery, perioperative PNI has been associated with long-term survival and major postoperative complications, whether assessed preoperatively or as a postoperative recovery trajectory [38–40], suggesting that nutritional recovery capacity may represent a broader prognostic principle across surgical settings.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective single-center design limits the generalizability of the findings, and although a post-hoc power analysis and bootstrap validation were performed, the sample size remains relatively modest, which may limit the statistical power of the analyses. Second, while donor preoperative eGFR and donor/recipient BSA ratio were included in the models, broader donor-quality metrics such as the Living Kidney Donor Profile Index (LKDPI) were not calculated, as some of the variables required for its derivation were not consistently available in our dataset. Incorporation of LKDPI in future studies may allow for a more comprehensive assessment of donor-related risk. Third, postoperative nutritional and inflammatory indices may have been affected by factors such as immunosuppressive treatment, prophylactic medications, occult infection, and perioperative fluid shifts. In addition, graft function was assessed using creatinine-based eGFR rather than a broader set of renal outcomes, such as proteinuria, delayed graft function, or biopsy-based findings. Although the bootstrap-derived median threshold remained close to 53, the wide resampling intervals for sensitivity and specificity indicate that this cut-off should be viewed as exploratory rather than definitive. At the same time, the study also has several strengths. These include the simultaneous evaluation of multiple readily available perioperative biomarkers, the incorporation of donor-related variables, standardized POD1 ultrasonographic assessment by a single radiologist, and the assessment of both early- and medium-term graft function in a relatively homogeneous living donor cohort. In addition, these findings may not be directly generalizable to deceased donor transplantation, where donor characteristics are more heterogeneous and delayed graft function is more common. Finally, although the present analysis is based on a limited sample and is restricted to early- and medium-term follow-up, longitudinal follow-up of this cohort is ongoing and may help clarify whether these findings remain relevant at 1 year and beyond.

Conclusion

In this single-center cohort of living donor kidney transplant recipients, POD30 PNI emerged as a consistent correlate of medium-term graft function, whereas early graft function appeared to be influenced more strongly by recipient age and donor-related factors, including donor preoperative eGFR and donor/recipient BSA ratio. Although POD30 PNI may provide complementary information on postoperative recovery, it should currently be regarded as an adjunctive monitoring marker rather than a stand-alone prognostic tool. The subgroup interaction and ROC findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Larger prospective studies with longer follow-up are needed to validate these findings and clarify the clinical utility of PNI in post-transplant risk stratification.
