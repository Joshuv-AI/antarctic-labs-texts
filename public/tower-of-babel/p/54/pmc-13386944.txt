Diagnosis, management, and clinical outcomes of childhood invasive aspergillosis
Authors: Asena Ünal Tolunay, Özlem Özgür Gündeşlioğlu, Aslı Yeşiloğlu, Ümmühan Çay, Fatma Tuğba Çetin, Gökçe Oğuz, Göksu Başargan, Hatice Yelda Çığşar, Elif Su Korkmaz, Ayşe Özkan, Mahir Serbes, Derya Alabaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo present our clinical experience in pediatric invasive aspergillosis and to identify independent risk factors and prognostic thresholds affecting survival.Materials and methodsWe retrospectively reviewed 30 pediatric patients diagnosed with proven or probable invasive aspergillosis at a tertiary care center between 2012 and 2025. Demographic, clinical, radiological, and laboratory data were analyzed. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to identify optimal prognostic cut-offs for laboratory markers. Multivariate logistic regression was used to identify independent associates of mortality while addressing the risk of overfitting.ResultsThirty patients were included (56.7% male; median age 114.5 months). The most common underlying diseases were hemato-oncological malignancies (53.4%), immunodeficiencies (23.3%), and non-malignant hematological diseases (23.3%). Fifteen patients (50%) were diagnosed with proven IA and 15 (50%) with probable IA. Neutropenia was seen in 22 patients (73.3%) and lymphopenia in 29 (96.7%). Crude and attributable mortality rates were 40% (n = 12) and 13.3% (n = 4), respectively. ROC analysis identified critical thresholds for mortality: ferritin > 3479 ng/mL (AUC: 0.823, p = 0.019), procalcitonin > 7.3 ng/mL (AUC: 0.745, p = 0.025), and absolute monocyte count < 0.01 × 10³/µL (AUC: 0.678, p = 0.048). In this cohort, multivariate analysis indicated that higher baseline ferritin levels were independently associated with increased mortality (OR: 1.12, 95% CI: 1.01–1.26, p = 0.038). However, hyperferritinemia was significantly more pronounced in patients with aplastic anemia (p = 0.001).ConclusionEarly diagnosis and aggressive antifungal therapy appear effective in limiting invasive aspergillosis attributable mortality. The integration of specific laboratory thresholds, particularly high ferritin and profound monocytopenia, may offer valuable insights for risk stratification in pediatric invasive aspergillosis. Future multicenter studies are warranted to validate these findings.

Introduction

Invasive aspergillosis (IA) is an opportunistic infection that develops in immunocompromised children, particularly in the presence of hematological malignancies and prolonged neutropenia [1–3]. Despite advancements in modern antifungal therapies, mortality rates in the pediatric population remain as high as 30% to 50% in the literature, primarily due to the complexity of diagnostic processes and the non-specific nature of clinical findings [2, 3]. In contrast, data defining “attributable mortality”—which specifies the proportion of deaths directly resulting from the fungal process—remain notably limited. The management of IA in children is a dynamic process that requires not only the administration of appropriate treatment but also the early identification of high-risk patients and potential poor prognostic outcomes [1, 4].

Although various risk factors, such as the duration of neutropenia and underlying diseases, have been identified for the development of IA in the literature, the sensitivity of complete blood count and basic biochemical parameters used in routine clinical practice for predicting prognosis remains controversial [2, 3]. This limitation directs clinicians toward seeking more objective laboratory indicators that are accessible at the time of diagnosis, provide rapid results, and can be directly associated with disease severity [1, 4]. In particular, the critical effector roles of monocytes—which constitute the second line of defense alongside neutrophils—in the phagocytosis of Aspergillus spores and the subsequent inhibition of hyphal germination, represent a significant area of investigation regarding their prognostic value in the course of pediatric IA [5, 6]. Nevertheless, it must be taken into account that markers such as ferritin can be influenced by iron-overload conditions like aplastic anemia, and procalcitonin levels by secondary bacterial infections, serving as potential clinical confounders.

The lower incidence of IA in childhood compared to adults complicates the collection of sufficient data regarding the natural course of the disease and the factors determining its prognosis. In addition to defining the demographic, clinical, and radiological characteristics of pediatric IA cases at our tertiary care center, this study aims to identify the determinants that affect survival and are directly associated with mortality through data analysis.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted by retrospectively reviewing the data of 30 pediatric patients who were monitored with a diagnosis of IA and whose data were accessible at Çukurova University Faculty of Medicine Hospital between 2012 and 2025. The study was was approved by the Çukurova University Faculty of Medicine Hospital Ethics Committee (Meeting No: 163, dated February 6, 2026). Due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized patient data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Çukurova University Ethics Committee. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients were identified through a comprehensive search of the hospital’s microbiology surveillance unit (for PCR and culture positivity) and pathology database (for Aspergillus-positive tissue specimens) (Fig. 1). Accordingly, only patients aged 0–18 years meeting the ‘Proven’ or ‘Probable’ IA criteria as defined by the EORTC/MSGERC (European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer/Mycoses Study Group Education and Research Consortium) consensus were included [7, 8]. Diagnostic criteria were applied as follows:

Since the study was designed to include only cases with definitive microbiological or histopathological evidence, ‘Possible’ IA cases—which lack these criteria—were not within the scope of this laboratory-confirmed cohort.

Demographic characteristics, underlying diseases, predisposing factors, clinical symptoms, and the duration of neutropenia were retrospectively recorded from the patients’ medical records. Laboratory parameters collected at the time of diagnosis included complete blood count [white blood cell (WBC), absolute neutrophil count (ANC), absolute lymphocyte count (ALC), absolute monocyte count (AMC), and platelet (PLT) count], C-reactive protein (CRP), serum ferritin, and procalcitonin (PCT) levels, alongside microbiological data (culture, galactomannan, and PCR).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was performed using SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Sciences) version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (minimum–maximum), depending on their distribution characteristics. The Mann-Whitney U test was used for intergroup comparisons, while Pearson’s Chi-square or Fisher’s Exact tests were employed for the analysis of categorical data. To determine the clinical utility of biochemical markers, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed. Optimal cut-off values for parameters found significant in predicting mortality (ferritin, procalcitonin, and absolute monocyte count) were identified by maximizing the Youden Index. To identify independent risk factors for mortality, a Multivariate Logistic Regression analysis was conducted. Given the limited sample size and the specific number of mortality events (n = 12), a parsimonious modeling strategy was adopted to minimize the risk of overfitting. Only variables demonstrating a strong clinical trend or statistical significance (p < 0.10) in univariate analyses were considered for the initial model. A backward stepwise elimination process was then applied to retain the most robust predictors. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Of the 30 patients included in the study, 17 (56.7%) were male and 13 (43.3%) were female, with a median age of 114.5 months (range: 27–203 months). The most common underlying diseases were hemato-oncological malignancies (53.4%), immunodeficiencies (23.3%), and non-malignant hematological diseases (23.3%), primarily aplastic anemia (Table 1).

Regarding the sites of involvement, 16 patients (53.3%) had pulmonary, 9 (30%) had paranasal, 2 (6.6%) had both pulmonary and hepatic, 1 (3.3%) had concurrent pulmonary and paranasal sinus, 1 (3.3%) had cerebral, and 1 (3.3%) had vertebral involvement. Analysis of the 15 patients who underwent diagnostic tissue biopsy or surgical debridement revealed that the primary anatomical sites for sampling were the nasal cavity (46.7%, n = 7) and lung tissue (40.0%, n = 6). Diagnosis was achieved via vertebral biopsy in one patient, while another case showed involvement in both the nasal cavity and lung tissue.

In terms of diagnostic distribution, 50% of the patients (n = 15) were diagnosed with proven IA through histopathological examination and/or positive culture from a sterile site. The remaining (50%, n = 15) were classified as probable IA based on clinical/radiological findings accompanied by PCR positivity and/or elevated galactomannan (GM) levels. The most common presenting symptoms were fever (73.3%) and cough (36.7%). Microbiological analysis revealed concurrent bacterial infection evidence, with blood culture positivity in 20% (n = 6) and serum GM positivity in 33.3% (n = 10) of the cases (Table 1).

Assessment of predisposing factors revealed that the presence of a central venous catheter (CVC) (76.7%, n = 23) and a history of recent chemotherapy (56.7%, n = 17) were the most prominent risk factors. At the time of diagnosis, lymphopenia was present in 96.7% (n = 29) and neutropenia was observed in 73.3% (n = 22) of the cases, with a median neutropenia duration of 25 days (Table 1).

Regarding the treatment process, 8 patients were initially started on combination therapy, while 22 patients received antifungal monotherapy. However, during follow-up, an additional antifungal agent was added for 21 patients, resulting in 96.7% (n = 29) of the cohort receiving combination therapy. The most frequently preferred combination regimen consisted of Liposomal Amphotericin B and Voriconazole (Table 2). The median duration of hospitalization for treatment was 47.5 days (range: 14–120 days).

During the follow-up period, the crude mortality rate was 40% (n = 12), while the attributable mortality rate directly associated with invasive aspergillosis was determined to be 13.3% (n = 4). The median survival time after diagnosis for deceased patients was 60 days (range: 14–160 days). Oral antifungal prophylaxis or maintenance therapy was planned for 94.4% (n = 17) of the survivors upon discharge. In univariate analyses of survival, no statistically significant impact was found for gender, age, diagnostic method, or predisposing factors (CVC, steroid use, history of chemotherapy) (Table 1). When underlying diseases were analyzed, the presence of non-malignant hematological diseases (a total of 7 patients: 6 with aplastic anemia and 1 with thalassemia major status-post bone marrow transplantation) was significantly higher among deceased patients compared to survivors (p = 0.046). Clinical monitoring of this patient group revealed more profound neutropenia, lymphopenia, and thrombocytopenia, along with a significantly longer duration of neutropenia. Additionally, both the duration of hospitalization and ferritin levels were found to be significantly higher in this group (p = 0.001). In the multivariate logistic regression model constructed to identify independent predictors of mortality, every 100 ng/mL increase in serum ferritin levels was found to independently increase the risk of mortality by 1.12 times (OR: 1.12; 95% CI: 1.01–1.26; p = 0.038). Although procalcitonin elevation (p = 0.098), absolute monocyte count (p = 0.100), duration of neutropenia, and the presence of underlying non-malignant hematological diseases exhibited a strong clinical trend toward mortality within the model, they remained at the threshold of statistical significance. To clarify the potential confounding effect of underlying diseases, we included ‘aplastic anemia status’ as a covariate in our refined multivariate analysis. Although ferritin levels were significantly higher in the aplastic anemia group (median: 6077 ng/mL) compared to other patients (p = 0.001), high ferritin levels maintained a strong statistical association with mortality even after adjusting for disease subgroup, suggesting that iron overload may independently contribute to poor outcomes regardless of the primary diagnosis.”

Discussion

Despite advances in diagnostic modalities and next-generation antifungal therapies, pediatric IA remains one of the most critical opportunistic infections, characterized by high mortality and morbidity, particularly in immunocompromised children. In our study, the crude mortality rate was 40%; however, only 4 patients (13.3%) died directly due to complications related to IA. Analysis of the clinical course of the remaining 8 deceased patients revealed sepsis confirmed by Acinetobacter baumannii growth in one case, toxic hepatitis in one case, refractory sepsis in three cases, and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome in three cases. The 40% mortality rate observed in our study is consistent with the wide range reported in the literature, which varies between 18.6% and 52.5% (2, 3, 9]. Similar findings are highlighted in studies, where mortality rates ranging from 33% to 44% have been reported across different centers, reaching up to 80% in high-risk hematological cohorts with pulmonary involvement [5, 6, 10]. The persistence of mortality at these levels despite improvements in modern antifungal and supportive care stems from the fact that the mortality burden is not solely attributable to IA.

In our study, the median survival time after diagnosis for deceased patients was 60 days (range: 14–160 days). The literature indicates that deaths typically occur within the first few weeks following diagnosis due to the aggressive nature and risk of vascular invasion associated with IA [1, 2]. The fact that the median survival time in our data extended to two months may be considered a reflection of the proactive screening (galactomannan and CT) strategies and the rapid initiation of aggressive antifungal combination therapies implemented at our center. The fact that the attributable mortality rate directly related to IA remained at 13.3% indicates that fungal infections can be controlled through early and appropriate intervention. However, this supports the finding that patients are lost during this prolonged period due to the severity of their underlying diseases (notably aplastic anemia) and complications such as concomitant resistant secondary bacterial sepsis [9, 10].

The protective effect of early diagnosis on mortality has been emphasized in numerous pediatric cohorts; appropriate treatment initiated at the time of diagnosis limits the spread of hyphal invasion into tissues and the vascular system, thereby reducing the risk of multiorgan failure [5, 6]. The fact that nearly all patients (96.7%) in our study received combination therapy during follow-up, with the median day of death being delayed to the 60th day, indicates that antifungal management is successful in preventing acute IA-related death. However, this prolonged survival process increases the duration of hospitalization, leaving patients vulnerable to secondary complications such as drug-related toxicity [1]. Furthermore, the increase in interventional procedures, such as surgical debridement, and invasive approaches alongside extended hospital stays, makes patients susceptible to secondary bacterial infections and organ failure, constituting a significant predisposing factor for crude mortality. This situation further underscores the aggressive nature of IA in the pediatric population and the necessity for early prognostic markers to predict survival.

In our cohort, serum galactomannan (GM) positivity was observed in only 33.3% of the cases. Although GM remains a key mycological criterion for the diagnosis of probable invasive aspergillosis (IA), its diagnostic performance in children has been reported to be variable in the literature. Pediatric studies and meta-analyses have demonstrated considerable heterogeneity in the sensitivity of serum GM, particularly among patients with hematological malignancies, primary immunodeficiencies, and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation recipients [11, 12]. Importantly, several studies have shown that prior exposure to mold-active antifungal prophylaxis or empirical antifungal therapy may significantly reduce circulating GM levels and decrease assay sensitivity [13, 14]. In line with this, a substantial proportion of our patients had received antifungal agents before diagnostic evaluation, which may partly explain the low GM positivity rate observed in our cohort. Furthermore, serum GM sensitivity is known to be lower in non-neutropenic children and in patients with chronic granulomatous disease or other immunodeficiencies, likely due to reduced angioinvasion and lower fungal burden. Previous pediatric reviews have also emphasized that the performance of serum biomarkers in children is less consistent than in adults and should always be interpreted together with clinical, radiological, and molecular findings [15, 16]. Therefore, our findings support the notion that negative serum GM results do not exclude IA, especially in pediatric patients already receiving antifungal therapy, and highlight the importance of combining GM testing with PCR and imaging modalities for diagnosis.

Although computed tomography (CT) findings play a pivotal role in early treatment decisions for IA in adults, the pediatric literature emphasizes that classic radiological signs (halo and air-crescent signs) are much rarer in children [4, 7, 17]. Thomas et al. demonstrated that non-specific nodules typically predominate at the time of diagnosis in pediatric patients [5]. Supporting this view, classic signs were detected in only 10% of our series, whereas non-specific parenchymal infiltrations were observed in 60%. This scarcity of characteristic radiological findings in our cohort reflects a well recognized challenge in pediatric IA management and highlights the limited reliability of imaging alone in children. This diagnostic ambiquity necessitates a more proactive and multimodal diagnostic approach. When imaging findings remain non-specific, clinicians may need to rely on more invasive or adjunctive diagnostic modalities, including BAL, early tissue biopsy, galactomannan testing, and highly sensitive PCR assays, in order to improve diagnostic accuracy and avoid delays in initiating life-saving antifungal therapy [1, 4]. In line with this, previous pediatric studies have shown that invasive procedures such as BAL and tissue biopsy significantly improve diagnostic yield in children with atypical radiological presentations [18, 19]. Furthermore, the importance of integrating clinical, radiological, and laboratory parameters in the evaluation of severe pediatric infections has also been emphasized in pediatric intensive care studies [20].

Iron is an indispensable cofactor for the nucleic acid synthesis and virulence of Aspergillus species, while the host attempts to sequester iron from the microorganism through the mechanism of “nutritional immunity” [21–24]. The independent correlation of hyperferritinemia with mortality observed in our study might initially be considered a reflection of severe infection-induced systemic inflammation [25]. However, the finding that ferritin levels in our series were significantly higher in the aplastic anemia (AA) group compared to other patients suggests that this condition cannot be explained solely by infection-related iron sequestration. Chronic iron overload resulting from recurrent transfusions in patients with hematological malignancies, and particularly AA, has been directly linked in the literature to the development of IA and decreased survival [26, 27]. Our data support the notion that elevated ferritin in this specific patient group symbolizes both severe bone marrow failure and an increase in labile iron fractions that the fungal pathogen can utilize. Consequently, the prognostic value of ferritin during the course of IA should be interpreted by considering the chronic iron burden and transfusion history associated with the underlying primary disease.

In the literature, hemato-oncological malignancies and prolonged neutropenia are recognized as the primary risk factors for IA [1, 2]. In our study, patients with non-malignant hematological diseases (particularly aplastic anemia) were observed to have significantly longer durations of hospitalization compared to the malignancy group. Clinical monitoring of this group revealed more profound neutropenia, lymphopenia, and thrombocytopenia, with neutropenia lasting statistically longer. This profound pancytopenia plays a critical role in increasing mortality by causing the complete collapse of the host’s neutrophilic and monocytic defense barriers against Aspergillus hyphae [5, 6]. Specifically, the recurrent erythrocyte suspension support required by these patients due to their primary disease emerges as the fundamental factor leading to elevated serum ferritin levels. Indeed, our analysis showing higher ferritin levels in the aplastic anemia group compared to other patients demonstrates that hyperferritinemia is not merely a marker of infection but also a reflection of chronic transfusion dependency and the resulting iron overload [25, 26]. This finding provides a clinical explanation for ferritin emerging as an independent risk factor in our logistic regression model, suggesting that this risk is associated with the severity of the underlying bone marrow failure rather than direct fungal virulence.

The prognostic utility of ferritin, procalcitonin (PCT), and absolute monocyte count (AMC) observed in our study aligns with the broader biomarker literature on severe systemic inflammation and pediatric sepsis, yet offers novel insights specific to invasive fungal disease. While hyperferritinemia and elevated PCT are well-established indicators of cytokine storm and secondary bacterial infections in critically ill children [25, 28], their specific roles in risk-stratifying pediatric IA have remained less defined. Our finding that a high ferritin threshold (> 3479 ng/mL) independently predicts mortality suggests that in the context of IA, ferritin serves as a dual marker of both severe iron overload—which fuels fungal virulence—and host immune exhaustion. Similarly, while procalcitonin is typically associated with bacterial co-infections, our results indicate its value as a proxy for the overall infectious burden and systemic inflammatory response in IA patients. Furthermore, the association of profound monocytopenia with poor outcomes underscores a state of ‘immune paralysis’ that has been described in severe sepsis but is particularly devastating in IA, where monocytes are crucial for inhibiting Aspergillus germination [5, 6]. By establishing specific cut-off values for these markers, our study provides an incremental contribution to the literature, transitioning from general associations of inflammation to actionable, disease-specific prognostic thresholds in pediatric invasive aspergillosis. The relationship between hyperferritinemia and mortality in pediatric IA is complex, particularly in the context of aplastic anemia. Patients with AA in our cohort exhibited the highest mortality rate (71.4%) and the highest ferritin levels, likely due to chronic transfusion dependency. While ferritin emerged as an independent factor in our model, it must be acknowledged that it likely functions as a surrogate marker for the severity of bone marrow failure and the associated exhaustion of host defenses. Nevertheless, the independent role of iron in facilitating Aspergillus virulence cannot be overlooked, as the fungus utilizes labile iron to sustain its growth and invasion. Therefore, ferritin should be interpreted as a composite biomarker reflecting both underlying disease severity and an environment conducive to fungal proliferation.

The fact that 96.7% of the patients in our study received combination therapy during follow-up can be attributed to the severe clinical course and the presence of concomitant comorbid factors. Although voriconazole monotherapy remains the first-line recommendation in guidelines, combination regimens are increasingly common in clinical practice for high-risk pediatric cases [1, 4]. While evidence regarding a definitive survival advantage remains limited [29, 30], the high rate of combination therapy in our cohort precluded a comparative analysis between different treatment regimens. In selected pediatric cases with high-risk factors such as profound neutropenia and malignancy, the combination of voriconazole and liposomal amphotericin B is frequently preferred. Observational data indicate that combination regimens are safe and generally well-tolerated in most cases; however, the lack of robust evidence demonstrating a survival advantage over monotherapy persists in the literature.

The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, its retrospective design and single-center nature may limit the generalizability of the results to different clinical settings or geographic regions. Second, the small sample size (n = 30) and the limited number of mortality events (n = 12) represent a significant statistical constraint. Although we employed a parsimonious modeling strategy to mitigate the risk of overfitting, the multivariate logistic regression results should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Third, the heterogeneity of the underlying diseases—particularly the presence of aplastic anemia—acts as a potential confounder for laboratory markers. Specifically, high ferritin levels in our cohort are heavily influenced by chronic transfusion burden and bone marrow failure severity, complicating the distinction between its role as an independent fungal risk factor and a surrogate marker for disease severity. Additionally, laboratory parameters were only evaluated at the time of diagnosis; therefore, the impact of dynamic changes in these biomarkers during antifungal therapy was not assessed. Finally, the high rate of pre-diagnosis antifungal prophylaxis may have suppressed the sensitivity of microbiological markers such as galactomannan. Despite these limitations, our study provides valuable real-world data and specific prognostic thresholds for pediatric invasive aspergillosis, a rare and high-mortality condition.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that while early diagnosis and aggressive antifungal therapy may reduce attributable mortality in pediatric IA, overall survival is primarily determined by underlying disease severity and associated complications. Laboratory biomarkers such as ferritin may provide valuable prognostic information; however, their interpretation should be individualized within the clinical context. Larger, multicenter prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and to establish robust prognostic models in pediatric invasive aspergillosis.
