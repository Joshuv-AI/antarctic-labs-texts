Virus envelope glycoprotein targeting bispecific T cell engager protects mice from lethal severe fever with thrombocytopenia virus infection
Authors: Xuefang Peng, Wei Wang, Yuexia Liang, Yi Jian, Qiaoyue Wei, Tong Yang, Chaojie Wang, Xiaohong Yin, Mingjuan Du, Hang Shang, Ruobing Liu, Xi Wang, Xing Shang, Nanxin Gong, Yanqin Xu, Chunlei Wang, Ning Cui, Leike Zhang, Yu Guo, Wei Liu, Hongkai Zhang, Hao Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummarySevere fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is an emerging hemorrhagic fever disease caused by the SFTS virus (SFTSV). Despite pandemic concerns arising from repeated instances of human-to-human transmission and a high fatality rate, effective anti-SFTSV interventions remain unavailable. Here, utilizing single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and flow cytometry data, we revealed that the deficiency and dysfunction states of T cells, particularly the impaired cytotoxicity and exhausted state of CD4+ T cells, were significantly associated with lethal consequences in SFTS patients. Using an infectious mouse model, we further observed that depletion of CD4+ T and CD8+ T cells was related to elevated viremia and increased fatality rates in SFTSV-infected mice. Accordingly, we designed virus envelope glycoprotein-targeting bispecific T cell engager (BiTE) antibodies to redirect T cells to eliminate SFTSV-infected cells, effectively rescuing mice from lethal SFTSV infection. Collectively, Gn-targeted BiTEs hold potential as a therapeutic option for treating SFTS.

Introduction

Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is a severe viral hemorrhagic fever (VHF) primarily transmitted by tick bites. The causative agent of SFTS was initially isolated in human patients in China in 20101 and named SFTS virus (SFTSV) belonging to the family Phenuiviridae within the class Bunyaviricetes. It is vital to note that the transmission of SFTSV infection has been frequently reported through direct contact with the blood or bodily fluids of SFTSV-infected patients,2,3,4 as well as direct contact with animals.5,6 In the recent decades, SFTS has been widely endemic in Asian countries, including China,7 Japan,8 South Korea,9 Vietnam,10 Pakistan,11 Myanmar,12 and Thailand,13 with a high mortality rate ranging from 12% to 50%. The rapid spread of tick vectors worldwide, the increasing numbers of human patients, and the multiple occurrences of human-to-human transmission cases have raised concerns about the potential SFTS pandemic, promoting the World Health Organization’s (WHO’s) prioritized research declaration on SFTS.14

Despite the high case fatality of SFTS, approved vaccines or specific antivirals are not currently available. While antiviral nucleotide analogs can suppress viral replication by inhibiting genome amplification, their clinical efficacy is limited and variable, as only a few antiviral drugs have been shown to reduce mortality in SFTSV-infected patients with low viremia and mild disease.15,16 Additionally, there are concerns surrounding the teratogenic potential and embryotoxicity of these antiviral nucleotide analogs. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop effective anti-SFTSV therapies.

SFTSV infection is often accompanied by the impairment of antiviral immune response.17 Our previous single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis provided a landscape of peripheral blood immune responses in surviving and deceased patients.18 Notably, T cell deficiency and exhaustion are commonly observed in patients, which may hinder the immune surveillance of antiviral T cells to eliminate virus-infected cells. Restoring SFTSV-specific T cell immune response holds promise to treat SFTSV infection. Bispecific T cell engager (BiTE) antibodies have been designed to treat infectious diseases.19,20,21 For instance, a phase I clinical trial (NCT03570918) is ongoing to evaluate the safety of a BiTE that binds to CD3 with one arm and to HIV-1 envelope glycoprotein (Env) with the other. BiTE can immediately stimulate T cells to kill virus-infected cells, and its efficacy does not depend on the development of an adaptive antiviral immune response. As a result, BiTE is a fast-acting therapy that has potential in the treatment of acute infectious diseases.

Herein, we characterized the peripheral T cells in surviving and deceased patients with SFTS and observed significant associations between the deficiency and dysfunction states of T cells and lethal consequences of the disease. These findings led us to investigate the potential of BiTEs as treatments for SFTS. The BiTE targeting the virus envelope glycoprotein Gn redirected T cells to kill virus-infected cells, leading to the elimination of SFTSV-infected cells and effectively rescuing mice infected with a lethal dose of the virus, primarily by activating interferon gamma (IFNG)-producing T cells. Therefore, a BiTE targeting the virus envelope glycoprotein Gn represents an effective therapeutic candidate for SFTS treatment.

Results

Single-cell sequencing revealed alterations in peripheral blood T cells between surviving and deceased patients with SFTS

To investigate the antiviral immune responses in the peripheral blood of patients with SFTS, we conducted a reanalysis of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from our previous study,18 encompassing 19 peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) samples isolated from 11 surviving patients (designated as SF.Sur group) and four deceased patients (designated as SF.Dea group) during the acute phase of the disease, as well as from four healthy controls (designated as HC group). A total of 88,377 cells were obtained from the 19 PBMCs following the amplification of quality control and filtering procedures. Using unsupervised clustering and canonical cell-type marker genes, we identified nine major cell types, including CD8+ T (CD3D+CD8A+), CD4+ T (CD3D+CD4+), natural killer (NK) cells (TYROBP+), B cell (CD19+CD79A+), plasma (MZB1+JCHAIN+), monocytes (CD14+CD68+), conventional type 1 dendritic cells (cDC1) (FLT3+CLEC9A+), conventional type 2 dendritic cells (cDC2) (CD1C+CLEC10A+), and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) (FLT3+CLEC4C+) (Figures 1A, S1A, and S1B). To provide an in-depth depiction of the changes in T cell populations in SFTS, we reclustered T cells into 10 subsets based on canonical markers (Figures 1C, S2A, and S2B). Five of these subsets are CD4+ T cells, including naive CD4+ T (CCR7+SELL+), IFN-I response CD4+ T (ISG15+IFI6+), helper CD4+ T (GATA3+RUNX1+), Treg (FOXP3+LGALS3+), and Th1-like cytotoxic CD4+ T (IFNG+TBX21+GZMK+GZMH+) (Figure 1D). CD8+ T cells were subdivided into four subsets, including naive CD8+ T (TCF7+IL7R+), effector CD8+ T (GZMA+GZMB+), exhausted CD8+ T (LAG3+TIGIT+), and proliferative effector CD8+ T (MKI67+GNLY+). In addition, a γδ T subset is characterized by high expression of TRGC1 and TRDC. In comparison with the HC group, naive CD4+ T, helper CD4+ T, and naive CD8+ T cells were significantly decreased in patients with SFTS, with a greater extent in the deceased patients (Figures S2C and S2D). IFN-I response CD4+ T and exhausted CD8+ T cells that were infrequently observed in the HC group were enriched in both SF.Sur and SF.Dea groups, with the highest proportion observed in the SF.Dea group (Figure S2D). Notably, survivors of SFTS displayed higher proportions of the Th1-like cytotoxic CD4+ T cells and effector CD8+ T cells compared to the HC group and deceased patients (Figure S2D).

To further delineate the developmental trajectories of T cell subpopulations, we performed separate trajectory analysis of CD4+ T and CD8+ T cells using Monocle 2. Naive CD4+ T cells differentiated into helper CD4+ T cells in the HC group; however, in patients with SFTS, a substantial portion of naive CD4+ T cells is differentiated into IFN-I response CD4+ T cells and subsequently into Treg and Th1-like cytotoxic CD4+ T cells at the terminal end (Figure 1E). Notably, naive CD4+ T cells predominantly differentiated into IFN-I response CD4+ T cells in deceased patients compared to survivors (Figure 1E), suggesting the lack of helper CD4+ T and Th1-like cytotoxic CD4+ T cells may be associated with fatal outcome of SFTS. Naive CD8+ T cells initially differentiate into effector CD8+ T cells following SFTSV infection, and a larger population of effector CD8+ T cells become exhausted in deceased patients (Figure 1F), indicating that virus infection is not efficiently resolved and that T cells become exhausted after persistently exposing to the virus.

Regarding the changes in T cell composition and developmental trajectory, we further compared the expression of immune checkpoints within T cell subsets. Our analysis revealed that a majority of T cell subsets exhibited elevated expression of multiple immune checkpoints in deceased patients (Figure 1G). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed the activation of apoptotic signaling pathway enriched in helper CD4+ T, Th1-like cytotoxic CD4+ T, and effector CD8+ T cells of deceased patients. Moreover, the exhausted CD8+ T cells in deceased patients were found to have an enhanced programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) signaling pathway. Surprisingly, the type I interferon pathway response and its upstream RIG-I like receptor signaling were elevated in numerous subsets of T cells from deceased patients. In contrast, SFTS survivors showed increased T cell activation and cytotoxicity capacity in effector CD8+ T cells (Figure 1H). These findings indicated that type I IFNs can limit T cell activation, differentiation, and survival in the deceased individuals. Collectively, the function of T cell subsets is significantly affected during the process of SFTSV infection.

Flow cytometry analysis was initially conducted to evaluate the abundance of T cells and their expression of PD-1 and TIM-3 (Figure S3). Our analysis revealed that SFTS patients displayed significantly decreased counts of T cells (Figures S4A–S4D) and reduced proportions of CD3+, CD4+, and CD8+ T cells compared to the HC group (Figure 2B). Notably, the decrease in CD3+, CD4+, and CD8+ T cell counts and the decline in CD3+ and CD4+ T cell proportions were more profound in deceased patients than in survivors of SFTS (Figures 2C and S4E–S4H), similar to observations from the scRNA-seq analysis. Moreover, we observed significantly upregulated expression of PD-1 and TIM-3 on both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in SFTS patients compared to the HC group (Figures 2D and 2F). In particular, deceased patients exhibited significantly higher proportions of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells expressing PD-1 compared to survivors during the acute phase of infection (Figures 2E and 2G). Next, we analyzed the expression of the cytotoxic markers IFNG and GZMB on T lymphocytes (Figure S5). Our results revealed significantly upregulated expression of IFNG and GZMB on both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in SFTS patients compared to the HC group (Figures 2H and S6A); moreover, survivors exhibited significantly higher expression levels of IFNG on both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Figure 2I), as well as upregulated GZMB on CD4+ T cells, compared to deceased patients (Figure S6B).

Furthermore, we investigated the relationship between the serum viral loads in SFTS patients and the expression levels of PD-1, IFNG, and GZMB on T lymphocytes. A higher viral load trended toward a positive correlation with higher proportions of CD4+PD-1+ T and CD8+PD-1+ T cells (Figures 2J and 2K). Conversely, viral load showed a trend toward a negative correlation with the proportions of CD4+IFNG+ T and CD8+IFNG+ T cells (Figures 2L–2M). Similar negative correlation was also observed between viral load and CD4+GZMB+ T cells but not CD8+GZMB+ subset (Figures S6C and S6D). Taken together, these findings suggest that SFTSV can suppress T cell immunity.

Depletion of T cells is associated with elevated viremia and increased mortality in mouse models

In the context of SFTSV models, it is observed that mice typically experience weight loss between 3 and 7 days post-infection (dpi), subsequently entering a recovery phase where they regain weight by 7–10 dpi. A threshold of 20% weight loss, but not natural death, is utilized as a benchmark to predict the survival or fatal outcome of the infection in mice. To investigate the impact of depleting conventional CD4+ T cells or CD8+ T cells on the pathogenesis of SFTSV, a lower dose of SFTSV (2 × 104 focus-forming units [FFUs] per mouse) was used so that most mice survive on 6–8 dpi, and CTL immunity has been elicited robustly by that time (Figure S7). CD4+ T or CD8+ T cells were depleted by treatment with anti-mouse CD4 antibody (300 μg per mouse) or anti-mouse CD8a antibody (300 μg per mouse) after the viral infection (Figure S8A). By monitoring the survival rates over a 12-day period, it was found that all the mice with the depletion of either CD4+ T or CD8+ T cells succumbed to SFTSV infection. In contrast, only 50% of the mice without depletion of T cells died from the infection (Figure S8B). Additionally, depleting CD4+ T or CD8+ T cells exhibited a significant increase in viral loads in serum and viral titers in spleen samples (Figures S8C and S8D). These results suggest that CD4+ T and CD8+ T cells may play some defensive role against viremia and viral spread, thereby supporting the dysfunction and deficiency of T cells, contributing to the pathogenesis of SFTSV.

In order to isolate Gn-binding antibodies, the biopanning of phage display scFv library against biotinylated Gn protein was performed, and Gn-binding antibodies were identified using phage ELISA (Figure S9A). Eight unique Gn-binding antibodies were obtained (Figure S9B). However, none of these monoclonal antibodies displayed neutralizing activity against SFTSV, while MAb4-5, which is a well-known SFTSV neutralizing antibody,22 can effectively inhibit virus propagation in vitro (Figure S9C). Among the BiTEs constructed from three antibodies with the highest Gn-binding affinity, 3A5-derived BiTE molecule significantly suppressed SFTSV infection (Figure S9D).

We further produced and characterized 3A5 BiTE and MAb4-5-derived BiTE, as well as an irrelevant Claudin18.2-targeting BiTE as negative control (Figure 3B). ELISA and surface plasmon resonance (SPR)-based sandwich assay results showed that 3A5 BiTE and MAb4-5 BiTE remained their ability to bind with Gn and CD3 (Figures 3C and S9E). Next, we investigated whether the anti-Gn/anti-CD3 BiTEs could induce T-cell-mediated cell killing activity to inhibit SFTSV infection in Huh-7 cells. SFTSV was inoculated with Huh-7 cells and co-cultured with T cells in presence or absence of BiTEs. Theoretically, anti-Gn/anti-CD3 BiTEs could recruit T cells to kill Gn-expressing SFTSV-infected cells, resulting in inhibition of virus replication in the infected cells. The results showed that 3A5-BiTE-treated Huh-7 cells exhibited superior resistance to SFTSV, and fewer cells were killed by virus infection (Figure S9F). The expression of SFTSV nucleocapsid protein (Np) was significantly reduced by 3A5 BiTE treatment compared to Claudin18.2 BiTE control (Figure 3D). Moreover, SFTSV viral titer in the supernatants of the cell coculture system was about 10-fold lower in 3A5 BiTE group compared to Claudin18.2 BiTE group (Figure S9G). Notably, MAb4-5-derived BiTE demonstrated no T-cell-mediated antiviral effect. When the co-culture system was supplemented with an anti-IFNG antibody, T-cell-mediated cell killing activity induced by 3A5 BiTE was significantly suppressed; however, neither GZMB nor perforin inhibitors exhibited such inhibitory effects (Figures 3E and 3F). Taken together, these findings support that 3A5 BiTE effectively inhibits SFTSV replication predominantly through IFNG-dependent T cell responses.

BiTEs demonstrated protective effects against lethal SFTSV infection in vivo

To assess the therapeutic effects of BiTEs against SFTSV infection in vivo, CD3-humanized C57BL/6J mice were pretreated with anti-IFNAR1 antibody (300 μg per mouse) one day prior to challenge with a high dose of SFTSV (4 × 104 FFUs per mouse). Following infection, the mice were treated with various BiTE for three times (Figure 3G). After a 12-day observation period, we noted that 3A5 BiTE treatment significantly improved the survival rate of SFTSV-infected mice to 83.3%, in contrast to the survival rate of 16.7% observed in mice treated with PBS, control BiTE, or MAb4-5 BiTE (Figure 3H). Notably, 3A5 BiTE treatment significantly decreased viral loads in serum and tissues (including spleen, liver, and lung) by one to two orders of magnitude (Figures 3I–3L). Additionally, the splenic enlargement induced by SFTSV infection was markedly alleviated, approaching the levels observed in uninfected mice (Figure 3M). Further histopathological analysis revealed that 3A5 BiTE treatment improved splenic pathological changes, including white pulp atrophy and increased megakaryocyte counts (Figure 3N). These findings demonstrate the efficacy of 3A5 BiTE in protecting mice against lethal SFTSV infection.

BiTE treatment provided therapeutic effects against lethal SFTSV infection dependent of IFNG production by T cells

To investigate whether 3A5 BiTE exerts its anti-SFTSV effects by regulating the T cell response, we conducted a flow cytometry analysis on spleen lymphocytes from mice treated with 3A5 BiTE or PBS in a prophylactic setting. Our analysis revealed that 3A5 BiTE treatment significantly increased the proportion and cell counts of CD4+ T, CD8+ T, and CD19+ B cells, while decreasing the proportions and cell counts of CD11b+Gr-1+ neutrophils and Foxp3+ Tregs, compared to the PBS control group (Figures 4A and S10). In addition, 3A5 BiTE treatment markedly upregulated the expression of IFNG in both CD4+ T and CD8+ T cells (Figures S11A and S11B). Theoretically, BiTEs can redirect T cells to counteract SFTSV infection while bypassing the induction of antigen-specific responses. We therefore investigated whether BiTEs could elicit virus-specific T cell responses. To further assess the impact of CD4+ T or CD8+ T cells on the therapeutic efficacy of 3A5 BiTE, we depleted these cell types in mice and evaluated the outcome (Figure 4B). Briefly, CD3-humanized C57BL/6J mice were pretreated with anti-IFNAR1 antibody (300 μg per mouse) one day prior to challenge with SFTSV (2 × 104 FFUs per mouse), and 3A5 BiTE treatment was initiated at one day after the viral infection. After a 12-day observation period, we found that 3A5 BiTE treatment significantly improved the survival rate of SFTSV-infected mice, while the therapeutic benefit was abolished in the mice lacking CD4+ T or CD8+ T cells (Figure 4C). Compared to mice treated with PBS, those treated with 3A5 BiTE exhibited a significant reduction in viral titers in their spleen samples, while this trend was reversed in mice with the depletion of CD4+ T or CD8+ T cells (Figure 4D). Furthermore, histopathological examination of the spleen samples from the 3A5 BiTE-treated mice with CD4+ or CD8+ T cells depletion showed pathological changes that were similar to or even more severe than those observed in PBS-treated mice (Figure 4E).

Then we evaluated the contribution of IFNG to 3A5 BiTE’s antiviral activity in CD3 humanized C57BL/6J mice. Treatment with an anti-IFNG neutralizing antibody abrogated the therapeutic effects of 3A5 BiTE in mice, as evidenced by the loss of survival benefit and reduction in viremia (Figure S12). These in vivo results align with our in vitro findings, indicating that the therapeutic effect of 3A5 BiTE against lethal SFTSV infection is primarily mediated by IFNG production by T cells.

We further analyzed the T cell compartment and identified five CD4+ T cell subpopulations and five CD8+ T cell subpopulations based on their transcriptomic signature (Figures 5D and 5E). In comparison with control BiTE, 3A5 BiTE treatment resulted in a significant increase in follicular helper CD4+ T, Th1, and proliferative CD4+ T, central memory CD8+ T, and effector CD8+ T cells, as well as a significant decrease in naive CD4+ T, Treg, naive CD8+ T, and exhausted CD8+ T cells (Figures 5F and S14), suggesting that the differentiation process of naive T cells into effector T cells was also enhanced. Pathway enrichment analysis showed that T cell activation and cytotoxicity pathways were upregulated in CD4+ T (Th1 and proliferative CD4+ T) and CD8+ T (effector CD8+ T and central memory CD8+ T) cells after 3A5 BiTE treatment (Figure 5G), consistent with the results from flow cytometry analysis (Figure S11A). In addition, cell proliferation was reduced in Tfh, effector CD8+ T, central memory CD8+ T, and exhausted CD8+ T cells, showing that 3A5 BiTE treatment promotes the T cell effector responses while inhibiting T cell memory. During 3A5 BiTE treatment, the responsiveness to type I IFN diminished in various subsets of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. CellChat analysis further revealed that 3A5 BiTE treatment enhanced the communications between CD4+ T cells and B cells (Figure 5H), which were identified as major SFTSV-infected cells in spleens.23 Co-immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that 3A5 treatment alleviated SFTSV-induced damage of spleen follicle, characterized by increased B cells (CD19+) and CD4+ T cells and more organized aggregates of B cell and CD4+ T cells (Figure 5I). Taken together, these results indicate that 3A5 BiTE treatment induces cytotoxic CD4+ T and CD8+ effector T cells and ameliorates T cells exhaustion, supporting the critical role of T cells in inhibiting viral propagation.

Discussion

In this study, we characterized the peripheral T cells in SFTS patients with survival or fatal outcomes and found that the deficiency and dysfunction of T cells, particularly the impaired cytotoxicity and exhaustion of CD4+ T cells, were associated with lethal consequences of this disease. In order to invigorate T cell immune response against the virus, we designed a Gn-targeted BiTE that exhibited robust therapeutic efficacy against SFTSV infection.

Previous reports have characterized SFTS by a severe loss of T cells and a marked increase in PD-1 levels in T lymphocytes.24 We reanalyzed our previously reported scRNA-seq data from a cohort of SFTS patients.18 Our analysis revealed a significantly higher exhaustion module score in SFTSV-infected patients compared to healthy controls, with the highest exhaustion score observed in the deceased SFTS patients. In this current study, we validated these findings at the protein level through flow cytometry analysis in an independent cohort of SFTS patients, demonstrating that exhaustion and dysfunction of CD4+ T cells were correlated with severe disease outcomes.

During viral acute infections, type I interferons (IFN-Is) have a variety of biological consequences.25 Precise regulation of IFN-I signaling is critical for combating infections while averting autoimmunity.26 The prevailing view of IFN-I’s function in viral infections emphasizes its role in suppressing viral replication. The significance of an early antiviral IFN-I response in countering viral invasion has been underscored during the COVID-19 pandemic.27 In non-severe cases of SARS-CoV-2 infection, the interferon response can even precede viral detection by PCR. In numerous animal models, deletion of IFNAR or downstream IFN-I signaling pathways promotes viral replication following acute infection. IFN-I also has a co-stimulatory effect on T cell activation, differentiation, expansion, and killing of virus-infected cells.28,29 However, increased studies have shown that IFN-I signaling has unexpectedly harmful roles during acute viral infections.30 IFN-I signaling can enhance morbidity through activation of abnormal inflammatory responses including production of many pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, during acute viral infection. In addition to its immunopathogenic effects, IFN-I exhibits potent immunomodulatory activity that is contingent on the timing of IFN-I exposure relative to T cell receptor signaling.31,32,33,34,35 Our previous study identified that the overexpression of IFN-stimulated genes across most immune cell types after SFTSV infection was related to high viremia and a hyperinflammatory response. Clinical trials have not shown efficacy of IFN-I in the treatment of SFTS.18 Elucidating the mechanisms underlying the association between interferon response and severe disease outcomes is expected to yield crucial insights for targeting this pathway in the treatment of SFTS. We found that the expression level of USP18 was significantly higher in deceased patients than in survivors.18 The inefficiency of interferon response in controlling SFTSV infection may be attributed to the upregulation of negative regulators such as USP1.36

Tregs can exert immunoregulatory effects on the activation and effector functions of innate and adaptive immune cells through various mechanisms, resulting in increased viral loads and mortality.37,38 However, we did not find a statistically significant difference in the proportion of Tregs between the SF.Sur (survivors) and SF.Dea (deceased) groups. Treg cells also play an important role in preventing immunopathogenic reactions during acute infection.39,40 Therefore, there is a safety risk in targeting Tregs for antiviral treatment since this may result in Tregs losing their protective function when present in inflammatory environments.

Reversing CD8+ T cell dysfunction is critical for the treatment of viral infections. Very recently, Ji et al. reported that treatment with an anti-PD-1 antibody showed modest virus inhibition efficacy in a human-immune-system-reconstituted mouse model.41 Andreata et al. identified a subset of CD8+ T cells in chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection that exhibits a form of dysfunction distinct from classic exhaustion. While blocking co-inhibitory receptors showed minimal effects, agonism of 4-1BB demonstrated the potential to rejuvenate these dysfunctional CD8+ T cells, thereby holding promise for the treatment of chronic HBV infection.42 Andreata et al. developed a cis-targeted CD8-IL2 fusion protein to selectively stimulate dysfunctional CD8+ T cells in chronic HBV infection. This intervention led to increased production of IFNG and GZMB, providing direct evidence of enhanced functionality.42 Consistently, our study showed that PD-1 blockade, as well as 4-1BB or OX40 agonism, modestly reduced viral loads and protected mice from infection-related mortality (data not shown). The antiviral effect of immune checkpoint blockade or costimulatory receptor agonism depends on the development of an adaptive virus-specific T cell response. However, this response may not be sufficiently elicited by the time of onset of death, thereby compromising the effect of these therapies. This recapitulated the fact that patients experienced a rapid worsening of symptoms following viral infection. Despite the efficacy of ICIs, there is an urgent need for therapies that can immediately inhibit viral replication or eradicate the virus. T cell engagers such as 3A5 BiTE represent a promising strategy to address this need by redirecting T cells to kill target cells immediately upon administration of the therapy. Additionally, this therapeutic strategy can target SFTSV-infected B cells, potentially reversing the impairment of B cell immunity such as antigen-presenting function and humoral immune response,18 thereby improving prognosis. Although the current mouse model recapitulates the rapid progression of SFTSV infection and impaired T and B cells after infection, it remains to be determined how long after infection the administration of BiTEs can effectively stop SFTSV replication and spread in infected patients because of differences in viral susceptibility and immune systems between mice and humans.

Both in vitro and in vivo results of the current study indicate that 3A5 BiTE exerts its antiviral effect primarily through the activation of IFNG-producing T cells. A key role for IFNG in T-cell-based immunotherapy has been highlighted in the context of solid tumors, acute myeloid leukemia, and chronic viral infection.42,43,44 IFNG may promote T cell recruitment, induce bystander killing, and activate the STING signaling pathway, thereby enhancing T cell-mediated cytotoxicity. Although GZMB usually contributes to the capacity of virus-specific T cells to eliminate virus-infected and tumor cells, our results indicated that the antiviral efficacy of BiTEs is independent of GZMB. This finding suggests the existence of mechanisms that inhibit GZMB-mediated killing of infected cells. Similar granzyme resistance has been reported in other infectious contexts. HIV-1 RNA-positive T cells expressing BCL2 and serine protease inhibitor 9 (SERPINB9) exhibit resistance to cell death.45 Similarly, the induction of SERPINB9 in alveolar macrophages by Mycobacterium tuberculosis inhibits apoptosis and promotes the survival of infected macrophages, through protecting against GZMB-triggered cellular damage.46

The efficacy of BiTE can likely be improved by replacing the current non-neutralizing Gn-binding scFv with a neutralizing antibody-derived scFv. Nevertheless, the epitope is of importance for the proper orientation and distance between T cells and infected cells. For example, we observed that BiTE derived from the neutralizing antibody MAb4-5have no T-cell-mediated virus inhibition activity. The BiTE molecules had a short serum half-life, necessitating administration by continuous IV infusion to maintain therapeutic serum concentrations as exemplified by blinatumomab.47 To address this, fusing BiTE to the Fc region has been shown to effectively extend its half-life.48 A similar approach can potentially be utilized in the design of anti-SFTSV BiTEs. Notably, the therapeutic opportunities of T cell engagers may be limited by significant toxicities such as cytokine release syndrome. In order to decouple the efficacy from the toxicity, efforts have been made to generate T cell engagers with lower affinity for the target CD3.49

In summary, the deficiency and dysfunction of T cells are associated with fatal outcome in SFTS patients. T-cell-centered immunotherapies such as virus-glycoprotein-specific T cell engager can reinvigorate T cells to kill SFTSV-infected cells, providing a promising therapy for the treatment of SFTS.

Limitations of the study

The limited number of fatal cases (12 deaths vs. 26 survivors) may compromise the statistical power of the analysis. Studies involving larger patient cohorts with continuous flow cytometry performed on samples collected throughout the entire disease course are required to validate the observed alterations in T cell counts and functions and to further elucidate the distinct roles of T cell subsets in the pathogenesis of SFTS. While the roles of IFNG, GZMB, and perforin in the antiviral activity of the 3A5 BiTE have been explored via pharmacological inhibition, CD3-humanized mice deficient in perforin, granzymes, or IFNG can provide unequivocal evidence to clarify whether perforin and granzymes are indispensable effector molecules that mediate the antiviral efficacy of BiTEs.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human subjects and specimen collection

A cohort study was performed at the Xinyang 154th Hospital, a designated hospital for SFTS in Xinyang city of Henan province, China, to identify the role of T cell response in SFTSV infection. A total of 38 adult patients with laboratory-confirmed SFTSV infection, including 26 surviving patients and 12 deceased patients, were sampled from May 6 to July 28, 2022, for flow cytometry analysis. Eight healthy controls who had comparable age and were excluded for SFTSV infection, were recruited as controls at the study period. Peripheral blood specimens were collected, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated for flow cytometry analysis. Serum samples were collected for viral quantification. The clinical protocol was reviewed and approved by the Committee for Ethical Review of the Xinyang 154th Hospital. All participants provided written informed consent for sample collection and subsequent analysis.

Animal models

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the Academy of Military Medical Sciences (IACUC-IME-2021-003). Six-week-old C57BL/6J were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., and humanized CD3e C57BL/6 mice (Cat. NO. NM-HU-00114) were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. Mice were housed under specific-pathogen-free conditions with daily cycles of 12 h light/12 h darkness, temperature of 22°C, and humidity of 50%. For the survival monitoring, mice were euthanized upon reaching the institutionally approved endpoint of weight loss of 20% or the study endpoint (12 days post-infection).

Cell lines

Vero and Raw264.7 cells were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), Huh-7 cells were purchased from the China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC). All the cells were cultured in the Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. HEK293FT cells were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific, and maintained in Freestyle 293 Expression Medium. All cell lines were regularly tested for mycoplasma contamination using Mycoplasma test kit.

Method details

Single-cell RNA-seq data analysis

The PBMC scRNA-seq dataset previously published for patients with SFTS (GEO accession: GSE175499) has undergone reanalysis. We reanalyzed the scRNA-seq data from 19 PBMC samples obtained from 15 SFTS patients, comprising 4 deceased individuals (SF.Dea group), 11 survivors (SF.Sur group), and 4 healthy controls (HC group).

The gene-by-cell barcode matrices were incorporated into the Seurat pipeline52 (version 5.0.1) to perform quality control, data normalization, dimensionality reduction, and clustering analyses. Cells exhibiting suboptimal quality were filtered out based on the following criteria: fewer than 100 genes per cell, more than 7,000 genes per cell, more than 50,000 UMIs (Unique Molecular Identifiers) per cell, and more than 20% mitochondrial content. Afterward, the top 2000 highly variable genes were chosen for principal component analysis (PCA), which was employed to reduce the dimensionality of the single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-Seq) dataset. We used the R package Harmony (version 1.0) to remove batch effects between samples.53 Dimensionality reduction and clustering were performed using the top 18 principal components and a resolution parameter of 0.2. Cells were then projected onto a two-dimensional space using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Differentially expressed genes for each cell cluster were identified using the FindMarkers function implemented in Seurat. Then, using the identified differentially expressed genes and known marker gene expression, each primary cell cluster was manually classified into known cell types.

The CD3+ T cells were isolated from the entire dataset and then subjected to independent downstream clustering analysis. The top 21 principal components were utilized for dimensional reduction and clustering, along with a resolution parameter set to 0.3. The T cell subsets were annotated based on the differentially expressed genes related to T cell function.

We calculated the average expression of genes within each cell types and utilized the R package ComplexHeatmap56 (version 2.8.0) to plot heatmaps, showcasing and comparing the average gene expression across different cell types.

We used Monocle55 (version 2.30.0) to infer the potential differentiation trajectories of CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells separately. The NewCellDataSet function was employed to generate a new Monocle object using the gene count matrix and the associated cell type information from the Seurat objects. We utilized the results from the estimateSizeFactors and estimateDispersions functions to normalize for variations in mRNA abundance across cells and facilitate subsequent differential gene expression analysis. The 1000 genes with the lowest q-values (FDR adjusted p-values), as defined by the differentialGeneTest function, were selected to define the cell trajectory. We employed the ReduceDimension function to achieve dimensionality reduction using the DDRTree method, while the orderCells function arranged the cells based on selected gene expression. The plot_cell_trajectory function is used to visualize the inferred cell trajectory and to depict the differentiation relationships among various T cell subpopulations.

Gene signature analysis between different cell groups

Gene set enrichment analysis57 was used to find the enriched pathways between different cell groups by the R package WebGestaltR54 v0.4.4. and Gene Ontology (GO), KEGG, Reactome, and the blood transcription modules (BTM) pathways58 were used in the GSEA analysis. And genes were ranked by log2-fold change using the FindMarkers function in Seurat, with 1,000 permutations employed to estimate the FDR for GSEA analysis.

Flow cytometry analysis of human PBMCs

The PBMCs were isolated from the patient’s blood as described previously.18 In brief, PBMCs were isolated via density gradient centrifugation with FicollPaque Plus. Then the PBMCs were analyzed by flow cytometry with florescent-conjugated cell surface markers, including FITC labeled anti-human CD45, PerCP/Cyanine5.5 anti-human CD3, PE anti-human CD4, PE/Dazzle 594 anti-human CD8a, Brilliant Violet 650 anti-human CD279 (PD-1), and Pacific Blue anti-human CD366 (Tim-3). For intracellular staining, cells were fixed for 30 min at room temperature by Fixation Buffer, then washed three times with Intracellular Staining Perm Wash Buffer, and stained with Pacific Blue anti-human/mouse Granzyme B and APC anti-human IFNG. Cell populations were analyzed with FACS Flow Cytometer (Beckman, CytoFLEX S) and FlowJo software (BD Biosciences).

Depletion of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in vivo

Six-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were pretreated with anti-IFNAR1 antibody (300 μg per mouse) and then intraperitoneally inoculated with SFTSV (2 × 104 FFUs per mouse) one day later. One hour after infection, mice were intraperitoneally injected with anti-mouse CD4 antibody (300 μg per mouse) or anti-mouse CD8a antibody (300 μg per mouse) for depletion of CD4+ or CD8+ T cells. Administration of anti-mouse CD4 antibody and anti-mouse CD8a antibody were also performed at 2 and 4 days after the viral infection. Serum and spleen samples were collected at 6 days post-infection and subjected to detection of viral loads and viral titers using RT-qPCR and immunological focus assay, respectively.

Phage display

Panning of naive single-chain variable fragment (scFv) library against Gn protein were performed as described previously.59 The phage library was incubated with biotinylated Gn-His fusion protein for 2 h at room temperature, and the phage-antigen complex was captured by Dynabeads M280 (Invitrogen). Bound phages were eluted using Glycine-HCl (pH 2.2) for 10 min at room temperature and neutralized with Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) to adjust the pH to 7.5. The eluted phages were amplified for the next round of bio-panning. After three rounds of panning, individual clones of phage were prepared and Gn binding positive clones were identified by phage ELISA with the signal-to-background ratio was higher than 2. The phagemid DNA was isolated from bacterial infected by these positive clones using a plasmid miniprep kit (QIAGEN).

Cloning and production of Gn-targeted 3A5 BiTE

The BiTEs were constructed by fusing Gn-binding scFvs (including 3A5 or MAb4-5 derived scFv) or Zolbetuximab derived Claudin18.2-binding scFv to Muromonab derived CD3-specific scFv via a glycine-serine (G4S)3 linker. BiTE molecule contained a His tag at the C-termini. The BiTEs were cloned into mammalian expression plasmid pTT5 vector (NovoPro). The mammalian expression plasmids were transfected into HEK293F to express BiTEs for 5 days in Freestyle medium. BiTE proteins were purified using HisTrap HP columns (GE Healthcare) and the purified proteins were buffer exchanged into PBS solution.

RT-qPCR assay

Viral RNA was extracted from serum or tissues like spleens, livers and lungs with the RNAprep TIANamp Virus RNA Kit (TIANGEN) and RNA Easy Fast Tissue/cell Kit (TIANGEN), respectively, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Viral RNA was measured using RT-qPCR. The primers and probe were designed based on the S segment of the SFTSV and listed in key resources table.

Immunological focus assay

Vero cell monolayers were infected with supernatant of SFTSV-infected samples and incubated under a methylcellulose overlay. Following 72 h post-infection, Vero cell monolayers were fixed with 4% formaldehyde in PBS, permeabilized by incubation with 0.5% Triton X-100 in balanced salt solution. Cells were then stained with rabbit anti-NP antibody and horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled secondary antibody, and the viral focus forming units were stained by an enhanced HRP-DAB (3,3′ Diaminobenzidine Tetrahydrochloride) chromogenic substrate kit (TIANGEN).

Flow cytometry analysis of SFTSV NP

Intracellular SFTSV NP expression was detected as previously described.51 In brief, cells were fixed with the Fixation Buffer 20 min at 4°C and washed twice with Permeabilization Solution. Then, the cells were stained with an anti-mouse SFTSV NP antibody (diluted 1:1000) overnight at 4°C. After washed three times, cells were incubated with FITC conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (diluted 1:1000) as a second antibody at 4°C for 1 h. The stained cells were detected using the Novocyte 3310 System (Agilent Technologies). Samples were analyzed using NovoExpress software (Agilent Technologies) or FlowJo software (BD Biosciences).

Induction of T cell mediated lysis of SFTSV-infected cells by BsAbs

Human T cells were isolated from PBMC using EasySepHuman T cell Enrichment Kit (STEMCELL), and cultured with IL-2 (100 U/mL) for 24 h. Huh-7 cells were seeded into 24-well plates at 5×104 cells the day before infection. Then Huh-7 cells were infected with SFTSV-HBMC16 strain with an MOI of 0.2 for 2 h, washed with PBS, and co-cultured with enriched human T cells at an effector: target ratio of 1: 1 in the presence of different BiTEs. After 48 h incubation, the cell supernatants were collected for detection of the viral titer, and the lysis of the infected cells was measured using flow cytometry after staining with Ghost Dye Violet 510 and an anti-mouse SFTSV NP antibody. Samples were analyzed using NovoExpress software (Agilent Technologies).

3A5 BiTEs protective efficacy in vivo

Six-to eight-week-old humanized CD3e C57BL/6 mice were pretreated with anti-mouse IFNAR-1 antibody (300 μg per mouse) at day −1, and then intraperitoneally inoculated with SFTSV (4 × 104 FFUs per mouse) at day 0. Two hours after infection, mice were intraperitoneally injected with 3A5 BiTE, MAb4-5 BiTE, control BiTE (10 mg/kg for each mouse) or the equal volume of PBS every 2 days for three times. For pathological detection, at 6 days post-infection, mice were sacrificed and tissues as well as serum were collected to measure viral titers using immunological focus assay and viral loads using RT-qPCR. Besides, spleen samples were fixed and stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and immunohistochemistry (IHC). Spleen samples were also subjected to flow cytometry analysis to examine the proportions of lymphocyte subsets.

ELISpot assays

Antigen-specific T cell responses were assessed using a mouse IFNG precoated ELISPOT kit. In brief, splenocytes were isolated from SFTSV-infected humanized CD3e C57BL/6J mice with or without 3A5 BiTE treatment. A total of 2 × 105 splenocytes were stimulated with either 2 × 104 SFTSV-infected Raw264.7 cells or heat-inactivated SFTSV (MOI = 10) for 24 hours at 37°C under 5% CO2. After incubation, the plate was washed and sequentially incubated with biotinylated antibody and streptavidin-HRP for 1 h at 37°C. Color development was performed using AEC substrate, the spots, representing virus antigen-specific T cells, were counted with an IRIS FluoroSpot/ELISpot reader (Mabtech).

Effects of interferon gamma, granzyme B, and perforin blockade on the antiviral activity of 3A5 BiTEs in vitro

T cells were expanded by stimulating PBMCs with ImmunoCult Human CD3/CD28 T cell Activator for 2 days, followed by culture in supplemented with IL-2 (100 U/mL) for 7 days. Huh-7 cells were seeded into 24-well plates (5 × 104 cells per well), infected with SFTSV (MOI = 0.2) for 2 h, and then co-cultured with T cells at an effector-to-target ratio of 1: 1. The co-culture was maintained either in the presence or absence of 3A5 BiTEs, with additional supplementation of either a granzyme B inhibitor (20 μg/mL), an IFNG neutralization antibody (0.5 μg/mL) or a perforin inhibitor (10 μM). After 48 h incubation, the SFTSV infection rates in cells and viral titers in the supernatants were assessed as above described.

Effect of interferon gamma blockade on the antiviral activity of 3A5 BiTEs in vivo

Six-to eight-week-old humanized CD3e C57BL/6 mice were pretreated with anti-mouse IFNAR-1 antibody, intraperitoneally inoculated with SFTSV (4 × 104 FFUs per mouse). On days 1, 3, and 5 post infection, mice received intraperitoneal injections of 3A5 BiTE, followed by either an anti-IFNG antibody (100 μg per mouse) or a perforin inhibitor (100 mg/kg for each mouse). Survival rates were monitored for 12 days post-infection. Serum and spleen samples were collected at day 6 post-infection and analyzed for viral titers using an immunological focus assay.

Effect of T cell depletion on the therapeutic efficacy of 3A5 BiTEs in vivo

Six-to eight-week-old male humanized CD3e C57BL/6 mice were pretreated with anti-IFNAR1 antibody (300 μg per mouse) and then intraperitoneally inoculated with SFTSV (2 × 104 FFUs per mouse) one day later. 3A5 BiTE treatment was administered at 1, 3, and 5 days after the viral infection. Strategies for the depletion of CD4+ or CD8+ T cells were carried out as mentioned above. For the survival monitoring, mice were euthanized upon reaching the institutionally approved endpoint of weight loss of 20% or the study endpoint (12 days post-infection). Spleen samples were collected at 6 days post-infection and subjected to immunological focus assay, flow cytometry analysis, H&E and IHC analysis.

Histopathology and immunohistology

Spleen samples were collected from the mice, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or performed with immunohistochemistry (IHC) using primary antibodies against anti-CD4 (D7D2Z) Rabbit mAb or anti-CD8 (D4W2Z). Immunohistochemical analysis of SFTSV was performed with a rabbit polyclonal antibody against SFTSV NP.18 Immunofluorescence staining of spleen sections were performed with use of anti-CD4 (D4W2Z) Rabbit mAb and anti-CD19 rabbit polyclonal antibody; cell nuclei were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Images were captured using an Olympus fluorescence microscope.

Flow cytometry analysis of mouse splenocytes

Place minced pieces of spleens in the 70 μm cell strainer and gently dissociate tissue through the strainer by pressing in a circular motion with rubber plunger of 10 mL sterile syringe. Then erythrocytes were lysed with red blood cell lysis buffer. Single cells were obtained using a 40 μm cell strainer. For multicolor fluorescence-conjugated antibody staining, cells were blocked with αCD16/32 antibody and stained with the Zombie Aqua Fixable Viability Kit to distinguish dead cells. Cell surface markers were stained with specific antibodies for 30 min at 4°C in the dark. Intracellular markers were stained with specific antibodies for 45 min at 4°C after the cells were fixed and permeabilized by using permeabilization buffer. Cells were detected using an LSR Fortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and analyzed using FlowJo software (BD Biosciences).

Preparation for scRNA-Seq library

Three days after the last antibody injection, single cell suspension of mouse splenocytes were prepared as described above. Excess erythrocytes were removed with the red blood cell lysis buffer, then the remaining cells were resuspended in RPMI-1640/2% FBS, and their viability was measured using Trypan blue. After the removal of dead cells using the Dead Cell Removal Kit, CD45+ immune cells were isolated from single-cell suspensions using CD45 (TIL) Microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were used for scRNA-Seq library construction when more than 85% of cells were in good condition, as determined by bench blue staining. The single-cell sequencing was performed using Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3ʹ Reagent Kits v3.1 (10× Genomics) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing was performed on NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina).

scRNA-seq analysis of immune cells from mouse spleens

scRNA-seq data analysis was performed by NovelBio Co.,Ltd. with NovelBrain Cloud Analysis Platform. The feature-barcode matrices were obtained by aligning reads to the mouse genome (mm10 Ensemble: version 100) using CellRanger v7.1.0. The HTODemux function in Seurat, with default parameters, was utilized to assign each cell to its original sample based on normalized hashtag counts. Cells contained over 600 expressed genes and mitochondria UMI rate below 10% passed the cell quality filtering and mitochondria genes were removed in the expression table. Seurat package was used for cell normalization and regression based on the expression table according to the UMI counts of each sample and percent of mitochondria rate to obtain the scaled data. PCA was constructed based on the scaled data with top 2000 high variable genes and top 10 principal components were used for UMAP construction. Utilizing graph-based cluster method, we acquired the unsupervised cell cluster result based the PCA top 10 principal components and we calculated the marker genes by FindAllMarkers function. The CD3+ T cells were isolated and underwent dimensionality reduction using the top 15 principal components. The T cell subpopulations were then annotated based on the differentially expressed genes related to T cell function. The AddModuleScore function was employed in Seurat with default settings to compute the score of each cell for a specifically defined gene set within different cell groups.

The CellChat package (version 1.1.3) was utilized to perform ligand-receptor analyses among CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, B cells and plasmas following Seurat analysis. The createCellChat function was used to convert the Seurat object into CellChat objects for both of the two groups: Control BiTE and 3A5 BiTE. We constructed cell communication networks using the computeCommunProb function. To examine differences in ligand-receptor interaction strength across different groups, interaction comparison analysis was conducted using the netVisual_diffInteraction functions.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Continuous variables were described as means and standard deviations (SDs) accorded with normal distribution or as medians and interquartile ranges accorded with nonnormal distribution, and categorical variables were described as frequencies and proportions. Student’s t test or nonparametric test (Mann-Whitney test) was used for comparisons of continuous variables between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used for comparison of continuous variables among multiple groups; chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for comparisons of categorical variables. r was estimated for the correlation analysis of two continuous variables. The Kaplan–Meier method and the log rank test were used to analyze time-to-event data for treatment effect analysis. A 2-sided p value <0.05 was considered as statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 19.0.
