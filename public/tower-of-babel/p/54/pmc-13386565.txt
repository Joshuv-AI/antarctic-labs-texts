Cost-effectiveness of intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) vs. angiography-guided revascularization in femoropopliteal occlusive disease
Authors: Pablo E Bretos-Azcona, Patricia Ruiz Gay-Pobes, Lucas Alvarez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesIntravascular ultrasound (IVUS)-guided revascularization offers clinical benefits compared to angiography-guided revascularization in patients with femoropopliteal occlusive disease. This study evaluates the cost-effectiveness of IVUS-guided versus angiography-guided revascularization from a Spanish payer perspective.MethodsA cost-effectiveness model combining a short-term decision tree and a long-term semi-Markov structure was developed to simulate clinical outcomes and costs over a lifetime horizon from the Spanish perspective. Transition probabilities, costs, and utility weights for health states including reintervention, amputation and death were derived from published literature and Spanish national databases. Health outcomes were measured in quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs), and costs were reported in 2025 Euros. One-way and probabilistic sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess model robustness.ResultsIVUS-guided revascularization was associated with improved clinical outcomes and reduced lifetime costs, dominating angiography-guided revascularization. In the base case, IVUS yielded 0.29 additional lifetime QALYs and €3,289 in lifetime cost savings per patient compared with angiography, resulting in a dominance of IVUS. Scenario analysis assuming equal amputation risk still favored IVUS. One-way sensitivity analysis identified the reintervention risk and costs as key drivers, and probabilistic sensitivity analysis confirmed that IVUS dominated in over 90% of simulations.ConclusionIVUS-guided revascularization is a cost-effective strategy for femoropopliteal occlusive disease in Spain, offering superior clinical outcomes and economic efficiency. These findings support broader adoption of IVUS in peripheral interventions.

Introduction

Femoropopliteal occlusive disease is a prevalent and progressive condition characterized by atherosclerotic narrowing and occlusion of superficial femoral and popliteal arteries and is particularly burdensome due to its complex anatomical challenges [1]. Patients with femoropopliteal occlusive disease often present with long, diffuse lesions, severe calcification, and chronic total occlusions that complicate revascularization efforts and contribute to frequent restenosis and limb-threatening ischemia [2, 3].

Over the past decades, endovascular therapy has become an increasingly used treatment for femoropopliteal artery disease [1, 2, 4, 5]. Conventional endovascular revascularization is predominantly guided by angiography, a modality that, despite being the clinical standard, is constrained by its limited ability to visualize arterial wall morphology and disease extent [6]. Angiography provides only a two-dimensional representation of the vessel lumen, frequently underestimating the true severity of atherosclerotic burden, vessel diameter, and calcification, particularly in anatomically complex lesions, calcified segments, and bifurcations [3, 7, 8].

These limitations often result in suboptimal device sizing and incomplete lesion preparation or treatment. The continued reliance on angiographic guidance has been identified as a critical limitation in endovascular therapy outcomes, contributing to persistent rates of restenosis and target lesion revascularization (TLR) [6, 9, 10].

In contrast, the adjunctive use of intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) provides enhanced visualization of vessel structure, offering superior accuracy in lumen area assessment and more comprehensive characterization of vascular morphology. By enabling precise measurement of vessel dimensions and lesion characteristics, IVUS supports optimal device sizing and facilitates real-time intra-procedural evaluation, including stent apposition and lesion coverage. These capabilities contribute to improved procedural outcomes and allow for the immediate identification of complications [7, 11].

Despite growing evidence supporting IVUS in peripheral interventions, its adoption remains limited compared with its established role in coronary procedures [6, 9]. This discrepancy highlights an important gap between clinical evidence and real-world implementation, particularly in peripheral artery disease (PAD), where practice patterns remain heterogeneous. Moreover, while the clinical benefits of IVUS are increasingly recognized, there is a relative lack of robust economic evidence to support its routine use, especially within specific healthcare systems such as Spain.

In this context, cost-effectiveness analyses are essential to support the integration of IVUS-guided strategies into clinical practice. Generating country-specific economic evidence may help bridge the gap between clinical efficacy and real-world adoption, particularly in femoropopliteal interventions.

Objective

The objective of this study is to assess the cost-effectiveness of IVUS-guided revascularization compared with angiography-guided revascularization in patients with femoropopliteal occlusive disease, from a Spanish payer perspective.

Methods

A health economic model was developed to evaluate the comparative cost-effectiveness of IVUS-guided versus standard angiography-guided revascularization in patients with femoropopliteal occlusive disease. The analysis was conducted from the perspective of the Spanish healthcare payer and adopted a lifetime horizon to capture long-term clinical and economic consequences. The model was designed to estimate both direct medical costs and health outcomes associated with each imaging modality. Outcomes were measured in quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs), while costs were reported in 2025 Euros.

The main result was expressed as the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), calculated as the ratio of the difference in costs to the difference in QALYs between IVUS-guided and angiography-guided strategies. To ensure consistency with pharmacoeconomic guidelines, both costs and health outcomes were discounted at an annual rate of 3%.

Model structure

The model structure was informed by previously published economic evaluations, including those assessing IVUS in femoropopliteal occlusive disease [12] as well as broader applications of IVUS [13] to reflect the clinical course of patients with FP lesions. The structure was also validated by expert clinicians.

Specifically, a two-part modeling approach was implemented. The model consists of a short-term decision tree capturing clinical events and resource use within the first year after the intervention, followed by a long-term semi-Markov model simulating disease progression over a lifetime horizon. This hybrid structure enables appropriate modeling of both immediate procedural outcomes and the chronic nature of peripheral artery disease (PAD). Model structure is shown in Fig. 1.

Each health state is associated with specific costs and health-related quality-of-life (HRQoL) weights (utilities), that collectively are used to estimate of lifetime costs and quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) for both IVUS- and angiography-guided strategies. The “post- revascularization”, “post-reintervention”, and “post-amputation” health states are modeled separately from their respective procedural states (“revascularization”, “reintervention”, and “amputation”) to accurately assign appropriate costs and utilities reflective of the relatively stable periods following acute interventions, as opposed to the higher cost and lower utility values associated with the procedures themselves.

Patient transitions

Patients transition between health states, first in the decision tree for the first year, and then every cycle, which are one year long, in the semi-Markov model. Transitions are governed by transition probabilities, enabling the simulation of disease progression.

The decision tree reflects events one year after the revascularization for both comparators, patients can then enter the “post-revascularization”, “reintervention”, “amputation”, or “death” states. Based on the health state at the end of the decision tree period, patients will transition into subsequent health states in the lifetime semi-Markov model. Patients who are alive and have not experienced any events at the end of the decision tree enter the “post- revascularization” health state. Patients who experienced a reintervention enter the “post- reintervention” state, those who had an amputation enter the “post-amputation” state, and patients that die remain in the “Death” state, which acts as absorbing state.

Once patients enter the health states in the semi-Markov model, they can transition between health states to reflect plausible clinical pathways following the initial revascularization procedure. From the “post-revascularization” state, patients may remain stable, undergo a reintervention, or progress to “amputation”. Patients who enter the “reintervention” state subsequently transition to “post- reintervention” or to “amputation” in the event of limb loss. From “post- reintervention”, individuals may remain stable, experience a repeat reintervention, or require an amputation. Those undergoing amputation can transition to the “post-amputation” state, from which no further clinical transitions occur apart from death. Importantly, all patients retain the possibility of transitioning to the absorbing state “death” from any health state within the model.

The model assumes that patients may undergo a single amputation of the affected lower extremity, after which they can no longer have a reintervention, reflecting the irreversible nature of major limb amputation. As such, transitions from the “amputation” or “post-amputation” states to “reintervention” are not permitted, since the anatomical target for revascularization no longer exists. This assumption is embedded within the state-transition structure to maintain internal consistency and reflect clinical practice. A half-cycle correction was applied to account for events that may occur within each cycle.

Model parameters

Model parameters were sourced from published clinical studies that reflect the natural history of femoropopliteal occlusive disease for both IVUS-guided and angiography-guided intervention strategies (Table 1). These studies include patients across a broad range of lesion complexity, encompassing TASC II A–D classifications. Three primary categories of parameters were required to populate the model: (1) transition probabilities, defining the likelihood of movement between health states over time; (2) direct medical costs associated with each health state; and (3) utility weights, representing health-related quality of life of each health state.

Transition probabilities

Annual transition probabilities for both the angiography and IVUS cohorts were derived from the most recent meta-analysis by Jang et al. (2025) [14], which pooled data from 19 studies and over 800,000 patients. Results from the femoropopliteal population were used to inform the transition probabilities in our cost-effectiveness model.

The annual probability of reintervention (defined as TLR and/or any surgical or endovascular revascularization for the target extremity) in the angiography cohort was 29.81%, and the probability of major amputation was 11.60% [14]. Transition probabilities for the IVUS-guided cohort were estimated by applying Risk Ratios (RR) to these baseline event probabilities. A RR of 0.51 was applied for reintervention, indicating a significant reduction in risk with IVUS [14]. A RR of 0.85 was used for major amputation in the base-case [14]. However, due to variability in the literature, an alternative RR of 1.00 showing no significant differences in major amputation was included in a scenario analysis [9, 15].

Following an initial reintervention, the risk of subsequent reinterventions increases substantially. Evidence from a comparative analysis of repeat versus first revascularizations indicates that a hazard ratio of 3.18 for repeat reinterventions over a de-novo intervention [16]. This hazard ratio was applied to the annual de-novo reintervention probabilities previously described to reflect elevated risk in the post-reintervention population. No significant differences in the risk of amputation or death following reintervention were reported for initial or repeat procedures [16]. Consequently, transition probabilities to amputation and death were held constant across first and repeat revascularizations.

All-cause mortality in the model was implemented as a time-dependent probability, where the probability of death at a given age and proportion of males and females is used. These were derived from Spanish national life tables published by the Spanish Institute of Statistics [17]. The modeled cohort has a starting age of 73 years, with 60.85% of male patients, based on epidemiological data reported by the Spanish Ministry of Health for ICD-10 code I74.3 [18].

To account for excess mortality associated with femoropopliteal occlusive disease in the angiography-based arm, a hazard ratio of 1.89 was applied to baseline mortality risks [19]. For individuals who experience a major lower limb amputation, an elevated hazard ratio of 2.18 was used to reflect increased mortality risk [20]. These adjustments allow the model to more accurately represent differential survival trajectories within the target patient population. For mortality in the IVUS group, consensus across the literature indicates no statistically significant difference between IVUS- and angiography-guided approaches (RR = 1) [9, 15].

Patients were assumed to remain within their initial treatment strategy throughout the model horizon, such that reinterventions in the IVUS arm were IVUS-guided and those in the angiography arm were angiography-guided.

Utility weights

Utility estimates for the “revascularization”, “reintervention”, “post- revascularization”, and “post-reintervention” health states were informed by EQ-5D-5L data reported in the EMINENT randomized trial, which assessed HRQoL in symptomatic femoropopliteal PAD patients. Baseline utility prior to revascularization was 0.70, increasing to 0.90 post-intervention, indicating substantial improvement in patient-perceived quality of life [21].

A utility value of 0.531, which is based on EQ-5D-5L outcomes from a retrospective registry-based study of patients who underwent major unilateral lower limb amputation due to vascular disease [22], was applied to the “amputation” and “post-amputation” health states. QALYs were calculated by summing the products of the time spent in each health state and the utilities for those states.

Costs

Health state costs were estimated to reflect the direct medical expenditures associated with each clinical event or status, consistent with the Spanish payer perspective. The costs of standard angiography-based revascularization and reintervention were derived from Spanish public regional cost databases. Single femoropopliteal revascularization was assigned a cost of €4,192 [23], and reintervention without complications was assigned a cost of €10,198 [24]. Recent publications in Europe have shown that reintervention cost in femoropopliteal occlusion patients may increase to €21,917 [25]. Therefore, a conservative cost of reintervention is used in our calculations.

IVUS-based revascularization and reintervention was associated with an extra cost of $1,333 (€1,570 in 2025 Euros) based on registry results [8]. Major amputation of a lower limb was assigned a cost of €18,107 [24]. A conservative assumption of the model is that “post-revascularization”; “post-reintervention” and “post-amputation” health states do not have any associated costs. All costs were updated to 2025 Euros. When cost data from earlier years were used, values were adjusted for inflation. Cost-effectiveness was evaluated with respect to an assumed willingness to pay threshold between €20,000 and €30,000 per QALY gained, as commonly cited in the Spanish setting.

Sensitivity analyses

One-way sensitivity analyses (OWSA) and probabilistic sensitivity analyses (PSA) were performed to assess the impact of parameter uncertainties within the model. OWSA involves varying one parameter at a time over its plausible range to assess its individual impact on the model’s outcomes and determine which parameters most influence the results. Upper and lower bounds were defined using published measures of uncertainty whenever available; when such information was not available, a ± 10% variation around the base-case value was applied to reflect plausible parameter uncertainty.

Additionally, probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) was conducted with 1,000 iterations to capture the joint uncertainty across all model parameters. In the PSA, parameter uncertainty was modeled by assigning probability distributions according to parameter type. Beta distributions were used for probabilities, proportions, and utility values; gamma distributions for cost parameters; lognormal distributions for relative measures such as hazard ratios and risk ratios; and normal distributions for continuous variables such as age. Standard errors were obtained from published sources where available or assumed to be 10% of the mean value when not reported. The results were plotted on the cost-effectiveness plane (Fig. 3).

Results

Base case

The IVUS-guided revascularization strategy dominates when compared with angiography-guided intervention. It was associated with both lower lifetime costs and improved lifetime QALYs, as summarized in Table 2. Total costs per patient were estimated at €29,427 in the IVUS arm versus €32,716 in the angiography arm, yielding a cost difference of –€3,289 in favor of IVUS. IVUS-guided management resulted in an average of 6.94 quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) per patient, compared with 6.65 QALYs for angiography-guided treatment (an incremental gain of 0.29 QALYs). Clinical outcomes also favored IVUS, with a 7.3% reduction in major amputations and a 38.4% reduction in reintervention risk over the modeled time horizon.

Base case results

A scenario analysis was conducted based on an alternative assumption for major amputations, as described in the transition probabilities section. A conservative assumption was applied whereby the risk of amputation in the IVUS cohort was assumed to be equivalent to that of the angiography group (i.e., RR = 1.00). Despite this, IVUS continued to dominate, with a cost reduction of €3,410 and an incremental gain of 0.14 QALYs. These results underscore the resilience of IVUS’s cost-effectiveness, even when potential advantages in amputation rates probabilities are not assumed.

One-way sensitivity analyses

OWSA results are summarized in Fig. 2 in the form of a tornado diagram, both for costs and QALYs. Reintervention costs, both for angiography and IVUS, IVUS reintervention RR, and IVUS revascularization cost are the variables that are impacting the most cost results. For QALYs, post revascularization utility, reintervention utility and mortality probabilities are having the greatest impact. These findings underscore the critical role of repeat interventions in determining the overall value of IVUS guidance, in addition to quality of life gains after the intervention.

One-way sensitivity analysis

IVUS-guided revascularization consistently remained a dominant strategy compared with angiography-guided treatment across all parameter ranges, demonstrating robustness of the base-case findings to uncertainty in model inputs.

Probabilistic sensitivity analyses

IVUS dominated angiography-based treatment in 90% of the 1,000 simulations, as shown in the cost-effectiveness plane (Fig. 3). The mean cost difference across simulations was €3,245 in favor of IVUS and the mean incremental effectiveness was 0.30 QALYs. These findings further reinforce the robustness of the base-case results under probabilistic uncertainty and support the economic efficiency of IVUS-guided revascularization.

Given that IVUS-guided revascularization was found to be a dominant strategy, the cost-effectiveness conclusions are not sensitive to the choice of willingness-to-pay threshold. The high proportion of simulations in which IVUS appears both cost-saving and more effective, the probability of cost-effectiveness remains consistently high across all plausible thresholds. Consequently, constructing a cost-effectiveness acceptability curve (CEAC) was not considered necessary. An additional analysis showed that IVUS-guided revascularization became cost-effective shortly after the first year following the index procedure (i.e., during the second cycle), and remained cost-effective throughout the remainder of the time horizon.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that IVUS-guided revascularization is a cost-effective strategy compared with standard angiography-guided intervention for patients with femoropopliteal occlusive disease in Spain. IVUS is not only associated with better lifetime clinical outcomes, notably through a reduction in reinterventions and major amputations, but it also is associated with lower lifetime healthcare costs despite a higher upfront procedural cost. Scenario analysis demonstrate that even if the same amputation probability is used for both cohorts, IVUS remains dominant. Therefore, the primary driver of this benefit is the significant reduction in repeat interventions with IVUS, as confirmed by the OWSA.

These findings align with the growing clinical evidence supporting IVUS-guided revascularization, despite its still limited adoption in peripheral artery disease compared with coronary interventions. In this context, the present study provides relevant economic evidence for the Spanish healthcare system, supporting the integration of IVUS into routine clinical practice.

From a clinical perspective, these results have important implications for practice. In the context of rising burden of PAD, integrating IVUS into endovascular workflows offers a sustainable approach to improving patient outcomes without increasing overall system costs. From a payer’s perspective, the results support reimbursement policies that position IVUS-guided intervention as a gold standard for treating femoropopliteal lesions.

While our study demonstrates that IVUS is cost- effective (i.e., it dominates angiography-guided revascularization), its value is particularly pronounced in anatomically complex lesions. The IVUS-DCB trial [26] reported that IVUS yielded the greatest impact in patients with symptomatic femoropopliteal disease classified as TASC II type C or D, with significantly lower reintervention rates at 12 months versus TASC II A or B patients. Given that IVUS is most often used in these more anatomically complex lesions [6], the cost-effectiveness of IVUS-guided revascularization in real-world practice may be even greater than estimated in this study.

We identified only one study by Lichtenberg et al. (2023) [12], that compared IVUS-guided versus angiography-guided PTA for femoropopliteal disease from the perspective of the German healthcare system. Over a lifetime horizon, they reported an incremental gain of 0.22 QALYs and modest cost savings of €46. While their findings similarly support the dominance of IVUS, our analysis demonstrates a greater health benefit (0.29 QALYs) and substantially higher cost savings (€3,289).

These differences can be attributed to differences in model structure and parameters, particularly regarding reinterventions. First, the model by Lichtenberg et al. (2023) permits only a single reintervention (TLR), without the possibility of subsequent events. This is a critical limitation, as evidence shows that the risk of additional reinterventions increases significantly following an initial one [16]. In contrast, our model allows for repeated transitions to the reintervention state, better reflecting the clinical course of femoropopliteal disease. As a result, Lichtenberg et al.‘s model likely underestimates both the frequency and cost impact of repeat interventions avoided through IVUS. Furthermore, the literature suggests that the cost of reintervention is substantially higher than that of the initial procedure [25], which may have also been underestimated in their analysis. Lastly, while our model is informed by data encompassing the full spectrum of lesion complexity (TASC II A–D), Lichtenberg et al. based their estimates on a trial restricted to TASC II A–C lesions, excluding the most complex cases where IVUS guidance is likely to provide the greatest benefit.

This study has several limitations. First, while the model uses Spanish-specific cost data, some clinical inputs, particularly those related to IVUS effectiveness, were derived from international studies. Although adjusted via risk ratios, local practice patterns and patient characteristics may differ. In addition, the model does not include costs associated with rehabilitation nor does it include indirect costs such as loss of productivity, which may underestimate the true societal benefit of IVUS. Given that IVUS-guided revascularization is associated with a reduction in major amputations, the inclusion of long-term rehabilitation and post-amputation costs would likely increase total costs to a greater extent in the angiography-guided arm, thereby further strengthening the cost-effectiveness of IVUS.

Another limitation of the model is the assumption that patients remain within their initial imaging strategy over time, such that reinterventions are performed using the same modality as the index procedure. In clinical practice, the choice of imaging modality for reinterventions may vary depending on operator preference, institutional protocols, or availability. This simplifying assumption may influence the estimated cost-effectiveness.

Moreover, most comparative studies included in the meta-analyses had follow-up periods limited to 12 months, which may be insufficient to accurately capture long-term outcomes. This limitation could impact our estimates, particularly for the risks of reintervention and amputation, which were applied uniformly over the entire time horizon due to the lack of robust longitudinal data on time-varying risks in this population. While our modeling framework reflects current best evidence, it is subject to the uncertainties associated with long-term clinical projections. In addition, the use of a one-year cycle length implies that utility values are applied uniformly within each cycle, which may not fully capture short-term fluctuations in quality of life following events such as reintervention or amputation, where disutility may be more acute and transient. In the absence of detailed data on time-dependent utility trajectories, this represents a pragmatic simplification.

Incorporating long-term real-world data in future analyses could enhance the robustness and external validity of these estimates. Additionally, the cost-effectiveness of IVUS should be assessed across broader PAD populations lesion types.

Conclusion

This study shows that IVUS-guided revascularization is a cost-effective strategy compared with angiography-guided intervention for femoropopliteal occlusive disease in Spain. By improving clinical outcomes while reducing long-term healthcare costs, IVUS represents a high-value approach for the management of this patient population.
