WUSTCA: enhanced UAV RF signal classification with wavelet transform and STCA attention mechanisms
Authors: Jiyu Liu, Zaolin Xia, Yuhui Chen, Hanning Sun, Shu Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) play a vital role in various civilian and commercial applications, necessitating accurate classification of their radio frequency (RF) signals. Current deep learning-based approaches struggle with high computational complexity, susceptibility to noise, and limited accuracy. This paper introduces a novel UAV signal classification framework that combines wavelet-based feature extraction with a hierarchical U-Net architecture, enhanced by split-time cross attention (STCA) and residual connectivity. The WUSTCA model effectively classifies UAV and controller signals, achieving an average classification accuracy of 96.6% for UAVs and 95.83% for UAV controllers on the CardRF dataset. By addressing challenges such as noise interference and signal diversity, this work provides a reliable and efficient solution for UAV signal classification, paving the way for real-time applications in complex environments.

Introduction

Low-altitude UAV operation is increasingly embedded in public safety, logistics, inspection, agriculture, and consumer services. In these scenarios, RF monitoring is often the earliest practical way to identify UAV activity because the communication link between a UAV and its controller can be observed before the aircraft is visually detected. Therefore, UAV RF analysis is not only a signal-classification problem, but also an operational requirement for airspace awareness, interference management, and security monitoring.

The recognition task considered in this work has three levels. First, a monitoring system must distinguish UAV-related RF emissions from controller-side signals. Second, it must identify the UAV model. Third, it must identify the controller model. These levels are summarized in Fig. 1. Challenges in UAV RF classification

UAV RF classification faces several practical challenges. First, raw RF recordings are high dimensional and may contain long IQ sequences, making direct classification computationally expensive and difficult to deploy in real-time monitoring systems1. Second, UAV transmissions are bursty and time varying. Their discriminative patterns may appear across short temporal segments rather than in a single stationary frequency profile, requiring models that preserve localized time-frequency information.

Third, real-world monitoring environments contain strong interference from Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, industrial transmitters, and other consumer wireless devices. These signals may overlap with UAV communication bands and distort the spectral structure used for classification2. While models similar to those in Nemer et al.3 perform well in controlled environments, they often struggle under open RF conditions. Existing datasets such as DroneRF4 also do not fully cover common signal disturbances, limiting practical assessment5.

Fourth, fine-grained UAV and controller recognition is affected by small inter-class differences. UAVs from related families may share hardware platforms, modulation patterns, or controller protocols, which makes their RF fingerprints highly similar. Finally, many existing studies report classification accuracy without complete latency, memory, and reproducibility details. This makes it difficult to evaluate whether a method is suitable for real-time or edge-assisted UAV monitoring.

Recent advances and remaining gaps

Recent work has explored machine learning, signal processing, and deep learning techniques for UAV RF classification. Classical methods such as SVM and KNN can be effective on hand-crafted features, but their performance degrades when the signal distribution changes. Generative and deep learning methods have been introduced to improve high-dimensional RF modeling. For example, AC-WGAN-based methods have been used to enhance UAV RF classification1, while PSD-image methods use CNNs to learn spectral signatures from transformed RF data6.

Other advances aim to improve robustness in complex wireless environments. Adaptive security methods based on DDPG and RIS-equipped UAV systems can reduce interference and improve communication reliability7. Hybrid RIS and AI-based optimization have also shown potential for improving noise resilience in secure wireless links8,9. However, these methods may introduce additional computational cost and are not always evaluated with inference latency or hardware constraints. Some studies also omit systematic testing under different SNR levels, which limits their usefulness for operational RF monitoring10–12.

Public datasets are essential for evaluating these methods. DroneRF4 has supported UAV RF research, but it has limited coverage of common interference and nonstandard transmitter conditions. The CardRF dataset provides a broader collection of UAV and controller RF signals, including interference from industrial and consumer devices. This makes it suitable for studying both coarse UAV/controller discrimination and fine-grained model classification.

Contribution of this work

This work proposes WUSTCA, a UAV RF signal classification framework that combines adaptive preprocessing, wavelet-based multi-scale feature extraction, and split-time cross attention (STCA). PELT-based transmission boundary detection and adaptive bandpass preprocessing are used to suppress irrelevant signal components before feature extraction. A level-4 Daubechies 4 (db4) wavelet decomposition converts each 1024-sample slice into temporally aligned multi-scale sub-bands, preserving localized transient and spectral structure.

The STCA module is then applied to the fused sequence representation to model cross-segment dependencies between early and late transmission patterns. This design provides a task-specific inductive bias for RF burst recognition rather than treating attention as a generic plug-in block. The resulting framework distinguishes UAV and UAV controller signals, performs fine-grained UAV and controller model classification, and reduces errors caused by out-of-band interference such as Bluetooth and Wi-Fi signals.

In addition, we provide a sliding-window deployment protocol based on 1024-sample windows with 50% overlap. We also report controlled analyses under Wi-Fi interference and varying SNR conditions, along with computational efficiency results, to better characterize false alarms, robustness, and near-real-time deployment feasibility. By combining CardRF-based evaluation with these additional analyses, the proposed framework provides a more realistic assessment of UAV RF signal classification in complex wireless environments1,10.

Related work

Recently, the rapid advancement of UAV technology has driven extensive research on signal classification and detection methods13,14. Complex and noise-prone commercial and industrial environments require robust detection mechanisms that can distinguish UAV emissions from controller signals and other wireless interference. Existing work can be broadly grouped into classical RF feature methods, hierarchical learning methods, deep learning models, interference-aware RF fingerprinting, and public UAV signal datasets.

Early research on UAV signal detection usually relied on classical machine learning techniques or heuristic signal processing methods. These methods, while effective in controlled environments, struggle to work in practice because of the high variability of UAV signals and their susceptibility to interference. Conventional models such as support vector machines (SVMs) and K-nearest neighbors (KNN) have limitations when handling high-dimensional RF signals in the presence of other wireless communications.

Hierarchical learning has been used to reduce the difficulty of UAV recognition by decomposing the task into multiple stages. Nemer et al. proposed a hierarchical learning model for UAV detection that improves classification accuracy by separating coarse-grained detection from fine-grained recognition15. This strategy is helpful for handling different UAV types, but its performance can still degrade under industrial equipment interference and dense wireless activity. Our study builds on these hierarchical concepts to cope with the complexity of UAV signal variations and improve recognition accuracy in noisy environments16.

Deep learning has become increasingly important for RF fingerprinting because convolutional and recurrent models can learn discriminative representations from high-dimensional IQ or time-frequency inputs. Akter et al.17 used a sequential convolutional neural network to enhance UAV surveillance capability, while Basak et al.18 employed deep residual networks to classify UAVs from RF fingerprints. Swinney and Woods also utilized residual blocks to classify UAV operation patterns by preserving information across deep network layers. These studies motivate the residual connectivity in our model, which helps reduce feature loss when distinguishing similar UAV models19.

Another difficulty in classifying UAV signals is interference from other RF signals such as Wi-Fi and Bluetooth. Ezuma et al. addressed this problem by developing a UAV classification system that utilizes RF fingerprinting in the presence of common wireless interference. Their results motivate the use of advanced signal filtering and preprocessing methods to mitigate interference effects before feature extraction20.

Public datasets have played a crucial role in advancing UAV signal research. The DroneRF dataset introduced by Swinney and Woods4 has been a valuable resource, providing a foundation for training and evaluating UAV classification models. However, as noted by Soltani et al.21, the dataset’s lack of nonstandard transmitter waveforms limits its effectiveness in diverse real-world scenarios. To address these limitations, the CardRF dataset introduced in this study provides a broader range of RF signals, including those influenced by interference from industrial and consumer devices, offering a more comprehensive foundation for UAV signal classification.

This study builds on these existing methods by integrating wavelet-based feature extraction, residual learning, and hierarchical classification. The use of wavelet transforms, as demonstrated by Medaiyese et al.14, allows for effective feature extraction by capturing both frequency- and time-domain characteristics of RF signals. By combining these extracted features with residual encoder–decoder neural networks, the proposed model aims to improve the precision and robustness of UAV signal classification in complex and interference-heavy environments22,23.

Wavelet-STCA alignment

After U-Net fusion, the feature map entering STCA is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{F}\in \mathbb {R}^{B\times T\times C}$$\end{document}. Each time index in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{F}$$\end{document} already aggregates all five aligned db4 sub-bands, so attention is computed over temporally ordered multi-scale descriptors rather than over a single-scale raw sequence. This alignment is important for RF modulation recognition, because it allows the attention weights to relate coarse envelope evolution to short-duration transient components within the same temporal coordinate system.

Loss function

From Eq. (19), we know the prediction classification results. The objective of the model is to minimize the classification error through a combination of different loss functions. To begin with, the cross-entropy loss30 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {L}_{\text {CE}}$$\end{document} is commonly used to measure the difference between the predicted class probabilities and the true labels. It is defined as:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L}_{\text {CE}}&= - \sum _{i=1}^{N} y_i \log (\hat{y}_i), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_i$$\end{document} is the true label and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y}_i$$\end{document} the predicted probability for the i-th sample, with N being the total number of samples in the dataset. In addition, to enhance the classification performance and maximize the margin between classes, we incorporate the hinge loss \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {L}_{\text {hinge}}$$\end{document}, which is particularly effective in classification tasks like those involving support vector machines (SVM). The hinge loss is defined as:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L}_{\text {hinge}}&= \frac{1}{N}\sum _{i=1}^{N} \sum _{j \ne y_i} \max (0,\, 1 - f_{y_i}(\mathbf{x}_i) + f_j(\mathbf{x}_i)), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The multiclass hinge loss31 penalizes predictions where any non-target class score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_j(\mathbf{x}_i)$$\end{document} is within a margin of 1 from the target class score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{y_i}(\mathbf{x}_i)$$\end{document}, encouraging larger inter-class margins. Finally, the total loss \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {L}_{\text {total}}$$\end{document} is a weighted combination of the cross-entropy and hinge losses:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L}_{\text {total}}&= \alpha \,\mathcal {L}_{\text {CE}} + (1-\alpha )\,\mathcal {L}_{\text {hinge}}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha =0.7$$\end{document} was selected via grid search over \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha \in \{0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9\}$$\end{document} on the validation set. By optimizing this combined loss, the model achieves both accurate class probability estimation and larger inter-class decision margins, improving its generalization ability.

Baseline implementation details

All deep learning baselines are trained using the AdamW optimizer with an initial learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$5\times 10^{-4}$$\end{document}, a cosine annealing schedule, a batch size of 64, and early stopping with a patience of 15 epochs monitored on validation macro F1-score. Training proceeds for a maximum of 120 epochs. The same class-frequency-weighted cross-entropy loss is applied to all baselines. For each baseline, hyperparameters follow the original publications where available; otherwise, a grid search over learning rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\in \{10^{-3}, 5\times 10^{-4}, 10^{-4}\}$$\end{document} is applied. WUSTCA contains 4.73M trainable parameters and requires 1.82 GFLOPs per inference sample. Compared to the CNN+LSTM+ATT baseline, which requires 2.14 GFLOPs and 5.8 ms latency, WUSTCA achieves lower computational cost at 1.82 GFLOPs and 4.1 ms end-to-end latency. This advantage comes from the parallelizable U-Net encoder–decoder and the split-time attention design, which avoids the sequential bottleneck of LSTM-based architectures.

The reported 4.1 ms includes the wavelet processing duration rather than only the neural-network forward pass. The memory footprint of 345 MB is moderate and suitable for GPU-based deployment. The measured inference latency indicates feasibility for near-real-time deployment in GPU-equipped systems. Deployment on resource-constrained edge devices remains an open challenge; model quantization and structured pruning for edge deployment are identified as important directions for future work.

Conclusion

This paper presents a UAV RF signal recognition framework that couples wavelet-based multi-scale decomposition with a U-Net encoder–decoder and split-time cross attention. The main contribution is not a claim of a fundamentally new attention theory, but a task-oriented integration in which db4 sub-bands are temporally aligned before STCA is applied to model cross-segment dependencies between early and late portions of each RF burst. Under this formulation, WUSTCA achieves the strongest overall performance on both UAV and controller classification tasks while maintaining lower computational cost than the strongest recurrent-attention baseline.

The revised experiments also provide a more operational characterization of the method. In addition to standard accuracy and macro F1 metrics, we report false-alarm rates on WiFi-dominated negative samples under multiple working conditions and controlled AWGN degradation across a wide SNR range. These results show that WUSTCA reduces WiFi-triggered false alarms and preserves a consistent performance margin over the strongest baseline as SNR decreases, supporting a qualified claim of improved noise tolerance rather than unconditional robustness.

Several limitations nevertheless remain. The new robustness analysis is based on controlled perturbations and curated interference conditions, so broader field testing with real co-channel emitters and more diverse traffic loads is still needed. Moreover, although the proposed sliding-window protocol and 4.1 ms GPU latency indicate feasibility for near-real-time processing in GPU-equipped systems, deployment on resource-constrained embedded platforms would still require compression and hardware-specific profiling.

Future work will therefore focus on large-scale field validation, stronger edge-oriented model compression, and extension to a broader set of UAV platforms and RF environments. These directions will further clarify the deployment boundary of WUSTCA while preserving the multi-scale wavelet-STCA design that underlies its recognition performance.
