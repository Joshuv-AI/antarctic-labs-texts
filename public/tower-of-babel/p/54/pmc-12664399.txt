WMC-Leafset: A dataset of wax gourd and Mangalore cucumber plants for leaf miner and pest infestation diseased object detection
Authors: Keerthi Prasad M A, Pushpa B R
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Wax gourd (Benincasa hispida (Thunb.) Cogn.) and Mangalore Cucumber (Cucumis melo L. subsp. agrestis var. conomon) are nutritionally rich, mineral-dense crops with a short growing cycle, making them a preferred choice for cultivation among farmers across the country. The Mangalore cucumber, also known as the culinary cucumber, Indian yellow cucumber, or Japanese pickling melon, is widely used in Asian cuisine for pickling. While proper nutrient management is essential for optimal growth, disease control poses a significant challenge in ensuring healthy yields, as disease can rapidly spread from one leaf to another, affecting larger areas of the field and reducing crop yield. Since cucurbits grow close to the soil, they spread across the ground, exhibit dense canopies, and often overlap with neighboring plants. Early detection is crucial to ensure sustainable cultivation, food security, and increased crop productivity. To address this challenge, we collected a dataset comprising 3200 images that includes image samples of Wax gourd and Mangalore cucumber plants affected by leaf miner, pests and image samples of healthy leaves. The Cucurbitaceae datasets that are available in the public domain lack representation of the Mangalore cucumber and Wax gourd varieties. To the best of our knowledge, no publicly available dataset exists for the Wax gourd. Moreover, existing datasets typically contain images captured under controlled greenhouse conditions with plain backgrounds, featuring a single leaf per image. They exhibit low background complexity and limit the scope to detect diseases at the object level, including multiple diseases present on a single leaf or plant. The uniqueness of the proposed dataset lies in addressing this gap by providing field-level images of cucurbits. These images capture variations in soil, overlapped leaves, complex background, varying angles and distances, weeds, and human interference. This makes the dataset suitable for training object detection models capable of identifying single and multiple disease instances, and it can also be effectively used for classification tasks to distinguish between healthy and diseased leaves. It supports advancement in deep learning, feature extraction, segmentation and pattern recognition tasks. Additionally, the dataset serves as a valuable resource for plant pathologists, agronomists and agricultural experts in disease detection, monitoring and management, thereby promoting sustainable agricultural practices. By offering open access, this dataset promotes collaboration within the scientific community to facilitate the development of robust disease detection, identification, and disease control, thus enhancing farming practices and increasing agricultural yields and advancing food security.

Background

Wax gourd and Mangalore cucumber are widely cultivated vegetables across seasons that are rich in nutrients and minerals and have short growing periods. Cucurbits are favored by farmers due to their low production costs, lesser marketing challenges and continuous demand throughout the year. Among the vegetable varieties, Cucumis Maderaspatensis stands out for its minimal water requirements and is often cultivated alongside other crops. Despite this, crops face significant threats from diseases and pests, a challenge that particularly impacts smallholder farmers whose livelihoods depend on healthy yields.

The rapid spread of leaf miners and pests in cucurbit cultivation represents a significant agricultural problem that requires immediate control measures. Leaf miners begin showing their first signs three to four days after adult insects deposit eggs beneath leaf surfaces by producing white twisted trails across leaf surfaces. These pests, primarily larvae of beetles, flies and sawflies and moths, consume chlorophyll, which decreases photosynthesis and plant energy production. The ability to spot leaf miner larvae through normal human vision is particularly challenging, making early intervention difficult. The plant's stems, flowers and fruits become affected by leaf miner infestation as it advances, thus leading to reduced growth and decreased yield. Four leaf miner species show particular impact on agricultural production among many others: the spinach leaf miner (Pegomya hyoscyami), vegetable leaf miner (Liromyza sativae), the American serpentine leaf miner (Liriomyza trifolii) and the citrus leaf miner (Phyllocnistis citrella). The undersides of leaves serve as the starting point for cucurbit pest infestations by aphids, whiteflies and mites, which use their mouthparts to obtain sap from the plant.

This defoliator damage initially appears as yellowing or stippling on the upper leaf surface and progresses to wilting and distortion as pest populations increase. These pests spread through wind, rain and the movement of infected material, or by their locomotion, quickly infesting neighboring plants. Honeydew excreted by some pests encourages sooty growth, further hindering photosynthesis. As the infestation intensifies, leaf curling, premature leaf drop, and stunted growth occur due to the plant's reduced ability to photosynthesis. This decreased photosynthetic capacity produces smaller fruit size and lower yield for producers. Severe infestations can lead to defoliation, limiting plants to further damage by secondary infections and leading to plant death. The feeding wounds created by pests also provide a way for plant pathogens to infect plants. Ultimately, the combined effects of direct feeding, disease transmission and loss of photosynthesis rapidly damage the plant and compromise future productivity.

In addition to feeding damage from the leaf miners and pests, these pests also successfully reduce chlorophyll content in leaves, resulting in reduced photosynthesis and, therefore reduced energy for plant growth. Additionally, cucurbit plants are susceptible to moisture, virus and bacterial disease and soil-borne pathogens throughout their life cycle from plantation to harvest. These collected images of cucurbits leaves exhibiting defoliator damage, diseases, and leaf miner activity can be leveraged to develop robust computer vision models for automated disease detection and pest diagnosis. By training deep learning and vision transformer techniques on these images, the models can learn to identify specific visual patterns associated with each condition. These images enable rapid and accurate detection of infestations and diseases in the field, even at early stages, facilitating timely intervention. Challenges such as noise removal, complex background and overlapping leaves can be leveraged during training to ensure models perform effectively under real-world conditions. To the best of our knowledge, there is no public dataset that exists in a complex background for the cucurbits (Wax gourd, Mangalore cucumber), comprising both the leaf miner and pest, making it a unique dataset for efficient computer vision models. Ultimately, computer vision-based tools trained on these images can empower farmers with real-time diagnostics, leading to improved crop management practices and reduced yield losses at the early stage. Such a system could also be integrated with robotic systems for automated pest control or targeted pesticide application.

Researchers have made numerous significant contributions in the field of disease detection and classification in cucumber plants. Sultana et al. [1] compiled a dataset comprising eight categories of cucumber diseases, five types of leaf diseases, and three diseased vegetables. This dataset was primarily used for disease classification tasks and has been applied in various studies employing convolutional neural networks (CNNs). Omer et al. [2] proposed a CNN-based method for disease classification using single leaf samples. In the study, Zhang et al. [3] collected 600 cucumber images affected by three diseases to perform disease classification. Omer et al. [4] worked on individual disease detection using an enhanced Yolov5 deep learning model, with a dataset containing four disease types in cucumber plants. Wang et al. [5] developed a deep learning model to assess disease severity using a self-created cucumber disease dataset. Liu et al. [6] conducted disease detection using a custom YOLOv11n model for a cucumber plant variety. The dataset includes five disease types captured under a complex background, although each image featured a single disease. Li et al. [7] designed a custom Yolo5n model using a self-constructed dataset consisting of three disease categories. Liu et al. [8] proposed an efficient detection model targeting three diseases, achieving better performance, as indicated by the mAP score. Lin et al. [9] devised a custom segmentation method for cucurbit powdery mildew disease using 50 image samples. Bilal et al. [10] utilised forty CNN layers to identify and classify five cucumber disease types. These works reveal that most existing datasets were created under controlled or fixed backgrounds, primary focus on disease classification or single disease identification at the leaf level, often neglecting overlapping biotic stresses. Many datasets were collected in research laboratories and studies conducted at the image and leaf levels. Additionally, the current publicly available dataset also lacks the two varieties of Mangalore cucumber and Wax gourd. The dataset captured limiting variability in lighting, occlusion, and background clutter, restricting the dataset's applicability for multi-disease detection. The dataset collected reflects a very limited scale and diversity, which makes it challenging for the models to handle real field scenarios where overlapping disease symptoms occur. The images captured in the field introduce challenges such as overlapping leaves, partial occlusion, scale variations and variable lighting, which are not reflected in the existing datasets. Further, existing datasets largely support the classification task, assigning a single label to an image. However, the agricultural scenario requires object detection where individual diseased regions are localized and classified. This highlights the need for a dataset that supports multi-disease detection within a single frame and enables models to identify multiple disease instances per leaf or plant and identification of subtle disease symptoms across early, intermediate and late stages in a complex background. Early detection of plant diseases offers a significant advantage to the farming community, enabling timely intervention to control the spread of infection. The proposed dataset enhances robustness and applicability of computer vision models for field deployment, supporting agronomists, pathologists and farmers in accurate disease identification, monitoring and decision making.

Data Description

In this work, we aim to create a dataset of two plant varieties, Wax gourd and Mangalore Cucumber, affected by two specific diseases: leaf miner and pest infestation. The dataset was created to support both disease identification, localization of affected regions and disease classification into pest and leaf miner damage, while addressing real-field challenges such as overlapping leaves, variations in lighting, and complex background containing human presence, weeds, polythene cover, plant stems and shadows.

Data validation

The image collection process started with the identification of farms cultivating these two crop varieties, and discussions were initiated with farmers about the plants, their growth stages, seed sowing dates, and the soil characteristics. Following this, the samples were collected manually, exhibiting different disease patterns and plant types. Further, a plant pathologist and botanist assisted in validating the presence of leaf miner, pest infestation and confirming the plant varieties of the collected samples. The experts guided in identifying diseases based on the morphological and visual features such as discoloration patterns, leaf texture changes and lesion structures. Subsequently, annotation of the images was carried out; after annotation, the entire dataset underwent a second round of expert validation to identify and remove any incorrectly marked or ambiguous regions. Finally, all validated and correctly labeled images were organized and stored in their respective folders. Farmers also contributed traditional insights by describing visual symptoms associated with the diseases in both crop varieties. The images were collected over several months to capture varying severity levels and developmental stages, ranging from early to mid and late phases of plant growth. Data collection

The data collection was carried out using smartphones to capture images at various resolutions, under different lighting conditions and from multiple capturing angles across different locations to ensure real-world diversity. The image samples were captured across various growth stages of the plants, exhibiting disease symptoms at the early, mid, and late stages. The images contain overlapping leaves, with each image featuring 1–12 leaves. These conditions introduce significant variability that makes the dataset particularly valuable for building and testing robust deep learning models. A total of 1500 images of Wax gourd, 1500 Mangalore cucumber image samples and 200 image samples of healthy leaves from both varieties were collected. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The dataset includes image samples of Wax gourd and Mangalore cucumber collected from different farms, cultivated under varied soil conditions and at different growth stages. Then, the collected image samples are subjected to pre-analysis, where imperfections are manually corrected [12]. Images are captured using mobile devices with different resolutions from the identified fields, along with adjustments in image distances.

Image annotation

The dataset was annotated with the Makesense image annotation tool. During labeling, both foreground and background elements, along with disease-affected regions, were annotated carefully to ensure the precise identification of leaf miners and pests. Due to the complexity of the real field environment, some disease symptoms were partially obscured by overlapping leaves and background elements. To address this, images were captured using mobile phone cameras at a distance of 1–1.5 feet and were zoomed in where necessary to annotate affected regions in the foreground, background and overlapping leaf areas. Each leaf image was carefully outlined using bounding boxes to capture precise boundaries, including partially occluded regions. The annotations were validated by experts who reviewed leaf miner and pest infestations as well as visual characteristics such as discoloration, lesions, and feeding patterns. The annotated labels are stored locally in YOLO YAML format, ensuring compatibility for training deep learning networks. The number of annotations in each image ranges from 5 to 50 due to the granular level of disease presence. Image augmentation

The dataset has been further augmented to represent a wide range of real-world agricultural scenarios. Geometric transformations include horizontal and vertical flips, shift-scale-rotate operations, independent scaling and rotations, as well as perspective distortions. Photometric augmentations address variations in lighting by applying adjustments to brightness and contrast, modifications to Hue, Saturation, and Value and blur effects such as motion, median, and Gaussian blur.

Methods

The collected dataset can be employed to develop a machine learning model for extracting leaf region from complex background, extracting visual features associated with disease characteristics and classifying affected samples. It also provides scope for training deep learning models to detect multiple diseases in cucumber leaves and analyze their progression over time. Overall, this dataset serves as a valuable resource for early disease detection and improved crop management practices in cucumber cultivation. Limitations

The proposed dataset was collected from the local agricultural fields in a specific region in Mysuru, Karnataka, which features a semi-arid tropical climate with distinct wet and dry seasons. As the data were gathered from a limited farm, some degree of geographic bias may be present. A temporal bias may also exist since these plant images were collected during the early and mid-monsoon seasons, potentially limiting seasonal variability in disease manifestation. The proposed study has potential in detecting leaf miner and pest infestation across two agricultural crop varieties, such as Wax gourd and Mangalore cucumber, under complex background conditions. The samples include only two crop varieties that address two disease types, which limits the generalizability of the derived model to other crops and disease types.
