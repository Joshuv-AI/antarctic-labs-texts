Investigating Person–Job Fit, Job Performance, Psychological Capital, and Job Burnout Among Pre‐Hospital Emergency Personnel: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Meraj Balaghi, Mostafa Bijani, Fatemeh Sarvi, Mohammadreza Razavi, Ali Mohammad Parviniannasab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimOne of the critical factors in achieving optimal job performance in high‐pressure environments, such as prehospital emergency services, is person–job fit. However, burnout, which results from continuous work‐related stress, can reduce job performance. In this context, psychological capital may act as a protective factor for job performance, although these relationships have been underexplored in previous studies. This study aimed to examine the relationships among person–job fit, job performance, psychological capital, and job burnout among prehospital emergency personnel.MethodsThis descriptive‐analytical study was conducted among 370 pre‐hospital emergency personnel in southern Fars Province, Iran, during 2024–2025. Participants completed a demographic questionnaire and standardized instruments assessing person–job fit, job performance, psychological capital, and job burnout. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27 and the PROCESS macro version 4.1.ResultsThe results indicate that person–job fit is positively associated with psychological capital and job performance. Conversely, person–job fit, psychological capital, and job performance are negatively associated with job burnout. In the PROCESS macro analysis, the total effect of person–job fit on job performance was significant. Furthermore, the mediating effects of psychological capital and job burnout in the relationship between person–job fit and job performance were also significant.ConclusionThe findings of this research confirm that the alignment between individuals and their jobs significantly enhances job performance. Additionally, psychological interventions aimed at improving psychological capital and reducing burnout in high‐stress environments, such as prehospital emergency settings, can be highly beneficial. These insights are valuable for managers and policymakers when designing training programs and interventions to enhance job performance.

Introduction

Human resources are widely recognized as the most valuable asset of any organization, with employee Job Performance (JP) serving as a key determinant of organizational success [1]. In the realm of pre‐hospital emergency care, personnel effectiveness is crucial in influencing the quality of services provided [2]. Individual JP is defined as “behaviors or actions that are aligned with organizational goals” [3]. This definition highlights the multidimensional nature of JP, emphasizing employee behaviors rather than merely outcomes, and encompassing actions that meaningfully contribute to organizational objectives [3]. Achieving optimal JP in high‐pressure environments like pre‐hospital emergency services requires a comprehensive understanding of its underlying determinants [4].

One important factor is Person–Job (PJ) fit, which refers to the congruence between an individual's traits and the requirements of the position [5]. This concept also includes an individual's ability to adapt to change and maintain safety under shifting conditions [6]. Research has shown that a PJ fit has been linked to improved performance; this relationship is neither inherently direct nor absolute [7]. Various psychological and contextual factors may mediate this relationship, with psychological capital (PsyCap) being particularly influential. PsyCap is a relatively recent construct within the field of positive organizational behavior, comprising four core components: hope, resilience, optimism, and self‐efficacy [8]. These psychological resources enhance employees' capacity to cope with workplace challenges and foster improved JP [9]. Conversely, job burnout—resulting from prolonged and unrelieved occupational stress—has detrimental effects on both psychological well‐being and performance outcomes [10]. Burnout is defined as a syndrome resulting from prolonged workplace stress, characterized by three main dimensions: emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and a reduced sense of personal accomplishment [11]. Studies indicate that employees with high PsyCap are better equipped to handle environmental stressors and are less susceptible to burnout [12]. In high‐stress environments such as pre‐hospital emergency care, personnel are consistently exposed to demanding and emotionally charged situations, making them particularly vulnerable to burnout and decreased efficiency [13]. Despite the importance of this issue, few studies have simultaneously examined the mediating roles of PsyCap and job burnout in the relationship between PJ fit and JP. Previous research has identified psychological factors as mediators that can enhance JP. These studies have emphasized the influence of cultural and social contexts on both JP and psychological factors [9, 14, 15, 16]. Additionally, other research has highlighted the roles of individual competence and organizational justice in advancing organizational goals, suggesting that, together with self‐efficacy, they provide a foundation for improving employees' JP [17, 18].

While previous studies have examined various factors individually—including job burnout and JP among prehospital emergency personnel—no research has specifically explored the interrelationships among these constructs within this population. Therefore, this study makes a significant contribution to the field by employing a multidimensional mediation approach to clarify the relationships between PJ fit, PsyCap, job burnout, and JP within the framework of the Job Demands‐Resources (JD‐R) model [19]. Second, this study was conducted within the collectivist cultural context of Iran. It addresses a gap in existing literature by exploring the relationships among these concepts, specifically in prehospital emergency personnel. A nuanced understanding of these mediating factors could significantly enhance both the quality of emergency services and employee performance. Furthermore, identifying these mechanisms may facilitate the development of effective interventions aimed not only at improving performance but also at protecting the psychological well‐being of emergency personnel. Accordingly, the present research sought to examine the relationships among personal PJ fit, PsyCap, job burnout, and JP among prehospital emergency personnel in southern Iran.

Theoretical Underpinning

This research employs the JD‐R model as the primary theoretical framework to explain the interactions among PJ fit, PsyCap, job burnout, and JP. According to Bakker and Demerouti [19], a key aspect of this model is the classification of all work conditions into two main categories: “job demands” and “job resources”. Furthermore, the JD‐R model posits that job demands and resources trigger two distinct processes: one associated with health deterioration and the other with motivation. In the health deterioration process, the intensity and/or frequency of job demands—such as heavy workload, time pressure, and role overload—can lead to job burnout; require additional effort from employees, which depletes their physical, emotional, and cognitive resources, ultimately leading to disengagement and reduced performance [19].

Thus, based on the literature review and the JD‐R model, we propose a model that assumes the existence of two sequential mediators of the relationship between PJ fit and the JP. This relationship is totally mediated by PsyCap and burnout, and below we have detailed and argued each sequence from this model, formulating specific hypotheses.

PJ Fit, PsyCap, and JP

Person–job (PJ) fit denotes the extent to which an individual's attributes are congruent with the specific requirements of a given role [20]. An extensive body of scholarship, predominantly anchored in the Job Demand–Resource (JD‐R) model, has consistently highlighted the centrality of self‐regulatory capacity and ongoing personal development in shaping JP. Empirical evidence further demonstrates that flexible work arrangements exert a statistically significant and favorable effect on JP [21, 22, 23]. In parallel, employees' subjective appraisals of job characteristics have been shown to exert a meaningful influence on performance outcomes [22].

To the extent that employees possess knowledge, skills, and abilities (KSAs) that align closely with job demands, they are more likely to execute their responsibilities with a high level of proficiency [20]. It can therefore be reasonably inferred that individuals characterized by stronger PJ fit will exhibit superior performance relative to those with weaker alignment [24]. Within the JD‐R framework, both Psychological Capital (PsyCap) and PJ fit are conceptualized as critical personal resources [19], the accumulation and mobilization of which are indispensable for effective goal attainment [25]. Individuals endowed with substantial personal resources tend to demonstrate elevated task‐related confidence and sustained perseverance in the face of adversity [26].

Avey et al.'s [27] meta‐analysis provides robust empirical support for this proposition, indicating that individuals with higher levels of PsyCap consistently achieve enhanced performance outcomes [27]. Similarly, Lin et al. reported a positive association between PJ fit and JP [28]. Notwithstanding these findings, Kristof‐Brown et al.'s meta‐analysis identified only a modest relationship between PJ fit and JP, thereby underscoring the absence of consensus within the extant literature. In light of these considerations, the following hypotheses are advanced [29]:PJ fit is positively correlated with JP. PJ Fit, Burnout, and JP

Within the JD‐R theoretical framework, burnout is conceptualized as a maladaptive outcome arising from a sustained imbalance in which job demands exceed available resources. Such disequilibrium may result from excessive workload, role ambiguity or conflict, diminished autonomy, or insufficient social support from supervisors and colleagues [30]. A growing body of empirical research has consistently demonstrated an inverse association between PJ fit and job burnout, such that lower levels of fit are associated with heightened burnout [31].

Consistent with the tenets of JD‐R theory, burnout exerts a deleterious effect on job performance by undermining employees' functional capacity and psychological resilience [19]. Employees experiencing elevated levels of burnout are less capable of effectively managing workplace demands and consequently exhibit diminished performance relative to their less‐burned‐out counterparts [32]. Taris' [33] meta‐analytic findings further substantiate this relationship, revealing a negative association between exhaustion, a core component of burnout, and performance. Similarly, Bakker and Demerouti [19] documented a significant negative relationship between burnout and in‐role performance among nurses and police officers.

Despite the accumulating evidence, empirical investigations specifically addressing the relationship between PJ fit and burnout among pre‐hospital emergency personnel remain limited in scope. In response to this gap in the literature, the following hypotheses are proposed:

PJ fit is negatively correlated with burnout, and Burnout is negatively correlated with JP.

PsyCap and Burnout

A substantial body of empirical evidence indicates that elevated levels of PsyCap are associated with reduced burnout, as documented across multiple studies, for example [34, 35]. Within the JD‐R theoretical framework, personal resources are conceptualized as protective assets that enhance employee wellbeing and mitigate susceptibility to burnout. Building on this conceptualization, it can be posited that individuals with higher levels of PsyCap are less likely to experience emotional exhaustion and cynicism over time, owing to the buffering and restorative functions of these internal resources.

Notwithstanding these insights, longitudinal research examining the relationship between PsyCap and burnout across multiple temporal points remains comparatively scarce. To address this limitation, the present study systematically investigates this relationship within the context of pre‐hospital emergency staff, thereby extending the existing evidence base. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed:PsyCap is negatively associated with burnout.

PsyCap and Burnout as Serial Mediators

The present study further advances the literature by examining the potential sequential mediating roles of PsyCap and burnout in the relationship between PJ fit and JP. This integrative approach constitutes a substantive contribution; insofar as prior research has predominantly treated these constructs as independent mediators rather than as components of a serial mediation process within a two‐wave design. Drawing upon JD‐R theory [19], personal resources are conceptualized as dynamic protective factors that not only enhance wellbeing but also facilitate adaptive functioning in demanding work environments. From this perspective, it is argued that pre‐hospital emergency personnel who actively engage in continuous learning and professional development are more likely to cultivate elevated levels of positive psychological states, including optimism, hope, self‐efficacy, and resilience. These psychological resources, in turn, are expected to attenuate subsequent experiences of exhaustion and cynicism, thereby fostering enhanced job performance. Within this conceptual framework, PsyCap operates as a positive mediating mechanism, whereas burnout functions as a negative mediating pathway in the relationship between PJ fit and JP.

Consistent with extant research, PsyCap, particularly when reinforced through thriving at work, exerts a substantial mitigating effect on burnout [32], which is widely recognized as a debilitating condition associated with diminished performance [33]. Consequently, it can be argued that pre‐hospital emergency staff exhibiting high levels of PJ fit are better positioned to navigate occupational challenges, sustain a constructive cognitive–emotional orientation, and maintain motivation in the pursuit of professional goals. Their enhanced resilience further enables more effective recovery from adverse experiences, thereby exerting a favorable impact on JP.

Given that prior studies have not systematically examined the sequential mediating effects of PsyCap and burnout in the relationship between PJ fit and JP, the present study addresses this critical gap. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed:

The relationship between PJ fit and JP is mediated by PsyCap and burnout.

The relationship between PJ fit and JP is mediated by PsyCap and burnout.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This cross‐sectional study, both descriptive and analytical, was conducted from October 2024 to January 2025. It adhered to the STROBE statement, which aims to improve the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology and serves as a checklist for conducting such research [36]. The study involved 370 pre‐hospital emergency personnel from the southern region of Fars Province, Iran. The study population included emergency staff such as nurses, emergency medical technicians, operating room technicians, and anesthesia technicians.

Participants and Sampling

Participants were selected through convenience sampling based on the following inclusion criteria: full‐time employment in pre‐hospital emergency services, a minimum of 1 year of work experience, and consent to participate in the study. Following standard guidelines for studies involving structural equation modeling, the minimum sample size was determined based on the number of questionnaire items, aiming for 5–10 participants per item [37]. Given the total of 67 items across the instruments used, a minimum sample of 335 participants was required. Accounting for an anticipated 10% dropout rate, the final target sample size was set at 370. Ultimately, 384 questionnaires were completed; however, 14 were excluded due to incomplete responses, resulting in a final sample of 370 participants for data analysis and a response rate of 97%.

Data Collection Procedures

Prehospital emergency personnel were enrolled in the study after the research purpose was explained to them and they provided written informed consent. Due to the multicenter nature of the study, the research team selected an experienced volunteer from each center to conduct face‐to‐face interviews and collect information from participants, including socio‐demographic data and responses to other questionnaires. Data collection took place before participants began their work shifts, in a quiet environment at the workplace. Each questionnaire requires approximately 25–30 min to complete. The instruments used in this study included a demographic information form (covering age, gender, marital status, position, educational level, employment type, weekly working hours, and service department), along with four self‐report questionnaires completed by the emergency personnel.

Person–Job Fit Questionnaire

The assessment of perceived person–job fit was conducted using the six‐item scale developed by Cable and DeRue, measured on a five‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) [6]. Vogal and Feldman reported the scale's reliability with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.84 and a validity coefficient of 0.67 [38]. In Iran, the questionnaire's reliability was confirmed in a study by Goodarzi and colleagues, with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.80 [39]. In the present study, Cronbach's alpha for this scale was estimated at 0.85.

Paterson Job Performance Questionnaire

This questionnaire was created and developed by Patterson in 1970. It employs a scale ranging from 1 (rarely) to 4 (always), yielding a total score between 15 and 60. In Iran, it was translated by Jalali, [40]. The reliability coefficient was reported as 0.84, calculated using the Spearman–Brown formula and the odd–even split‐half method. Additionally, experts reviewed and confirmed the content validity of the questionnaire [40]. In the current study, Cronbach's alpha was exceptionally high at 0.98, indicating excellent internal consistency.

Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI)

Burnout was assessed using the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI), developed by Maslach in 1981. This inventory comprises 22 items that evaluate three dimensions of burnout: emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment. Scores for each subscale are summed, with higher scores in emotional exhaustion and depersonalization indicating greater burnout, while higher scores in personal accomplishment reflect lower levels of burnout. The original MBI demonstrated Cronbach's α value ranging from 0.77 to 0.89 [41]. In Iran, Peyghambari et al. validated the instrument, reporting Cronbach's alpha values of 0.95 for emotional exhaustion, 0.88 for depersonalization, 0.84 for personal accomplishment, and 0.94 for the total burnout score, confirming the instrument's reliability [42]. In the present study, Cronbach's alpha coefficients were 0.93 for emotional exhaustion, 0.83 for depersonalization, 0.90 for reduced personal accomplishment, and 0.96 for the total scale.

Luthans' Psychological Capital Questionnaire (PsyCap)

PsyCap was assessed using Luthans' Psychological Capital Questionnaire (PCQ, 2007), which consists of 24 items divided into four subscales: hope, resilience, optimism, and self‐efficacy. Each subscale contains six statements rated by participants on a six‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). The original questionnaire demonstrated good internal reliability, with Cronbach's alpha values exceeding 0.70 for each subscale [8]. In Iran, the scale's reliability was confirmed by Bahadori Khosroshahi and colleagues, who reported a Cronbach's alpha of 0.85 [43]. In the present study, Cronbach's alpha coefficients were 0.95 for hope, 0.92 for resilience, 0.93 for self‐efficacy, 0.73 for optimism, and 0.96 for total scale, indicating strong internal consistency across all dimensions.

Data Analysis

Initially, descriptive analyses were conducted using SPSS 27.0, including sample counts, percentages, mean values, standard deviations, and missing values to characterize the demographic features of the total sample. Additionally, Pearson's correlation analysis was employed to examine the relationships among person–job fit, job performance, PsyCap, and job burnout among pre‐hospital emergency personnel. Subsequently, IBM SPSS AMOS 27.0 software was used to assess model fit indices, including the χ²/degrees of freedom ratio (χ²/df), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), goodness of fit index (GFI), comparative fit index (CFI), and Tucker–Lewis index (TLI). The following criteria were applied to determine model adequacy: χ²/df < 5.00, RMSEA < 0.08, SRMR < 0.08, GFI > 0.90, CFI > 0.90, and TLI > 0.90 [44]. Prior to evaluating model fit indices, data normality was verified using Shapiro–Wilk tests. To test the hypotheses, demographic variables (age, gender, marital status, position, educational level, employment type, weekly working hours, and service department) were controlled for in the analysis of the dependent variable; none of these variables was significant predictors. Hayes' PROCESS macro version 4.2 (Model 6) was employed to investigate the mediating roles of PsyCap and job burnout in the relationship between person–job fit and job performance. Bootstrap confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using 5000 samples. A two‐tailed p value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics Consideration

This study strictly adhered to the principles outlined in the revised Declaration of Helsinki, an internationally recognized ethical guideline for physicians and researchers conducting human subject research. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, ensuring their understanding of the study's nature and their right to withdraw at any time. The confidentiality of participants' personal information was guaranteed. Additionally, participants were explicitly informed about the measures implemented to maintain the anonymity and confidentiality of their data. For further ethical oversight, approval for the study was granted by the Research Ethics Committee of Larestan University of Medical Sciences, Larestan, Iran (code: IR.LARUMS.REC.1404.010).

Common Method Bias

Common Method Bias (CMB) presents a significant challenge in behavioral research, particularly when the same respondents provide data for both independent and dependent variables. This bias can lead to either inflated or diminished correlations between constructs, ultimately compromising the validity of research findings. In this study, Harman's single‐factor test was employed to assess CMB. The results indicated that a single factor accounted for only 44.30% of the variance, which is below the 50% threshold. Additionally, a confirmatory factor analysis was conducted, incorporating an unmeasured latent common method factor (Δχ²/df = 9202–2144 = 4.29), which is below the critical value of 5. Both assessments confirmed that the data collected in this research showed no evidence of CMB [45].

Discussion

This research aimed to investigate the relationship between person–job fit and job performance, considering psychological capital (PsyCap) and job burnout as sequential mediators among pre‐hospital emergency workers. The results demonstrated a connection between person–job fit and job performance, as well as a relationship between person–job fit and psychological capital. These findings align with those of Chou et al., who highlighted the positive impact of job fit on performance, suggesting that a better match between an individual and their job enhances employee effectiveness [49]. Similarly, Farooqui et al. identified a significant relationship between person–organization fit and job performance, proposing that such congruence serves as a strategic human resource approach that promotes retention, increases motivation, boosts productivity, and ultimately improves overall performance [50]. In contrast to our findings, another study did not confirm a significant positive relationship between job performance and job fit [51]. The discrepancies among these studies may be attributed to mediating variables; in the present study, a protective factor—psychological capital—was included, which may contribute to improved job performance.

This study further advances current knowledge by simultaneously examining the effects of PsyCap and job burnout on job performance—a relationship that has not been previously explored, particularly among prehospital emergency workers. According to the DJ‐R theory, when individuals experience significant emotional exhaustion, they must exert additional effort to manage job‐related stress. This increased effort depletes their available resources, ultimately leading to suboptimal performance [52]. Therefore, it is essential to consider personal resources, such as PsyCap and person–job fit, as critical components of the JD‐R theory when designing psychological interventions in management and policy aimed at improving individual performance. A growing body of literature supports the link between person–job fit and job performance, especially when mediating variables are considered [53, 54]. Consistent with the current findings, previous studies have also reported a positive relationship between person–job fit and PsyCap, a result confirmed by multiple empirical investigations [51, 52].

The results further revealed an association between person–job fit and job burnout among pre‐hospital emergency personnel. Numerous studies have emphasized that alignment between job demands and individual preferences can significantly reduce burnout [53, 54]. The JD‐R model suggests that excessive job demands combined with insufficient organizational and/or personal resources can lead to burnout, which aligns with the findings of this study [19]. When job characteristics align with an employee's interests, competencies, values, and personality traits, individuals are more likely to experience job satisfaction, a sense of purpose, and belonging. Such alignment can lower occupational stress, foster resilience, and enhance intrinsic motivation—factors that collectively reduce the risk of burnout.

Additionally, the study revealed a statistically significant inverse relationship between psychological capital (PsyCap) and job burnout among pre‐hospital emergency workers. A study noted that nurses with higher PsyCap tend to experience less burnout [55]. This inverse relationship has been consistently documented across various studies [12, 56]. We illustrated the role of PsyCap as an individual resource that contributes to employee well‐being and mitigates burnout. Our analysis of this relationship was grounded in JD‐R theory, and the findings aligned with existing literature [12, 57]. PsyCap, by strengthening cognitive confidence and promoting proactive behavior, enables employees to engage more effectively with their roles. Additionally, fostering optimism and positive expectations, it contributes to enhanced performance. Therefore, PsyCap may serve as a valuable intervention for reducing job burnout [58].

Another important finding of this study is that emergency personnel with higher levels of Psychological Capital (PsyCap) perform better. PsyCap motivates individuals to utilize their personal resources effectively, and those with greater PsyCap tend to be more confident in successfully accomplishing tasks and persist despite difficulties and challenges. This conclusion is supported by the limited empirical research available in the literature [51, 59]. The results also revealed a statistically significant negative relationship between job burnout and job performance among pre‐hospital emergency staff. We examined how burnout relates to job performance and anticipated that burnout would pose serious challenges for both employees and organizations. As expected, our findings indicated that higher burnout levels are associated with lower job satisfaction and reduced job performance. These findings align with earlier empirical studies [51, 55, 60, 61]. Burnout impairs performance by contributing to emotional exhaustion, reduced motivation, and depersonalization [62]. Research has shown that factors such as role conflict, workload pressure, extended shifts, interpersonal tensions, limited autonomy, and inadequate recognition can lead to emotional exhaustion. This exhaustion may result in emotional withdrawal and detachment from both patients and professional responsibilities—referred to as depersonalization. Consequences of emotional exhaustion and depersonalization include diminished self‐efficacy, lower job satisfaction, reduced organizational commitment, increased turnover intentions, and elevated levels of burnout [63]. Liu et al. reported a high prevalence of mild burnout among pre‐hospital emergency staff and emphasized the urgent need for hospital and department administrators to prioritize staff mental health and implement timely preventive interventions [64].

The results of this study demonstrate that person–job fit, PsyCap, and job burnout collectively explain 71% of the variance in job performance. Furthermore, the proposed mediation model was supported. The indirect effect of person–job fit on job performance, sequentially mediated by PsyCap and job burnout, was found to be statistically significant. This finding aligns with theoretical frameworks such as Luthans' PsyCap Model [8] and the Person–Environment Fit Model [65]. According to these models, when individuals perceive a strong alignment between themselves and their job, they are more likely to exhibit higher levels of self‐efficacy, hope, optimism, and resilience. These attributes, which constitute the core dimensions of PsyCap, play a crucial role in preventing burnout and ultimately enhancing job performance. PsyCap and burnout served as sequential mediators in the relationship between person–job fit and performance. This study is the first to integrate the roles of these two factors within such a mediation framework. Consequently, our findings extend the JD‐R theory by indicating that personal competence fosters the development of additional personal resources, which act as protective factors against stress and contribute to improved performance. Moreover, this research provides longitudinal evidence supporting the JD‐R theory's perspective on the influence of personal resources on burnout [19]. Avey et al. also demonstrated that PsyCap, as an internal psychological resource, not only directly enhances job performance but also facilitates better occupational outcomes by mitigating psychological stress and burnout [66].

Strengths and Limitations

Among the key strengths of this study are the sufficiently large sample size and the use of psychometrically robust instruments with demonstrated reliability and validity. Nevertheless, the study is subject to certain limitations. Chief among these is the reliance on self‐report measures for data collection. Self‐reported data are inherently vulnerable to biases such as social desirability, misinterpretation of questionnaire items, and inaccuracies resulting from mental fatigue or lack of motivation. These issues may compromise the precision with which complex psychological constructs like PsyCap and burnout are assessed. Another important limitation is the study's cross‐sectional design, which prevents drawing firm conclusions about the direction and cause of the observed relationships. Establishing causal or temporal relationships between variables would necessitate the use of longitudinal or experimental research designs

Practical and Theoretical Implications

It is recommended that hospital managers, policymakers, and officials believe in their duties more than in the past and consider the mental and physical health of employees in stressful work environments, such as prehospital emergencies, to improve employees' job performance. Conducting research on psychological interventions aimed at improving PsyCap and reducing job burnout in work environments is recommended to improve the performance of prehospital personnel. These findings can be useful for managers and health organizations in designing training programs and interventions to improve individuals' job performance.

The results of this research carry significant implications for management. Firstly, achieving the optimal alignment between employees and their respective tasks and responsibilities has historically been a primary objective for human resource managers within organizations. It is highly recommended to implement more comprehensive programs that incorporate counseling and employee assistance services to help reduce employee stress and establish a support system, enabling employees to effectively preserve their psychological well‐being.

Consequently, substantial investments are allocated towards human and financial resources, which include meticulous recruitment and selection processes, comprehensive and targeted training, as well as diverse placement and promotion strategies. The findings of this research validate that the fit between individuals and their jobs has a remarkably positive impact on job performance, underscoring the importance of investing in person–job alignment as it enhances employees' creative performance.

Conclusion

The findings of this study suggest that person–job fit can enhance job performance among pre‐hospital emergency personnel by fostering PsyCap and reducing job burnout. These insights deepen our understanding of the psychological mechanisms underpinning job performance and shed light on the intricate interplay of psychological and behavioral processes in high‐stress occupational environments, such as emergency care settings, where personnel routinely face substantial psychological and physical demands. Accordingly, it is recommended that human resource managers and healthcare policymakers implement targeted interventions—such as training programs, resilience‐building workshops, and initiatives aimed at fostering hope and optimism. Moreover, adopting strategies to better align individual characteristics with job roles may serve as an effective means of improving job performance and minimizing burnout in high‐pressure work environments like emergency services.
