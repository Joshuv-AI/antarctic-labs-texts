St John’s Wort (Hypericum perforatum) Fresh Plant Tincture for Patients with Mild to Moderate Depression: A Prospective Observational Study
Authors: Natalie Kalbermatten, Reinhard Saller
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHigh-dose dry extract preparations of St John’s wort (Hypericum perforatum) (hd-SJW) are equally effective as synthetic antidepressants (SAD) for mild to moderate depression as shown by various randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Other available preparations of SJW such as fresh plant tinctures are dosed lower and no clinical study about their effect has been published so far. The aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of a SJW fresh plant tincture (C-SJW-ft) on depressive symptoms and its tolerability in patients comparable to patients reported in hd-SJW RCTs applying the same outcome, the Hamilton Depression Scale 17 (HAM-D 17).MethodsProspective observational study of outpatients with mild to moderate depressive episodes (ICD-10/DSM-IV) treated with C-SJW-ft. Outcomes were HAM-D 17 change from inclusion to 6 weeks (42 days ± 7 days), percentage of responders (>50% decline), and frequency and type of adverse events. Descriptive analysis was performed. From a systematic literature review of RCTs of hd-SJW applying HAM-D 17 as efficacy outcome at 6 weeks, the characteristics of the included patient population and the outcomes were extracted for comparison (rRCTs [“reference RCTs”]).ResultsFifty-two evaluable patients were included, 1 patient dropped out (switch to SADs). Of the 51 (per protocol [PP] “expanded”) patients, 42 completed the second HAM-D 17 within 42 ± 7 days (PP “strict”). The included patient population was comparable to the 10 identified rRCTs with hd-SJW for age (51/50.6 years [mean, pp strict/pp expanded], 40.2–51.4 years [mean, rRCTs] and gender (76%/75% female [pp strict/pp expanded], 54–86% [rRCTs]). The baseline HAM-D 17 was slightly lower (PP strict: 16.4 [mean, range 9–28]; PP expanded 16.9 [9–28) compared to rRCTs (19.7–22.8). The decline of HAM-D 17 in patients treated with C-SJW-ft (baseline/end of observation period) was 49% (PP strict) and 52% (PP expanded) comparable to the decline of 45–59% in the rRCTs of hd-SJW; likewise, responders were 50% and 57% compared to 42–70%. Adverse events were lower (4%) compared to 20–39% in the rRCTs.ConclusionIn an observational prospective patient cohort with mild to moderate depressive episodes corresponding to patients included in RCTs of hd-SJW, applying the same efficacy outcome and timeframe, C-SJW-ft showed comparable effects and good tolerability. Further clinical trials seem justified.

Introduction

Depression is a highly prevalent disorder characterized by low mood, diminished interest, and loss of pleasure. A depressive episode is defined as a period of at least 2 weeks with depressive symptoms and can be classified as mild, moderate, and severe based on the number, type, and severity of symptoms present and degree of functional impairment [1]. Treatment consists of psychotherapy and/or antidepressants, such as tricyclics (TCA), monoamine oxidase inhibitors, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRI), serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, and others [2]. However, there is a long-lasting debate and concern about the effectiveness of antidepressants because it is unclear if they have short-term benefits over placebo, as shown in a recent reanalysis of a network-meta-analysis (MA) [3], and long-term balance of benefits and harms is often understudied [4].

Apart from synthetic antidepressants, extracts of St. John’s wort (SJW, Hypericum perforatum L.) are prescribed widely for the treatment of depression [5]. Today, the total extract SJW is seen as active constituent [6] while isolated compounds such as pure hypericin showed effects in pharmacological models for depression only in very high doses not reached by extracts [7] and compounds without antidepressive activity when isolated, such as rutin, were relevant for the antidepressive activity of the extract [8]. A large body of evidence exists about the effectiveness of high-dosed dry extracts of SJW (hd-SJW) over placebo and non-inferiority to SAD, summarized in several systematic literature reviews (SLR) and MAs including a Cochrane review [5, 9–13]. Incidence of adverse events (most common: gastrointestinal symptoms, dizziness, tiredness) was significantly lower in the hd-SJW groups than in the SAD groups [12, 14]. A major concern which might limit more widespread use of hd-SJW are the potential drug interactions by an induction of cytochrome P450 enzymes (especially CYP3A4) and/or P-glycoprotein, which can lead to altered plasma levels of certain substances (e.g., immunosuppressants, antiretrovirals, cytostatics, contraceptives) [15]. The extent of this induction seems to correlate, at least to a certain extent, with the concentration of hyperforin in the preparation. With daily doses of <1 mg hyperforin, no significant interactions are to be expected, which is why dry extract preparations with low hyperforin have been developed [15]. However, it is unclear if hyperforin also adds to the beneficial effects of hd-SJW extracts [16, 17].

Apart from these hd-SJW dry extracts, other preparations such as liquid ethanolic extracts of dried plants (tinctures) or fresh plants (fresh plant tinctures, also “mother tinctures”) are among the approved preparations in the regulatory monographs of SJW by the Herbal Medicinal Products Committee by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) [18] or European Scientific Cooperative of Phytotherapy [19] and are registered as herbal medicines in German-speaking countries [20]. Their recommended daily dose according to the monographs, based on the drug equivalent (i.e., amount of dried plant used to produce the extract), is variable, but lower than the dose in the standardized dry-extract preparations [18, 19]. To our knowledge, no RCT or formal observational study investigated such liquid ethanolic extracts of SJW so far. The aim of this work was to assess the effects and tolerability of such a liquid ethanolic extract, a fresh plant tincture of SJW (C-SJW-ft), in patients with a depressive episode under real-life conditions; and to compare to the results with published literature of RCTs with hd-RCTs.

Patients and Methods

Study Design

This was a multicenter, prospective observational study. Patients were treated according to routine practice, with dosage and duration of therapy according to the treating physician. Physicians documented the course of the therapy with so-called Anwendungsbelegen [21], a documentation template for observational data from the Swiss regulatory drug board Swissmedic for the registration of herbal medicines. By the time of the data collection (start 2005), there was no need for ethical approval for collecting observational data in Switzerland. The data collection was part of the MD-thesis of the first author (N.K.).

Physicians

Outpatient physicians of any specialty who had ordered C-SJW-ft before were identified from address lists from the sales partner of the manufacturer. First, qualitative face-to-face interviews with a selection of the identified physicians were conducted by the research assistant (N.K.) with the aim to assess if C-SJW-ft might have a potential for patients with depression and if collecting observational data on its use might be feasible. Physicians were asked about their experience with the use of C-SJW-ft for depression in general, their preferred dose, about its effectiveness, side effects, and about practicability of data collection including applying a psychometric tool such as the HAM-D 17 [22] in clinical routine. An informal, exploratory content analysis of the interviews was conducted. The results of the interviews were discussed in the study team (N.K., R.S.) and served as basis for the concept of this study. The interviewed physicians were then invited to participate in the study.

Patients

Adult patients of any age with a diagnosis of a mild or moderate depressive episode (diagnosis according to ICD-10 or DSM-IV) where the treating physician decided to start C-SJW-ft were eligible. Patients were excluded if another pharmacological or non-pharmacological therapy for depression was started concomitantly. Other therapies and/or comorbidities were not formally recorded.

Study Medication

The production of C-SJW-ft (in German: Ceres Hypericum Urtinktur) follows the regulation of the German Homeopathic Pharmacopeia for production of herbal mother tinctures (regulation 3a according to the HAB [“Homöopathisches Arnzeibuch”]) [23]), i.e., cutting the blooming fresh plants, adding a defined amount of 86% (m/m) ethanol (which leads, in the case of SJW, to an alcohol concentration of 58% (m/m) in the fresh plant tincture), macerate the plant-alcohol-mixture for at least 10 days while regular stirring, and finally, press. The manufacturing process of C-SJW-ft includes some additional features: plants obtained only from organic or biodynamic agriculture or from wild harvesting, harvesting by hand instead of by machines at a defined stage (2/3 in buds, 1/3 in bloom), cutting by hand, milling/squeezing the alcohol-plant-mixture rhythmically about 15–20 min in a specially developed mill imitating a mortar, and storing the pressed tincture during average 2 years in a dark room with constant 14–18°C.

The dosage of C-SJW-ft in this observational study was according to the treating physician. The manufacturer’s recommended dose was 3 × 3 drops per day in average [24]. For regulatory purposes, the quantitative determination of compounds is not required for fresh plant tinctures according to the HAB; however, for this study, the concentrations of hypericin, hyperforin, and flavonoids (calculated as rutin) were determined.

Data Collection

The participating physicians were visited personally by the research assistant (N.K.) for instruction of study procedures. In addition to the study documents (patient demographics and basic data, HAM-D 17 [22] start and beginning), they received a leaflet “Diagnostic Criteria of the Depressive Episode,” where the criteria were listed in accordance with ICD-10 and DSM-IV [25, 26].

Data Collected

Patient demographics and basic data: gender, age, any concomitantly started therapy for depression (exclusion criteria), initial dosage of C-SJW-ft, change in dosage including reason for it, and adverse events (free text) including severity (mild, moderate, severe), course (disappeared without interruption of therapy/had to be treated/disappeared after stopping therapy/did not disappear after stopping therapy/appeared again after re-exposure/did not appear again after re-exposure), and relation to study drug (a = certain, b = probable, c = possible, d = not probable, e = excluded, f = not evaluable).

The HAM-D 17 was applied at study start and at the end of the evaluation. It is a clinician administered tool consisting of 17 items (typical features of depressive episodes), with a maximal score of 52. It is widely used in trials of antidepressants where their effectiveness is assessed based on the regression of the overall score [22].

Data Collection Procedures

At the beginning of the therapy, the physician completed patient demographics and basic data and the HAM-D 17. After 6 weeks of therapy (with a scope of 7 days), the HAM-D 17 was completed again, and any changes in dose and adverse events were documented. Study forms were then sent back to the study center. For each documentation of a treatment course, physicians received 50 Swiss Francs as compensation. After 5 months, the physicians were actively contacted by phone by the research assistant (N.K.) to clarify potential ambiguities.

Sample Size

Given the exploratory nature of this study, no sample size analysis was performed. We considered a number of 50 patients as a potentially informative sample, as it was also recommended by the regulatory board for this type of observational data [21].

Data Analysis

After receipt of the study documents, the data were entered in an Excel file and analyzed descriptively using the statistic software SPSS. Average HAM-D 17 scores at the beginning and after 6 weeks and relative score decrease were calculated. Responders (i.e., patients with a reduction of the HAM-D 17 total score between baseline and after 6 weeks of 50% or more), a common definition in trials for interventions against depression [27], were determined.

Identification, Selection, and Analysis of Reference Studies

To identify RCTs of SJW in patients with mild to moderate depression which applied HAM-D 17 as outcome instrument, a SLR of SLRs and MA of RCTs of SJW for depression was performed in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines [28]. We decided for this pragmatic approach because several high-quality SLR/MA of RCTs of interest had already been published and the SLR was not the main aim of this work. SLRs and MA were eligible if they included RCTs of SJW (any preparation) for depression. The electronic databases PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Cochrane Library were systematically searched from database inception until January 23, 2023 {search string: ([“St John’s wort” OR hypericum] AND [“systematic literature review” OR meta-analysis] AND depressi*)}. Only studies in English or German were considered.

All the included RCTs in the included SLRs or MA were then screened for eligibility. The eligible population was adult patients with mild to moderate depression, the intervention of interest was treatment with SJW (any preparation), the primary outcome was HAM-D 17 measured at inclusion and after 6 weeks, the study design of interest RCTs (SJW versus SAD or placebo, with a neutral or positive outcome favoring SJW). Conference abstracts, books, scholarly reviews, and case reports were excluded. All potentially eligible RCTs were studied at full text level and discussed in the study team (R.S., N.K.). Quality of included studies was not formally assessed. The following data were extracted from the included RCTs: study medication, daily dose, comparison (placebo and/or SAD), sex, age, study settings, included/analyzed participants, mean HAM-D 17 score verum/placebo at the beginning, duration of therapy, change of HAM-D 17 score verum/comparison group(s) (mean, percentage), efficacy, safety (frequency and type of adverse events), and authors’ conclusion. No MA of data was conducted.

Additionally, a scoping search for observational studies was performed in PubMed, Embase, and Scopus (search string: [St John’s wort OR hypericum] AND depress* AND observation*) with the aim to complement our data with already published observational data with comparable features. Eligible were studies with adult patients with mild to moderate depression treated with SJW (any preparation) and measured HAM-D 17 at beginning and after 6 weeks.

Results

Timeframe

Data collection took place from August 2005 to January 2007.

Physicians

A total of 25 physicians were contacted by phone, 15 agreed to have an interview. All were residents in German-speaking Switzerland. Results of the interviews suggested that C-SJW-ft for depression seemed to have a potential and its documentation was likely to be feasible. Of the interviewed physicians, 9 (60%) agreed to participate in the investigation, and a 10th physician joined later. Reasons for not participating in the data collection were mainly time constraints. Eight of these 10 physicians submitted patient documentations, 7 were general practitioners (GPs) and one was psychiatrist for children and adolescents.

Patient Flow

Documentations of 55 patients were submitted. Of these, 3 were excluded (2: diagnosis other than mild to moderate depression [hypothyroidism and postpartum adaption disorder, respectively], 1: started concomitant other therapy for depression). One 20-year-old male patient wished to start SAD after 3 weeks treatment with C-SJW-ft. The treating physician assessed this event as not related to C-SJW-ft. This patient was not evaluated for HAM-D 17 scores but for safety (ITT-population, n = 52; PP population, n = 51). Of the 51 evaluable patients (“PP expanded”), in 42 the second HAM-D 17 was completed within the defined timeframe (42 days ± 7 days) (“PP strict”), and in 9 the second HAM-D 17 was completed before or after (range: 21–70 days) (Fig. 1, patient flow).

Patient Population, Dose of C-SJW-Ft, HAM-D 17 Results, and Adverse Events in the Present Observational Study

A total of 76%/75% patients were female (pp strict/pp expanded). Mean age was 51/50.6 years. Initial HAM-D 17 score was 16.4/16.9 (mean, median: 16/16). Daily dosage (drops) of C-SJW-ft was 12 drops/11 drops. HAM-D 17 reduction was 48.6%/51.7%, and percentage of responders were 50%/56.9%.

A 58-year-old female patient reported increased flushes. The treating physician assessed the relation to C-SJW-ft as “probable.” No other AE was reported. Frequency of adverse events was therefore 4% (2/52) (Table 1).

The database search of SLRs yielded 57 results (56 after removing of duplicates). Eleven were SLRs of RCT’s of SJW extracts against SAD and/or placebo for depression. The Cochrane review by Linde and Knüppel [41], published in 2005 and updated in 2008, was taken as reference for all studies published before 2007, therefore SLRs published earlier (n = 5) were not considered (Fig. 2, PRISMA-Diagram). Patient Population, HAM-D 17 Results, and Adverse Events in the Reference Studies

The rRCTs included between 70 and 388 subjects (total number of subjects: 2276) from one to 40 centers (in most cases GP and/or psychiatric practices). The proportion of female subjects in the various hypericum subgroups in the reference studies was 56%–86% and of male subjects 14%–44%. Mean age was 40.2–51.4 years and the initial HAM-D 17 score was between 19.7 and 22.8 (mean). The score reduction of the hypericum groups in the reference studies from baseline until study end at 6 weeks ranged from 45% to 59%, the responder rate ranged from 42% to 70%. The rRCTs with lowest baseline HAM-D (Behnke: 20.0, Kalb: 19.7; Laakmann: 20.3, 20.9) had a decline of 50%, 55%, and 42%/49%, respectively, responders were 55%, 62%, and 39%/49%, respectively; the rRCTs with highest baseline HAM-D (Kasper: 22.8, 22.6; Philipp: 22.7; Uebelhack: 22.6; Woelk: 22.4) a decline of 58%/57%, 59%, 48%, and 53%, respectively, responders were 70%/61%, 67%, 59%, and 43%, respectively. Reductions of HAM-D 17 scores in the placebo groups (7 studies) were 15, 17, 29, 37, 37, 41, and 45%; responders were 6, 32, 33, 39, 42, 43, and 48%.

Frequency of patients experiencing adverse events in the hypericum groups ranged from 20 to 39%. Relation to study medication was not assessed in half of the studies [16, 29, 30, 33, 35], no relation between adverse event and study medication was seen in two studies [31, 37]. In the three other studies, adverse events that were possibly or probably related to SJW were dry mouth, headache, nausea, dizziness [34, 36], sweating, asthenia [36], constipation, palpitation [34], GI-disorders, menstrual disorders, and photosensitivity reactions [32] (Table 1).

Search for Observational Studies

The scoping search yielded 49 hits. Forty-five hits were not original studies; 3 hits were original observational studies of SJW but not eligible [38–40]. One was a SLR of observational studies (n = 17) [41], with one of the included observational studies fulfilling the inclusion criteria [42]. This study, however, included patients with very high HAM-D scores at the beginning, not corresponding to the diagnosis of mild to moderate depression. Therefore, it was not used as reference.

Discussion

In this observational study, outpatients with mild to moderate depressive episodes treated with C-SJW-ft over 6 weeks showed a comparable decrease of depression intensity, measured with the HAM-D 17, as patients treated with hd-SJW in RCTs, and C-SJW-ft was well tolerated. The comparability of our population with the populations of the rRCTs was given in terms of gender (more females than males), age (middle age), and diagnosis (mild to moderate depressive episode). Due to the non-interventional nature of our study with the aim to have as little interference as possible on the real-life situation, some of the parameters differ slightly from those of the rRCTs. First, physicians could not always see patients after 6 weeks exactly, which led to the fact that only 42 patients completed the observational period within 42 days ± 7 days from study start (PP “strict”) and 9 patients had a longer (n = 8) or shorter (n = 1) observation period (PP “extended”). However, only 2 of the rRCTs included an actual duration of study treatment in the results part, and it is unclear if all the others were exactly sticking to 6 weeks. Several RCTs with SJW have been published with a duration of 8 weeks or longer with comparable results as with 6 weeks [43–45]; therefore, we see no major limitation in this difference of our data from the rRCTs. Further, we did not define a minimal HAM-D 17 score for inclusion. Physicians were instructed both orally and in written form about the importance to document patients with mild to moderate depression only, and the diagnosis criteria according to DMS-IV and ICD-10 were part of the study material. Our HAM-D 17 score at the beginning was lower (PP “expanded”: mean 16.9/median 16, PP “strict”: mean 16.4/median 16) than in the rRCT (mean HAM-D 17 in the SJW groups: 19.7–22.8). The reduction of HAM-D 17 scores in our observational studies (49%/52%) is comparable to the 3 RCTs with the lowest baseline HAM-D 17 score with 42%–55% [16, 29, 31], whereas in the 4 RCTs with the highest baseline HAM-D 17 scores a slightly higher range of decline (48%–59%) was reported [32, 34, 36, 37]. For responders our study reported 55%/57%, the lowest baseline RCTs 39%–55% and the highest baseline RCTs 43%–70%. Since in the RCT’s of hd-SJW slightly higher effects (decline HAM-D 17, responders) are reported for patients with higher baseline HAM-D 17 scores than lower baseline HAM-D 17 scores, a common phenomenon in depression trials [46], the results of our observational study with lower baseline HAM-D 17 score remain comparable to the rRCTs. In trials for antidepressants, pronounced placebo-effects are a well-known, widely discussed phenomenon [3, 47]. In the 7 rRCTs with a placebo arm, reduction of HAM-D 17 score (range 17–41%, median 37%) and percentage of responders (16–48%, median 39%) were considerable, but clearly lower than in our observational study (reduction HAM-D 17 48%/52%; responders 50%/57%). In the present study, there was only one patient (2%) with a documented adverse event that was assessed as “likely related” to C-SJW-ft (increased flushing). This adverse event is yet unknown for SJW and was not reported in the selected RCT’s.

To our knowledge, this is the first published study of a fresh plant tincture of SJW for depression. However, observational studies [48] as well as RCTs [49–51] showing clinical effects of other fresh plant tinctures for other indications have been published before, equally applying lower doses of drug equivalent or specific compounds than dry extract preparations from the same plant used for the same indication. Fresh plant tinctures were among the traditional preparations of herbal medicine including SJW before the development of standardized dry extract preparations in the 1980s [52]. Their usual dose is around 3 × 20 to 3 × 30 drops (3–4.5 mL)/day, corresponding, in the case of SJW, to around 300–450 mg drug equivalent/day (usual drug to extract ratio [DER] around = 1:10). With the development of dry extracts of SJW in the 1980s, the dose was increased and lies between 1,500 and 12,600 mg drug equivalent in the extracts used in the rRCTs (Table 2), but the optimal dose is a matter of debate since then. Noteworthy, no dose-finding studies have been performed [53]. The extracts on the reference studies, which reflect the currently available SJW dry extracts on the market in German-speaking countries, differ markedly in terms of daily intake of extract, drug equivalent, hypericin, and hyperforin. However, despite this variety, all of them showed an antidepressive effect over placebo or were equivalent to SAD (online suppl. Table S2). Recently, Nicolussi et al. [54] presented an analysis of clinical efficacy induced by different daily doses of (different) SJW extracts from 28 clinical trials where they identified a plateau at 180 mg native extract dose per day, with higher doses not yielding a better effect on the HAM-D 17 [54]. Even though different dry extracts of SJW are not fully comparable due to differences in extraction solvents and methods and the trials with lower doses are older and therefore study methodology might no longer comply with current standards, these data add interesting insight to the discussion about an optimal dose, also considering the concern of drug interactions if hyperforin intake is more than 1 mg/day. In summary, the optimal dose of SJW extracts is not clear, varies among extract types, and was generally lower before the development of the dry extracts [55].

In the present observational study, the prescribed daily dose of the examined SJW-extract (C-SJW-ft) was again lower (mean: 12 drops PP “expanded”/11 drops PP “strict,” corresponding to a drug equivalent of below 100 mg/day [Table 1]), which is lower than in any dry extract on the market and also lower than in other tinctures and fresh plant tinctures available. Physicians were free to choose a dose; the manufacturer’s recommended average daily dose was 9 drops. According to the manufacturer’s literature, this low dose reflects clinicians’ and patients’ experience of a clinical effect when using the manufacturer’s fresh plant tinctures [24]; similar doses have also been used in a qualitative study of another fresh plant tincture by the same manufacturer [56]. Reasons why SJW extracts could be effective in a markedly lower dose than clinically studied dry extract preparations can only be hypothesized. It is generally accepted today that the total SJW extract is the active compound, not isolated substances. However, about 30–50% of the compounds in SJW extracts are not yet structurally assigned [6]; among them could be further constituents directly or indirectly contributing to the overall clinical effectiveness. Manufacturing processes could have an influence on the availability of such yet undefined constituents in extracts. For example, harvesting time has been shown to influence the content of components such as hypericin and rutin [57] and the drying process was shown to have an influence on the contents of volatile substances [58]. Recently, Barmaverain et al. [59] showed that C-SJW-ft exhibited a higher antioxidant level than other commercially available fresh plant tinctures. While clinical significance of this finding is currently not known, antioxidant capacity might serve as marker for quality of herbal fresh plant tinctures. Other factors, such as a positive influence of the rhythmic grinding in the mortar on the quality of the extract, similar to rhythmic pharmaceutic processes used in anthroposophic pharmacy [60], could widen the spectrum of hypothesis, but clearly need further research.

The sensory qualities of taste and smell of tinctures and fresh plant tinctures [61], “building a bridge” from the drug to the herb with its unique characteristics, may contribute to the observed effects. In the physician interviews carried out to plan this study, several of the participating physicians stated to share characteristic aspects of SJW [62] and its analogy to the depressed individuum with their patients when starting therapy with C-SJW-ft. Such an individualized approach choosing plant-based remedies by considering aspects of analogy is an element of “whole system approaches” such as anthroposophic medicine [63]. According to a large body of evidence, the context of a treatment-situation is an important part of real-life effectiveness of therapeutic measures [64, 65]. Physicians and patients’ expectations toward SJW for depression could add to the purely pharmacological effect in a real-life setting, as shown in a secondary analysis of a large SJW trial [66, 67]. Further, qualitative data suggest that patients may attribute specific meanings to similarly produced fresh plant tinctures [56]. To foster these non-pharmacological aspects in real-life treatment concepts to optimize therapeutic response seems sensible. In summary, our study results imply that the use of low-dosed SJW preparations such as C-SJW-ft might make sense clinically, despite the markedly lower intake of drug equivalent and well-known compounds such as hypericine and hyperforine compared to hd-SJW extracts.

Our prospective, observational study has several limitations. First, the non-interventional character of this study without a direct control group obviously does not allow a statement about the causal relationship between the use of C-SJW-ft and the change in HAM-D 17 scores. Bias of physicians and patients, psychological support of GPs, placebo effect, and regression to the mean could be, among others, alternative explanations. However, other observational studies of SJW extracts with slightly different designs have yielded similar results [41], and the careful selection of reference RCTs by SLR supports our observational findings. Second, the identified RCTs were found by extraction of results from a SLR of SLRs and MAs, we might have missed RCTs not reported in the 6 SLRs/MAs. Third, the number of treating GPs (n = 8) might lead to an overvaluation of individual factors, on the other side, 6.4 (mean) patients per GP contributes to reduction of variability of GPs. The fact that the HAM-D 17 is a physician-, not a patient-reported instrument, makes the intensity of depression dependent of the clinician’s judgment; however, all identified RCTs used that outcome tool, and the comparability of our results with RCT evidence was one of our main goals. Fourth, the management of major depression at baseline was not included in our inclusion criteria, it remains unknown, if patients might have just finished treatment with an SAD or psychotherapy or were on ongoing psychotherapeutic treatment without telling the GP. However, in none of the 10 rRCTs (online suppl. Table S2) such information is reported.

Conclusion

This is the first study of a fresh plant tincture of SJW (C-SJW-ft) in patients with depression. It informs clinicians and patients about the real-life use, good tolerability, and positive course of depressive symptoms in patients receiving C-SJW-ft despite the significantly lower dosage compared to the widely studied dry extract preparations. As results from real-life observational studies gain importance in medicine [68], our data may inform practice as a pilot study and may serve as a basis for further research.
