Background

Extrachromosomal circular DNA (eccDNA) has recently emerged as a promising non-invasive biomarker with significant potential for cancer detection and monitoring [1, 2]. The distinctive structural characteristics of eccDNA, particularly its covalently closed circular conformation that lacks the terminal ends of linear DNA, render it resistant to exonuclease-mediated degradation [3, 4]. This molecular stability is exemplified by experimental data showing that after 48 h in plasma, only 14.84% of circular DNA underwent degradation compared to 21.59% of linear DNA [3]. The combination of this stability with its detectable presence in various body fluids, including plasma, establishes eccDNA as a highly suitable analyte for liquid biopsy applications [2]. Moreover, the unique junctional sequences characteristic of eccDNA molecules provide a critical advantage in specificity, enabling targeted detection strategies that effectively distinguish eccDNA from the background of linear cell-free DNA fragments [2].

Tumor-derived eccDNAs originating from specific genomic loci, such as ALK and ETV6, have demonstrated considerable diagnostic value across multiple cancer types when detected in both tissue specimens and plasma [2]. Intriguingly, small eccDNAs derived from specific genomic loci demonstrated diagnostic performance in both tissue (AUC ≥ 0.8) and plasma (AUC > 0.9) samples. When combined with conventional biomarkers like CEA/CA19-9, these small eccDNAs significantly improved diagnostic accuracy [1, 2], suggesting their potential as stable, cost-effective molecular tools for cancer screening. Beyond diagnostic applications, eccDNA demonstrates substantial potential for cancer monitoring. The detection of cell-free eccDNA in circulation provides opportunities for dynamic surveillance of minimal residual disease following therapeutic interventions such as tumor resection [2]. Furthermore, the identification of patient-specific eccDNA signatures shared between tumor tissue and plasma offers a promising approach for developing personalized biomarkers to track therapeutic efficacy and disease progression [1, 5].

The biogenesis of eccDNA involves multiple molecular mechanisms, including DNA damage repair pathways, DNA replication, R-loop formation, breakage-fusion-bridge cycles, chromothripsis, as well as episome and translocation-deletion-amplification models [6–9]. These diverse formation mechanisms give rise to heterogeneous eccDNA populations within tumor cells, with sizes ranging from hundreds of base pairs to several megabases. Based on their size and genomic origins, eccDNAs can be classified into several subtypes, including small polydispersed DNA, microDNA, telomeric circles, and extrachromosomal rDNA circles, each of which exhibits distinct biological functions [7]. Notably, eccDNAs frequently harbor amplified oncogenes and regulatory elements, contributing to tumor heterogeneity, malignant progression, and therapeutic resistance [10, 11]. However, the comprehensive characterization of eccDNAs remains challenging due to structural complexity, low abundance, and sequence heterogeneity.

Recent advances in long-read sequencing technologies and computational algorithms have significantly enhanced eccDNA detection and analysis [1]. Our previous research established a novel Nanopore sequencing pipeline for investigating eccDNAs [2]. Current eccDNA identification tools primarily rely on either short-read, such as AmpliconArchitect [12], Circle-Map [13], and ECCsplorer [14], or long-read sequencing approaches such as NanoCircle [15], CReSIL [16], and Flec [17]. However, these approaches rely exclusively on sequencing data while neglecting other omics layers that may provide critical biological insights, despite the growing prevalence of multi-omics integration across various research domains [18, 19]. To address this limitation, we developed a multimodal deep learning framework that integrates distinct biological data types: DNA sequences, single nucleotide variations/insertions-deletions (SNV/InDel), structural variations (SV), DNA modifications, RNA modifications, and RNA expression profiles. Our model employs an adaptive gated fusion mechanism to dynamically weight the contributions of each modality while effectively suppressing noise from sparse signals. Additionally, we implemented a masking strategy to filter non-biological artifacts, substantially improving identification specificity.

Beyond identification performance, the multi-omics integration in this study serves as an analytical framework to evaluate the relative contributions of different biological modalities to eccDNA formation. This approach provides the first comprehensive multi-omics characterization of eccDNA associated features including systematic correlation analyses across different modalities. Our multi-omics analysis demonstrates that the DNA sequence contributes the highest proportion to eccDNA identification. Furthermore, we demonstrate that conventional unmapped read sets contain eccDNA derived sequences representing an underutilized resource for biomarker discovery. We established a pipeline for eccDNA identification that supports candidate sequence analysis and enables the recovery of these hidden targets from unmapped read sets. For practical and cost-effective application, we ultimately provide a convenient web server that requires only DNA sequences for eccDNA identification.

Methods

Patients and samples

Colorectal cancer patients were recruited from Beijing Hospital. The study utilized 10 sets of patient-matched multi-omics data, comprising paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues from 5 individuals. All participants had a histopathologically confirmed diagnosis of stage II or III disease within one year, and had not received any prior chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or radiation therapy. Surgical resection provided matched tumor and adjacent normal tissue samples from five patients. All cases exhibited microsatellite stability, and no limitations were placed on age, sex, or smoking history in this preliminary study. Fresh tissue specimens were promptly harvested, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and subsequently evaluated by expert pathologists to ensure sample integrity.

Nanopore sequencing

Genomic DNA was isolated from tissue samples using the MagAttract HMW DNA Kit (Qiagen). DNA concentration and purity were assessed using both the Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer and Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). DNA was purified and concentrated with AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter) and quantified using a Qubit® 3.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies). Nanopore sequencing was conducted on the PromethION platform (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) equipped with R9.4.1 chip. Following quality control according to the manufacturer's protocol, prepared DNA libraries were loaded for sequencing. Raw signal acquisition and basecalling were performed using MinKNOW (v23.11.8) [20]. Subsequently, Dorado (v0.7.2) was used to convert the raw POD5 signal files into BAM formats for downstream analysis. For accurate detection of modified bases, we utilized the super-accurate basecalling model dna_r10.4.1_e8.2_400bps_sup@v5.0.0.

Total RNA extraction was performed using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol, including RNA precipitation, washing, and solubilization. RNA quality assessment was conducted using a Capillary Electrophoresis System, with a minimum of 300 ng poly(A)-tailed RNA retained for subsequent Oxford Nanopore Direct RNA Sequencing. Library preparation followed the manufacturer's guidelines (version: DRS_9195_v4_revB_20Sep2023). Sequencing was performed on the PromethION platform (Oxford Nanopore Technology) equipped with an FLO-PRO004RA sequencing module. The process utilized the EXP-WSH004 flow cell wash kit and SQK-RNA004 Direct RNA Sequencing Kit (Oxford Nanopore Technology). Following successful quality control, prepared libraries were loaded onto the sequencing chip. Raw signal data acquisition was carried out using MinKNOW software (v23.11.8) [20] with rna004_130bps_hac@v3.0.1. The pipeline of eccDNA sequencing and other tumor dataset were same with our former study [2, 21]. We utilized a novel sequencing workflow based on the Nanopore sequencing platform to profile naturally existing full-length small eccDNAs. This methodology involves the selective removal of linear DNA background using exonucleases, followed by high-efficiency enrichment of circular molecules via rolling circle amplification. By leveraging the long-read capabilities of the Nanopore platform, we can capture full-length sequences that span the junction sites, enabling the precise reconstruction of the original eccDNA at the single-molecule level (Fig. 3A) [2, 21].

Illumina sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted and quantified via Nanodrop 8000 and Qubit dsDNA HS Assay. Fragmentation to 180–280 bp was performed using a Covaris Library preparation utilized the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit, including end repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation with IDT for Illumina indexes. Size selection was performed using AMPure XP beads. Target capture employed the Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon V7 kit, followed by post-capture PCR amplification. Library quality was verified using the Agilent 5400 Fragment Analyzer and qPCR quantification. Paired-end 150 bp sequencing was conducted on Illumina NovaSeq 6000.

Sequence signal processing

For eccDNA flanking sequence analysis, genomic regions spanning 100 bp upstream and downstream of each eccDNA were extracted, with fragments sized 200–1000 bp selected for further analysis. DNA sequences were computationally transformed into one-hot encoded vectors, incorporating a masking matrix to differentiate authentic base signals from zero-padded regions. A comprehensive bioinformatics pipeline was implemented for variant detection: initial read preprocessing was performed using fastp (v0.20.0) [22], followed by alignment to the GRCh38 reference genome via BWA-MEM (v0.7.17) [23] and PCR duplicate removal using sambamba (v0.7.1) [24]. Base quality score recalibration was subsequently conducted with GATK (v4.2.0.0) [25–27], utilizing established variant databases (1000 Genomes Project, dbSNP v138, Mills indels), prior to variant calling with GATK Mutect2 (v4.2.0.0) [25–27] and quality-based filtration. The resulting allele frequency data were systematically organized into SNV/InDel signal matrices. Structural variant analysis involved long-read alignment through Minimap2 (v2.1) [28], read correction using Racon (v1.5.0) [29], and variant calling via NanoSV (v1.2.4) [30]. DNA and RNA modification profiles were respectively characterized employing modkit and xPore (v2.0) [31], with quantitative modification rates integrated into subsequent analytical models. Finally, eccDNA expression was quantified by aligning reads with Minimap2 (v2.1) [28] and counting them with Nanocount [32].

Multimodal deep learning model-eccDNA identifier eccDNAOmix

We developed a multimodal deep learning framework, eccDNAOmix, to identify eccDNA by integrating seven distinct biological data types.

Dataset construction and threshold optimization

The study utilized 10 sets of matched multi-omics data derived from tumor and adjacent normal tissues across 5 colorectal cancer patients. The total number of eccDNA samples used for training and validation is 84,676, alongside an equal number of negative control samples, totaling 169,352 samples. Because these individual eccDNA events are molecularly independent, this dataset provides sufficient data instances for the neural network to converge reliably during training. To establish a control group, we randomly generated negative controls within the same genomic background using bedtools shuffle [33] based on the BED files of the eccDNAs. During generation, we constrained the negative samples to match the true eccDNAs in both sequence count and length distribution, ensuring no genomic positional overlap with any known eccDNAs.

The combined dataset was randomly split at the sample level into a 70% training set of 118,546 samples and a 30% validation set of 50,806 samples, maintaining a 1:1 class balance in both subsets. This split maximizes the use of our multi-omics data for model training while respecting the molecular independence of individual events.

To determine the actual classification threshold while avoiding data leakage, we implemented a dynamic threshold optimization strategy based on Youden’s J statistic [34]. Given that our model employs a weighted binary cross-entropy loss during training to address potential internal batch imbalances, the output probability distribution undergoes an intrinsic shift. To establish a decision boundary, the optimal threshold was calculated utilizing the probability distributions derived from the independent training set. The testing set was isolated during this optimization phase. The derived optimal thresholds were subsequently blindly applied to the validation set and web server implementation, ensuring an evaluation of the model's discriminative capability.

The shared MLP consisting of two fully connected layers with an intermediate ReLU activation maps these descriptors to a compressed bottleneck subspace with a reduction ratio of 8, before expanding them back to the original channel count C to produce the final broadcastable gating weights.

Positional features, including SNV/InDel and SV data, contain dependencies over long genomic intervals. Explicit masking was applied to eliminate zero-padding interference. To avoid hard-coded static bin sizes, the 1D-CNN's receptive field dynamically aggregated these positional signals. To extract local sequence contexts, these positional matrices were first processed by a shallow CNN feature extractor before being fed into Transformer encoders with sinusoidal positional encoding and a dropout rate of 0.5. For DNA 5mC/5hmC, DNA 6 mA, RNA modifications, and RNA expression, features reflect overall biological abundance or frequency matrices within the eccDNA region and lack linear sequence-motif continuity. To capture complex non-linear decision logic from these quantitative matrices, we employed a frozen XGBoost model as a deterministic feature hasher [38]. We first trained an XGBoost model configured with 150 estimators, a maximum depth of 5, a learning rate of 0.1, and a subsample rate of 0.8 exclusively on the training set. For each input sample, the leaf node indices across all 150 trees were extracted using the apply mechanism. To transform these high-dimensional discrete decision pathways into a format suitable for neural network integration, they were pre-projected into compact 32-dimensional dense vectors using a sparse feature building module. These vectors, reshaped to a tensor of (B, 32, 1), are subsequently fed into a specialized sub-network termed SparseModalityNet. Within this module, the pre-hashed features are processed by a lightweight projection network comprising a point-wise 1D-Conv layer with a kernel size of 1 purely for linear dimensionality reduction without modeling local continuity, followed by batch normalization, LeakyReLU activation, and adaptive average pooling, before a final fully connected layer. This architecture further aligns the XGBoost-derived features into the shared 32-dimensional latent space, enabling end-to-end joint optimization of the downstream neural network pathways via gradient descent, while the XGBoost parameters remain frozen as a deterministic feature hasher.

To validate the stability, generalizability, and robustness of our model, we implemented both leave-one-patient-out (LOPCV) and standard tenfold cross validation on the unified dataset.

Baseline model construction

To validate the architectural rationale of the eccDNAOmix framework, we constructed three comparative baselines under identical evaluation conditions to target different components of the multi-omics integration. The baseline 1 was a global end-to-end MLP designed to evaluate the necessity of structural modeling by replacing the entire proposed framework. In this configuration, all seven modalities including continuous DNA sequences were flattened and concatenated into a single vector before being processed by a global MLP, which eliminated all feature extractors. The baseline 2 utilized a local modality replacement strategy incorporating logistic regression (LR) and MLP architectures. This approach preserved the deep 1D-CNN extractor exclusively for the continuous DNA sequence modality while replacing the original extractors for all other non-sequence modalities with LR coupled with MLPs to test alternative feature selection methods. Finally, the baseline 3 partial-MLP was constructed to address the specific mapping of discrete quantitative features. This third configuration retained the extractors for DNA sequences, structural variations, and mutation modalities. It specifically replaced only the point-wise 1D-CNN modules with kernel_size = 1 within the quantitative pathways with standard MLPs. The controlled setup enabled us to evaluate the impact of independent weights versus shared weights on discrete feature dimensionality reduction for modalities such as RNA expression and epigenetic modifications.

eccDNA in unmapped read set

Due to the stochastic fragmentation during library preparation, eccDNA junction sequences are frequently disrupted and randomly broken, which prevents them from being aligned to the reference genome and leads to their erroneous classification as unmapped reads. To address this issue, we first performed sequence polishing by Racon (v1.5.0) [29] to correct the raw Nanopore long reads with high-accuracy Illumina short-read data, thereby significantly enhancing base-calling precision. Subsequently, using Nanopore DNA sequencing and eccDNA sequencing data, we aligned both candidate eccDNA sequences and their junction-spanning reads against the unmapped read sets to recover and validate these eccDNA molecules. To validate a 472-bp candidate eccDNA identified from unmapped reads, PCR amplification was performed using the following primers: forward, 5 ‘-TTGGCAAGAGTGGCTGCCTT-3’ and reverse, 5 ‘-CATCTGTTCTGCGTGGCTCC-3’.

Multimodal feature correlation analysis

To normalize eccDNA biological signal lengths, we implemented linear interpolation to rescale variable-length signals to a uniform size. Genomic element enrichment for eccDNA was performed using HOMER [41]. Our multimodal feature correlation analysis employed weighted cross-modal correlations across all pairwise modality combinations. The analysis incorporated four key analytical features. First, Spearman’s rank correlation was used to detect nonlinear monotonic relationships. Second, variance-weighted prioritization of biologically relevant signals was performed via principal component analysis. Third, automatic dimensional alignment was applied to accommodate varying numbers of principal components across modalities. Finally, direction-independent assessment of association strength was conducted using absolute correlation values, optimized for preliminary biological feature screening. The analytical pipeline initiated with dimensional alignment of principal components for each modality pair, followed by computation of component-wise Spearman correlations. These values were aggregated via a weighted average based on the mean explained variance from both modalities' principal components. This process generated a symmetric correlation matrix that quantifies inter-modal relationships among latent features, where elevated values indicated stronger associations between dominant variation patterns. Our integrated approach synergized the dimensionality reduction advantages of PCA with robust correlation metrics, while maintaining each modality's intrinsic information hierarchy through variance-based weighting.

Results

Multimodal deep learning identifies eccDNA

Through comprehensive eccDNA sequencing and multi-omics profiling of tissue samples, we acquired multimodal data encompassing DNA sequences, SNV/InDel, SV, DNA modifications, RNA modifications, and RNA expression profiles. Individual feature extraction pipelines were implemented for each modality to preserve unique information content. The extracted features were fused using an adaptive gated module that learned modality-specific weights via sigmoid-activated gates, dynamically weighting contributions based on identifiable relevance. This fused 320D representation then passed through a classification head with batch/layer normalization and dropout (p = 0.6) for eccDNA identification (Fig. 1). Crucially, a masking matrix was deployed throughout training to exclude padding signals and selectively process biologically meaningful regions.

Model convergence occurred after 20 epochs, with loss stabilization (Fig. 2A) and a final AUC of 0.844 (Fig. 2B). To evaluate modality-specific contributions, we leveraged the masking framework by ablating other modalities during ROC analysis which revealed the top-performing modalities including DNA sequences (AUC = 0.822) (Table 3), 6 mA modifications (AUC = 0.563), and 5mC/5hmC modifications (AUC = 0.536). Inter-modality correlation analysis demonstrated weak pairwise relationships (< 0.3), indicating neither significant interactions nor model redundancy (Fig. 2C). The distribution of first principal components exhibited both distinct and overlapping patterns across all modalities (Fig. 2D), indicating an optimal balance between informational uniqueness and fusibility among modalities. This characteristic is ideal for multi-modal model construction, where each modality provides distinctive features while maintaining sufficient commonality to enable effective integration, and it indicates no latent subgroups exist within individual modalities. The observed variations in peak widths reflected biologically plausible levels of intra-modality sample heterogeneity.

The detailed metrics

To comprehensively evaluate model robustness, we performed both internal cross validation and independent external validation. First, the standard tenfold cross validation and LOPCV yielded mean AUCs of 0.819 and 0.798, respectively, resulting in a minimal performance difference of 0.021 (Supplementary Table S1 and S2). To assess the generalizability of the DNA sequence modality, we applied it to an independent external dataset comprising eccDNA sequencing data from 15 colorectal cancer patients from our previous study [9]. We defined high-confidence eccDNA sequences as those supported by more than two rolling circle reads. These sequences served as input to the model. The ability to accurately recall them was evaluated under dynamic thresholding strategy. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1, the patient-level recall scores ranged from 0.515 to 0.745, achieving a median recall of 0.704.

Furthermore, the three ablation baselines confirmed our decoupled and specialized architectural design (Supplementary Table S3). Compared to the proposed eccDNAOmix (AUC = 0.844), the global end-to-end MLP baseline 1 with an AUC of 0.808, demonstrating that directly flattening sequence-based and positional modalities discards essential topologies and degrades overall predictive performance. The local modality replacement baseline 2 (LR + MLP) plateaued at 0.831, highlighting that our tree-based hashing and attention mechanisms capture non-linear multimodal relationships more effectively than standard linear feature selection methods. Finally, the partial-MLP baseline 3, which exclusively replaced the point-wise CNNs in the quantitative pathways with MLPs, achieved a highly competitive AUC of 0.837. This marginal difference aligns with expectations, as both architectures are conceptually suitable for mapping discrete features. However, it indicates that the weight-sharing mechanism of our point-wise CNN provides beneficial implicit regularization, efficiently preventing the model from overfitting on highly sparse quantitative data and offering slightly superior performance with higher parameter efficiency.

The global features of eccDNA

All eccDNAs were uniformly extended to standardized lengths for comparative analysis of multimodal signal distributions (Fig. 3A). RNA modification signals displayed enrichment within the terminal 100 bp region, while other modalities showed no significant positional patterns. Size distribution analysis revealed that eccDNAs measured in our research predominantly ranged between 200–1000 bp (Fig. 3B). Genomic annotation revealed that eccDNAs exhibited significant preferential enrichment to promoters (log₂(obs/exp) = 1.504), while being markedly underrepresented in intergenic regions (log₂(obs/exp) = −0.195) (Fig. 3C), suggesting potential functional interactions with regulatory elements. Furthermore, the expression profile of eccDNA-associated transcripts, quantified using TPM (Transcripts Per Million), revealed that the majority of eccDNA exhibited expression levels concentrated in the 101 to 104 TPM range, with a density peak around 0.1–0.40 (log scale) (Fig. 3D).

Identification of eccDNAs from unmapped read set and single-roll amplified candidates

In our previous multiple tissue eccDNA sequencing data analysis [2], a substantial number of reads supported by only a single rolling circle event were identified. Such candidate eccDNAs were typically discarded due to insufficient rolling circle amplification thresholds. However, eccDNAOmix enables the identification of these putative eccDNAs. Therefore, by integrating eccDNA sequencing with eccDNAOmix, we could improve eccDNA identification.

The concept of sequencing unmapped read sets refers to the analysis of sequence reads that do not align to a reference genome. These unmapped reads are often overlooked, yet they can contain valuable biological information. During sequencing library preparation, both eccDNAs and linear DNAs undergo fragmentation, which randomly disrupts eccDNA junction sequences. These broken junctions become undetectable during genome alignment, resulting in their erroneous classification within unmapped read sets (Fig. 4A). Our comparative analysis revealed that these unmapped sequences (Fig. 4B) contained eccDNAs. Using Nanopore DNA sequencing and eccDNA sequencing data, we aligned both eccDNA sequences and their junction reads against the unmapped read sets. The mean proportion of mapped eccDNA to total eccDNA was 15.860 for eccDNA sequences and 3.378 for junction sequences (Fig. 4C). Similarly, the mean proportion of mapped reads to total reads was 7.015 for eccDNA sequences and 5.113 for junction sequences (Fig. 4D). The detection of eccDNA within unmapped read sets underscores a pivotal function of eccDNAOmix in identifying eccDNA from conventionally overlooked sequencing data. Candidate sequences within unmapped reads were then subjected to probability identification by eccDNAOmix (Fig. 4E). High-probability candidate was then experimentally validated through PCR amplification and agarose gel electrophoresis analysis (Fig. 4F).

MAP2K1, derived from chromosome 15q22, encodes Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase Kinase 1 (MEK1), which is the key regulator in the RAS signaling pathway with established roles in melanocytic tumorigenesis [42, 43]. In colorectal cancer, we identified an eccDNA originating from the oncogene MAP2K1, which was supported by only a single read featuring one rolling circle amplification event (Supplementary Fig. 2). Despite its low read support, this candidate eccDNA received a high circularization identification score of 0.895 from eccDNAOmix.

Discussion

EccDNA, characterized by its exceptional molecular stability and cancer-specific attributes, is positioned as a robust non-invasive biomarker with potential for oncology. The analysis of eccDNA therefore marks a diagnostic advancement, bearing substantial implications for early cancer detection, multi-cancer screening, and personalized monitoring of treatment response and disease dynamics. Through the integration of multi-omic data encompassing DNA sequences, DNA/RNA modifications, and RNA expression profiles, we constructed a computational model and evaluate how different biological features contribute to eccDNA formation. Performance evaluation demonstrated that DNA sequence exhibited high identification capability, while the contribution of other modalities remained minimal. This observation aligns with established biological mechanisms governing eccDNA formation, which primarily involve DNA damage repair pathways and the breakage-fusion-bridge cycle. Both of these processes are intrinsically linked to the underlying DNA sequences.

Analysis of eccDNA distribution characteristics further revealed significant enrichment of RNA modifications in the 100 bp region flanking junction boundaries which likely stems from the preferential enrichment of eccDNA within promoter regions, where the downstream flanking region can often correspond to the 5' UTR, a known hotspot for RNA modifications such as m6A. Consequently, amplified modification signals were detected adjacent to eccDNA junctions.

Our validation showed that eccDNAs were often misclassified as unmapped reads in conventional sequencing data. Therefore, our model can be applied to these sets to detect candidate eccDNA. However, a naive implementation that processes all possible sliding window sequences from the unmapped reads would be computationally prohibitive, as this approach scales quadratically (O(n2)) with input size. Thus, additional filtering criteria are required to prioritize high-confidence candidates prior to identification analysis.

In rolling circle amplification sequencing, reads derived from eccDNAs that underwent only one rolling cycle are typically filtered out as noise. Our analysis revealed that a subset of these single-roll eccDNAs were derived from tumor-associated genes and might possess functional relevance. Integration with our identification tool offers a strategy to evaluate their potential functional significance. Finally, the model serves as a quantitative metric for assessing the accuracy of eccDNA library construction methods. Identifications generated under single-variable protocol adjustments provide measurable feedback to guide iterative refinement.

Recognizing that requiring multi-omics layers for routine eccDNA identification is costly and impractical for many clinical applications, we implemented a dual-mode strategy. To facilitate broad adoption, we developed a web server capable of performing robust identifications based solely on DNA sequences. In this application, the masking strategy of our fully trained model was once again leveraged during the identification phase to enable sequence-based inference by setting the masks of missing modalities to zero. This flexibility ensures that the framework remains highly accessible for users with limited data, while advanced researchers can still utilize the comprehensive multi-omics pipeline on GitHub for integrated high-throughput analysis.

The current strategy is evaluated against a generalized genomic background, and is therefore not suited for distinguishing eccDNAs from highly similar linear DNA regions. We validated the model predictive performance through tenfold cross validation, leave-one-patient-out cross validation, and external validation on an independent eccDNA sequencing dataset. Despite observed patient-level heterogeneity, the performance of the model across these validation approaches reinforces confidence in the applicability of the framework for future eccDNA research.

The comparative baseline analysis further validates the architectural choices of eccDNAOmix. The global MLP baseline resulted in a decrease in performance, underscoring the necessity of specialized extractors for capturing the topologies of continuous sequences. In contrast, the local modality replacement baseline using LR and MLP achieved competitive results. Since this specific baseline preserved the extractor exclusively for the DNA sequence while employing standard selections for other modalities, its predictive capability further reinforces our finding that the DNA sequence plays a dominant role in eccDNA identification. Furthermore, the partial-MLP baseline provided critical insights into processing discrete quantitative modalities. Conceptually, a multilayer perceptron utilizes independent weights for different features, which theoretically offers higher representational capacity given massive training data. Conversely, our employed point-wise CNN with a kernel size of 1 shares the same weights across features. Given the specific sample size relative to the highly sparse and high dimensional discrete decision paths generated by the initial hashing module, this weight sharing mechanism acts as an implicit regularization mechanism. It efficiently prevents the network from overfitting the sparse quantitative data, allowing our architecture to slightly outperform the partial-MLP baseline with an AUC of 0.844 compared to 0.837. This justifies its selection for modeling discrete features in the final framework while maintaining higher parameter efficiency.

Conclusions

By integrating multi-omics and eccDNA sequencing, this study provides the first comprehensive characterization of eccDNAs and serves as an analytical framework to evaluate the relative contributions of different biological modalities to eccDNA formation. This approach enables systematic correlation analyses across different modalities and demonstrates that the DNA sequence contributes the highest proportion to eccDNA identification. Furthermore, the framework helps identify hidden targets from conventionally unmapped read sets, which represent an underutilized resource for biomarker discovery. For practical application, we provide a web server that requires only DNA sequences for eccDNA identification. By leveraging this dominant identifiable role and providing an accessible single-modality tool, our work proposes a practical computational framework for advancing discovery in colorectal cancer.
