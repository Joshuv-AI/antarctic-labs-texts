Automated analysis of expressive prosody in Dutch patients with behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia and primary progressive aphasia
Authors: Lize C. Jiskoot, Olaiya Aro, Liset de Boer, Jackie M. Poos, Julie F. H. De Houwer, Tine Swartenbroekx, Elise G. P. Dopper, Esther van den Berg, Harro Seelaar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDPrevious studies have demonstrated impaired expressive prosody in patients with frontotemporal dementia (FTD), but have been conducted in English‐speaking cohorts. To examine whether findings generalize across languages, we replicated prior work in Dutch patients.METHODSWe examined expressive prosodic features in semi‐structured speech samples from patients with behavioral variant FTD (bvFTD; n = 17), primary progressive aphasia (PPA; n = 74) and controls (n = 45). We assessed group differences, classification abilities, and associations with neuropsychological tests and gray matter volume.RESULTSPatients with non‐fluent variant PPA (nfvPPA) showed the narrowest f0 range and shortest speech duration. f0 range separated patients with reduced prosody (nfvPPA/bvFTD) from those with relatively preserved prosody (semantic variant PPA/logopenic variant PPA). Lower f0 range correlated with reduced fluency and frontotemporal atrophy.DISCUSSIONViewing expressive prosody as a multidimensional system helps explain why FTD syndromes become dysprosodic in different ways and highlights its diagnostic relevance. Future studies with larger patient samples in less canonical subtypes are needed to replicate and extend our findings.

BACKGROUND

Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) constitutes a clinically and pathologically heterogeneous spectrum of young‐onset form of dementia. The core FTD spectrum disorders include behavioral variant FTD (bvFTD) and primary progressive aphasia (PPA). 1 Language and speech disorders are an integral part of the FTD spectrum, both in bvFTD and the three subtypes of PPA—that is, semantic variant PPA (svPPA), non‐fluent variant PPA (nfvPPA), and logopenic variant PPA (lvPPA). 2 , 3 , 4 Language impairments found in bvFTD are among others disorders in naming, word retrieval, single‐word comprehension, and semantic processing. 5 , 6 Specific disorders in PPA include impaired naming and single‐word comprehension in svPPA, agrammatism and speech apraxia in nfvPPA, and impaired single‐word retrieval and repetition of longer words and sentences in lvPPA. 7

Increasing research is focusing on the possible use of speech and language characteristics for the early detection of dementia and differentiation among clinical subtypes. 8 Developments in automated analysis algorithms have opened up an avenue for measuring changes in speech expression in an objective, quantitative manner. 9 Prosody, a multifaceted acoustic phenomenon resulting from variations in speech intensity, rhythm, and length, that is, the pitch contour, 10 is one of the characteristics that can be reliably extracted from speech samples. Previous studies indeed demonstrated that expressive prosody is impaired in patients with FTD, including bvFTD, 9 PPA, 11 and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). 12 The neuroimaging underpinnings of impairments in expressive prosody are found in predominantly the frontal lobe, insula, and temporal lobe, 12 areas known to be affected in the earliest disease stages of FTD. 13

Most research into expressive prosody impairments in FTD has been performed in the English language. 9 , 11 , 12 In non‐tone languages like English and Dutch, both part of the Germanic linguistic family, pitch is used to signal focus, making them variable stress languages, that is, one syllable is more prominent than others, and the position of stress is fixed within a word, but differs from word to word. 14 A comparative study of fundamental frequency (f0)—one of the prosodic aspects of speech—in English and Dutch speakers showed that for English changes of f0 are important as they help accentuate separate words and word combinations, while f0 range is narrower in Dutch. 15 Results like these highlight the importance of replicating the studies into expressive prosody impairments in a Dutch patient sample to evaluate the specificity of the expressive prosody impairments in the FTD spectrum.

In this study, we examined differences between patients with bvFTD and PPA in measurements of prosody: f0 range, speech duration, pause duration, and pause‐to‐speech ratio. We investigated the acoustic measures’ classification abilities and explored associations among the prosody measurements, neuropsychological tests, and gray matter (GM) volume.

METHODS

Participants

We included cross‐sectional data of 91 patients, of which 17 met diagnostic criteria for bvFTD: 16 18 were diagnosed with nfvPPA, 25 with svPPA, and 31 with lvPPA. 7 None of the patients with bvFTD had motor speech or other motor abnormalities at the time of diagnosis. One patient with bvFTD (GRN mutation) developed parkinsonism in the course of the disease. None of the patients with nfvPPA had motor speech abnormalities at the time of diagnosis. Seven patients with nfvPPA (38.9%) developed an atypical parkinsonism (either progressive supranuclear palsy [PSP], n = 3, or corticobasal syndrome [CBS], n = 4) later in their disease course. A lumbar puncture was performed in 22/31 (71.0%) patients with lvPPA. Of those performed, 20 (90.9%) were congruent with Alzheimer's pathology. Diagnoses were made in multidisciplinary consensus meetings of the Alzheimer Centre of the Erasmus MC University Medical Centre (EMC; Rotterdam, the Netherlands). We also included data from 45 controls, who were part of an ongoing FTD cohort study. 17 The data were gathered between April 2016 and January 2024. All participants were native Dutch speakers.

Procedure

All participants underwent a clinical interview with the participant and an informant (incorporating the Clinical Dementia Rating plus National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center frontotemporal lobar degeneration module [CDR‐plus‐NACC‐FTLD] 18 ), medical history, neurological examination, neuropsychological assessment, and brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The picture description task of the Dutch Comprehensive Aphasia Test (CAT‐NL) 19 was administered as part of the neuropsychological assessment. Other tests administered were the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), 20 Boston Naming Test (BNT), 21 category (animal) and letter fluency, 22 Visual Association Test (VAT), 23 Trail Making Test (TMT), 24 and Clock Drawing. 25

Comprehensive aphasia test picture description task

The oral version of the picture description task from the CAT‐NL, 19 an adaptation of the test originally developed for the English language, 26 was used to elicit semi‐structured speech samples. The picture is a black‐and‐white line drawing of a domestic scene, in which on the right side a man is asleep on a chair, while on the left side a young girl tries to warn him that on the shelves behind a cat is trying to catch a fish from the fish tank, knocking over a row of books that is about to fall on the man's head. Speech analysis on picture description tasks proved to be a reliable method in bvFTD and PPA. 27 , 28

Speech sample collection and processing

Speech samples were collected using a handheld recorder (Tascam DR‐22WL 2‐channel). Patients were instructed to describe the picture in as much detail as possible, using full sentences. Examiners were instructed to record for a minimum of 60 seconds, including speech and silent pause segments, to increase the degree of reliability of the speech samples. Prompts were offered when needed (e.g., after longer periods of silence or when the patient only gave a very short description of the picture), but were minimized to ensure uninterrupted speech as much as possible. Samples were recorded with one channel, sampling rate ranging from 8 to 44.1KHz, and bandwidth of 16 bit. The resulting audio files were processed using a validated automated speech analysis protocol 9 consisting of three steps: speech activity detection, pitch tracking, and f0 extraction. To time‐segment audio files, we used a speech activity detector developed by the Linguistic Data Consortium. 29 To check the accuracy of the speech activity detector, we manually reviewed the time‐segmented files in Praat (v6.4.05). 30 We excluded segments that could interfere with pitch tracking (e.g., interviewer prompts, background noise). Speech segment durations were calculated by subtracting the start time from end time of each time segment. We excluded silent pauses that were at the beginning or the end of the audio file and/or directly following an interviewer prompt. We performed pitch tracking using the Praat pitch tracker 31 and an open‐source script. 32 The limits for pitch tracking were set at 75 to 300 Hz. We extracted f0 values for the 10th to 90th percentiles for each segment, after which we calculated the mean f0 value for each 10‐percentile bin per subject. As Hz can be subject to individual confounds, we converted all f0 data to semitones (pitch range on a logarithmic scale) as described previously. 9 As a result, all first 10th percentiles were zeroed so that the 90th percentiles in semitones represent the f0 range.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: The authors conducted a systematic review of the literature using traditional sources, such as PubMed, and recent studies on expressive prosody in patients with frontotemporal dementia (FTD) spectrum disorders. While previous research has shown that expressive prosody is impaired in FTD, much of this work has focused on English‐speaking cohorts. This study aimed to replicate and extend these findings in a Dutch‐speaking population, thus evaluating the cross‐linguistic validity of prosodic impairments in behavioral variant FTD (bvFTD) and primary progressive aphasia (PPA). Interpretation: Our study provides robust evidence that expressive prosody, particularly pitch variation (f0 range), is significantly impaired in Dutch patients with bvFTD and PPA, supporting the view that prosody is a crucial diagnostic feature in differentiating between these subtypes. We demonstrated that f0 range is a reliable marker for distinguishing bvFTD from PPA and that the pause‐to‐speech ratio serves as a valuable tool for differentiating subtypes of PPA. The findings underline the role of expressive prosody as an integrated system influenced by multiple neural circuits, providing a deeper understanding of the neurobiological mechanisms underlying FTD spectrum disorders. Future directions: Future research should focus on expanding these findings by including larger and more diverse patient samples, particularly from less canonical subtypes such as mixed/atypical PPA or right temporal variant FTD. Additionally, longitudinal studies are needed to explore the progression of prosodic changes and their relation to cognitive decline. Further investigation into the genetic variants of FTD, including presymptomatic mutation carriers, will be crucial to understanding the role of prosody as an early diagnostic marker and its potential for use in clinical practice.

MRI acquisition and (pre)processing

Volumetric 3D T1‐weighted MRI images were available for 79/91 patients (86.8%) and 41/45 controls (91.1%). We performed visual quality control on all scans. DICOM images were corrected for gradient non‐linearity distortions and converted to NifTI format. We pre‐processed the images in a VBM pipeline in SPM25 in Matlab R2025a (MathWorks). The images were normalized to a template space, segmented, and rigidly aligned. The segmentations were spatially normalized to a DARTEL template by applying the flow fields of all individual scans. We smoothed the images using a 6 mm full width at half maximum isotropic Gaussian kernel.

Statistical analysis

A sensitivity power analysis was conducted using G*Power (v3.1.9.7) to determine the minimum detectable effect size for the statistical analyses in this study. The analysis was based on a fixed‐effects, omnibus, one‐way analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with five groups and three covariates. The analysis (total sample n = 136, α = 0.05, power = 0.80) indicated that the study was adequately powered to detect a minimum effect size of f = 0.25 (moderate effect size). We performed statistical analyses using SPSS Statistics v28.0.1.0 (IBM Corp.) and GraphPad Prism 5. We compared continuous demographic data between groups using one‐way analyses of variance for normally distributed data, or Kruskal–Wallis tests for non‐normally distributed data. We analyzed between‐group differences in sex with Pearson Χ 2 tests. We compared the acoustic variables between patients and controls using one‐way ANCOVAs for normally distributed data or Quade tests for non‐normally distributed data, with age, sex, and CDR‐plus‐NACC‐FTLD as covariates. To investigate classification abilities of the acoustic variables, we performed multinomial logistic regression analyses. We determined sensitivity and specificity from area under the curve (AUC) by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses to investigate the classification abilities of the acoustic variables. An AUC > 0.80 was considered to have excellent discrimination abilities. 33 Optimal cutoff levels were determined by the highest Youden index. 34 We investigated associations between f0 range and neuropsychological tests with partial correlations (Spearman rank), controlling for age, sex, and CDR‐plus‐NACC‐FTLD. We explored the relationship between f0 range and GM volume with multiple regression models, thresholded at P < 0.001 (uncorrected). Visualization of statistical maps on a standard Montreal Neurological Institute template for anatomical localization was performed in MRIcroGL (v1.2.20220720).

Acoustic measures of prosody

Differences between patients and the control group in f0 range, speech duration, pause duration, and pause‐to‐speech ratio are displayed in Figures 1 and 2 and Table S1 in supporting information. There were differences in f0 range between groups (F[4131] = 3.712, P = 0.007). Patients with bvFTD (P = 0.045), nfvPPA (P = 0.001), svPPA (P = 0.007), and lvPPA (P = 0.006) had a lower f0 range than controls. Patients with nfvPPA had a lower f0 range than patients with bvFTD (P = 0.026). Also, speech duration (F[4128] = 11.874, P < 0.001), pause duration (F[4131] = 9.036, P < 0.001), and pause‐to‐speech ratio (F[383] = 10.693, P < 0.001) were different between groups. Patients with nfvPPA (P < 0.001) and lvPPA (P < 0.001) had shorter speech durations than controls. Patients with nfvPPA had shorter speech durations than patients with bvFTD (P = 0.002) and svPPA (P < 0.001). All patients had longer pauses than controls (all P < 0.001), but no significant differences between patient groups were found. Patients with nfvPPA (P < 0.001) and lvPPA (P < 0.001) had higher pause‐to‐speech ratios than controls, and patients with nfvPPA had higher pause‐to‐speech ratios than patients with bvFTD (P < 0.001), svPPA (P < 0.001), and lvPPA (P = 0.005). There were no differences in acoustic measures between patients with nfvPPA who later developed extrapyramidal or pyramidal motor features consistent with PSP or CBS later in their course and those who did not (all P > 0.05).

Classification abilities of acoustic measures

The results of the multinomial logistic regression analyses are shown in Table 2. The main predictor of clinical phenotype was f0, with a lower f0 range increasing the likelihood of a diagnosis of nfvPPA, svPPA, or lvPPA over bvFTD. A longer speech duration increased the likelihood of being diagnosed with svPPA over nfvPPA. A longer pause duration differentiated all patients from controls, and a longer pause duration increased the likelihood of being diagnosed with nfvPPA over lvPPA. A higher pause‐to‐speech ratio increased the likelihood of being diagnosed with svPPA, nfvPPA, or lvPPA over bvFTD. ROC analyses (Figure S1 in supporting information) showed that f0 as a predictor of bvFTD versus control did not reach significant classification between groups (P = 0.176). Speech duration, pause duration, and pause‐to‐speech duration, however, were single predictors (all P < 0.001). Speech duration (P = 0.026) and pause duration (P < 0.001) were predictors of svPPA versus control. f0 as a predictor of nfvPPA versus control (P = 0.046) best thresholded at 4.9 semitones (sensitivity = 77%, specificity = 62%). Speech and pause duration were also predictors (P = 0.034 and P < 0.001, respectively). Speech duration (P < 0.001), pause duration (P < 0.001), and pause‐to‐speech ratio (P < 0.001) were predictors of lvPPA versus control. ROC analyses performed between patient groups (Figure 3) revealed that f0 range a predictor of bvFTD versus svPPA did not reach significant classification between groups (P = 0.445); however, pause duration (P = 0.025) came out as predictor. f0 range as a predictor of bvFTD versus nfvPPA (P = 0.027) best thresholded at 3.5 semitones (sensitivity = 94%, specificity = 56%). Also, speech duration (P = 0.001) and pause‐to‐speech ratio (P = 0.001) were predictors. Comparing patients with bvFTD versus svPPA, f0 range was a predictor (P = 0.001), best thresholding at 4.9 semitones (sensitivity = 77%, specificity = 76%). Also, speech duration (P < 0.001) and pause‐to‐speech ratio (P < 0.001) came out as predictors. Only f0 range was a predictor of nfvPPA versus lvPPA (P = 0.006), best thresholding at 4.9 semitones (sensitivity = 77%, specificity = 71%). Comparing patients with svPPA versus lvPPA, speech duration (P = 0.040) and pause‐to‐speech ratio (P = 0.033) came out as predictors.

Cognitive correlates of prosody

f0 range correlated with phonemic fluency in patients (bvFTD/PPA combined; r = 0.285, P = 0.034). In patients with nfvPPA, f0 range negatively correlated with semantic fluency (r = −0.542, P = 0.020) and phonemic fluency (r = −0.554, P = 0.017). No significant correlations between neuropsychological test scores and f0 range were found for patients with bvFTD, svPPA, or lvPPA (P > 0.05).

Neuroimaging correlates of prosody

When all patient groups were combined, a lower f0 range correlated with right lateralized GM volume loss in areas spanning the frontal lobe, temporal lobe, and cerebellum (Table S2 and Figure S2 in supporting information). In the frontal lobe, associations were found in the prefrontal cortex, including the inferior, medial superior, and middle frontal gyrus. Additional involvement was observed in the dorsomedial and ventromedial prefrontal cortex. In the temporal lobe, correlations were located in the superior and inferior temporal gyrus and temporal pole, including anterior medial regions overlapping with the amygdala. In bvFTD, a narrower f0 range was associated with GM volume loss in a large cluster in the right anterior and medial temporal lobes, left middle frontal gyrus and inferior temporal gyrus, cerebellum, right putamen, and caudate. In svPPA, a lower f0 range correlated with lower GM volume in a cluster in the left temporal lobe, including the temporal pole, middle and inferior temporal gyrus, and left superior frontal gyrus. In nfvPPA, a lower f0 range was associated with GM volume loss in a large area incorporating the right anterior and posterior superior temporal gyrus and right frontal lobe (e.g., inferior and superior frontal gyrus, orbitofrontal gyrus). In lvPPA, a narrower f0 range correlated with lower GM volume in the right frontal lobe, right anterior insula, and cerebellum (Figure 4).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we examined differences between patients with bvFTD, PPA, and controls in measurements of prosody—f0 range, speech segment duration, pause segment duration, and pause‐to‐speech ratio—extracted from semi‐structured Dutch speech samples.

Our automated acoustic pipeline for the extraction of prosody was previously used in English‐speaking patients with bvFTD, 9 PPA, 11 and ALS. 12 The results of these studies were to a large degree replicated in the current study. The most marked finding was the narrowest f0 range, shortest speech duration, and longest pause duration in patients with nfvPPA, consistent with the results of Nevler et al. 11 This impaired intonation (reduced f0 range) and dysfluency (less speech and frequent pauses) coincide with the clinical symptoms characteristic of this PPA subtype, in which patients demonstrate agrammatism in language production and speech apraxia. 7 The correlation found between f0 range and verbal fluency may reflect expressive dysprosody affecting both intonation and fluency. 11 Speech‐segment duration and pause duration likely reflect partially distinct, though overlapping, motor (speech planning/execution) and cognitive (e.g., lexical retrieval, executive control) processes, consistent with evidence that speech timing varies with task complexity in bvFTD. 35 Upon further inspection of patients with nfvPPA, only one patient had concomitant dysarthria (5.6%); therefore, we do not expect this to be a confound in our study. Similar to the study by Nevler et al., 9 patients with bvFTD in our study had narrower f0 ranges than controls. The authors attributed the reduction in pitch range to social‐emotional changes associated with this subtype, that is, patients lose their ability to regulate their expressive prosody, suggesting that dysprosody is at least partially a behavioral disorder. 9 Of note is that the f0 range in patients with nfvPPA and bvFTD is comparable to those in the studies by Nevler et al., 9 , 11 while f0 range is markedly lower in Dutch patients with svPPA, lvPPA, and controls. The latter could reflect cross‐linguistic differences between the two languages, as Dutch speakers typically use a smaller f0 range than English speakers, 14 while the former could suggest prosodic flattening in both bvFTD and nfvPPA that may be primarily driven by disease‐related mechanisms (e.g., speech apraxia, affective blunting) overriding premorbid cross‐linguistic differences.

We showed that f0 range can reliably separate bvFTD from nfvPPA, svPPA, and lvPPA—thereby potentially providing a useful differential diagnosis marker between behavioral and language subtypes. However, no differences in f0 range were observed between language subtypes, indicating limited discriminatory power of prosody measurements within the PPA spectrum. Nevler et al. 11 demonstrated that the likelihood of the diagnosis of nfvPPA was increased by a factor of 2.9. Comparably, in our study, a reduction of the measured f0 range by one semitone, using the reciprocal of the regression, would increase the likelihood of having nfvPPA over being a control by a factor of 2.7. Moreover, between‐group comparisons in patients with PPA indicated a lower pause‐to‐speech ratio in lvPPA compared to nfvPPA, and regression analyses showed that a lower pause‐to‐speech ratio increased the risk of being classified as lvPPA over nfvPPA. This is a particularly interesting observation, as standard language tests can be subject to examiner bias and often fail to differentiate between nfvPPA and lvPPA. 36 These findings underscore the potential of automated speech timing measures, and specifically pause‐to‐speech ratio, as an objective and scalable tool to support the differential diagnosis of PPA subtypes in clinical settings. 37 f0 best thresholded at 4.9 semitones in the comparison between nfvPPA and controls, bvFTD and svPPA, and nfvPPA and lvPPA. This is comparable to the number found in Nevler et al., 11 discriminating well between nfvPPA and controls. The fact that the same cut‐off points appear across multiple contrasts between patient groups and replicates prior findings indicates this threshold to be robust and potentially a clinically usable rule of thumb to separate patients with reduced prosody (nfvPPA/bvFTD) from patients with relatively preserved prosody (svPPA/lvPPA).

Upon investigation of the neuroimaging correlates of expressive prosody in the total patient sample, a lower f0 range correlated with GM volume loss in predominantly right frontal, temporal, and cerebellar areas. While its neural basis is still a subject to debate, 38 a number of previously published studies have indicated expressive prosody as a lateralized and predominant right hemispheric function. 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 Of note is the involvement of the right superior temporal gyrus in expressive prosody, replicating the study by Geraudie et al. 40 in patients with svPPA and semantic behavioral variant FTD. Very interesting is that analyses in the individual patient groups showed unique patterns of GM atrophy to be associated with f0 range decline. In patients with bvFTD, a right‐lateralized pattern of atrophy in the frontal, anterior, and medial temporal lobes and basal ganglia was found. Known to be particularly affected in bvFTD, 43 the amygdala, putamen, and caudate form a broader cortico–striatal–limbic circuit that among other gates and refines the emotional “coloring” of speech; damage to this circuit leads to affective dysprosody. The association between a flattened emotional intonation and disruption of striatal dopaminergic innervation is well known from Parkinson's disease, suggesting that the basal ganglia plays a prominent role in modulating the speech motor plan as a function of affective state. 44 In svPPA, f0 range correlated with left temporal pole volume. The central involvement of the left temporal pole in the semantic network could result in linguistic dysprosody in svPPA: patients lose semantic knowledge of social‐emotional concepts and abstract semantic representations of emotions, limiting their prosodic range. 39 In nfvPPA, a lower f0 range was associated with GM volume loss in a large area in the right frontal lobe. Though degeneration of the left inferior frontal gyrus has been found to cause typical symptoms of nfvPPA, 7 focal lesions of the right inferior frontal gyrus have been associated with motor dysprosody. 45 Our results could indicate that the investigation of the neural correlates of f0 range impairment helps us to understand the underlying mechanism as to why patients become dysprosodic and how this could differ among FTD subtypes.

Strengths of our study include the use of a well‐validated pipeline, 9 , 11 , 12 allowing an automated extraction of objective and reliable measurements of prosody from speech recordings of patients with bvFTD and PPA. As Speech Activity Detector (SAD) is language independent, it could be applied to our Dutch speech samples without reprogramming of the pipeline, which in the future can facilitate cross‐linguistic comparisons between patients with different language backgrounds. Importantly, language‐specific prosodic norms should be considered when interpreting measures such as f0 range. We therefore interpret prosodic measures primarily in a relative, between‐group framework rather than relying on absolute thresholds. This supports application across languages, although language‐specific normative data will be needed for clinical use. Most audio samples were recorded prior to the development of our pipeline and therefore not controlled for sound quality. We have incorporated multiple quality control measures in the processing of the audio samples to minimize pitch‐tracking inaccuracies. Importantly, although variability in recording conditions may introduce additional noise, it also reflects the heterogeneity of real‐world clinical data acquisition. As such, our findings may have increased ecological validity and better generalizability to routine clinical settings, in which strict standardization of audio quality is often not feasible. Though larger than previously reported studies in FTD, a limitation of this study is the relatively small sample sizes of the individual diagnostic subgroups. While the observed effect size falls within the moderate range, the small sample sizes may have limited the generalizability and robustness of our findings. Future studies with larger patient samples are needed to replicate and extend these findings. Although the CAT‐NL picture description task can be considered ecologically valid and speech analysis on picture description tasks has proved to be a reliable method, 28 it may not elicit as much emotion as a more emotionally evocative picture. 38 Task choice may influence sensitivity to impairment across PPA variants. Picture description tasks provide a semi‐structured context but may place different cognitive and linguistic demands than more spontaneous speech, potentially affecting the detection of subtype‐specific differences. Future studies could incorporate complementary spontaneous speech tasks to further enhance clinical sensitivity. Currently we have only included patients with a canonical FTD variant in our study. A direction for future research would be to also include patients with right temporal variant FTD and mixed/atypical PPA, as previous studies in English‐speaking patients showed that patients with right‐temporal variant FTD had a lower f0 range than patients with svPPA. 39 Last, longitudinal analyses will be essential to further characterize the progression of language deficits across the disease course. In particular, such approaches may clarify whether prosodic measures are sensitive to within‐individual change over time, especially in light of differing progression trajectories across syndromes, as well as potential differences in language profiles between FTD mutations.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, we assessed prosodic differences between patients with bvFTD and PPA using features derived from semi‐structured speech samples. As prior research on expressive prosody in FTD has predominantly focused on the English language, extending this work to Dutch enhances the cross‐linguistic validity of the findings. f0 range could reliably separate bvFTD from PPA—thereby potentially providing a useful differential diagnosis marker between behavioral and language subtypes of FTD. Importantly, reduced f0 range appears to capture a common behavioral manifestation of dysprosody across FTD subtypes. While in nfvPPA this likely reflects motor speech planning and execution deficits in bvFTD and related syndromes it may arise from alterations in affective or executive modulation of speech. Thus, acoustic measures such as f0 range provide a robust, syndrome‐spanning marker of prosodic impairment, even though the underlying mechanisms may differ. Future studies with larger patient samples in less canonical subtypes are needed to replicate and extend our findings.
