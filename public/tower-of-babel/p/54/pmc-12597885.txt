Long-Term Surgical Outcome for Idiopathic Acute Rupture of Mitral Chordae Tendineae in Infants
Authors: Yuji Tominaga, Kenta Imai, Motoki Komori, Keisuke Shibagaki, Rieko Kutsuzawa, Shota Kawai, Kenichi Kurosaki, Isao Shiraishi, Shigemitsu Iwai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAcute rupture of mitral valve chordae tendineae in infants is rare and rapidly leads to cardiorespiratory failure. Acute progression with slight mitral valve annulus dilatation and valve degeneration limits the surgical treatment options. This study investigated the long-term surgical outcomes and factors influencing prognosis.MethodsWe assessed 20 infants who underwent surgical treatment for acute mitral valve chordal rupture between 2001 and 2015. The median age was 5.5 months (IQR: 4.5-7.2). Seventeen experienced cardiogenic shock. Twenty had severe mitral regurgitation with a mitral valve diameter z-score of 1.1 (0.06-2.5). The infants underwent mitral valve repair primarily with artificial chordae reconstruction. Mitral valve replacement was performed when regurgitation control was unsuccessful.ResultsThe follow-up period was 12.9 (10.6-14.4) years, with no mortality. The number of prolapsed leaflet segments was 3 (1-3). Twelve patients (60%) exhibited intraoperatively yellowish oedematous degeneration of the mitral valve. Although 13 underwent mitral valve repair at initial surgery, 3 were converted to mitral valve replacement before discharge. Consequently, 10 required mitral valve replacement because of uncontrollable mitral regurgitation before discharge. Univariable logistic regression revealed that mitral valve degeneration (odds ratio: 21, P = .015) and the number of prolapsed leaflet segments (odds ratio: 4.6, P = .025) were significantly associated with mitral valve replacement before discharge.ConclusionsPrompt valve surgery yields good long-term outcomes. Mitral valve repair using artificial chordae reconstruction is a reasonable option for infants. Mitral valve degeneration and numerous prolapsed segments are associated with uncontrollable mitral regurgitation.Keywords: mitral valve chordae tendineae rupture, artificial chordae reconstruction, mitral valve degeneration

INTRODUCTION

Surgical management of mitral valve (MV) regurgitation in the paediatric population remains challenging because of the heterogeneity of lesions, the requirement for growth potential, and the necessity to avoid MV replacement (MVR).1–5 In particular, infantile idiopathic acute MV chordae tendineae rupture is rare and rapidly leads to cardiorespiratory failure; thus, it often requires emergency surgery.6–9 The aetiology remains unknown. Acute progression with slight MV annulus dilatation and valve degeneration limits the surgical treatment options.7–9 Notably, some patients present with multiple ruptured chordae or inflammatory or immunogenic degeneration of the valves or subvalvular tissues.6–9 The heterogeneity of the affected lesions may influence the success or failure of MV repair.1 However, only a few reports exist on the outcomes of surgical treatment because of the relative paucity of this condition.7–9 Therefore, in the current study, we aimed to investigate the long-term surgical outcomes and factors influencing prognosis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the WMA Declaration of Taipei. The use of stored clinical data was approved and monitored by the Institutional Review Board of the National Cerebral and Cardiovascular Center (approval number: R19092). No biological specimens were stored or used for future unspecified research. The requirement for patient consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Patient selection and clinical evaluation

Patients aged <1 year who underwent MV surgery for severe mitral regurgitation (MR) due to acute rupture of mitral chordae tendineae at our institution between 2001 and 2015 were included in this study. Laboratory and intraoperative findings of MV lesions were not consistent with bacterial infection, connective tissue disorders, rheumatic fever, or ischaemic disease. Follow-up data were collected from medical, operative, and echocardiographic records. Follow-up was conducted through scheduled outpatient visits, with clinical evaluations and echocardiographic assessments performed at least once a year, and ended on 21 December 2022. Follow-up was completed in all patients (100%).

The MR grade and the peak trans-mitral pressure gradient (PG) were evaluated using colour Doppler echocardiography. The number of prolapsed leaflet segments was defined as the number of prolapsed segments out of 6 segments: A1-A3 and P1-P3.

Medical and surgical treatment

Initial treatment included medical support, such as intravenous infusion of inotropes, diuretics, and vasodilators, and mechanical ventilation. If the circulatory and respiratory failure were uncontrollable, surgical repair of the ruptured chordae tendineae was immediately considered.

Regarding the surgical technique, we first attempted MV repair and then converted it to MVR in cases in which MR was difficult to control. Bicaval venous and ascending aortic cannulations were used to establish cardiopulmonary bypass, and the MV was exposed through a right-sided left atriotomy. The infants underwent MV repair with artificial chordae reconstruction using CV-5 expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE) sutures (WL Gore & Associates, Inc, Newark, DE, USA) with pledgets and Kay-Reed annuloplasty or edge-to-edge repair.6–10 During the first surgery, conversion to MVR was implemented if regurgitation control was unsuccessful (primary MVR). Posterior translocated supra-annular MVR was performed as previously reported.11 Mattress sutures were placed onto the anterior annulus, and the posterior section of the mechanical valve cuff was sutured onto the posterior left atrial wall away from the MV annulus. We implanted ATS AP valves (Medtronic Inc., Minneapolis, MN, US).

The indications for repeat MV surgery were symptomatic severe MR or backward pulmonary hypertension due to patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM) or prosthetic valve failure.12

Study design

First, we summarized the characteristics of the initial MV surgery. Second, we evaluated patients’ postoperative course. The primary end-point was death. The secondary end-points were redo-MV surgery, cerebrovascular events, and ablation for atrial tachycardia. Regarding the follow-up period, patients were censored when an event occurred, and only the last follow-up time was counted as an event for patients without an event.

Third, patients were divided into 2 groups to investigate the factors associated with uncontrolled MR. Mitral valve repair was initially attempted in all patients. During the first surgery, conversion to MVR was performed if regurgitation control was unsuccessful (primary MVR). If MR became severe soon after the initial MV plasty, MVR was performed before discharge (early MVR conversion). These 2 scenarios—primary MVR and early MVR conversion—were grouped together as the early MVR group. Ten patients who were discharged after successful MV plasty alone were classified as the MV plasty group. We then compared the parameters between the early MVR and the MV plasty groups. Finally, we examined the factors associated with uncontrolled MR in patients who initially underwent MV plasty and then underwent primary MVR or early MVR conversion using a logistic regression model.

Statistical analyses

Data were presented as median and IQR. The incidence of events was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier curves. A logistic regression model was used to assess the risk of early MVR (primary MVR and early MVR conversion). Odds ratios and 95% CIs were calculated. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro 17 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

RESULTS

Preoperative characteristics

Twenty patients (12 girls, median age: 5.5, IQR: 4.5-7.2 months) were included. Twenty patients without a history of significant illnesses exhibited prodromal symptoms, such as cough or fever, at a median of 7 (IQR: 2.8-18) days before the MV surgery. Before surgery, 14 patients (70%) experienced cardiogenic shock, and cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) was performed on 3 patients (15%). Fifteen patients (75%) underwent emergency surgery within 24 h of admission because of uncontrolled cardiorespiratory failure.

Preoperative MR grade was severe in all patients, with a median mitral annulus diameter of 16.1 mm (14.8-19.2; z-score: 1.1 [0.06-2.5]) and a left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVDd) of 27.4 mm (25.9-30.4; z-score: 1.7 [0.32-3.2]). One patient had a chordal rupture of the tricuspid valve. The cardiothoracic ratio on chest radiography was 56% (54%-59%). The brain natriuretic peptide level was 3119 (2572-4820) pg/mL (Table 1).

First surgical treatment for severe MR

The median weight at surgery was 7.1 (5.9-8.0) kg. The cardiopulmonary bypass time was 160 (135-215) min, and the aortic cross-clamp time was 115 (91-162) min. During the initial surgery, the prolapsed leaflets were anterior, posterior, and both in 4, 8, and 8 cases, respectively. The number of prolapsed leaflet segments was 3 (1-3). Twelve patients (60%) exhibited yellowish-to-reddish oedematous degeneration of the leaflets and/or papillary muscles (Table 1). Representative findings for an MV with severe degeneration are shown in Figure 1. No vegetation was observed in any of the 20 patients.

MV plasty was the first attempted intervention in all patients. In 13 (65%) patients, MR was controlled using MV repair. Thirteen patients (65%) underwent MV repair using 2 (1-3) artificial chordae reconstructions. Kay-Reed annuloplasty, edge-to-edge repair, and both methods were performed in 5, 2, and 3 cases, respectively. The MV diameter after MV plasty was 13.5 (13-14.3) mm, and MR grade was less than mild. Seven patients (35%) underwent MVR (primary MVR) using the posterior translocation method because of the difficulty in controlling MR (Figure 2).

Three infants required MVR 19 (14-22) days after MV repair because of worsening MR (early MVR conversion). The reasons for MR exacerbation were additional chordal rupture in 1 patient, progressive degeneration of the leaflets in 1 patient, and an unknown reason in 1 patient. The size of mechanical valves was 16 mm in these 3 infants.

At the most recent evaluation, 12 (IQR: 8.2-14) years after the first MV surgery, MR grade of the 10 patients who were discharged after undergoing MV plasty alone was less than mild in 8 and moderate in 2 patients (Figure S1). Regarding mitral stenosis, the median PG was 2.6 (1.2-5) mmHg. None of the patients required surgical reintervention during the follow-up period. Three of the 10 patients had a mean trans-mitral PG of 20 (16-21) mmHg after primary MVR and early MVR conversion and required re-MVR 9.3 (9.2-10.4) years after the first MVR because of PPM (Figure 2). The 10-year reoperation-free rate was 74% (Figure 3B).

Cerebrovascular events were observed in 4 patients at 0.13 (0.01-0.28) years after the first MV surgery; however, 2 patients had no sequelae. CPR was performed on 3 of these 4 patients preoperatively. The cerebrovascular event-free rate was 80% at 10 years (Figure 3C).

Regarding arrhythmia, 2 patients underwent postoperative ablation for atrial tachycardia at 9.1 and 14 years after the first surgery, respectively. Two patients who underwent early MVR conversion required pacemaker implantation (PMI) for sick sinus syndrome at 0.31 and 1.1 years after the first MV surgery, respectively (Figure 3D).

DISCUSSION

In this retrospective, single-centre study, we reviewed the data of 20 infants who underwent MV surgery for idiopathic acute mitral chordae tendineae rupture with a median follow-up period of 12.9 years.

The acute mitral chordae tendineae rupture had a rapid onset, with a median LVDd z-score of 1.7 and MV diameter z-score of 1.1, indicating that they were only slightly enlarged. Furthermore, the preoperative condition deteriorated, and 15 patients (75%) required emergency surgery. However, no mortalities occurred, and the overall survival rate was 100% during the follow-up period, which was a satisfactory outcome. Ten patients (50%) were discharged after undergoing MV plasty alone, whereas the remaining 10 (50%) were discharged after MVR, including primary MVR and early MVR conversion, which was performed when MR was not controlled by MV plasty. There were 3 cases of late re-MVR due to PPM. The 10-year redo-MV surgery-free rate was 74%. Univariable logistic regression revealed that the degeneration of the MV leaflets and papillary muscles and the number of prolapsed leaflet segments, particularly more than 3 segments, were significantly associated with MVR before discharge.

Idiopathic acute mitral chordae tendineae rupture is characterized by the sudden rupture of the mitral chordae tendineae, followed by a few days of common cold-like prodromal symptoms in a healthy infant.6–9 Sudden onset of mitral chordae rupture is frequently misdiagnosed as ‘severe pneumonia’, and delayed treatment may lead to mortality. The direct cause of this disease remains unclear. Infections and immunological abnormalities, such as viral infections, Kawasaki disease, maternal anti–Sjögren’s-syndrome-related antigen A antibodies, and myxomatous degeneration of the MV, are considered to trigger its onset.6,9,13,14 In molecular biological research, a recent study reported that transforming growth factor-β signalling was associated with myxomatous MV degeneration.15 However, the details of the aetiology remain unknown and require further study.

MV repair is the first surgical option for children because MVR in infants always requires upsizing reoperation as the patient ages.1–3,10 However, an unenlarged mitral annulus in cases of acute mitral chordae tendineae rupture limits the degree of valve annuloplasty and edge-to-edge repair. Regarding MV repair using artificial chordae, Kay-Reed annuloplasty and edge-to-edge repair have to be performed to control MR without leading MV stenosis. At the 12-year follow-up, there was no MR progression or artificial chordae rupture. As reported previously, artificial chordae reconstruction is considered an effective choice for infantile idiopathic mitral chordae tendineae rupture.4,6–9,10

In cases in which MR control was difficult and MVR was required, the posterior translocated MVR was performed because of the patient’s small stature.11 Another method involving the insertion of a mechanical valve with a short-segment ePTFE skirt into the supra-annular position has been reported.16 Both methods are considered suitable for safe MVR in the neonatal period and early infancy. However, future studies are required to determine which method has better long-term outcomes. Notably, some recent studies have reported the use of a transcatheter pulmonary valve device composed of a balloon-expandable stent and valved bovine jugular venous conduit in the mitral position for MVR in infants. These choices may be suitable for patients with preoperative resuscitation and cerebral insults. However, this approach was associated with a high incidence of infective endocarditis.17 Furthermore, children with mechanical valves had a higher event-free survival rate than those with bioprosthetic valves in the mitral position.18 The use of mechanical valves for uncontrolled MR is acceptable.

Compared with the long-term survival rate associated with other MV surgeries, the survival rate in this study was satisfactory.1–4,6,9 Four of 20 patients experienced cerebrovascular complications, 3 of whom required preoperative CPR. The cerebrovascular complications were limited to the perioperative period. There were no sequelae in 2 patients, whereas the remaining 2 patients had mild sequelae and could perform daily activities without assistance. Based on the findings in previous reports, preoperative CPR can be a risk factor for cerebrovascular complications, and careful postoperative management is required.19,20 Regarding anticoagulation therapy, careful follow-up is necessary. The patients visited the outpatient unit at least once every 3 months for adjustment of the warfarin dosage and control of the prothrombin time-international normalized ratio. Two patients who underwent early MVR conversion required PMI for sick sinus syndrome postoperatively. Cerebral complications and PMI may be problematic in the early postoperative period, whereas atrial tachycardia requiring ablation may be a concern in the remote postoperative period. In addition, there was no postoperative infective endocarditis during the follow-up period. In 3 cases, re-MVR was performed because of PPM derived from post-capillary pulmonary hypertension.12 Reoperations were completed without any problems. Careful follow-up and serial echocardiography are necessary in cases in which PPM progresses with age.

Ten patients in the early MVR group who underwent primary MVR and early MVR conversion required MVR before discharge because of difficulty in controlling MR by MV repair. Univariable analysis showed that the degeneration of leaflets or papillary muscles and the number of prolapsed leaflet segments were significantly associated with early MVR. These results suggest that MVR may be an option for shortening the operative time, reducing the surgical burden of the disease, and avoiding early reoperation.

This study has several limitations, particularly the small sample size owing to the rarity of this disease, its retrospective design, and the long timeframe of patient inclusion. Therefore, the statistical power may be insufficient to provide robust evidence and estimation. Thus, multicentre studies with larger sample sizes are required to validate the results of this study.

In addition, although this study analysed only cases up to 2015 to ensure long-term follow-up, we have since treated 4 more infants with idiopathic acute rupture of the mitral chordae tendineae. These were not included in the analysis, but their presentation was similar, and all were successfully managed with valve plasty using artificial chordae. The smaller number of new cases may reflect changes in referral patterns, our hospital’s relocation, the COVID-19 pandemic, and Japan’s declining birth rate.

In conclusion, prompt surgery for acute MV chordae tendineae rupture yields good long-term outcomes with no mortality. MV repair using artificial chordae reconstruction does not require reoperation during the follow-up period and is a reasonable option for infants. It is most desirable for MV repair to be successful; however, degeneration of the leaflets and papillary muscles and a large number of prolapsed segments are associated with primary MVR or early MVR conversion.
