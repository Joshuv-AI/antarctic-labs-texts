Novel Use of Dual-Lumen Microcatheter for False Lumen Hematoma Decompression
Authors: Elizabeth D.A. Chan, Zeeshan Ahmed, Aun Yeong Chong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundManagement of iatrogenic coronary dissection is essential for all interventional cardiologists considering its incidence and high associated mortality.Case SummaryA 66-year-old woman undergoing elective left and right heart catheterization for investigation of shortness of breath experienced iatrogenic catheter-induced ostial right coronary artery dissection. Use of intravascular ultrasound and repurposing a dual-lumen microcatheter enabled successful wiring of true lumen and percutaneous management.DiscussionNovel use and repurposing of available coronary equipment in the form of a dual-lumen microcatheter provided additional tools for management of this dangerous complication.Take-Home MessagesCoronary dissection regardless of mechanism can be fatal and difficult to manage given size differences of the true and false lumen. Use of a dual-lumen microcatheter placed into the false dissection lumen with negative pressure applied can decompress the hematoma and enable wiring of the true lumen for percutaneous management.

She reported a 3-year history of exertional shortness of breath, noticeable on activities such as carrying heavy items or vacuuming. She also reported a 1-year history of atypical chest pain with no relation to exercise, lasting up to 15 seconds and described as a central squeezing with no radiation or associated symptoms. She denied any symptoms at rest.

Prior to the procedure, the patient had undergone transthoracic echocardiography, which demonstrated normal left ventricular and right ventricular size and function with no regional wall motion abnormalities. She was noted to have grade 2 diastolic dysfunction with no significant valvular pathology. The patient had also undergone stress testing in the form of a Persantine myocardial perfusion scan, which suggested a small burden of ischemia in the right coronary artery (RCA) and left anterior descending artery (LAD) territories with 2.9% ischemia and 0% left ventricular scar burden.

Physical examination was unremarkable, with normal baseline electrocardiogram and normotensive and normal preprocedural blood panel including hemoglobin 137 g/L and creatinine 86 μmol/L.

Past Medical History

The patient's cardiovascular history included hypertension, dyslipidemia, previous transient ischemic attack in 2014, and obesity (body mass index: 32 kg/m2). She was currently being managed for heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF).

Noncardiac concerns included nephrolithiasis with previous lithotripsy, depression, and cholecystectomy with previous abdominal hysterectomy.

Differential Diagnosis

Based on the patient's risk factors, the question underlying the investigation was to delineate between potential angina equivalent versus pulmonary hypertension versus HFpEF.

Investigations

Right heart catheterization was performed via the right antecubital fossa using a 6-F slender sheath under local anesthetic with a 5-F Swan-Ganz catheter. This demonstrated normal right-sided pressures with no evidence of pulmonary hypertension, in keeping with diagnosis of HFpEF.

We proceeded to left heart catheterization via the right radial artery using a 6-F radial sheath. There was difficulty engaging the RCA using a 5-F JR4 catheter, with only nonselective shots obtained. After JR4 catheter manipulation, the patient developed severe spasm and arm pain. After administration of analgesia, nitroglycerin via radial sheath, and additional sedation, a 5-F Ikari left 3.5 catheter was delivered for left coronary artery shots; no significant coronary disease was demonstrated. Despite the administration of additional medication, the patient continued to experience severe spasm with ongoing discomfort. Therefore, to obtain diagnostic RCA imaging, with the aid of a 0.035-inch 145-cm J-curved guidewire and Co-Pilot, we used the Ikari left 3.5 to engage the RCA.

Interventional Management

The patient developed chest pain with ST-segment elevation, however she remained hemodynamically stable.

Given severe right radial artery spasm, access was switched to the right femoral artery with ultrasound-guided single puncture with a 6-F sheath for additional support for intervention. Wiring attempts aiming for the true lumen using an Abbott Hi-Torque Balance Middle Weight Universal II (BMW U2) workhorse wire were not successful. We were able to place the BMW U2 wire into the false lumen with relative ease. Position was confirmed using a Boston Scientific Opticross HD intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) system. The IVUS images demonstrated the significant size mismatch between true and false lumen. The false lumen was much larger, with compression of the true lumen because the dissection had created a significant hematoma, hence it was difficult to selectively wire the true lumen (Figure 2).Figure 2Intravascular Ultrasound Images of True and False Lumen(A) Intravascular ultrasound located in the false lumen demonstrating the smaller true lumen. To be able to wire the true lumen, we recognized that decompression of the false lumen and hematoma was required. We used a Biotronik ReCross dual-lumen over-the-wire (OTW) microcatheter placed onto the BMW U2 wire in the false lumen to achieve this. A 20-mL luer-lock syringe was attached to the second tip lumen hub with negative pressure sustained manually. A small amount of blood was aspirated back into the syringe.

The advantage of the ReCross is that it has 3 exit ports and aspiration performed through the 2 distal OTW ports, and by withdrawing proximal to the dissection and rotating, a second wire may be introduced and directed to the true lumen while the wire in the rapid exchange system (in the false lumen) stabilizes the equipment. Alternatively, as in this case, the second wire was introduced alongside the dual OTW lumen catheter with the catheter serving to decompress the false lumen and increasing the chances of wiring the true lumen. Finally, if the previous maneuvers fail, a bailout strategy using a tapered, high penetrative wire to re-enter the true lumen may also be considered. Sustained manual decompression via the ReCross microcatheter enabled wiring of the true lumen using a second workhorse wire, the Terumo Runthrough NS extra floppy coronary guidewire, with true lumen positioning confirmed via IVUS. Once the true lumen position was confirmed, the BMU U2 wire and dual-lumen system were removed from the false lumen, followed by IVUS-guided vessel sizing and stenting with postdilatation using noncompliant balloons. Outcome and Follow-Up

After the iatrogenic complication, the patient was admitted overnight for monitoring, with full explanation of events surrounding the complication. The patient experienced a periprocedural infarction, which was demonstrated with a peak postprocedural troponin T level of 1309 ng/L. A repeat echocardiogram demonstrated normal left ventricular function with no regional wall motion abnormalities with no change from previous. She was discharged after 24 hours of observation. She has since been seen in the follow-up clinic and is asymptomatic from a cardiac perspective.

Discussion

Iatrogenic catheter-induced coronary dissection is mechanical trauma to the coronary endothelium causing a separation of the vessel layers as a result of contact with the catheter and jetting of contrast medium. This can result in antegrade propagation down the coronary artery, retrograde propagation into the aorta, or remain localized with bleeding into the subendothelial space. Guiding catheters cause the majority of catheter-induced coronary dissections, with one retrospective study suggesting they account for 75% of all iatrogenic dissections.3

Iatrogenic catheter-induced coronary artery dissections are not infrequent, with clinical incidence estimated as roughly 0.1%, and they can be fatal.4,5 Given this, recognition and management of coronary dissection is essential for all interventional cardiologists.

When encountering difficulty wiring the true lumen owing to obstruction by the larger false lumen secondary to hematoma formation, having additional techniques and using available equipment can provide alternative options for management. For this case, by using a dual-lumen OTW catheter for manual decompression, alternative, more aggressive bailout techniques for dissection management were avoided, such as the retrograde approach, dissection and re-entry with Stingray, or the use of cutting balloons.6 The natural history of ostial dissections culminates in vessel occlusion. Prompt recognition and treatment of an ostial dissection through percutaneous coronary intervention is of paramount importance. However, in severe cases resulting in hemodynamic compromise with the artery unable to be opened percutaneously, emergency coronary artery bypass grafting is required, which is associated with an operative mortality of 20.8%.7

Dual-lumen catheters are widely used and available, commonly employed within the chronic total occlusion arena or for complex bifurcation intervention.1 Here, we describe a novel use of a dual OTW microcatheter with 3 exit ports to decompress the false lumen as well as different strategies to re-enter the true lumen.

Conclusions

While relatively infrequent, catheter-induced coronary artery dissections can occur during routine angiography, but they are more common during percutaneous coronary intervention related to use of guiding catheters. These can be fatal and must be recognized and managed swiftly to ensure the best outcomes.

Hematoma size and bleeding into the subendothelial space caused by the dissection propagation, either antegrade or retrograde, causes a large false lumen compressing the true lumen. Therefore, wiring the true lumen can be very tricky. We have shown that when encountering difficulties wiring the true lumen, a dual-lumen microcatheter placed into the false lumen can be used to decompress the hematoma, thereby relieving the pressure and increasing the likelihood of successful true lumen wiring to enable definitive percutaneous management.
