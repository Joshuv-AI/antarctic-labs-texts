Prediction of therapeutic targets and drug candidates for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma by network-based computational framework
Authors: Jinyuan Lu, Weishan Liang, Yixue Liang, Jiajia Ren, Chengyang Guo, Xia Li, Jianquan Pan, Weidong Zhang, Saisai Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains one of the most lethal malignancies, with limited therapeutic options and an urgent need for novel treatment strategies. This study presented a network-based computational framework for in silico genome-wide therapeutic target identification and drug discovery.MethodsA network-based computational framework was developed that integrates transcriptomic data from multiple independent PDAC cohorts to construct gene co-expression networks. Prognostic gene signatures were incorporated to identify feature modules via hypergeometric enrichment analysis. Key regulatory genes within these modules were prioritized using multi-metric centrality analysis. Functional relevance of candidate targets was assessed using single-cell ribonucleic acid (RNA) sequencing, genome-wide clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) dependency datasets, and clinical survival analyses. To enable therapeutic translation, this study implemented a transcriptome-guided drug discovery strategy to systematically match pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC)-specific gene expression signatures with compound-induced transcriptional profiles from the Library of Integrated Network-based Cellular Signatures (LINCS) database. Candidate compounds were ranked using a cross-cohort consensus scoring approach and subsequently evaluated in vitro using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), Western blotting, and cell viability assays.ResultsComprehensive analysis of multi-cohort PDAC transcriptomic data identified 14 key genes with high network centrality, including three potential oncogenic drivers: Cytoskeleton associated protein 2 like (CKAP2L), kinesin family member 18A (KIF18A), and minichromosome maintenance 10 replication initiation factor (MCM10), which exhibited tumor-specific overexpression and poor prognostic associations. Single-cell RNA sequencing (ScRNA-seq) revealed their predominant expression in epithelial tumor cells, while CRISPR screening confirmed functional essentiality. The transcriptome-guided drug discovery approach matched these targets with LINCS perturbation profiles, identifying mitoxantrone/OTSSP167 (CKAP2L inhibitors), PF-3758309 (KIF18A inhibitors), and vorinostat (MCM10 inhibitors) as candidate therapeutics. Experimental validation using qRT-PCR, Western blotting, and cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assays collectively demonstrated the dose-dependent therapeutic effects of these compounds, showing significant mRNA suppression, corresponding decreases in protein levels, and potent anti-proliferative activity in PDAC cells.ConclusionThis study establishes a robust, network-based framework for therapeutic target discovery and transcriptome-driven drug prediction in PDAC. It identifies three novel, functionally essential oncogenic drivers and proposes clinically actionable compounds, offering a scalable approach for addressing undruggable targets in cancer therapy.

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is among the most lethal solid tumors, with a 5-year survival rate below 10% and a median survival of less than six months (Buscail et al., 2020, Lu et al., 2025). Its incidence continues to increase, and PDAC is projected to become the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide (Halbrook, Lyssiotis, Pasca di Magliano, & Maitra, 2023). This poor prognosis is primarily attributed to late-stage diagnosis and early metastatic spread (Song et al., 2023, Qian et al., 2025). More than 80% of patients are diagnosed with unresectable disease, and even among those eligible for surgery, the 5-year survival rate remains under 30% (Kleeff et al., 2016). Current therapeutic options for PDAC are limited. First-line regimens such as gemcitabine or FOLFIRINOX (folinic acid, fluorouracil, irinotecan, and oxaliplatin) extend survival by only 4–6 months and are frequently associated with substantial toxicity (Conroy et al., 2018, Neoptolemos et al., 2018). Targeted therapies offer limited benefit, with poly ADP-ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibitors effective in only 5%–7% of patients harboring breast cancer susceptibility gene (BRCA) mutations, and kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog G12C (KRASG12C) inhibitors benefiting just 1.6% of cases (Golan et al., 2019, Waters and Der, 2018, Zhen et al., 2015). Moreover, immunotherapies have demonstrated minimal efficacy due to the highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment characteristic of PDAC (Moffitt et al., 2015). These challenges highlight the urgent need to identify novel therapeutic targets and drug candidates that can overcome current treatment limitations and improve outcomes for PDAC patients.

Traditional methods for therapeutic target discovery include experimental screening, structure-based prediction, and genomic profiling. These methods have shortcomings such as high costs, dependence on prior structural knowledge, and a narrow focus on mutated or differentially expressed genes. Moreover, they frequently overlook the regulatory interactions among genes that underlie complex diseases (Martincorena et al., 2017). In contrast, network-based approaches offer a more comprehensive framework by integrating multiple data layers, including genomic alterations, signaling pathways, and tumor-microenvironment crosstalk (Jiang, Ye, Tan, & Bao, 2025). Incorporating transcriptomic data into these networks enables the identification of novel therapeutic targets at a genome-wide level (Tian et al., 2025). Recently, several network construction strategies have been developed, including those based on gene co-expression (GCN), protein-protein interactions (PPI), regulatory relationships, and multi-omics integration. For instance, gene co-expression network analysis has been used to identify colony stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF1R) as a therapeutic target in epilepsy (Srivastava et al., 2018), while protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis has identified apolipoprotein E (APOE) as a regulator of microglial activity in Parkinson’s disease (Doostparast Torshizi et al., 2024). Despite these advances, the application of network-based strategies for therapeutic target discovery in PDAC remains limited. One key challenge is that many targets identified through genome-wide network inference are not readily druggable. Fortunately, the emergence of large-scale drug perturbation datasets has significantly expanded the potential of network-based drug discovery, enabling genome-wide identification of candidate therapeutics. A major advantage of transcriptome-based perturbation resources is their ability to identify functionally active compounds without requiring prior knowledge of target structure or biochemical function (Tian et al., 2023). Among these resources, the Integrated Network-based Cellular Signatures (LINCS) database is particularly notable, encompassing over three million gene expression profiles for more than 20 000 compounds tested across over 200 human cell lines (Stathias et al., 2020). In this context, this study developed a network-based computational framework for in silico therapeutic target identification and drug discovery.

Here, this study first applied a network-based computational framework for genome-wide identification of therapeutic targets in PDAC by integrating transcriptomic profiles from multiple independent patient cohorts (n = 801). This approach leverages patient-derived data to construct gene-centric disease signatures that reflect the regulatory architecture of PDAC. A robust prognostic gene signature identified through comprehensive survival analyses was subsequently mapped onto conserved gene co-expression network modules to prioritize candidate targets with high network centrality and strong clinical relevance. To identify potential therapeutic compounds targeting these key genes, this study developed a genome-wide drug discovery framework. Specifically, a transcriptome-guided strategy was implemented to match PDAC-specific transcriptional perturbation profiles, defined by the expression patterns of key oncogenic drivers, with compound-induced gene expression signatures from the LINCS database. Unlike traditional structure- or ligand-based approaches, this strategy enables the identification of functionally active compounds, including those targeting transcriptionally defined or structurally undruggable genes. Top-ranked compounds for each prioritized gene were validated in PDAC cell lines using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), Western blotting, and cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) proliferation assays, confirming dose-dependent suppression of target gene expression and inhibition of tumor cell proliferation.

Methods and materials

Data and preprocessing

Transcriptomic profiles of 460 PDAC tumor samples and 338 normal samples were acquired from four major genomic resources: clinical proteomic tumor analysis consortium (CPTAC, https://proteomics.cancer.gov/programs/cptac) (Cao et al., 2021), the cancer genome atlas (TCGA, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), genotype-tissue expression project (GTEx, https://gtexportal.org/) (Consortium et al., 2020), and gene expression omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) accessions GSE28735 (Zhang et al., 2013), GSE62452 (Yang et al., 2016), and GSE71729 (Moffitt et al., 2015). Specifically, the dataset comprised 135 tumor and 21 normal samples from CPTAC, 93 tumor and 171 normal samples from TCGA, and 232 tumor and 149 normal samples from the integrated GEO datasets. Tumor and normal tissue types were curated based on the sample annotations provided by each respective database. Expression data from the three GEO datasets were merged into a single dataset, hereafter referred to as “Metadata”. GEO count data were processed using R (v4.4.0), including: (1) Normalization for cross-sample comparisons; (2) quality control to exclude samples with over 20% missing values; and (3) dimensionality reduction via principal component analysis (PCA). Batch effects in the Metadata cohort were corrected using the ComBat function (sva package, v3.40.0), followed by dataset integration with the limma package (v3.56.0). All datasets included complete survival time and status information.

The drug perturbational profiles data were obtained from the newly released LINCS datasets (https://clue.io/data/CMap2020#LINCS2020). Replicate-collapsed differential expression signatures (Level 5 dataset) of the 978 landmark gene were used in our analysis pipeline. For accessing LINCS2020 signatures, this study used cmapPy Python library. This research focuses on gene expression signatures induced by drug at the standardized condition of 10 μmol/L concentration and 24-hour exposure. The chosen time point and concentration represent the most frequent treatment condition present in the dataset.

Functional enrichment analysis

Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the enrichGO function in the R package clusterProfiler, which uses the hypergeometric distribution to estimate whether a list of genes are significantly enriched in each GO pathways. P < 0.05 was used to identify significantly enriched pathways.

GCN analysis and gene module identification

To identify gene modules with high topological cohesion, this study constructed a weighted gene co-expression network using pairwise Spearman correlation coefficients calculated across all tumor samples. Only gene pairs with correlation coefficients in the top 1% were retained to define the edges of an undirected network, with nodes representing genes and edge weights reflecting co-expression strength. This study therefore used a top 1% absolute-correlation cutoff to balance biological relevance and structural simplicity, as in recent studies (Figueiredo et al., 2021, Sheinin et al., 2025). Community detection was performed using the Walktrap algorithm (Dimitrakopoulos et al., 2018), which identified modules based on structural similarity derived from short random walks. where A is the adjacency matrix and ki is the degree of node i. For each node, the t-step transition probability distribution Pit was computed, and the mean distribution across all nodes in a community C was calculated as:PCt=1|C|∑i∈CPit

To quantify the structural similarity between communities, a degree-normalized Euclidean distance was used:rCi,Cj=‖D-0.5PCit-D-0.5PCjt‖

where D is the diagonal degree matrix. Communities with the smallest structural distance were iteratively merged to generate a hierarchical modular decomposition. Modules containing more than 20 genes and an average clustering coefficient greater than 0.6 were retained for downstream analysis, following previous studies (Li et al., 2022, Loers and Vermeirssen, 2022) that demonstrated this threshold effectively preserved biologically coherent and topologically cohesive modules.

Concordance analysis of prognostic genes

Suppose there are two prognostic gene lists: List 1 containing n genes and list 2 containing M genes. Let L denote the total number of background genes that are commonly measured in both datasets. Among the k overlapping genes between list 1 and list 2, let s represent the number of genes showing the same prognostic direction. The probability of observing at least s consistent genes by chance is estimated using a cumulative hypergeometric distribution:P=1-∑i=0s-1MiL-Mn-iLn

Here, P < 0.05 indicates that the overlap is statistically significant. To quantify the level of consistency, this study defines a concordance score as s/k, which ranges from 0 (no consistent prognostic direction) to 1 (all overlapping genes have the same prognostic direction). A higher s/k score reflects stronger concordance between the two lists.

The hypergeometric distribution model was also used to test whether pro-oncogenic signatures or protective signatures significantly overlap with the genes involved in a functional module.

Network centrality analysis and key gene identification

The PDAC-specific GCN-derived network module (referred to as PGCN module) was constructed by integrating the PDAC-specific driver module with the global GCN network. Degree centrality, closeness centrality, and betweenness centrality were employed to identify key proteins within the PGCN module. Degree centrality was applied to identify key proteins with the highest number of direct interactions within the PGCN module. Closeness centrality was used to detect proteins that can rapidly communicate with all other nodes, as indicated by the inverse of the average shortest path length. Betweenness centrality was employed to pinpoint bottleneck proteins that serve as critical intermediaries across shortest paths between node pairs. These measures are well-established indicators of node significance in biological networks (Bidkhori et al., 2018, Bozhilova et al., 2021).

Permutation tests were performed 1 000 times to identify statistically significant key proteins for each network centrality measure. In each test, a randomized network preserving the original degree distribution was generated by rewiring at least 66% of the edges within the PGCN module. Centrality measures were recalculated in each randomized network to establish empirical null distributions. Empirical P values were determined based on the cumulative distributions of each centrality measure, and proteins with an empirical P ≤ 0.01 in any centrality metric were considered significant. All network analyses were implemented using the Python package NetworkX (v2.2) (Hagberg, Schult, & Swart, 2008).

Single cell analysis

Single-cell transcriptomic data from the GSE156405 (Lee et al., 2021) dataset were processed using the Seurat (v4.0.2) package in R. Cells with fewer than 200 or more than 5 000 detected genes or with > 10% mitochondrial RNA content were excluded. Gene expression was log-normalized, and the top 2 000 highly variable genes were selected. PCA was performed on scaled data, and the first 30 components were used for unsupervised clustering with a resolution of 0.5. Two-dimensional embedding was performed using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE).

CRISPR-based gene effect analysis

Gene essentiality profiles was quantified using genome-wide CRISPR knockout screening data from DepMap 21Q2 (Tsherniak et al., 2017). Lower gene effect scores indicate greater gene essentiality.

Drug discovery

To identify potential therapeutic agents targeting specific genes, this study developed an innovative three-step drug discovery framework that integrates patient-derived gene expression profiles with perturbational transcriptomic signatures from the LINCS database. This strategy begins by stratifying tumor samples from large-scale transcriptomic datasets based on the expression levels of a gene of interest. Specifically, the top 10% and bottom 10% of samples are designated as high and low expression groups, respectively. Differential expression analysis between these groups yields gene signatures comprising the top 150 upregulated and bottom 150 downregulated genes, reflecting transcriptional programs associated with the gene’s dysregulation.

Next, to translate module-level findings into actionable predictions, this study adopted a gene-centric stratification strategy that focuses on central, functionally relevant genes as intervention points within the disease module, consistent with network-medicine principles (Sadegh et al., 2021, Zhang et al., 2022, Zhou et al., 2020). The reversal potential of 1,691 compounds was then systematically evaluated using LINCS perturbational profiles generated in YAPC cells. YAPC, a human pancreatic cancer cell line derived from malignant ascites, is the only pancreatic cancer model available in the LINCS drug perturbation dataset. To ensure comparability across compounds, only signatures obtained under the standardized exposure condition of 10 μmol/L for 24 h were included, as this represents the most frequently profiled treatment setting in the dataset. For each compound, this study ranked the expression changes of the 978 landmark genes (Level 5 z-scores) and applied a Kolmogorov-Smirnov-like statistic to separately calculate enrichment scores for the upregulated (ksupi) and downregulated (ksdowni) signature components:a=maxj=1tjt-V(j)n,b=maxj=1tV(j)n-j-1tksi=a,ifa>b-b,otherwise

where Vj denotes the position of the j-th gene from the input signature within the ranked drug-induced expression profile, t is the size of the signature, and n is the total number of landmark genes. The final connectivity score is defined as:S=0,ifksupi=ksdowniksupi-ksdowni,otherwise

A negative S score indicates inverse enrichment and suggests that the compound may counteract the disease-associated transcriptional state. Compounds exhibiting statistically significant reversal (P < 0.05) are considered candidate therapeutics functionally antagonizing the gene’s pathological effects. This computational procedure infers functional antagonism at the transcriptional level within the LINCS L1000 connectivity-mapping framework and does not assume or test direct physical binding between a compound and the encoded protein in this study.

To enhance robustness across datasets and reduce context-specific variability, this study applied a rank-based consensus strategy. For each gene, ranked lists of significantly enriched compounds from multiple cohorts were aggregated using the geometric mean of ranks, thereby prioritizing agents with consistent signature reversal potential. The top three compounds per gene were selected as the most promising therapeutic candidates.

Benchmarking of different methods

To evaluate the predictive accuracy of the network-medicine (NM) framework, its classification performance was compared with that of three representative algorithms: Random walk with restart (RWR), least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), and the structural network controllability-based personalized driver genes and combinatorial drug identification algorithm (CPGD). All methods were implemented according to published protocols (Guo et al., 2021, Tang et al., 2017, Wen et al., 2021) using identical PDAC datasets (CPTAC, TCGA, and Metadata) and uniform preprocessing pipelines to ensure fairness.

For each method, method-derived features were used to train a binary classifier that distinguishes tumor samples from normal samples. Classification performance was evaluated using stratified 10-fold cross-validation: In each fold, the classifier was trained on the training subset and tested on the held-out subset to obtain predicted probabilities for the tumor class. The out-of-sample predictions from all folds were aggregated to construct receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. The area under the ROC curve (AUC), together with sensitivity and specificity at the Youden-optimal cutoff, was used to quantify overall classification performance.

Cell viability assay

A total of 5 × 103 cells/well were seeded into 96-well plates. After 24 h, cells were treated with mitoxantrone, OTSSP167, PF-3758309, or vorinostat at concentrations of 0, 0.3, 1, 3, and 10 μmol/L. The concentration gradient was designed to include a vehicle control group (0 μmol/L) and to incorporate the reference concentration used in the LINCS-based drug discovery analysis (10 μmol/L), together with lower concentrations (0.3, 1, and 3 μmol/L), thereby enabling characterization of the dose–response relationship and accurate evaluation of compound-induced inhibition of cell viability. Cells were incubated for another 48 h at 37 °C. Cell viability was then assessed using a CCK-8 kit (A098250227, Beyotime, Shanghai, China), followed by a 1 h incubation. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a BMG LABTECH POLARstar Omega microplate reader (BMG LABTECH, Ortenberg, Germany).

Cell viability (%) = (Asample − Ablank)/(Acontrol − Ablank) × 100

Where Asample is the absorbance of treated cells, Acontrol is the absorbance of untreated control cells, and Ablank is the absorbance of medium without cells.

Statistical analyses

Both the univariate Cox proportional hazards regression model and the Kaplan-Meier method were used to evaluate the association of gene expression with overall survival (OS). For Cox analysis, this study used a R package named ‘survival’ in which the input data is the expression values of each gene across all samples of patients, OS and survival outcomes of patients (death or alive). For Kaplan-Meier analysis, this study classified the patients into two groups based on the expression of each gene and examined their prognoses. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were estimated and compared using the log-rank test. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between tumor and normal tissue groups were identified using DESeq2. The Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method was applied to control the false discovery rate (FDR), with genes showing FDR < 0.05 considered statistically significant DEGs.

All experiments were repeated at least three times. Statistical differences between the control vehicle and treatment groups were calculated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Significance was determined with a threshold of P values < 0.05. All statistical analyses for validation results were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0.0 software, while computational analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.0 and Python 3.9.7.

Result

Computational framework for genome-wide target and drug prediction

This study developed a network-based computational framework for in silico genome-wide target identification and transcriptome-guided drug discovery in PDAC. The analytical procedure encompassed three interconnected parts (Fig. 1). Transcriptomic and clinical data were collected and integrated from multiple independent PDAC cohorts (Fig. 1A). Prognostic gene signatures were projected into PDAC-specific co-expression networks constructed from multi-cohort transcriptomic data. Modules enriched for survival-associated genes were identified and prioritized. Within top-ranked modules, candidate key genes were selected based on multi-metric centrality scores and validated using degree-preserving permutation tests. To ensure functional and clinical relevance, these candidates were further evaluated by genome-wide CRISPR dependency datasets and single-cell RNA-seq to confirm essentiality and tumor-specific expression (Fig. 1B). Transcriptome-guided drug discovery was conducted by matching PDAC-specific transcriptional perturbation profiles, centered on core disease genes, with drug-induced gene expression signatures from the LINCS database. Identification of pro-oncogenic and protective signatures

This study applied both Kaplan-Meier analysis and univariate Cox model to investigate the associations of the mRNA expression levels of genes with the patients’ OS in CPTAC, TCGA and Metadata PDAC cohorts. A Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed by classifying the patients into two groups with high and low expression of the investigated gene by selecting an optimal cut off yielding the lowest log-rank P-value. Meanwhile, this study performed the univariate Cox analysis by calculating the hazard ratio of each gene. Prognostic genes were classified into two distinct signatures: Pro-oncogenic signatures, where high expression correlated with poor survival, and protective signatures, where high expression correlated with favorable outcomes. Intersection analysis across all three cohorts revealed 332 consistently significant pro-oncogenic signature genes (Fig. 2A) and 416 protective signature genes (Fig. 2B). Functional enrichment analysis showed that the pro-oncogenic signature genes were significantly enriched in cell division and cell cycle pathways. In contrast, the protective signature genes were significantly enriched in the angiogenesis, vasculogenesis, cell migration and cell differentiation pathways (P < 0.05, Fig. 2C), which are well-known hallmarks in cancer (Hanahan, 2022). Identification of functional modules in GCN

GCN analysis is a powerful method to identify the hub genes that drive the key cellular signaling pathways, including a set of co-expressed or functionally associated genes during tumor development (van Dam, Võsa, van der Graaf, Franke, & de Magalhães, 2018). The conservative co-expressed pattern may confer a selective advantage for tumor cells if it could be validated in independent datasets. This study computed Spearman correlation coefficients for all possible gene pairs using mRNA expression profiles from PDAC patients across CPTAC, TCGA, and Metadata cohorts. To balance the sensitivity for detecting the functional gene modules and the robustness for validation in independent datasets, gene-gene links involved in the top 1% with the highest correlation coefficients were extracted to build the GCN. This study obtained around 1.9 × 106 gene-gene links with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.46 to 1 in the co-expression network of the CPTAC cohort. Then, this study used a random walks-based algorithm, Walktrap (Dimitrakopoulos et al., 2018), to identify the gene modules with high transitivity based on the topology of the GCN. To discover functionally meaningful modules in which genes show a good connectivity, the modules with more than 20 genes and clustering coefficients higher than 0.6 were extracted. Finally, this study obtained 43 modules (M1−M43) in the CPTAC cohort (Fig. 2D). Further, this study investigated the association of each module with the pro-oncogenic signature and protective signature genes by concordance analysis (Table S1). The significant overlap was found between M35/M19 modules and pro-oncogenic signature genes (hypergeometric test, P < 0.05). Based on network topology and gene signature overlap analysis, this study classified M35 and M19 modules as pro-oncogenic modules due to their significant associations with pro-oncogenic signatures (hypergeometric test, FDR < 0.05). Concurrently, the M3, M4, M12, and M16 modules were identified as protective modules, significantly enriched for protective signatures (hypergeometric test, P < 0.05). Then, this study investigated the biological function of these modules by functional enrichment analysis. It was found that the genes involved in the pro-oncogenic modules were significantly enriched in cell division, cell cycle, DNA replication and p53 signaling pathways (P < 0.05, Fig. 2D). The genes involved in the protective modules were significantly enriched in biological processes such as vasculogenesis, cell migration, differentiation, cell growth, and immune T cell proliferation, as well as in the cAMP-mediated signaling pathway (P < 0.05, Fig. 2D).

Based on the same methodology, this study obtained approximately 1.8 × 106 gene-gene links with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.79 to 1 in the GCN of the TCGA cohort. This study identified 35 modules (M1–M35) using the same cutoff criteria (Fig. 2E). Among them, M26, M32, and M34 showed significant overlaps with pro-oncogenic signature genes, while M3, M8, and M12 were significantly associated with protective signature genes (hypergeometric test, P < 0.05, Fig. 2E, Table S2). Accordingly, these modules were classified as pro-oncogenic and protective, respectively. In the Metadata cohort’s GCN, this study identified approximately 6 × 105 gene-gene links with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.47 to 0.98. Applying stringent cutoff thresholds (Fig. 2F), a total of 19 distinct modules (M1–M19) were detected. Among them, M5, M9, and M13 modules showed significant overlap with pro-oncogenic signature genes, while M15 module demonstrated a significant association with protective signature genes (hypergeometric test, P < 0.05, Fig. 2F, Table S3). Based on these findings, these modules were similarly categorized as pro-oncogenic and protective.

This study next conducted a cross-cohort comparison of pro-oncogenic modules. Notably, the CPTAC M35 module showed complete overlap with the TCGA M34 module (100%) and substantial overlap with the TCGA M32 (98%), M26 (92%), and Metadata M13 modules (78%) (Fig. 2G). Functional enrichment consistently revealed that these modules were involved in tumor-promoting processes, including mitotic cell cycle, nuclear division, and DNA replication. Visualization of individual genes within this module (Fig. 3A) highlighted its reproducibility across cohorts and its strong association with poor prognosis in PDAC patients. As a key module in PDAC progression, M35 thus emerges as a cross-cohort representative pro-oncogenic module, underscoring its potential as both a therapeutic target and a biomarker. Identification of genome-wide candidate therapeutic targets

Analysis of network topology could provide insight into the importance of a given gene in the network. The genes with high centrality are considered as key genes in the network, and this study predicted that these are potential targets that are worthwhile to be further evaluated. Degree, betweenness, and closeness centrality were computed to identify key regulatory genes within the PDAC-specific co-expression network. These metrics were used to assess the importance of each gene in network connectivity (Bidkhori et al., 2018; Bozhilova et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2008). This study then calculated the Spearman correlation of each centrality measure between the three pro-oncogenic modules identified from the CPTAC, TCGA, and Metadata datasets. As shown in Fig. 3C, the correlation coefficients for degree, closeness, and betweenness between M35 module from the CPTAC cohort and M34 module from the TCGA cohort were 0.64, 0.64, and 0.42, respectively (P < 0.05). Similarly, the correlation coefficients between M35 module from the CPTAC cohort and M13 module from the Metadata cohort were 0.73, 0.7, and 0.35 (P < 0.05). These results suggest that the topological structure of the pro-oncogenic modules identified from the CPTAC cohort is highly similar to those in the TCGA and Metadata cohorts, despite the different racial and geographical characteristics of the three independent cohorts. Based on these findings, this study designated M35 module as the PGCN module for downstream analyses.

To robustly identify statistically significant key genes, this study performed permutation testing based on a degree-preserving network rewiring strategy. The PGCN module was randomized 1 000 times by rewiring at least 66% of its edges while preserving node degree distributions. Centrality metrics were recalculated in each randomized network to construct empirical null distributions. A gene was considered statistically significant if its centrality in the original network exceeded the 99th percentile of the corresponding null distribution (P < 0.01). Proteins consistently identified as significant across all three metrics were defined as key nodes. This analysis was performed using a custom method based on NetworkX (Hagberg, Schult,& Swart, 2008), supporting multiple null models including edge rewiring and degree-preserving node shuffling. Using this strategy, 14 key genes were identified: BUB1 mitotic checkpoint serine (BUB1), CKAP2L, non-SMC condensin I complex subunit H (NCAPH), TPX2 microtubule nucleation factor (TPX2), aurora kinase A (AURKA), cyclin dependent kinase 1 (CDK1), centrosomal protein 55 (CEP55), exonuclease 1 (EXO1), hyaluronan mediated motility receptor (HMMR), KIF18A, kinesin family member 2C (KIF2C), MCM10, non-SMC condensin I complex subunit G (NCAPG), and pituitary tumor-transforming 1 (PTTG1). These molecular hubs exhibited robust associations with poor clinical outcomes across multiple analytical approaches. As demonstrated in Fig. S1, hazard ratio (HR) analyses revealed particularly strong prognostic associations for BUB1 [HR = 1.58, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.23−1.96 and AURKA (HR = 1.67, 95% CI 1.25−2.06], with all 14 key genes showing statistically significant risk prediction (P < 0.01). The clinical validity of these findings was further confirmed through survival analyses in three independent patient cohorts (Fig. S2−S4), where high expression of these key genes consistently correlated with markedly shorter overall survival compared to low expression groups. The KIF2C, HMMR, CDK1 emerged as particularly noteworthy, demonstrating the strongest prognostic stratification power across all validation datasets. This molecular triad, along with other identified hubs, appears to play fundamental roles in cell cycle regulation and chromosomal stability, as evidenced by their network centrality measures and functional annotations. Notably, AURKA and PTTG1 have demonstrated translational potential and are currently being evaluated in clinical trials (NCT03517995, NCT02709993). The convergence of evidence from computational, transcriptional, and clinical analyses strongly implicates these key genes as master regulators of PDAC progression.

Expression levels of the 14 key genes were visualized using gene expression data from tumor and adjacent normal samples in the CPTAC cohort. As shown in Fig. 3D, these pro-oncogenic key genes exhibited markedly low expression in normal tissues but were significantly upregulated in tumor tissues. Given that these key genes encode proteins involved in mitosis and cell cycle regulation, this expression shift may suggest that their low expression maintains normal cellular turnover and metabolism, while increased expression contributes to tumor initiation or progression. Further, this study investigated the alteration coverage of these key genes in CPTAC tumor tissues. The results showed that over 95% of patients, excluding CDK1, presented high expression of pro-oncogenic key genes, which was associated with poor survival outcomes (Fig. 4A), suggesting that most of the PDAC patients may carry the common molecular alterations induced by the pro-oncogenic key genes but not for their anti-tumor mechanisms. To confirm the representative features of these key genes at the single-cell level, this study examined their expression patterns across distinct cell types using single-cell RNA-seq data (Fig. 4B, C). The t-SNE visualization revealed that these genes were primarily enriched in epithelial and proliferative T cell populations, suggesting cell-type-specific expression of oncogenic markers. To further evaluate the 14 pro-oncogenic key genes, this study conducted comprehensive literature mining. Notably, 11 of these genes have been extensively reported in relation to PDAC progression and prognosis (Table S1), underscoring the robustness and clinical relevance of our network-based screening strategy. In contrast, although CKAP2L and KIF18A have been noted in transcriptomic comparisons of PDAC (Kawai et al., 2025), their PDAC-specific functional characterization remains limited. Moreover, studies investigating MCM10 in PDAC are scarce, prompting us to propose these genes as potential regulatory candidates for further exploration. This study next evaluated their prognostic significance across three independent patient cohorts comprising CPTAC, TCGA and Metadata. All three genes showed consistent associations with poor overall survival, with high expression levels marking reduced patient outcomes in each dataset. This cross-cohort reproducibility highlighted their potential clinical relevance as unfavorable prognostic indicators (Fig. 4D).

This study next evaluated the functional dependency of these three candidate genes using genome-wide CRISPR knockout screening data from DepMap 21Q2 (Tsherniak et al., 2017). Gene effect scores were derived using the CERES algorithm, which integrates sgRNA depletion patterns with genomic copy number corrections to estimate gene essentiality across cancer cell lines. The CERES model generates normalized gene effect scores, where a score of 0 reflects the median dependency of nonessential genes, and a score of −1 corresponds to the median of pan-essential genes, such as those involved in ribosomal biogenesis and RNA splicing. Remarkably, gene effect profiling revealed that CKAP2L, KIF18A, and MCM10 were broadly essential across multiple pancreatic cancer cell lines. As shown in Fig. 4E–F, these genes ranked among the most critical dependencies in PDAC models, with average CERES scores approaching those of canonical essential genes (Meyers et al., 2017). For example, KIF18A reached a score of −1.85 in HPAF11 pancreatic cancer cells, established from human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma tissue. MCM10 scored −0.76 in BXPC3 pancreatic cancer cells, derived from human pancreatic adenocarcinoma, and CKAP2L showed −0.35 in CAPAN1 pancreatic cancer cells, established from human pancreatic carcinoma metastatic to the liver, as shown in Fig. 4F. These findings were corroborated by RNAi-based depletion data, which showed similar trends across multiple models (Fig. 4E). The convergence of survival association, network centrality, and gene essentiality strongly implicates CKAP2L, KIF18A, and MCM10 as previously unrecognized but functionally indispensable oncogenic drivers in PDAC.

In addition, to further assess the reliability of the NM framework, this study compared it with three representative algorithms: RWR, LASSO, and CPGD. Using identical PDAC datasets (CPTAC, TCGA, and Metadata) and consistent preprocessing across all methods, the NM framework consistently achieved higher sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing tumor from normal samples. Specifically, NM obtained AUC values of 0.987 3 (CPTAC), 0.993 2 (TCGA), and 0.816 7 (Metadata), outperforming all other methods (Fig. S5).

Identification of candidate drugs for therapeutic targets

Given the lack of known inhibitors for several key oncogenic drivers identified in this study, this study hypothesized that systematic reversal of gene expression programs induced by gene dysregulation could enable rational discovery of candidate small-molecule therapeutics. To this end, this study developed a transcriptome-guided drug discovery framework specifically aimed at single-gene targets without existing inhibitors. By integrating patient-derived transcriptomes with large-scale perturbational signatures, our approach exploits the transcriptional reversibility of target-associated oncogenic states to identify compounds with potential antagonistic effects.

As illustrated in Fig. 5, the strategy consists of three sequential steps. First, the gene-specific expression signatures were constructed by stratifying tumor samples into high and low expression subgroups for each target gene, retaining the top 150 upregulated and bottom 150 downregulated genes as representative transcriptional footprints of the dysregulated state. Second, this study assessed the reversal potential of 1 691 compounds profiled in YAPC cells (10 μmol/L, 24 h) from the LINCS database. Notably, YAPC is the only pancreatic cancer-derived cell line included in the LINCS compendium, making it a uniquely relevant model for PDAC-specific drug perturbation analysis. For each compound, this study calculated a connectivity score (S) that quantifies the anti-correlation between the drug-induced expression profile and the disease signature using a Kolmogorov-Smirnov-like statistic. Connectivity scores were used to rank 1 691 compounds based on their ability to reverse the disease-associated transcriptional signature. Significantly negative scores (P < 0.05) indicated stronger reversal potential. Finally, to ensure robustness across biological contexts and mitigate dataset-specific noise, this study aggregated compound ranks across cohorts using the geometric mean, thereby prioritizing consensus hits with consistent reversal potential.Fig. 5Integrative framework for therapeutic targets-based drug discovery. This method is particularly suited for targets where no known inhibitors exist, and unlike structure-based approaches, it leverages endogenous transcriptional perturbations to infer functional antagonism. As shown in Fig. 5, this strategy revealed that cobimetinib, mitoxantrone, and OTS-167 were strong candidates targeting CKAP2L, while triclosan, PF-03758309, and belinostat reversed KIF18A-associated profiles, and vorinostat, AZ-628, and PD-0325901 were top-ranked for MCM10. These findings highlight the potential of expression-based drug discovery to reveal unanticipated therapeutic vulnerabilities in previously undruggable targets.

Validation of drug effect

To functionally validate the drug-target interactions predicted by the integrative network-based framework, this study selected four representative compound-gene combinations based on the following criteria: (1) the compound ranked within the top three candidates for a given target gene, as determined by the geometric mean of CMAP reversal scores across individual gene perturbation features; (2) the compound was commercially available and demonstrated favorable pharmacological properties, including reported bioavailability and tolerability. Both mitoxantrone and OTSSP167, predicted for CKAP2L, fulfilled these criteria and were therefore included in validation experiments. In contrast, although multiple top-ranked compounds were predicted for KIF18A and MCM10, only PF-3758309 and vorinostat, respectively, were commercially available. As a result, these four compounds were selected for in vitro assays to represent the most accessible and computationally prioritized candidates.

For subsequent in vitro validation, SW1990 human pancreatic cancer cells were selected because they are a well-established and commonly used cellular model in PDAC research. SW1990 cells were treated with each candidate compound at concentrations of 1, 3, and 10 μmol/L. The qRT-PCR analysis revealed dose-dependent suppression of target gene expression (Fig. 6A–D). Specifically, CKAP2L transcript levels were markedly reduced by both mitoxantrone and OTSSP167, while MCM10 and KIF18A expression were significantly downregulated by vorinostat and PF-3758309, respectively. To assess whether the three targets are functionally coupled rather than isolated, this study additionally performed a simultaneous qRT-PCR assay for CKAP2L, KIF18A, and MCM10 (Fig. S6). SW1990 cells were treated with mitoxantrone or OTSSP167, PF-3758309, or vorinostat (MCM10 axis) at 1, 3, and 10 μmol/L. Across doses, each compound produced a dose-dependent reduction of its cognate gene and, importantly, significantly decreased the other two genes in the same assay (P < 0.05). These data indicate positive correlation and cross-target suppression among the three hubs, consistent with a shared proliferative/mitotic program. Consistent with the transcriptional data, Western blotting analysis confirmed a substantial reduction in target protein levels following treatment (Fig. 6E–H). At 10 μmol/L, the concentration consistent with that used in the LINCS-based transcriptomic screening, densitometric analysis showed that CKAP2L protein expression decreased by over 60% in response to mitoxantrone or OTSSP167, while KIF18A and MCM10 levels declined by more than 50% after treatment with vorinostat and PF-3758309, respectively. To assess the phenotypic impact of these molecular perturbations, cell viability assays were performed using compound concentrations of 0, 0.3, 1, 3, and 10 μmol/L. All four compounds reduced cell viability in a dose-dependent manner, with IC50 values of 3.14 μmol/L (mitoxantrone), 4.24 μmol/L (OTSSP167), 4.09 μmol/L (PF-3758309), and 5.23 μmol/L (vorinostat) (Fig. 6I–L). These findings suggest that pharmacological inhibition of CKAP2L, KIF18A, and MCM10 effectively impairs PDAC cells growth. In summary, our approach successfully identified actionable drug candidates, confirming CKAP2L, KIF18A, and MCM10 as viable therapeutic targets. In addition, to assess multi-cell-line sensitivity, this study performed the same CCK-8 assay in PANC-1 and CFPAC-1 cells, consistent with SW1990, all four candidates produced clear, dose-dependent inhibition (Fig. S7). Discussion

PDAC is characterized by substantial molecular heterogeneity, with established subtypes including classical, basal-like, and exocrine-like as defined by the Collisson classification, as well as normal and activated stroma identified by the Moffitt classification (Collisson et al., 2011). These transcriptional and stromal subtypes exhibit distinct biological behaviors and therapeutic responses. Classical tumors are relatively sensitive to first-line chemotherapy such as FOLFIRINOX, whereas basal-like and activated stromal subtypes are associated with poor prognosis and marked resistance. The dense desmoplastic stroma and immunosuppressive microenvironment not only hinder drug delivery but also limit the efficacy of immunotherapy. Different subtypes of patients respond differently to chemotherapy. Low response rates and drug resistance continue to hinder effective PDAC treatment (Bryant et al., 2019, Chan-Seng-Yue et al., 2020).

This study proposed an integrated strategy combining therapeutic target identification with transcriptome-guided drug discovery to uncover novel treatment opportunities for PDAC. Using this approach, this study identified three promising therapeutic targets involved in mitosis and cell cycle regulation: CKAP2L, KIF18A, and MCM10. CKAP2L encodes a mitotic spindle-associated protein predominantly expressed during the G2/M phase and contributes to the transcriptional elongation of mitosis-related genes (Yumoto et al., 2013). KIF18A, a kinesin motor protein, regulates chromosome congression and spindle dynamics during mitosis, with peak expression also observed at the G2/M phase (Chen, Le, Li, Liu, & Chen, 2024). MCM10 acts as a replication licensing factor essential for initiation and elongation of DNA replication, mainly functioning during the S phase to maintain replication fork stability and ensure fidelity of DNA synthesis (Masnovo et al., 2024). Notably, these targets have been implicated in clinically relevant cancer phenotypes. CKAP2L promotes chromosomal instability and tumor progression, and its inhibition enhances therapeutic efficacy in non-small cell lung cancer (Monteverde et al., 2021). Furthermore, CKAP2L expression correlates with immune-related biomarkers such as tumor mutational burden (TMB) and microsatellite instability (MSI), suggesting its potential as a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy response (Yi et al., 2023). KIF18A represents a mitotic vulnerability in tumors with high chromosomal instability (CIN), where its inhibition selectively disrupts cellular proliferation (Cohen-Sharir et al., 2021). MCM10 overexpression is linked to genomic instability, replication stress, and poor clinical outcomes across various malignancies, it has been proposed as a prognostic biomarker in breast and ovarian cancers and as a selective vulnerability in rapidly dividing tumor cells (Ahmed et al., 2024, Chen et al., 2023). Importantly, the biological functions of these targets also suggest potential relevance to drug resistance mechanisms in PDAC. CKAP2L and KIF18A key regulators of mitotic fidelity, and their inhibition may help suppress CIN, which is strongly associated with clonal evolution and adaptive resistance (Crowley et al., 2022, Hosea et al., 2024). KIF18A inhibition has been reported to activate the spindle assembly checkpoint and to preferentially impair CIN-high tumor cells, thereby potentially sensitizing them to microtubule-targeting agents and other therapies that induce mitotic stress (Payton et al., 2024). MCM10 is required for replication stress tolerance and genome stability, and its depletion induces replication catastrophe and cell death, indicating a therapeutic vulnerability that may increase sensitivity to DNA-damaging agents or other treatments that induce replication stress (Ahmed, Sasikumar, Laha, & Das, 2024). Consistent with these mechanisms, our simultaneous qRT-PCR showed that perturbing one hub down-regulates the other two, suggesting that pharmacologic pressure on any single axis can impose network-level constraints on the shared mitotic/replication program. This multi-node coupling may reduce single-path escape routes and thereby helping delay resistance. Collectively, these gene-specific mechanisms suggest that our prioritized targets not only sustain PDAC proliferation but also represent vulnerabilities that could attenuate adaptive resistance by suppressing CIN and replication-stress-tolerance pathways. Consistent with these findings, these genes were significantly upregulated in tumor tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues in our analysis. Moreover, broad upregulation of these targets was observed across PDAC patients, implying that most patients could potentially benefit from therapies targeting these genes, despite individual variations in disease state.

We further developed a clinically relevant drug discovery strategy by integrating patient-derived transcriptomic profiles with drug-induced gene expression signatures. Our approach addresses a critical limitation in conventional target-based drug discovery: Lack of inhibitors for transcriptionally or functionally essential oncogenes, especially those traditionally considered “undruggable” due to absence of ligand-binding pockets or targetable domains. CKAP2L, KIF18A, and MCM10 exemplify such targets, as no clinically approved inhibitors specifically developed for PDAC currently exist. Leveraging the LINCS perturbation database, this study systematically modeled the transcriptional impact of pharmacological agents and computationally identified compounds capable of reversing oncogenic expression programs driven by these genes. This expression-based reversal framework bypasses structural constraints, exploiting tumor transcriptomic plasticity to identify functionally antagonistic compounds even for previously refractory targets. The capacity to repurpose clinically approved drugs or well-characterized tool compounds for novel oncogenic drivers highlights the translational potential of our method. Moreover, although the L1000 assay quantifies 978 landmark genes to ensure experimental reproducibility and cross-platform comparability, our computational framework itself is not restricted to this gene set. Because the network construction and signature derivation are based on correlation and differential-expression analysis, the same pipeline can be directly applied to full-transcriptome datasets by omitting the landmark-gene filtering step. This scalability enables broader application of the framework as large-scale RNA-seq perturbation resources continue to grow. Using this correlation-driven approach, this study prioritized four candidate compounds per target. In vitro validation confirmed that mitoxantrone and OTSSP167 effectively suppressed CKAP2L, PF-3758309 inhibited KIF18A, and vorinostat downregulated MCM10, collectively reducing PDAC cell proliferation. The concordance between computational predictions and experimental results underscores the robustness and accuracy of our framework.

Mitoxantrone, a topoisomerase II inhibitor initially developed for acute leukemia and approved for prostate cancer (Hakenberg et al., 2019, Larson et al., 2012), has demonstrated inhibition of tumor cell proliferation in breast cancer and immunosuppressive effects in multiple sclerosis models (Burstein et al., 2021, Neuhaus et al., 2006). OTSSP167, an inhibitor of maternal embryonic leucine zipper kinase (MELK), targets cancer stem cells and suppresses tumor growth in breast cancer and acute myeloid leukemia models (Bridges et al., 2021, Okur and Yerlikaya, 2019, Zhang et al., 2021). PF-3758309, an inhibitor of p21-activated kinase 4 (PAK4), was designed to impair tumor cell proliferation and survival. Vorinostat, a histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor approved for cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (Olsen et al., 2007), effectively inhibits tumor growth in various solid tumors, including lung and breast cancers (Lin et al., 2021, Peterson et al., 2021). Multi-line CCK-8 results and low HPNE cytotoxicity support tumor-selective activity at low-micromolar, clinically plausible exposures, while highlighting the need for follow-up pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) to solidify translational relevance. Given that CKAP2L, KIF18A, and MCM10 are mitotic and cell cycle-dependent genes crucial for tumor proliferation, this study hypothesizes that combined targeting of these genes may synergistically inhibit tumor growth. Additionally, the combination of these compounds with first-line treatments, such as gemcitabine, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, or anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) monoclonal antibodies, merits further investigation. Notably, PF-3758309 combined with gemcitabine has shown significant synergistic anti-tumor effects in an orthotopic murine PDAC model. Recent preclinical data from kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS)-mutant models, including PDAC, support combining KRAS inhibitors with agents that heighten replication or mitotic stress. For instance, the KRAS^G12D inhibitor MRTX1133 has been shown to synergize with afatinib, a pan-erythroblastic oncogene B (ErbB) receptor tyrosine kinase blocker, and with avutometinib, a rapidly accelerated fibrosarcoma (RAF)/mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase (MEK) clamp, thereby producing greater tumor suppression than either agent used as monotherapy (Gulay et al., 2023, Toyokuni et al., 2025). Likewise, combining KRAS^G12C inhibitors with WEE1 G2 checkpoint kinase (WEE1) inhibitors produces deeper and more durable responses (Yamamoto et al., 2024). These findings align with the known roles of MCM10, KIF18A, and CKAP2L; targeting these processes may sensitize KRAS-mutant PDAC to KRAS-pathway blockade by increasing replication or mitotic stress. Accordingly, this study will prioritize this line of investigation in the next phase of studies.

The integrative computational framework established here is broadly applicable to other cancers or complex diseases, particularly when conventional target discovery is limited. Unlike traditional approaches relying on predefined shRNA or CRISPR perturbation datasets, our method utilizes patient-derived transcriptomic profiles to construct gene-centric disease signatures. By stratifying tumor samples based on candidate gene expression, this study generated disease-relevant transcriptional footprints enabling robust connectivity analysis against compound-induced expression profiles in the LINCS database. This strategy identifies functionally antagonistic compounds via gene expression reversal, independent of prior structural knowledge or direct gene perturbation data. Crucially, therapeutic target identification is performed genome-wide by integrating network biology, transcriptomics, and clinical data. This study constructed PDAC-specific gene co-expression networks from three independent patient cohorts, followed by module detection and multi-dimensional centrality analysis to prioritize key regulatory genes. Candidate targets were validated through single-cell RNA sequencing, survival analysis, and genome-wide CRISPR dependency data, confirming their biological relevance, functional indispensability, and tumor specificity. The modularity and generalizability of this approach make it well-suited for identifying druggable vulnerabilities in tumors lacking actionable mutations or established targets, as well as for repurposing compounds in diseases constrained by target inaccessibility or economic factors. However, while the framework provides multi-level evidence supporting PDAC relevance of CKAP2L, KIF18A, and MCM10, the PDAC-specific causal mechanisms remain to be experimentally validated. Future studies integrating CRISPR perturbation and rescue, mitotic-instability assays, and target-engagement validation will be essential to delineate their mechanistic contributions to chromosomal instability and tumor progression.

Conclusion

This study presents a novel network-based computational framework for genome-wide identification of potential therapeutic targets in PDAC. By integrating multi-cohort transcriptomic datasets, single-cell RNA sequencing, and genome-wide CRISPR dependency screens, this study identified 14 pro-oncogenic targets, including three previously underexplored and functionally relevant drivers: CKAP2L, KIF18A, and MCM10. To complement this, this study developed a transcriptome-guided drug discovery strategy that systematically matches PDAC-specific transcriptional signatures with LINCS-derived drug-induced expression profiles, enabling single-gene-focused therapeutic predictions. This integrative approach successfully leds to the identification and experimental validation of small-molecule antagonists against previously undruggable oncogenic drivers, offering a promising direction for precision medicine in PDAC. With the continuous advancement of artificial intelligence, big data analytics, and high-throughput screening technologies, this framework holds strong potential for further automation and intelligent adaptation across other cancer types and complex diseases, significantly enhancing the efficiency and precision of target discovery and drug development.
