Flow field regulation and sediment particle flocculation mechanisms in ultrasound-assisted electrocoagulation intervention
Authors: Yifeng Liu, Wenwen Bai, Jiahua Wei, Zhen Qiao, Shangyao Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUltrasound-assisted electrocoagulation (US-EC) shows promise in improving flocculation through cavitation and flow field regulation, yet the mechanisms of flow field evolution and particle size control under acoustic-electric coupling remain underexplored. In this study, an acoustic-electric coupling observation platform for suspended sediment flocculation was established. The motion response characteristics of gas–solid and mixed phases under the intervention of acoustic-electric coupling were observed by employing Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV). Then, the effects of different ultrasonic frequencies and current densities on flow field characteristics, particle size distribution, and flocculation behavior were systematically explored, and ultimately, the synergistic mechanism of US-EC in particle fragmentation and aggregation was revealed. According to the experimental results, low-frequency (28 kHz) ultrasound, owing to a strong cavitation effect, can effectively fragment medium-sized particles while enhancing the specific surface area and fluid disturbances. Meanwhile, high current density (40 A/m2) can accelerate electrolytic reactions and promote rapid reduction of Zeta potential, thereby promoting efficient particle aggregation. Under the operating condition of 28 kHz + 40 A/m2, the fluid system maintained a moderate flow velocity (11.01 mm/s) with uniform vorticity distribution, leading to a dynamic balance between disturbance intensity and floc structural stability (15 ≤ η/D < 25, 10−6 ≲ ε ≲ 10−4 m2 s−3), which significantly enhanced flocculation efficiency. Accordingly, the average particle size increased from 18.89 μm to 60.98 μm, with a sedimentation rate of 87.96 %. Overall, the US-EC process follows a three-phase evolution path: “ultrasound-induced fragmentation, EC-driven aggregation, gravity-induced sedimentation”. The flow velocity and vorticity collaboratively regulate the particle migration paths, collision efficiency, and floc stability, while Zeta potential exhibits a control effect on particle aggregation rate. These mechanisms complement each other temporally to enhance the overall treatment performance.Keywords: Ultrasound-assisted electrocoagulation (US-EC), Suspended sediment particles, Particle image velocimetry (PIV), Flow field characteristics, Zeta potential

Introduction

Suspended sediment particles are widely present in rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and industrial wastewater (e.g., mining drainage and construction slurry wastewater) [[1], [2], [3]], acting as important pollutants in water treatment. These particles typically carry negative charges, and the electrostatic repulsion between charges forms an energy barrier that hinders particle aggregation [4]. Currently, flocculants are commonly used in water treatment to promote the flocculation and settlement of suspended matters by utilizing mechanisms like bridging adsorption [5], net trapping [6], and charge neutralization [7] between flocculants and sediment particles. Additionally, electrocoagulation (EC) treatment can induce the anode to release metal ions by imposing a current. These ions combine with hydroxide ions to form hydroxide flocculants that adsorb suspended matters and promote flocculation [8]. The gas flotation effect at the anode and cathode [9] is conducive to the aggregation of low-density particles at the solution surface, thereby achieving solid–liquid separation. Unfortunately, the application scope of EC is subjected to various limitations due to issues of electrode passivation and energy consumption [10]. Therefore, exploring the effects of multi-field coupling on the flocculation process represents an important research direction for expanding and promoting the application of EC.

In recent years, the ultrasound-assisted EC (US-EC) technology has been widely applied in wastewater treatment, demonstrating effective results in removing a range of different pollutants. Emerick et al. [11] studied the combined application of ultrasound and EC for treating pork slaughterhouse wastewater (experimental conditions: aluminum and aluminum/iron electrodes at 5 A, 6 V for 60 min); when using aluminum electrodes, the removal rates reached 97 % for color, 97.4 % for turbidity, 93 % for BOD, and 90 % for oils/greases. Gündüz et al. [12] investigated the efficiency of US-EC in treating dye wastewater under an ultrasound frequency of 40 kHz and a current density of 25 mA/cm2; it was reported that a dye removal rate of 95 % was reached in just 30 min, demonstrating significantly increased treatment efficiency and reaction rate. This result showcased the strong capability of US-EC in treating high-concentration wastewater in short durations. Nassar et al. [13] applied US-EC for oil and grease (O&G) removal from restaurant wastewater; under optimal conditions (current density 53.2 A/m2, electrode spacing 2.4 cm, electrolysis time 30.5 min), the O&G and COD removal rates reached 95.4 % and 75.9 %, respectively. Meanwhile, Response Surface Methodology (RSM) and Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) analyses indicated that ultrasound significantly reduced the formation of passivation layers on electrode surfaces, thereby effectively improving the electrode reaction rate and treatment efficacy. Halkijevic et al. [14] explored the combination of ultrasound and EC for pesticide wastewater treatment; under the conditions of aluminum and iron electrodes at 20 A for 20 min, the imidacloprid removal rate reached 100 %, highlighting the superior efficiency of US-EC for treating high-concentration pesticide wastewater.

The effects of US-EC in suspended sediment flocculation mainly manifest in three aspects. Firstly, the intense shock waves generated by ultrasonic cavitation shatter large pollutant particles into smaller ones. This process increases the specific surface area of particles, and promotes their contact with flocculants, thereby improving the removal efficiency [15]. Secondly, the highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (∙OH) and hydrogen radicals (∙H) generated during cavitation possess potent oxidizing capabilities. They can disrupt chemical bonds in organic pollutants and decompose them into smaller molecular intermediates, potentially leading to mineralization under certain conditions [16]. Finally, ultrasound can induce the formation and collapse of micro-bubbles in the liquid through cavitation, causing extreme localized pressure fluctuations and instantaneous high-energy release that generates intense shock waves and rapid temperature spikes. These phenomena effectively disrupt the passivation layers on electrode surfaces to enhance electrode activity, intensify liquid turbulence [17], and consequently accelerate reaction rates between pollutants and flocculants [18,19].

It is noteworthy that the existing research predominantly focused on the macro-scale performance of US-EC technology in terms of pollutant removal efficiency and energy consumption optimization, while studies on the dynamic evolution of flow field and particle size regulation mechanisms at the micro-scale remain scarce. Such a research context limits understanding about the flocculation process and its regulation mechanisms under acoustic-electric coupling.

In summary, this paper aimed to address two key issues: 1) The evolution characteristics of flow field and its differences under varying acoustic-electric parameter combinations; 2) How acoustic-electric coupling regulates the particle size evolution in suspended sediment. With a particular focus on suspended sediment, this study employed the Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV) to visually observe the evolution of flow field under the synergistic action of US-EC. Through comparative analysis of flow field characteristics, sediment particle size, and Zeta potential under the acoustic field alone, electric field alone, and coupling conditions, the pivotal role of US-EC in sediment particle size regulation and flocculation mechanisms was revealed. Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on the pollutant removal efficiency from a macroscopic perspective, this study systematically visualized and quantitatively analyzed the evolution of flow field and particle size regulation during suspended sediment flocculation at the microscopic level. Then, combining with PIV flow field observations, particle size distribution, and Zeta potential analysis, a novel methodological framework was proposed, providing strong support for elucidating the synergistic mechanisms of US-EC and advancing the in-depth development of this technology in terms of mechanistic research and optimized applications.

Materials and methods

Experimental platform

The experimental platform comprises an acoustic-electric intervention system, a measurement system for monitoring key parameters (PIV, particle size, Zeta potential), and a flocculation system for flocculation tests. More specifically, the PIV system consists of a high-speed camera (2560 × 1920@2000 fps), a laser source (30 mJ@1 kHz), a synchronization controller, and the related software. The laser emits vertically from 100 cm above the flocculation tank, while the high-speed camera is positioned frontally to capture front-view images under externally synchronized exposure (10 A/200 fps). The particle size is measured using an LISST-200X analyzer (measurement range 1–500 μm, 36 particle size grades, volume concentration resolution 0.1 μL/L, temporal resolution 1 s), which can provide real-time volume concentration and average particle size data for different particle sizes. The Zeta potential of the particle surface is determined using a Nano9200SZ Zeta potential analyzer (Haixinrui Technology, Beijing) at 25°C with ± 0.1 mV precision. The flocculation tank is dimensioned 220 mm × 220 mm × 140 mm. The acoustic-electric intervention system consists of an electric field intervention device and an ultrasonic intervention device. The former is equipped with aluminum electrode plates (165 mm × 180 mm × 4 mm) with 115 mm spacing, powered by an adjustable DC supply (0–30 A, 0–30 V; 0.01 A/V resolution). For ultrasonic intervention, ultrasonic transducers are evenly distributed on both sides of the flocculation tank, with 5 transducers arranged on each side (total ultrasonic power 600 W; calorimetric power density 27.46 W/L; operating at 28 kHz, 42 kHz, 53 kHz, or 77 kHz) (Fig. 1).

Experimental conditions and procedure

All sediment samples used were taken from typical river sections in the upper reaches of the Yellow River, and were cleaned and dried properly for later use. The test solution was prepared by mixing 6.776 L of purified water with 2 g of NaCl, and 0.1 g of sediment. The experimental conditions cover three groups: under electric field alone, under acoustic field alone, and under acoustic-electric coupling. For tests under electric intervention alone, the current densities of 10 A/m2, 20 A/m2, 30 A/m2, and 40 A/m2 were considered, covering low, medium, and high ranges, in order to investigate the effect of electric field intensity on the sediment flocculation process. For tests under acoustic intervention alone, the ultrasonic frequencies of 28 kHz, 42 kHz, 53 kHz, and 77 kHz were considered, in order to investigate the effect of ultrasonic cavitation on flow field disturbances under different frequencies. For tests under acoustic-electric coupling, the four current densities and four ultrasonic frequencies mentioned above were combined to form a total of 16 operation conditions.

During US-EC treatment, the prepared test solution was first placed onto the observation platform. Prior to testing, the laser pulse frequency was set to 200 Hz with dual-beam intensity at 10 A. The light guide arm was adjusted properly to ensure the laser irradiation plane being parallel to the camera observation plane, and the focal distance was adjusted to focus the camera view on the laser irradiation plane. The test solution was stirred evenly and settled for 60 s before initiating intervention. After setting the parameters to predetermined values according to each operating condition, the intervention devices and the camera were powered on simultaneously, allowing the camera to synchronously record the particle motion images. Upon completion of each test, particle images were exported by extracting every other frame (time interval: 5000 μs between consecutive images) for further analyses.

To minimize the interference of experimental devices on flow field observations, a sampling measurement method was applied to analyze the particle size evolution and Zeta potential. Specifically, an electric pipette was used to take samples from the middle part of the flocculation tank every 300 s, and the particle size and Zeta potential were measured within 60 s to ensure the timeliness and accuracy of the data. Under each operating condition, the particle size distribution was characterized by the time-averaged volume concentration of each particle size level, while the Zeta potential was taken as the mean of three repeated measurements. Additionally, each operation condition was tested for three times to acquire the mean values for analyses in order to improve the reliability of the experimental results. A full test circle lasted for 40 min: During the first 30 min, the electric and/or ultrasonic fields were applied continuously, while for the remaining 10 min, all interventions were stopped, allowing the solution to settle naturally, in order to observe the settling behavior of particles. All tests were conducted at room temperature (20 °C).

The Zeta potential analyzer was calibrated first using standard solution (−40 mV ± 6 mV) at 25 °C before use. The calibration process included three measurements, with an average potential value of −40.5 mV, indicating a standard deviation of 0.471 mV. The measurement precision of the instrument is ±0.1 mV, meeting the experimental requirements.

The LISST-200X particle size analyzer was calibrated using purified water. By analyzing the detector signals and comparing the measurement results (blue bars) with the factory background data (red solid line) and current background data (blue dashed line), it was found that the measurement results were basically consistent with both the factory background data and current background data in each detector channel (as shown in Fig. 2). This high agreement indicates a normal operating status, confirming that the background signals were accurate and within the expected range.

Image processing

The particle motion images collected were divided into refined windows, and the average displacement Δx of particles within each window was obtained by performing cross-correlation operations at the same position in the two images. Then, based on the scale calibration coefficient (0.048 mm/pixel) and the known time interval Δt, the velocity u was calculated by: u = Δx/Δt, which refers to the velocity of the fluid micro-cluster in each window. Results and analysis

Flow field velocity evolution under different physical fields

Fig. 3 presents the spatial distribution characteristics of flow velocity at 900 s under 8 different operating conditions. Under the action of electric field alone, bubbles generated from the cathode and anode surfaces rose along the direction of the electrode plate, and tended to aggregate toward the center of the flow field upon contacting the liquid surface and collide, driving the fluid to form a circulating flow with a relatively orderly streamline distribution [21]. As the current density increased from 10 A/m2 to 40 A/m2, the bubble generation rate gradually accelerated and the range of circulating flow expanded, forming more complex multi-vortex structures at the center area of the flow field. Under the action of acoustic field alone, the flow distribution exhibited an irregular pattern, with no dominant flow direction and drastic changes in the local velocity vector. Due to the strong cavitation effect of low-frequency ultrasound (28 kHz), the high-velocity zone grew larger.

Velocity contours at 900 s under 8 typical operating conditions.

Under acoustic-electric coupling, the flow characteristics demonstrated a significant effect of synergistic enhancement. Compared to the action of a single field, the extreme value of flow velocity and the distribution range of high-value zone were both markedly increased. Current density manifested a critical regulation role, that is, once the current density exceeded 20 A/m2, the acoustic-electric coupling effect would lead to a substantial increase in the extreme value of flow velocity. Especially, under the operating condition of high current density (40 A/m2) coupled with high ultrasonic frequency (77 kHz), the significantly increased flow velocity amplitude and high-flow-velocity zone confirm the synergistic enhancement effect of acoustic-electric coupling on improving the macro-flow intensity of the fluid system. The flow velocity evolution processes under other operating conditions and at different time sequences are detailed in the supplementary figures S1–S3. As can be seen, flow field velocity is a key parameter characterizing the macro-flow behavior of a fluid system, and its spatiotemporal evolution effectively reflects the regulatory effect of external physical fields on the internal flow state. Under multi-field coupling conditions, variations in flow velocity distribution can not only reveal the differential characteristics of flow intensity and stability but also provide an important basis for subsequent analyses of the mass transfer behavior and local micro-flow field structures.

Fig. 4 presents the time-averaged velocity heatmap of the flow field under different combinations of ultrasonic frequency and current density. The first row in the figure corresponds to the results obtained under the acoustic field alone, while the last column corresponds to the results obtained under the electric field alone. Specifically, under the electric field alone, the time-averaged flow velocity increased monotonically from 1.61 mm/s to 7.32 mm/s as the current density rose from 10 A/m2 to 40 A/m2. On the contrary, under the acoustic field alone, the time-averaged velocity decreased monotonically from 4.57 mm/s to 1.97 mm/s as the ultrasonic frequency rose from 28 kHz to 77 kHz.

Heatmaps of time-averaged flow velocity under varying operating conditions (first row: under acoustic field alone; last column: under electric field alone).

Under the coupling action, the time-averaged velocities corresponding to all parameter combinations were higher than those under a single physical field, demonstrating a clear synergistic enhancement effect. This is particularly prominent under the combination of high ultrasonic frequency (77 kHz) and high current density (40 A/m2), where the flow velocity increased to 27.67 mm/s, significantly higher than that under the acoustic field alone (1.97 mm/s) or electric field alone (7.32 mm/s). The results highlight that acoustic-electric coupling has great advantages in enhancing flow velocity. When the ultrasonic frequency remained constant, the flow velocity increased continuously with the increase of current density, exhibiting a stable enhancement effect. When the current density remained constant, at 30 A/m2 and 40 A/m2 conditions, the flow velocity increased monotonically with the increase of ultrasonic frequency. However, at 10 A/m2 and 20 A/m2 conditions, the flow velocity showed a decreasing trend at the beginning and then began to increase with ultrasonic frequency, manifesting overall small fluctuations.

Flow field vorticity evolution under varying physical fields

Fig. 5 presents the spatial distribution characteristics of vorticity at 900 s under 8 typical operating conditions, with the vortex intensity represented by the color scale transitioning from blue to red, where red denotes a stronger vortex intensity. In the figure, Omega is a vorticity indicator, which measures the local rotational intensity, with Omega = 0.52 serving as the criterion for vortex boundaries [22]. Under the electric field alone, low current density (10 A/m2) tended to form sparse but large vortices, while high current density (40 A/m2) tended to form dense and refined vortices, indicating that electrolysis-generated bubbles can enhance the local shearing effect. Under the acoustic field alone, variations in ultrasonic frequency had no significant impact on the size or distribution of vortex structure.

Vorticity distributions at 900 s under varying operating conditions.

The acoustic-electric coupling action significantly altered vorticity distribution characteristics, manifesting as synchronized reductions in vortex size and intensity, a notable increase in vortex count, and a more fine-grained and uniform distribution. The increase in current density and ultrasonic frequency both contributed to a reduced vortex scale and an increased density of small-scale vortices, which promoted the flow field to exhibit a higher local activity and a more uniform disturbance distribution. Especially, under the operating condition of 77 kHz + 40 A/m2, the vortex structure showed the most refined and densest pattern. Under the coupling action, external excitation not only affected flow velocity distribution but also resulted in the formation and evolution of vortex structures, thereby imposing a significant regulatory effect on the flow stability and mixing efficiency of the fluid system. The evolution processes of vorticity under other operating conditions and time sequences are detailed in the supplementary figures S4–S6.

Fig. 6 presents the normalized heatmaps of vortex count, average vortex area, and maximum vortex area. The results indicate that under all conditions, the vortex count and average vortex area exhibited a significant negative correlation. Under acoustic-electric coupling, current density is the primary factor regulating vortex count. When the ultrasonic frequency remained constant at 28 kHz, as the current density increased from 10 A/m2 to 40 A/m2, the vortex count was increased by 6.69 times (from 0.07452 to 0.57367) while the average vortex area was reduced by 57.46 % (from 0.93693 to 0.39858). High current densities significantly promoted the generation of small-scale vortices by intensifying electrolytic disturbances. Ultrasonic frequency showed a remarked impact on vortex scale. When the current density remained constant at 40 A/m2, as the ultrasonic frequency increased from 28 kHz to 77 kHz, the maximum vortex area was reduced by 66.11 % (from 0.15158 to 0.05137). High-frequency ultrasound significantly enhanced the generation efficiency of small-scale vortices but weakened the ability to maintain large-scale vortices. Although the operating condition of 77 kHz + 40 A/m2 achieved the maximum vortex count, the fragmented vortex structures resulted in an excessive shear force, which is detrimental to the floc stability. In comparison, the operating condition of 28 kHz + 40 A/m2 maintained larger vortex scales and moderate turbulence intensity, ensuring effective particle collision while avoiding an excessive shear force to the flocs.

Particle size evolution under varying physical fields

Current density is the dominant factor controlling particle size distribution (PSD) and exerts a significant effect on the particle aggregation process. The synergistic effect of low-frequency ultrasound (28 kHz) and electric field can markedly enhance the flocculation efficiency of sediment particles, leading to a rightward shift and increase in peak particle size while substantially broadening the width of the particle size spectra. Fig. 7 illustrates the temporal PSD evolution characteristics corresponding to 8 typical operating conditions. Under the acoustic field alone, the PSD curves corresponding to varying operating conditions showed a gentle evolution process; especially, during high-frequency (77 kHz) ultrasound treatment, the PSD curve remained nearly constant, demonstrating strong particle stability and suppressed particle aggregation [23].

PSD curves under varying operating conditions.

In comparison, under the electric field alone‌, with the increase of current density, the amounts of flocculants generated during the EC process significantly increased, and the particles quickly aggregated, evidenced by right-shifted PSD curves, rapid increase of peak particle size, and widened particle size spectra. Under the US-EC coupling intervention, the control over particle size was significantly enhanced, reflecting a positive synergistic effect between ultrasound and EC. Notably, the optimal flocculation performance was achieved at the operating condition of 28 kHz + 40 A/m2. Under this optimal condition, the particle size increased rapidly over time, and the PSD curve shifted significantly to the right within 15–20 min, indicating a faster floc formation rate; subsequently, during 20–30 min, the curve gradually narrowed, with the peak particle size remaining stably at about 101 μm, indicating sufficient particle aggregation and stable floc structures. Moreover, analysis of settlement characteristics showed that large flocs had basically settled after the first 5 min, and the particle concentration significantly decreased after 10 min, indicating that the formed flocs were not only large in size but also had excellent settling performance. The particle size evolution processes under other operating conditions and different time sequences are detailed in the supplementary figures S7–S9.

The aggregation process of particles driven by physical fields is cooperatively regulated by multiple factors, exhibiting distinct frequency and current density dependencies. Fig. 8 comprehensively compares the evolution characteristics of average particle size, total particle concentration, and Zeta potential under intervention by different physical fields. Under the acoustic field alone, ultrasonic frequency had a significant impact on the average particle size and total particle concentration, while its impact on Zeta potential was minor. Additionally, low ultrasonic frequency (28 kHz), due to its intense cavitation strength, could effectively break down the sediment particles, reducing the average particle size from 53.24 μm to 27.36 μm within 15 min. This size reduction increased the specific surface area, leading to an elevation in total particle concentration from 15.56 ppm to 29.23 ppm. Thereafter, as ultrasound treatment continued, the acoustic streaming disturbances were intensified, which in turn promoted particle collisions and reaggregation, causing the average particle size to rise to 51.61 μm. In contrast, under a high-frequency condition (77 kHz), with weakened cavitation effects, the particle fragmentation phenomenon was limited, demonstrating a variation range in average particle size of only 7.4 % throughout the entire process. More specifically, the particles primarily underwent localized oscillations around ultrasonic pressure nodes, suppressing their aggregation behavior while allowing the system to maintain a stable, dispersed state [24].

Particle size characteristics under varying operating conditions. (a) Average particle size; (b) Total particle concentration; (c) Zeta potential.

Under the electric field alone, the magnitude of current density critically affected the surface charge states and flocculation process of particles. During the electrolysis process, the absolute value of Zeta potential continuously decreased toward 0 mV, indicating that the cations released by electrolysis effectively neutralized the particle surface charges to reduce the stability of the liquid system. Under a high current density (40 A/m2), Zeta potential rapidly increased from −12.33 mV to 1.57 mV within 5 min, while under a low current density (10 A/m2), charge neutralization slowed down significantly, with Zeta potential reaching only −9.62 mV at 5 min and −1.35 mV at 20 min, indicating a suppressed particle aggregation rate. Moreover, the average particle size and total particle concentration showed increasing trends with the increase of current density, accompanied by a high particle settling rate under a high current density.

Compared to single-field intervention, acoustic-electrical coupling treatment significantly accelerated the processes of particle size evolution, concentration accumulation, and charge neutralization, demonstrating a notable synergistic enhancement effect. Under the operating condition of 28 kHz + 40 A/m2, influenced by the strong cavitation effect of low-frequency ultrasound, the average particle size rapidly decreased to 18.89 μm within 15 min, reaching the minimum value of all operating conditions. Further, under the action of EC, the average particle size significantly increased to 60.98 μm and the total particle concentration climbed to 816.23 ppm at 30 min, both reaching the maximum values of all operating conditions. In the subsequent settling phase, the system showed extremely high settling efficiency, with the total particle concentration decreasing from 1084.54ppm after settling for 5min to 130.55 ppm after settling for 10 min (a sedimentation rate of 87.96%), completing a continuous process of “intensive fragmentation – accelerated aggregation – efficient sedimentation.” Under the operating condition of 77 kHz + 10 A/m2, the Zeta potential remained at −11.21 mV by 10 min, without showing significant fluctuations in the average particle size or total particle concentration. This suggests that the particle flocculation process had not yet effectively started. Such phenomenon may stem from enhanced pressure node effects under a high ultrasonic frequency: the particles mainly undergo localized oscillations around pressure nodes with restricted mobility, making them difficult to contact with cations released by the electric field; thus, the fluid system maintains a stable, dispersed state, suppressing the occurrence of particle aggregation [24,25].

Discussions

Synergistic analysis of acoustic-electric coupling

During the US-EC process, the coupling effects between acoustic and electric fields can significantly improve the momentum transfer efficiency and turbulence intensity of the fluid system, forming multi-scale flow structures conducive to particle collision and aggregation. The enhanced flow field disturbances provide a macroscopic dynamic basis, while intensified vorticity can optimize the particle motion path and local collision efficiency at the microscale, thereby facilitating an efficient flocculation process. However, when the turbulence intensity is too high, the strong turbulent shear force may destroy the formed large flocs, leading to their fragmentation and particle redispersion. Therefore, reasonable regulation of turbulence intensity is crucial for promoting particle aggregation and maintaining floc stability. During the flocculation process, excessively strong turbulences should be avoided as much as possible to achieve a balance between particle aggregation and floc stability.

Under the acoustic field alone, low-frequency ultrasound provides sufficient time for the expansion and contraction of cavitation bubbles due to a long wavelength and slow oscillation period of the sound waves. Therefore, the bubbles formed are larger in size, able to release shock waves with a higher intensity during collapse, accompanied by strong micro jets that trigger localized high-rate flows. Accordingly, the shear force generated can cause particle fragmentation, reduce the average particle size, and promote the initial dispersion of particles [26,27]. In contrast, the cavitation bubbles generated by high-frequency ultrasonic waves are smaller, so that the shock waves released during bubble collapse have a lower intensity, resulting in a weaker fragmentation effect. Nevertheless, high-frequency acoustic field can generate more cavitation bubbles with higher oscillation frequencies, which can form high-density, small-scale disturbances in the flow field [28]. In a high-frequency acoustic field‌, particles tend to oscillate around the pressure nodes due to the ultrasonic nodal effect, impeding significant particle aggregation within short durations. Sepcifically, oscillate occurs around pressure wave nodes, thereby suppressing significant aggregation within a short period of time. In addition, the ultrasonic power dissipation effect further elucidates the frequency dependence of ultrasonic action. This effect refers to the irreversible conversion of input acoustic energy into heat through viscous friction and cavitation processes. In this study, the ultrasonic power density was 88.5 W L−1, while the calorimetrically measured power densities at different frequencies were 32.07 W L−1 (28 kHz), 28.77 W L−1 (42 kHz), 22.44 W L−1 (53 kHz), and 26.56 W L−1 (77 kHz), with an overall average of 27.46 W L−1. Among these frequencies, the lowest power dissipation occurred at 28 kHz, indicating that a greater proportion of the input energy was effectively utilized for cavitation and microstreaming, thereby enhancing particle breakage and dispersion. This observation is consistent with the characteristics of low-frequency ultrasound, which generates larger cavitation bubbles and stronger collapse-induced shock waves. In contrast, as the frequency increases, energy dissipation becomes more pronounced, leading to reduced cavitation intensity and consequently diminished particle breakage efficiency. Nevertheless, higher-frequency acoustic fields produce a greater number of smaller cavitation bubbles, which induce more uniform and high-density microscale disturbances within the flow field.

Electric field can generate large amounts of bubbles through electrolytic reaction, which drive the local transport of surrounding particles during their ascent, collision, and reflux. Concurrently, the anodically-produced flocculants adsorb suspended particles to form aggregates, which tend to settle when reaching a critical particle size. Under low current density conditions, the bubble generation rate is reduced, and the bubble-induced disturbances to the flow field become gentle, yielding a low flocculant production with weak adsorption performance. Under high current density conditions, the rapid generation and rupture of bubbles significantly increase the turbulence dissipation rate, promoting frequent contact between flocculants and particles; this accelerates the particle aggregation and sedimentation processes, manifested as a rapid growth followed by a decline in particle size [29,30].

To further clarify the relationship between flow disturbance and floc evolution, the Kolmogorov vortex scale to particle size ratio (η/D) and turbulence dissipation rate (ε) were selected as quantitative indicators (Fig. 9). The acoustic–electric coupling effect significantly modifies the matching characteristics among η, D, and ε through the synergy of hydrodynamic disturbances (cavitation, bubble dynamics, energy cascade) and interfacial electrical regulation (electro-neutralization), and the experimental data combined with flow field reconstruction indicate that the behaviors can be classified into three empirical regions:

Variations of the Kolmogorov ratio η/D and turbulent energy dissipation rate ε under varying operating conditions (the X-axis positions “1–24” correspond to the following operating conditions in sequence: 10 A/m2, 20 A/m2, 30 A/m2, 40 A/m2, 28 kHz, 42 kHz, 53 kHz, 77 kHz, 28 kHz + 10 A/m2, 28 kHz + 20 A/m2, 28 kHz + 30 A/m2, 28 kHz + 40 A/m2, 42 kHz + 10 A/m2, 42 kHz + 20 A/m2, 42 kHz + 30 A/m2, 42 kHz + 40 A/m2, 53 kHz + 10 A/m2, 53 kHz + 20 A/m2, 53 kHz + 30 A/m2, 53 kHz + 40 A/m2, 77 kHz + 10 A/m2, 77 kHz + 20 A/m2, 77 kHz + 30 A/m2, and 77 kHz + 40 A/m2).

(1) The under-disturbance region: η/D ≥ 25, ε ≲ 10−6 m2 s−3. In this region, the Kolmogorov vortex scale η is significantly larger than the average particle size D, indicating that the flow field energy is primarily distributed at scales much larger than the flocs, so that microscale shear cannot effectively transmit to the flocs. At this moment, the disturbance intensity is low, and the electrostatic repulsion barriers as well as the diffusion boundary layer effect still dominate, leading to a low effective collision frequency and limited adhesion efficiency. Typical conditions belonging to this region include: 10 A/m2 (electric field alone), 42 kHz (acoustic field alone), and 28 kHz + 10 A/m2. Under these conditions, floc growth was limited, accompanied by a poor sedimentation performance, highlighting that weak disturbances cannot overcome interfacial barriers between particles.

(2) The balanced region: 15 ≤ η/D < 25, 10−6 ≲ ε ≲ 10−4 m2 s−3. In this region, the relative scale difference between the Kolmogorov vortex scale η and the average particle size D is at a moderate level. Microscale disturbances in the flow field can effectively transmit to the flocs, promoting the collision and aggregation between particles. At this moment, the disturbance intensity is at a moderate level, which not only facilitates particle collision and adhesion but also helps avoid microscale fragmentation. The combined action of microscale shear force and electro-neutralization then improves the particle aggregation efficiency and stability. Typical conditions belonging to this region include: 30 and 40 A/m2 (electric field alone), 28 kHz (acoustic field alone), and acoustic field coupled with medium-to-high current densities. Specifically, the operating condition of 28 kHz + 40 A/m2 corresponds to η/D ≈ 17, located in the middle of the balanced region and showing optimal responses as follows: 1) The time-averaged velocity increased from 7.32 mm/s to 11.01 mm/s, while the vortex scale and count remained favorable for uniform mixing and local re-dispersion; 2) The particle size first decreased to 18.89 μm (due to short-term fragmentation caused by cavitation), then rapidly recovered to 60.98 μm and stabilized, with the particle concentration reaching 816.23 ppm; 3) The absolute value of Zeta potential significantly decreased and approached to 0 mV, where electro-neutralization and the short-range van der Waals force synergistically promoted a stable particle aggregation process. Overall, this operating condition achieved a dual balance of “moderate disturbance – stable particle aggregation.”

(3) The over-shear region: η/D < 15, ε ≳ 10−4 m2 s−3. In this region, the difference between the Kolmogorov vortex scale η and the average particle size D is further reduced. Microscale disturbances strongly act on the floc structure, leading to a significant local shear effect. At this moment, the disturbance intensity increases markedly, and the flow field is filled with large amounts of small-scale vortices, sharply increasing the particle collision frequency. However, the excessive shear force also causes a fragmentation effect, resulting in weakened floc stability. Meanwhile, the excessive shear force causes excessive local stresses on the surfaces of particles, triggering frequent fragmentation-reaggregation cycles, while effective aggregation activities are strongly inhibited. Typical conditions belonging to this region include the combinations of high-frequency acoustic field with high current densities, such as 77 kHz + 40 A/m2 (η/D = 5.14), where the time-averaged flow velocity was as high as 27.67 mm/s. Under this operating condition, the disturbance intensity was excessively large, so that even under a high current density, the flocs struggled to maintain continuous growth: specifically, the average particle size only decreased to 27.22 μm, then slowly recovered to 43.29 μm, failing to form stable large structures. Further comparisons showed that under the operating condition of 77 kHz + 10 A/m2 (η/D = 14.92), where the ultrasonic frequency was the same, the Zeta potential remained at −11.21 mV after 10 min, indicating that under low-current–density conditions, a high shear strength even hinders electro-neutralization at the particle surface, thereby weakening the flocculation effect. The over-shear region is typically characterized by: “reduced turbulence scale – enhanced local shear – fragmentation dominance.” Although the frequency of particle collisions was increased, excessive disturbances destroyed the aggregation structure and inhibited electro-neutralization, ultimately deteriorating the sedimentation performance.

Particle size regulation mechanisms under acoustic-electric coupling

Fig. 10 illustrates the distribution characteristics of sediment concentration differences for 36-grade particle sizes within 5-min time intervals. For instance, the bar corresponding to 5 min represents the concentration difference between 5 min and the initial time (0 min); the bar corresponding to 10 min represents the concentration difference between 10 min and 5 min, and so on so forth. Intervals with positive differences are displayed in orange color, indicating an increase in particle concentration for that size range. Intervals with negative differences are displayed in blue color, indicating a decrease in particle concentration. The increase in particle concentration may stem from the aggregation of smaller particles or the fragmentation of larger flocs into flocs of that size range. Conversely, the decrease in particle concentration may result from the adsorption and aggregation of particles of that size into larger structures or their conversion into smaller particles through fragmentation. It can be seen that aggregation, fragmentation, and reorganization continuously alternate during the US-EC process, with different mechanisms dominating at different stages.

Distribution of differences in sediment particle concentration in 5-min intervals.

Different ultrasonic frequencies exhibit significant variations in the particle size range of fragmentation. Low-frequency ultrasound (28 kHz) demonstrated the most pronounced fragmentation effect on medium-sized particles (61.2–195 μm). Under the current density of 10 A/m2 and 20 A/m2, the concentration of medium-sized particles decreased significantly within the first 5 min, while the concentration of small particles (1.21–61.2 μm) increased notably, indicating that low-frequency ultrasound can effectively fragment larger particles into smaller ones. As the reaction time extended, the fragmentation effect gradually accumulated, with the critical fragmentation size decreasing from 31.6 μm at 10 min to 22.7 μm at 15 min, reflecting a continuously strengthening trend of the fragmentation effect exerted by low-frequency ultrasound. In contrast, high-frequency ultrasound (e.g., 77 kHz) exhibited a significantly weakened fragmentation effect and a narrowed range of action. Under the operating condition of 77 kHz + 10 A/m2, the range of effective fragmentation within the first 5 min was concentrated in 51.9–140 μm, with the overall particle size distribution approaching to stabilization. This indicates that high-frequency ultrasound tends to maintain the original particle size structure and has a limited fragmentation effect on sediment particles. Overall, low-frequency ultrasound possesses a stronger fragmentation capability and a broader range of action, while high-frequency ultrasound requires the EC process to further optimize the particle size structure.

Current density has a significant regulatory effect on the relationship between fragmentation and aggregation by controlling the generation rate of coagulants. Under a low current density (10 A/m2), the coagulant generation rate remained at a low level, and the ultrasonic fragmentation effect dominated, with the fluid system exhibiting a continuous fragmentation trend and a notably delayed aggregation process. For instance, under the operating condition of 28 kHz + 10 A/m2, up to 20 min, the concentration of small particles (1.21–1.6 μm) just began to decline, while the concentrations of medium-sized and large particles (1.89–273 μm) gradually increased. This indicates that although small particles had begun to be adsorbed to form flocs, the structures remained unstable, and the system as a whole was still in the fragmentation-dominated stage. In contrast, under a high current density, the coagulant generation rate significantly increased, and the EC effect took precedence in system behavior, markedly enhancing the aggregation process. Under the operating condition of 28 kHz + 40 A/m2, after 10 min of treatment, the concentration of small particles (1.21–2.23 μm) rapidly decreased, while the concentration of medium-sized particles (2.63–31.6 μm) significantly increased. This indicates that high current density can quickly promote the adsorption of small particles to form stable medium-sized flocs, reflecting a significant enhancement in coagulation efficiency and improved particle size control capability. Additionally, high current density also indirectly affects the critical particle size range during ultrasonic fragmentation. Under the operating condition of 53 kHz + 10 A/m2, the particle size range subjected to fragmentation was 72.2–230 μm at 5 min, while under the operating condition of 53 kHz + 40 A/m2, the range narrowed to 61.2–140 μm. This suggests that the coagulants generated under higher current densities can interact with particles at the early stage of fragmentation, causing larger particles to aggregate into flocs prematurely, thereby inhibiting continuous fragmentation over a broader size range. In contrast, under a low current density, the coagulation effect tends to be delayed, and the fragmentation range becomes wider, making the system more prone to producing a large number of small particles.

Evolution mechanisms of suspended sediment flocculation under acoustic-electric coupling

Under the action of acoustic-electric coupling, the flocculation evolution process of suspended sediment particles exhibited distinct staging characteristics, which can be divided into an initial fragmentation-dominated stage, an intermediate floc formation stage, and a later sedimentation stage (Fig. 11). The dominant mechanisms in each stage are determined by the alternating or synergistic effects of ultrasonic cavitation and EC.

Mechanisms of US-EC for removing sediment particles.

In the initial stage (0–10 min), the strong shock waves and microjets generated by the ultrasonic cavitation effect acted on the suspended sediment particles, causing medium-sized particles to fragment into a large number of smaller particles. In this stage, the generation rate of small particles was significantly higher than their consumption rate in the flocculation process. Therefore, the system as a whole exhibited a “fragmentation-dominated” feature, with the particle distribution tending to become finer. Although stable flocs had not yet formed, ultrasonic fragmentation effectively increased the specific surface area and active sites of the particles, providing a prerequisite for the efficient aggregation in subsequent stages.

In the intermediate stage (10–30 min), the EC effect gradually strengthened and replaced ultrasonic fragmentation as the dominant mechanism. The coagulants produced by electrolysis weakened the electrostatic repulsion between particles through electrical neutralization and adsorption bridging, promoting the aggregation of small particles. Simultaneously, ultrasonic treatment improved the mixing and mass transfer conditions, allowing the coagulants to be distributed more uniformly and fully contact with the suspended sediment particles. In this stage, the fluid system showed a decreasing trend in the concentration of small particles and an increasing trend in the concentration of medium-sized and large flocs. Meanwhile, the particle structures gradually stabilized, with the mechanism of action shifting from “fragmentation-dominated” to “aggregation-dominated.” Overall, this stage represents a critical transition period under the coupling of acoustic and electric fields, which determines the formation rate and structural stability of the flocs.

In the later stage (30–40 min), the sedimentation speed significantly increased, mainly due to the continuous influence of the fragmentation and reorganization effects in previous stages. Following the action of the first two stages, the flocs gradually formed more compact structures as they grew continuously, thereby increasing their effective density. As the floc size increased, the fractal dimension showed a downward trend, reflecting the densification of the structure, which not only enhanced the sedimentation stability but also accelerated the sedimentation process [31]. Under turbulent conditions, the compact floc structures formed during early reconstruction were particularly prominent, especially in the small-to-medium size range, where the sedimentation speed was noticeably increased [32]. Over time, the concentrations of small and medium-sized particles in the fluid system gradually decreased, while the number of larger flocs gradually increased and stabilized, eventually allowing the sedimentation process to reach a steady state and achieve efficient removal of suspended solids.

Conclusions

In this study, a US-EC experimental platform was established to systematically investigate the flow field characteristics and sediment flocculation behavior under varying ultrasonic frequencies and current densities by integrating PIV, Zeta potential measurements, and PSD analysis. Then, the mechanisms of particle fragmentation, aggregation, and sedimentation under the coupling of acoustic-electrical fields were elucidated. Specifically, the key conclusions are as follows:

(1)Low-frequency ultrasound (28 kHz) can significantly reduce particle size through strong cavitation, thereby enhancing the particle collision efficiency, while high-frequency ultrasound (77 kHz) primarily induces small-scale disturbances that maintain a disperse particle distribution, which is unfavorable for floc formation.

Low-frequency ultrasound (28 kHz) can significantly reduce particle size through strong cavitation, thereby enhancing the particle collision efficiency, while high-frequency ultrasound (77 kHz) primarily induces small-scale disturbances that maintain a disperse particle distribution, which is unfavorable for floc formation.

(2)Current density promotes the coagulant adsorption rate by controlling coagulant generation and accelerating the release of metal cations. Meanwhile, metal cations neutralize particle surface charges to reduce the absolute value of Zeta potential and accelerate particle aggregation.

(3)Acoustic-electric coupling demonstrates an optimal synergistic effect under the operating condition of 28 kHz + 40 A/m2. Under this condition, moderate flow velocity and vorticity distribution could effectively promote particle migration and collision; meanwhile, the turbulent scale and average particle size achieved a balanced state to avoid excessive shear damage to the formed flocs. Thus, the final performance was significantly better than that under EC treatment along.

Thus, the final performance was significantly better than that under EC treatment along.

(4)The US-EC process can be summarized as a three-stage mechanism of “ultrasound-induced fragmentation, EC-driven aggregation, gravity-induced sedimentation,” where different physical fields play their respective roles at various stages, ultimately promoting efficient particle flocculation and settlement.

(5)In practical engineering, factors such as hydraulic boundaries, electrode configurations, and acoustic attenuation may cause scale effects that impact flow, electric fields, and particle behavior, thereby affecting mass transfer and floc stability in US-EC. Future work should combine multi-scale experiments and simulations to map key parameters and optimize US-EC for treating highly polluted waters.
