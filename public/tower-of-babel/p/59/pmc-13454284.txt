Physical function impacts hearing without mediation from systolic blood pressure
Authors: Jean Neils-Strunjas, Meisam Arjmandi, Morteza Hajihosseini, Nicholas Riccardi, Cara Vandergriff, Mike Leonard, Sarah Newman-Norlund, Samaneh Nemati, Samin Ashjaei, Laura Droege, Nadra Salman, Lorie Donelle, Kesheng Wang, Daniela B Friedman, Leonardo Bonilha, Julius Fridriksson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe common cause theory of age-related hearing loss and age-related disorders of cognition suggests that there are shared underlying physiological variables that may be modified with intervention. This study examined the interrelationships among physical function (PF), systolic blood pressure (SBP), hearing, and cognition in 150 adults aged 50–80 years old, utilizing a Bayesian generalized structural equation modeling approach. The findings suggest that every 10-point increase in PF results in a decrease of hearing threshold by 1.35 dB HL (95% CrI: -0.232, -0.038) independent of SBP, while every 10-year increase in age increases SBP by 7.5 mm Hg (95% CrI: 0.417, 1.098) and increases hearing threshold by 8.78 dB HL (95% CrI: 0.608, 1.152). This indicates a more active lifestyle is associated with better hearing and that while SBP increases with age, it does not mediate the relationship between PF and hearing. Sex differences were observed in hearing outcomes, with males exhibiting poorer hearing. Additionally, there was only a minimal effect of PF on cognition, and this modest improvement was not explained by systolic blood pressure in our analytical sample. These results underscore the complexity of the pathways linking cardiovascular health, sensory function, and cognitive aging. Introduction

Aging is associated with decline in physical1,2, sensory3–5, and cognitive functions6,7, with cardiovascular health posited as the common underlying factor4,8–11. The “common cause” theory suggests that age-related changes in cardiovascular health may simultaneously influence hearing and cognitive abilities12–19. Furthermore, sensory deficits may be prodromal for cognitive deficits with decline in hearing as one of the earliest potential markers4,20. However, evidence on the mechanisms underlying these associations—particularly the role of physical function (PF) and its relationship to systolic blood pressure, hearing, and cognition—remains limited21–25. A secondary analysis of a randomized trial testing the effect of hearing intervention vs. health education control in older adults aged 70–84 years with untreated mild-to-moderate hearing loss and without substantial cognitive impairment, found no efficacy signal for a benefit of treatment on performance measures of physical function. Hence, improving hearing function did not improve physical function, allowing for the possibility that physical function impacts hearing26.

PF is the ability of an individual to perform physical activities; one method of assessment in older adults is self-reported abilities27,28. PF is positively associated with physical activity, particularly with measures that assess the ability to carry out moderate to intensive exercise29,30. Moreover, older adults show improvement in PF following an exercise program31–33 suggesting that PF is a clinically meaningful, potentially modifiable risk factor in aging.

Previous studies have shown that physical activity can reduce the risk of cognitive impairment and dementia22,34 and that hypertension is a major risk factor for cognitive decline in older adults35. The Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial (SPRINT) was a large clinical trial to determine the benefit of a systolic blood-pressure target of less than 120 mm Hg (intensive treatment) compared to a systolic blood-pressure target of less than 140 mm Hg (standard treatment) in 50 + year olds36. Among patients who were at increased cardiovascular risk, targeting a systolic blood pressure of less than 120 mm Hg resulted in lower rates of major adverse cardiovascular events and lower all-cause mortality than targeting a systolic blood pressure of less than 140 mm Hg, both during the randomly assigned therapy and at follow-up36. The SPRINT-MIND, a sub‐study of SPRINT evaluated cognitive outcomes and found that intensive blood pressure (BP) control reduced risk of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) by 19% and reduced the risk of developing either MCI or probable dementia (PD) by 15%. There was also a statistically non‐significant reduction of 17% in PD alone37. Other studies have supported the SPRINT-MIND findings and reported that lowering blood pressure with physical activity in middle-aged and older adults has a beneficial effect on cognition38,39. A meta-analysis suggested that moderate to vigorous physical activity implemented for 12 weeks or longer had the greatest impact on BP management38. Together these findings highlight the potential of physical activity and blood pressure management as modifiable intervention targets for reducing risk of cognitive decline and dementia.

High blood pressure, physical activity, and hearing loss have been implicated as risk factors for cognitive decline in aging40–47 with PF being closely related to physical activity. In this study, we aim to examine a model in which PF influences hearing and cognition directly or indirectly via systolic blood pressure, while controlling for age and sex. We hypothesized that higher PF is associated with lower systolic blood pressure, which in turn may lead to better cognitive performance on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and better hearing sensitivity, as indicated by lower pure-tone audiometry thresholds (PTT) (see Fig. 1). Identifying a relationship among these variables in individuals aged 50 years and older would be highly valuable, as aging is inevitable, but PF and blood pressure may represent modifiable risk factors for hearing loss and cognitive impairment. While other research has examined these factors, we know of no other study that has tested a Bayesian generalized structural equation model (GSEM).

Methods

Participants and design

A cross-sectional sample of 150 community-dwelling adults aged 50 to 80 years old were recruited from the University of South Carolina (USC) and surrounding community as part of the Aging Brain Cohort (ABC) Study at USC (ABC@USC)48. The study protocol was approved by the University of South Carolina Institutional Review Board (Pro00089569), and all participants provided written informed consent. The ABC@USC research center focuses on studies on adults with neurologically intact aging. IRB approval was obtained on August 21, 2019. Data collection began in September 2019 to present day with a hiatus from April 2020-June 2021 due to COVID-19 safety protocols. Prior to enrollment, interested individuals complete a screening survey to determine their eligibility. Participants were eligible in the ABC@USC cohort if they were between 20 and 80 years of age, were willing and able to provide informed written consent in English, were MRI-compatible (i.e., no metal implants, not claustrophobic, able to lie supine for one hour, maximum girth < 60 inches, and BMI < 42). Individuals were excluded if they had acute or chronic medical conditions limiting study participation, severe current illnesses (e.g., cancer), or self-reported psychiatric diagnoses (e.g., schizophrenia or severe mood disorders) or dementia that would interfere with completion of study procedures. Demographic data was collected by completion of a questionnaire and clarification with interview. Cross-sectional data collection assessed participants at one time point with no intervention.

Blood pressure measurement

Brachial blood pressure was measured for each participant using an OMRON Digital Bronze Blood Pressure Monitor Model BP5150 by a trained study staff in a quiet testing suite. Participants’ legs were uncrossed, and the arm cuff was placed on the left upper arm (unless physical preference due to wound or injury). Participants were instructed to sit quietly with feet flat on the floor, their arm by their side resting on the arm rest of the chair while measurements were taken in sequence after a 5-minute rest period. Blood pressure was taken twice consecutively and the average SBP (mm Hg) was used in analyses.

Cognitive assessment

General cognitive function was measured using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) version 8.1, a widely validated screening tool for mild cognitive impairment49. The measure was administered by a certified examiner in a quiet room. Participants were not excluded in the current study based on MoCA score; however, scores ≥ 26 on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA; maximum = 30) are commonly interpreted as cognitively intact49. Analysis of the full 0–30 MoCA range captures subtle cognitive changes and retains statistical power, therefore, we treated MoCA as a continuous variable50.

Hearing assessment

To evaluate hearing sensitivity, participants underwent standard pure-tone audiometric screening following our established clinical protocols48. Air-conduction thresholds were measured bilaterally at octave and inter-octave frequencies ranging from 0.25 to 8 kHz, including 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 8 kHz. This frequency range captures both low and high-frequency hearing sensitivity and is commonly used to assess age-related changes in auditory function51,52. Consistent with typical patterns of age-related hearing loss, participants exhibited elevated thresholds at higher frequencies, reflecting the characteristic high-frequency sloping configuration of age-induced hearing impairment53–55. Consistent with our prior studies56,57, there were no statistically significant differences across ears. Although prior studies often use a pure-tone average (PTA) restricted to 0.5–2 kHz in the better-performing ear, this convention is clinically motivated and primarily reflects speech audibility, making it less sensitive to audiometric configuration differences central to age-related hearing loss55,58,59. Contemporary frameworks emphasize that age-related hearing loss is not uniform across frequencies and reflects multiple underlying mechanisms (i.e., sensory, metabolic, neural), many of which manifest prominently at higher frequencies55. Therefore, to avoid underestimating high-frequency contributions and obscuring variability, the mean pure-tone threshold across both ears was used. This mean threshold served as a representative index of each participant’s overall hearing sensitivity in subsequent analyses.

Physical function assessment

The RAND 36-Item Health Survey (Version 1.0), also referred to as the Medical Outcomes Study (MOS) 36-Item Short-Form Health Survey (SF-36)60 measures eight health concepts, one of which is physical functioning. The survey of physical functioning is based on a self-report of ability to engage in daily physical activities and was found to correlate with measures of physical performance such as gait speed28,61. The RAND 36-Item Survey has been translated into more than 55 languages and has been widely used in the United States, Europe, and much of Asia62,63. The instrument was explicitly designed for general adult populations across the lifespan with a sensitivity to detect changes with aging and potential decline64.

Scoring the RAND 36-Item Health Survey is a two-step process. First, pre-coded numeric values are recorded according to a scoring key. In addition, each item is scored on a 0 to 100 range so that the lowest and highest possible scores are 0 and 100, respectively. A high score indicates a more favorable health state. Scores represent the percentage of total possible scores achieved. In step 2, items from the same scale are averaged together to create eight scale scores.

Physical functioning includes ten items numbered 3–12. The items are about activities during a typical day and pose the question, does your health now limit you in these activities? If so, how much? Responses are ranked on a 3-point scale: “Yes, limited a lot,” “Yes, limited a little,” and “No, not limited at all.” Participants provide ranked responses to see whether they experience great limitations to no limitations on each of the ten items ranging from vigorous activities (e.g., running, strenuous sports), walking upstairs, and self-care functional activities (e.g., dressing). Items that are left blank (missing data) are not considered when calculating the scale scores. Hence, scale scores represent the average for all items in the scale that the respondent answered. Data from the Medical Outcomes Study (n = 2471) reported an Alpha of 0.93 indicating high reliability and internal consistency62 of this measurement tool.

Statistical analysis

Data from multiple assessment sheets were merged based on participant IDs, and only participants aged ≥ 50 years who had complete data for bilateral pure-tone hearing thresholds, blood-pressure readings, MoCA total score, and the RAND-36 were included in the analyses. (n = 150). Participant characteristics, such as age, sex, ethnicity, and education level, were summarized using means and standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. A Bayesian generalized structural equation model (GSEM) was employed to address the non-normal distribution of variables and the small sample size using the brms package in R version 4.4.065 and to examine the hypothesized pathways between PF, blood pressure, hearing loss, and cognition. Age and sex were included as covariates in all equations to control for potential confounding effects.

Non-recursive models were estimated on observed variables using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling with four chains with 4000 iterations each, 2000 warm-up iterations per chain, and default priors for all parameters in the brm function. Model convergence was assessed through visual inspection of trace plots for key parameters and posterior predictive checks for outcomes (hearing loss, blood pressure, and cognition). MCMC trace plots and posterior predictive checks are available in Supplemental Figure S1. The diagnostics exhibited satisfactory model convergence and adequate prediction of observed data patterns. Indirect effects were calculated as the product of path coefficients, and total effects were computed as the sum of direct and indirect effects. Bayesian credible intervals (95% CrI) were calculated for all effect estimates. Effects were considered significant if their 95% credible intervals excluded zero and showed consistent direction across posterior samples. We defined consistent positive direction across posterior samples as a positive probability of more than 0.95 and consistent negative direction across posterior samples as a positive probability of less than 0.05. A positive probability close to 0.5 or inclusion of zero in 95% credible intervals indicated no significant effect. Additionally, posterior distributions of path coefficients were graphed to show uncertainty in the parameters’ estimations.

Results

The analytic sample included a total of 150 participants, of which 148 were White non-Hispanic or Latino individuals (98.6%) and 111 were females (74.0%). Additionally, 84.0% (n = 126) reported at least a college-level education (College/University, Graduate School, or Post-Graduate Degree), with the remainder having a high school or primary school education. Thirty-eight (25.3%) participants reported taking one or more antihypertensive medications (i.e., blood-pressure-lowering drugs). As shown in Supplementary Table S1, the quarter of participants using agents were spread across several pharmacologic classes; this heterogeneity, combined with the small subgroup size, further reduced statistical power in modeling medication effects. Antihypertensive use is therefore presented descriptively but was not entered as a model covariate as it additionally introduced risks for multicollinearity with SBP (mm Hg). and statistical over-adjustment (e.g. measured SBP already embodies the net pharmacological effect antihypertensives). Mean values reported ± SD were as follows: age, 64.3 ± 7.2; systolic blood pressure (SBP), 131.4 ± 15.3 mm Hg; pure-tone threshold (PTT), 23.4 ± 13.2 dB HL; Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), 26.5 ± 2.5; and PF, 84.0 ± 19.4 (Table 1). All variables were entered into the model as continuous variables; however, it should be noted that average measures of SBP are consistent with Stage 1 hypertension66–68, 23.4 with normal hearing69, and 26.5 with normal cognition.

Physical function

PF was significantly associated with better hearing (β = -0.135; 95% CrI= [-0.232, -0.038]; positive probability = 0.004), indicating that higher levels of PF are linked to better hearing sensitivity in older adults. While the association between PF and SBP favor a very small negative effect, they indicate an 18.7% chance the effect is actually positive; since this is greater than our 5% cutoff defined in the methods section this result is considered non-significant (β = -0.054; CrI= [-0.176, 0.066]; positive probability = 0.187), similarly MoCA total score (β = 0.013; CrI = [-0.009, 0.035]; positive probability = 0.873) showed a strong chance of positive effect (Table 2). Contrary to our hypothetical model, indirect effects of PF on hearing and cognition via SBP were negligible and not statistically significant (Fig. 2), indicating that SBP does not serve as a meaningful mediator in the relationship between PF and either hearing or cognition in our proposed model.

Systolic Blood Pressure (SBP)

SBP was not significantly associated or consistently associated with either PTT (β=-0.018; CrI = [-0.149, 0.110]; positive probability = 0.393) or MoCA total score (β = 0.001; CrI = [-0.029, 0.031]; positive probability = 0.536), as shown in Table 2. These findings suggest that, within this sample of adults, SBP does not appear to be a meaningful predictor of hearing loss or cognitive performance.

Age and Sex

The results showed that age emerged as a strong and consistent predictor of both elevated SBP and hearing loss. Specifically, age was positively associated with SBP (β = 0.752; 95% CrI = [0.417, 1.098]; positive probability = 1.00) and hearing loss (β = 0.878; 95% CrI = [0.608, 1.152]; positive probability = 1.00), indicating that older adults in the 50 to 80-year age range are at higher risk for increased SBP and greater hearing loss. Additionally, age showed a marginal but consistent negative association with cognitive performance (β = -0.057; 95% CrI = [-0.122, 0.006]; positive probability = 0.036) (Table 2). The credible interval slightly overlapped zero, and the direction of the association suggests a minimal age-related decline in cognitive function within this sample (see Table 2).

Male sex was consistently associated with poorer hearing (β = 4.876; CrI = [0.647, 8.996]; positive probability = 0.99). Although male sex was not significantly associated with SBP (β = 3.584; CrI = [-1.564, 8.918]; positive probability = 0.91), the direction of association was consistently positive. The association between male sex and cognition was neither significant nor strongly consistent (β = -0.265; CrI = [-1.250, 0.742]; positive probability = 0.29) (Table 2).

Discussion

This study examined the interrelationships among PF, systolic blood pressure, hearing, and cognition in adults aged 50 years and older, utilizing a Bayesian generalized structural equation modeling approach to evaluate the common cause theory. Some pathways in the model were supported while others were not.

Hearing and PF in Older Adults

We found a direct effect of PF on hearing, with every 10 RAND score unit increase in PF corresponds to, on average, 1.35 dB HL decrease in hearing, which is consistent with previous cohort studies showing that regular physical activity reduces the risk of impairment in sensory function such as hearing loss70–73. It is important to note that this effect size is modest as the maximum difference in RAND score (30 units) corresponds to only a ~ 4 dB HL change in hearing. However, when considered in the context of the pervasive nature of hearing loss among older adults, the potential benefit of maintaining physical function and hearing is significant at the population level. Moreover, while hearing intervention (e.g. hearing aids) can result in improved hearing function, preventing hearing loss is more effective. In the current study self-reported ability to participate in vigorous (e.g. running) and moderate (e.g. walking more than a mile) activity were surveyed. Similarly, Assi et al.’70 found greater odds of not reporting vigorous activities and walking for exercise in people with worse hearing. Assi et al.’s survey70 included, “In the last month, did you ever spend time on vigorous activities that increased your heart rate and made you breathe harder” (e.g., working out, swimming, running)? (yes/no), and “In the last month, did you ever go walking for exercise? (yes/no).” The results of the current study support the hypothesized link between higher PF levels and better peripheral hearing sensitivity in older adults and corroborate a direct pathway from PF to hearing, independent of SBP. These findings are consistent with research on physical activity in animals74 and humans75, which suggests that physical activity may reduce the effects of age-related hearing loss and peripheral hearing sensitivity through several physiological mechanisms including enhanced vascular function and cerebral blood flow, reduced inflammation and oxidative stress, improved metabolic regulation, and protection against microvascular damage in the cochlea. Also, regular physical activity may promote neurotropic support and mitochondrial health76 and optimize the immune system77,78, which are critical for maintaining the function of the cochlear hair cells and auditory neurons.

Cognition and Physical Function in Older Adults

PF had a marginal effect on cognition contrary to results in other studies where greater physical activity was associated with better PF79 and cognition22,80,81. It is possible that the MoCA did not provide the sensitivity needed to identify the relationship between PF and cognition. Several studies found a relationship between physical function (mobility, dynamic balance, and aerobic endurance), working memory82,83 and executive function84,85.

Systolic Blood Pressure, Hearing, Cognition, and PF

SBP was not significantly associated with hearing or cognition and did not mediate the effect of PF. This finding challenges the vascular common-cause hypothesis as a central explanatory mechanism in this sample. Another study with participants over the age of 65 did find a difference in categories of hearing loss and participant self-report of hypertension. Participants were asked: “Since the last interview, has a doctor told you that you had high blood pressure or hypertension/diabetes? [yes/no]).”70. In comparison with our results, the inclusion of older participants and a broader definition of hypertension and diabetes may explain the disparate findings. The impact of PF on hearing is complex and is likely to involve factors beyond lowering systolic blood pressure. Our results suggest that the indirect path via SBP was unsupported, which indicates the need to explore non-vascular mechanisms in future studies (e.g., metabolic, neurotrophic). It is possible that SBP alone may not adequately reflect the broader cardiovascular profile relevant to cochlear health. Future research should explore measures of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) related pathology such as tau protein17, oxidative stress (OS), mitochondrial DNA damage, low-grade inflammation, decreased immune function, and stem cell depletion86.

A cross-sectional study was conducted on data from the 2018 Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey. The study included 5,860 people, 60–114 years of age (M = 82.78) with self-reported history of hypertension, 84.3%-91.8% of whom were using antihypertensive drugs. Cognitive impairment was defined as a score of < 18 on the Chinese version of the Mini-Mental State Examination and 10.9% or 639 participants with cognitive impairment were identified. Demographics of the population were also collected. Bivariate Logistic regression analysis and the restricted cubic spline (RCS) model were used to analyze the relationship between blood pressure control and cognitive impairment. No significant correlation was found between blood pressure control and cognitive impairment nor hearing loss in patients aged < 80 years, whether in the hypotensive or hypertensive groups73. Similarly in the present study, SBP did not mediate the relationship between PF and either hearing or cognition, nor did it directly predict these outcomes in our sample of 50 to 80-year-olds when age and sex were controlled.

While hypertension is a known risk factor for cognitive impairment and dementia87 and recent intervention studies have shown that lowering SBP is likely to improve cognition in people with cardiovascular risk (SPRINT)37, lowering SBP may not uniformly improve cognition in a general population of healthy middle aged and older adults. Hypertensive participants were found to benefit from intensive exercise39, although in another randomized controlled trial conducted over 6 months, high intensity exercise conducted three days/week did not improve systolic blood pressure nor general cognition in participants with hypertension and subjective memory decline80.

Impact of Age and Sex

Hearing loss. Age was a strong predictor of both higher SBP and greater hearing loss, with a 10-year increase in age corresponding to, on average, a 7.52 (mm Hg) increase in SBP and an 8.78 dB HL increase in PTA. These findings are consistent with the aging literature and support the model’s inclusion of age as a key covariate. With respect to our model, this result reinforces age as a central driver of both vascular and auditory decline. Age was found to be a robust predictor of both increased SBP (mm Hg) and hearing loss (dB HL), consistent with previous literature4. Our findings indicated that male sex was more likely to result in 4.88 dB HL higher hearing threshold reflecting poorer hearing than the female sex. These findings align with epidemiological data88 and a cohort study89 which also found that males are at greater risk for hearing loss.

Cognition. Age had a small and marginally significant negative effect on cognition, with a 10-year increase in age corresponding to, on average, a 0.57-point decrease in cognition score, which may reflect sample characteristics (e.g., no dementia). Age-related cognitive decline may be buffered in healthier, more educated populations; 84% of our sample had high educational attainment, which is known to mitigate cognitive decline90–92. The lack of significant sex effect on SBP and cognition in the present study should be interpreted cautiously due to the small number of male participants.

Measurement of Hearing and PF

Comparison of the results of our study with past research should consider methods of measurement of hearing, PF, cognition, and blood pressure. Different quantification measures of hearing have been used including pure tone testing of 4-frequency (0.5, 1.0, 2.0, and 4.0 kHz) pure-tone average for each ear and analysis of the better-hearing ear’s pure-tone average (PTA)70,71, and determination of categories70,71 of degree of hearing loss: PTA greater than or equal to 25 dB HL at the better ear, mild hearing loss as PTA 25 to less than 40 dB HL in the better ear, and moderate or greater hearing loss as PTA greater than or equal to 40 dB HL in the better ear. Alternatively, other research has used a hearing questionnaire such as the Disability Questionnaire (DLQ)72. We measured pure-tone average across 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 8 kHz of both ears and treated PTA as a continuous variable.

Multiple instruments have been used to assess PF and physical activity. Across studies of cognition and physical activity39, PF85, and hearing and physical activity70, a consistent pattern emerges: the capacity for moderate to intensive physical activity appears pivotal. One interpretation is that reduced PF limits engagement in rigorous activities, thereby increasing susceptibility to hearing loss. It is also possible that impaired PF and hearing loss share underlying pathological mechanisms independent of systolic blood pressure. Although hearing loss might theoretically restrict PF, the mild deficits observed in this sample make that explanation less likely. Notably, PF was associated with hearing thresholds but not with MoCA scores, suggesting that the mechanisms involved may specifically affect peripheral auditory rather than general cognitive processes.

Contrary to the common ‘vascular’ cause hypothesis specific to SBP, other pathological changes in the auditory system may account for the association between PF and hearing. Metabolic or neurotrophic pathways may mediate the relationship between PF and auditory function25,86.

Future directions

We analyzed SBP, cognition, and hearing in adults 50–80 years of age using a scientifically sound model and Bayesian generalized structural model (GSEM) statistical analysis, which uncovered a strong relationship between PF and pure-tone hearing thresholds that was not observed between PF and cognition. Some limitations of the study should be considered. Our population was white, predominately female and well-educated; therefore, our findings cannot be generalized to other populations until tested. Additionally, our research was cross-sectional, and this limits our ability to make conclusions about causation. The ABC@USC research center is collecting 5-year follow-up data on its original cohort. Longitudinal data analysis would allow us to determine if the same relationship between PF and hearing exists over time with the potential to identify participants who decreased, maintained, or increased their physical activity and the impact of change in physical activity on change in physical function and hearing. One study followed middle-aged women and found that women who experienced better physical function over time reported engagement in healthy behaviors including physical activity93. We could also determine whether the relationship between PF and SBP and cognition evolves over time. While systolic blood pressure did not have a direct or mediating effect on hearing and cognition, it does not rule out the possibility that other blood pressure values could yield a different result. Another potential limitation of the current study is self-reported PF; however, possibly due to ease of administration, the questionnaire was completed by all participants. Additionally, another study of physical activity and brain structure found a strong correlation between self-reported physical activity and accelerometer-measured physical activity94.

A key limitation of this study is the relatively modest sample size, which may reduce statistical power, limit the precision of parameter estimates, and increase uncertainty around effect sizes. With smaller samples, conventional frequentist approaches can yield unstable estimates and wide confidence intervals, particularly in models with multiple covariates or complex structures. However, the use of a Bayesian analytical framework in this study partially mitigates these concerns, by incorporating weakly prior information, the Bayesian approach stabilizes estimation, reduces overfitting, and provides more realistic uncertainty quantification through posterior distributions. Nonetheless, findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution, and replication in larger, independent cohorts is warranted to confirm the robustness and generalizability of the results. There may be additional confounding variables that we did not include in the current model but could be explored in future research. For example, hearing loss is associated with depression95 and could limit physical activity and physical function. We excluded participants with severe depression but not those with depressive symptoms.

Another limitation of this study is that participants were taking varying numbers of antihypertensive medications, and it was not feasible to adequately control for this heterogeneity in the statistical models. Differences in the number of prescribed agents may reflect variations in disease severity, treatment response, or comorbidity burden, which could independently influence the outcomes of interest. As a result, residual confounding related to treatment intensity cannot be ruled out, and this should be considered when interpreting the findings.

A final potential limitation is that our measure of cognition may have lacked sensitivity, especially when participants with dementia were excluded. Other measures of cognition may be more sensitive than the Montreal Cognitive Assessment, such as the NIH toolbox List sorting Working Memory task96 or the Trail making Test80 which could reveal sensory and cognitive associations in a high cardiovascular risk population97. However, we selected the MoCA for the current study because of its wide clinical use in numerous countries98–101.

While our results do not support a vascular common cause; other physiological measures have the potential to show a common explanation for hearing loss and cognitive decline. Additionally, a Convergent Pathological Impact hypothesis has been proposed4 where common pathological processes associated with Alzheimer’s disease (AD) or vascular disease impact both the cochlea and the ascending pathway, leading to hearing loss, while simultaneously affecting the medial temporal lobe and dementia. Furthermore, the lack of a mediating or direct effect of systolic blood pressure suggests that vascular control alone may not account for hearing outcomes in some populations, indicating the need to consider additional mechanisms such as metabolic and inflammatory pathways and oxidative stress102.

The findings have implications for clinical assessment and management of age-related hearing loss in adults. Assessment of PF could help identify individuals at increased risk for hearing impairment. These results further support a multidomain approach to healthy aging, in which maintaining PF may contribute, albeit modestly, to preserving hearing function.

Conclusion

PF is directly associated with better hearing in older adults, independent of systolic blood pressure. Age is a strong risk factor for increased SBP and hearing loss, while SBP itself does not appear to mediate the relationship between PF and sensory or cognitive outcomes. These findings highlight the importance of promoting physical activity for sensory health in aging populations and suggest that pathways beyond vascular health may be involved.
