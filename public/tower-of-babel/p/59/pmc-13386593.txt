Comparative efficacy of analgesic techniques in total hip arthroplasty
Authors: Tim Cheok, Morgan Berman, William Jordan, Alexander Beveridge, Thomas Smith, Craig Morrison, Mehul Mayank, Ruurd L. Jaarsma, Job N. Doornberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsWe compared the analgesic efficacy of commonly used analgesic techniques in patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty (THA). This included femoral nerve block, supra- and infrainguinal fascia iliaca block, lumbar plexus block (LPB), intrathecal morphine (ITM), periarticular injection (PAI), pericapsular nerve group block (PENG), iliopsoas block (IPB), anterior, lateral, and posterior quadratus lumborum block (QLA/QLL/QLP), lateral femoral cutaneous nerve block (LFCNB), and placebo.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Embase, The Cochrane Library, and Web of Science from their date of inception through to December 2025 was performed. The primary outcome of interest was postoperative day 1 (POD1) pain scores. Secondary outcomes of interest include pain levels at 12 hours postoperatively, opioid consumption within 24 hours after surgery, time to first mobilization, and hospital length of stay. Clinical and statistical significance were evaluated for each outcome.ResultsWe identified 112 studies. No analgesic technique demonstrated a clinically meaningful reduction in POD1 pain scores. IPB + PAI showed clinically meaningful reduction in 12-hour pain scores when compared with placebo. Similarly, both ITM and IPB + PAI showed clinically meaningful reduction in opioid consumption. Statistically significant reduction in time to first mobilization was seen in the PENG + LFCNB, QLL, PENG, and suprainguinal fascia iliaca block groups as compared with placebo. The hospital length of stay was shorter in the ITM and PENG groups as compared with placebo.ConclusionWithin the limitations of this study, clinically meaningful reduction in pain scores was not observed in most analgesic techniques following THA. ITM demonstrated reduction in 24-hour opioid consumption with a shorter length of hospital stay, while PENG demonstrated shorter time to mobilization as well as a shorter length of hospital stay. Interpretation of these results should consider the substantial heterogeneity encountered in the measurement of 24-hour morphine consumption, as well as the potential side effects of ITM.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2026;7(7):979–993.

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is a highly successful procedure for the treatment of hip osteoarthritis and displaced femoral neck fracture, with a success rate of more than 90%.1,2 In recent years, there has been a push towards early mobilization and discharge in patients undergoing THA, as prolonged hospital stay is associated with an increased risk of perioperative morbidity.3-5 The 2021 PROSPECT guidelines by the European Society of Regional Anaesthesia and Pain Therapy advocated for the use of femoral nerve block (FNB), fascia iliaca block (FIB), lumbar plexus block (LPB), and periarticular infiltration analgesia (PAI). Intrathecal morphine (ITM) may be considered if spinal anaesthesia is used, although it must be emphasized that this method has a substantial side-effect profile, and adequate postoperative pain relief can be achieved without its use.6 While FNB, FIB, and LPB provide good analgesic relief, there is a theoretical concern regarding delayed mobilization and increased risk of falls.7-11 More contemporary anaesthetic techniques, such as the pericapsular nerve group block (PENG), which are theoretically motor-sparing, have since been introduced.12

Previous network meta-analyses have yielded conflicting results, relying heavily on ranking statistics without accounting for the minimal clinically important difference (MCID), which may have resulted in overinterpretation of clinically insignificant findings.13-16 Furthermore, Lee et al13 have also considered differing approaches for FIB and quadratus lumborum block as a single group. These different approaches provide varying amounts of local anaesthetic spread, which may influence the degree of sensory blockade achieved. Neither of these meta-analyses measured time to mobilization or hospital length of stay, which are crucial components in same-day arthroplasty. The primary outcome of this study is thus to compare the pain levels on postoperative day 1 (POD1) measured using the visual analogue scale (VAS) following several commonly used analgesic techniques for perioperative pain management in patients undergoing a THA. Secondary outcomes of interest include pain levels at 12 hours postoperatively, opioid consumption within 24 hours after surgery, time to first mobilization, and hospital length of stay in patients undergoing THA.

Methods

The systematic review was performed based on the recommendations of PRISMA.17 This systematic review protocol was registered with PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420261288091).

Search strategy and selection criteria

We performed a systematic search of the literature across PubMed, Embase, The Cochrane Library, and Web of Science from their date of inception through to 28 December 2025 using the search terms shown in Supplementary Material Appendix 1. Studies describing the analgesic efficacy of commonly used analgesic techniques following THA were evaluated. The techniques studied include FNB, suprainguinal FIB (FIBS), infrainguinal FIB (FIBI), ITM, LPB, PAI, PENG, iliopsoas plane block (IPB), anterior quadratus lumborum (QLA), lateral quadratus lumborum (QLL), posterior quadratus lumborum (QLP), lateral femoral cutaneous nerve block (LFCNB), and placebo. For the purpose of this review, placebo was defined as either sham treatment or no treatment. A list of treatment acronyms is summarized in Table I. The primary outcome of interest was the POD1 pain score measured using the 0 to 10 scale VAS. Secondary outcomes of interest include pain levels at 12 hours postoperatively, opioid consumption in the first 24 hours after surgery (measured using milligram morphine equivalent), time to first mobilization, and hospital length of stay. Unlike the PROSPECT study,6 we specifically included contemporary fascial plane blocks.

Following the literature search, the identified titles and abstracts were then screened by two reviewers (TC and TS) for relevance and consideration, and compiled into a provisional list. The full texts in this provisional list were then assessed independently by the same two reviewers for inclusion. Both reviewers had consensus of the included articles and agreed upon the included articles. Where consensus could not be achieved, the article was scrutinized in full by a third reviewer (MB). Studies included were required to: 1) only include patients undergoing THA; 2) be a randomized study; 3) have compared at least two intervention groups of interest; and 4) provide the primary outcome of interest. We excluded: 1) non-randomized studies; 2) studies with insufficient extractable data to perform the meta-analysis; 3) studies which have been retracted; 4) studies without appropriate comparators; 5) continuous analgesic regimens; and 6) non-human studies. In studies where a combination of two eligible analgesic techniques was used, these were included and analyzed separately. However, we excluded those with three or more techniques combined due to poor generalizability. In studies with multiple intervention groups that fell under the same category, but otherwise met the inclusion/exclusion criteria, the investigators discussed the study and included only the best performing group in the final analysis.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of each study, as well as the outcomes of interest were collected. A frequentist network meta-analysis was then performed. Firstly, network geometry analysis was performed for each outcome of interest to illustrate the direct treatment comparisons. Following this, the assumptions of the network meta-analysis were analyzed. Heterogeneity was assessed using the τ2 statistic, with lower values indicating lower absolute heterogeneity. Next, a global inconsistency model was fitted for each outcome of interest and assessed using Wald’s test. Further to this, a global consistency model was also applied to inspect for the assumption of transitivity. A substantial difference in the estimated between-studies SD would indicate violation of this assumption.18 Key results of both primary and secondary outcomes were illustrated using a forest plot, with the placebo group used as the reference comparator. A league table summarizing group comparisons for all outcomes was then generated. Where possible, sub-group analysis of each outcome based upon the surgical approach was performed.

The methodological quality of each included article was assessed independently by two reviewers (AB and WJ) using the RoB 2 tool weighted by sample size.19 Comparison-adjusted funnel plot analysis with Egger’s test of funnel plot asymmetry was performed to assess for publication bias. Statistical analyses were conducted using a combination of Stata (v. 19.5, StataCorp, USA) and R Statistical Software (v. 4.5.1, R Core Team, 2025; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria).20,21 Utilizing random effects models, mean difference (MD) and its corresponding 95% CI was used to illustrate the effects of each treatment arm. Where statistical significance was encountered, we assessed whether the results then met the threshold for clinical significance. For this study, clinical significance was defined as an effect size greater than the MCID of the outcome measure. Based on previous literature, the MCID was 1.86 for pain measured using VAS22 and 10.00 mg for opioid consumption.23 There is no established MCID for time to mobilization or hospital length of stay, thus any statistically significant finding was deemed clinically significant. Lastly, a sensitivity analysis removing studies published prior to 2005 was performed to confirm that there was no substantial difference in overall results. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Sensitivity analysis performed removing studies conducted prior to 2005 showed no substantial difference in outcomes for POD1 pain scores, 24-hour opioid consumption, time to mobilization, and hospital length of stay. Due to network disconnection, the same analysis was not able to be performed for 12-hour pain scores, although sequential trial removal of studies performed prior to 2005 also did not demonstrate any substantial difference in key outcomes.

Discussion

This network meta-analysis demonstrated that no regional analgesic technique or combination achieved a clinically meaningful reduction in POD1 pain following THA. This finding is in contrast with the more recent reviews by Yang et al14 and Lee et al.13 When compared with placebo, clinically meaningful reduction in pain scores at 12 hours was seen in the IPB + PAI group. Similarly, both ITM and IPB + PAI showed clinically meaningful reduction in opioid consumption in the first 24 hours postoperatively. These findings should be interpreted in the context of known dose-dependent adverse effects of intrathecal opioids, which were inconsistently reported across included studies and could not be analyzed. In addition, it should be noted that the certainty of evidence for IPB + PAI is low, as there are only 40 patients in that group. Statistically significant reduction in time to first mobilization was seen in the PENG + LFCNB, QLL, PENG, and FIBS groups as compared with placebo. Only ITM and PENG were effective in reducing the hospital length of stay as compared with placebo. Sub-group analyses and sensitivity analyses to remove studies conducted prior to 2005 did not show any substantial difference in the overall results.

Although the PROSPECT guidelines favoured the use of FNB, FIB, and LPB, the findings of this meta-analysis found that ITM and several fascial plane blocks may reduce morphine consumption, and promote early mobilization and discharge from hospital.6 Fascial plane blocks, such as PENG, refer to a regional anaesthetic technique in which a potential space between two discrete fascial planes is injected with local anaesthetic, allowing its spread to nerves travelling within the plane and its adjacent tissues.136 The spread of the block is, however, limited by the boundaries of the fascia. This is in contrast with traditional nerve blocks, such as the FNB, FIB, and LPB, where blockade of the nerve within its compartment is volume-dependent.137-139 The more controlled nature of fascial plane blocks has generally been perceived to provide targeted sensory blockade with limited effect on motor branches of the same nerve. In our analysis, not all of the fascial blocks outperformed traditional blocks, and thus judicious use of these contemporary techniques is advocated. Providers should consider the patients’ treatment goals, as well as resource availability and operating room efficiency when selecting the appropriate analgesic technique. Where a fascial plane block is desired, the PENG block may be beneficial due to reduced time to first mobilization, as well as a shorter length of hospital stay, and is less technically demanding.

The key strength of this present study is that this is the first network meta-analysis which incorporates MCID in the interpretation of the findings. Nevertheless, several limitations need to be acknowledged. First, the τ2 value for the measurement of opioid consumption was 9.19, indicating potential heterogeneity in this outcome measure. This may be due to the variable postoperative analgesic regimen, cultural perception of pain, and the conversion formula used to derive milligram morphine equivalents.140-142 It is also important to note that the decrease in opioid consumption in patients with ITM should be weighed, in fact it is in itself an opioid being administered to the patient. ITM is also a long-acting opioid that carries with it a requirement for additional monitoring in the postoperative period for adverse effects including respiratory depression, delayed respiratory depression, sedation, nausea, vomiting, and pruritus. Thus, it has hospital resource implications extending beyond the operating theatre. Second, we did not control for intraoperative factors, such as the type and dosage of local anaesthetic used in the block, presence of adjuvants, surgical approach, and the use of robotic-assisted surgery.143-152 These factors have the potential to affect pain outcomes, early mobilization, and hospital length of stay. It is important to note that there was no significant global inconsistency noted in all outcomes, and therefore it is unlikely that controlling for these variables would have resulted in a substantially different outcome. Additionally, surgical approach was poorly recorded in almost half of our studies, making our sub-group analysis prone to bias. Third, we did not include the erector spinae block in our study, despite it being regarded as a fascial plane block. There is a substantial variability in the spread of local anaesthetic when this block is used.153,154 Fourth, we excluded continuous infiltration analgesic techniques from our review, as this may artificially increase length of stay in hospital and time to mobilization. Fifth, the use of a frequentist approach, as compared with a Bayesian approach for network meta-analysis may be criticized.155,156 A previous simulation study did not reveal any meaningful differences between the two approaches.157 Sixth, adverse effects of techniques such as postoperative haematoma, local anaesthetic toxicity events, prolonged neuropraxia, and opioid-related side-effects, as well as patient satisfaction and long-term opioid consumption were not investigated. Seventh, time to mobilization and discharge may be influenced by institutional factors, such as antiquated protocols and lack of physiotherapy services after-hours. Eighth, the use of POD1 pain is arbitrary and could limit the interpretation of any early benefits. Ninth, we did not consider the additional financial cost and potential disruption to theatre efficiency involved in regional anaesthetic administration. Furthermore, as we had selected the best performing group in studies with multiple intervention groups which fall under the same category, it is possible that selection bias was introduced. Lastly, the use of MCID as a threshold for clinical significance may also be questioned. The MCID can be calculated using a variable number of techniques, thus reducing its reliability.158,159 Additionally, as pain can be context-dependent, it is possible that different thresholds may exist with different patient population group and at different postoperative timepoints. Although there is no universally accepted standard, further work is being done to incorporate MCID and certainty of evidence into the derivation of ranking statistics, making this an alternative for future network meta-analyses on this topic.160-162

In conclusion, within the limitations of our study, clinically meaningful reduction in POD1 pain scores was not demonstrated for any analgesic technique nor combination thereof. Only the IPB + PAI group had a clinically meaningful reduction in pain scores at 12 hours postoperatively, although the certainty of this evidence is low. ITM was associated with reduced 24-hour opioid consumption and shorter hospital stay, while PENG-based techniques were associated with earlier mobilization and shorter length of stay. Interpretation of these results should consider the substantial heterogeneity encountered in the measurement of 24-hour morphine consumption, as well as the potential side-effects of ITM.

Take home message

- Most analgesic techniques following total hip arthroplasty did not confer clinically meaningful reduction in pain scores on postoperative day one.

- However, the use of pericapsular nerve group block or intrathecal morphine may facilitate early patient discharge.
