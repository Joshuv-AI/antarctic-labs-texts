Oculocardiac Reflex during Electrode Placement in Trigeminal Schwannoma Surgery via Cavernous Sinus Approach
Authors: Sapna Yadav, Ruchi Verma, Nidhi Singh, Sumit Sachan, Devendra Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The oculocardiac reflex (OCR) is well known in ophthalmic surgery, but its occurrence during neurosurgical procedures is rare and often unexpected. Mechanical stimulation of trigeminal pathways at the skull base can cause significant hemodynamic changes; however reflex activation during preparatory steps rather than tumor manipulation is rarely described. We report an intraoperative event in a 22-year-old male undergoing resection of a cystic trigeminal schwannoma via a subtemporal transcavernous approach under general anesthesia, during which periorbital needle electrodes were placed for cranial nerves III, IV, and VI monitoring. During electrode insertion, the patient developed sudden bradycardia from 72 to 34 beats per minute with a brief hypertensive response, followed by persistent bradycardia and eventual decrease in blood pressure. Immediate removal of the stimulus led to rapid recovery of hemodynamic stability without the need for anticholinergic therapy, and the surgery proceeded without further issues. This case shows that even minor periocular manipulation during neurosurgical preparation can trigger OCR. Early detection of unusual triggers, awareness of the characteristic biphasic cardiovascular response, and good communication between surgical and anesthesia teams are essential to prevent hemodynamic instability. Further documentation of similar cases may help identify contributing factors and improve preventive strategies.

Introduction

The oculocardiac reflex (OCR) is typically defined as a greater than 20% decrease in heart rate from baseline in response to ocular or periocular manipulation. 1 It represents a well-characterized brainstem reflex arc involving afferent fibers of the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve and efferent parasympathetic fibers carried by the vagus nerve. 2 Although most often encountered during strabismus correction, extraocular muscle traction, and other ophthalmic procedures, OCR is not limited to the orbit. Similar trigeminocardiac responses have been reported in craniofacial and skull-base surgeries where mechanical stimulation of trigeminal branches can occur. 3

Among these, the cavernous sinus region is a particularly high-risk anatomic corridor. It contains cranial nerves III, IV, V1, V2, and VI close to the cavernous segment of the internal carotid artery. Surgical manipulation in this area—especially during approaches to trigeminal schwannomas, meningiomas, or vascular lesions—can inadvertently stimulate trigeminal afferents and trigger profound vagal reflexes. 4

While OCR and other variants of the trigeminocardiac reflex are traditionally associated with direct surgical manipulation, they may also occur during seemingly minor preparatory steps, such as placement of monitoring electrodes or traction on periorbital tissue. Recognizing such atypical triggers is essential for anesthesiologists and surgeons involved in skull-base procedures.

Case Presentation

A 22-year-old male with no significant medical history presented with a one-year history of right-sided suboccipital headache and diplopia on right lateral gaze. He reported no vomiting, seizures, loss of consciousness, visual blurring, trauma, or systemic illness. Neurological examination revealed paresis of the right lateral rectus muscle, while the rest of the cranial nerves and systemic examination were normal. Baseline vital signs (BP 110/70 mm Hg, HR 72 bpm) and routine laboratory tests were within normal limits. Preoperative MRI brain (axial post-contrast T1 image) showing a well-defined extra-axial heterogeneously enhancing mass lesion epicentered in the right Meckel's cave, with extension toward the cavernous sinus and compressing the right medial temporal lobe, consistent with a trigeminal schwannoma ( Fig. 1 ). Surgical excision was planned using a subtemporal transcavernous approach. After induction, total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) was initiated with a 1:1 combination of propofol and ketamine. The depth of anesthesia was adequate, with BIS values maintained between 40 and 50 during electrode placement and tumor exposure. Intraoperatively, neurophysiological monitoring was planned for cranial nerves III, IV, and VI (extraocular movements) and cranial nerve VII (facial electromyography). During placement of periorbital needle electrodes for ocular monitoring ( Fig. 2 ), the patient experienced a sudden, severe bradycardia with the heart rate dropping to 34 beats per minute from 72 bpm. This was initially accompanied by a transient hypertensive surge (188/109 mm Hg), followed by sustained bradycardia with blood pressure falling to 101/49 mm Hg ( Fig. 3A and B ), but it never reached hypotensive levels, as we were able to control the further decline by promptly removing the electrode stimulus. After immediate removal of the periorbital needle electrodes, the heart rate and blood pressure rapidly returned to baseline without pharmacological intervention. The electrodes were subsequently reapplied in the same position with modifications: they were placed more superficially, with avoidance of deep insertion, minimal periocular manipulation, and excessive traction. Adequate depth of anesthesia was reconfirmed, and close hemodynamic monitoring was maintained. No recurrence of the OCR was observed after reapplication. The surgery proceeded smoothly, and the patient recovered well postoperatively without neurological or cardiovascular complications.

Discussion

This case highlights an unusual presentation of OCR during neurosurgical preparation. Unlike the typical association with extraocular muscle traction, the reflex here was triggered by periorbital electrode placement. The observed biphasic response, characterized by bradycardia and transient hypertension followed by persistent bradycardia and a decline in blood pressure, aligns with the known physiology of the trigeminocardiac reflex. 1 The initial hypertensive response may be due to brief sympathetic activation, followed by vagal dominance that causes bradycardia and hypotension. 1 The transcavernous approach positions the surgical field close to the trigeminal afferents. Filis et al reported a definite but low incidence of trigeminocardiac reflex (TCR) during neurosurgical procedures, including cavernous sinus operations. 2 A recent case report described TCR complicating the excision of a trigeminal schwannoma, underscoring its clinical significance. 3 Additionally, large series of trigeminal schwannomas have highlighted the anatomic difficulties and the importance of preserving cranial nerves during surgery near the cavernous sinus. 4

The trigeminocardiac reflex is now understood to be part of a broader group of brainstem reflexes with important perioperative consequences. 5 6 The literature emphasizes that factors such as anesthetic depth, selected agents, and careful monitoring during skull-base surgery affect the occurrence and severity of this complication. 1 5 6 What makes this case particularly distinctive is the atypical trigger, i.e., the placement of electrodes around the orbit for monitoring, rather than tumor manipulation. From a practical perspective, several measures may reduce the risk of OCR during needle electrode placement for ocular motor monitoring. These include gentle and superficial insertion of periorbital electrodes, avoiding repeated attempts or excessive traction on periocular tissues, and ensuring adequate depth of anesthesia prior to electrode placement. Maintenance of normoxia and normocarbia, continuous ECG and invasive blood pressure monitoring, and clear communication among the neurophysiology, surgical, and anesthesia teams are essential. Anticholinergic agents should be readily available, although immediate cessation of the triggering stimulus remains the most effective first-line intervention.

Conclusion

This case underscores that OCR may occur even during seemingly minor preparatory steps, such as electrode placement, rather than only during direct surgical manipulation. Although traditionally associated with ophthalmic procedures, OCR can also occur during neurosurgical operations, including transcavernous approaches for trigeminal schwannomas. Current evidence suggests that TCR should be anticipated whenever trigeminal afferents might be stimulated, especially during cavernous sinus or Meckel's cave surgeries. 2 4 5 6 Its occurrence during periorbital electrode placement emphasizes the importance of vigilance during preparatory steps. Recognizing the biphasic response and acting quickly are essential to avoid adverse outcomes.
