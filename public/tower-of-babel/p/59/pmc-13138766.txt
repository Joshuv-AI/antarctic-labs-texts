A case of melanoma with atypical presentation
Authors: Çağla TEZDUYAN, Merih YALÇINER, Elif Berna KÖKSOY
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A case of melanoma with atypical presentation Malignant melanoma, a cancer type with a high mortality rate and increasing incidence worldwide, primarily affects the skin but can also occur in various other organs and tissues, including the respiratory tract. Although primary lung malignant melanomas are rare, they are often diagnosed at advanced stages due to their asymptomatic nature, and their atypical presentations may lead to misdiagnosis as other malignancies. In this case report, a mass lesion almost completely filling the right lung initially led to the consideration of small cell lung carcinoma, but a definitive pathological diagnosis could not be obtained in subsequent studies. The diagnosis was confirmed by soft tissue biopsy taken from the anterior thoracic wall. Considering clinical, pathological, and radiological evaluations, possible diagnoses included sarcoma, small-cell lung cancer, and melanoma. The patient, diagnosed through multiple tissue sampling and detailed dermatological examination, presents an interesting atypical case. This case highlights that rare variants of melanoma can be mistaken for other cancer types, such as lung cancer, sarcoma, or lymphoma. Patients may not always present with a noticeable skin lesion; therefore, a meticulous skin examination is crucial in such cases. Malignant melanoma is a noteworthy disease, considering its increasing incidence and early diagnosis holds vital importance for appropriate treatment and management. Keywords: Atypical presentation, rare melanoma variants, lung cancer, skin lesion, malignant melanoma

INTRODUCTION

Malignant melanoma is a cancer type with a high mortality rate and has a rapidly increasing incidence all over the world. Survival rates of patients with melanoma depend on the stage of the disease at the time of diagnosis, and early diagnosis is vital to reduce mortality and morbidity (1).

In this case report, following a detailed differential diagnosis process, a rare malignant melanoma variant was eventually diagnosed.

CASE REPORT

While a 46-year-old female patient was being investigated with complaints of weakness, cough, pain on the right side of the chest, and shortness of breath for approximately 1.5 months, the bronchoscopic biopsy was performed from the infiltrated area at the entrance of the basal segment of the right bronchus by the pulmonologists in 02/2023.

Histopathological examination revealed a small round-cell malignancy. Immunohistochemical analysis showed negativity for high molecular weight- low molecular weight cytokeratin (HMW-LMW CK), Thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1), P40, CD45, synaptophysin, and chromogranin-A, while CD56 was positive. Although clinical and radiological findings primarily suggested small round cell malignancy, the lack of keratin, TTF1 positivity, and the negative results for neuroendocrine markers other than CD56 did not allow for a definitive conclusion. Since other malignancies that could demonstrate similar morphology could not be ruled out, the patient was transferred to our care.

The patient was consulted to our clinic for the purpose of a diagnostic and treatment plan and the patient was admitted to our clinic.

On computed tomography (CT) of the chest, a mass of approximately 116 x 80 x 71 mm in size, located in the mediastinum, encircling both the main bronchi, compressing the left atrium, pulmonary veins on the right and the right main pulmonary artery and narrowing the lumen, pushing the mediastinal structures to the left was detected. In addition, pleural-based nodular lesions, the largest of which was 85 x 72 x 69 mm in size, showing heterogeneous contrast enhancement pattern, appearing invading the chest wall were observed (Figure 1).

A tru-cut biopsy was performed from the anterior wall of the right chest.

During follow-up, pleural effusion developed on the right, and a pleural catheter was placed. Cytological assessments repeatedly made from the pleural fluid were inconclusive.

The patient’s first biopsy, which was interpreted histopathologically as a malignant small round cell tumor, was reassessed with additional studies.

In the tumor, focal weak to moderate CD56 positivity, widespread strong vimentin positivity, widespread strong nuclear SOX10, focal strong human melanoma black-45, and focal para nuclear desmin positivity drew attention. These findings were suggestive of an undifferentiated malignant small round cell tumor morphology/amelanotic features, indicating infiltration of malignant melanoma (small cell melanoma). Additional studies revealed that HMW- LMW CK, insulinoma-associated protein 1, chromogranin-A, synaptophysin, CD45, CD3, CD20, CD99, p40, TTF1, myogenin, and Wilms’ tumour 1 were negative. Desmin, SOX10, HMB-45, BRAF VE1, and CD56 were widely and strongly positive. Melan-A showed widespread but weak to moderate staining, and S100 exhibited suspicious positivity involving only a few cells. Ki-67 staining revealed a high proliferation rate in the majority of tumor cells. Based on these findings, the diagnosis was reported as compatible with malignant melanoma, consistent with the small cell variant, and the pathologists’ final diagnosis was amelanotic malignant melanoma infiltration in 04/2023. The patient was subsequently consulted to dermatology for dermoscopic examination, and a biopsy was taken from a slightly raised, blue-gray colored lesion, approximately 1 cm in size on the back of the neck of the patient. Positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET- CT) was performed for staging, and pathological fluorodeoxyglucose activity was observed in the mediastinal invading mass lesions that filled almost the entire right lung, in the large area of the liver in the right lobe (Figure 2). The biopsy result was the same as the first mass taken from the lung and was compatible with amelanotic melanoma.

DISCUSSION

Malignant melanoma usually occurs on the skin but can also occur in other organs and tissues such as the respiratory tract, oral cavity, liver, esophagus, larynx, cervix, ovary, vagina, and gallbladder (2). Most cases of malignant melanoma of the respiratory system are metastatic at the time of diagnosis, accounting for 0.01% of all primary lung tumors and 0.4% of all malignant melanomas (3).

In this case report, based on a mass lesion that almost completely filled the right lung, small cell lung carcinoma was initially considered, but a definitive pathological diagnosis could not be obtained in the subsequent studies. The diagnosis was confirmed by a soft tissue biopsy taken from the anterior thoracic wall. Considering clinical, pathological, and radiological aspects combined, possible diagnoses were sarcoma, small-cell lung cancer, and melanoma. The patient, who was diagnosed with multiple tissue sampling and a detailed dermatological examination, is interested in this atypical presentation.

Juan Peng et al. have reported a case with a primary malignant melanoma of the lung and discussed that presenting symptoms could include cough, sputum, hemoptysis, chest tightness, and chest pain (4). However, some patients may be asymptomatic and may be detected incidentally on routine imaging. The most common radiological findings are solitary, central endobronchial masses (3,5). Although our patient’s tumor’s primary location appears to be skin of the back of the neck, the mass in the lung was centrally located.

CONCLUSION

In summary, rare variants of melanoma can imitate various cancers, including lung cancer, sarcoma, or lymphoma. Patients do not necessarily present to the physician with a noticeable skin lesion. We emphasize that detailed skin examination should be performed in such cases, for it may lead to the diagnosis of melanoma, the incidence of which appears to be increasing.
