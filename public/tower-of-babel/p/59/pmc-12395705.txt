CCHCR1 links P-body proteins to the centrosome and is required for ciliogenesis through interacting with OFD1 and PCM1
Authors: Junshan Zhang, Qiwen Wu, Shanqiang Zhang, Stephen Cho Wing Sze, Chunman Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProcessing bodies (P-bodies) are nonmembranous ribonucleoprotein (RNP) granules located in the cytosol that function as assembly hubs for RNA storage and degradation. Although there are reports indicating that certain P-body proteins are also present at the centrosome and participate in primary cilia development, how these P-body proteins localize to the centrosome remains unclear. In mammalian cells, coiled-coil alpha-helical rod protein 1 (CCHCR1) is localized to both the P-bodies and centrosomes, where it interacts with the P-body component enhancer of mRNA-decapping protein 4 (EDC4) as well as a range of centriolar satellite components, yet its cellular function remains poorly characterized.MethodsBiotin identification (BioID) coupled with mass spectrometry, immunoprecipitation (IP), glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down, and acceptor bleaching fluorescence resonance energy transfer (AB-FRET) assay were used to explore and identify protein–protein interactions. Gene overexpression, RNA interference-based gene knockdown, CRISPR–Cas9-mediated gene knockout, and immunofluorescence (IF) were applied to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanism.ResultsWe identified that CCHCR1 interacts with oral-facial-digital syndrome 1 protein (OFD1) via its C-terminal coiled-coil domain. The centrosomal localization of CCHCR1 is determined by OFD1 and pericentriolar materials 1 (PCM1). We also found that CCHCR1 recruits P-body proteins to the centrosome through interacting with EDC4 via its N-terminal coiled-coil domain. Depletion of either CCHCR1 or P-body components EDC4 and DEAD-Box Helicase 6 (DDX6) impairs ciliogenesis.ConclusionsCCHCR1 acts as a linker that recruits P-body proteins to the centrosome and is essential for cilia development. The recruitment of P-body proteins to the centrosome via CCHCR1 is also one of the mechanisms by which PCM1 and OFD1 are involved in ciliogenesis.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11658-025-00780-0.

Background

In most eukaryotic cells, centrosomes function as the major microtubule organizing center (MTOC), playing key roles in a wide variety of cellular processes, including cell motility, polarity, mitosis, and ciliogenesis [1–4]. Each centrosome is composed of a pair of centrioles surrounded by pericentriolar materials (PCM) and centriolar satellites [5, 6]. In quiescent or post-mitotic cells, the older one of the centriolar pairs (mother centriole) can transform into basal body that organizes the formation of the primary cilium, a hair-like protrusion extending from the surface of cells [7, 8]. Primary cilia are highly specialized organelles that serve as both sensors and effectors of extracellular stimuli [9, 10]. Formation of cilia is a highly regulated process that requires the coordinated assembly of a variety of proteins and lipids. The key steps of cilia formation include recruitment of specialized proteins to basal bodies, assembly of the ciliary vesicle, formation of the transition zone, and extension of the microtubular axoneme [11–13]. Many centriolar proteins, such as centrosomal proteins (CEP) 164, CEP 97, and CP110, play pivotal roles in ciliogenesis [14, 15]. In addition, centriolar satellite proteins such as OFD1, PCM-1, and CEP131 are critical regulators of this process [16–31].

P-bodies, or processing bodies, are nonmembranous cytosolic ribonucleoprotein (RNP) granules that serve as assembly centers for RNA storage or degradation. In addition to messenger RNA (mRNA) and microRNA, a wide array of proteins involved in RNA metabolism are enriched in P-bodies, such as the decapping complex (Dcp1, Dcp2, and EDC4), 5′ to 3′ exonuclease Xrn1, translational inhibition factors (DDX6 and 4E-T), and regulators of miRNA-mediated silencing (Ago1-4, GW182) [32, 33]. As dynamic structures, P-bodies can move along microtubules that may facilitate their assembly and disassembly in response to cellular signals. In addition, a pair of P-bodies are always identified at the centrosome [34]. This phenomenon is not due to the arrival of P-bodies at the minus end during random movement along microtubules, as suppressing the expression of certain P-body proteins through RNA interference negatively affects the formation of primary cilia in human astrocytes [35]. Consequently, it is plausible that a specific mechanism exists to recruit P-bodies or P-body proteins to the centrosome, and this process plays an essential role in ciliogenesis.

The CCHCR1 protein, which contains multiple coiled-coil domains, was once believed to play crucial roles in the pathology of psoriasis [36–38]. In mammalian cells, it both localizes at P-bodies and centrosomes [39, 40]. Mass spectrometry analysis has also identified P-body protein EDC4 and centrosome proteins PCM1, CEP131, and astrin as CCHCR1-associated proteins. In a previous study, we first showed that CCHCR1, through its interaction with astrin, recruits CEP72 and MCPH proteins to the centrosome, ensuring normal centrosome replication and microtubule organization [41]. However, there are still numerous issues that require further clarification. What are the functions of the interaction between CCHCR1 and other centrosomal proteins and P-body proteins? Is there any connection between a P-body and centrosome through CCHCR1? Here, we report that CCHCR1 is a crucial factor connecting P-body protein with centrosomes. We found that CCHCR1 interacts with EDC4 via its N-terminal coiled-coil domain and with OFD1 through its C-terminal coiled-coil domain, thereby as a linker recruiting P-body proteins to the centrosome, and is essential for cilia development.

Materials and methods

All antibodies and main reagents used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1. The oligonucleotide sequences for primers, siRNAs, and gRNAs used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Plasmids

CCHCR1-GFP and CCHCR1-mCherry have been previously described [41]. pCIneo-lambdaN-HA-HsEDC4 is from Addgene (no. 66597). To construct the CCHCR1-mCherry-EGFP fusion plasmid, the CCHCR1-EGFPN1 plasmid was subjected to double digestion with SalI and BamHI. The mCherry DNA fragment, which was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the mCherryN2 plasmid as a template and then subjected to the same double digestion, was inserted between CCHCR1 and EGFP. For the construction of GFP-OFD1, GFP-OFD1 fragments (1-132, 133-600, 601-1012), the pGEX4T-1-OFD1 plasmid (a gift from Dr. Andrew M. Fry) was used as a template to amplify the full-length or fragments of OFD1. Then, the amplified products were inserted into the pEGFP-C1 vector (Clontech), respectively. For (CCHCR1ΔN)-GFP construction, mRNA was extracted from RPE1 (CCHCR1ΔN) cells and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. Using this cDNA as a template, the CCHCR1ΔN sequence was amplified by PCR. Amplified DNA was subcloned into the mammalian expression vector pEGFP-N1 (Clontech). The GFP-PCM1 is a gift from Dr. Merdes [42]. All constructed plasmids were verified by DNA sequencing.

Cell culture

HeLa cells (human cervical carcinoma cell) were sourced from the China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC) and obtained through Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd, a distributor in Wuhan, China (cat. no. CL-0101). Human Telomerase Reverse Transcriptase-immortalized Retinal Pigment Epithelial (hTERT-RPE1) cells were obtained from EDITGENE in Guangzhou, China (cat. no. EDC2024064-W064). HeLa and RPE1 cells were obtained from the China Center for Type Culture Collection (CTCC). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 100 units/ml penicillin with 100 mg/ml streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment. For serum starvation, full medium-cultured RPE1 cells were changed to DMEM only for 24 h.

Transfection

For plasmid transfections, cells were seeded into culture dishes 24 h in advance to achieve a confluence of 80–90% during the day of transfection. Then, for each 35-mm culture dish, 2 μg of plasmid DNA and 4 μl of Lipofectamine 3000 were mixed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The mixture was added to the cells, which were subsequently cultured for an additional 24–48 h. For siRNA transfection, cells were seeded into culture dishes 24 h in advance to achieve a confluence of 60–80% during the time of transfection. Then, for each 35-mm culture dish, a mixture of 120 pmol of siRNA and 6 μl of Lipofectamine 3000 was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions, the mixture was added to cells, and the cells were cultured for the next 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were digested and seeded at a 1:3 ratio, and cultured for another 24 h, and then transfected again. Subsequent operations were performed 24–48 h following the second transfection.

(Co)-immunoprecipitation (IP)

For each reaction, 2 × 106 cells were lysed with 1 ml of RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP40, and protease inhibitor cocktail) and incubated on ice for 30 min. Lysates were centrifuged at 16,000g for 30 min at 4 °C. Then, 800 μl of supernatant was combined with 2 μg of the corresponding antibody or 2 μg of nonspecific IgG of the same species as a control. The mixtures were rotated for 2 h at 4 °C, and then 30 μl of washed 50% protein A/G magnetic beads were added. For GFP- or mCherry-based immunoprecipitations, the supernatants were directly mixed with 30 μl of washed 50% anti-GFP(VHH) or anti-mCherry (VHH) agarose beads. The mixtures were continuously rotated at 4 °C overnight. After washing four times with lysis buffer, the beads were boiled in 40 μl of 2× sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) loading buffer for SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE).

GST pull-down assay

Each CCHCR1 fragment was subcloned into the pGEX-4T-1 vector. Recombinant plasmids and the pGEX-4T-1 vector were transformed into E. coli BL21, and recombinant protein expression was induced at 18 °C with 0.1 mM IPTG. The bacteria were harvested, resuspended, sonicated, and centrifuged at 12,000g for 30 min. Supernatants were incubated with glutathione magnetic beads for 2 h at 4 °C. After four washes with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), each tube of beads was added to lysates from 2 × 106 HeLa cells overexpressing either HA-EDC4 or GFP-OFD1. After incubation overnight at 4 °C, beads were washed with PBS four times again and boiled in 2× SDS loading buffer.

Acceptor bleaching fluorescence resonance energy transfer (AB-FRET) assay

HeLa cells were seeded onto cell culture coverslips to achieve a confluence of 80–90% at the time of transfection. Recombinant plasmids containing EGFP or mCherry in various combinations were co-transfected into cells using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent. After allowing the cells to incubate for 24 h, confocal imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM800 confocal microscope. The fluorescence intensity of GFP and mCherry was recorded three times before photobleaching. Then the mCherry signal in the region of interest (ROI) was subjected to continuous bleaching 20 times with 100% power of 543 nm light. The fluorescence intensity of GFP was measured seven times after bleaching. Each group of experiments was repeated three times, with 1–4 ROIs collected each time. The corresponding fluorescence intensities were averaged and normalized for plotting. After using a cross-correlation routine to align the pre-bleach and post-bleach images and calibrating the image background, the change in GFP fluorescence intensity before and after photobleaching was calculated using the formula EF = (GFPpost − GFPpre)/GFPpost × 100% to determine the FRET efficiency. The interaction strength between GFP and mCherry were quantitatively analyzed by calculating the FRET efficiency. To ensure the accuracy of the results, positive (CCHCR1-GFP-mCherry fusion protein) and negative (GFP and mCherry expressed separately) controls were set up for the experiment.

Western blotting (WB)

Proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE electrophoresis and transferred to a nitrocellulose filter membrane. The membrane was blocked with blocking buffer (5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) containing 0.1% Tween 20) for 1 h, and then probed with primary antibody and diluted in the blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C. After washing four times with Tris-buffered saline with Tween® 20 (TBS-T), the membrane was incubated in horseradish peroxidase-conjugated mouse or rabbit secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. After washing four times with TBS-T, the membrane was developed with enhanced chemiluminescent substrate and exposed using a chemiluminescent imaging system.

Immunofluorescence microscopy

Cells grown on coverslips were fixed with cold methanol at −20 °C for 10 min. For GFP- or mCherry-transfected cells, 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) was used first for 15 min at room temperature and then fixed in −20 °C methanol for 10 min. After being blocked with blocking buffer, cells were in turn probed with primary and secondary antibodies coupled to Alexa Fluor 488, Alexa Fluor 555, or Alexa Fluor 647. To stain DNA, cells were incubated with 1 μg/ml 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 10 min. Fluorescence images were collected with a Carl Zeiss LSM800 confocal laser scanning microscope and processed using ImageJ software when necessary.

Immunofluorescence-based quantification of protein levels at centrosomes

The detailed protocol for quantifying changes in protein expression levels at the centrosome has been described by Majumder and Fisk [43]. Briefly, fixed and immunofluorescent double-labeled cell slides were imaged using a 63× oil immersion objective (with a 1.4 numerical aperture) on an LSM800 confocal microscope. Images of different samples were acquired for different fluorophores along the Z-axis with the same exposure time, and the total intensity projection of each image stack was obtained. In cells where the distance between two centrosomes is less than 2 µm, a small square (typically 20–30 pixels per side) is drawn around each centrosome and the selected area is marked. Subsequently, a larger square (typically 24–35 pixels per side) is drawn around the first square and the selected area of the large square is marked. The area (A) and total fluorescence intensity (F) of each fluorophore in each box (S for small box and L for large box) are then obtained. The background-corrected fluorescence intensity of each fluorophore is analyzed using the formula described by Howell et al.: F = FS − [(FL − FS) × AS/(AL − AS)]. The normalized fluorescence intensity of the protein of interest is obtained by calculating the ratio of the background-corrected fluorescence intensity of its fluorophore to that of the fluorophore used for the chosen internal standard (γ-tubulin in this case). When analyzing cells with well-separated centrosomes (a distance greater than 2 µm between the two centrosomes), each centrosome is analyzed individually by drawing two separate sets of boxes. The background-corrected fluorescence intensities of each fluorophore for the two centrosomes are calculated according to the previously described method and then summed to obtain the total centrosomal level of each protein within the cell. The normalized intensity of the protein of interest is then obtained by calculating the ratio of its total centrosomal intensity to the total centrosomal intensity of the internal standard.

CRISPR–Cas9 deletion of CCHCR1 N-terminal fragments in RPE1 cells

A set of gRNAs targeting exon2 of the CCHCR1 gene were subcloned into lentiCRISPR version 2 vector. These plasmids were transfected into RPE1 cells, and cells were then selected with growth medium containing 2.0 μg/ml puromycin. After 2 weeks, puromycin-resistant clones were separated, and the genomic DNA was extracted for PCR and sequencing. The successfully knocked-out clones were further cultured and expanded.

Statistical analysis

Comparison of the rates of ciliogenesis were analyzed using chi-squared test and student's t-test was used to compare cilia length. Normalized intensity of centrosomal proteins (based on immunofluorescence) were analyzed using non-parametric tests due to non-normal data distribution. Kruskal-Wallis H test was employed for comparisons across three or more experimental groups, while Mann-Whitney U test was used for pairwise comparisons between two groups. For AB-FRET and WB results, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was utilized and the Dunnett's t-test was used for further comparison between the control group and experimental groups. All statistical analysis were carried out in SPSS 19.0 and GraphPad Prism8. The difference was considered statistically significant with P < 0.05. Statistical results are described in detail in the figure legends.

Results

CCHCR1 interacts with OFD1 via its C-terminal coiled-coil domains

In previous studies, Ling et al. and we identified several candidate CCHCR1 binding partners that are primarily categorized into two groups, one is P-body-associated proteins, such as EDC4, DCP1A, and XRN1, and the other is centrosome-associated proteins, including CEP131, SPAG5, PCM1, and CEP72 [39]. We also identified CCHCR1 binding proteins by utilizing the biotin identification (BioID) technique in conjunction with mass spectrometry [41]. Upon comparing our findings with those of Ling et al., we found that the centrosomal protein OFD1 only appeared in our results, with a relatively high score (Table 1). To ascertain the association of CCHCR1 with OFD1, co-immunoprecipitation experiments were conducted with lysates of HeLa cells using CCHCR1 and OFD1 antibodies, individually. In this study, we used the CCHCR1 polyclonal antibody from Proteintech instead of the CCHCR1 rabbit polyclonal antibody from Santa Cruz, which was previously used, because the latter was discontinued [41]. Western blotting demonstrated that the antibody from Proteintech recognized a ~90 kDa band in the lysate of HeLa cells, but not in the lysate of CCHCR1 knock out (KO) HeLa cells, confirming the specificity of this antibody (Supplementary Fig. S1). The results showed that CCHCR1 and OFD1 can be co-immunoprecipitated (Fig. 1a). Next, we examined whether CCHCR1 and OFD1 colocalize. When the expression level of the transfected CCHCR1-mCherry was low, its signal was primarily concentrated in the centrosome and its surrounding areas, showing co-localization with OFD1 (Fig. 1b). This co-localization also existed between CCHCR1 and other pericentriolar satellite proteins, such as PCM1 and CEP131 (Supplementary Fig. S2). To determine the interacting regions between CCHCR1 and OFD1, GST pull down assays were performed. Firstly, three GST-tagged CCHCR1 truncation constructs (residues 1–340, 341–440, and 441–782), each containing a coiled-coil domain, were purified with glutathione beads, then mixed with exacts from HeLa cells overexpressing GFP-OFD1. The results showed that aa 441–782, which contained the C-terminal coiled-coil domain of CCHCR1 pulled down OFD1 (Fig. 1c). We also confirmed that the N-terminus of CCHCR1 can bind to EDC4 (Fig. 1c). Accordingly, when CCHCR1 domains 1–340 and 441–782 were fused with GFP and transfected into HeLa cells separately, both exhibited punctate structures, however, the former accumulated with P-body components, such as EDC4, DCP1A, and DDX6, while the latter accumulated with centriolar satellites, such as OFD1 and PCM1 (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. S3). These results indicate that CCHCR1 binds to the P-body or centrosome through different domains at its N-terminus and C-terminus, respectively. To define the region in OFD1 required to interact with CCHCR1, GFP-tagged OFD1 fragments were co-expressed with CCHCR1-Cherry in HeLa cells and precipitated with GFP antibody (Fig. 1e). The results showed that the N-terminal region of OFD1 (aa 1–132), which contains the LiSH motif, binds to CCHCR1, while the C-terminus (aa 601–1012) also exhibits a weak interaction (Fig. 1f). When co-expressed in HeLa cells, GFP-OFD1 (aa 1–132) and CCHCR1-Cherry not only accumulated around the centrosome but also dispersed throughout the cell in a granular form, and exhibited extensive co-localization, whereas aggregates of GFP-OFD1 (aa 133–600) separated from that of CCHCR1-Cherry (Fig. 1g). Although the GFP-OFD1 (aa 601–1012) displayed a small number of dot-like patterns that overlapped with that of CCHCR1-Cherry, it was mainly diffusely distributed throughout the cytoplasm and nucleus (Fig. 1g). These results indicate that CCHCR1 binds to the region containing the LiSH motif at the N-terminus of Ofd1 via the third coiled-coil domain. If the protein-binding partners under detection are close enough to each other (i.e., within a distance of 1–10 nm), the energy from the GFP FRET donor can be transferred to the mCherry FRET acceptor. Centrosomal localization of CCHCR1 is dependent on OFD1 and PCM1

Next, we investigated whether CCHCR1 and OFD1 influenced each other’s localization at the centrosome. In CCHCR1 knockout (KO) HeLa cells, no significant change was observed in the localization of OFD1 and PCM1 at the centrosome (Fig. 3a and b). In the previous study, we found that depletion of PCM1 reduces the CCHCR1 signal at the centrosome, although residual signal persists [41]. It has also been reported that depletion of PCM1 eliminates the OFD1 signal in the pericentriolar satellite, but the centriolar signal was conversely enhanced [22]. Therefore, we compared the effects of depleting PCM1 or OFD1 on CCHCR1 localization at the centrosome. Western blotting confirmed the efficiency of RNA interference (Fig. 3c). When PCM1 or OFD1 was individually depleted, CCHCR1 still showed residual signals at the centriole (Fig. 3d and e). Co-staining with the mother centriole marker CEP164 indicates that these residual CCHCR1 signals mainly reside in the mother centriole (Fig. 3f) [44]. We observed that depletion of OFD1 increased the protein level of PCM1 (Fig. 3c), suggesting that PCM1 may functionally complement OFD1 as a compensatory mechanism. To verify this, we performed a double depletion of OFD1 and PCM1 in HeLa cells. Indeed, upon simultaneous depletion of both proteins, the centrosomal signal intensity of CCHCR1 was further reduced (Fig. 3d–f). We also observed that P-body component EDC4 was present at the centrosome in HeLa cells (Fig. 4a, left panel). To further validate the bridging role of CCHCR1, we transfected CCHCR1-mCherry or an empty mCherry vector into CCHCR1 knockout (KO) HeLa cells and performed co-immunoprecipitation experiments using an OFD1 antibody, with rabbit IgG serving as a negative control. The results demonstrated that OFD1 co-precipitated with EDC4 only in lysates expressing CCHCR1-mCherry (Fig. 6d). Consistent with this, immunofluorescence analysis revealed that in CCHCR1 KO cells expressing only mCherry, EDC4 and OFD1 localized to P-bodies and the centrosome, respectively, with no overlap (Fig. 6e, upper panel). When CCHCR1-mCherry was expressed at low levels, its signal predominantly localized to the centrosome, co-localizing with OFD1, and EDC4 also accumulated at the centrosome (Fig. 6e, middle panel). In contrast, when CCHCR1-mCherry was expressed at higher levels, its signal not only localized to the centrosome but also formed punctate aggregates in the cytoplasm, where it co-localized with EDC4. Notably, OFD1 also accumulated in these cytoplasmic aggregates (Fig. 6e, lower panel).

Similar interactions were observed between other P-body components and centriolar satellite proteins, such as EDC4 and PCM1, EDC4 and CEP131, as well as DDX6 and OFD1, PCM1, and CEP131, further supporting the role of CCHCR1 as a bridge between P-bodies and centriolar satellites (Fig. 6f–h). These findings suggest that endogenous CCHCR1 is primarily localized to the centrosome, where it recruits P-body components. However, when CCHCR1 is overexpressed, it can also recruit centriolar satellite proteins to P-bodies.

We have previously reported that CCHCR1 plays a role in centrosome duplication [41]. Given that CCHCR1 recruits P-bodies to the centrosome, we sought to determine whether P-bodies are also involved in centrosome duplication. However, our findings indicate that knockdown of either DDX6 or EDC4 does not significantly alter the cell cycle (data not shown). Discussion

As a centrosome and P-body localized protein, we previously suggested that CCHCR1 plays an important role in microtubule organization and mitosis. However, its correlations with P-body remains elusive [40, 41]. Currently, it is only known that CCHCR1 localizes to the P-body by interacting with EDC4. So far, we have not found any effect of CCHCR1 on the formation of P-bodies, as CCHCR1 KO HeLa cells have a similar number of P-bodies compared with that of wild-type cells, and their response to arsenite is also similar (our unpublished data). This suggests that CCHCR1 is not involved in maintaining the structure of the P-body. Further research is required to determine whether it affects the function of P-bodies, especially in terms of mRNA processing. It is worth noting that astrin, as a centrosomal protein that interacts with CCHCR1, participates in the transport of raptor to the stress granules. Given that numerous P-body components are also involved in the formation and function of stress granules, it is worth investigating whether CCHCR1 plays roles in stress granules or other inflammatory bodies [50, 51]. The significant finding of this study is that CCHCR1 recruits P-body proteins to the centrosome through its interaction with EDC4. Although there have been occasional reports of P-body components appearing in the centrosome or basal body in the past, to our knowledge, this study is the first to comprehensively reveal how the main components of the P-body are recognized and targeted to the centrosome [34, 35]. We show that the coiled-coil domains at the N-terminus and C-terminus of CCHCR1 function as two arms, binding to EDC4 and OFD1/PCM1, respectively, thereby recruiting P-body components to the centrosome (Fig. 9). What remains to be further confirmed is whether the aggregation of these P-body components to the centrosome is merely a moonlighting function or whether they may bind with specific mRNA, thereby acting as complete P-bodies to communicate with the centrosomes. As more and more mRNAs are found and exhibit regulatory functions on the centrosome, the latter is more noteworthy and will be the focus of our next work [52].Fig. 9Schematic diagram illustrating CCHCR1 recruitment of P-body components at the centrosome and participating in cilia development. a OFD1 and PCM1 determine the centrosomal localization of CCHCR1, which subsequently recruits P-body components to the centrosome through interacting with EDC4, ultimately promoting cilia formation. b When CCHCR1 is depleted, the localization of OFD1 and PCM1 remains unchanged, but P-body proteins are no longer recruited to the centrosome, resulting in hindrance of cilia development

Almost all centriolar satellite proteins, including OFD1, CEP131, and PCM1, play crucial roles in cilia development. PCM1 is involved in both the removal of CP110 and the docking of the mother centriole with vesicles [24, 31]. Recruitment of CP110 to the satellites for its removal from the mother centriole may depend on CEP290, while the localization of CEP290 in the basal body relies on the CEP131-CEP162 module [28]. In addition, CEP131 also interacts with BBS4, jointly regulating ciliary trafficking of the BBSome [29]. The effect of OFD1 on ciliogenesis is rather complex, as it has two pools: the centriole and the centriole satellite. Metabolic elimination of OFD1 from the centriole satellite via autophagy or ubiquitination may be one of the prerequisites for initiating cilia formation [19, 20, 53]. After treating RPE1 cells with serum-free medium, we indeed observed a reduction of OFD1 at the centriole satellites, accompanied by an enhancement of centriole localization. However, knockdown of OFD1 in RPE1 cells resulted in a decrease in the number of cilia. In the mother centriole, OFD1 promotes the recruitment of distal appendages, which is crucial for the docking of the mother centriole to the cellular membrane and its transition to the basal body, as well as for cilia generation [18, 21, 22, 49]. In this study, we demonstrate that OFD1 recruits P-body proteins to the centriole through CCHCR1, a process that is also crucial for cilia formation. Considering that primary cilia mediate multiple signaling pathways, including Shh, Wnt, and PDGFRα, it is worth further investigating whether these signaling pathways regulate the formation and function of intracellular P-bodies or stress granules via an OFD1/PCM1-CCHCR1-EDC4 pathway.

CCHCR1 has been previously reported to be associated with diseases such as psoriasis and diabetes, but the exact mechanism is far from being elucidated [36–38, 54, 55]. Interestingly, both primary cilia and the signaling pathways they are involved in appear to play roles in the pathological processes of these two diseases [56, 57]. Compared with normal skin samples, an increase in ciliated epidermis has been observed in psoriatic skin samples [56]. It is worth noting that upon insulin stimulation, insulin receptors can specifically accumulate on the cilia of pancreatic B cells. Especially, when BBS4 or OFD1 are depleted from islet B cells, insulin receptor-dependent signaling is impaired, manifesting as reduced phosphorylation of downstream nodes (Akt kinase and p55/p85 regulatory subunits of phosphatidylinositol-3 kinase) [58]. Therefore, an in-depth investigation into the effects of CCHCR1 on cilia development will undoubtedly deepen our understanding of the pathological processes underlying psoriasis and diabetes.
