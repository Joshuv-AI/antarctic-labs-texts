Glutaminase activity maintains NK cell cytotoxicity through metabolic regulation of effector function
Authors: M. Jedlička, T. Feglarová, V. Švubová, E. Mašínová, V. Graman, K. Kuglerová, J. Szabová, F. Jahan, M. Korhonen, K. Kuželová, M. Hortová-Kohoutková, J. Frič
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNatural killer (NK) cells are responsible for monitoring and eliminating malignant or virus-infected cells. To become activated, NK cells must upregulate oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis to meet the high energetic demands associated with cytotoxic and effector functions. While glutamine can also fuel the tricarboxylic acid cycle through its conversion to alpha-ketoglutarate, the precise role of this pathway in NK-cell cytotoxic activity is unclear.ResultsTo investigate NK-cell dependency on glutamine, we selectively inhibited kidney-type glutaminase to prevent glutamine metabolism. We analysed the metabolism and cytotoxicity of expanded primary NK cells, treated or not with glutaminase inhibitor. Glutaminase inhibition significantly reduced oxidative phosphorylation and led to a significant decrease in NK cell cytotoxic function. Furthermore, glutaminase inhibition reduced protein synthesis in activated NK cells. Meanwhile, supplementation with alpha-ketoglutarate rescued both the metabolic and cytotoxic capacities of primary expanded NK cells.ConclusionsOur findings highlight the importance of glutaminase activity in supporting NK cell respiratory metabolism and cytotoxic function, and the need for caution when combining glutaminase inhibitors with NK cell-based therapies.

Background

Natural killer (NK) cells are a key immune subset responsible for tumour cell surveillance. Their unique balance of activating and inhibitory receptors enables them to eliminate target cells without prior sensitisation. Following cytokine stimulation (e.g. IL-15), NK cells translate mRNA into effector molecules such as granzyme B and perforin, and load these into cytotoxic granules [1].These preformed granules are subsequently released upon target recognition, accompanied by increased surface expression of lysosomal-associated membrane protein-1 (LAMP-1; CD107a), a marker correlated with degranulation levels [2]. Moreover, NK cell activation by target cells triggers the generation of new granules [3], supporting serial killing—the ability of one NK cell to eliminate multiple target cells in succession [4]. Immunometabolism plays a crucial role in mediating these functions: under steady state conditions, NK cells primarily rely on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) but during activation, they must upregulate both OXPHOS and glycolysis to meet the high energetic demands associated with cytotoxic responses [5].

NK cell metabolism could be regulated by various stimuli depending on nutrient abundance and the target present [6–9]. For example, the research done by Schimmer et al. have shown that murine NK cells ability to upregulate fatty acid metabolism is crucial for response to retroviral infection [10]. On the other hand, the lipid accumulation in human and murine NK cells led to limited antitumor response [11]. Moreover, in murine model the amino acid abundance showed indispensable role for NK cell activation with the glutamine presence as a key [12].

Glutamine also contributes to the NK-cell energy supply by supporting the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle through its conversion to alpha-ketoglutarate (α-KG) [13], and as such, has been implicated in regulating NK cell effector functions [12]. The precise role of glutamine in regulating primary NK-cell cytotoxic function, however, remains unclear. Interestingly, many tumour cells exhibit high glutamine dependency, and as such, components of the glutamine pathway have emerged as potential targets in cancer therapy [14–17]. NK cells have gained attention for their potential use in cell-based immunotherapies against various tumours. Indeed, the efficacy and targeting of immunotherapeutic NK cells have been studied for decades [18], but NK cell-based therapies have yet to successfully replicate promising experimental outcomes in large-scale healthcare protocols [19, 20]. Large numbers of NK cells required for adoptive immunotherapy in acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) patients are typically obtained through in vitro expansion [21, 22]. However, one potential barrier to successful clinical application lies in the discrepancy between the nutrient-rich conditions used for NK cell expansion in vitro, and the hostile nutrient-depleted tumour microenvironment encountered in vivo in patients [23, 24]. Improving our understanding of the key metabolic pathways driving primary NK-cell effector function, and their regulation may help explain the limited success of current NK cell-based immunotherapies. The current knowledge points out the competition for nutrients and abundance of tumour produced metabolites in the tumour microenvironment as one of the causes of immune cell inhibition [8, 25].

Here, we hypothesised that blocking the glutamine pathway by inhibiting kidney-type glutaminase (GLS1) would impair the cytotoxic properties of immunotherapeutic NK cells. To test this hypothesis, we expanded human primary NK cells and treated them with GLS1 inhibitor in order to see the role of GLS1 in metabolism and its influence on cytolytic functions. Our findings reveal that GLS1 activity supports NK cell metabolism and cytotoxicity, and that glutaminase inhibition uncouples degranulation from killing while impairing protein synthesis.

Materials and methods

Cultivation of K562

K562 cells (chronic myeloid leukaemia) (ATCC; Manassas, Virginia, USA) were routinely cultured in IMDM (Gibco; Waltham, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biosera, Cholet, France), 1% GlutaMAX (Gibco; Waltham, USA), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco).

NK cell isolation, expansion and stimulation

Buffy coats from anonymized healthy donors were purchased from the Transfusion Center of the Institute of Hematology and Blood Transfusion (Prague, Czechia) and General University Hospital in Prague (Prague, Czechia). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated via density gradient centrifugation using Lymphoprep™ (STEMCELL Technologies; Vancouver, Canada), followed by CD3⁻CD56⁺ negative magnetic separation using an autoMACS® Pro Separator (Miltenyi Biotec; Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). NK cells were expanded using irradiated K562 cells (100 Gy, 15 min), added at a 1:5 ratio in X-VIVO™ 20 medium with Gentamicin and Phenol Red (Lonza; Basel, Switzerland), supplemented with 5% human serum (Capricorn; Ebsdorfergrund, Germany). NK cells were seeded (0.2 × 10⁶ cells/ml) with irradiated K562 cells (1 × 10⁶ cells/ml) in expansion medium containing IL-2 (1000 IU/ml) and IL-15 (20 ng/ml) (both from R&D; Minneapolis, USA). From day 5 onward, fresh medium was added every second day to support continued cell expansion for 14 days.

Primary human expanded NK cells (hereafter referred to as NK cells) were treated with the kidney-type glutaminase (GLS1) inhibitor Bis-2-(5-phenylacetamido-1,3,4-thiadiazol-2-yl)ethyl sulfide (BPTES) (20 µM, Sigma-Aldrich; Cat. no. SML0601, Burlington, Massachusetts, USA) [26] for 18 h prior to assays, unless otherwise stated. For rescue experiments, NK cells were co-treated with BPTES (20 µM) and dimethyl alpha-ketoglutarate (DMK; 3.5 mM Sigma-Aldrich; Cat. No. 349631) [27] for 18 h prior to assays.

A total of 5 × 10⁵ expanded NK cells were labelled for 30 min in 50 µL PBS containing LIVE/DEAD™ Fixable Blue Dead Cell Stain (1:100 dilution; Invitrogen, Waltham, USA). For surface marker staining, cells were incubated in 50 µL FACS buffer (PBS with 0.2% FBS and 2 nmol/L EDTA) for 30 min in the dark at room temperature with the antibodies listed in Table 1.

SCENITH and flow cytometry analysis

Single Cell Energetic metabolism by profiling Translation inhibition (SCENITH™) purchased from GammaOmincs (Marseille, France) was performed as previously described [28] and according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, untreated and treated NK cells were incubated with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; control), 2-deoxy-glucose (2DG; 100 mmol/L), oligomycin (O; 1µmol/L), a combination of 2DG and oligomycin (DGO), or Harringtonine (H; 2 µg/mL). Puromycin was then added directly to the cells (10 µg/mL) without washing, and cells were incubated for 45 min at 37 °C.

Following incubation, cells were washed with cold PBS and harvested by incubation with PBS containing 5 mmol/L EDTA for 10 min at 4 °C. Fc receptor blockade (BD Biosciences for human cells) and fixable viability dye (eBioscience) were applied for 15 min at 4 °C in the dark. Cells were then washed and stained with a surface antibody mix comprising anti-CD56-APC Fire 750 (1:25), anti-CD16-BV650 (1:25), anti-CD107a-PE (1:200; BD Life Sciences), and anti-Puromycin-AF647 (GammaOmics), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, in PBS with 0.5% BSA and 0.02% sodium azide (PBA) for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark.

Data acquisition was performed using the Cytek Aurora flow cytometer, and data were processed and analysed using FlowJo software.

Calcein-AM release assay and serial killing assay

NK-cell cytotoxic activity against K562 cells was assessed by calcein-AM release assay. Calcein-AM (Thermo Fisher Scientific; C3100MP, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, K562 cells were resuspended in 1 ml of X-VIVO™ 20 medium without serum at a final concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cells/ml, incubated with 10 µM calcein-AM for 30 min at 37 °C with occasional shaking, and washed twice in complete culture medium. The cells were then rested for an additional 30 min at 37 °C.

Labelled K562 target cells were seeded in a 96-well round-bottom plate at a fixed density of 5,000 cells/well, and NK effector cells were added at various effector-to-target (E: T) ratios (ranging from 9:1 to 0.3:1) to a total volume of 200 µL per well. After a 4-hour incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, 65 µL of supernatant from each well was collected and transferred to a new plate. Calcein-AM release, indicating target cell lysis, was measured using a fluorescence reader (Tecan; Crailsheim, Germany; excitation/emission 494/517nm).

NK cell serial killing activity was assessed using repeated calcein-AM release assays. Briefly, cells were treated with BPTES 18 h before the assay and washed. Degranulation assay

Briefly, K562 cells were labelled with CellTrace™ Violet Cell Proliferation Kit, for flow cytometry (Invitrogen™, Waltham, Massachusetts, US) according to manufacturer protocol, counted and mixed with NK cells in 1:5 ratio (NK cells 2 × 105; K562 4 × 104) in 96 well plate with U bottom in complete media and incubated (37 °C, 5%CO2) for 1 h. After 1 h of incubation the anti-CD107a antibody was added and the cells were incubated for another 2 h in presence of antibody. After the incubation period, the cells were washed and stained for live/dead and cell surface markers (CD56, CD16) as described in section Flow cytometry. The antibodies used are listed in Table 1. The cells were than fixed (see the section Flow cytometry) and analysed on Cytek Aurora.

ELISA analysis

The concentrations of the cytolytic factors granzyme B (DY2906-05) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ; DY285B) in supernatants from BPTES-treated and control NK cells were measured by ELISA (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, Minnestota, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 384-well plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were coated overnight at room temperature with capture antibodies and washed twice with washing buffer before blocking for 1 h at room temperature. Samples and standards were diluted and incubated in the plate for 2 h at room temperature, followed by two washing steps. Detection antibodies were then added and incubated for a further 2 h, followed by two additional washes and the addition of streptavidin-HRP for 20 min. After two final washes, substrate solution was added and incubated for 20 min before stop solution was applied. Optical density was measured at 450 nm using a Tecan Spark plate reader (Tecan, Crailsheim, Germany).

Seahorse XF cell metabolism analysis

NK-cell respiration and energy metabolism were analysed using a Seahorse XFp (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, USA). The oxygen consumption rate served as an indicator of mitochondrial respiration, while the extracellular acidification rate served as an indicator of anaerobic glycolysis. NK cells (2 × 10⁵ cells/well) were seeded in poly-D-lysine-coated XF cell culture plates (Gibco; A3890401) in 50 µL of Seahorse assay medium (prepared according to the manufacturer’s protocol), briefly centrifuged (1 min at 200 g), and incubated for 25 min at 37 °C without CO₂ before the addition of 130 µL of assay medium. The Cell Mito Stress Test and Glycolytic Stress Test (Agilent) were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Seahorse data were corrected for well-to-well differences in cell number and analysed using Wave software (Agilent).

RNA isolation and RtPCR analysis

RNA was isolated from a minimum of 3 × 10⁶ cells. Cell pellets were resuspended in TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen; 15596018) and stored at − 80 °C. RNA extraction was performed using the Quick-RNA MagBead Kit (Zymo Research; R2132, Irvine, California, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription was carried out using SuperScript™ IV Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen; 18090200), also following the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative real-time PCR (rtPCR) was performed using a LightCycler480 system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) with the following TaqMan probes: GAPDH (Thermo Fisher; Hs02758991_g1), Granzyme B (Thermo Fisher; Hs00188051_m1), and interferon-gamma (Thermo Fisher; Hs00989291). Data were analysed by delta-delta CT method.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, Boston, USA). Data were tested for normality and are presented as the means ± SD and analysed by a paired and unpaired, one sample t-test or Willcoxon test. Statistical significance is indicated as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

Results

Glutaminase inhibition decreases OXPHOS in NK cells

To test their dependency on GLS1, we first expanded NK cells from healthy donor PBMCs for 14 days in the presence of irradiated K562 cells and IL-2 and IL-15 cytokines. We inhibited glutamine metabolism by adding a selective GLS1 inhibitor, BPTES, 18 h before the end of the culture period [14]. We then compared BPTES-treated versus untreated expanded NK cells by Mito Stress Test (which measures oxygen consumption following exposure to compounds that disrupt respiratory chain homeostasis).

Results of the Mito Stress Test showed reduced respiratory metabolism in BPTES-treated NK cells compared with controls (Fig. 1a), with significantly reduced basal respiration (Fig. 1b) and maximal respiration (Fig. 1c), and spare respiratory capacity (Fig. 1d). Thus, OXPHOS operates closer to its maximum capacity in BPTES-treated NK cells than in controls. These findings indicate glutamine usage contributes to oxidative metabolism in NK cells. We therefore investigated by SCENITH whether oxidation of amino acids is important for activated/degranulated CD107a + NK cells. Briefly, for SCENITH assay NK cells were mixed with target cells and stained for CD107a expression. For calculation of metabolic parameters, the cells were gated as follows (Fig. 1e) cell trace violet-, live, CD56 + CD16+, CD107a+. We noted significantly enhanced capacity of these cells to oxidise fatty and amino acids (Fig. 1f) as well as slightly lower glucose (Fig. 1g) and mitochondrial (Fig. 1h) dependence and slightly lower glycolytic capacity (Fig. 1i).

Glutaminase inhibition impairs NK cell-killing abilities

Active OXPHOS and glycolysis are essential for maintaining NK cell effector functions [5]. Based on our analysis of metabolism we further investigated whether NK cell cytotoxicity is influenced by glutaminase inhibition. To do so, we conducted a cytotoxic assay against K562 target cell line. As expected, BPTES treatment significantly reduced the cytotoxic capacity of NK cells, with a dose-dependent reduction with increasing concentrations of BPTES (Fig. 2a). Importantly, the observed decrease in killing capacity was not due to BPTES toxicity, as NK cell viability remained unaffected in the presence of the inhibitor (Fig. 2b). Since the most profound effect was observed with the presence of 20µM inhibitor, we further continued with this concentration. The highest effect of 20µM inhibitor was observed at low effector-to-target (E: T) (Fig. 2c).

Given that the therapeutic efficacy of NK cells relies on their sustained activity following adoptive transfer, we next assessed whether the effects of BPTES treatment persist over time. We treated NK cells with BPTES 18 h prior to the first cytotoxic assay (day 1), and then conducted three consecutive killing assays every 24 h, on days 2, 3 and 4. BPTES-treated NK cells exhibited a trend in reduced cytotoxic activity across all 4 days (Fig. 2d), indicating a lasting impairment of effector function following glutaminase inhibition. We thus conclude that glutaminase activity is necessary for immediate and probably also sustained NK-cell cytotoxic functions.

Limited release of effector molecules causes NK cell cytotoxicity impairment

Based on the impaired cytotoxicity of BPTES-treated NK cells observed in both the standard (Fig. 2c) and multiday (Fig. 2d) cytotoxicity assays, we next queried whether surface expression of selected activating, inhibitory, and senescence markers might be altered and collaborate on lower cytotoxicity [9]. We analysed the surface expression of selected markers in control and treated NK cells (Fig. 3a). While most markers showed no change in surface expression following BPTES treatment, we detected a significant increase in CD57 and a significant decrease in HLA-DR/DP/DQ expression. Although the role of CD57 in NK cell effector function is defined as pro-cytotoxic [29, 30], our results demonstrate decreased cytotoxicity in CD57high NK cells. On the other hand, decreased HLA-DR/DP/DQ expression—previously associated with functionally responsive NK cell phenotypes [31]—is consistent with the diminished cytotoxic activity observed (Fig. 2a, c). However, the changes in expression are probably very small in effects size and do not explain the observed phenotype by itself.

As surface marker expression alone did not fully explain the observed functional differences, we next assessed the release kinetics of the key effector molecules, Granzyme B and IFNγ [32]. We measured their secretion by ELISA at multiple time points following target cell engagement (5, 15, 30, 60, 120, and 180 min). Granzyme B release was higher in control NK cells from the earliest time point (Fig. 3b). Interestingly, gene expression analysis revealed no differences in granzyme B transcript levels between groups (Fig. 3c), suggesting a defect in granule release rather than transcriptional regulation.

Given the immunoregulatory role of NK cells, we also examined IFNγ secretion in control and BPTES-treated cells (Fig. 3d). IFNγ release was consistently lower in treated cells at all time points, with no differences in gene expression (Fig. 3e). These findings indicate that BPTES-treated NK cells exhibit impaired secretion of effector molecules following target cell recognition, ultimately contributing to their reduced cytotoxic capacity.

Glutaminase inhibition deregulates the NK-cell killing machinery

Given the impaired granzyme B release observed in BPTES-treated NK cells (Fig. 3b–c), we next investigated whether this defect reflected altered granule exocytosis or a failure to replenish granule content. To explore this, we performed a degranulation assay and monitored surface expression of the degranulation activity marker, CD107a (Fig. 4a). Despite their reduced killing ability, BPTES-treated cells exhibited slightly higher levels of degranulation than untreated cells, as indicated by increased surface expression of the degranulation activity marker CD107a, compared to control cells. Notably, BPTES treatment alone did not induce degranulation in the absence of target cell engagement (Fig. 4b). These findings confirm that elevated degranulation does not directly correlate with enhanced cytotoxicity (Fig. 2a) [2].

Given the reduced granzyme B release into the supernatant in BPTES-treated cells, we also examined intracellular granzyme B levels in control and treated NK cells before (spontaneous) and after (activated) exposure to target cells (Fig. 4c). Prior to activation, spontaneous granzyme B levels were similar between groups; however, following target cell engagement, intracellular granzyme B levels were significantly lower in BPTES-treated NK cells (Fig. 4d), even though the decrease was rather small.

These findings, along with the discrepancy between increased degranulation and reduced granzyme B in the supernatant, suggested a defect in granule replenishment rather than release alone. To investigate this possibility, we assessed the capacity of activated NK cells to synthesise proteins (Fig. 4e). Single-cell analysis of puromycin incorporation revealed a significantly marked reduction in protein synthesis in BPTES-treated NK cells following activation (Fig. 4f). Together, glutaminase inhibition led to increased degranulation (1,18x) but decreased (0,8x) intracellular granzyme B levels and impaired protein synthesis (0,6x) in activated NK cells, implying that these cells are unable to effectively replenish their pool of cytotoxic granules.

α-KG improves respiratory metabolism and rescues the cytotoxic properties of BPTES-treated NK cells

In our final analyses, we wanted to determine whether the impaired cytotoxicity of BPTES-treated NK cells was due to reduced conversion of glutamine to glutamate and α-KG. We performed a rescue experiment by administering dimethyl α-KG (DMK; 3.5 mM) [27] concurrently with BPTES. Results of the Mito Stress Test revealed a slight increase in oxidative respiration in NK cells treated with both compounds compared to those treated with BPTES alone (Fig. 5a). Given that α-KG enters the TCA cycle downstream of glutaminase, we expected DMK supplementation to restore basal mitochondrial respiration. Unexpectedly, basal respiration in the BPTES + DMK group, however, was lower than in BPTES-only cells (Fig. 5b). However, the α-KG might contribute also to NK cell homeostasis through its antioxidative functions and usage as a precursor for glutamate and glutathione, which could explain the observed decrease [33, 34]. Despite this, maximal respiration (Fig. 5c) and spare respiratory capacity (Fig. 5d) showed a tendency to increase, indicating partial restoration of mitochondrial function following DMK supplementation.

Finally, cytotoxicity assays demonstrated that BPTES + DMK-treated NK cells (at a 9:1 effector-to-target ratio) exhibited killing capacities comparable to control cells and significantly higher than BPTES-treated cells alone (Fig. 5e). These findings suggest that reduced α-KG availability contributes to the decline in respiratory metabolism and effector function observed in glutaminase-inhibited NK cells.

Discussion

Glutamine plays a vital role in NK cell homeostasis, but its contribution to effector functions in primary, in vitro expanded NK cells is unclear. Here we tested whether GLS1 inhibition influences NK-cell metabolism and cytolytic activity. We found that glutaminase inhibition alters both the metabolic profile and cytotoxic capacity of these cells. Possible dependence of NK cells on GLS1 was also underscored by the enhanced capacity of activated and degranulated cells to oxidise fatty and amino acids. Notably, even low concentrations of BPTES (5 µM) impaired the cytotoxic function of expanded NK cells, while higher concentrations (20 µM) had the most pronounced effect at low effector-to-target ratios, suggesting a disruption in serial killing ability. These findings are of particular relevance given the growing interest in glutamine pathway inhibitors as anticancer agents—including in AML [26, 35]—and the parallel development of NK cells as potential off-the-shelf products for cell-based immunotherapy [36]. Our results, however, raise important concerns regarding the concurrent use of NK cell-based therapies with glutaminase inhibitors. As glutaminolysis is essential for sustaining elevated levels of OXPHOS, such treatment combinations may compromise therapeutic efficacy.

The impaired cytotoxicity observed in BPTES-treated NK cells prompted us to examine their activating and inhibitory surface markers. Interestingly, we detected an increase in CD57 expression following glutaminase inhibition. CD57 is a well-established marker of proliferative senescence in CD8⁺ T cells [37], and in NK cells, its expression has been associated with reduced proliferative capacity [38]. CD57 upregulation is also linked to NK cell maturation [29, 30] and the presence of CD57⁺ NK cells has been correlated with improved outcomes in several cancers [30]. Despite this, the BPTES-treated NK cells displayed poorer cytotoxic performance than control cells, even though that CD57 expression would imply the opposite. This suggests that, in the context of glutaminase inhibition, CD57 upregulation may reflect altered differentiation or stress responses rather than functional maturity.

Flow cytometry analysis showed no significant differences in the expression of other activating or inhibitory receptors between control and BPTES-treated NK cells, suggesting that both populations retained the potential to respond to target cells. BPTES-treated NK cells, however, exhibited impaired release of effector molecules, despite no reduction in granzyme B or IFNγ gene expression. This finding points to a possible defect in granule exocytosis rather than transcriptional regulation. The in vitro time-lapse imaging would be an option to prove the defect in granule exocytosis [39]. As the K562 target cell line does not express Fas ligand (CD95L) nor is sensitive to TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL)-induced apoptosis, the cytotoxicity observed in our assays relies exclusively on the granzyme/perforin pathway, ruling out death receptor-mediated killing. NK cells are typically capable of killing targets following minimal degranulation events—approximately 10% of their granule content [39]. BPTES-treated cells, however, displayed increased CD107a expression upon target engagement, indicating frequent degranulation but ineffective killing. This observation shows that CD107a expression alone does not reliably correlate with killing efficiency. The results clearly show that direct measurement of live or dead target cells is essential in the determination of killing efficiency by cytotoxic cells. Ineffective killing may also delay detachment from target cells, potentially impairing subsequent serial killing [40].

This variability, together with the observed defects in IFNγ production, points to a broader impact of metabolic disruption on both cytotoxic and cytokine effector functions in NK cells. In parallel, elevated OXPHOS and glycolysis are essential for IFNγ production in both murine and primary human NK cells [41, 42] consistent with our observation that BPTES-treated NK cells, which exhibited reduced metabolic activity, also produced less IFNγ. Interestingly, in murine NK cells, BPTES treatment did not affect IFNγ production, possibly because their metabolism was less affected by glutaminase inhibition [12]. An alternative explanation for the reduced secretion of effector molecules—such as granzyme B and IFNγ—despite unchanged gene expression, is a limited capacity for de novo protein synthesis. In addition, single-cell analysis revealed comparable intracellular granzyme B levels between untreated and BPTES-treated NK cells prior to activation, likely due to the 13-day expansion period before BPTES administration. Following activation, however, intracellular granzyme B levels declined more substantially in BPTES-treated cells. This contrast—reduced granzyme B secretion into the supernatant alongside greater intracellular depletion upon activation—suggests a defect in the protein synthesis machinery necessary for de novo granule replenishment.

To enable rapid cytotoxic responses, NK cells maintain a reservoir of preformed granules that are quickly mobilised upon activation. These cells must also rapidly synthesise new granules to replenish their stores during ongoing killing [3]. Our flow cytometry single-cell puromycin incorporation assays revealed reduced protein synthesis in activated BPTES-treated NK cells, suggesting that these cells are unable to replenish their cytotoxic granules effectively, thereby compromising their killing efficiency. This data plays a role in the context of adoptive transfer. The immunotherapeutic NK cells might be able to kill target cells in basic in vitro assays, however their ability to kill for a longer time in patients might be reduced because of TME caused inhibition [23].

Glutaminase catalyses the conversion of glutamine to glutamate, which can be further metabolised to α-KG and enter the TCA cycle. We showed that glutaminase inhibition reduces OXPHOS, indicating that glutamine contributes to TCA cycle activity in NK cells. The extent of this contribution, however, seems to be context-dependent. In murine NK cells, glutamine provides only a minor contribution to TCA cycle function and energy production [12]. By contrast, murine decidual NK cells rely on glutamine for OXPHOS, and downstream metabolites such as α-KG have a regulatory role in their activity [43]. Our findings demonstrate that α-KG supplementation partially rescues OXPHOS in NK cells and significantly restores their cytotoxic function at a 9:1 effector-to-target ratio. The observed decrease in basal respiration after the α-KG supplementation is probably linked to redox homeostasis, since the α-KG might be used also for glutamate and glutathione synthesis [34]. Contrary to our results, a previous mouse study reported no impact of BPTES-mediated glutaminase inhibition on OXPHOS or effector function [12]. In that model, murine NK cells were expanded for only 6 days with IL-15 and preactivated with IL-2 and IL-12 prior to analysis. We thus propose that IL-12 pre-activation served as a dominant stimulus, maintaining the cytotoxic phenotype despite BPTES treatment [44]. Taken together, our study provides new insights into the role of glutamine metabolism in regulating the cytotoxic function of expanded primary human NK cells. A key strength of our study lies in a use of human primary NK cells. However, our data are limited by a strictly in vitro approach. Future research should investigate whether the human primary NK cell effector functions rely on GLS1 function in vivo and focus on the signalling interplay influenced by α-KG.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings identify GLS1 activity as a critical metabolic regulator of NK cell cytotoxicity, supporting both sustained oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and effector protein synthesis. Given the increasing interest in glutaminase inhibitors for cancer treatment, including AML [15, 16, 45], our results highlight a potential risk of combining such agents with NK cell-based therapies. These findings highlight the importance of evaluating metabolic compatibility when designing combinatorial immunotherapeutic strategies.
