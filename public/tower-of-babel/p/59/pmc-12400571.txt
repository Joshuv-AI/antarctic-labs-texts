Assessment of maternal age along with other risk factors for childhood disabilities: a cross-sectional study in thirty-eight countries
Authors: Efty Islam Arpon, Sujan Kumar Naha, Sazzad Hossain Mithu, Jamal Uddin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildhood disability can have a negative effect on children’s general productivity and quality of life. It is a significant social and economic issue worldwide. This study’s primary goal is to examine the effect of maternal age on child disability in two groups: under-five (2 to 4 years) children and children between 5 and 17 years of age.MethodsThis research utilizes the most recent multiple indicator cluster survey (MICS) datasets of thirty-eight countries surveyed between 2015 and 2024. The survey sample was chosen with two-stage stratified cluster sampling. We identified 3218 disabled children out of 51,547 observations and 72,126 disabled children out of 446,716 observations from the groups of 2 to 4 years and 5 to 17 years, respectively. The outcome variable was child disability, which was binary. The study utilized bivariate analysis (chi-square) to examine the prevalence of child disability across various countries, with a focus on maternal age.ResultsIn the dataset, approximately 18.4% of children were found to be disabled among all children under 18 years of age. 7% of 2–4-year-olds and 19.75% of 5–17-year-olds had some form of disability. Compared with mothers aged 21–34 years (18.3%), mothers who gave birth at ages under 20 years and over 35 years had a greater percentage of disabled children (20.6% and 18.5%, respectively). Central Asia had the highest percentage of disability (24.0%), and Oceania and North America had 16.4% disabled children.ConclusionPresent findings show that, to lessen the total burden of childhood disability, maternal age at birth must be considered. Our research indicates that a mother’s optimal window for childbearing is between the ages of 20 and 34.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-05991-0.Keywords: Child disability, Early childhood disability, Functional difficulty, Mother’s age at birth, LMIC (low–middle income country), ADHD (attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder)

Introduction

Early childhood is crucial for helping children reach their full potential. Although advances in medicine and public health have reduced death rates for many pediatric illnesses, child disability remains a major issue, with a notable increase in the prevalence of developmental impairments, behavioral and mental health conditions, such as attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), autism, and other mental health disorders over the past 50 years [1–4]. Children who were born with a genetic disorder that impacts their physical, mental, or social development, who experienced a severe injury, nutritional deficit, or infection that led to long-term functional challenges, or who were exposed to environmental pollutants that caused developmental delays falls into the definition of disability. Children who had traumatic childhood situations and went on to develop anxiety or depression are also considered as disability [5].

Notably, people with disabilities, the world’s largest minority group, make up 10–25% of the population [6, 7]. Early in the 1990 s, according to government statistics, approximately 6.0% of young people were regarded as impaired [8]. The prevalence of childhood disabilities has continued to rise, rising by 15.6% between 2001 and 2002 and 2010–2011, where approximately 6 million children were classified as handicapped in 2010–2011 [4]. The National Health Interview Survey data from 1960 to 2010 revealed an increase in reported childhood disability from 2 to 8% [9]. Previous studies reported a 19.0% pooled prevalence estimate for moderate motor impairment and 40.5% for mild motor impairment, and 7–10% of children have severe motor disabilities [2, 10].

Additionally, a low quality of life and greater need for social services are also linked to disability [11–13]. Child disability, a major concern for future generations, often stems from poor nutrition, infections, a lack of developmental stimulation, and early adverse experiences, leaving nearly 250 million children unable to reach their full potential [14]. According to the World Bank, approximately one in five of the world’s poorest people who earn less than $1 per day are estimated to have some form of disability [15]. Pregnancy-related factors such as extreme stress, malnutrition, infections, and exposure to toxins increase the risk of giving birth to a child with disability [16]. Early parental involvement influences mental development, whereas teen mothers face greater risks of premature and low-birth-weight babies, which can lead to chronic illnesses and developmental challenges [15–17]. Moreover, for teenage-mothers in developing nations, inadequate nutrition during pregnancy is a particularly serious issue, which is a possible cause of child disability [18, 19]. Additionally, an increased risk of intellectual disability may be observed in children born to mothers with diabetes [20].

A child’s impairment has several repercussions. It affects not only the family but also the economy. Having a child with disability in a household can make parenting more stressful, affecting mental and physical health, making it harder to find affordable daycare, and influencing decisions on employment, education, and family planning. It may also lead to feelings of blame, guilt, or low self-esteem [21]. Children with disabilities face fewer educational opportunities, reducing their chances of higher education, which in turn limits human capital accumulation and leads to unemployment, lower wages, and chronic poverty [22–24]. Furthermore, they also cause a decline in maternal employment [25].

While there are many causes of child disabilities, to our knowledge, no recent research has fully explored the impact of maternal age during pregnancy on child disability in recent years, on global scale. All the previous research focused on particular disability problems, either on ADHD, intellectual, autism spectrum disorder, neurodevelopmental motor disorders or physical disability etc. But, in this study, we were able to give a scenario on motor, vision, hearing, intellectual, and learning disabilities altogether on multiple countries. This paper aims to address this gap by examining how maternal age across different age groups affects child disability. Additionally, it will make it easier and more effective for planners and policymakers to act on this critical issue.

Methods

Data source & study design

This investigation utilized data derived from retrospective cross-sectional studies of the latest Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS), typically financed mainly through government and UNICEF contributions in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). MICS is a global survey that is carried out in LMICs every three to five years under the same methodology. The respondents are usually mothers or other caregivers of children in the household, and the populations covered include children and women aged 15–49 years. The study period ran from February 7, 2024, until December 1, 2024 during which data were collected and analyzed, any dataset released after June were excluded from the analysis.

The survey sample was chosen via a two-stage, stratified cluster sampling procedure to recruit the participants. The enumeration areas (EAs) chosen for census enumeration were the primary sampling units (PSUs) chosen at the initial stage. In each sample EA, a household listing was completed, and at the second stage, a sample of households was chosen. MICS data are gathered through in-person interviews with respondents via a series of internationally recommended questionnaires that are tailored to each nation’s unique requirements. The MICS program asks mothers for information on vaccinations, childhood illnesses, and infant care for children under five. The women provided both written and verbal consent [26]. For more comprehensive guidelines, please visit https://mics.unicef.org/.

The multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS) operationally define “disability” or “functional disability” as the presence of difficulty in completing basic functional tasks across numerous domains of daily living. This approach is based on the International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health (ICF) framework, which views disability as a multidimensional phenomenon caused by the interaction of individual impairments and environmental barriers rather than just a medical or social issue [27–29]. The MICS uses the UNICEF/Washington Group Child Functioning Module (CFM) to assess childhood disability. This is the official and standardized tool included in the MICS questionnaire. It is developed jointly by UNICEF and the Washington Group on Disability Statistics, designed to assess functional difficulties in children aged 2–4 years and 5–17 years, with age-appropriate questions. Data synchronization

The latest standard MICS data from 2015 to 2024 for 38 LMICs were obtained from the website https://mics.unicef.org/surveys. We conducted our analyses via the children (BH) and mothers’ (WM) dataset, as our study included children (aged 2–4 years and aged 5–17 years) and their mothers. Our research included 38 countries (Afghanistan, Algeria, Bangladesh, Benin, Central African Republic, Chad, Comoros, Dominican, Congo, Eswatini, Fiji, Ghana, Guinea, Guyana, Honduras, Iraq, Kyrgyz, Lesotho, Madagascar, Malawi, Mongolia, Nepal, Pakistan (with 4 regions: Balochistan, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab and Sindh), Samoa, Sierra, State of Palestine, Gambia, Togo, Tonga, Trinidad, Tunisia, Turkmenistan, Turks, Tuvalu, Uzbekistan, Vietnam, Yemen, Zimbabwe) out of a potential 122 countries. We first picked only up-to-date datasets of these countries surveyed between 2015 and 2024 and found 76 country surveys in total, since datasets before 2015 might not accurately reflect the current situation of child impairment because inadequate data. Then, we excluded 18 country datasets from these 76 country datasets (Belize, Cote d’Ivoire, Kiribati, Kosovo under UNSC res. 1244, Kosovo under UNSC res. 1244 (Roma, Ashkali, Egyptian Communities) Lao People’s Democratic Republic, Mali, Mauritania, Mongolia Khuvsgul Aimag, Mongolia Nalaikh District, Nigeria, North Macedonia, North Macedonia Roma Settlements, Paraguay, Sao tome and Principe, Senegal, Serbia Roma settlements, Suriname) because of insufficient observation. Moreover, some countries (like Korea etc.) were not chosen because of the unavailability of the public domain. The rates of unresponsiveness in the aforementioned countries are not reported. This is attributable to the unavailability of MICS survey reports to the public or their lack of availability in English in specific nations. Eleven countries were excluded such as Argentina, Belarus, Cuba, Georgia, Jamaica, Kazakhstan, Mexico, Montenegro, Montenegro (Roma Settlements), Pakistan (Gilgit-Baltistan), Serbia since they had BH dataset unavailable, which arises merging problem of the datasets. Seven countries like Azerbaijan, Costa Rica, Nauru, Qatar, Serbia, Thailand, Vanuatu etc. released after our analysis period, so we had to exclude them too.

To analyze the survey datasets, we used sample weights for each dataset to improve accuracy and reliability to reduce bias. Sample weights are essential for calculating standard errors and help reduce bias caused by disproportionate sampling and nonresponse effects. Consequently, omitting weights from the analysis occasionally leads to markedly biased estimates. Missing data were handled through listwise deletion. We strictly followed the MICS guidelines while merging the datasets. To make interpretation and analysis easier, all levels of categorical explanatory variables were precisely described. After extracting the study variables from the datasets of each country, we pooled them into a single dataset.

Outcome variables

The outcome variable “functional disability” is a binary variable classified as “Yes = 1 and No = 0.” We consider any functional disability, whether it is related to hearing, vision, mobility, and motor skills, speech and language, learning, and emotions, reported by the respondents, as affirmative.

Explanatory variables

The primary independent variable was the maternal age during child birth, which was classified into five categories: “1” for giving birth before the age of 20, “2” for between the ages of 20 and 24, “3” for between the ages of 25 and 29, “4” for between the ages of 30 and 34, and “5” for 35 or more. This research analyzed several modifiable risk factors alongside the principal factor, including various sociodemographic variables, such as child age, child sex, child BMI, area, the mother’s wealth index, the mother’s functional disability, the mother’s education, birth order, having twins, ever breastfeeding and child abuse.

Statistical analysis

We conducted a comprehensive analysis of descriptive statistics related to child disability, considering factors such as country, type of residence, and economic status. Bivariate analysis (chi-square) was employed to investigate child disability across countries, considering the age of mothers, which formed the foundation of our research. We also tried to give a clear overview between the continents to see which continent is affected most by child disability, which may help countries in these continents to take necessary steps to improve child health, policy makers to strengthen government policy. Additionally, for inferential statistics, we used the logistics model to see the odds of mothers on child disability. Factors significant (p < 0.05) in bivariate model was included in the multivariable model. We also checked VIF values and ROC curve of the model. Weighted estimates are used for all reported findings in the tables and figures to appropriately represent national statistics. STATA version 17 was used for all the statistical analyses [30]. All the graphs were generated via Excel and RStudio, while the tables were created via SPSS version 27 and formatted via excel.

Results

The results presented below are all taken from the most recent MICS survey between 2014 and 2024. According to the dataset, a total of 18.4% of all children under the age of 18 have a disability. We identified 3590 children (2 to 4 years old) who were disabled out of 51,547 cases and 88,270 children with disabilities (5 to 17 years old) out of 446,716 cases. The proportions of disability among children aged 2 to 4 years and children aged 5 to 17 years were approximately 20% and 7%, respectively (Fig. 1). However, for children aged 2–4 years, 1.29% of the responses were missing, and for children aged 5–17 years, 0.01% of the responses were missing. After that, we separated the children into two groups: children with disabilities and children with typical development. Among them, of all male and female children, 7.7% of males and 6.2% of females were found to be disabled in the under-five age group. Additionally, for children aged 5–17 years, 20.0% and 18.1% of males and females, respectively, were found to be disabled (Tables 1 and 2).

The five countries with the highest percentage of child disability (aged 2–4) among mothers under 20 years were Ghana (26.8%), Togo (26.5%), Samoa (24.0%), Malawi (13.5%), and Zimbabwe (14.6%) (Table 3). The countries with the highest percentage of disability among mothers aged 20–34 years were the Central African Republic (13.5%), Chad (10.7%), Eswatini (12.5%), Ghana (13.1%), and Lesotho (14.7%) (Table 3). Finally, the five countries where mothers were 35 years of age or older were Afghanistan (11.0%), the Central African Republic (13.0%), Pakistan-Baluchistan (11.4%), Togo (10.1%), and Tonga (14.6%) (Table 3). Similarly, for children with disabilities aged 5–17 years, the five countries with the highest percentage of child disability among mothers aged under 20 years were Afghanistan (32.9%), the Central African Republic (34.7%), Comoros (35.6%), Guinea (39.2%), and Kyrgyz (51.1%) Table 4. Mothers aged between 20 and 34 years were Afghanistan (35.1%), the Central African Republic (31.4%), Chad (28.6%), Comoros (23.6%), and Samoa (24.8%) (Table 4). Finally, the percentages of child disability among mothers aged 35 years or older were 41.0% in Afghanistan, 33.3% in the Central African Republic, 33.1% in Chad, 23.1% in Congo, and 27.1% in Pakistan–Baluchistan (Table 4).

Considering the top 5 most populous countries, in Table 3, the prevalence of child disability in Algeria and Bangladesh was the lowest (3%), whereas that in Chad was the highest (10.4%). The Democratic Republic of the Congo and Pakistan have intermediate rates of 8.4% and 6.9%, respectively. In Table 4, the disparities are more pronounced. Afghanistan presented the highest proportion of children with functional difficulties (35.8%), followed by Chad (29.1%), Iraq (22.3%), Yemen (21.3%), and Pakistan (18.1%).

Sociodemographic variables

Table 1 shows that among children under five years of age, the percentage of male children who are disabled is 7.7%, whereas that of females is 6.2%. In terms of their BMI, the percentage of children with disabilities and obesity was 10.3%, which was the highest percentage among the categories. In contrast, 6.8% were thin, 7.8% were underweight, 5.7% were normal, and 3.5% were overweight. The percentage of missing values for this variable is 3.7%. The number of children with disabilities decreases as a child’s age increases (two; 8.8%, three; 6.5%, four; 6.4%). A similar scenario was observed for mothers at childbirth, where the higher the mother’s age while giving birth was, the lower the child disability rate. Among children aged 2–4, child disability rates declined from 8.1% (from mothers aged < 20) to 7.1% [19–23], 6.8% [24–28], 7.0% [29–34], and 6.8% (from mothers aged ≥ 35). The percentage of missing values for this variable was 0.03%. Conversely, the percentage increases with the birth order of children under five (two to three; 5.8%, four to six; 7.2%, seven or greater; 8.4%). Regarding mothers’ education level, the percentage of child disability is 7.7% for mothers who took pre-primary education or no education, 7.8% for secondary, 4.9% for higher secondary, and 4.3% for mothers who took higher education.

Mothers with functional difficulties (seeing, hearing, walking, cognition, self-care, communication) had a higher proportion of children with disabilities (15.3%) than mothers with no disability problems (6.2%), and 8.9% of the observations were missing for this variable. Additionally, among mothers who had twins had 7% child disability, and among mothers who had no twins, 6.6% of the children were disabled. Children who were never breastfed had a 9.1% disability rate, which was 0.4% greater than that of children who were breastfed. Nonetheless, the missing value is 76.4% for this variable. Furthermore, the percentages of child disability in urban and rural areas are 5.8% and 7.4%, respectively. Finally, if we look at the mother wealth index—poorest, lower middle, middle, upper middle, and richest—child disability was 7.4%, 7.1%, 6.7%, 7.0%, and 5.7%, respectively. A total of 11.7% child disability occurred among children who were abused verbally or physically. A total of 3.9% of the variables were missing values.

Similarly, Table 2 shows that for children with child disability aged 5–17 years, the percentages of male and female children were 20.8% and 18.7%, respectively. Mothers with functional difficulties account for 31.3% of children with disabilities, which is 14.2% more than mothers with no functional difficulties; the missing value is 11.13%. The child disability rate is deliberately higher among twins (19.7%), whereas the rate for children with no twins is 20.5%. The disability rates were 21.9%, 19.0%, and 17.1% for children aged 5–9, 10–14, and 15–17 years, respectively. According to our findings, child disability increases as birth order increases. For mothers with first parity, the child disability rate was 17.6%; for those with a parity of 2–3, it was 17.9%; for those with a parity of 4–6, it was 20.1%; and for those with a parity of 7 or over, it was 25.5%. However, mothers’ education levels show the opposite scenario, where mothers who had preprimary or no education account for 22.2% of those with child disability, 18.3% with primary education, 16.5% with secondary education, and 13.7% with higher education; 0.04% of the values are missing. The proportion of children with disabilities among various maternal age groups were 21.9% for mothers aged less than 20 years, 20% for mothers aged 20–24 years, 19.8% for mothers aged 25–29 years, 18.9% for mothers aged 30–34 years, and 20.4% for mothers aged over 35 years or above. Mothers living in urban areas have a lower percentage of child disability (18.8%) than do mothers living in rural areas (20.2%). The mothers’ wealth index substantially contributed to child disability, with the poorest, lower middle, middle, upper middle, and richest contributing 20.1%, 19.9%, 20.4%, 20.1%, and 17.5%, respectively. Among the children who were abused, 31.6% were disabled. However, 23.5% of the values were missing.

Compared to mothers aged 20–24, those under 20 had 12% higher crude odds of having children with disabilities (OR = 1.12; p = 0.012), but the association became non-significant after adjustment (aOR = 1.08; p = 0.111). Mothers aged 30–35 showed 8% lower crude odds (OR = 0.92; p = 0.002) compared to mothers aged 20–24, which also lost significance after adjustment. No significant associations were found for mothers aged 25–29 or ≥ 35 years. Children aged 5–9 had significantly higher odds than those aged 2–4 (OR = 3.76; aOR = 3.74; p < 0.001), with similar trends for ages 10–14 and 15–17. Female children had consistently lower odds than males (OR and aOR = 0.87; p < 0.001). Children of mothers with no or low education had significantly higher odds (18%) of having children with disabilities compared to mothers with high education, even after adjustment (e.g., aOR = 1.18 for no education; p = 0.001), with similar patterns for primary and secondary levels. Additionally, mothers in rural areas had slightly higher crude odds than urban ones (OR = 1.09; p < 0.001), but after adjustment, the association became marginally protective (aOR = 0.95; p = 0.048). Mothers from the richest households had significantly lower odds tan those from the poorest in both crude (OR = 0.87) and adjusted models (aOR = 0.83; p < 0.001), while other wealth quintiles showed no significant associations after adjustment. Compared to Afghanistan, children from all other countries had significantly lower odds (p < 0.001) of becoming disable. Bangladesh (aOR = 0.18), Mongolia (aOR = 0.15), and Nepal (aOR = 0.30) showed the lowest odds. Countries like the Central African Republic (aOR = 0.82) and Chad (aOR = 0.73) had higher odds than others but still lower than Afghanistan.

In the unadjusted model presented in Table 5, mothers aged 20–24 exhibit 11% lower odds of having disabled children compared to mothers under 20, with this result being statistically significant (p < 0.05). Mothers aged 25–29 have 12% lower odds compared to the reference group, also statistically significant. The effect is slightly stronger than for the 20–24 group (OR = 0.88; 95% CI: 0.80–0.96, p = 0.003). Individuals aged 30-34 exhibit 18% reduced odds, supported by a highly significant p-value (OR = 0.88; 95% CI: 0.80–0.96, p = 0.003), indicating a more robust association compared to younger age groups (under 20 years). Mothers aged 35-39, shows 18% lower odds (OR = 0.82; 95% CI: 0.75–0.90, p < 0.001), with a highly significant p-value, suggesting a stronger association than in younger age groups. Mothers aged 35 or older have 13% lower odds (OR = 0.87; 95% CI: 0.79–0.96, p = 0.004), also significant, though the effect size is slightly less pronounced than for the 30–34 group.

Discussion

Health, functional capacity, quality of life, and overall development are interrelated aspects of human well-being. However, child disability remains a significant concern in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), such as Afghanistan, Benin, the Central African Republic, China, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Iraq, Nigeria, and Yemen. Our findings align with those of previous studies, highlighting the persistent challenges in these regions. Notably, the prevalence of child disability is particularly high in Afghanistan and China. Factors such as early marriage and advanced maternal age contribute to birth complications, leading to adverse child health outcomes ([31].

Our findings indicate that child disability increases as maternal age decreases, with the highest risk observed among mothers who gave birth before the age of 20. For children under five, mothers who gave birth after the age of 35 years experienced relatively lower rates of child disability. However, for children age between 5 and 17 years, younger mothers and mothers age over 35 had the high percentage of having children with disabilities. Though medical facilities frequently classify women in their 40 s as having high risk of pregnancy and childbirth, taking frequent prenatal care, talking to medical expert for their advice and their possible strategies may resist the risks [32]. Our study suggests that mothers whose age is less 20 had 12% higher odds to have disable children compared to mothers age 20–24. A study reported a 36% increased risk of attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and learning disabilities (LD) among children born to younger mothers compared with those born to older mothers [33]. This heightened risk may be attributed to various health complications associated with early childbirth, including labor and delivery complications [34]. Furthermore, poor nutrition during pregnancy is a major problem for adolescent mothers in LMICs, and it may contribute to the impairment of their unborn children [18, 19]. Additionally, early maternal age during first childbirth has been linked to chronic conditions such as diabetes, chronic lung disease, hypertension, and reduced physical performance later in life [35].

An important finding of our study is that male children exhibit greater functional disability than female children across both age groups do, which is consistent with previous research [36–38]. Female children are 13% less likely to become disabled compared to male children. This disparity may be attributed to the greater vulnerability of the male immune system, as female children tend to have greater resistance to infectious diseases [39]. There is enough evidence that men are generally more susceptible to infections than women, with higher prevalence and severity across various pathogens, including parasites, fungi, bacteria, and viruses [40–43].

Our study reveals that mothers with functional disabilities are more likely to have children with disabilities than mothers without functional disabilities. This finding is supported by a study from Bangladesh, which indicated that mothers experiencing functional difficulties are 23% more likely to have children with disabilities [27]. Furthermore, the age of the child significantly influences the prevalence of disability, with younger children being more susceptible to disabilities. The relationship between a mother and a child with disabilities, along with factors such as family climate, family relations, social support, problem-focused coping, and effective management, serves as predictors that enhance the child’s cognition, social skills, and daily living skills thus reduces disabilities [44].

Moreover, our study revealed a positive association between birth order and child disability, with an increase in parity correlating with a greater likelihood of having a disabled child. This finding is consistent with other studies [45]. Additionally, an interesting pattern emerged regarding twins. Twins aged between two and four years exhibit a higher rate of disability than their nontwin peers in the same age group do. This is likely due to obstetric complications (such as preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, preterm labor, postpartum hemorrhage, fetal distress etc.) of twin pregnancies. Twin pregnancies present more challenges for the mother and the fetus than singleton pregnancies, which can result in poor neonatal health at birth and subsequent health complications. However, twins aged 5–17 years have a lower rate of disability than non-twin children within the same age range do.

Breastfeeding plays a crucial role in a child’s physical development [46]. Our study supports this finding, showing that children who are breastfed have a lower rate of disability than those who are not breastfed at an early age.

Urbanization significantly impacts child development. Our research shows that the rate of child disability is notably higher in rural areas than in urban areas. This finding is supported by a study conducted in the U.S [47]. Additionally, another study indicated that the prevalence of child disability tends to decrease over time with increased urbanization [48].

Mothers are often seen as the primary influencers of their children’s well-being, and a child’s health is closely linked to the mother’s education [49]. Our study supports this finding, showing that child disability rates decrease as maternal education level increases. Additionally, family socioeconomic status plays a significant role in child disability. Children from wealthier families experience lower disability rates than those from poorer households do. Evidence indicates that children living in poor households have a 35% greater prevalence of disabilities than those in the wealthiest households do [50].

Child abuse is a critical yet underrecognized contributor to child disability, with 11.7% of cases linked to early maltreatment. Abuse-induced neurobiological alterations, including reduced brain volume and structural deficits, contribute to long-term behavioral and health complications [51, 52]. Moreover, childhood maltreatment significantly elevates morbidity and mortality risks, leading to persistent health impairments in adulthood. Addressing child abuse is essential for mitigating the prevalence of disability [53, 54].

Lastly, Child disabilities are predominant in Asian region comprising 19.39% out of total population of children. On the other hand, North America comprises the lowest, 12.35%. The greatest prevalence of intellectual disability (6.0% [95% UI: 4.5–7.5%]), hearing loss (4.7% [95% UI: 4.3–5.0%]), and vision loss (3.7% [95% UI: 3.2–4.2%]) was observed in South Asia, while the highest prevalence of epilepsy (1.2% [95% UI: 1.0–1.5%]) was observed in Latin America and the Caribbean, according to a previous study [55]. Most of the LMIC’s countries fall into these regions. These countries are vulnerable to dealing with public health due to limited capacity to respond to health emergencies. Therefore, governments and international communities should increase their efforts in these regions to ensure that child disability is reduced to zero.

Strengths and limitations

The existence of bias due to different survey time points and the cross-sectional nature of the data cannot be proven. Some factors that were statistically insignificant (at 95% confidence interval) but nonetheless relevant to the research topic were included in the model. Statistical significance has not been achieved for all age groups of mothers. Additionally, all the continent doesn’t have the similar sample size (such as Europe and Central Asia only constitute only 0.66% of the data), which might also arise biasness in the result. Furthermore, because we used a secondary data source, we had limited control over which correlated variables to include in the analysis. Data inaccessibility, missing variables, and limited observations affected the analysis, whereas missing values, particularly for maternal wealth (31%), may impact the accuracy of the results. Additionally, the study focuses solely on maternal age, although other factors, such as genetics and environmental influences, contribute to child disability. Future research should address these gaps for a more comprehensive understanding.

Despite these drawbacks, we were able to present a clear scenario on learning, intellectual, hearing, vision, and movement disorders across several countries in this study. All intricate survey designs were considered, and appropriate data analysis techniques were implemented. The MICS is a recognized survey conducted in developing countries every three to five years, primarily funded by government and UNICEF contributions in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). This dataset is publicly available and represents the country as a whole. Each year, numerous papers are published utilizing this dataset, which is collected through a random sampling design employing advanced methodologies. We anticipate that the countries included in our analysis are adequately represented by these datasets. The results of this study can guide future research, influence policy, and enhance clinical practice, while also serving as a benchmark for progress. This study has the advantage of having a sizable and nationally representative sample size, and the findings are generalizable across numerous countries. Using the most recent survey, the data was collected. We are certain that our findings will stimulate further research and inform policymakers about the discrepancies in maternal age and child disability that need to be addressed.

Conclusion

Our study highlights the critical role of maternal age during birth in child disability. The findings indicate a greater percentage of disability among children born to mothers at both early and later reproductive ages. Given that child disability poses a significant challenge to the development of LMICs, targeted government interventions are essential. Awareness programs, particularly in rural areas, should emphasize the importance of optimal maternal age to mitigate risks associated with adverse birth outcomes and reduce the prevalence of child disability.
