Transplacental transfer of ravulizumab in a pregnant woman with neuromyelitis optica: a case report
Authors: Anu Jacob, Azza Mahmoud, Luai Eldweik, Asia Mubashir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) is a rare relapsing autoimmune disease of the central nervous system (CNS). Management during pregnancy is challenging due to limited safety data for disease-modifying therapies.Case presentation We report a case of a 41-year-old woman with aquaporin-4 (AQP4) IgG positive NMOSD who was switched from rituximab to ravulizumab during pregnancy. Ravulizumab was initiated shortly after conception and continued throughout pregnancy. Pregnancy was uneventful and she delivered a healthy term infant. At birth, umbilical cord blood testing revealed detectable ravulizumab levels and low C5 activity, confirming transplacental transfer of ravulizumab. The infant, now 4 months old, remains well.Conclusions This is the first reported case of ravulizumab use during pregnancy, with documentation of transplacental drug transfer and neonatal complement inhibition. Despite laboratory evidence of pharmacologic exposure, the neonate remained clinically well. These findings suggest that ravulizumab may be a viable treatment option for NMOSD during pregnancy when traditional agents are contraindicated or ineffective. However, further studies and longitudinal monitoring of exposed infants are essential to establish safety and clinical guidelines.Keywords: NEUROIMMUNOLOGY

Case presentation

A 41-year-old Emirati woman presented in March 2021 with cervical myelitis and area postrema syndrome in September 2021. She was diagnosed with aquaporin-4 (AQP4) IgG positive neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) and was begun on satralizumab in November 2021. However, while on satralizumab, the patient developed optic neuritis. She was switched to rituximab in June 2022. She experienced another relapse of thoracic myelitis just before her planned 6-month rituximab dose in December 2022. CD19+B cell count was found to be elevated at the time of this relapse. So, it was decided to continue rituximab, given every 5 months, and she remained stable. In June 2024, she had an unplanned pregnancy. Considering the risks of rituximab-induced B-cell depletion in neonates, it was discontinued. Extensive counselling regarding alternative treatment options, including maintenance steroids, Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and azathioprine, was done. She refused these. She also refused eculizumab, a complement C5 inhibitor with favourable pregnancy safety data due to the 2 weekly infusion requirement. So, she was counselled about ravulizumab, which has a similar mechanism of action but extended dosing interval (every 8 weeks following induction). She was also counselled about the lack of data on safety in pregnancy (though it was expected to be similar to eculizumab). She was keen to proceed and received two doses of meningococcal vaccination 1 month apart, after which she was begun on ravulizumab in September 2024.

Ravulizumab dosing was based on the patient’s weight on treatment day, following manufacturer’s guidelines. On day 1, the patient received a loading dose of 2700 mg, followed by a maintenance dose of 3300 mg on day 15 and then every 8 weeks thereafter.

Patients obstetric team was educated about NMOSD and risks of infections to mother and fetus/neonate. She remained clinically stable without new neurological deficits or relapses; neurological examination was unchanged from baseline, aside from residual left optic atrophy. Serial foetal growth assessments were unremarkable. Foetal anomaly scan done at 26 weeks of pregnancy was normal. She delivered at term, 6 weeks after her last ravulizumab dose and resumed treatment 1 week postpartum. At the time of delivery, maternal and neonatal serum samples were collected and analysed at the Mayo Clinic Laboratories (Rochester, MN,USA) for C5 complement activity and ravulizumab concentrations.

Testing of neonatal umbilical cord blood revealed ravulizumab concentration at 59.3 mcg/mL, confirming in utero exposure. Functional C5 complement level was low (8 U/mL; reference range: 29–53 U/mL), indicating complement blockade. Simultaneous maternal serum sample demonstrated similarly suppressed functional C5 complement levels (7 U/mL reference range: 29–53 U/mL) and a ravulizumab concentration of 218 mcg/mL, consistent with therapeutic levels during maintenance dosing (§amp;gt; 175 mcg/ mL).

The neonate was healthy and showed no signs of infection, remained asymptomatic and was discharged without complications. The neonate received hepatitis B vaccination but did not receive BCG vaccination (live vaccine), which is planned later. Ongoing paediatric follow-up was arranged to monitor for infectious risks and immune function.

Discussion

This report presents the first documented case of ravulizumab use during pregnancy .

NMOSD is a rare relapsing autoimmune disease of the central nervous system. Disease activity during pregnancy is of particular concern, as 77% of patients experience relapses in the intrapartum and postpartum period.1 The pathogenesis involves AQP4-IgG antibodies binding to astrocytic water channels, initiating complement activation. The cleavage of complement component C5 into C5a and C5b leads to membrane attack complex (MAC) formation and astrocyte injury2

There are currently four approved disease-modifying therapies for the treatment of AQP4-IgG positive NMOSD. These are eculizumab, inebilizumab, satralizumab and ravulizumab.

Eculizumab is a humanised monoclonal antibody that inhibits C5 by preventing its cleavage. When administered every 2 weeks, eculizumab reduced NMOSD relapse risk by 94.2% compared with placebo in the phase 3 PREVENT trial (NCT01892345).3 Ravulizumab binds to the same C5 epitope as eculizumab but has a longer half-life, allowing for an extended dosing interval (8 vs 2 weeks). In the CHAMPION-NMOSD trial, ravulizumab reduced the risk of relapse by 98.6%.4

Although eculizumab has been used during pregnancy in conditions such as atypical haemolytic uraemic syndrome and paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria with some success5 (table 1), there are no published data on ravulizumab use during pregnancy. A few reports (table 1) have described its use in the postpartum period for the treatment of atypical haemolytic uraemic syndrome.6 B-cell–depleting therapies like rituximab are also effective in preventing NMOSD relapses. However, rituximab crosses the placenta and has been associated with transient B lymphocytopenia in neonates7

In our case, detectable levels of ravulizumab were identified in the cord blood (indicating transplacental transfer), accompanied by reduced functional C5 complement in serum—indicating complement blockade. This finding confirms transplacental transfer of ravulizumab, likely mediated by the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn), a well-established mechanism for IgG and monoclonal antibody transport across the placenta during the third trimester.

Complement component C5 plays a critical role in immune defence by enabling formation of the MAC, which is essential for lysis of encapsulated bacteria, particularly Neisseria meningitidis. This risk is well-documented in adults and children treated with eculizumab or ravulizumab, and it is presumed that neonates exposed in utero could be similarly vulnerable. Notably, neonates already have an immature immune system and are inherently at higher baseline risk for severe bacterial infections. The additive effect of complement blockade could further impair their ability to mount effective responses against such pathogens.

In this case, however, despite laboratory evidence of suppressed functional C5 complement and detectable ravulizumab levels in the neonate, no clinical signs of infection or immunodeficiency were observed during the neonatal period or early follow-up. This is consistent with prior studies on eculizumab-treated mothers (table 1), where most neonates were born healthy but continued to receive close infectious disease follow-up.8

This favourable outcome suggests that while pharmacologic complement inhibition does occur trans-placentally, the clinical consequences may not be severe in all cases—though caution remains essential. Prophylactic vaccination, infectious disease consultation and long-term paediatric monitoring are advisable until more data are available on neonatal outcomes in this context. In this case, the family was appropriately counselled, and the infant remained well at 4 months of follow-up

Our patient is grateful that her baby was born healthy and that she remained relapse-free during pregnancy and had a safe delivery. She hopes her experience will offer reassurance and guidance to other women experiencing similar treatment decisions during pregnancy.

Conclusion

This is the first reported case of ravulizumab use during pregnancy, with documentation of transplacental drug transfer and neonatal complement inhibition. Despite laboratory evidence of pharmacologic exposure, the neonate remained clinically well. These findings suggest that ravulizumab may be a viable treatment option for NMOSD during pregnancy when traditional agents are contraindicated or ineffective. However, further studies and longitudinal monitoring of exposed infants are essential to establish safety and clinical guidelines.
