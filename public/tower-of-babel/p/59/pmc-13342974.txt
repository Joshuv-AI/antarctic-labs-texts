Sources, impacts, and mechanisms of non-pharmacological artifacts in processed electroencephalography monitoring during anaesthesia and intensive care: A scoping review
Authors: Alina Beg, Clara N Kim, Alan Tung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProcessed EEG (pEEG) monitoring is widely used to assess depth of hypnosis during anaesthesia and sedation in intensive care. However, these systems are susceptible to non-brain-related artifacts. Although previous reviews have identified sources of artifacts, their specific effects on electroencephalographic signals and derived hypnotic indices have not been systematically synthesised. This scoping review aims to summarise non-pharmacological artifact sources affecting pEEG monitoring in perioperative and ICU settings, including their impact on hypnotic indices and underlying mechanisms.MethodsA three-step search strategy was followed for this scoping review as detailed in a previously published protocol. Studies involving non-pharmacological, non–brain-related artifacts in human patients undergoing pEEG monitoring during anaesthesia or critical care were included. Databases searched were PubMed, Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, and Web of Science, alongside grey literature. Searches were conducted on 19 June and 11 November 2025. English-language articles or those with English translations were included. Screening and data extraction were performed using Covidence.ResultsOf 152 screened articles, 48 met inclusion criteria (31 case reports, 7 case series, 10 studies). pEEG Monitors included BIS (36 articles), Entropy (10), and SedLine (six). Thirty-one artifact types were identified: 13 were patient-derived and 18 were non–patient-derived. The most common artifacts were related to cardiac or neuromuscular activity. Thrity-seven articles reported artifacts falsely elevating hypnotic indices; 2 resulted in no change, and 2reported a decrease.ConclusionsProcessed EEG Hypnotic indices are vulnerable to a broad range of non-pharmacological artifacts from both patient and environmental sources. This review categorises these artifacts and summarises their effects and mechanisms, highlighting important limitations in interpreting pEEG data clinically.Keywords: artifacts, depth of anaesthesia monitoring, processed electroencephalography

Because hypnotic indices can be distorted by many artifacts—especially in patients with EEG suppression, isoelectricity, or low-amplitude signals—clinicians should be comfortable with reviewing the raw EEG waveform and not rely solely on the processed index.

Processed EEG (pEEG) monitors use EEG to assess the depth of hypnosis in patients undergoing surgery or intensive care to guide anaesthesia or sedation. Using proprietary algorithms, pEEG systems generate simplified, numerical hypnotic indices that supposedly reflect a patient’s state of consciousness. Available indices include the Patient State Index (Masimo SedLine®), Bispectral Index (Medtronic BIS™), Wavelet Anesthetic Value for EEG (NeuroWave NeuroSense®), State Entropy (GE Healthcare Entropy™), and the Narcotrend Index (MonitorTechnik Narcotrend®).

However, hypnotic indices are susceptible to interference from artifacts unrelated to brain activity. Artifacts may cause fluctuations in the hypnotic index, which may mislead clinicians to titrate anaesthesia inappropriately to the actual situation. Thus, an awareness and understanding of the factors that interfere with pEEG is necessary to understand the limitations of current pEEG monitors for future education and training purposes.

Previous narrative reviews on pEEG have noted several common sources of artifacts affecting the raw EEG, including muscular activity, electrical line noise, patient movement, and electronic device interference.1, 2, 3 However, many more new sources of artifacts have been published since the publication of these narrative reviews. Furthermore, a rigorous scoping review has not been conducted before to summarise published sources of known non-pharmacological and non–brain-related artifacts causing unexpected fluctuation in the hypnotic index. Thus, the purpose of this scoping review is to summarise the artifacts’ effects on the hypnotic indices and EEG and the artifacts’ purported mechanism.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology for scoping reviews and is reported in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) extension for scoping reviews guidelines and checklist (S6). 4,5 This study did not require ethics review. The study protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework on 19 June 2025 (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/KF6JV), and published in January 2026.6

Search strategy

A three-step search strategy was implemented, as detailed in the published protocol.6 In summary, an initial structured search was conducted across major bibliographic databases (including PubMed, CINAHL [EBSCO], MEDLINE [Ovid], Embase [Ovid], Cochrane Library [Wiley], CINAHL [EBSCOhost], and Web of Science [Web of Science]), followed by a targeted search of grey literature using Google Scholar, with screening limited to the first five pages of results. Search strategy can be found in the Supplementary Document (Supplementary File S1). Reference lists of included studies were manually reviewed to identify additional relevant sources. Searches covered from database inception through 19 June 2025. The search was repeated on 11 November 2025 for any new articles.

Eligibility criteria

We included full-text articles/studies and grey literature involving human patients of any age undergoing care in anaesthesia or intensive care settings in which pEEG monitoring with a numerical hypnotic index was used. pEEG monitoring was defined as a commercially available device that used EEG to generate a number that was reflective of the patient’s depth of hypnosis (hypnotic index). Eligible pEEG devices included commercially available monitors such as BIS (Medtronic), SedLine (Masimo), Entropy (GE/Datex), NeuroSense (NeuroWave), and Narcotrend, among others.

The review focused on non-pharmacological artifacts that interfere with pEEG monitoring such that the hypnotic index no longer accurately reflects clinical depth of hypnosis. These included extracerebral physiological artifacts (e.g. neuromuscular activity) and technical or electrical sources of interference. We excluded studies examining pharmacological effects on EEG, artifacts directly attributable to cerebral or neural activity, devices limited to research use, monitors without a numerical hypnotic index, non-clinical settings, non-human studies, and studies conducted outside anaesthesia or critical care contexts. Only articles published in English or with an English translation were included.

Study selection

Search results and articles were imported into Covidence software.7 After removal of duplicate articles, title and abstract screening was performed independently by at least two reviewers using predefined eligibility criteria, followed by full-text review of potentially relevant studies. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus or by consultation with an additional reviewer. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were recorded. The study selection process is summarised using a PRISMA flow diagram.

Data extraction

Two independent reviewers extracted data using a standardised, pilot-tested extraction form developed in Covidence. Extracted variables included patient characteristics, clinical context, type of pEEG monitor, number of cases, suspected source of artifact, its effect on the EEG signal and hypnotic index, and proposed mechanisms of interference. The extraction form was refined iteratively to ensure consistency, with any modifications documented.

Data analysis

Extracted data were synthesised descriptively and presented in tables, mapping sources, and effects of non-pharmacological artifacts across pEEG devices. Patient-derived sources, in which the artifact came from the patient themselves physiologically, was further divided into cardiovascular, neuromuscular, or other sources. For non-patient-derived causes of artifacts, they were subcategorised into external devices—in which the artifactual sources came from outside the patient (i.e. external to the skin)—or internal devices, where the source of the artifact was generated from within the patient. Although some articles used the term of ECG to describe the artifact as cardiac in nature, because that the term technically refers to “an instrument for recording the changes of electrical potential”, we used the term “cardiac activity” instead in this scoping review.8 Similarly, for EMG, a term that also technically refers to a diagnostic test, we used the term “neuromuscular activity” to mean electrical muscular activity as the source of the artifact in this article.9

Results

The scoping review’s initial search found 152 articles. After removing duplicates, screening. and assessing for eligibility, 48 articles were included in the final data extraction (Fig. 1 PRISMA). Proportionate agreements for inter-rater reliability for title and abstract screening were 91.5% (AT-AB) and 86.0% (AT-NK) (Supplementary File S2).

PRISMA flow diagram. PRISMA, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses.

Of the 48 articles, 31 were single-patient case reports, 7 were case series (ranging from two to five patients), and the remaining 10 were clinical studies. Of the 371 patients described in these articles, 202 were female (Table 1). Ages ranged from 3 months to 81 yr old. ASA physical statuses ranged from 1 to 6. A total of 43 articles involved patients under general anaesthesia, with 18 of them being total intravenous anaesthesia. Various pEEG monitors were used, including BIS (36 articles), Entropy (10 articles), SedLine (six articles), and others (CONOX, NicoletOne, Narcotrend, Cerebral State Monitor, Lifescan).

The top two sources of artifacts were patient-derived: neuromuscular activity and cardiac activity. Sixteen articles described neuromuscular activity-related artifacts in the pEEG monitor, of which 11 articles noted that the neuromuscular artifact was detected by the pEEG monitor. Patients in 12 articles had received neuromuscular blocker, although the timing between the administration of the neuromuscular blocker to artifact detection varied. The pEEG monitors affected included BIS (10 articles), Entropy (five articles), SedLine (three articles), cerebral state monitor and CONOX (one article each).

For cardiac activity, we found six articles and noted five articles describing the patients having EEG with low amplitude, suppression, and isoelectricity or both. Five out of six articles involved patients under general anaesthesia with ages ranging from 25 to 78 yr old.

The pEEG monitors affected included BIS (4 articles), Entropy (two2 articles), and Nicolet monitor (1 article). Raw data and full analyses are summarised in Supplementary Tables (Supplementary Files S3-S5).Thirty seven of the articles reported that the artifact caused the hypnotic index to falsely increase. Two articles reported that the hypnotic index (BIS) was falsely lower because of cardiac activity artifact (one case in which the BIS EEG montage was placed in the post-auricular position instead of the forehead,16 and another case of high amplitude electrical cardiac activity during EEG isoelectricity17). Two articles noted that the hypnotic index was unchanged but the raw EEG waveforms were affected (superficial temporal artery pulsation,44 left ventricular assist device placement33).

Discussion

This scoping review summarizes the published literature on non-pharmacological artifacts that may affect EEG waveforms and hypnotic indices derived from processed EEG (pEEG) monitors. We identified 31 distinct artifact types and systematically catalogued their effects on raw EEG signals, hypnotic indices, and the proposed underlying pathophysiological mechanisms.

After examining the articles’ clinical contexts, we did not observe any obvious patterns linking artifact occurrence with demographic characteristics, anaesthetic technique, ASA physical status, surgical category, surgical category, or type of pEEG monitor. However, given the rarity and diversity of reporting variables, we were unable to confirm this through statistical methods.

For cases involving electrocardiographic activity interfering with the pEEG, our review did find that a significant proportion (83%) of these patient cases had EEG suppression, isoelectricity, or low amplitude. This may create conditions in which non-cerebral electrical activity could disproportionately influence the pEEG monitor’s EEG interpretation. These cases reported ECG-like waveforms observed on the EEG channels, synchronising with the cardiac cycle, and resulted in spurious fluctuations in hypnotic indices despite stable anaesthetic conditions. Notably, these phenomena were observed across different pEEG platforms, patient ages, and ASA physical status classifications. Collectively, these findings suggest that low-amplitude or suppressed EEG states represent a key vulnerability for cardiac activity interference.

Two principal mechanisms have been proposed to explain how cardiac activity may contaminate EEG signals under these circumstances. The first involves direct detection of cardiac electrical fields. Cardiac sources, including the QRS complex and paced spikes, generate relatively large electrical potentials that can be detected by frontal EEG electrodes positioned close to the scalp and torso, manifesting as stereotyped, periodic deflections time-locked to the ECG.57 The R wave alone measures approximately 1 mV on the body surface, a magnitude sufficient to interfere with cerebral EEG signals when cortical activity is attenuated.10,58 Consistent with this, pacemakers and epicardial leads have been identified in our review as sources of artifacts affecting pEEG, and they generate pulses in volts. The degree of contamination may further depend on electrode orientation relative to the cardiac electrical vector, and anatomic factors such as neck length, tissue composition, and respiratory changes in cardiac position, all of which can influence current spread to the scalp.11 Importantly, electrical cardiac artifact spans a broad frequency range (approximately 0.05–80 Hz), overlapping with delta through beta bands and thereby directly contaminating features used by pEEG algorithms.59

A second postulated mechanism relates to the detection of a mechanical movement, such as arterial pulsation. Arterial pulsation may cause motion artifact and induce minute electrode or cable motion. This theory may also explain why other cardiovascular sources of artifacts affect pEEG, including cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), carotid artery pulsation, and intra-aortic balloon pump.18, 19, 20,55 Cyclic forces generated by CPB pumps can transmit mechanical vibrations through the extracorporeal circuit, operating table, and patient, leading to subtle head or scalp motion that manifests as sharp transients or low-frequency waveforms time-locked to pump cycles or arterial pulsation. Similarly, intra-aortic balloon pump inflation and deflation generates mechanical vibrations that can be transmitted through the thorax and monitoring leads, producing pulse-synchronous artifact.

Neuromuscular activity, whether derived from patient movement, motor evoked potentials, or the neural integrity monitor EMG endotracheal tube, understandably would impact the raw EEG waveform and the hypnotic index, given that neuromuscular activity occurs at higher frequencies that pEEG monitors typically interpret as the wakeful state. Although updated algorithms have improved in discounting high-frequency neuromuscular activity from their hypnotic index calculations (i.e. BIS 3.4 update had improved EMG detection and filtering), we continue to find articles noting the neuromuscular interference despite these updates.60,61 Filtering these neuromuscular signals continues to be a challenge for hypnotic index algorithms, and the use of paralytics should be administered to rule out neuromuscular interference.

Forced-air warming systems have been reported in five articles to falsely elevate BIS values. The authors confirmed the source of the artifact as the forced-air warming system by noting normalisation of the BIS monitoring after turning off the warming system, or after placing a barrier between the warming blanket plastic flap and the forehead sensor.21, 22, 23,45,46 Of the five articles, three noted elevated EMG detection despite the use of neuromuscular blockers. Guignard and Chauvin attributed the artifact to airflow-induced vibration of EEG electrodes or leads.46 Collectively, these data indicate that forced-air warming artifacts likely resulted from high frequency mechanical vibration of electrodes or adjacent tissues.

Our work emphasises the importance of reviewing the raw EEG waveform and not solely relying on the hypnotic indices provided by the pEEG monitor. When higher-than-expected hypnotic indices occur and are not explained by the administration of pharmacological agents (i.e. ketamine, nitrous oxide) or intraoperative emergence, we suggest reviewing the raw EEG, identifying the degree of neuromuscular artifact detected, and determining whether the onset of the hypnotic index elevation coincided with device usage.3 The top two sources—patient’s cardiac activity and neuromuscular activity—should be readily identifiable by reviewing the raw EEG. Cardiac artifact typically appears as large spikes coinciding with pulsation, whereas neuromuscular artifact may be determined by the pEEG monitor’s own detection of electromyographic or artifact activity, which then subsequently decreases after the administration of additional neuromuscular blocker. If these two artifact sources have been ruled out, then we approach the differential starting from the surgery (i.e. hammering, electrocautery, endoscopic shaver, etc.), patient (i.e. an intra-aortic balloon pump, cardiopulmonary bypass, motor evoked potentials, pacemaker, neural integrity monitored endotracheal tube, etc.), to the pEEG sticker montage (i.e. forced air warmer, temperature sensor, etc.), and then the equipment issues (i.e. contaminated connectors).

Several limitations to our study may limit the comprehensiveness and applicability of our scoping review. Restricting our search to articles published in English or having an English translation may lead to missed sources. Searching using commercial pEEG monitor names may lead to exclusion of other devices, although we attempted to minimise this issue by including “processed EEG” in our search strategy. The replicability and veracity of some of the published artifactual sources’ effects on pEEG monitoring may be questionable, depending on the original authors’ published attempts to isolate the cause of the interference and whether other authors have published similar findings. Although we attempted to be specific about the version of pEEG monitor used, many articles excluded this detail. Thus, newer versions of the pEEG monitor with updated software may reject artifacts described in the literature. Nonetheless, our intent with this scoping review was not necessarily to report active issues with pEEG monitors, but rather to raise awareness of the wide range of artifactual sources, so as to help anaesthesiologists and intensive care clinicians with the differential when the hypnotic index does not appear to accurately reflect the patient’s true depth of hypnosis.

Conclusion

Hypnotic indices can be affected by many different sources, both patient-derived and non–patient-derived. Reviewing the raw EEG or density spectral array and noting the timing of onset will help troubleshoot sources of artifacts when faced with unexplained changes in the hypnotic index that do not reflect the clinician’s perception of the patient’s state of hypnosis.
