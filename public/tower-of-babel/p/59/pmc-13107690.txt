Prevention and management of radiation dermatitis for patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma: a best practice implementation project
Authors: Min Hou, Wenhuan Zhong, Kefu Shi, Duoduo Pei, Liping Qi, Binbin Hou, Hui-xia Feng, Jiali Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRadiation dermatitis(RD) is one of the most common acute toxicities of radiotherapy, affecting approximately 85%~95% of patients. Severe RD can substantially impair quality of life and may compromise treatment continuity, thereby reducing the effectiveness of radiotherapy. Despite the availability of clinical guidelines, RD prevention and management in routine practice remain inconsistent.AimThis best practice implementation project aimed to implement an evidence-based strategies for the prevention and management of RD in patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), improve compliance with best-practice recommendations, and reduce severe RD.MethodsThis was an evidence-based audit and feedback project that used a three-phase approach at a tertiary cancer center in China. We conducted an evidence-based audit-and-feedback project using the JBI Practical Application of Clinical Evidence System (JBI PACES) and Getting Research into Practice (GRiP) framework at a tertiary cancer center in China. Phase 1 involved developing 15 evidence-based audit criteria and conducting a baseline audit (22 patients; 26 nurses). Phase 2 identified barriers to best practice and implemented targeted GRiP-informed strategies. Phase 3 conducted a follow-up audit using the same criteria to evaluate changes in compliance and inform sustainability.ResultsAfter implementation, the incidence of severe RD(≥ Grade Ⅲ) decreased from 9.09%(2/22) to 0% (0/22 cases) at follow up. A comparison between the pre-implementation and post-implementation findings showed significant improvements for all audit criteria, and patients’ compliance with RD management protocol increased to between 59.09% and 100%. Notably, compliance increased from 0% to 100% for system-level criteria (protocol availability and multidisciplinary team establishment) and several key practice criteria.ConclusionsImplementing this evidence-based RD prevention and management program improved practice compliance and reduced severe RD among patients with NPC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04548-5.Keywords: Radiation dermatitis, Implementation science, Nasopharyngeal carcinoma, Quality improvement

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is an endemic malignancy in Southern China and Southeast Asia, with particularly high incidence in some high-risk areas. China accounts for approximately 47% of the global NPC burden, and remains among the countries with the highest incidence worldwide [1]. Radiotherapy is a cornerstone of NPC treatment. However, treatment strategies are stage-dependent, with radiotherapy alone typically used for early-stage disease and concurrent chemoradiotherapy commonly used for locoregionally advanced disease [2]. Consequently, many patients undergo prolonged and intensive treatment courses, during which radiation dermatitis (RD) is a common acute radiation-related toxicity. RD may present with erythema, pigmentation, dry or moist desquamation, exudation, and, in severe cases, ulceration and necrosis [3]. Because ionizing radiation affects structures beyond the epidermis, it can impair microvasculature and tissue repair, delay healing, and increase the risk of skin breakdown and secondary infection [4]. Therefore, effective prevention and timely management of RD are important for maintaining skin integrity and supporting ongoing treatment.

RD occurs in approximately 85%–95% of patients receiving radiotherapy for NPC [5]. Although advances in radiotherapy techniques and supportive care have reduced the incidence of severe RD overall, patients receiving head and neck radiotherapy remain at relatively high risk because of regional characteristics such as skin folds, moisture from perspiration, and ultraviolet exposure, which may aggravate skin reactions [6]. Previous studies have reported that the incidence of grade III RD in the head and neck region can be as high as 49%. Moderate-to-severe RD can increase symptom burden, and psychological distress, impair quality of life, and in some patients may contribute to treatment interruption or modification [7]. Management is stratified by severity and daily skin care, prevention and treatment of secondary skin infection, and appropriate dressings and protectant [8].

Nurses play an essential role in RD management, including risk identification, assessment, prevention, intervention, evaluation of outcomes, and patient education [9]. The Radiotherapy Nursing Outpatient Clinic at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center provides full-cycle symptom management for patients receiving radiotherapy. RD is a major reason for clinic visits, and NPC-related RD constitutes a large proportion of the workload [10]. There is a high demand from patients for nursing care related to RD. However, gaps remain between current practice and best evidence [11]. These include inconsistent identification of high-risk factors and variability in the content and delivery of education and nursing interventions, which may contribute to suboptimal outcomes, particularly for Grade II–III RD. A standardized, system-wide protocol is therefore needed to support consistent risk assessment, skin assessment frequency, evidence-based prevention and management, and follow-up for NPC patients [12].

Methods

Design and implementation framework

This study was a best practice implementation project designed using the JBI Practical Application of Clinical Evidence System (JBI PACES) [13]and Getting Research into Practice (GRiP) framework [14]. As shown in Fig. 1, the project followed a structured three-phase audit-and-feedback cycle. (1) Phase 1 involved forming a multidisciplinary project team, operationalizing evidence-based criteria, and conducting a baseline audit to assess compliance with the evidence-based criteria; in parallel, a baseline knowledge survey on RD prevention and management was administered to nurses using a self-developed questionnaire (Supplementary File 1), and baseline data on RD severity were collected. (2) Phase 2 focused on reviewing and reflecting on the baseline findings, identifying barriers to evidence uptake, and implement GRIP-informed strategies. (3) Phase 3 comprised a follow-up audit using the same criteria to evaluate changes in compliance. RD severity outcomes were also compared before and after implementation to support sustainability planning and future practice improvement.

JBI PACES/GRiP evidence implementation flow for Radiation Dermatitis (RD) prevention and management

Setting

The project was conducted in the Radiation Oncology Department of Sun Yat-sen university cancer centre, a tertiary cancer center in China. The project was implemented in two care settings: the oncology radiotherapy nursing clinic which focuses on managing radiation-induced toxicities, and Radiotherapy Ward One, which primarily admits patients with NPC.

Participants and data sources

Audit data were obtained from patients, nurses, and relevant clinical and administrative documentation. The audit included 22 eligible patients and 26 nurses in the baseline phase, and the same audit framework was applied in the follow-up phase (with different patient cases depending on the audit period). Eligible patients were adults with pathology-confirmed NPC who were receiving radiotherapy during the audit period. Detailed patient characteristic were shown in Table 1 and all patients were treated with volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT). Nurses involved in NPC radiotherapy care were included in the nursing audit. 26 nurses participated in the project, with a mean age of 34.31 ± 6.43 years and mean working tenure of 12.25 ± 7.34 years. The group comprised 24 females (92.3%) and Twenty-five nurses held at least a bachelor’s degree, including three with a master’s degree or higher. Six nurses were oncology advanced practice specialist nurses.

Audit criteria

Audit criteria were derived from the best available evidence on prevention and management in patients with NPC. The criteria covered system readiness (e.g., protocol availability and multidisciplinary support), nurse practice (e.g., assessment, documentation, patient education, and monitoring), and patient-level participation (e.g., self-monitoring and self-care behaviors). Compliance with each criterion was assessed as“met” or “not met” according to predefined operational definitions (Table 3).

Phase 1: Team establishment and baseline audit

Stakeholders, including patients and clinical staff, were engaged throughout the project. A multidisciplinary project team (n = 14) was established and included the project executive director, an evidence-based nurse, department leadership, head nurses, radiation oncologists, radiation therapists/technicians, specialist nurses, and wound specialists. Before the baseline audit, the project team held meetings to finalize audit criteria, define operational indicators, and standardize data collection procedures. Patients included in each audit were selected consecutively from those who met eligibility criteria during the audit period. Audit data were collected through patient interviews, nurse interviews/knowledge assessment, direct observation where applicable, and review of electronic and paper-based records, as specified for each criterion in Table 3. The baseline audit was conducted from February 1 to March 18, 2024, and included patients who received radiotherapy between November 17, 2023 and January 26, 2024. Compliance results were entered into Microsoft Excel to calculate criterion-level compliance rates. RD severity (Grade II or higher) was also recorded.

Phase 2: GRiP-based barrier analysis and strategy implementation

After the baseline audit, the results were presented to both the project team members and the clinical staff. Based on the Knowledge-to-Action framework, the project team conducted a barrier analysis from three perspectives: the evidence itself, the practice environment, and potential adopters, then developed strategies to overcome these barriers. Through feedback from nurses, potential implementation barriers were identified. Subsequently, A structured action plan was developed, employing institutional resources to formulate barrier-mitigation strategies for facilitating evidence-based practice implementation. A GRiP report was then shared with the nursing staff, followed by a communication meeting to introduce the project objectives and clarify individual roles.

Phase 3: Follow-up audit

A second audit was conducted using the same criteria in the baseline audit. This audit spanned 3 weeks using the same number of patients and the same nurses from 10 November 2024 to 1 December 2024.

Data analysis

Compliance data changes were analyzed using descriptive statistics in Microsoft Excel 2019, and displayed as percentage variations from the initial baseline.

Ethical considerations

According to the notice issued by the National Health Commission of China [15] regarding the ethical review procedures for human life sciences and medical research, continuous quality improvement projects do not fall under the scope of ethical review. These projects do not involve new technologies or new diagnostic and treatment standards, but are intended to help institutions provide medical services based on the best evidence (evidence summaries). The risk involved is lower than minimal risk; therefore, ethics approval was waived by the Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center. Although ethics approval was waived, we still obtained verbal informed consent from all patients and nurses involved, explaining the purpose and process of the project. Patients and nurses have the right to refuse participation. All collected data were anonymized to protect the privacy of participants. This investigation conforms with the Principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Phase 1: Baseline audit

Baseline audit results are presented in Fig. 2. Compliance was 0% for nine criteria (1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 9, 10, 13, and 16), indicating the absence of a written protocol and formal MDT, incomplete documentation of RD risk assessment, lack of use of a validated patient-reported tool for severe RD impact, no routine patient photo documentation, limited availability and distribution of standardized education materials, lack of individualized RD care documentation, and gaps in infection-control competency. Higher baseline performance was observed for Criterion 11 (nurse training in RD education/management; 96.15%) and Criterion 6 (regular RD assessment; 65.38%). Criterion 8 showed that 86.36% of patients reported receiving RD education before radiotherapy. However, correct use of the RTOG scale (Criterion 4) was limited (42.31%), and only 4.54% of patients demonstrated adequate knowledge of daily RD self-care (Criterion 12). Compliance with evidence-based interventions was also low, including appropriate silicone dressing use for Grade ≥ II RD (Criterion 14; 9.09%) and correct use of skin protectants (Criterion 15; 18.18%). Among the 22 audited patients, the incidence of Grade III RD was 9.09% (2/22).

Barrier 1: Radiotherapy involves complex workflows across multiple departments, creating coordination challenges in the absence of structured MDT support. To address this barrier, an MDT was established including department leadership, radiation oncologists, head nurses, radiation therapists, oncology specialist nurses, nutrition specialist nurses, wound/ostomy nurses, and evidence-based nursing specialists. The MDT oversaw protocol development, implementation, supervision, and quality control. MDT meetings were held monthly to discuss cases and provide coordinated guidance. Patients with Grade I–II RD were followed up weekly, and those with Grade > II RD were followed up twice weekly.

Barrier 2: There was no standardized RD management protocol, leading to inconsistent practices. The MDT developed a protocol covering(1) standardized assessment, (2) structured patient education, (3) evidence-based interventions for Grade≥ⅡRD, and (4) systematic follow-up and monitoring. Training was provided to nursing staff, and compliance monitoring was conducted by head nurses and specialist nurses to support implementation.

Barrier 3: RD has multiple risk factors, but no standardized approach to risk stratification was in place. The team developed an RD risk assessment tool based on literature review, an existing Grade III RD risk prediction model, and expert consultation (Supplementary File 2). The tool includes 12 items and classifies patients as low or high risk. High-risk patients were scheduled for clinic assessment twice weekly, whereas low-risk patients were scheduled once weekly.

Barrier 4: Staff had limited access to updated knowledge and skills training on RD prevention and care. A structured training plan for physicians, ward nurses, and outpatient specialist nurses was implemented. Six training sessions (two online) were delivered, each lasting one hour. Training content was based on the evidence summary and included RD manifestations, risk factors, assessment tools, prevention and treatment strategies, daily care, medication precautions, and use of the electronic assessment system. Knowledge acquisition was assessed after training using questions or electronic quizzes.

Barrier 5: Patients and caregivers had limited knowledge and did not consistently adhere to daily self-care measures. Education was delivered using multimodal, patient-friendly approaches, including face-to-face teaching, ward-based looping educational videos, and distribution of written materials (handbooks and patient diary forms). Caregiver involvement was encouraged. An electronic health management platform was also developed to support symptom reporting, photo submission, timely reminders, and real-time nurse–patient communication.

Barrier 6: Implementing the protocol increased perceived workload for nurses. To improve efficiency, the risk assessment form and RD observation record were integrated into the nursing and hospital information systems with automated scoring. The system was linked to portable tablets with camera functions, enabling bedside assessment, photo capture, and direct documentation, thereby reducing duplication and travel time between the bedside and nursing station.

Phase 3: Follow-up audit and postimplementation survey

A follow-up audit was conducted after implementation. No cases of severe (Grade III) RD were identified at follow-up. System-level changes included development of a written RD management protocol and establishment of an MDT, both improving from 0% to 100% compliance (Criteria 1 and 2; Fig. 2). Several additional criteria also reached 100% compliance at follow-up, including use of a validated patient-reported tool for severe RD impact (Criterion 5), availability of standardized RD education materials (Criterion 9), provision of education materials to patients/caregivers (Criterion 10), individualized RD care delivery (Criterion 13), and infection-control competency/protocol application (Criterion 16). Compliance with RD risk-factor assessment increased from 0% to 68.18% (Criterion 3), and correct use of the RTOG grading scale improved from 42.31% to 73.08% (Criterion 4). Regular RD assessment tailored to patient condition improved to 92.31% (Criterion 6). Patient photo documentation increased to 59.09% (Criterion 7). All patients reported receiving more targeted education from nurses (Criterion 8), and correct use of skin protectants improved substantially (Criterion 15).

Discussion

Interpretation of data

This project used the JBI audit-and-feedback approach to strengthen evidence-based RD prevention and management for patients with NPC. Across two audit cycles (22 patients and 26 nurses per audit), compliance improved for all criteria, and the incidence of severe RD (Grade III) decreased from 9.09% to 0%. These findings indicate that a structured implementation program can improve consistency of care and may reduce clinically meaningful RD outcomes.

A written RD management protocol was developed from the best available evidence and implemented with oversight from an MDT. The MDT (radiation oncologists, nurses, radiation therapists, wound specialists, and nutrition specialists) supported protocol development, addressed implementation barriers, and provided ongoing quality control. Multidisciplinary collaboration has been shown to improve coordination and effectiveness of care in quality-improvement initiatives [16]. This team forms an organic combination that is patient-centered and supported by a group of multidisciplinary experts, ensuring that patients receive standardized and individualized dermatitis prevention and management in inpatient, outpatient, and radiation therapy settings. Firstly, the best available evidence recommends obtaining detailed patient medical histories related to individual risk factors for risk identification [17], although there are no validated assessment tools. We have developed a risk assessment tool to address this clinical need and collaborated with the hospital’s IT department to integrate it into the nursing system, which achieved a significant increase in evaluation rates, rising from 0% to 68.18%. This is likely related to the application of electronic information systems, which can reduce documentation time, improve work efficiency, and increase motivation [18].

Secondly, assessing the grade of RD correctly is the first step in providing targeted care. However, since the department has not specified which dermatitis grading tool to use, nurses choose between the two common grading tools, CTCAE or RTOG, based on their preference, resulting inconsistency assessments in health care record. Therefore, it was difficult to compare the changes of skin conditions and evaluate effects of skin management. In the first audit, the correct rate of nurses use the RTOG grading system was 42.31%, The reason may be that the differences between the two assessment tools, leading to confusion in the content of the assessments. RTOG has been shown to have high rates of inter-observer variability when compared with other established systems [17]. After the intervention, nurses were required to use the RTOG as the skin grading tool and provided targeted training to improve the accuracy of assessments. Although the accuracy of assessments improved, it still did not reach the target value of 100%, further training should be enhanced. In addition, nurses use electronic follow-up platform assess the patient’s skin-related subjective symptoms and their severity(criterion 5), as well as evaluate the impact of RD on daily life, social interactions, and emotional well-being. If the patient’s symptoms are moderate or severe, nurses conduct online inquiries to help patients cope with their symptoms and distress, which exemplifies holistic nursing care. Electronic platform may provide an effective strategy for improving symptom distress, self-efficacy, and fatigue among patients with cancer [19].

All nurses received training on RD and required to provided personalized guidance and supervision based on the patient’s dermatitis grade according recommendations [20]. For GradeⅠor lower, the focus is primarily on prevention, including daily care measures. For GradeⅡor higher, in addition to daily care measures, the approach includes the use of skin protectants and dressings, wound management, measures to promote wound healing, and procedures for handling infectious wounds. The criterion on the patient include daily self-observation of the radiation site by photo documentation(criteria 7), health education acceptance rate(criteria 8), possession of a written educational manual(criteria 9), mastery of daily care measures(criteria 11), usage rate of wound dressings for GradeⅡRD(criteria 13), and correct use of skin protectant(criteria 14). Compliance rates reached 90.91%~100% for all criteria except numbers 7 and 11.

Strategies to overcome barriers

Several barriers hindered the implementation of management protocol of dermatitis, including the patient’s adherence to regular assessments, lack of time, other professionals’ knowledge, and communication between nurses and patients [21]. Strategies to overcome these barriers included measures to remind patients, knowledge reinforcement to nurses, the application of the digital assessment and follow-up platform, improving the accessibility of educational materials (educational manuals and videos) and targeted reinforcements.

Challenge faced in this project

Although the overall outcome of this implementation was positive, we encountered challenges at various stages of the project. One challenge we faced was that, despite implementing a stamp on the radiotherapy process sheet to remind patients to visit the radiotherapy nursing clinic for RD risk assessment and health education, a small number of patients still did not comply. This may have been because the stamp was not noticeable or the patients did not take it seriously. To address this, we created a reminder slip that included the time, location, and a QR code for appointments, which the radiotherapy reception staff distributed along with the radiotherapy process sheet. This significantly improved the rates of risk assessment and health education acceptance.

Another challenge we faced was that, for outpatients with severe RD, nurses use wound swabs to determine if the wound is infectious. However, nurses do not have the authority to prescribe this test, requiring patients to see a doctor again to have it prescribed. This results in longer waiting times, and complicated procedures, which limit both nurses’ and patients’ adherence to managing infectious wounds. Therefore, collaboration between medical staff is one of the challenges we face. As with any change in practice, it understandably took a period of time for medical staff to fully accept the change.

Limitation of this project

Accurate grading of RD is foundational for selecting appropriate interventions. At baseline, grading practices were inconsistent because staff used different scales (CTCAE or RTOG), which reduced comparability across assessments and hindered monitoring of skin changes. Targeted training and standardization to the RTOG scale improved correct grading; however, performance did not reach the prespecified target, suggesting a need for continued training and calibration. Inter-observer variability is a known challenge in RD grading systems.

Sustainment of practice

The project achieved 100% compliance with eight best practice criteria and reduced the incidence of severe (Grade III) radiation dermatitis to 0%. At the hospital’s level, our evidence-based nursing practice plan for RD has been incorporated into the hospitals nursing guidelines. At the departmental level, the RD management protocol has been embedded in the annual education and training plan to promote consistent implementation, and we will continue to use the electronic system for assessment, monitoring, and follow-up. To support long-term sustainability, we plan to conduct six-monthly audits of criteria compliance.

Conclusions

The implementation of this project promotes compliance with evidence-based criteria among NPC patients for RD management and successfully accomplished most of all predefined objectives, which demonstrated how EBP improves clinical practice behavior. Notably from the audit results, there were no cases of Grade Ⅲ RD in the NPC patients undergoing radiotherapy, accompanied by substantially improved patient compliance with RD management protocols.
