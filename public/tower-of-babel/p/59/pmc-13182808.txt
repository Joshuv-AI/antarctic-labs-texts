Fluid Reservoir Characteristics in Established Scleral Lens Wear, Part II: Evaluation of Tear Exchange and Midday Fogging
Authors: Jennifer Swingle Fogt, Ellen Shorter, Jennifer S. Harthan, Cherie Nau, Amy Nau, Muriel Schornack
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Tear exchange is less robust with scleral lenses compared to other modalities. This study evaluates tear exchange with scleral lens wear and examines relationships between tear exchange and optical density of the fluid reservoir and patient-reported midday fogging in established scleral lens wearers.Methods:Participants reported if they experienced midday fogging. Tear exchange was evaluated by observing sodium fluorescein uptake into the post-lens fluid reservoir after 10 seconds, and 1, 2.5, and 5 minutes. Optical density of the fluid reservoir was measured using Scheimpflug imaging. Sensitivity, specificity, and negative and positive predictive value (NPV and PPV) of tear exchange as a predictor for fogging were calculated.Results:Tear exchange occurred within 5 minutes in 64% of eyes. The difference in fluid reservoir optical density for eyes with and without tear exchange was greatest when tear exchange occurred within 10 seconds. Tear exchange between 1-5 minutes was not predictive for fogging. Tear exchange occurring within 10 seconds showed low sensitivity and NPV, but high specificity (87%) and PPV (71%) for predicting fogging.Conclusion:Tear exchange occurred in a majority of eyes in this study. Scleral lens wearers with a rapid tear exchange rate were more likely to report midday fogging.

Introduction

Tear exchange during scleral lens wear has been reported to be less robust than with other contact lens modalities.1 Studies by Tan et al. used a modified slit lamp and novel image processing to quantify 2-D fluorescence patterns (fluorograms) during soft lens and scleral lens wear. 2,3 These studies found that fluorescein decay rates with scleral lenses were only 1-10% of those seen with soft lenses. Paugh reported tear elimination rates of less than 1% per minute in scleral lens wearers, compared to 35% per minute in non-lens wearers and over 6% per minute with soft lenses.1 Corneal rigid lenses have been shown to allow for considerably higher rates of tear exchange than soft lenses; Kok et al. reported no statistical differences in tear clearance from the anterior surface of the eye with or without a corneal rigid lens.4 The alignment of the scleral lens landing zone with the ocular surface may influence tear exchange. Nau et al. used flurophotometry to measure fluorescein concentration in the post-lens fluid reservoir and found that after 2 hours of wear, a scleral lens with a spherical landing zone retained 46% of the original concentration, while a quadrant-specific landing zone retained 99%.5

The studies referenced above used specialized instruments to precisely measure changes in fluorescein concentration beneath a scleral lens. However, these tools can be cumbersome and are not easily integrated into routine clinical practice. A less complicated approach to evaluating fluorescein exchange during scleral lens wear was described by Tan et al. in a study of healthy neophyte volunteers.3 Fluorescein was applied to the superior bulbar conjunctiva 20-30 minutes after scleral lens application, and an observer recorded the time elapsed until fluorescein appeared within the post-lens fluid reservoir. This technique found that fluorescein appeared in the post-lens fluid reservoir in less than 2 minutes in 61.7% of eyes, while in 33.3% it was not detected within 5 minutes.3 Although this approach does not provide precise quantification, it offers a quick and practical way to assess whether tear exchange occurs and an estimate of its rate.

The desirability of tear exchange during scleral lens wear remains under some debate. Introducing fresh tears into the post-lens reservoir may support corneal health by supplying oxygen and reducing hypoxic complications.6 Tear exchange may also help dilute debris and metabolic waste in the post-lens reservoir.7 However, tear exchange has also been implicated as a contributor to midday fogging, a result of the ingress of debris entering the post-lens fluid reservoir.8 To date, no prospective clinical trials have compared visual or physiological outcomes of scleral lens wear with and without tear exchange, and there is no conclusive evidence that tear exchange increases debris accumulation in the post-lens fluid reservoir.

Many studies assessing tear exchange during scleral lens wear have been conducted on healthy volunteers. Single-center studies involving habitual scleral lens wearers often include small sample sizes. The purposes of this multicenter study are to evaluate the presence or absence of tear exchange in established scleral lens wearers, to assess the impact of landing zone design on tear exchange, to explore the relationship between tear exchange and the optical density of the post-lens fluid reservoir, and to determine whether participant-reported midday fogging is associated with tear exchange or fluid reservoir optical density.

Methods:

This prospective, observational study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of The Ohio State University. Three clinical sites participated: Illinois College of Optometry, Chicago, IL; University of Illinois at Chicago, Chicago, IL; and Mayo Clinic, Rochester, MN. Informed consent was obtained prior to data collection, and all procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Data were recorded using REDCap, a secure web-based application.9,10

Established scleral lens wearers were eligible to participate if they had worn the same scleral lens for at least 6 months, a minimum of 5 hours per day, and at least 6 days per week. There were no restrictions on the indication for lens wear, and lenses could be worn in one or both eyes. All study procedures were conducted after at least 2 hours of lens wear on the day of the visit. Participants were recruited during routine follow-up visits. Consecutive eligible patients were invited to participate, and no renumeration was provided. Data were collected from both eyes when applicable, but only data for one eye per participant was included in the analysis.11

Demographic data including age, gender, ethnicity, and indication for scleral lens wear were recorded for each participant. Lens parameters for each eye were noted, including diameter and landing zone design (spherical, bitoric, or quadrant-specific). Participants were also asked whether they experienced midday fogging.

During the study visit, Scheimpflug imaging was performed using a Pentacam (Oculus, Wexlar, Germany). Images captured the scleral lens, post-lens fluid reservoir, and ocular structures, as described by Schornack et al. 12 After imaging, sodium fluorescein was applied to the ocular surface using moistened Fluorescein Sodium Ophthalmic Strips (1.0mg). Uptake of sodium fluorescein was used as a clinical surrogate for tear exchange, with the assessments for the presence of fluorescein in the post-lens fluid reservoir occurring at 10 seconds, 1 minute, 2.5 minutes, and 5 minutes after instillation while using a biomicroscope.

Scheimpflug image analysis followed previously published methods in order to measure the depth and optical density of the fluid reservoir.12,13 The central cornea was estimated as the midpoint between superior and inferior iris margins on a vertical image. The measurement cursor was positioned midway between the posterior surface of the scleral lens and the anterior surface of the cornea at this location. The densitometry value of the fluid reservoir was also found at this position and was calculated by the instrument’s software as a percentage of optical density. This measurement reflects any debris or haze occurring in the fluid reservoir, and was used as an objective measurement of midday fogging as previously reported.

Statistical Analysis

Data from one eye per participant were analyzed. For bilateral lens wearers, the eye was randomly selected using a random number generator. Statistical analyses were performed using Minitab (Version 21.3.1,Minitab, LLC, State College, PA). A two-sample t-test was used to compare the mean optical density of the post-lens fluid reservoir between lenses with and without tear exchange at each time point. Bonferroni testing was completed for multiple comparisons. Binary logistic regression assessed the effects of landing zone design and lens diameter on tear exchange at each time point. A regression analysis was used to assess haptic landing zone upon optical density. A two-sample t-test compared post-lens fluid reservoir density between participants with and without self-reported midday fogging. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) of midday fogging based on observed tear exchange at each time point were also calculated.

Results

Thirty-six eyes were analyzed. The study population consisted of 23 female and 13 male participants with a mean ± standard deviation of 49 ± 16 years (range 21 to 77 years).

Following sodium fluorescein application on the superior ocular surface, dye migration into the post-lens reservoir was observed in 7 eyes (19%) within 10 seconds, 16 (44%) within 1 minute, 19 eyes (53%) within 2.5 minutes, and 23 eyes (64%) within 5 minutes.

Binary logistic regression revealed that neither landing zone design (spherical, toric, or quadrant-specific) nor lens diameter significantly predicted tear exchange at any of the assessed time points. Regression analysis found that landing zone design did not have a significant impact upon optical density.

Comparison of post-lens fluid reservoir densitometry values revealed no statistically significant differences in optical density between eyes with and without tear exchange at 10 seconds, 1 minute, 2.5 minutes and 5 minutes after dye application. The largest difference in mean optical density was observed when comparing the eyes with and without tear exchange at the 10 second mark, but this did not reach statistical significance (p=0.09). Bonferroni testing requires a p-value of less than 0.01 for finding statistical significance as a result of the multiple comparisons. Discussion

Tear exchange, as indicated by the presence of sodium fluorescein in the post-lens fluid reservoir after instillation outside the lens, was observed in 64% of eyes in this sample of established scleral lens wearers using a simple, clinically applicable method. This proportion aligns with findings reported by Tan et al., who used a similar technique.3 In their study, fluorescein was observed in the post-lens reservoir within 1 minute in 43.3% of eyes, within 2 minutes in 61.7% of eyes, and within 5 minutes in 66.7% of eyes. In the current study, tear exchange was observed in 44% of eyes at 1 minute, 53% within 2.5 minutes, and 64% within 5 minutes. Although this method does not provide a precise quantification of tear exchange rate, it provides a practical way for clinicians to determine whether tear exchange is occurring. Faster fluorescein migration may suggest a more robust exchange rate. Tan’s simplified technique, as reproduced in this study, appears to be suitable for routine clinical use. Taken together, Tan’s studies and the current investigation clearly demonstrate that tear exchange can, in fact, occur during scleral lens wear.

Tear exchange is typically expressed as a percentage change in fluorescein concentration over time, rather than as an absolute volume. Skidmore et al. reported high variability in fluorescein intensity among both new and experienced scleral lens wearers.14 Both Tan and Skidmore also noted that the fluid reservoir beneath scleral lenses is considerably larger than that under soft or corneal rigid lenses.3,14 As a result, even a small percentage change in fluorescein concentration in the fluid reservoir of a scleral lens may represent a substantial volume of tear exchange. These differences in fluid reservoir volume of tears across lens modalities complicate direct comparisons of tear exchange. Additionally, variability in reservoir volume between individual scleral lens fits makes it difficult to define an “average” or “ideal” tear exchange rate. This study found no significant association between landing zone design or lens diameter and the likelihood of observing fluorescein in the post-lens reservoir following external dye application. This contrasts with findings from Nau et al., who reported a significant decrease in fluorescein concentration beneath lenses with spherical landing zones over two hours, while levels remained stable under lenses with quadrant-specific landing zones.5 It is important to note that these two studies used different methods to assess tear exchange, which may contribute to the differing results. Nevertheless, the apparent contradiction is noteworthy. The influence of lens diameter, landing zone design, and the degree of alignment between the lens and ocular surface on tear exchange warrants further investigation. Differences in fitting philosophy may also play a role. Practitioners who prioritize tear exchange may be able to achieve it across a range of lens designs.

Tear exchange has been implicated as a contributing factor to midday fogging, which is thought to result from debris accumulation in the post-lens fluid reservoir.15–17 While this loss of transparency is assumed to cause fogging symptoms, the origin of the debris remains unclear.18 If the debris originates from the ocular surface beneath the lens, increased tear exchange might help reduce fogging. Conversely, if debris enters the reservoir from outside the lens through tear exchange, minimizing exchange could be beneficial.

This study examined whether tear exchange affects fluid reservoir transparency by analyzing fluid reservoir optical density. No statistically significant differences in fluid reservoir density were found between eyes with and without observed tear exchange at any measured time point. Although tear exchange predicted fogging symptoms about 50% of the time when observed between 1 and 5 minutes, its predictive value increased to 71% when exchange occurred within 10 seconds of fluorescein application, suggesting that rapid tear exchange may be more strongly associated with fogging. The 87% specificity shows high diagnostic accuracy when assessing NaFl uptake into the fluid reservoir at 10 seconds as a test for midday fogging; and the 71% PPV reflects the high probability of the presence of midday fogging when NaFl uptake occurs at this same time point.19 Interestingly, there was no statistically significant difference in fluid reservoir optical density between participants who reported midday fogging and those who did not. These findings align with previous studies that found no correlation between tear exchange and midday fogging.14,20 While tear exchange may influence reservoir transparency or fogging symptoms, it is likely not the sole factor. Furthermore, reduced reservoir clarity does not fully explain patient-reported symptoms.

While the role of tear exchange in midday fogging is of interest, its impact on corneal metabolism during scleral lens wear may be even more clinically significant. Most studies assessing corneal metabolism focus on changes in corneal thickness as an indicator of corneal hypoxia. Oxygen availability is especially critical in eyes with epithelial compromise, stromal edema, or endothelial dysfunction. However, tear exchange, an additional potential source of corneal oxygen, has rarely been considered or quantified in studies. This gap may reflect the challenges of accurately measuring tear exchange rather than a lack of recognition of its importance.

Perhaps because of the challenges of quantifying tear exchange, traditional scleral lens fitting guidelines have focused primarily on spatial relationships between the lens and the ocular surface, without offering specific recommendations regarding tear exchange during wear.21–23 While corneal and limbal clearance and proper landing zone alignment are essential, tear exchange may also play a critical role in the physiological outcomes of scleral lens wear. In a healthy, non-lens-wearing eye, the tear film continuously refreshes the ocular surface, delivering oxygen and nutrients while removing metabolic waste. Scleral lenses disrupt this natural balance more than soft or corneal rigid lenses.1,24 Designing scleral lenses to promote tear exchange could help replicate the eye’s natural environment, potentially improving oxygen delivery and supporting long-term ocular health.

Studies involving habitual scleral lens wearers have inherent limitations. In this cross-sectional observational study, it was not possible to control all variables that could influence tear exchange. Participants in this study wore their habitual lenses, and no changes in parameters of those lenses were made. Prospective evaluation of changes in lens diameter or landing zone design was beyond the scope of this protocol. No previous studies existed upon which to base a sample size calculation for assessing sodium fluorescein uptake in the fluid reservoir of habitual lens wearers. Despite this limitation, assessing tear exchange in patients successfully wearing their own lenses provides clinically relevant insights. Furthermore, this study confirms that tear exchange occurs not only in neophyte volunteers wearing diagnostic lenses (as reported by Tan) but also in established scleral lens wearers with optimized fits. It also highlights the complex relationship between tear exchange, post-lens fluid reservoir clarity, and patient-reported midday fogging. The lack of strong associations among these factors underscores the multifactorial nature of midday fogging and suggests that reduced reservoir clarity alone does not fully explain patient symptoms.

Precisely quantifying tear exchange in scleral lens wear remains challenging, but this study demonstrates the utility of a simple method that can be easily integrated into clinical practice. Given the importance of maintaining healthy corneal metabolism through adequate oxygen delivery and waste removal, routine assessment of tear exchange may be beneficial for all scleral lens wearers. Managing external sources of tear film debris, such as cosmetic use, allergies, meibomian gland dysfunction and particulate-containing medications, is also important. In patients at higher risk for metabolic complications intentionally promoting tear exchange may help reduce the risks associated with scleral lens wear.
