Case of ossification of the ligamentum flavum causing cauda equina compression
Authors: Joseph Graham, William Cato-Addison
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Ossification of the ligamentum flavum (OLF) is characterized by heterotopic bone formation within the ligamentum flavum. It typically causes progressive, chronic spinal canal stenosis, but only rarely acute cauda equina syndromes.Case Description: A 29-year-old patient presented with cauda equina syndrome characterized by saddle numbness, urinary incontinence, and bilateral foot drop. She underwent an urgent decompression for a disc herniation, at which time the surgeons encountered severe OLF, causing cauda equina compression. After performing a computed tomography scan to assess the extent of ossification, the patient was taken back to the operating room for completion of the original laminectomy with extension to an adjacent level. Her postoperative recovery was uneventful.Conclusion: Here, a 29-year-old presented with cauda equina syndrome attributed to a herniated lumbar disc and single-level OLF.

INTRODUCTION

Ossification of the ligamentum flavum (OLF) is characterized by heterotopic bone formation within the ligamentum flavum. It has been reported more frequently in East Asian males and may cause chronic and progressive spinal canal stenosis.[4] Here, we present a 29-year-old female whose cauda equina syndrome was due both to a lumbar disc herniation and segmental level severe OLF.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 29-year-old female presented with cauda equina syndrome. Over a short period, she developed severe bilateral leg pain, progressive saddle numbness, urinary incontinence, and bilateral foot drop. An urgent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) lumbar spine picked up cauda equina compression at the L4-5 level due to central disc herniation with degenerative dorsal facet hypertrophy [Figure 1]. However, intraoperatively, an ossified midline lesion was encountered dorsal to the theca. Consideration was given to extending the incision and fenestrating the canal at an adjacent segment, but OLF is commonly multi-segmental, and it was unclear how many segments of the patient’s lumbar spine were involved. Drilling was aborted because of concerns of iatrogenic injury to the cauda equina. When the STAT computed tomography (CT) scan [Figure 2] demonstrated the OLF at L4-5, the patient was taken back for decompression at L3-4 and L4-5, along with an L4-5 discectomy. Within 2 postoperative months, the bilateral footdrop improved, along with her bladder function.

DISCUSSION

MR and CT scans are the gold standard for identifying cauda equina compression due to OLF. The MR best demonstrates compression of neural tissues (i.e., the cauda equina in this case) and the disc herniation, while the CT more readily defines the attendant OLF.[1] Notably, anatomically, the ligamentum flavum extends from C2 to S1 in a segmental fashion, being located on the undersurface of each lamina and extending to the superior border of the lamina below.[5] OLF most frequently involves the thoracic spine (particularly T9–T12), is less common in the cervical spine, and is rare in the lumbar region.[4]

The surgical strategy depends on the extent and pattern of the ossification. In this case, surgical options included a multilevel laminectomy, although bilateral laminotomies (fenestration) were performed at the L3-4 level, and a full laminectomy at L4-5. Notably, this patient significantly improved postoperatively due to the timely recognition and treatment of her cauda equina syndrome. CONCLUSION

This study highlights a consideration of performing preoperative CT scans for patients presenting with acute cauda equina syndromes who have a hypointense signal on MR studies dorsal to the theca, which is not consistent with the normal appearance of the ligamentum flavum. In future cases of segmental lumbar OLF encountered intraoperatively, adjacent-level fenestration may be considered to establish a safe thecal plane before obtaining CT imaging to assess possible multilevel involvement.
