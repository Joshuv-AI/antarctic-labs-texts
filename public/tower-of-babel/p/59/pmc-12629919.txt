Evaluation of the inhibitory effects of emodin on Neospora caninum invasion and proliferation in vitro
Authors: Feixue Liu, Jianhua Li, Xin Li, Xu Zhang, Liuzhenxiu Yan, Lanbi Gao, Zhenzhen Liu, Pengtao Gong, Nan Zhang, Xichen Zhang, Xiaocen Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe impact of neosporosis on the livestock industry has worsening each year. Globally, annual losses were estimated to exceed $2.4 billion, with abortion rates reaching up to 47 % in affected herds. But no effective vaccines or drugs are currently available for prevention and treatment. This study aimed to investigate the inhibitory effects of emodin on Neospora caninum (N. caninum) infection and its potential mechanisms. The appropriates safe concentration of emodin was determined by the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. The anti-N. caninum effects of emodin in Vero cells, MDBK cells, macrophages, as well as the combined effects of emodin and niclosamide in Vero cells, were further verified using qPCR or Giemsa staining. Subsequently, the effects of direct incubation of tachyzoites with emodin on N. caninum invasion and proliferation were also examined. The activation of necroptosis by emodin was evaluated by measuring Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) levels in the supernatant and assessing cellular pMLKL levels. The CCK-8 results indicated that emodin exhibited low toxicity to Vero cells, MDBK cells, and macrophages. The qPCR results showed that emodin reduced the number of N. caninum in Vero cells, MDBK cells, and macrophages. Giemsa results showed that the number of tachyzoites within the parasitophorous vacuole was decreased accordingly. Notably, emodin directly affected tachyzoites, reducing its invasion capability by 55 %–63 % and its proliferation capacity by 62 %–88 %. Additionally, the combination of emodin with niclosamide significantly enhanced its antiparasitic effects compared with emodin alone. Western blot analysis showed that emodin significantly increased pMLKL levels and enhanced LDH release, assisting N. caninum in activating necroptosis. However, the inhibitory effects of emodin on N. caninum invasion and proliferation were not significantly altered in Mlkl−/− macrophages. In conclusion, emodin inhibited N. caninum infection may through a necroptosis-independent mechanism by directly targeting the parasite, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic candidate for neosporosis.Keywords: Neospora caninum, Emodin, Tachyzoites, Therapy, Drugs combination

Introduction

Neosporosis, a major cause of abortion in ruminants, is caused by the protozoan parasite Neospora caninum (N. caninum). Global studies report high N. caninum seroprevalence in aborted cattle (47 %) and fetuses (35 %), with even higher rates in Israeli sheep (67.4 %), confirming its role as a major abortifacient, another research estimated annual losses were to exceed $2.4 billion (Nayeri et al., 2022; Reichel et al., 2013; Tirosh-Levy et al., 2022). In endemic regions such as Argentina, N. caninum infection has been reported to cause severe annual economic losses of $33 million in dairy cattle and $12 million in beef cattle (Campero et al., 2023). Unfortunately, there are currently no effective vaccines or drugs for the prevention and treatment of neosporosis. The commercially available inactivated vaccine, Bovilis® Neoguard, was withdrawn from the market due to its low protective efficacy and may have increased the risk of early embryonic death (Weston et al., 2012). Thus, there is an urgent need to develop effective therapeutic agents to control N. caninum infection.

Compounds and extracts from different sources and targets were found to have anti-N. caninum activity. Some extracts of plant origin such as cordycepin and Piperaceae plant extracts have been found to have potential anti-N. caninum effects (Huang et al., 2024; Leesombun et al., 2017). In addition to plant extracts, Inonotus obliquus polysaccharide derived from the medicinal Inonotus obliquus have recently been shown to have excellent anti-N. caninum effects (Tang et al., 2024). Compounds screened for specific targets have shown promising antiparasitic effects: Endochin-like Quinolones (ELQs) class of compounds block the energy metabolism of N. caninum by targeting the mitochondrial complex III of N. caninum, which ultimately leads to death of N. caninum (Anghel et al., 2021). The Bump Kinase Inhibitors (BKIs) class of compounds inhibit the N. caninum invasion and proliferation signaling pathway by targeting calcium-dependent protein kinases (Imhof et al., 2021). In addition, some compounds that have demonstrated efficacy in other fields have also been discovered to have potential anti-tachyzoite effects, such as fluridone, triclosan, ezetimibe, and niclosamide (Ybañez et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2019; Larrazabal et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2025). However, no drug has yet been successfully translated into clinical application. Therefore, further exploration is necessary to identify more effective anti-N. caninum compounds.

Emodin, a naturally occurring anthraquinone derivative found in various medicinal herbs such as Rheum palmatum, Polygonum cuspidatum, and Fallopia multiflora, has been reported to exhibit anticancer, hepatoprotective, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial activities (Dong et al., 2016). Moreover, accumulating evidence suggests that emodin possesses antiparasitic properties against Leishmania, Trypanosoma cruzi, Toxoplasma gondii, Theileria annulata, and Plasmodium (Lin et al., 2017; Kiriyanthan et al., 2023; Adeyemi et al., 2023; De Lima et al., 2017; Guetchueng et al., 2023). Among the strategies for developing anti-N. caninum drugs, modulating the host immune response has emerged as a promising approach. Studies have demonstrated that regulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) to activate pyroptosis can effectively help the host control N. caninum infection in vivo (Li et al., 2020). Furthermore, evidence suggests that the execution of protein mixed lineage kinase domain-like (MLKL) mediated necroptosis may contribute to pathogen cleared (Zhang et al., 2022). In Leishmania research, inhibition of caspase-8 has been shown to trigger necroptosis, thereby eliminating Leishmania parasites in neutrophils (Barbosa et al., 2018). Based on this, we hypothesize that regulating necroptosis may also serve as an effective strategy for controlling N. caninum infection. Studies have shown that emodin induces necroptosis in prostate cancer cells and glioma cell line U251, thereby inhibiting tumor cell growth (Zhou, et al., 2020, 2023). These findings suggest that emodin may also have potent anti-N. caninum activity.

This study aimed to investigate whether emodin exhibits anti-N. caninum invasion and proliferation effects in Vero cells, MDBK cells, and macrophages. Subsequently, the direct impact of emodin on tachyzoites was further determined. After confirming anti-N. caninum activity of emodin, its potential synergistic effects when combined with niclosamide were further explored. Finally, Mlkl−/− macrophages were used to validate whether anti-N. caninum activity of emodin depends on its effects on necroptosis. This provides insights into the therapeutic potential of emodin for neosporosis.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Fetal bovine serum (FBS) and RPMI 1640 medium were purchased from VivaCell (Shanghai, China). Percoll was purchased from GE Healthcare (California, USA). DMEM/high medium was purchased from M&C Gene (Beijing, China). Thioglycollate medium was purchased from BD Pharmingen (Maryland, USA). Emodin, toltrazuril, niclosamide, and z-Val-Ala-Asp fluoromethyl ketone (zVAD) were procured from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China). VX-765 was purchased from Selleck (Shanghai, China). lipopolysaccharide (LPS) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was purchased from APExBIO (Houston, TX, USA). Giemsa staining and RIPA buffer kit were purchased from YEASEN (Shanghai, China). 2 × qPCR SYBR Green Mix was purchased from Abm (Jiangsu, China). Protease, and phosphatase inhibitors were procured from Boster (Wuhan, China). Bicinchoninic acid assay kit was purchased from Sangon (Shanghai, China). Cytotoxicity detection kit was purchased from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). phospho-MLKL antibody was purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). MLKL antibody was purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Glyceraldehyde-3-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (GAPDH) and goat anti-rabbit antibody were purchased from Proteintech Group (Wuhan, China). Rabbit anti-rat antibody was purchased from BOSTER (Wuhan, China). Rabbit anti-mouse antibody was purchased from HuaBio (Hangzhou, China).

Culture of parasites and cells

Neospora caninum

Neospora caninum (Nc-1 strain) tachyzoites were inoculated into Vero cell monolayers and maintained in RPMI 1640 medium containing 1 % FBS and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in 5 % CO2. Neospora caninum tachyzoites purification was performed via density gradient centrifugation using 40 % percoll for 30 min, as described in a previous protocol (Wang et al., 2020).

Vero and MDBK cells

Vero (African green monkey kidney) cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin. MDBK (Madin-Darb bovine kidney) cells were maintained in DMEM/high medium containing 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin.

Peritoneal macrophages

All procedures of animal manipulation and protocols were approved by the Jilin University (IACUC Permit Number: SY202007001). Macrophages were isolated from 8-week-old wild type (WT) C57BL/6J mice (n = 9) or congenic Mlkl−/− mice (n = 3), which were obtained from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Mice were acclimatized for 7 days prior to experiments. The mice were acclimatized, housed in individually ventilated close-top cages (5 mice/cage), and maintained on a 12 h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water.

The peritoneal macrophages were collected from mice 4 days post-intraperitoneal injection of 2.5 mL 3 % thioglycollate medium. The collected macrophages were counted and allowed to adhere to plates in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10 % FBS for 12 h. Macrophages were washed with PBS to remove non-adherent cells, and maintained in RPMI 1640 medium containing 1 % FBS.

Cell viability assay

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was performed to determine appropriate concentrations of emodin that minimize its impact on cells viability. In this experiment, 20 mM emodin, 30 mM toltrazuril, and 10 mM niclosamide stock solutions were prepared with DMSO according to the manufacture's protocol. Vero cells (1 × 104/well) were treated with 0, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, and 10 μM emodin for 24 h in the 96-well plates. MDBK cells (1 × 104/well) were treated with 0, 2, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, and 40 μM emodin for 24 h in the 96-well plates. Macrophages (1 × 105/well) were treated with 0, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18, and 20 μM emodin for 24 h in the 96-well plates.

Parasite invasion assay with emodin-treated cells

Vero cells (5 × 105/well) were pre-treated with DMSO, 30 μM toltrazuril (positive control), 2 μM emodin, 4 μM emodin, and 6 μM emodin for 3 h in the 12-well plates. MDBK cells (2 × 105/well) were pre-treated with DMSO, 30 μM toltrazuril, 1 μM emodin, 2 μM emodin, 4 μM emodin, 6 μM emodin, 8 μM emodin, and 10 μM emodin for 3 h in the 24-well plates. Macrophages (1.5 × 106/well) were pre-treated with DMSO (negative control), 30 μM toltrazuril, 4 μM emodin, 8 μM emodin, and 12 μM emodin for 3 h in the 12-well plates. Tachyzoites of N. caninum were added to each well at a multiplicity of infection (MOI, parasite: cell) = 1. After 3 h of infection, cells were washed and replaced with fresh medium to remove drug. Cell samples were collected for parasite burden detection or Giemsa staining after 12 h of infection (Fig. 1). The median inhibitory concentration (IC50) of emodin against N. caninum invasion was calculated using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc.).

Schematic representation of the experimental designs. This image was drawn by Figdraw.

Parasite proliferation assay with emodin-treated cells

Vero cells (5 × 105/well), MDBK cells (2 × 105/well), or macrophages (1.5 × 106/well) were infected with N. caninum tachyzoites (MOI = 1) for 3 h. Then, Vero cells were treated with DMSO, 30 μM toltrazuril, and emodin (2, 4, and 6 μM) for 21 h. MDBK cells were treated with DMSO, 30 μM toltrazuril, and emodin (4, 6, and 8 μM) for 21 h. Macrophages were treated with DMSO, 30 μM toltrazuril, and emodin (4, 8, and 12 μM) for 21 h (Fig. 1). After treatment, samples were collected for parasite burden detection or Giemsa staining. The IC50 of emodin against N. caninum proliferation was calculated using GraphPad Prism 8.0.

Parasite invasion assay with emodin-treated tachyzoites

Tachyzoites (5 × 105/tube) were incubated with 2, 4, and 6 μM emodin for 2 h, washed once with PBS, and used to infect Vero cells (5 × 105/well) for 3 h in the 12-well plates (Fig. 1). The cells were collected and used quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) to detect parasite burden.

Parasite proliferation assay with emodin-treated tachyzoites

Tachyzoites (5 × 105/tube) were incubated with 2, 4, and 6 μM emodin for 2 h, washed once with PBS, and used to infect Vero cells (5 × 105/well) for 24 h in the 12-well plates (Fig. 1). The cells were collected and used qPCR to detect parasite burden.

Compound combination assay

To evaluate the combined antiparasitic efficacy of emodin and niclosamide, a compound combination assay was performed. Vero cells (2 × 105/well) were infected with N. caninum tachyzoites (MOI = 1) for 3 h. Then, Vero cells were treated with DMSO, 30 μM toltrazuril, emodin (1, 2, and 4 μM), niclosamide (0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 μM), 1 μM emodin + 0.1 μM niclosamide, 1 μM emodin + 0.2 μM niclosamide, 1 μM emodin + 0.3 μM niclosamide, 2 μM emodin + 0.1 μM niclosamide, 2 μM emodin + 0.2 μM niclosamide, 2 μM emodin + 0.3 μM niclosamide, 4 μM emodin + 0.1 μM niclosamide, 4 μM emodin + 0.2 μM niclosamide, and 4 μM emodin + 0.3 μM niclosamide for 21 h. After treatment, samples were collected for parasite burden detection. To assess the interaction between emodin and niclosamide, the combination index (CI) was calculated using the SynergyFinder web application (Version 3.0). Results were analyzed according to the ZIP models. Drug interactions were classified based on CI values: CI ≥ 10 indicated synergy, CI between −10 and 10 was considered additive, and CI < −10 denoted antagonism.

Necroptosis activation assay

Macrophages (3 × 106/well) were infected with N. caninum tachyzoites (MOI = 1) for 3 h in the 6-well plates, followed by treatment with 4, 8, and 12 μM emodin for 9 h. Macrophages treated with 100 ng/mL LPS and 25 μM zVAD for 4 h served as the positive control for necroptosis activation, and these cells were neither infected with N. caninum nor treated with emodin. Unstimulated macrophages were used as the negative control. Except for the positive and negative control groups, all other groups were supplemented with 25 μM VX-765 to enhance necroptosis signaling.

Mlkl−/− macrophages were used to verify whether the anti-N. caninum effect of emodin was related to necroptosis. Briefly, WT and Mlkl−/− macrophages (1.5 × 106/well) were pre-treated with DMSO or 12 μM emodin for 3 h in the 12-well plates. Neospora caninum tachyzoites (MOI = 1) were added to each well for 12 h in invasion assay. WT and Mlkl−/− macrophages (1.5 × 106/well) were infected with N. caninum tachyzoites (MOI = 1) for 3 h. Then, macrophages were treated with DMSO or 12 μM emodin for 21 h in proliferation assay (Fig. 1). After treatment, samples were collected for parasite burden detection.

CCK-8 assay

After treatment, the cells were washed twice with PBS, then 100 μL of medium and 10 μL of Cell CCK-8 reagent were added to per well. The cells were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 1 h, and the reaction was terminated when the absorbance at 450 nm of the 0 μM emodin group reached approximately 1.0. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Infinite 200 Pro, TECAN, Männedorf, Switzerland). The 50 % effective cytotoxic concentration (CC50) of emodin on Vero cells, MDBK cells, and macrophages based on the cell viability after gradient concentration treatment with emodin was calculated using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Specifically, the selection index (SI) was calculated using the following formula: SI = CC50/IC50.

qPCR analysis

The number of intracellular N. caninum tachyzoites were quantified by qPCR, following a previously described method (Collantes-Fernández et al., 2002). Briefly, a standard curve was generated using 105, 104, 103, 102, and 101 tachyzoites and their corresponding Ct values. The Ct value of the Nc5 gene was determined from 200 ng of DNA using a 2 × qPCR SYBR Green Mix. The parasite burden was then calculated by comparing the Ct value to the standard curve. The primer sequences used were: Nc5-forward: 5′-ACTGGAGGCACGCTGAACAC-3′, Nc5-reverse: 5′-AACAATGCTTCGCAAGAGGAA-3'. The cycling conditions: 95 °C 3 min, (95 °C 15 s, 60 °C 1 min) × 40 cycles.

Wright-Giemsa staining

Vero or MDBK cells climbing sheets were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. After fixation, the cells were stained with a Giemsa staining kit according to the procedure. The slides were washed twice under running water, mounted, and observed under a microscope (BX43F, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) for counting and imaging. Three random fields of view were captured for each slide, and the number of cells infected with N. caninum was counted in each image. Percent of cells infected with N. caninum (%) = (number of cells infected with parasites/total number of cells) × 100. The number of tachyzoites in one hundred parasitophorous vacuoles (PVs) were counted, and the number of PVs containing 1, 2, 4, 8 and above tachyzoites was recorded separately.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay

The release of LDH in culture supernatants was quantified using a commercial cytotoxicity detection kit according to the manufacturer's protocol. Cell culture supernatant (120 μL) was mixed with 60 μL LDH working solution and incubated in the dark for 30 min. Absorbance values at 490 nm were recorded using a microplate reader.

Western blotting

Cell samples were lysed using RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid assay kit. Samples were mixed with loading buffer and boiled for 10 min. Proteins were separated using 10 % separating gel and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes via wet transfer. The membranes were blocked with 5 % BSA and incubated overnight with primary antibodies: anti-phospho-MLKL (1:2000), anti-MLKL (1:1000), and anti-GAPDH (1:1000). The membranes were then incubated for 1 h with corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies: goat anti-rabbit (1:10000), rabbit anti-rat (1:10000), and rabbit anti-mouse (1:10000). Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagent and imaged by a chemiluminescence detection system (6100, Clinx Science Instruments, Shanghai, China).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism 8.0. Each experiment was performed with three biological replicates, and each biological replicate included three technical replicates. All data were statistically analyzed, and representative results are shown as mean ± S.E.M. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired Student's t-test was applied. Multi-group analyses utilized one-way ANOVA with Dunnett's post hoc test for treatment versus control comparisons, or Tukey's test for all pairwise comparisons. A P < 0.05 threshold defined statistical significance.

Results

Emodin inhibited the invasion and proliferation of N. caninum in Vero cells

Vero cells were used as non-immune cells to detect the antiparasitic ability of emodin. The CCK-8 assay results revealed that emodin at concentrations above 6 μM reduced cell viability below 80 %, whereas cells maintained above 90 % viability at 3 μM (Fig. 2A). The CC50 value of emodin against Vero cells was calculated to be 17.49 μM. Based on viability results, 2, 4, and 6 μM concentrations were selected for further tests in Vero cells.

(C) Flowchart of N. caninum proliferation assay in a Vero model and qPCR detection of the proliferation level of N. caninum in Vero cells. (D) The SI value of emodin inhibiting the invasion and proliferation of N. caninum in Vero cells. The results of qPCR analysis revealed that 4 and 6 μM emodin significantly inhibited parasite invasion by 40 % and 57 %, respectively, compared with DMSO group, whereas 2 μM emodin had no inhibitory effect (Fig. 2B). The IC50 value of emodin in inhibiting N. caninum invasion in the Vero cells was calculated to be 5.17 μM. Similarly, parasite proliferation decreased by 47 % and 62 % at 4 and 6 μM emodin, respectively, whereas 2 μM emodin had no significant effect (Fig. 2C). The IC50 value of emodin in inhibiting N. caninum proliferation in the Vero cells was calculated to be 4.51 μM. The SI values of emodin against N. caninum invasion and proliferation in Vero cells were 3.38 and 3.88, respectively (Fig. 2D). The SI values indicated that emodin was a safe and effective anti-N. caninum compound in the Vero cells.

Emodin controlled the number of tachyzoites in the PVs of the Vero cells

Giemsa staining was used to detect the effect of emodin on tachyzoites in PVs (Fig. 3A). Infection rates and tachyzoite numbers per PV were then quantified. Quantification revealed that in the invasion model, the infection rate decreased from 24 % in the DMSO group to 12 %, 9 %, and 3 % following 2, 4, and 6 μM emodin treatment, respectively (Fig. 3B). In the proliferation model, emodin showed modest inhibition on infection rate from 76 % in the DMSO group to 51 % at 4 and 6 μM (Fig. 3C). By counting the number of tachyzoites within each PV, we found that in the invasion model, the DMSO group mainly contained PVs with 4 or ≥8 tachyzoites, while the 2 and 4 μM emodin-treated groups mainly contained PVs with 4 tachyzoites, and the 6 μM group was dominated by PVs with 2 or 4 tachyzoites (Fig. 3D). In the proliferation model, PVs with ≥8 tachyzoites were predominant in the DMSO and 2 μM emodin groups, whereas the 4 μM group mainly contained PVs with 4 or ≥8 tachyzoites, and the 6 μM group primarily contained PVs with 2 or 4 tachyzoites (Fig. 3E).

Emodin inhibited the invasion and proliferation of N. caninum in MDBK cells

Given that N. caninum primarily infects ruminants, the MDBK cell model was employed in this study to further evaluate the anti-N. caninum efficacy of emodin. CCK-8 assays revealed that emodin at concentrations above 10 μM reduced cell viability below 80 %, whereas cells maintained above 90 % viability at 5 μM (Fig. 4A). The CC50 value of emodin against MDBK cells was calculated to be 29.25 μM. Based on viability results, 4, 6, and 8 μM concentrations were selected for further tests in MDBK cells. The results of qPCR analysis revealed that 4, 6, 8, and 10 μM emodin significantly inhibited parasite invasion by 52 %, 51 %, 71 %, and 73 %, respectively, compared with DMSO group, whereas 1 and 2 μM emodin had no inhibitory effect (Fig. 4B). The IC50 value of emodin inhibit N. caninum invasion in the MDBK cells was calculated to be 4.9 μM. Similarly, parasite proliferations were decreased by 48 %, 59 %, 67 %, and 71 % at 4, 6, 8, and 10 μM emodin, respectively, whereas 1 and 2 μM emodin had no significant effect (Fig. 4C). The IC50 value of emodin in inhibiting N. caninum proliferation in the MDBK cells was calculated to be 4.66 μM. The SI values of emodin against N. caninum invasion and proliferation in MDBK cells were 5.97 and 6.28, respectively (Fig. 4D). The SI values indicated that emodin was a safe and effective anti-N. caninum compound in the MDBK cells.

Emodin controlled the number of tachyzoites in the PVs of the MDBK cells

Similarly, Giemsa staining was employed to assess the invasion rate of N. caninum into MDBK cells and to quantify the number of tachyzoites in the PVs (Fig. 5A). Quantification analysis revealed that in the invasion model, the infection rate was significantly decreased from 51 % in the DMSO group to 33 %, 22 %, and 10 % following treatment with 4, 6, and 8 μM emodin, respectively (Fig. 5B). In the proliferation assay, emodin exhibited a modest inhibitory effect on the infection rate, which decreased from 50 % in the DMSO group to 37 %, 30 %, and 33 % at 4, 6, and 8 μM, respectively (Fig. 5C). By counting the number of tachyzoites within each PV, we found that in the invasion model, the DMSO group mainly contained PVs with 4 or ≥8 tachyzoites, while the 4 and 6 μM emodin-treated groups mainly contained PVs with 2 or 4 tachyzoites, and the 8 μM group was dominated by PVs with 1 or 2 tachyzoites (Fig. 5D). In the proliferation model, PVs with ≥8 tachyzoites were predominant in the DMSO and 4 μM emodin groups, whereas the 6 μM group mainly contained PVs with 4 or ≥8 tachyzoites, and the 8 μM group primarily contained PVs with 1 or 2 tachyzoites (Fig. 5E). In summary, emodin markedly decreased the infection rate and the number of tachyzoites per PV of MDBK cells.

Emodin inhibited the invasion and proliferation of N. caninum in macrophages

The cytotoxicity of emodin on macrophages was assessed over 24 h. The toxicity exhibited a dose-dependent relationship, with a CC50 value of 25.03 μM. While macrophage viability was unaffected at 4 μM, it declined significantly at higher concentrations, reaching 90 % at 8 μM and 84 % at 12 μM (Fig. 6A). Based on these results, 4, 8, and 12 μM were selected for macrophages experiments in this study. Invasion and proliferation assays were used to evaluate whether emodin exerted an anti-N. caninum effect in macrophages. qPCR analysis revealed that emodin reduced parasite invasion by 49 %, 69 %, and 71 % at 4, 8, and 12 μM emodin groups, respectively, compared with DMSO group (Fig. 6B). Similarly, parasite proliferation was reduced by 28 %, 49 %, and 63 % at 4, 8, and 12 μM emodin groups, respectively (Fig. 6C). Notably, emodin showed significantly stronger inhibition of N. caninum invasion than toltrazuril.

Emodin directly inhibited the invasion and proliferation ability of tachyzoites

Giemsa staining analysis indicated that emodin more effectively inhibited the invasion rate of N. caninum in the invasion model compared to the proliferation model. The invasion and proliferation ability of tachyzoites was analyzed after treatment with emodin (Fig. 7). The results showed that emodin pretreatment significantly impaired tachyzoite invasion, with reductions of 55 %, 62 %, and 63 % at 2, 4, and 6 μM emodin groups, respectively (Fig. 7A). Similarly, parasite proliferation was markedly decreased by 62 %, 82 %, and 88 % at 2, 4, and 6 μM emodin groups, respectively (Fig. 7B). These data demonstrate that direct exposure to emodin substantially compromises the ability of tachyzoites to invade host cells and to proliferate within them.

Combination therapy of emodin enhanced the anti-N. caninum effect

The above results demonstrated that emodin exhibited a strong anti-N. caninum effect, particularly when directly targeting the parasite. Next, we explored the potential of combining emodin with niclosamide to compensate for its limitations in combating N. caninum. The results showed that compared with 0.1 μM niclosamide group, 2 μM emodin + 0.1 μM niclosamide and 4 μM emodin + 0.1 μM niclosamide further inhibited the proliferation of N. caninum by 18 % and 27 %, respectively. Compared with 0.2 μM niclosamide group, 2 μM emodin + 0.2 μM niclosamide and 4 μM emodin + 0.2 μM niclosamide further inhibited the proliferation of N. caninum by 24 % and 25 %, respectively. Compared with 0.3 μM niclosamide group, 4 μM emodin + 0.3 μM niclosamide further inhibited the proliferation of N. caninum by 19 % (Fig. 8A). SynergyFinder was used to further analyze the synergistic effects of the two compounds. The two-dimensional synergy plot and ZIP synergy score plot showed that emodin and niclosamide exhibited obvious synergistic effects, with a synergy score of 17.467 (Fig. 8B and C). Based on these findings, the combination of 4 μM emodin and 0.2 μM niclosamide was selected for further evaluation of its effects on N. caninum invasion and direct parasite treatment. Surprisingly, there was no significant difference in the inhibitory effect on N. caninum invasion between the 4 μM emodin +0.2 μM niclosamide group and the 4 μM emodin group and 0.2 μM niclosamide group (Fig. 8D).

Subsequently, the effects of direct treatment with the compound combination on the invasion and proliferation of tachyzoites were also examined. The results showed that, compared to the niclosamide group, the 4 μM emodin + 0.2 μM niclosamide group also further significantly reduced N. caninum invasion by 5 % and proliferation by 27 % (Fig. 8E and F). Taken together, these findings demonstrated that the combination of emodin and niclosamide exerted synergistic inhibitory effects on the proliferation of N. caninum, while also enhancing their direct antiparasitic activity.

Emodin inhibited the invasion and proliferation of N. caninum in macrophages through necroptosis-independent manner

Previous studies have reported that emodin can activate necroptosis in different cells (Zhou, et al., 2020, 2023). Therefore, we next investigated whether emodin could facilitate N. caninum-induced necroptosis in the macrophages and examined the potential role of necroptosis in emodin's anti-N. caninum effects. Compared with the N. caninum-infected group, LDH release increased by 11 %, 12 %, and 21 % following treatment with 4, 8, and 12 μM emodin, respectively (Fig. 9A). Western blotting analysis showed that pMLKL was markedly elevated in emodin-treated macrophages compared with N. caninum-infected group, with densitometric quantification showing 1.1-fold, 1.3-fold, and 1.7-fold increases at 4, 8, and 12 μM emodin groups, respectively (Fig. 9B and C). Overall, emodin treatment led to increased LDH release and elevated pMLKL levels in N. caninum-infected macrophages in a concentration dependent manner.

Unfortunately, no significant differences in invasion were observed between WT and Mlkl−/− macrophages in the N. caninum groups (Fig. 9D). In the emodin-treated group, parasite invasion was slightly increased in Mlkl−/− macrophages compared to WT macrophages, but this difference failed to reach statistical significance (Fig. 9D). In the proliferation assay, parasite proliferation was 54 % higher in Mlkl−/− macrophages compared with WT macrophages, indicating that MLKL-mediated necroptosis participated in controlling N. caninum proliferation (Fig. 9E). However, parasite proliferation remained comparable between WT and Mlkl−/− macrophages in the emodin-treated groups (Fig. 9E).

Discussion

Neospora caninum infection is one of a major cause of abortion in ruminants, resulting in considerable financial damage in the livestock industry. However, effective vaccines or therapeutic drugs are still lacking. In this study, we found that the natural anthraquinone derivative emodin partially inhibited the invasion and proliferation of N. caninum in Vero cells, MDBK cells, and macrophages. Moreover, emodin exerted a direct detrimental effect on tachyzoites, impairing their ability to invade and replicate. Notably, the antiparasitic effect of emodin was further enhanced when combined with niclosamide compared to niclosamide alone.

Previous studies have reported that emodin significantly inhibits T. gondii growth in vitro by targeting the parasite rather than host cells (Adeyemi et al., 2023). Similarly, pre-incubation of N. caninum tachyzoites with emodin for 2 h significantly impaired their subsequent abilities of invasion and replication, indicating a direct toxic effect. Nonetheless, the precise underlying mechanism responsible for this damage is still unknown and warrants additional research. Interestingly, emodin did not affect T. gondii invasion, whereas it significantly inhibited N. caninum invasion in this study. This difference may stem from variations in secreted proteins between the two parasites. In studies on T. gondii, emodin's inhibitory mechanism is reportedly independent of oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis. There is evidence suggesting that emodin controls Plasmodium by inhibiting FIKK8 kinase, which belongs to the apicomplexan-specific FIKK kinase family (Lin et al., 2017). Thus, similar kinases may serve as potential targets of emodin in N. caninum. Additionally, previous research has shown that emodin disrupts the cytoskeleton of Trypanosoma cruzi by binding to membrane-associated microtubules, thereby inhibiting parasite replication and flagellum growth. Whether emodin affects the cytoskeleton of N. caninum remains to be explored.

To evaluate the safety and anti-N. caninum efficacy of emodin in bovine cells, MDBK cells were used as a model. We found that emodin exhibited greater safety in MDBK cells, with a CC50 value of 29.25 μM, which was higher than the 17.49 μM observed in Vero cells. This suggests that emodin may be tolerated at higher concentrations in cattle, thereby increasing its therapeutic potential. At safe concentrations of 4, 6, and 8 μM, emodin significantly inhibited both invasion and proliferation of N. caninum, and more effectively reduced the number of tachyzoites within PVs. Taken together, these findings highlight MDBK cells as a relevant in vitro system for evaluating drug safety in cattle and suggest that emodin has promising potential as a candidate for the treatment of neosporosis.

The use of a single compound may lead to the development of drug resistance, and excessive dose can result in host toxicity. Combining drugs that target different aspects of N. caninum infection not only enhances therapeutic efficacy but also enables dose reduction, thereby minimizing potential side effects (Sun et al., 2016). Several studies have reported successful combination therapies against N. caninum. For instance, a combination of artemisinin, pyrimethamine, and methylene blue significantly improved parasite clearance in vitro, demonstrating efficacy even at reduced doses (Pereira et al., 2018). In another study, combination ELQs with BKIs exhibited synergistic effects by targeting distinct parasite proteins. Specifically, BKI-1748 combined with ELQ-316 or ELQ-334 showed synergistic activity, whereas its combination with ELQ-422 did not. Furthermore, in a mouse model, BKI-1748 combined with ELQ-334 achieved 100 % fetal survival and parasite clearance, whereas its combination with ELQ-316 failed to reproduce the strong in vitro synergy (Anghel et al., 2021). This study found that the inhibitory effect of emodin combined with niclosamide on N. caninum proliferation did not increase proportionally with higher doses. The combination of 4 μM emodin and 0.2 μM niclosamide appeared to achieve the strongest inhibition of N. caninum proliferation. Compared with treatment using emodin or niclosamide alone, the combined treatment of N. caninum showed stronger inhibitory effects on N. caninum proliferation than on invasion. This suggests that emodin and niclosamide exert a greater synergistic effect in suppressing N. caninum proliferation ability.

Our previous study identified niclosamide as a highly promising anti-N. caninum compound (Liu et al., 2025). Niclosamide exhibited a much stronger antiparasitic effect than emodin in the model of compound treatment of cells. A particularly interesting observation emerged when comparing the antiparasitic effects of niclosamide and emodin in the model of compound treatment of tachyzoites. The superior efficacy of niclosamide became less pronounced. This suggested that niclosamide exerted its inhibitory effect through both host and parasite targets, whereas emodin primarily targets the parasite itself. Many parasite-targeting compounds act by inducing mitochondrial damage. For example, phenothiazine dyes inhibit N. caninum replication by disrupting mitochondrial membrane potential and promoting ROS production (Pereira et al., 2020). However, emodin does not follow this classic pathway, as it is a potent antioxidant.

Some studies have reported that emodin can act as a necroptosis agonist, activating necroptotic cell death (Zhou, et al., 2020, 2023). Necroptosis is an essential mechanism in the host's immune response to pathogenic infections. For example, in Leishmania infection, inhibition of caspase-8 has been shown to promote necroptosis in neutrophils, thereby suppressing parasite replication (Barbosa et al., 2018). Our findings demonstrated that emodin enhanced LDH release and elevated pMLKL expression in infected macrophages, supporting its role in facilitating N. caninum-induced necroptosis. These observations highlight necroptosis modulation as a potential mechanistic target for the discovery of novel anti-N. caninum compounds. Our results showed that although Mlkl deficiency affected macrophage control of N. caninum proliferation, emodin exerted similar effects in both WT and Mlkl−/− macrophages. This directly indicates that MLKL-mediated necroptosis is not the primary mechanism by which emodin controls N. caninum. Together with the preceding data, these findings suggest that emodin likely acts through a direct destructive effect on N. caninum, although the precise underlying mechanism remains to be further investigated.

In summary, emodin has the potential to treat neosporosis, but one limitation of this study is the lack of in vivo experiments. The complex physiological conditions in vivo may not fully replicate the synergistic effects observed in vitro. Nevertheless, based on our in vitro findings, emodin appears to be a promising candidate for combination therapy against N. caninum. Moreover, combination therapeutic compounds with vaccines are an emerging research direction (Imhof et al., 2022), as it may enhance vaccine-induced protection while mitigating potential antagonistic interactions between multiple compounds. Exploring the potential synergy between emodin and vaccine-based strategies will may be a key focus of future research.

Conclusions

In conclusion, emodin effectively inhibits the invasion and proliferation of N. caninum in Vero cells, MDBK cells, and macrophages. Emodin treatment reduced both the N. caninum infection rate and the number of tachyzoites per PV. Notably, emodin exhibited stronger inhibition of N. caninum invasion than the reference drug toltrazuril and directly impaired tachyzoite invasion and proliferation. Moreover, the combination of emodin and niclosamide displayed synergistic effects, particularly in suppressing N. caninum proliferation, suggesting a potential strategy to enhance therapeutic efficacy. Mechanistic investigation revealed that anti-N. caninum effects of emodin in macrophages are independent of MLKL-mediated necroptosis, although MLKL contributes to controlling parasite proliferation under normal infection conditions. Collectively, these findings highlight emodin as a safe and effective anti-N. caninum compound with promising potential for combination therapy in controlling parasitic infection.
