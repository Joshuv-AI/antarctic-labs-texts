Cryopreservation increases sperm DNA fragmentation in normozoospermic Libyan men: the role of oxidative stress and the protective effect of melatonin
Authors: Asma A Al Bijou, Jehan A Ilfergani, Tarek S Bader, Muetaz M Feetouri, Ibtihag S Alogali, Yousef M A Hasen, Mahmoud A Aloriby, Farag A Bleiblo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCryopreservation of sperm is routinely used in assisted reproduction technology (ART) for male fertility preservation. However, this method has been associated with oxidative stress and DNA fragmentation that may impair sperm quality. Additionally, antioxidant interventions such as melatonin supplementation have not been thoroughly explored in this setting. Although Libya is reported to have one of the highest global prevalence rates of male infertility, Libya-specific data remain limited. This study aimed to determine the effect of a single freeze–thaw cycle on sperm DNA fragmentation and oxidative stress markers, and to evaluate whether melatonin has an impact on post-thaw oxidation profiles. This prospective cohort study was conducted at the Fertility and Reproductive Medicine Center, Beirut Hospital, Benghazi. Semen samples of 104 normozoospermic Libyans were evaluated before and after freezing. DNA fragmentation index (DFI) was measured by sperm chromatin dispersion (SCD) test, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) were quantified by using luminol-enhanced chemiluminescence. In a subset of ejaculates, aliquots were supplemented with 2 mM of melatonin prior to cryopreservation. Cryopreservation was associated with a statistically significant increase in DFI (46.3 ± 18.3% to 60.0 ± 23.0%; p < 0.001) and ROS levels (3.2 × 10³ to 14.7 × 10³ RLU/s; p < 0.001). Smokers presented significantly higher DFI at both pre-freeze and post-thaw evaluations (p < 0.001). We detected a positive correlation between ROS and post-thaw DFI (r = 0.68; p < 0.001). Melatonin-treated samples exhibited moderate but significant differences in ROS (12%, p = 0.045) and DFI (11%, p = 0.004) compared to untreated aliquots. These findings suggested that the freeze–thaw process may contribute to oxidative and genomic stress in spermatozoa, while melatonin supplementation appears to provide limited protection. Larger, multicenter studies incorporating ART endpoints are required to determine the potential translational relevance of these findings.KEYWORDS: Cryopreservation, DNA fragmentation, oxidative stress, melatonin, reactive oxygen species, semen analysis

Introduction

Male factor infertility affects 17.5% of men globally, with a 77% increase from 1990, equal to over 56 million cases currently affected [1]. Despite the progress made in ART, conditions including endocrine-disrupting exposures, obesity, and delayed paternity continue to affect fertility outcomes [2,3]. The Middle East North Africa (MENA) region, including Libya, has one of the highest global rates of infertility, with total and primary infertility rates of 22.6% and 3.8%, respectively [4]. In Libya, male infertility is most commonly due to abnormal semen profiles, genital tract infections, or Y-chromosome microdeletions [4,5]. Fertility clinics have only recently been introduced in Libya. Their delayed establishment, influenced in part by sociocultural perspectives on ART, has restricted the availability of local data and limited research on infertility.

Sperm cryopreservation is widely used in ART; however, the freeze–thaw procedure may result in the generation of oxidative stress, which could damage sperm motility, membrane lipids, and DNA stability. Increased sperm DNA fragmentation (SDF) is associated with reduced fertilization potential, impaired embryo development, and adverse pregnancy outcome [6–8]. Therefore, minimizing cryo-induced damage remains an important objective for optimizing ART outcomes.

Oxidative stress is considered a major mediator of cryopreservation injury. ROS are more harmful to spermatozoa due to their lipid-rich membranes, low capacity of antioxidants, and limited capacity of DNA repair [9–11]. Cryopreservation induces ROS production and, therefore, may result in breaks of DNA strands. Although several studies have reported these effects in other populations, data from North Africa are limited. Despite the prevailing use of tobacco [12,13] in Libya, the potential for smoking-related exacerbation of oxidative stress and DNA damage has not yet been evaluated.

Melatonin, which is an indoleamine produced by the pineal gland, is a potent antioxidant with multiple protective mechanisms, including direct ROS scavenging, enhancement of enzymatic defenses, and stabilization of mitochondrial membranes. These effects are particularly relevant to spermatozoa, which are highly susceptible to oxidative stress during cryopreservation. Experimental studies have reported that melatonin can reduce oxidative injury to sperm, including structural and DNA damage [14]. However, clinical findings in human sperm cryopreservation remain limited, variable, and inconclusive [15], providing a rationale for further evaluation.

This study aimed to investigate the effect of a single freeze–thaw cycle on the DFI and the level of ROS, as well as the other seminal parameters in normozoospermic Libyan males. It also explored the association of ROS and post-thaw DNA damage, the impact of lifestyle factors, and whether melatonin supplementation might alleviate cryo damage under experimental conditions. To our knowledge, no previous research has examined these combined factors in a Libyan cohort; therefore, the present study provides initial insights into oxidative responses to cryopreservation and the possible role of antioxidant supplementation.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This was a prospective single-center cohort study conducted between October 2024 and April 2025 at the Fertility and Reproductive Medicine Center, Beirut Hospital, Benghazi, Libya. The study was designed according to the WHO Laboratory Manual for the Examination and Processing of Human Semen (6th Edition) to ensure methodological standardization. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Boards of Beirut Hospital (IRB-BH-FRM/2024/009) and the Libyan Academy (REC-LA-BIO/0224/033), in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and national data protection regulations. Participants provided written informed consent, and their confidentiality was protected throughout the study.

Participants were recruited consecutively from men attending the clinic for fertility preservation or diagnostic evaluation. Eligibility was determined based on normozoospermic status as defined by WHO criteria. Only normozoospermic men were considered for inclusion. Sperm samples were obtained after 2−7 days of abstinence and analyzed before and after cryopreservation. A subset of samples was pre-treated with the antioxidant melatonin (2 mM) prior to freezing. The objectives of this study were to assess DFI, ROS level, and semen parameters following thawing. Furthermore, we investigated the influence of smoking as well as the possible mitigating effects of melatonin supplementation.

Participant recruitment and sample size

From October 2024 to April 2025, men referred to the Fertility and Reproductive Medicine Center, Beirut Hospital, for cryopreservation or diagnostic purposes were screened for their eligibility. Using the Cochran formula for categorical data (95% confidence, 5% margin of error, p = 0.5), the target sample size was initially 209. Ultimately, 104 participants were enrolled, primarily due to high screening failure rates from exclusionary medical or reproductive conditions and the application of eligibility criteria. Despite this reduction, the final 104 cohort provided sufficient statistical power for the primary outcome analyses. Eligibility was based solely on the screening semen analysis (sperm concentration ≥16 × 10⁶/mL, total motility ≥42%, and morphologically normal forms >4%); no age or BMI thresholds were applied. In addition, progressive motility was not an entry criterion and was evaluated later as a baseline outcome on the study ejaculate collected for cryopreservation. Since BMI was not an eligibility criterion, the cohort included normal-weight, overweight, and obese participants, and BMI was incorporated as a covariate in the analysis. We excluded participants presenting with leukocytospermia (≥1 × 106 WBC/ml), oligozoospermia (sperm concentration <15 million/ml), severe asthenozoospermia (progressive motility <32%), or teratozoospermia (normal morphology <4%). Additional exclusion criteria were the presence of systemic diseases, testicular or genital infection, drug abuse, previous exposure to gonadotoxins (e.g. radiation and industrial chemicals), clinical varicocele, scrotal trauma history, and use of medications affecting spermatogenesis.

Semen collection and baseline evaluation

The participants were requested to refrain from ejaculation for 2 to 7 days before sample collection. Men produced semen samples by masturbation in a temperature-controlled room adjacent to the laboratory into sterile pre-labeled containers. All samples were transported to the laboratory within 5 min post-collection and incubated at 37 °C for a maximum of 60 min to ensure liquefaction. The semen parameters, including volume, color, and consistency, were registered. Concurrently, pH was measured using a pH meter (calibrated daily with buffer solutions pH 4.0, 7.0, and 10.0). Viscosity was measured using the length of thread by aspiration and classified as watery, moderate, or highly viscous. Semen analysis was carried out according to WHO recommendations. Sperm concentration was measured in 5−10 µL aliquots, diluted 1:20 or 1:100 with Sperm Active medium, and then counted on an improved Neubauer hemocytometer. Motility was estimated by placing a 10 µL droplet of semen on a prewarmed microscope slide and investigating it through phase contrast microscopy at 37 °C. Sperm were classified as rapidly progressive (A), slowly progressive (B), non-progressive (C), or immotile (D), with reference values of ≥42% for total motility (A + B + C) and ≥30% for progressive motility (A + B). Morphology was assessed on smears stained with Papanicolaou and air-dried, according to Tygerberg criteria. At least 200 spermatozoa per slide were analyzed; if at least ≥4% of sperm had normal forms, the morphology was considered normal. The sperm vitality test was performed by eosin—nigrosine stain, which could distinguish between viable sperm (no stain) and nonviable ones (pink stain). Peroxidase-positive WBC evaluated by the Endtz test was considered within the normal range if their levels were less than 1 × 10⁶ WBC/ml. On the cryopreservation day, a new ejaculate (‘study ejaculate’) was collected and analyzed. Values from this sample (including progressive motility) may differ from the screening sample because of normal within-man biological and pre-analytical variability (e.g. abstinence interval, liquefaction/viscosity, timing and temperature of assessment).

Reagents and supplies

We used sperm active medium (Gynemed, Germany) for washing and handling sperm. This medium, which contains balanced salts and ions, buffers such as HEPES or bicarbonate, was supplemented with human serum albumin (HSA) to support sperm motility and survival during in vitro handling. The Sperm Store (Gynemed, Germany) was used for standard cryopreservation, with the addition of a glycerol-based cryoprotectant supplemented with antioxidants, such as Vitamin E and glutathione, to reduce oxidative damage to sperms. Additionally, sperm DNA fragmentation was assessed using the SCD method following the WHO guidelines and manufacturer's instructions (Candore, India). Rapid freezing was performed using Sperm Freeze Solution (Uitrolife, Sweden), which is a proprietary solution that contains permeable and non-permeable cryoprotectants with the aim of allowing extensive cooling and ultrastructure stability at minimal temperature. We used laboratory sterile compatible disposable which included presterilized labeled plastic straws (IMV Technologies, France), variable volume micropipettes, and DNase/RNase-free pipette tips (Eppendorf, Germany), improved Neubauer hemocytometers (Hausser Scientific, USA), soda lime glass Pasteur pipettes (Fisher Scientific, USA), and microcentrifuge tubes (Eppendorf, Germany). We also used a temperature-sensitive incubator (Binder, Germany) for semen liquefaction and short-term incubation, while controlled-rate freezing at the rate of −1 °C/min and long-term storage were done using a programmable freeze unit (Cryologic, Planer, UK) and liquid nitrogen tanks (Taylor-Wharton, USA) at −196 °C, respectively. Thawing was performed using a constant-temperature water bath set at 37 °C (Grant Instruments, UK). Analysis of semen, as well as associated assays, was performed using an Eppendorf 5810 R centrifuge and a Nikon Eclipse E200 phase-contrast/bright-field light microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Cryopreservation protocol

The frozen semen was equilibrated at room temperature (22–25 °c) for 10 min after liquefaction. Cryopreservation was performed with the stepwise addition of an equal volume of cryoprotectant Sperm Store media (Gynemed, Germany) and gentle titration after each addition to avoid osmotic shock. After equilibration for 10 min, plastic straws (0.5 ml) were loaded with the semen cryoprotectant mixture, sealed, and placed into a programmable freezer at a rate of −1 °C decrease of temperature per minute until the −80 °C temperature was reached. Long-term storage was performed at −196 °C, in which straws were plunged directly into liquid nitrogen. To quickly freeze the samples, they were first held for 10 min at room temperature and then exposed to the vapor of liquid nitrogen prior to plunging the samples into the liquid nitrogen for long-term storage. An equal volume of Sperm Freeze Solution (Uitrolife, Sweden) was slowly added to the liquid semen. The samples were then packed into 0.5 ml straws and suspended 1–3 cm above liquid nitrogen for approximately 30 min to achieve vapor phase cooling. The straws, following pre-cooling, were plunged into liquid nitrogen before being stored (where necessary) until they were used.

Thawing and post-thaw sample handling

Thawing of cryopreserved semen samples was performed in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol (SpermFreeze™ Solution, Vitrolife AB, Sweden), employing either immersion in a 37 °C water bath for 5 min or equilibration at ambient room temperature (22–25 °C) for 30 min. Frozen straws were removed using cryogenic tools designed for this purpose and thawed in a 37 °C water bath for 5 min or at room temperature (22−25 °C) for 30 min, as recommended by the manufacturer. The thawed contents were transferred stepwise into sterile tubes and then diluted with 1 ml of Sperm Active medium. The suspension was then centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube and discarded, whereas the pellet was resuspended very carefully. After the post-thaw cycle, ejaculates were re-evaluated as per the same basic protocols, to allow the pre- and post-cryopreservation assessments of each sample to be comparable.

Melatonin supplementation protocol

Melatonin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was prepared as a concentrated stock, protected from light, and stored at −20 °C. On the day of use, an aliquot was diluted in Sperm Active medium. It was added to the assigned ejaculate aliquots immediately before mixing with the cryoprotectant to reach a final concentration of 2 mM. The 2 mM melatonin concentration was selected based on recent human sperm cryopreservation studies demonstrating its efficacy [14,15]. Samples were kept at room temperature for 10 min, as in the pre-freeze protocol. Cryopreservation then proceeded without change, using either the controlled-rate method with Sperm Store or the rapid-freezing method with Sperm Freeze Solution. Matched control aliquots from the same ejaculate were processed in parallel without melatonin. Thawing and all post-thaw assessments were performed in the same way for treated and control samples.

ROS quantification

Seminal ROS were measured by luminol-enhanced chemiluminescence using a CE-marked single-tube luminometer (Turner BioSystems, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) with photon counting detection and automatic signal integration. The assay was performed according to established clinical protocol using unprocessed seminal luminometry. After complete liquefaction, semen was gently mixed; 400 µL aliquots were dispensed into disposable tubes. Post-thaw tests were measured within 15 min after thaw, with the same manipulation. Chemiluminescence was triggered by adding 10 µL of luminol working solution (5 mmol/L in DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich/Merck, St. Louis, MO, USA) to each fraction. A negative control contained PBS (Gibco/Thermo Fisher Scientific) plus luminol, while a positive control included PBS, luminol, and H₂O₂ (10 mmol/L stock in PBS, VWR International; final 25 µmol/L). Each tube was acquired for 200–300 seconds, and the resulting stable plateau of 60 seconds was averaged and recorded as relative light units (RLU) s−1; blanks were subtracted from those values. Since all assays were performed on the same volume of semen and luminol concentration, data are presented as RLU/s (not RLU/s per 10⁶ sperm) for within-subject comparisons according to the recommendations for single-tube luminometry. This procedure reliably detected the cryo-induced ROS rise. We reported (mean increase from ~3.2 × 10³ to ~14.7 × 10³ RLU/s in the ROS subset) and aligns with validated clinical implementations of luminol semen ROS testing.

Data collection, management, and quality control

Information on participating subjects was prospectively documented on standardized case report forms specifically designed to record the clinical and laboratory characteristics. These included demographic information, lifestyle, relevant medical history, semen parameters, cryopreservation methodology and markers of DNA fragmentation. Information was collected in a safe and secure electronic record that was only accessible to authorized users. Data processing and quality control were performed using validated laboratory information management system (LIMS) with double-entry verification and audit trails. Each data entry was manually checked for accuracy by trained personnel. Quarterly calibration of all the laboratory equipment was performed to ensure the reliability of the procedures. The sperm morphology and DNA fragmentation tests underwent a proficiency test as recommended by WHO (2021). The principles of data protection were followed, and all the results adhered to the guidelines of the GDPR and the institutional ethics committee. Personal data were stored in and securely encrypted in separate servers, without connection to analytic data sets.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics v27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± SD or median [IQR], and categorical data as n (%). Normality was examined using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q–Q plots, and homogeneity of variances was tested with Levene's test. When assumptions for parametric tests were not met, non-parametric tests were used. Categorical variables such as smoking and alcohol status were dummy-coded (e.g. smoker = 1, non-smoker = 0) for inclusion in correlation and regression analyses. Comparison (pre/post) within subjects was conducted with paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Between-group comparisons (e.g. melatonin vs control) were analyzed using independent t-tests, Welch's correction (in the event of unequal variances) or Mann–Whitney U tests in instances of non-normality. Categorical variables were compared by χ² or Fisher's exact test. Correlation analyses of semen parameters, ROS, and DFI were calculated using Pearson's r or Spearman's ρ, depending on the distribution and linearity of the data, with 95% confidence intervals. Multivariable linear regression was performed to evaluate predictors of DFI: (i) baseline DFI on age, BMI, smoking, and alcohol; (ii) post-thaw DFI on baseline DFI and the same covariates. VIF (<5 cut-off) was tested for multicollinearity. Results of the regression are presented as standardized β, R², and partial R². Effect sizes included Cohen's dx (paired), Hedges' g (independent), and standardized β (regression), all with 95% CIs. Results were significant at α = 0.05 (two-tailed). Outcome measures were post-cryopreservation (i) DFI (primary endpoint) or semen parameters (primary endpoint and secondary endpoint) changes; exploratory analyses were reported without adjustment for multiple testing. Post-hoc sensitivity analysis showed that with n = 104 paired samples, the study had 80% power to detect an effect size of 0.28 SD (Cohen's dx). For the ROS subset (n = 20), the detectable within-subject effect size was ≈ 0.63 SD. The minimal detectable correlations were |r| ≈ 0.27 (n = 104) and |r| ≈ 0.59 (n = 20).

Results

Baseline characteristics of the cohort

We analyzed the baseline characteristics of the study cohort with 104 normozoospermic men (Table 1). We determined a mean age of 35.1 ± 5.8 years (range: 22–54) and a mean BMI of 26.5 ± 4.0 kg/m² (range: 19.2–36.8). According to BMI classifications, 41% had normal weight, 38% overweight, 18% class I obese, and 3% class II–III obese. Among the participants, 57% were current smokers, 12% were ex-smokers, and 31% were never-smokers. Regarding alcohol usage, 6.7% were current users, 13% former users, and 80.3% had never used alcohol. None of the participants presented with a history of chronic systemic illnesses, testicular disease, or medication known to potentially influence spermatogenesis. These results provide a baseline for a healthy population. These results establish a clinically healthy baseline population for subsequent semen analyses.

Baseline of semen quality before cryopreservation

Baseline semen parameters were evaluated within 60 min of ejaculation according to the WHO criteria. Semen volume was between 1.50 and 5.60 ml, with a mean ± SD was 2.96 ± 0.79 ml (CV = 27%, skewness = 0.37, Table 2), and no sample was below the WHO lower reference limit of 1.4 ml. The mean sperm concentration was 49.22 ± 18.21 × 10⁶/ml (range: 18–118 × 10⁶/ml; CV = 37%; skewness = 0.69), with only 3% of samples below the WHO threshold of 16 × 10⁶/m. Total sperm counts averaged 145 ± 60 × 10⁶ (range 40−310 × 10⁶), all exceeded the WHO cut-off of 39 × 10⁶, with 15% of men having counts above 200 × 10⁶. The mean total motility (progressive + non-progressive) was 53.8 ± 14.7% (range: 18–86 while non-progressive motility averaged 22.26 ± 10.88% (range: 5%–55%). Mean progressive motility was 31.06 ± 13.18% (10 being the lowest, 70 the highest), 57% of samples were below the WHO lower limit of 32%, whereas mean non-progressive motility was 22.26 ± 10.88% (5 the lowest, 55 the highest). The mean DFI, measured using the SCD test, was 46.34 ± 18.32% (range: 18.3–82.5%), with 76% of the cohort exceeding the clinically relevant threshold of 30%. Correlation analysis suggested that sperm concentration had a strong correlation with total motile count (r = 0.892, p < 0.001), while progressive motility also correlated with total motile count (r = 0.508, p < 0.001). A weak but significant negative correlation between progressive and non-progressive motility was found (r = –0.262, p < 0.01). In contrast, DFI was not significantly associated with sperm concentration (r = 0.106, p = 0.286) or total motile count (r = 0.145, p = 0.141). Notably, a strong correlation was observed between pre- and post-thaw DFI (r = 0.973, p < 0.001), as shown in Table 3 and illustrated in Figure 1.

Cryopreservation-induced changes in semen quality

In paired comparisons of 104 samples before and after a single freeze–thaw cycle, noticeable alterations in semen quality parameters were detected (Table 4). We observed a reduction in ejaculate volume from 2.96 ± 0.79 ml before freezing to 1.00 ± 0.00 ml post-thaw (Δ = –1.96 ml; t = 25.41, p < 0.001). We also found that the sperm concentration increased from 49.22 ± 18.21 to 72.92 ± 27.85 × 10⁶/ml (Δ = +23.70 × 10⁶/ml; t = −19.70, p < 0.001). Progressive motility decreased from 31.06 ± 13.18% to 18.41 ± 10.18% (Δ = –12.64%; t = 19.73; p < 0.001), while non-progressive motility declined from 22.26 ± 10.88% to 18.46 ± 10.89% (Δ = –3.80%; t = 8.85; p < 0.001). The immotile sperm increased slightly from 1.88 ± 1.42 to 2.10 ± 0.82% but this difference was not statistically significant (Δ = +0.22%; p = 0.122). We observed no significant change in total motile sperm count values before and after freezing (27.33 ± 14.14 vs. 28.35 ± 16.71 × 10⁶; Δ = +1.02 × 10⁶; t = −1.68, p = 0.096). Furthermore, we observed a slight decrease in vitality from (75.4 ± 17.2%) to (73.0 ± 17.3%) (Δ = –2.4%; t = 2.32, p = 0.022). Sperm chromatin integrity was also affected, with the DFI increasing from 46.34 ± 18.32% before freezing to 59.99 ± 23.02% after thawing (Δ = + 13.65%; t = –20.77; p < 0.001). Following thawing, 91% of samples exceeded the 30% DFI threshold.

Influence of lifestyle on semen quality

After analyzing the structured questionnaire and clinical data, we found that lifestyle factors influenced semen quality parameters. Tobacco use had measurable effects on semen quality parameters, with the greatest impact observed on sperm chromatin integrity. Smokers displayed significantly higher mean DFI level compared to never smokers at baseline (mean DFI: 57.2 ± 12.6% versus 32.1 ± 14.5%, t = 9.46, p < 0.001; Cohen's d = 1.84) as shown in Table 5. After cryopreservation, we observed further higher increase with post-thaw DFI rates of 75.9 ± 13.4% in smokers, compared to 39.2 ± 15.0% in never-smokers (t = 13.1, p 0.10; Cohen's d < 0.35). In contrast, we observed no difference between smokers and non-smokers for ejaculate volume (2.91 ± 0.70 ml vs. 3.00 ± 0.85 ml; t = 0.57, p = 0.57), sperm concentration (48.0 ± 17.5 × 10⁶/ml v. 50.8 ± 17.3 × 10⁶/ml; t = 0.71, p = 0.48), or progressive motility (30.7 ± 13.1% vs. 31.4 ± 13.3%; t = 0.24, p = 0.81). In respect to alcohol consumption, we observed no associations with semen parameters. Among them, only 7 participants (6.7%) were current alcohol users. In this subgroup, we found no significant differences versus non-users for DFI, motility, sperm concentration, or ejaculate volume (all p > 0.10: Cohen's d < 0.35).

Regression-based predictors of sperm DNA damage

We used regression-based analysis to identify independent predictors of sperm DNA fragmentation before and after cryopreservation. Using multiple linear regression with forward entry covariates (age, weight, height, BMI, alcohol, and smoking) revealed that cigarette smoking was the unique significant predictor of DFI at baseline, accounting for 44% of the variance in the DFI (partial R² = 0.44; β = 0.68; t = 9.06; p 0.30). At post-thaw, DFI was entered as the dependent variable, baseline DFI was the most significant predictor, accounting for 55% of the variance (partial R² = 0.55; β = 0.81; t = 12.82; p < 0.001). We also observed that cigarette smoking accounted for an additional 4% of the variance in post-thaw DFI (partial R² = 0.04; β = 0.20; t = 4.11; p < 0.001). In contrast, age, weight, height, BMI, and alcohol consumption did not predict post-thaw DFI (all p ≥ 0.18). Impact of melatonin supplementation on oxidative stress and DNA integrity during sperm cryopreservation

We evaluated 20 ejaculates to define the oxidative stress profile linked to human sperm cryopreservation. We detected that ROS levels increased almost 5-fold after freezing, rising from 3.2 ± 1.1 × 10 ³ RLU/s before freezing to 14.7 ± 4.2 × 10 ³ RLU/s post-thaw (t = 11.3, p < 0.001; Cohen's d = 2.52), corresponding to an approximate 4.6-fold increase. These experiments are summarized in Table 7 and highlight that all batches were associated with an acute surge of oxidative stress following cryopreservation, as illustrated for single samples in Figure 3. We also found that in this subset of 20 ejaculates, the DFI increased significantly from 45.8 ± 17.9% before freezing to 59.5 ± 22.8% after thawing (t = 8.9, p < 0.001), corresponding to a relative increase of 1.3 times. Moreover, a significant positive correlation was found between post-thaw ROS production and DFI (r = 0.68, p = 0.004) (Figure 4). These results demonstrated a dose-dependent ROS induction on chromatin damage. To determine the effects of antioxidant supplementation in attenuating cryopreservation-induced oxidative and genotoxic stress, a paired experiment (n = 20 ejaculates) was conducted. Each semen sample was split into two aliquots: one underwent standard cryopreservation, and the other was treated with 2 mM melatonin prior to freezing. Post-thaw ROS levels were 12% lower in melatonin-treated than in untreated controls (p = 0.045). In the meantime, DFI was reduced by 11% in men receiving melatonin (p = 0.004) (Figure 5). In addition, correlation analysis indicated that post-thaw ROS levels comprised a portion of 46% of the observed differences in the difference in DFI (ΔDFI), r = 0.68 (χ² = 41.24, p < 0.001).

Discussion

In this study, we detected that cryopreservation resulted in a marked elevation of DFI and ROS. There was a strong positive correlation between post-thaw DFI and ROS. DFI before and after cryopreservation was significantly higher in heavy smokers than in nonsmokers. Significantly, supplementation of the cryopreservation medium with melatonin resulted in a small but statistically significant decrease in both DFI and ROS levels after thawing.

Semen cryopreservation greatly impairs the integrity of the sperm's DNA. It has been reported that post-thaw induced sperm DFI by approximately 26% [16], whereas our data showed a higher increase by 29% points, exceeding the 7–8 point increase observed in recent meta-analyses [17]. These discrepancies may be due to patient cohorts, method of assay, or freezing protocols rather than reflecting a fundamentally biological effect. Our results suggest that oxidative stress is a key contributor to sperm DNA damage. We observed a strong correlation between ROS levels and the DFI, consistent with the known role of ROS in causing DNA single strand breaks and base adducts [18]. These findings support earlier reports of cryo-induced increases in DFI and highlight oxidative stress as a major pathway.

Our results emphasize the negative effect of smoking on sperm chromatin integrity. There was a significantly higher DFI at baseline and post-thaw for smokers than for non-smokers, consistent with other observational studies linking tobacco use with poorer semen quality and higher sperm DNA damage [19]. Heavy smoking (>10–20 cigarettes/day) leads to more reduction in sperm count, motility, and morphology, and an increased DFI [19]. The effects of the number of cigarettes smoked on sperm quality could potentially be attributed to oxidative stress. Cigarette smoke induces a high burden of ROS and a decrease in antioxidants in the male genital system [20], resulting in molecular damage in sperm membrane lipids, proteins, and DNA. In line with this concept, our study demonstrated a strong positive correlation between ROS generation and DFI. Spermatogenesis occurs over approximately 70–74 days, which suggests that there may be a window of recovery following smoking cessation. The evidence for DFI improvement in this period is inconclusive. Kulaksiz et al. reported an increase in semen volume, sperm concentration, and total sperm count after three months of abstinence [21], but sperm DNA fragmentation was not tested. Another study reported improvement in seminal parameters without corresponding DFI reduction [22]. These results suggested that while semen quality may recover within months, DNA integrity may require prolonged cessation or adjunctive antioxidant therapy for improvement. Considering our finding of a ROS–DFI association, robust prospective cohort or interventional studies are necessary to elucidate the timeline and degree of DFI recovery after smoking cessation. Our results and these reports suggest that oxidative stress plays a critical role in smoking-induced sperm DNA damage.

Our study provides preliminary evidence that melatonin may contribute to reducing cryopreservation-induced oxidative damage in human sperm. After the addition of the cryoprotectant, the post-thaw DFI and ROS values were reduced in samples treated with melatonin. The protective effect of melatonin aligns with its antioxidant characteristics as it enters the plasma membrane of sperm, attaches to mitochondria, and quenches ROS within them [23]. This role is supported by earlier animal and in vitro human studies showing that melatonin reduces oxidative stress and DNA damage during sperm preparation and preservation [24,25]. In human semen, melatonin supplementation was associated with improved motility, viability, and DNA integrity under oxidative stress [26]. We have extended these results by demonstrating an 11% decrease in DFI in cryopreserved human sperm. However, the clinical significance of this decrease is not yet clear. A DFI greater than 30% has been associated with higher miscarriage rates and lower neonatal birth weight in in vitro fertilization (IVF) and intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), while values between 20–30% are linked to reduced intrauterine insemination (IUI) success rates [27,28]. Since most post-thaw samples in our study remained above 30%, the reduction may not reflect real improvements in reproductive outcomes. Additional studies, including ART outcomes such as pregnancy rates and live birth rates, are required to elucidate the clinical implications of this effect.

This study has some limitations. The single-center design and modest sample size, especially in the ROS-assessed subgroup, limit generalizability. The cross-sectional design prevents causal inference and does not allow evaluation of recovery dynamics after interventions such as smoking cessation. We assessed only laboratory outcomes (DFI, ROS, motility) and did not include ART endpoints such as fertilization or live birth, which limits clinical interpretation. In addition, our cryopreservation protocol and melatonin dose represent only one approach, and outcomes may differ under alternative conditions and approaches. Moreover, while the SCD assay is widely used in clinical laboratories, it is more prone to observer variability and less reproducible than the flow-cytometric SCSA, which represents a methodological limitation. Future multicenter and longitudinal studies with clinical endpoints are needed to confirm and extend these findings.

Conclusion

The present study shows that cryopreservation impairs sperm chromatin integrity through the effect of oxidative stress, where smoking exacerbates this dysfunction. The supplementation of melatonin in cryoprotectant medium decreased ROS and SDF, which might have a protective function. These findings provide preliminary evidence that exogenous antioxidants may reduce cryopreservation-induced damage, but the reductions observed are unlikely to be clinically relevant, as post-thaw DNA fragmentation often remained above levels linked to poor reproductive outcomes. Larger-scale multicenter and long-term studies with clinical endpoints (fertilization, pregnancy, and live birth) are required to confirm these data. Further studies on lifestyle, antioxidant strategy, and broader biomarkers (biological outcomes/offspring health/epigenetic) may enhance fertility preservation and reproductive care in the future.
