Urological outcomes and management of abnormal placental presentations: a retrospective study at a Lebanese center
Authors: Georges Yared, Mohamad Moussa, Maroun Matar, Mustapha Nahle, Charlotte El Hajjar, Pierre Mourad, Ahmad EL Moghrabi, Christopher Massaad, Kariman Ghazal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlacenta Accreta Spectrum (PAS) involves abnormal placental adhesions, leading to severe complications like postpartum hemorrhage. Such cases often require cesarean hysterectomies, which are associated with significant risks of urinary tract injuries.MethodsThis retrospective study reviewed medical records of 220 patients diagnosed with PAS at Rafik Hariri University Hospital, Beirut, from 2007 to 2022. Patients were categorized based on ultrasound findings into two groups: Group 1 included 84 with Placenta Previa Accreta; Group 2 comprised 72 with Low Risk Placenta Previa, 52 with High Risk, and 12 with Placenta Accreta involving bladder injury. We assessed preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative management.ResultsOf the 187 patients undergoing hysterectomy, 60 (32%) experienced urinary tract injuries, including bladder and ureteral injuries. Despite significant surgical risks, a multidisciplinary approach effectively managed patient care, preventing urinary tract infections.ConclusionManaging PAS via cesarean hysterectomy poses a substantial risk of urinary tract injuries, primarily due to altered pelvic anatomy. Prophylactic ureteral stent placement, tailored to each patient, along with rigorous postoperative monitoring, is crucial to mitigate complications like urinary infections and genitourinary fistulae.Keywords: Placenta previa, placenta accreta spectrum, cesarean hysterectomy, urinary tract injuries, urethral stent vesical catheter, surgical complications

Introduction

Placenta Accreta Spectrum (PAS) was originally defined in 1937 as an abnormal adherence of the placenta, either partially or wholly, to the uterine wall, often due to an incomplete or absent decidua layer. The prevalence of PAS ranges between 1 in 300 and 1 in 2,000 births [1,2]. A significant increase in the incidence of PAS has been observed in recent decades, with studies indicating a rise from 0.12 to 0.31% of births between 1970 and 2010 [3]. PAS has been traditionally categorized into three types based on the depth of myometrial invasion: accreta, where the placental villi attach to the decidual layer; increta, involving deeper invasion into the myometrium; and percreta, characterized by complete penetration of the myometrium by the placental villi, potentially breaching the serosa and invading nearby organs, such as the bladder, broad ligament, or sigmoid colon [1,2,4]. Moreover, the changes in pelvic anatomy and vascularization provide the pathophysiological foundation for urological issues in PAS during surgical interventions [1,2]. Unusual placenta-myometrial adhesion, especially when accompanied by a cesarean scar, makes surgery more difficult and raises the possibility of serious complications [4].

The primary risk factor identified for PAS is a history of cesarean delivery, with the increasing incidence of PAS mirroring the rise in cesarean delivery rates over the past decades. Additional risk factors include advanced maternal age, multiparity, placenta previa, and a history of uterine surgery or curettage [5,6]. PAS is notably associated with significant risks of mortality and morbidity, primarily due to postpartum hemorrhage, which often necessitates blood transfusions and/or hysterectomy [5,7].

The surgical management of PAS, particularly through cesarean hysterectomy, can be complicated by various types of urinary tract injuries, impacting both the bladder and ureters [6,7]. Risk factors for these injuries include distorted pelvic anatomy, extensive fibrosis, and neovascularization. Consequently, effective screening and prenatal diagnosis are crucial for ensuring safe delivery in specialized Placenta Accreta Centers of Excellence, where a multidisciplinary team, including access to intensive neonatal and maternal care, is available and for managing these complications [6–8].

It is also worth noting that ultrasound is the primary diagnostic modality, especially when it comes to the early detection of PAS. In particular, placental lacunae, myometrial thinning, and hypervascularity are important ultrasonography criteria which can be used as critical indicators that point out any abnormal placental invasion [9,10]. In addition, according to the FIGO guidelines, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can also offer more details pertaining placental topography [11].

A multidisciplinary approach is therefore necessary for the surgical care of PAS, and different methods are used to reduce urological complications.

Vesico-uterine dissection and the implantation of prophylactic ureteral stents are becoming more widely acknowledged as successful methods of preventing surgical damage [12].

The decision between conservative treatment and surgical hysterectomy is still up for debate, especially when comparing cases that are discovered during surgery to those that are discovered during pregnancy [13]. Urologists’ experience can improve intraoperative procedures to prevent injuries and improve postoperative surveillance for complications, making their involvement in the management of PAS crucial [14]. In particular, the decision between conservative treatment—such as placental retention with methotrexate—and cesarean hysterectomy is related to multiple factors. Criteria including the patient’s hemodynamic stability, their future fertility goals, and the extent of placental invasion play a crucial role in the decision-making process.

From this perspective, there is an urgent need to address the increasing prevalence of PAS and its related urological complications, especially in tertiary care settings where data is scarce. The aim of this study is therefore to evaluate the urological outcomes and management strategies which are associated with abnormal placental presentations over a 15-year period.

Methodology

Study design

The study involved a retrospective analysis of 220 pregnant women diagnosed with Placenta Accreta Spectrum (PAS) who were treated at Rafic Hariri University Hospital between 2007 and 2022.

Study population and eligibility criteria

The research involved a retrospective review of prospectively collected perioperative data, supplemented by retrospective analysis of imaging and pathology results from patient medical records. The study population was divided into two groups: Group 1 comprised women with Placenta Previa Accreta identified through ultrasound, and Group 2 included women with other placental presentations on ultrasound. Data collection

Medical records from 2007 to 2022 were reviewed for mean age at diagnosis, parity, presence of placenta previa, previous cesarean deliveries, and prior uterine surgery or curettage. Records of patients who underwent peripartum hysterectomy were specifically examined to identify the type of hysterectomy and resultant urinary tract injuries, diagnosed either intraoperatively or postoperatively, with a focus on management and outcomes.

Imaging and diagnostic criteria

Ultrasound findings were then interpreted by board-certified radiologists with over ten years of experience in this field of imaging. Indeed, it is important to note that the diagnosis heavily relied on key sonographic markers, including the following:

On the other hand, when it comes to severe cases, pelvic MRI was performed in these cases to evaluate the topography of placental invasion.

Surgical management and histopathology

Surgical management was guided by a multidisciplinary team. The multidisciplinary team (MDT) included obstetricians, nurses, urologists, surgeons, pediatricians, and radiologists. Urologists conducted rigid cystoscopies to evaluate bladder involvement and placed ureteric catheters during and post-Cesarean sections. They also assisted gynecologists in performing cystotomies and necessary repairs during hysterectomies. Postoperatively, all patients were managed in intensive care. Moreover, we note that the need to perform prophylactic ureteral stent placement or vesico-uterine dissection was made on the basis of preoperative imaging and intraoperative findings.

Statistical analysis plan

Data was analysis using the SPSS version 25. All the results were presented in function of the two study groups. The first group includes women with Placenta Previa Accreta shown on the ultrasound and the second groups includes women with other Placenta presentation on the ultrasound.

All nominal variables were presented by frequencies and percentages. Quantitative variables were presented by means and standard deviation. Tests used in the bivariate analysis were the chi-square test, Fisher exact test, independent t-test, and the Mann-Whitney test. p-Value was set at 5%.

Results

Women were distributed into two groups according to the findings ultrasound about state of placenta. The first group included 84 women with Placenta Previa Accreta (38.2% of the total population). Discussion

The American College of Obstetrics and Gynecology (ACOG) notes an increasing PAS rate over four decades, primarily due to more cesarean deliveries and other factors like placenta previa, advanced maternal age, and prior uterine surgery [8,15,16]. Delivery in experienced centers with multidisciplinary teams is advised [8]. Rafik Hariri University Hospital (RHUH) in Lebanon, a key tertiary care center, has extensive experience with PAS patients.

Syrian refugee (SR) women in Lebanon show altered reproductive patterns post-displacement, with increased cesarean deliveries and maternal complications, and decreased pregnancy spacing and breastfeeding rates [17]. The ongoing economic crisis in Lebanon has affected healthcare resources, notably limiting the availability of advanced imaging modalities, such as MRI and impacting blood bank reserves. These constraints likely influenced the preferential use of ultrasound for diagnosis and may have affected perioperative management decisions. Syrian refugee women also face additional challenges including delayed prenatal care and resource limitations, which may exacerbate PAS severity and complicate management.

Marshall et al.’s systematic review revealed a statistically significant elevated risk of placenta accreta and hysterectomy in women with placenta previa and three or more cesarean sections [18]. A comprehensive study spanning two decades with over 64,000 births indicated that women with two or more prior cesarean sections faced a significantly increased risk of developing PAS [19]. Correspondingly, our study found a higher prevalence of Placenta Accreta Spectrum (PAS) among multiparous women with at least one previous cesarean delivery, an average age of 33 years, signs of placenta previa in 94.3% of cases, and a history of uterine surgeries, predominantly curettage and myomectomy.

Advanced maternal age (AMA) is linked to sclerotic vascular changes affecting the myometrium, impacting placental development [20,21]. These histopathological alterations can disrupt normal placental development and are implicated in the pathophysiology of conditions, such as placenta accreta spectrum (PAS) [22,23]. Studies indicate that with advancing maternal age, the risk of vascular conditions, such as hypertensive disorders and placental complications like previa markedly increases. This highlights the association between AMA and adverse peri-operative outcomes [24,25].

Histopathological examinations have demonstrated that advanced maternal age correlates with vascular sclerotic changes in the myometrium. These changes may compromise the integrity of the uteroplacental blood flow, which is critical for delivering oxygen and nutrients to the fetus [26]. Importantly, the increased prevalence of cesarean sections also compounds the risks associated with AMA, particularly concerning the development of PAS. Studies indicate that cesarean sections can lead to scarring of the uterine wall, thereby predisposing women to abnormal placentation [24].

In the context of PAS, the combination of AMA and prior uterine surgeries, such as cesarean deliveries, significantly raises the likelihood of severe complications, including hemorrhage and the necessity for hysterectomy [23,27,28].

Previous placenta previa was also identified as a key risk factor for PAS development. Additionally, data from a UK obstetric surveillance study demonstrated a notable increase in PAS incidence—from 1.7 per 10,000 women overall to 577 per 10,000 among women who had both a prior cesarean section and placenta previa, underscoring the compounded risk factors [21].

Out of the 220 patients included in the study, 187 underwent cesarean hysterectomy. This procedure is key in the management of PAS and is directly associated with the risk of urinary tract injuries due to the altered pelvic anatomy and increased surgical complexity in these cases.

With no bladder invasion found in PAS patients and a low injury rate of 3% in non-PAS cases (p = 0.507), our results further demonstrate that bladder injuries were indeed uncommon in both the PAS and non-PAS groups. This implies that even though PAS may make surgical procedures more difficult, the risk of bladder damage can be successfully reduced by using surgical techniques, such as vesico-uterine dissection [29,30]. Our study’s low incidence of bladder injuries is in line with the current body of research whereby it is shown that placental diseases present in changing pelvic morphology can in fact increase the risk of such injuries [31,32].

When it comes to ureter injuries, our data showed no significant differences between PAS and non-PAS cases (p = 0.677), with a low prevalence of roughly 2.7% in both categories. This conclusion is also in line with the existing literature, which shows that preventive ureteral stent use is a widespread practice to prevent problems from ureter injuries during difficult procedures, which are still uncommon [29,33,34]. Following best practices in surgical settings is crucial, as it is palpable in our study through the ureteral stent placement are similar across both groups (79.8% in PAS vs. 77.2% in non-PAS; p = 0.656) which emphasizes on a standardized approach to managing ureteral complications [30,33,34].

Furthermore, there were no significant differences between PAS and non-PAS cases in our examination of intraoperative bleeding and transfusion rates, and the estimated blood loss was comparable (1422.62 mL in PAS vs. 1443.01 mL in non-PAS; p = 0.876). This implies that when properly handled, the surgical complications linked to PAS does not always result in higher morbidity [30,35]. In addition, it is also worth noting that multidisciplinary treatment is essential for effective postoperative recovery, as evidenced by the fact that the mean length of hospitalization was the same for both groups (5.74 days for non-PAS vs. 5.45 days for PAS; p = 0.633) [30,35].

The incidence of PAS in our study demonstrates a notable prevalence, particularly among women with prior cesarean deliveries and those diagnosed with placenta previa. Current evidence from international cohorts indicates varying rates of PAS, often influenced by regional cesarean section rates. For instance, Jauniaux et al. [36] report an incidence rate of 3–15% among pregnancies complicated by placenta previa, while higher rates up to 1.7 per 10,000 pregnancies overall have been documented, escalating to about 577 per 10,000 in high-risk groups, such as those with multiple prior cesarean deliveries [36,37].

The findings from our Lebanese study reveal that women with previous C-section deliveries represent a substantial portion of PAS cases. These findings reaffirm existing data from international sources where a history of cesarean delivery serves as a significant risk factor [36].

In terms of surgical outcomes, our study reports similar intraoperative complications and blood loss relative to the global trends. For example, mean estimated blood loss and rates of urinary tract injuries align with findings from Baldwin et al. [38], which noted that the clinical management of PAS cases often mirrors standard obstetric protocols, thus minimizing complications. In contrast, our findings showed slightly elevated blood loss (1422.62 mL in PAS cases) compared to the reported 1–15% rates of significant bleeding in cases requiring hysterectomy [38]. Nonetheless, it is worth noting that our cohort shows a comparable surgical framework that includes the strategic use of ureteral stents as preventive measures against injuries [39].

Moreover, the surgical techniques employed, including the frequency of vertical incisions in PAS cases (95.2%), align with expectations in tackling such complex obstetric scenarios [40].

Disparities in healthcare resource availability directly influence management outcomes in PAS cases. Our study denotes that 86.8% of women with Placenta Previa Accreta had medical coverage, which echoes systemic differences noted in global literature. For example, substantial variability exists in the management of PAS, often exacerbated by regional healthcare resources. In particular, highly-equipped facilities in urban centers tend to report lower maternal morbidity compared to those in lower-resource settings [41].

On another level, according to our results, there is a lack of significant differences in urinary tract injury rates between PAS and non-PAS cases. This echoes the importance of careful surgical planning and the participation of multidisciplinary teams in reducing the risks associated with complex surgeries [29,30,35]. In addition, when considering high-risk situations, prophylactic ureteral stents seem to help maintain low complication rates, even though there is some conflicting evidence about their effectiveness in preventing ureteral injuries [29,33,34]. Our study’s conclusions support the use of standardized surgical techniques, preoperative planning, and early diagnosis as they can assist in lowering the incidence of urinary tract injuries and associated consequences.

Limitations

This retrospective study is subject to several inherent limitations. The reliance on historical medical records for the purpose of this study may have resulted in selection bias and an incomplete capture of the data since documentation standards at the hospital have evolved over the past 15-years. In addition, some inconsistent documentation may affect the accuracy of some variables. These factors limit our ability to control for confounding factors fully and should be considered when interpreting the results. Furthermore, despite our well-established ultrasound criteria, the operator-dependent nature of ultrasound and variation in image interpretation remain potential limitations, which may affect the accuracy of our findings. Another limitation of the present study is the absence of a multivariate regression analysis to identify independent risk factors for urinary tract injuries. Future prospective studies should consider incorporating multivariate techniques to better elucidate these risk factors. We also encourage future studies to perform stratification based on invasion depth or preoperative risk scores. Additionally, another limitation of this study is the absence of long-term follow-up data regarding outcomes, such as ureteral strictures and urinary incontinence. Future prospective studies should aim to evaluate these long-term sequelae.

On another level, it is important to note that the findings of this study are derived from a single tertiary care center which may limit the generalizability of our results. Potential differences can stem from regional variations in clinical practices and the availability of resources in different healthcare centers. From this perspective, prospective studies should take this limitation into consideration and perform multicenter to validate these findings and develop a more comprehensive understanding of PAS management across different healthcare settings.

Finally, although comparing PAS severity and surgical complications between Syrian refugees and Lebanese patients would be insightful, the current dataset does not allow for a robust subgroup analysis. Future prospective, multicenter studies should aim to address these differences.

Conclusion

All in all, it is worth noting that our 15-year retrospective study does shed the light on the value of multidisciplinary treatment concerning the surgical and clinical therapy of placenta accreta spectrum (PAS) diseases.

According to our research, there were few urinary tract injuries, especially those involving the bladder and ureters, and there were no significant differences between the PAS and non-PAS groups. While ureter injuries happened in both groups at a low rate of about 2.7%, it is important to mention that bladder injuries were uncommon, and additionally, no bladder invasion found in PAS patients. From this perspective, our findings shed the light on how surgical methods, such as vesico-uterine dissection, can correct the structural abnormalities which can stem from placental diseases. Furthermore, 80% of cases involve the use of prophylactic ureteral stents, which emphasizes the importance of standardized preventative measures in reducing ureteral problems.

Operative outcomes, which encompass the estimated blood loss, transfusion rates, and the length of hospitalization, were comparable to non-PAS cases despite the higher surgical complexity linked to PAS. This comes at a time when PAS patients displayed higher rates of vertical and fundal uterine incisions. This illustrates how the possible morbidity linked to PAS can be mitigated by early diagnosis, preoperative planning, and adherence to established surgical procedures.
