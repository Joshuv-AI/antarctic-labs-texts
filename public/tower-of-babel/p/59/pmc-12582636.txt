Visualizing Real‐World Pain Treatment Pathways in Chronic Disease: A Sequence‐Based Analysis of Polypharmacy in Systemic Lupus Erythematosus
Authors: Nathan Le, Martha O. Kenney, Andrew Walker, Tricia Park, Yashaar Chaichian, Michael H. Weisman, Anushka Irani, Selen Bozkurt, Titilola Falasinnu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo examine real‐world patterns in pain management among adults with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), we focused on the sequencing of pain modalities, polypharmacy, and demographic variation.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of electronic health records from adults with SLE treated at a single academic medical center between 2005 and 2024. We included patients who received at least one pain‐related therapy, including glucocorticoids, opioids, nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), antidepressants, anticonvulsants, muscle relaxants, and nonpharmacologic interventions. We used sequence analysis and Sankey diagrams to map treatment trajectories and compared patterns across two periods: 2005 to 2014 and 2015 to 2024. Polypharmacy was defined as receiving five or more pain‐related prescriptions within 12 months. We examined trends by age, sex, and race or ethnicity.ResultsAmong 769 patients, initiation of glucocorticoids and opioids declined over time (58%–51% and 28%–22%, respectively), whereas NSAID use increased (19%–29%). The median number of pain prescriptions decreased, and polypharmacy prevalence dropped from 53% to 45%. In recent years, younger adults and men received more prescriptions, with 69% of men receiving five or more compared with 43% of women. Racial disparities narrowed overall in recent years but persisted in sensitivity analyses excluding glucocorticoids.ConclusionPain management in SLE is shifting away from high‐risk therapies; however, prescribing intensity remains high and varies by demographic group. Visualizations serve as a complementary lens to traditional prescription counts, allowing representation of polypharmacy not only by volume but also by the diversity and sequencing of therapeutic classes over time.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic pain is increasingly recognized as a disease entity in its own right, with complex biologic, psychological, and social dimensions that extend beyond its origins in tissue damage or inflammation. 1 , 2 Yet, in many chronic disease contexts—including systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)—pain remains undertreated, frequently overshadowed by disease activity metrics and often approached as a symptom to be managed rather than a trajectory to be understood. 3 This conceptual gap has profound clinical consequences: patients with SLE often cycle through pharmacologic and nonpharmacologic therapies with limited relief, experience high rates of polypharmacy, 4 , 5 and face elevated risks for opioid‐related harm. For example, between 11% and 35% of patients with SLE are prescribed long‐term opioid therapy, and patients with SLE have been found to experience higher rates of opioid‐related overdoses than the general population. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9

Despite this burden, pain‐specific management pathways in SLE remain poorly characterized in both guidelines and practice. This is especially problematic in the context of opioid minimization efforts because the absence of clear, effective alternatives can lead to fragmented, trial‐and‐error approaches to care. 10 , 11 A recent study identified shifting pain management trends in SLE and related autoimmune rheumatic diseases. 12 Nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drug (NSAID) use steadily increased among newly diagnosed patients with SLE, peaking at 38% by 2020, whereas opioid use declined from ~35% to 20%. Use of muscle relaxants and physical therapy also rose modestly, whereas topical treatments remained consistently low. However, little is known about whether—and how—prescribing patterns for pain in SLE have evolved over time. In addition, no study has examined whether treatment sequences differ by demographic characteristics, such as age, sex, and race or ethnicity. Addressing these questions is critical to understanding the real‐world burden of pain and the extent to which treatment pathways may reflect or reinforce disparities in care. This approach would allow pain in SLE to be framed not simply as a byproduct of immune dysregulation but as a longitudinal treatment target in its own right.

In this study, we leverage nearly two decades of electronic health record (EHR) data to examine real‐world pain management strategies among adults with SLE. Using sequence analysis and data visualization techniques—including Sankey and sunburst diagrams—we map the trajectories of pain treatment over time, capturing both shifts in clinical practice and demographic patterns in prescribing. We represent polypharmacy in two complementary ways: by quantifying the number of pain‐related prescriptions patients received over a 12‐month period and by visually illustrating the diversity and sequencing of pain medication classes over time. Our primary hypothesis is that the proportion of patients with five or more pain prescriptions in a one‐year period decreased from the 2005 to 2014 period to the 2015 to 2024 period, reflecting a shift in clinical practice toward reduced reliance on polypharmacy. Our secondary hypothesis is that the use of corticosteroids and opioids as initial pain therapies has declined over time in favor of other treatment modalities. These findings have implications not only for SLE but also for broader efforts to inform pain treatment guidelines across chronic and complex conditions.

METHODS

Study design and patient population

The Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the Stanford School of Medicine approved this study (IRB# 53750). We retrospectively queried EHR to identify adult patients who visited outpatient rheumatology clinics, including a joint immunology–dermatology clinic, at an academic medical center in Northern California from January 2005 to November 2024. The inclusion criteria were patients with at least two documented diagnoses of SLE recorded at least three months apart, based on International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Version code 710.0 or International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Version codes M32.1, M32.8, or M32.9.

Pain management modalities

Treatment modalities were selected based on established use in pain management for rheumatic diseases and prior literature on prescribing trends in this population. 12 , 13 , 14 We classified pain management modalities using structured prescription and procedural data from EHRs. Medications were grouped into the following therapeutic classes: opioids (eg, fentanyl, morphine, and oxycodone), NSAIDs (eg, ibuprofen, naproxen, and meloxicam), acetaminophen, anticonvulsants (eg, gabapentin and pregabalin), antidepressants (eg, amitriptyline, duloxetine, and milnacipran), skeletal muscle relaxants and benzodiazepines (eg, alprazolam, cyclobenzaprine, and tizanidine), and glucocorticoids (eg, prednisone, methylprednisolone, and dexamethasone). A comprehensive list of drug names for each category is provided in Supplementary Table 1. In addition to pharmacologic therapies, we identified pain interventions based on procedure codes (Current Procedural Terminology [CPT] codes), which included intraarticular injections, nerve blocks, epidurals, radiofrequency ablation, vertebroplasty or kyphoplasty, neurostimulation, discograms, botulinum toxin injections, and acupuncture. We also captured nonpharmacologic rehabilitative modalities, including physical and occupational therapy services. Supplementary Table 2 provides the full listing of intervention categories and corresponding CPT codes. Patients were required to have at least 12 months of follow‐up data after their first recorded pain prescription. Sociodemographic and lifestyle variables, including age at first recorded pain prescription, sex, and insurance status, were also collected.

Sequence analysis and temporal trends in pain treatment and polypharmacy

To analyze patterns in pain management, we used sequence analysis, a method for exploring patterns in treatment trajectories over time. 15 , 16 , 17 The study focused on patients prescribed any pain treatment modality for SLE during the study period (2005–2024), with the date of the first prescription serving as index date. For each patient, we tracked the initiation and subsequent use of glucocorticoids, opioids, NSAIDs, anticonvulsants, antidepressants, skeletal muscle relaxants and benzodiazepines, topical pain medications, and nonpharmaceutical modalities such as physical therapy. We recorded treatment sequences for up to 12 months after the index date. Transitions between treatment modalities were defined as switches from one therapy to another, the addition of new therapies, or therapy discontinuation. We identified and quantified the most common treatment sequences and compared patterns across two time frames: 2005 to 2014 and 2015 to 2024. These dates were chosen to reflect the corresponding changes in chronic pain management guidelines and treatment options by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. 18 , 19 Polypharmacy was defined as having five or more pain prescriptions during the one‐year period of observation, and sociodemographic characteristics of patients meeting this criterion were assessed. 20 We used two‐proportion z‐tests to compare the proportion of patients with five or more pain prescriptions between the 2005 to 2014 and 2015 to 2024 periods across demographic subgroups. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 to identify meaningful changes in high‐prescription patterns over time.

Visualization of treatment transitions

Sankey diagrams were used to depict the flow of patients through different pain management modalities during the first year after treatment initiation. These diagrams depict the magnitude of transitions between and among treatments, with nodes representing specific pain management modalities (eg, glucocorticoids, opioids, and NSAIDs) and connecting lines (flows) indicating transitions between modalities. The thickness of the lines is proportional to the number of patients following a specific transition. These visualizations highlighted common treatment pathways and bottlenecks, such as frequent escalation to more intensive therapies such as opioids. In addition, we generated sunburst plots to show the proportion of cases corresponding to specific sequences of medications. The center of the sunburst diagram represents the initiation of pain management, with each subsequent layer indicating transitions to different modalities. Patients who remained on the same treatment modality were represented by nonbranching segments, whereas those transitioning to new therapies were shown as branching segments. The full source code of the analysis is freely available on the Open Science Framework. 21 All analyses and visualizations were conducted using Python and R, with provided examples showcasing the most frequent treatment groups.

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted two sensitivity analyses to examine the robustness of our findings by excluding opioids and steroids, two pain‐related modalities with complex clinical indications. First, we excluded opioids to explore treatment pathways. Opioids are not disease modifying and carry a high risk of adverse effects and dependency. 22 , 23 Their use in rheumatology is generally reserved for short‐term pain relief when other treatments are insufficient. 24 By removing opioids from the analysis, we aimed to characterize patterns of pain management that more closely reflect sustained and potentially safer approaches to chronic pain treatment. Second, steroids were included as a pain‐related modality due to their common real‐world use for treating inflammation‐associated pain in SLE, such as arthritis or serositis. 25 However, steroids are primarily prescribed for disease activity rather than pain, especially in cases involving serious organ complications such as lupus nephritis. 26 In these instances, the primary goal of steroid therapy is not pain relief but rather the suppression and management of the underlying inflammatory process. 27

Sex differences in prescribing patterns

We observed notable shifts in the frequency of pain management prescriptions within one year of treatment initiation across the study period (Table 2). The median number of prescriptions decreased from 5.0 (interquartile range [IQR] 2.0–13.0) in 2005 to 2014 to 4.0 (IQR 2.0–9.0) in 2015 to 2024. The proportion of patients who received five or more prescriptions significantly declined from 52.5% to 45.3% (P = 0.047), indicating a broader move away from polypharmacy. Sex‐specific trends reversed over time. In 2005 to 2014, 53.2% of women and 43.3% of men received five or more prescriptions. By 2015 to 2024, however, a larger proportion of men (68.6%) than women (43.0%) received five or more prescriptions, reflecting a significant increase among men (P = 0.041) and a significant decrease among women (P = 0.007). Median prescription counts rose in men from 3.5 to 7.0 and declined in women from 5.0 to 4.0.

Age differences in polypharmacy

In 2005 to 2014, patients aged 27 to 40 years had the highest prevalence of five or more prescriptions (59.5%), whereas 47.1% of those aged 18 to 26 years received multiple prescriptions. In contrast, during 2015 to 2024, 70.0% of patients aged 18 to 26 years received five or more prescriptions, compared with 42.5% among those aged 66 and older. Although these differences did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.071 and 0.066, respectively), they suggest a growing treatment burden in younger patients.

Racial and ethnic disparities in pain management

Black patients had the highest proportion receiving five or more prescriptions (63.6%), with a median count of 14.0, compared with 5.5 in White patients and 5.0 in Asian patients. By 2015 to 2024, these differences narrowed: 47.1% of Black patients, 46.8% of White patients, and 37.0% of Asian patients received five or more prescriptions, with the decline among Asian patients reaching statistical significance (P = 0.0428). Ethnicity and insurance status showed no statistically significant changes over time, although we observed modest reductions in polypharmacy among patients with both private and public insurance coverage. Sensitivity analyses, excluding opioids, showed similar trends (Supplementary Table 3). However, we found wider disparities by race when excluding steroids, in which 38% of White patients were given five or more prescriptions, compared with 28% of Black patients and 31% of Asian patients (Supplementary Table 4).

Steroids were the dominant initial treatment in 2005 to 2014, with 224 patients (58%) starting with this modality, but their use decreased to 195 patients (51%) in 2015 to 2024. DISCUSSION

This study offers a real‐world, longitudinal view of pain management among patients with SLE, highlighting evolving prescribing practices over two decades. We observed a decrease in the median number of pain‐related prescriptions accompanied by a more diverse range of pain management modalities in recent years. Notably, the reduction in the use of steroids and opioids as initial pain management modalities was accompanied by an increase in NSAIDs and alternative therapies such as anticonvulsants and pain interventions. These findings are in accordance with previous research. 12 These changes likely reflect a growing emphasis on minimizing long‐term steroid and opioid exposure due to their well‐documented side effects, particularly in chronic disease contexts like SLE. 28 , 29 , 30

Our visualizations serve as a complementary lens to traditional prescription counts, allowing us to represent polypharmacy not only by volume but also by the diversity and sequencing of therapeutic classes over time. 31 For instance, the sunburst diagram reveals that nearly half of patients initiated on acetaminophen transitioned to more intensive therapies such as opioids, anticonvulsants, or glucocorticoids, suggesting suboptimal relief and a fragmented care experience, consistent with patient preferences. 32 The presence of frequent transitions and branching sequences underscores the absence of consensus around effective first‐line treatments for pain in SLE. These patterns reflect the real‐world clinical dilemma of navigating pain management without robust guidance specific to autoimmune populations. Similarly, the Sankey diagrams highlight escalation pathways and bottlenecks—for example, from NSAIDs to opioids—raising questions about how treatment decisions are made when earlier options are insufficient or poorly tolerated. Such insights can inform future efforts to develop algorithmic decision support or personalized care pathways rooted in patient‐reported outcomes and comparative effectiveness.

The use of steroids and opioids in SLE pain management remains contentious, even with the advent of newer therapies. For instance, a study of 49,413 patients with SLE (90% women, mean age 50 years) found that 69% received glucocorticoids, with an average of five prescriptions in the first year, and 53% had at least one opioid prescription, of which 35% involved chronic use. 6 In patients initiated on belimumab (n = 1,710), glucocorticoid use decreased by 9%, with average daily dose reducing significantly from 14.5 to 11.9 mg (P < 0.001) in the six months following initiation, 6 although the prevalence of opioid use remained unchanged. Our findings similarly indicate that a substantial proportion of patients with SLE are treated with glucocorticoids for disease control and opioids for chronic pain management. However, we observed that patients who started with acetaminophen often transitioned to additional or alternative medications, including steroids, NSAIDs, opioids, or muscle relaxants and benzodiazepines. A recent review of chronic pain treatment strategies in rheumatology suggested that acetaminophen should be used as front‐line therapy for pain in rheumatic inflammatory diseases; 33 however, patients with these conditions show a considerable and statistically significant preference for NSAIDs compared with acetaminophen. 32 This highlights the need for further research to identify more effective initial pain management strategies that minimize polypharmacy and cycling through therapies.

Demographic variations in pain management patterns were also observed, suggesting that age and sex influence treatment decisions. Younger patients in more recent years are more likely to receive a broader range of pain management prescriptions. This finding contrasts with established understanding of pain trajectories across the lifespan, which associate aging with increased chronic pain prevalence and a corresponding rise in the use of pain management modalities. 34 For example, data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey indicate that prescription pain medication use increases with age, rising from 5% in adults aged 20 to 39 years to 15% in those aged 60 years and older. 35 However, our findings align with research suggesting that in the chronic disease contexts, younger patients may experience high pain severity and more intensive medication use. For instance, younger patients with cancer have been shown to report higher pain scores (5.14 vs 3.59, P = 0.01) and use opioids more frequently than older patients (115‐ vs 95‐mg oral morphine equivalent per day), highlighting potential age‐related differences in pain perception and management. 36 Additionally, older patients were more likely to use acetaminophen (33.0% vs 10.7%, P = 0.02) and less likely to use NSAIDs (11.0% vs 28.6%, P = 0.02), suggesting an age‐related preference for nonopioid analgesics in pain management. 36

Regarding gender, men with SLE were more likely to receive five or more pain prescriptions compared with women (69% vs 43%) with a median of 7 prescriptions for men versus 4 for women. Recently, Zahlan et al examined gender differences and found that the prevalence of polypharmacy (use of ≥10 medications) was slightly higher in men (31%) compared with women (25%). 37 However, we recently found that women with SLE had a higher (53%) prevalence of chronic overlapping pain conditions (eg, fibromyalgia, migraine, and chronic low back pain) than men (34%). 38 This incongruence may stem from gender differences in pain reporting, prescribing patterns, or health‐seeking behaviors. Men with SLE, who might delay seeking treatment or report pain differently, may be prescribed more medications once they seek care, potentially leading to higher polypharmacy despite a lower prevalence of chronic overlapping pain conditions. Additionally, clinicians may perceive pain in men with SLE as more severe or refractory, contributing to a greater reliance on multiple medications for pain management in men. Because the literature and common practice have traditionally supported the concept that lupus in men is felt to be more severe because of the presence of increased organ damage, factors such as diagnostic bias leading to later recognition of the disease are suspected but not adequately addressed. 39 Similarly, racial and ethnic differences in prescribing patterns may stem from systemic inequities, including differences in access to specialty care, cultural variation in pain communication, and implicit bias in provider decision‐making. 40 , 41 These structural and interpersonal factors could contribute to the observed differences in polypharmacy and should be investigated in future studies using qualitative or mixed‐methods approaches. Future research should explore the intersection of clinician bias, patient preferences, and prior treatment experiences to better understand the gendered dynamics of pain management in SLE.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, because it was conducted at a single academic center where tertiary care is the norm, the findings may not generalize to other settings, especially those with different patient demographics or care practices. Additionally, as patients may have received pain management care outside of this center, our analysis could underestimate the overall health care use if these external treatments were not captured in our dataset. We were only able to assess the prescription of a medication, and we were unable to know if it was actually filled. In addition, it is common practice that physicians taking care of patients with SLE may prescribe glucocorticoids or other modalities with the instruction that the patient should decide when and how much each drug will be used; this complication could not be captured in our analysis. 42

Secondly, we focused exclusively on prevalent cases of SLE, which may not fully represent the treatment patterns for newly diagnosed patients. We were unable to account for the duration of SLE, which may significantly influence pain management strategies. Without this information, we are unable to evaluate whether treatment patterns differ between patients with long‐standing disease and those newly diagnosed. Future studies should address this by examining pain management in newly diagnosed SLE cases. Furthermore, expanding the scope to include other autoimmune rheumatic diseases could provide a more comprehensive view of pain management practices across similar patient populations. Thirdly, we did not account for time intervals between prescriptions or how many refills were issued before a change was made. Fourthly, we did not collect data on SLE disease activity, preventing us from analyzing how changes in pain treatment modalities align with disease activity over time.

Fifthly, as prescription indications were not captured, it was not possible to distinguish between pain treatments prescribed specifically for SLE‐related pain versus unrelated pain conditions. Thus, we could not confirm whether medications were prescribed for SLE‐related pain or unrelated conditions. For instance, the use of antidepressants or antiepileptics could reflect treatment for depression or seizures rather than pain. 43 , 44 These uncertainties highlight the need for more granular data that links prescriptions explicitly to specific pain diagnoses or causes. Such data would allow for a clearer understanding of the drivers behind pain management decisions in patients with SLE. This limitation complicates interpretation of treatment sequences and suggests that future studies should aim for greater indication specificity, such as through linkage with diagnosis codes or natural language processing of clinical notes. Sixthly, we were unable to assess whether treatment transitions were associated with improvements in pain outcomes, functional status, or quality of life. This precludes conclusions about the effectiveness or appropriateness of different pain management strategies. Future work should link prescribing sequences to patient‐reported outcomes and longitudinal measures of disease burden to clarify the clinical relevance of observed treatment pathways. Seventhly, over‐the‐counter treatments such as nonprescription NSAIDs or acetaminophen are not consistently recorded in the EHR and were only captured if prescribed. Similarly, procedural interventions such as intravenous lidocaine infusions and behavioral treatments like pain psychology referrals were rarely documented and thus excluded.

Nevertheless, this study has several notable strengths. By using a comprehensive EHR dataset from well‐established patients in an academic center, we were able to capture detailed, longitudinal data on pain management modalities for patients with SLE over nearly two decades. The use of sequence analysis and Sankey diagrams provided nuanced insights into the progression and transitions between therapies, highlighting shifts in treatment preferences over time. Additionally, the large sample size allowed us to examine demographic factors such as age, sex, and race or ethnicity, providing valuable information on how these characteristics influence treatment patterns. This approach offers a real‐world perspective on pain management strategies, which can inform future clinical guidelines and encourage more personalized approaches to care.

These findings underscore the urgency of developing targeted, evidence‐based pain management guidelines for patients with SLE and similar conditions. Although we were unable to assess pain relief or functional outcomes, our findings suggest that prescribing patterns in SLE reflect a distinct and iterative process that may warrant its own framework of clinical understanding. Future research should link treatment sequences to patient‐reported outcomes to determine whether different trajectories result in meaningful improvements in pain or function. As the field of rheumatology continues to embrace precision approaches, our study contributes real‐world evidence that can help shape more equitable and effective care strategies—grounded in patient complexity.
