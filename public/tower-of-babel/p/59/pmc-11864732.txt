Mediastinal Hematoma as a Potential Complication of Endovascular Intracranial Intervention
Authors: Salvador F. Gutierrez‐Aguirre, Otavio F. De Toledo, Eduarda M. Freitas, Victor H.C. Benalia, Amin Aghaebrahim, Ricardo A. Hanel, Eric Sauvageau
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo highlight the rare incidence of mediastinal hematoma as a complication associated with transradial access, as reported in both cardiovascular and neurovascular fields. Interventionists must be aware of this potential risk and the strategies to manage it.Case PresentationA 66‐year‐old woman presented to an outside hospital's emergency department with 2 months of dizziness and vertigo. A right posterior communication artery aneurysm was discovered incidentally on workup images. On discussing potential options, including observation, the patient opted for endovascular intervention. The aneurysm was treated using a Woven Endobridge Single‐Layer Sphere (SLS) intrasaccular flow disrupter device. After the procedure, the patient was extubated but subsequently required reintubation because of the context of stridor. Radiologic evaluation revealed a mediastinal hematoma, which was suspected to be a direct complication from the transradial access. This required immediate treatment with coil embolization of the perforated vessel at the subclavian artery originating from the costocervical trunk.ConclusionMediastinal hematoma is a rare complication after neurointerventional procedures by transradial access. Prompt diagnosis and appropriate treatment are critical to managing this potentially lethal condition.

Transradial access (TRA) is a widely used approach in interventional procedures, including percutaneous coronary intervention and cerebral angiography. 1 However, mediastinal hematomas can occur in ≈0.74% of coronary procedures and have been associated with elevated mortality rates. 2 We present a case report where a subclavian artery branch perforation resulted in a wide mediastinal hematoma during an endovascular aneurysm embolization.

Case Presentation

A 66‐year‐old woman with comorbidities of hypertension and type 2 diabetes presented with a 2‐month history of dizziness and vertigo. Imaging revealed an incidental right posterior communication artery saccular aneurysm. On referral to our institution and considering the expected natural history, we discussed alternative treatment modalities, including observation, surgical clipping, and endovascular treatment. Ultimately, at the patient's request, the endovascular intervention was chosen.

Angiography was performed using a transradial approach, using a 5F slender introducer sheath, a 0.035‐in. guidewire, and a 5F Simmons catheter. A roadmap was used for the navigation of the radial and brachial arteries. The radial sheath and the diagnostic catheter were exchanged for a 6F Neuron MAX (Penumbra, CA) and a SOFIA EX. over an exchange glide wire Aristotle 14 wire, and Via 27 were used to access the aneurysm. The embolization was performed using a Woven EndoBridge device (MicroVention, Aliso Viejo, CA).

The patient developed stridor postprocedure, requiring immediate reintubation, and then was transferred to the neurocritical care unit, where hypotension was documented. Computed tomography angiography showed soft‐tissue attenuation and stranding diffusely throughout the visualized portions of the mediastinum, suspicious for mediastinal bleeding, along with a nodular focus of active contrast extravasation identified in the right superior mediastinum to the right of the proximal thoracic esophagus (Figure 1).

Consequently, the patient was taken to the angiosuite by the neurointerventional team to control the bleeding using a transradial approach. Using an Excelsior SL‐10 (Stryker Neurovascular, CA), the microcatheter was then advanced through a 5F Impress Berenstein (Merit Medical, UT) to the subclavian artery over a Transend EX microwire (Boston‐Scientific, Marlborough, MA). Microcatheter injection in the vessel of interest depicted active contrast extravasation (Figure 2); hence, coil embolization was successfully performed on a ruptured branch at the subclavian artery originating from the costocervical trunk (Figure 3). The complete angiogram demonstrated no further evidence of contrast extravasation. Posttreatment, the patient remained stable and intubated.

The following day, the patient demonstrated no further signs of hemodynamic or respiratory distress. Under otolaryngology supervision, she was extubated on the fourth postoperative day and returned to the neurocritical care unit in a stable state. Her recovery progressed steadily, leading to her discharge on the eighth day of her hospitalization. She fully recovered in the short‐term.

Discussion

Transfemoral access is still the most frequently used approach for peri‐interventional procedures, but TRA is emerging as a viable alternative in the endovascular literature. 3 This report illustrates a rare subclavian artery branch perforation with respiratory symptoms attributable to mediastinal hematoma during an intervention angiogram via TRA.

TRA is an appealing alternative to reduce complications without affecting outcomes. 4 Some publications have shown that access site complications for neuroendovascular procedures are 1.8% for TRA compared with 3.2% for transfemoral access. 5 Our complication has been previously reported in the cardiac literature. 2 It has been associated with various mechanisms and risk factors, including but not limited to perforation, dissection, pseudoaneurysm, atherosclerosis, tortuosity, anatomic variants of the subclavian vessel and its branches, the use of anticoagulants, and the use of non–supersmooth guide wires. 6 , 7

In our case, the perforation of the subclavian branch likely occurred during the advancement of the wire into the subclavian artery while accessing the carotid arch. The 0.035 wire can be navigated inadvertently in a smaller vessel subclavian artery branch, which may have a similar downward excursion than the brachiocephalic trunk.

Early diagnosis and proper management are crucial for the patient's prognosis. After experiencing cardiopulmonary symptoms, such as chest pain and dyspnea, following transradial catheterization, it is essential to conduct imaging. Although an X‐ray may show mediastinal widening, its sensitivity is limited as it relies on the bleeding volume. Therefore, it may be reasonable to proceed directly to contrast‐enhanced computed tomography to confirm the bleeding. However, it is important to consider a dye‐induced allergic reaction or a bronchial asthma attack as a differential diagnosis and put the patient on an intravenous corticosteroid and antihistamine injection. 6

If there is progressive bleeding and worsening hemodynamic status, it is recommended to perform angiography with endovascular embolization. Local pressure alone may not be effective in treating mediastinal hematoma because of the presence of the collarbone and costal bones. It is important to emphasize the need for a multidisciplinary assessment to ensure airway patency with intubation, maintain or improve hemodynamic status through transfusion, and involve an interventional team for vessel hemostasis. 6 , 7 Depending on its size, there are various strategies for embolizing the vessel: for large arteries, a stent may be used, whereas for medium‐small branches, options such as microcoils, gel sponge, and glue could be considered. 7 In our case, we preferred coiling to avoid the extravasation of liquid embolization agents to the mediastinum.

Continuous monitoring of the distal wire tip is mandatory to prevent unexpected harmful navigation and vascular injury. This is the first case of mediastinal hemorrhage in the transradial approach related to neurointerventional procedures. This is a complication specific to TRA, and the neurointerventionist should be aware.

Conclusions

Mediastinal hematoma is a rare complication after cerebral angiography performed by TRA. Prompt diagnosis and appropriate treatment are critical to manage this potentially lethal condition.
