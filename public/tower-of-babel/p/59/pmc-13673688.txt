ABSTRACT
BackgroundTandem repeat expansions have been implicated in various neurological conditions. Here, we present a novel hypermethylated CCG repeat expansion on Xp22 in the 5’UTR of BCLAF3 in males with neurodevelopmental disorders.MethodsWe used patient-derived fibroblasts and neuronal models from a family with BCLAF3 repeat expansions to generate multiomic data and investigate downstream molecular consequences of the repeat expansion. To identify additional affected individuals with BCLAF3 repeat expansions, we screened methylation arrays (n = 12,375) and short-read genomes (n = 15,963) from probands with neurodevelopmental presentations. We also characterized BCLAF3 repeat expansions in the general population using long-read sequencing data (n = 793) and population-level short-read sequencing data (n = 410,076).ResultsLong-read sequencing validated hypermethylation of expanded repeats. Patient-derived cells showed repressed BCLAF3 RNA and protein expression. We show that the BCLAF3 CCG repeat expansion constitutes a previously uncharacterized fragile site (FRAXG) that shifts the surrounding chromatin compartment from open euchromatin to closed heterochromatin. Using our multiomic screening approaches, we identified three additional unrelated males and one related male cousin with long-read sequencing validated (n = 2) or short-read sequencing predicted (n = 2) repeat expansions. In one family, the BCLAF3 repeats segregate with more severe phenotypes than expected for the primary diagnoses. Long-read sequencing in three carrier mothers showed skewed X-inactivation against the repeat expansion, highlighting the potential deleterious effect of an allele with an expansion. Expansions were absent in long-read sequencing data from control populations. Assessment of the BCLAF3 repeat expansion in the UK Biobank indicates that it may be ~ 20X rarer than FMR1 repeat expansions.ConclusionsCCG repeat expansions in the 5’UTR of BCLAF3 likely constitute a novel genetic etiology associated with X-linked neurodevelopmental phenotypes in males. Future work will be essential to delineate the phenotypic spectrum and determine a disease pathomechanism.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13073-026-01729-4.

Background

Approximately half of the individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders remain without an identifiable molecular cause after undergoing extensive genetic testing [1, 2]. While technological advances have allowed us to detect more variants in an individual’s genome than ever before, determining the consequences and disease-causing potential of those variants remains challenging. Tandem repeat expansions (TREs) can be both difficult to detect and interpret. TREs are short (2–6 bp) or variable number (≥ 7 bp) repeating motifs that can expand from tens to hundreds and thousands of repeats long and are known to cause > 50 human disorders, with 16 being recognized in the last 10 years [3, 4]. TREs can cause disease through a variety of different mechanisms, often disrupting gene expression, affecting gene function, forming toxic protein aggregates, or altering genomic stability. It is thought that the brain is especially susceptible to the deleterious effects of repeat expansions, as illustrated by the fact that most repeat expansion disorders result in neurological and movement phenotypes. CG-rich repeat expansions often occur in regulatory elements and act through epigenetic mechanisms to cause disease. For example, a CGG repeat expansion in the 5 prime untranslated region (UTR) of FMR1 undergoes DNA hypermethylation and leads to gene silencing as the pathophysiological mechanism of Fragile X syndrome (MIM: 300624), the most common inherited cause of intellectual disability in males. Similarly, in Baratela-Scott syndrome (MIM: 615777), a rare skeletal dysplasia, some individuals carry a GGC repeat expansion in the 5’UTR of XYLT1 that silences gene expression to cause disease [5]. In a recent study [6], we investigated DNA methylation from 582 individuals with genetically unresolved pediatric epilepsies and identified a rare and novel hypermethylated CCG repeat expansion in BCLAF3, which was not previously associated with disease.

Here, we use various molecular approaches to investigate the hypermethylation of the rare CCG repeat expansion in the 5’UTR of BCLAF3, which we find constitutes a previously unknown fragile site and leads to transcriptional silencing of BCLAF3. Through global collaborations, we screen human DNA methylation (12,375 individuals) and genome sequencing (15,963 individuals) databases and identify three additional unrelated individuals with neurological disease who have confirmed or predicted BCLAF3 repeat expansions that may contribute to neurological phenotypes. We delineate the population distributions of BCLAF3 repeat sizes in both short- and long-read sequencing data and find BCLAF3 repeat expansions in genetically unresolved cases with neurological diseases. In one family with two different primary diagnoses, we found the BCLAF3 repeat expansion segregates with more severe phenotypes in two males. Overall, our study highlights the importance of using multiomic approaches for both identifying and understanding the molecular consequences of repeat expansions and suggests that the BCLAF3 repeat expansion is a novel cause of neurological and neurodevelopmental phenotypes.

Methods

Cohorts

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital (SJCRH). Written informed consent was provided by parents or legal guardians of Proband 1 with IRB approval from SJCRH and the national ethics committee of New Zealand (NZ HDEC). Samples from Proband 1 and family were collected by a qualified health professional in accordance with hospital policies.

We analyzed long-read sequencing data from lymphoblastoid cell line-derived DNA from controls recruited for the 1000 Genomes Project (1kGP) [7] to determine accurate reference population estimates of BCLAF3 CCG repeat expansion lengths. This data included PacBio HiFi data from 293 1KGP controls (147 males) as part of the HPRC/HGSVC cohort [8, 9] and Oxford nanopore data from 500 1kGP controls (232 males) [10, 11]. All 5 superpopulations are represented in both datasets. The 293 PacBio HiFi data include 96 African (AFR, 33%), 61 East Asian (EAS, 21%), 53 Admixed American (AMR, 18%), 45 South Asian (SAS, 15%), and 38 European (EUR, 13%). The 500 Oxford nanopore data include 163 AFR (33%), 103 SAS (21%), 95 EAS (19%), 72 EUR (14%), and 67 AMR (13%). Lymphoblastoid cell lines were originally obtained from the NHGRI Repository at the Coriell Institute for Medical Research where all have been previously consented to broad data sharing.

The EpiSign Knowledge Database (EKD) includes methylation data from clinical, peripheral blood DNA methylation profiles from disorder-specific reference cohorts and normal controls (general population samples of various ages and racial backgrounds) housed at the London health Sciences Centre Molecular Diagnostics Laboratory. We screened 12,375 DNA methylation array data from cases in the EKD (7,136 reported males) for the BCLAF3 outlier differentially hypermethylated region after being split by XX/XY genotypes for analysis of chrX due to the random X-inactivation in XX genotypes.

Data from the National Genomic Research Library (NGRL) consists of samples from two different sources: the National Health Service (NHS) Genomic Medicine Service (GMS) and the 100,000 Genomes Project (100kGP) [12]. For 100kGP, after ethical approval (14/EE/1112), participants were enrolled in the 100kGP if they or their guardian had given research consent for the proband and relatives to participate. They were recruited by health care professionals at Great Ormond Street Hospital, according to the eligibility criteria. For NHS GMS, as part of standard diagnostic pathway in the NHS in England, genome sequencing was performed for specific clinical indications as detailed in the National genomic test directory for rare and inherited disease after informed consent. Between both sources, we collectively genotyped srGS data for BCLAF3 repeat expansions in 31,111 controls (15,269 males); 3,673 unsolved probands (1,918 males); and 15,963 unsolved probands (9,158 males).

Over 500,000 participants in the UK Biobank were enrolled in the UK under electronic, broad consent between 2006 and 2010 [13]. A variety of data measurements and samples (blood, urine, and saliva) were collected at baseline and longitudinal follow-up. From the set of 490,416 individuals with Illumina 150-bp paired-end srGS data, we first removed samples that we were notified had withdrawn their consent for inclusion in the study or, using srGS quality metrics, had values ≤ 2 for either “NRD genotyping” (UK Biobank data field f32063) or “Freemix verify BAM ID” (f32062). We also utilized pairwise kinship coefficients provided by the UK Biobank, and for samples with second-degree relationships or higher (kinship coefficient > 0.0883), we retained a single unrelated individual. We performed global ancestry prediction using ~ 64,000 high-quality LD pruned single nucleotide variants (SNVs) with minor allele frequency > 5% [14], identified 411,266 individuals with European ancestry, and focused on our analysis in European individuals only. In total, we were able to genotype the BCLAF3 repeat expansion in 410,076 individuals (221,004 females and 189,072 males) and the FMR1 repeat expansion in 410,028 individuals (221,004 females and 189,024 males).

DNA methylation array analyses

DNA methylation array data from fibroblasts and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) were obtained from DNA extracted using standard protocols, with approximately 250–500 ng of DNA bisulfite converted. The Illumina Infinium MethylationEPIC v1.0 (850 K array) or the Illumina Infinium MethylationEPIC v2.0 (935 K array) bead chip arrays were processed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Based on available arrays, the fibroblasts were run on EPIC v1.0, and iPSCs were run on EPIC v2.0.

RNA-seq of patient-derived fibroblasts

RNA was extracted using the Quick-RNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research) from dermal fibroblasts (all from passage 6) established from skin punch biopsies for Family 1 (n = 4) as described in the results. RNA-seq for all samples was performed in a single batch using standard Illumina short-read sequencing practices, and the reads were processed using the “nf-core/rnaseq” pipeline aligning to hg38 (version 3.7 running with Nextflow version 21.10.6) [15, 16]. Removal of the adapter sequences was performed using Trim Galore!, and low-quality reads were eliminated with FastQC [17]. Subsequently, reads were aligned to a reference genome using the STAR aligner [18]. Gene expression quantification was performed using Salmon [19], which estimates transcript abundance. Lowly expressed genes with an average expression across all samples of < 1 count (normalized counts) were filtered out, and age at sample collection was modeled out as a covariate. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 [20]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to assess variation within the dataset. All samples most closely clustered with their own replicates as expected. The mother’s RNA-seq data clustered away from the proband (Proband 1), brother, and father on PC2. Given that all samples were run in the same batch, we suspected that sex effects (XX vs. XY) drove this variation on PC2. Based on these sex differences, that the mother also carries the repeat expansion, and that we are most interested in assessing chrX, we removed the mother’s data from the differential expression analyses to mitigate any confounding effects due to the presence of two X chromosomes and the repeat expansion.

RNA-seq dropout analysis

To determine gene “dropout,” the OUTRIDER algorithm [21] was applied to RNA-seq data for Family 1 against 36 other internal controls and 139 publicly available fibroblast samples [22] for a total of 179 RNA-seq samples analyzed. Our data was aligned to hg19 using the same “nf-core/rnaseq” pipeline (version 3.7 running with Nextflow version 21.10.6) but with counts quantified using RSEM [23] to match the methods of the publicly available data matrix and are only used for this specific outlier analysis. PCA displayed subtle and overlapping batch groupings between our data and the publicly available controls that were corrected out using ComBat with the sva R package [24]. Genes with Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM) < 1 were removed as lowly expressed genes (n = 5,279). Results were considered significant if they had a padj<0.05 and a z-score cutoff of ± 2.

Genome sequencing screening

Short-read genome sequencing (srGS) was performed on peripheral blood-derived DNA using standard Illumina short-read sequencing techniques and bioinformatic approaches. ExpansionHunter (v5) was used for short-read tandem repeat genotyping using catalog-based method on n = 684 genome sequencing samples. The GRCh38 catalog entry was included as a .json file to ExpansionHunter with the following parameters: “LocusStructure”: “(CCG)*”, “ReferenceRegion”: “chrX:19990922–19990973.” The exact catalog entry used is located in Additional File 1: Data S1 [25]. The sex (argument --sex) was specified as the reported sex (as either male or female) to assist with correct genotyping of BCLAF3 located on chrX. If reported sex was not available, sex was predicted as evidenced by reads mapping to SRY. Samples flagged with low depth at the BCLAF3 locus were removed from downstream analysis (n = 127 samples). “LowDepth” was defined as the overall locus depth being below 10x or number of reads spanning one or both breakends being below 5. Additionally, n = 69 samples had only ‘NA’ values listed for the derived BCLAF3 genotype estimate and were thus excluded, leaving a remaining 488 samples (319 controls, 136 unsolved probands, and 33 solved probands) with BCLAF3 genotyping estimates for the cohort analysis.

Nanopore LRS

Long-read sequencing (LRS) on the Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) platform was used to investigate the CCG BCLAF3 repeat expansion for 10 individuals presented in this study. More information on the ONT methods can be found in the Supplementary Methods. Detailed quality control information (longest pure CCG, base quality, mapping quality, and strand bias assessment) for all individuals genotyped for BCLAF3 repeat expansions with nanopore sequencing is in Additional File 2: Fig. S1 [25] and Additional File 1: Data S2.

PacBio LRS

PacBio High Fidelity (PacBio HiFi) sequencing was performed for Proband 1 on a Revio with chemistry v13.0.0.205983 according to standard protocols. A total of 5,788,249 reads were sequenced with a mean read length was 17,440 bp where 93.8% of bases obtained a quality of at least Q30.

Genomics England analyses

Short-read genomes in the Genomics England database were genotyped using ExpansionHunter (v5) and the same BCLAF3 catalog entry (Additional File 1: Data S1). Pileup plots were created with REViewer [26]. To assess the enrichment of the BCLAF3 expansions in unsolved cases, we performed a Fisher’s test at each repeat size (Additional File 1: Data S3), contrasting the number of unsolved cases with greater than or equal to each repeat size versus controls.

At the time of recruitment to the 100,000 Genomes Project, a subset of the cohort gave whole blood samples in PaxGene tubes for RNA-seq. Full information on the NGRL RNA-seq cohort and sequencing protocols is available: https://re-docs.genomicsengland.co.uk/rna_seq/. Briefly, samples were sequenced using Illumina 2 × 100 bp paired-end reads on the NovaSeq 6000 following library prep using the Stranded Total RNA Prep with Ribo-Zero Plus. Read mapping and QC was performed by Genomics England, using the Illumina DRAGEN RNA-Seq Pipeline v3.8.4, which also quantified gene expression in transcripts per million (TPM) across the cohort. OUTRIDER [21] was run via the DROP pipeline (v1.3.3) on RNA-Seq data from 5,412 individuals (2,760 reported male).

Results

Hypermethylation of a rare CCG repeat expansion in the 5’UTR of BCLAF3 in a male proband with genetically unresolved pediatric epilepsy

We identified a rare hypermethylated differentially methylated region (DMR) in the 5’ UTR of BCLAF3 (Xp22) in a male proband with genetically unresolved developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE, Proband 1, Fig. 1A, Supplementary Phenotype data) that was not present in any of our 612 controls [6]. Using targeted (adaptive sampling) Nanopore LRS from blood, we discovered a rare CCG repeat expansion of 493-1,238 CCG repeat units (GRCh38/hg38 chrX:19990923–19990973, REF = 17 repeats, Fig. 1B, C, Additional File 2: Fig. S2) that was inherited and expanded from the proband’s mother, who harbored a repeat expansion of 238–473 CCG repeat units on her longer allele. PacBio HiFi long-read sequencing refined the CCG repeat expansion as being 703–906 CCG repeat units (Additional File 2: Fig. S3). The region of hypermethylation in the proband extends beyond the annotated repeat into the surrounding CpG island and part of the CpG shore at chrX:19990186 − 19991423 (GRCh38), beginning 59 bp upstream (-) and ending 402 bp downstream (+) further than DNA methylation array probes could detect. Haplotype phasing combined with DNA methylation 5-methylcytosine (5mC) of blood-derived DNA from the mother showed near full hypermethylation against the haplotype with the expanded allele and at other sites on chromosome X, such as at PIGA (Xp), SYN1 (Xp) FMR1 (Xq) and AR (Xq), suggesting completely skewed X-inactivation (Additional File 2: Fig. S4). PCR and Sanger sequencing of a haplotype-resolved, intronic single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) in the proband’s brother confirmed he inherited the non-expanded allele from his mother (repeat size of 15 triplet repeats) (Additional File 2: Fig. S5, Table S1). Thus, we characterized hypermethylation of a rare CCG repeat expansion in the 5’UTR of BCLAF3 in a proband with genetically unresolved DEE as a candidate etiology that requires further genetic and molecular evidence to support a potential role in disease.

Long-read sequencing refines population characteristics of the BCLAF3 CCG repeat expansion

Because long-read sequencing provides the most accurate repeat-size determination, we assessed 293 PacBio HiFi long-read control samples (50% female) from the Human Pangenome Reference Consortium (HPRC) [8] and the Human Genome Structural Variation Consortium (HGSVC) aligned to the telomere-to-telomere (T2T) reference genome (T2T-CHM13v2.0). We confirmed a peak length of 15 CCG repeat units and a maximum observed length of 23 CCG repeat units (Fig. 1D, Additional File 1: Data S4) in this cohort. We also assessed 500 Nanopore long-read control samples (54% female) from the 1000 Genomes Project [10] (1kGP) aligned to the GRCh38 reference genome and found a peak length of 15 CCG repeat units and a maximum observed length of 22 CCG repeat units (Additional File 2: Fig. S6, Additional File 1: Data S5). Since larger cohort sizes of tens to hundreds of thousands of individuals are required to make accurate population estimates but are not yet available for long-read, we also turned to DNA methylation array data [27]. Based on DMR outlier analysis of 23,116 Illumina 450 K methylation arrays (8,027 males), no rare hypermethylation events were detected in the 5’UTR of BCLAF3, even though probes overlapped this region. If DNA hypermethylation is a proxy for large expansions in males (as shown), then it is unlikely that any of the male individuals in this study [27] harbor the repeat expansion. No conclusions can be made from the DNA methylation array data for females, since they may harbor skewed X-inactivation that is undetectable by haplotype-agnostic DNA methylation array data. Collectively, we have confirmed a population distribution of the BCLAF3 repeat in 793 long-read genomes (1,207 haplotypes) consistent with the repeat lengths represented in the T2T and GRCh38 reference genomes. Furthermore, available DNA methylation array data shows that BCLAF3 hypermethylation is rare (< 1 in 8,027 males or < 0.01%).

The BCLAF3 CCG repeat expansion silences BCLAF3 gene expression in patient-derived fibroblasts

We find that the BCLAF3 5’UTR is hypermethylated and that BCLAF3 expression is silenced in fibroblasts from Proband 1 as evidenced by DNA methylation array analysis (Additional File 2: Fig. S7), nominal RNA-seq expression values (Fig. 2A), OUTRIDER Dropout analysis [21] of RNA-seq data compared with 179 other fibroblast samples (Fig. 2B, C), differential expression analysis compared to the brother and father (Additional File 2: Fig. S8, Additional File 1: Data S6), and tandem RNA DNA fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH, Fig. 2D-F, Additional File 2: Fig. S9, Supplementary Methods, Table S2). Using probes that mark open (BRD4) and closed (HP1α) chromatin, we confirm that BCLAF3 is silenced through an epigenetic mechanism that is coupled with changes to the surrounding chromatin architecture (Fig. 2E, F). Interestingly, this leads to decreased expression of another nearby gene MAP7D2 (see Supplementary Methods for details). Altogether, our RNA investigations confirm that BCLAF3 is silenced in the proband harboring the BCLAF3 hypermethylation and repeat expansion, indicating that the CCG repeat expansion is associated with both chromatin and gene regulatory changes.

BCLAF3 protein localizes to the nucleus under normal conditions and is not expressed in patient-derived neurons

We next sought to determine BCLAF3 protein localization and confirm a lack of BCLAF3 protein expression in patient-derived cellular models. We endogenously tagged the BCLAF3 gene with HA (3X) and Scarlet3 using CRISPR technology in iPSCs (Additional File 2: Fig. S10, Table S3) reprogrammed from fibroblasts (Proband 1 and brother). We first confirmed that the BCLAF3 hypermethylation was maintained after reprogramming in the proband compared to the brother (Additional File 2: Fig. S11A). Since BCLAF3 is not expressed in iPSCs, we differentiated the iPSCs into 2D forebrain neurons. We found that BCLAF3 is robustly expressed in the brother and localizes to the nucleus (Additional File 2: Fig. S11B), supporting a potential nuclear function. We did not detect BCLAF3 protein expression in the proband’s cells, as expected. Thus, the hypermethylation of the BCLAF3 CCG repeat expansion silences both RNA and protein expression.

The BCLAF3 CCG repeat expansion is a previously unknown folate-sensitive fragile site

Using the family’s mitotic fibroblast samples, we performed DNA FISH with colored probes flanking the BCLAF3 repeat to investigate whether the BCLAF3 CCG repeat expansion could be a rare, previously unknown folate-sensitive fragile site. We coupled DNA FISH with DAPI nuclear staining after inducing thymidine stress to visualize the formation of micronuclei, which form at fragile genomic regions. The fluorescence from the centromeric and telomeric DNA FISH probes were monitored to determine whether micronuclei breakage occurred at the BCLAF3 site. In the proband’s fibroblasts, 12/55 (21.8%) micronuclei outside of the nucleus contained signal from the BCLAF3 telomeric probe with the centromeric probe signal remaining within the macronucleus, demonstrating that a DNA break had occurred between the BCLAF3 centromeric and telomeric probes in these cells (Fig. 3). These breaks resulted in the generation of acentric fragments containing the distal portion of the X chromosome. This evidence supports that the region contained between the probes is fragile. Cells from the mother (38 analyzed micronuclei), father (42 analyzed micronuclei), and brother (34 analyzed micronuclei) had no FISH probe signals (Additional File 2: Fig. S12). This data indicates that the BCLAF3 repeat expansion in the proband (493-1,238 CCG repeats) is indeed a previously uncharacterized fragile site, hereby referred to as FRAXG.

Epigenetic screening of 12,375 DNA methylation arrays from affected individuals identifies a male proband with BCLAF3 hypermethylation

We next sought to identify additional cases to support the role of the repeat expansion in disease. We first screened 12,375 DNA methylation arrays (58% male) from blood DNA in the EKD v5 for the rare differentially hypermethylated region in the 5’UTR of BCLAF3. We identified one additional male proband (Proband 2) harboring rare outlier hypermethylation (Fig. 4A) who had a primary diagnosis of William’s syndrome (7q11 deletion). He had been added to the database to search for a secondary diagnosis to explain symptoms that were more severe than expected for his primary diagnosis, including absence seizures, absent speech, and problems sleeping (see Supplementary Phenotype data). We performed DNA methylation arrays and targeted LRS (see Methods) on blood-derived DNA from Proband 2 and his mother (II-2) (Fig. 4B, Additional File 2: Fig. S13). We found a hypermethylated repeat expansion of ~ 280–706 CCG repeat units in the proband that had been inherited (without expansion) from the mother with ~ 214-1,009 CCG repeat units (Fig. 4B, Additional File 1: Data S7). Haplotype phasing of the LRS data with methylation status also indicates that the expanded BCLAF3 haplotype is nearly fully hypermethylated compared to the non-expanded haplotype (Fig. 4B). Skewed X-inactivation was additionally seen in mother (II-2) across chrX at MAOA (Xp) and PCDH19 (Xq, Additional File 2: Fig. S14).

Of interest, the mother’s sister (aunt of the proband, II-3) has two sons (cousins of the proband, cousin III-2 and cousin III-3) with Fragile X syndrome. The younger cousin III-3 had a phenotype that was more severe than expected (see Supplementary Phenotype data), including speech delay (first words at 4 years) and difficulty with feeding and drinking. Therefore, we hypothesized that cousin (III-3) and the aunt also carried the BCLAF3 repeat expansion and that it could be contributing to more severe phenotypes in this family. We profiled the aunt and two cousins with DNA methylation arrays and whole genome LRS. We confirmed hypermethylated FMR1 repeat expansions in all three family members using genome LRS [aunt: ~180–558 CGG repeat units, cousin (III-2): ~208–858 CGG repeat units, and cousin (III-3): ~361-1,105 CGG repeat units (Additional File 2: Fig. S15, S16)]. Neither the proband with William’s syndrome nor his mother have FMR1 expansions (Additional File 2: Fig. S17). While both the aunt (~ 172-1,237 CCG repeat units) and the more severely affected cousin III-3 (~ 541–816 CCG repeat units with 1 unmethylated read with only 71 CCG repeats) had BCLAF3 repeat expansions (Fig. 4C, Additional File 2: Fig. S18), cousin (III-2), whose phenotype is consistent with Fragile X, did not. While DNA methylation analysis identified hypermethylation of BCLAF3 (Fig. 4C) in cousin (III-3), there is modest evidence (1 read spanning support out of 41 total reads at pileup) of a lower-level mosaic contraction down to 71 repeats that ablates the DNA hypermethylation. The aunt’s data shows skewed X-inactivation at the BCLAF3 locus (Fig. 4C), the FMR1 locus (Additional File 2: Fig. S15), and other loci on chrX (Additional File 2: Fig. S14). Thus, expansion and hypermethylation of the BCLAF3 CCG repeat is segregating with more severe disease in males in this family (Fig. 4D).

Meiotic crossover between BCLAF3 and FMR1 in a family with multiple repeat expansions on chrX

Given that each male cousin inherits one X chromosome from their mother (aunt of the proband), it is not possible for both cousins to harbor the FMR1 repeat expansion but not both harbor the BCLAF3 repeat expansion unless there was a recombination event that took place between the aunt’s two X chromosomes during meiosis. We analyzed SNPs from the Nanopore genome sequencing along the X chromosome to phase the haplotypes that were inherited from the aunt to the two cousins. Using this strategy, we identified a 28.5 kb region (chrX:80831290–80859740 GRCh38) on Xp21.1 in which the recombination event likely occurred (Fig. 4E, Supplementary Methods). Thus, we provide an explanation for the genetic data shown in Fig. 4D, where there is genetic discordance of inherited variants on chrX in two brothers.

Short tandem repeat genotyping of 15,963 srGS data identifies individuals with unresolved neurological disorders and expanded BCLAF3 CCG repeats

After confirming that we could detect validated BCLAF3 repeat expansions in srGS data as outliers (Additional File 2: Fig. S19-S21, Additional File 1: Data S7, S8, Supplementary Methods), we next genotyped the BCLAF3 repeat in srGS data from the UK NGRL [12], which consists of blood-derived data from both the NHS GMS and the 100kGP. We identified 15,963 affected individuals with genetically unresolved neurological disorders (49% male). As controls, we used 31,111 unaffected individuals (> 40 years old for 100kGP) or affected individuals with non-neurological conditions as negative controls (57% male) and 3,673 individuals with solved neurological disorders (52% male). We used Expansion Hunter [29] (v5) to estimate BCLAF3 repeat sizes across all the genotyped individuals (Fig. 5, Additional File 1: Data S3). Overall, we identified 6 genetically unresolved neurological disorder cases (3 males, 3 females) with predicted repeat expansions (≥ 57 CCG repeats, a cutoff based on our previous positive control data, Additional File 1: Data S3, S7). Six controls (5 female) also had predicted repeat expansions ≥ 57 CCG repeats. Given that we have identified unaffected carrier mothers of affected sons, repeat expansions in non-neurologically affected females is expected. One male control with a non-neurological phenotype (eye) had an estimated 113 CCG repeats. Thus, we were interested to characterize the repeat expansion further in the identified cases with neurological phenotypes and the male control with a non-neurological phenotype.

Somatic mosaic CCA interruption leads to incomplete gene silencing of BCLAF3 in a male with an eye phenotype

An individual with one of the longest predicted genotypes (113 predicted CCG repeats) was a male proband (Proband 3) with macular dystrophy used as a negative control (Additional File 2: Fig. S22). Genotyping of the mother indicates that the expansion was inherited from her (101 predicted CCG repeats). Nanopore LRS for this sample, available through the 100kGP ONT rare disease pilot, indicated that the male had an expansion of ~ 360-1,089 repeats. However, upon further inspection of the motif composition in the inserted sequences, we noticed that some reads (5/13 (38% of fully repeat-spanning reads) had interrupting ‘CCA’ motifs instead of pure ‘CCG’ motifs (Fig. 6A). The ‘CCA’ motif interruption is likely a somatic mosaic mutation and not due to the presence of two X chromosomes.

We hypothesized that this would lead to partial (rather than complete) BCLAF3 silencing as the ‘CCA’ interruption would not be expected to be hypermethylated given the absence of CG dinucleotides, and therefore, could allow some active BCLAF3 expression. Blood RNA-seq data for the same individual confirmed that BCLAF3 is expressed, as visualized by the presence of reads mapping to BCLAF3 transcript (Fig. 6B), albeit at decreased levels. Using OUTRIDER [21], BCLAF3 is detected as a significant outlier for decreased expression in this sample (padj=4.258 × 10− 32, zScore=-14.61, log2FC=-2) compared to the cohort (n = 5,412). Across all 5,412 individuals, there was a mean BCLAF3 expression of 15.89 TPM (standard deviation of 3.52 TPM). The case had a mean TPM of 4.01, which is > 3 standard deviations away from the mean, but still expressed to a modest degree (Fig. 6C). Incomplete loss of BCLAF3 in this male individual could provide an explanation for why this individual does not display a neurological phenotype like the rest of the identified male carriers of the BCLAF3 repeat expansion. This finding also aligns with emerging data from other repeat expansions where repeat interruptions and mosaicism affect epigenetic state and phenotype severity [30, 31]. Importantly, in our case, reads with ‘CCA’ motifs are not evident in the srGS data, and LRS is required to fully capture this motif.

BCLAF3 srGS outliers in the NGRL have genetically unresolved neurological disorders

The six other affected individuals (3 male, 3 female) with BCLAF3 repeat expansion predictions ≥ 57 CCG repeats have genetically unresolved neurological disorders. In 2/6 of these cases (2 male), the predictions appeared to be real from manual inspection of read data, and the BCLAF3 repeat expansions are thought to potentially contribute to the individual’s phenotypes. DNA was not available for follow-up LRS validation in any of these cases. The longest expansion (102 predicted CCG repeats) was for a male individual (Proband 4) with epilepsy, inherited from his mother (longer allele with 84 predicted CCG repeats) (Additional File 2: Fig. S23, Supplementary Phenotype data). Another male individual (Proband 5) with intellectual disability had a repeat expansion of 99 predicted CCG repeats, inherited from his mother (longer allele with 103 predicted CCG repeats) (Additional File 2: Fig. S24). Additional detailed clinical information is not available for this case.

In 2/6 of cases, the contribution of the BCLAF3 repeat expansion to their phenotypes is uncertain. We identified a female individual (Proband 6) with an inherited epilepsy syndrome and a BCLAF3 repeat expansion of 63 predicted CCG repeats (Additional File 2: Fig. S25). Her mother did not have a predicted repeat expansion (father unavailable), but the paternal grandmother who also has epilepsy was genotyped at 117 predicted CCG repeats, corroborating a likely paternal transmission. Thus, the BCLAF3 repeat expansion is segregating with seizure phenotypes in at least two family members. Also, while it seems from preliminary srGS assessment that the repeat expansion may have contracted over generations, with 117 predicted CCG repeats in paternal grandmother and only 63 predicted CCG repeats in female proband, we could not make any final conclusions without data from the father and LRS to determine methylation status and a more accurate estimation of true repeat length. Another female (Proband 7) with 71 predicted CCG repeats (Additional File 2: Fig. S26) has renal disease and moderate intellectual disability with a family history of intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder, and febrile seizures. No family members were available in the NGRL, and DNA was unavailable for further validation. Therefore, the contribution of a BCLAF3 repeat expansion to the phenotypes of these female cases is uncertain.

We also investigated the other 2/6 cases (1 male, 1 female). Follow-up studies using LRS in the male with a low-confidence EH prediction of 59 CCG repeats (Additional File 2: Fig. S27) revealed that this was a false-positive prediction (Proband 8). The remaining individual is a female with epilepsy and a BCLAF3 repeat expansion of 83 predicted CCG repeats on the longer allele (Proband 9, Additional File 2: Fig. S28A). Follow-up nanopore LRS of this female proband genotyped her repeat expansion as ~ 321–587 bp or ~ 107–196 CCG repeats with a TRGT [32] call of 117 CCG repeats (Additional File 2: Fig. S28B). Methylation analysis of LRS data indicated that she did not harbor hypermethylation of the repeat expansion or have any evidence of skewed X-inactivation (Additional File 2: Fig. S28C). This suggests Proband 9 is potentially a BCLAF3 premutation carrier, and that the threshold for hypermethylation for BCLAF3 is somewhere beyond 117 CCG repeats. The multiomic data for all individuals characterized with long-read sequencing throughout this manuscript is in Additional File 1: Data S9 and summarized in Table 1.

Phenotypic features of affected individuals with BCLAF3 CCG expansions identified to date

In total, we have identified five affected males (four unrelated males and one male relative) where we believe that the BCLAF3 CCG expansions may be contributing to the individuals’ phenotypes (Table 1). Three of these cases (Proband 1, Proband 2, and Cousin III-3) were validated with long-read sequencing and DNA methylation analysis. The other two cases (Proband 4 and Proband 5) have predicted repeat expansions ≥ 57 CCG repeats by srGS, which require future validation. All 5 individuals have intellectual disability, at least 3/5 have epilepsy, and at least 2/5 have autism spectrum disorder. Phenotypic information was limited for many of the cases identified from large databases leading to an uneven depth and quality of clinical data across ascertainment sources. Pedigrees of all cases are in Additional File 2: Fig. S29. More detailed phenotype information can be found in the Supplementary Phenotype data.

Assessing the rarity of BCLAF3 repeat expansion predictions from srGS in the UK Biobank

To further assess the population characteristics of the BCLAF3 CCG repeat predictions in short-read genome sequencing data, we leveraged 410,076 srGS data from the UK Biobank. Genotyping with ExpansionHunter (v5) showed that there was an average CCG repeat length of 15.83 repeats across the UK biobank and a median CCG repeat length of 15 repeats, consistent with our original assessment of the BCLAF3 repeat in 488 srGS data (Additional File 2: Fig. S19A) and the TRatlas [33]. The rarity of this repeat expansion is confirmed by the relatively low number of potential carriers in the UK biobank (Additional File 1: Data S10). We identified 52 females with predicted repeat expansions of ≥ 57 BCLAF3 CCG repeats out of 221,004 females assessed (1 in 4,250 females, allele frequency = 0.012%), and 25 males with predicted repeat expansions of ≥ 57 BCLAF3 CCG repeats out of a total number of 189,072 males assessed (1 in 7,563 males, allele frequency = 0.013%). Phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) showed no significant associations with 3,749 quantitative and 24,715 binary human traits. Manual inspection of sequencing reads support predictions in most of these individuals with only three males that look like false positives but are still included for consistency. Importantly, it is not possible to estimate what proportion of individuals with predicted repeats ≥ 57 BCLAF3 CCG are expanded and pure enough to pass a threshold of hypermethylation given that the expanded repeat is well beyond the limitations of srGS read lengths.

For a comparison of allele frequencies, FMR1 CGG expansions ≥ 57 predicted CGG repeats are considerably more common in the same dataset (410,028 srGS samples genotyped) (Additional File 1: Data S11) and likely reflect the rate of the premutation in the population (1:130–250 females and 1:800 males [34, 35] ). We identified 1,027 females with repeat expansion predictions of ≥ 57 FMR1 CGG repeats out of 221,004 females assessed (1 in 215 females, allele frequency = 0.23%), and 492 males with repeat expansion prediction of ≥ 57 FMR1 CGG repeats out of a total number of 189,024 males assessed (1 in 384 males, allele frequency = 0.26%). Thus, predicted FMR1 CGG expansions of ≥ 57 repeats have an allele frequency that is ~ 19.2X as frequent in females and ~ 20X as frequent in males than BCLAF3 CCG expansions.

Discussion

Here, we highlight how genome-wide, multiomic discovery approaches (Additional File 1: Data S12) are used to identify hypermethylation of a rare and novel X-linked CCG repeat expansion in BCLAF3 that may contribute to neurodevelopmental phenotypes in in 5 males (n = 3 validated with long-read sequencing and DNA methylation analysis and n = 2 predicted with srGS). While phenotypic features in these individuals include intellectual disability (5/5), epilepsy (3/5), and autism (2/5), the identification of additional cases is required to both refine and expand the phenotypic spectrum. Based on LRS across all the individuals characterized in this study, we believe that the threshold for hypermethylation is somewhere between 117 and 172 CCG repeats, but a greater number of individuals with intermediate-sized expansions are required to define a more precise cutoff. Importantly, we find that srGS underestimates the true expansion sizes of BCLAF3 repeat expansions, highlighting the importance of LRS for assessing repeat expansion length, motif composition, and DNA methylation status of CCG repeats.

LRS data from three mothers was able to provide a haplotype-resolved view of DNA methylation on chrX. We determined that they all have skewed X-inactivation at the BCLAF3 locus with their expanded allele nearly fully hypermethylated and their non-expanded allele nearly fully hypomethylated compared to other female individuals with random hyper and hypomethylation across both alleles. Skewed inactivation is expected to be protective by allowing for them to maintain BCLAF3 expression from their unexpanded allele despite harboring a fully methylated, expanded repeat on the other allele. We suspect that a lack of X-inactivation skewing in this context would have led to partial hypermethylation of her non-expanded allele in addition to the full hypermethylation of the expanded allele, leading to partial loss of expression of BCLAF3 levels and potentially disease phenotypes. Importantly, as more female BCLAF3 expansion carriers are identified and characterized with long-read sequencing, allele-specific methylation levels may serve as an approach to quantify disease penetrance as has been suggested in other studies [36]. Furthermore, skewed X-inactivation was also observed in these mothers at multiple additional loci on the X chromosome beyond the BCLAF3 locus.

It is important to consider both the length of the repeat expansion and the exact motif composition in relation to disease [37, 38]. Since hypermethylation is a part of the mechanism that results in gene silencing, disruptions to the CpG composition of the expansion can ablate downstream effects, as we observed in an affected control with macular dystrophy and a somatic mosaic ‘CCA’ interruption. This case also highlights how another mutation pattern can exist in the population that has a protective effect on potentially deleterious gene expression consequences that can only be fully characterized with long-read sequencing.

The TRatlas database profiled the BCLAF3 CCG repeat (TR167036) and suggests that no individuals (n = 338,963 individuals) harbor repeats ≥ 57 triplets [33]. Intriguingly, there are likely some overlapping samples between our UK Biobank analysis and the TRatlas database, which yielded different results even though both use ExpansionHunter predictions. This highlights the importance of using multiple reference databases/data when working with repeat expansion predictions, as it may alter downstream interpretations. While our NGRL cohort of cases with genetically unresolved neurological disorders had a comparable allele frequency for females with BCLAF3 repeat predictions ≥ 57 (0.014% compared to 0.012% in UK Biobank), our disease cohort allele frequency for males with BCLAF3 repeat predictions ≥ 57 was nearly twice as frequent than males in the UK Biobank (0.022% compared to 0.013% in UK Biobank). If the UK Biobank results reflect real, hypermethylated CCG repeat expansions, we also cannot rule out the possibility of decreased or variable penetrance.

BCLAF3 encodes the highly conserved BCLAF1 and THRAP3 family member 3 protein, which is a relatively uncharacterized protein with very little established literature. Bulk tissue transcriptomics in the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) portal indicates that BCLAF3 transcripts are ubiquitously expressed across all human tissues, including the brain. Based on publicly available single-cell brain atlas datasets [39, 40], BCLAF3 is broadly expressed across early brain developmental cell types, including progenitors and early neurons, as well as mature neuronal cell types. This supports that BCLAF3 may be important in neurodevelopment and brain function. Future work will be essential to understand and tease apart the downstream regulatory effects of the repeat expansion itself on the chromatin architecture and the regulatory alterations induced by loss of BCLAF3 expression through its putative function of regulating transcription or splicing. An inherent limitation of our molecular characterization is that it does not establish what the downstream cellular or neuronal functional consequences might be in terms of a direct pathogenic mechanism underlying the repeat expansion.

Conclusions

Establishing the disease causality of rare variants often requires a multi-pronged approach to both identify additional unrelated individuals with similar phenotypes and perform downstream functional experiments to establish molecular phenotypes that can be used to understand the biology of the disease. A remaining and important question is how the identified hypermethylation of the BCLAF3 CCG repeat expansion could mechanistically cause disease. It will be important to determine if phenotypes could be caused by silencing of BCLAF3 alone or whether a pathogenic mechanism is dependent on the CCG repeat expansion, representing a more nuanced pathophysiological mechanism where the repeat expansion itself results in multiple deleterious effects. If phenotypes are present in experimental models, these models can also be used to test whether restoration of BCLAF3 expression or excision of the repeat expansion can rescue associated phenotypes. If so, these models can be used to test potential therapeutic modalities that are already being developed for other repeat expansion disorders. It may also be important to study the BCLAF3 repeat expansion directly in the brain, if possible, since blood-derived and fibroblast-derived DNA methylation and expression data may not fully reflect brain tissue patterns as has been shown for FMR1 and other repeat expansions via long-read sequencing data [41]. In summary, our work emphasizes the importance of large-scale collaborative efforts to maximize sample sizes for rare diseases, especially those with initial n-of-1 molecular findings. Using a variety of genomic, epigenomic, and transcriptomic approaches, we were able to both characterize the downstream regulatory consequences of a CCG repeat expansion in the 5’UTR of BCLAF3 and screen additional multiomic cohorts to identify affected cases, thereby establishing this repeat expansion as a potential novel disease etiology.
