Exerkine dysregulation links visceral adiposity to skeletal muscle impairment in end-stage heart failure with reduced ejection fraction: proteomic evidence for a cardio–adipose–muscle axis
Authors: Marta Załęska-Kocięcka, Maciej Mazuruk, Karolina Szcześniak, Piotr Łaba, Marta Kacperska, Łukasz Nogajski, Maksymilian Nowakowski, Maciej Mączewski, Hanna Czerwińska, Miłosz Rosa, Piotr Olbryś, Aleksandra Mączyńska, Jarosław Kuriata, Piotr Kołsut, Zuzanna Wojdyńska, Ilona Michałowska, Aleksandra Paterek, Przemysław Błyszczuk, Przemysław Leszek, Michał Mączewski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) is associated with profound alterations in body composition, skeletal muscle dysfunction, and impaired exercise capacity. Exerkines representing exercise-responsive signaling molecules released by skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, and other organs may mediate systemic metabolic communication between tissues. However, their role in advanced HFrEF and their relationship with adiposity and skeletal muscle characteristics remain poorly understood.MethodsWe studied 73 patients with end-stage HFrEF and 16 healthy controls. Body composition was assessed using computed tomography, including visceral (VAT), subcutaneous (SAT), and epicardial adipose tissue (EAT), as well as skeletal muscle quantity (psoas muscle index, PMI) and quality (psoas muscle density, PMD). Functional performance was evaluated using handgrip strength (HGT) and the 6-min walk test (6MWT). Circulating exerkines were quantified using the Olink technology. Associations between proteins and clinical variables were assessed using age- and creatinine-adjusted linear models with false discovery rate correction.ResultsAmong patients with HFrEF, 36% were obese and 38% exhibited central obesity independent of BMI. Muscle strength and muscle quality were strongly associated with functional capacity. VAT correlated with muscle mass but not with muscle quality or performance. Compared with controls, HFrEF patients demonstrated elevated inflammatory and metabolic stress-related exerkines including CXCL8, CCL2, IL-6, TNF, IL-15, GDF15, FGF21, ANGPTL4, CTSB, DCN, and resistin. In contrast, proteins associated with muscle integrity and regenerative signaling (myostatin, BDNF, IL-7, SPARC) were significantly reduced. In HFrEF patients leptin strongly correlated with adiposity measures. Metabolic stress mediators (GDF15, IL-15, FGF21, CTSB) were inversely associated with muscle quality and functional performance, whereas myostatin positively correlated with muscle quality, strength, and exercise capacity. BDNF was inversely associated with frailty.ConclusionsAdvanced HFrEF is characterized by a dysregulated exerkine network linking adiposity, skeletal muscle quality, and functional performance. Four biologically coherent axes were identified: a leptin-driven adiposity axis, a metabolic stress–muscle quality axis, a myostatin-related muscle function axis, and a neurotrophic frailty axis. These findings support the presence of a systemic cardio–adipose–muscle signaling network in end-stage HFrEF and identify candidate molecular mediators of sarcopenia and functional decline.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03190-4.Keywords: Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, Adipose tissue, Obesity, Skeletal muscles, Sarcopenia, Exerkines, Inflammation

Background

Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) is increasingly recognized as a systemic disorder characterized by multi-organ dysfunction extending beyond the failing myocardium [1]. In addition to impaired cardiac performance, patients exhibit profound alterations in skeletal muscle structure and function, endothelial dysfunction, metabolic derangements, and abnormalities in adipose tissue distribution and signaling. These extra-cardiac manifestations contribute substantially to exercise intolerance, reduced quality of life, and adverse prognosis, underscoring the need to better understand the integrative pathophysiology of HFrEF [2]. Although body composition assessment is strongly recommended in HFrEF due to its clinical and prognostic relevance, it remains insufficiently standardized in current guidelines [1]. This gap highlights the importance of further research, making the present study particularly relevant in advancing the understanding of systemic alterations in HFrEF.

Exercise training is a cornerstone of HFrEF management and has been consistently associated with improvements in functional capacity and clinical outcomes [3]. In recent years, exerkines, circulating factors released in response to physical activity, have emerged as potential mediators of these beneficial effects [4, 5]. Exerkines include cytokines, growth factors, enzymes, and peptides that modulate inflammation, mitochondrial function, substrate metabolism, and tissue remodeling. Several exerkines, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-15 (IL-15), myostatin, fibroblast growth factor-21 (FGF21), and decorin, influence skeletal muscle metabolism, regeneration, and protein turnover, while others regulate lipid mobilization and adipose tissue function [4, 6]. Through these mechanisms, exerkines contribute to the systemic benefits of physical activity and play an important role in maintaining metabolic and anabolic–catabolic balance. In HFrEF and other populations, exerkines are thought to facilitate inter-organ crosstalk, modulate inflammation, enhance metabolic efficiency, and promote favorable remodeling in skeletal muscle and the cardiovascular system [4, 5].

However, patients with end-stage HFrEF are typically characterized by a markedly sedentary lifestyle, driven by severe symptoms, frailty, and exercise intolerance. This avoidance of physical activity likely results in a reduction or absence of exercise-induced exerkine signaling, accompanied by a shift toward a chronic pro-inflammatory and catabolic molecular milieu. Such a dysfunctional exerkine profile may contribute to the development and progression of skeletal muscle myopathy, alterations in adipose tissue depots, and worsening systemic manifestations of heart failure [4, 5].

Alterations in exerkine signaling have increasingly been implicated in cardiometabolic diseases, including heart failure. For example, circulating FGF21 concentrations are elevated in patients with heart failure and may reflect metabolic stress and mitochondrial dysfunction [7], while IL-6 regulates lipid mobilization and adipose tissue metabolism, linking skeletal muscle activity with systemic metabolic homeostasis [5]. These pathways are particularly relevant in heart failure, where metabolic dysregulation, systemic inflammation, and impaired muscle function coexist and may contribute to progressive exercise intolerance and frailty.

Adipose tissue depots also play an important role in cardiometabolic signalling. Epicardial adipose tissue (EAT), in particular, is a metabolically active endocrine organ capable of modulating myocardial metabolism and local inflammatory pathways through paracrine and vasocrine signaling [8, 9]. Imaging-based assessment of adipose tissue volume and density can therefore provide insights into systemic metabolic disturbances and cardiovascular risk [10, 11]). Other adipose depots may also contribute to this cardiometabolic signalling network. Visceral adipose tissue (VAT) is a major source of pro-inflammatory cytokines and adipokines that influence systemic metabolism and cardiovascular risk, whereas subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) is thought to exert more metabolically protective effects through lipid storage capacity and secretion of anti-inflammatory mediators [12]. At the same time, skeletal muscle—constituting approximately 40% of body mass—represents a major source of exerkines, and muscle wasting observed in chronic heart failure may substantially disrupt this endocrine network, contributing to metabolic deterioration and disease progression.

These observations suggest that exerkine dysregulation may represent a key mechanistic link between physical inactivity and multi-organ impairment in advanced HFrEF. Improved understanding of this relationship could clarify disease progression and reveal novel therapeutic targets. Circulating exerkines may also serve as biomarkers of skeletal muscle dysfunction, adipose tissue abnormalities, and overall functional status.

We hypothesized that circulating exerkine profiles are altered in advanced HFrEF compared with healthy individuals, that these alterations are associated with distinct body composition and muscle function phenotypes, and that coordinated exerkine patterns reflect interconnected cardio–adipose–muscle signaling axes. Unlike prior studies focusing on single exerkines [7, 13], we adopt a comprehensive panel approach to examine their interrelationships.

Accordingly, this study aims to characterize the exerkine profile in patients with end-stage HFrEF and assess its associations with skeletal muscle mass and function, physical fitness, and adipose tissue distribution. By doing so, we seek to identify candidate biomarkers and mechanistic pathways to inform future therapeutic strategies targeting the systemic nature of HFrEF.

Methods

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The research protocol was approved by the local ethics committee (No. IK.NPIA.0021.28.2026/23), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion. All data generated or analyzed during this study are included in this published article and its supplementary information files.

Study population

Study arm 1 (HFrEF group) comprised 73 patients with end-stage heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) undergoing left ventricular assist device (LVAD) implantation (all males). Study arm 2 (healthy control group) consisted of 16 apparently healthy subjects (all males). Among the HFrEF patients, 62 underwent chest computed tomography (CT) imaging within 7 days prior to LVAD implantation. Peripheral venous blood samples were collected from HFrEF patients on the day preceding LVAD surgery and from control participants during routine health examinations.

Functional assessments

Six Minute Walk Test (6MWT) was performed in accordance with the current American Thoracic Society guidelines [14]. The test was conducted in an enclosed, flat corridor measuring 30 m in length, marked at 3-m intervals. Patients were instructed to walk as far as possible within six minutes at their own pace, with permission to slow down, stop, or rest if necessary, without interrupting the timing of the test. The outcome measure was the total distance covered in meters. When a patient was unable to perform the test, a value of 0 m was recorded. Hand Grip Test: handgrip strength (HGT) was assessed using a Jamar hand dynamometer. The measurement was performed in a seated position, with the arm adducted to the trunk and the elbow flexed at 90°. One measurement was obtained for the right and left hand. In patients hospitalized in the intensive care unit, the assessment was performed in a seated position in bed (head of bed elevated 60–90°), while maintaining 90° elbow flexion. For statistical analysis, the average of the right and left HGT values was used as a continuous variable. Frailty Index: Frailty was assessed using the Tilburg Frailty Index (TFI) questionnaire. The instrument evaluates three domains of functioning: physical, psychological, and social. The total score ranges from 0 to 15 points, with higher scores indicating a greater level of frailty. In this study, frailty was defined as a TFI score ≥ 5.

Computed tomography and ultrasound imaging and analysis

CT scans were acquired using a dual-source scanner (NAEOTOM Alpha; SOMATOM Force, Siemens Healthineers) following intravenous administration of an iodine-based contrast agent. Detailed acquisition parameters are provided in the Supplementary Material. In-plane pixel size ranged from 0.27 × 0.27 mm to 0.96 × 0.96 mm, and slice thickness was 0.4 or 0.6 mm. Post-processing and image analysis were performed using 3D Slicer software (version 5.2.2; Boston, MA, USA). Epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) was defined as adipose tissue located between the outer myocardial wall and the visceral layer of the pericardium (Fig. 1A), with attenuation values ranging from − 180 to − 30 Hounsfield units (HU), as previously described [15, 16]. EAT density was quantified as mean attenuation (in HU), with higher values indicating greater tissue density. Visceral adipose tissue (VAT) and subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) volumes were quantified using the TotalSegmentator extension within the 3D Slicer platform. Adipose tissue was identified using a predefined attenuation range of − 190 to − 30 HU [17] (Fig. 1B). Anatomical boundaries extended from the dome of the diaphragm to the inferior border of the L5 vertebra [18]. Both VAT and SAT were indexed, by dividing them by height squared in meters (iVAT and iSAT, respectively). Psoas muscle assessment was performed at the level of the L3 vertebral body. The craniocaudal extent of segmentation was standardized between the midpoints of the adjacent intervertebral discs above and below L3 (Fig. 1D). Muscle segmentation was conducted using the TotalSegmentator extension within the 3D Slicer platform. The Psoas Muscle Index (PMI) was calculated as the sum of left and right psoas cross-sectional areas divided by height squared in meters. Psoas muscle density (PMD) was measured as the mean density (in HU) of the segmented psoas muscles. Diaphragmatic crura thickness was measured using the origin of the celiac trunk as a standardized landmark, in both horizontal and vertical planes, and cross-sectional area (CSA) was calculated (Fig. 1D-E). A Sarcopenia Index was derived, according to recommendations by the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP) [19] to summarize skeletal muscle status, based on three muscle-related parameters: PMI, PMD, and handgrip strength (HGT). Ultrasound assessment of quadriceps muscle thickness (QMT) was performed to evaluate peripheral skeletal muscle mass. Measurements were obtained with a high-frequency linear transducer (7–12 MHz). Participants were examined in the supine position with the upper body elevated at 45°, after at least 10 min of rest. The measurement site was standardized at the midpoint between the anterior superior iliac spine and the superior border of the patella. Total quadriceps muscle thickness was defined as the sum of rectus femoris (RF) and vastus intermedius (VI) thickness. RF thickness was measured as the distance between its superficial and deep aponeuroses, whereas VI thickness was measured between the deep surface of the rectus femoris and the anterior surface of the femur (Fig. 1C). To avoid tissue compression, a generous amount of ultrasound gel was applied and minimal probe pressure was maintained. Two independent investigators performed five consecutive measurements of each assessed muscle: right and left rectus femoris (RF) and right and left vastus intermedius (VI). For statistical analysis, the mean value of the summed right and left quadriceps muscle thickness was used. Inter- and intra-observer reproducibility were assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient. Intra-observer variability was calculated based on repeated measurements performed by the same investigator, whereas inter-observer variability was determined by comparing measurements obtained independently by the two investigators.

Blood sample collection and proteomic profiling

Blood samples were collected into EDTA tubes and centrifuged at 1,500 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Plasma aliquots were stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

High-throughput plasma proteomic profiling was performed using the Olink technology (Olink Proteomics, Uppsala, Sweden). Plasma samples were thawed on ice and randomized prior assay. Protein abundance was reported as Normalized Protein eXpression (NPX) values on a log2 scale. Samples and assays passing Olink’s internal quality control criteria (including inter-plate and positive/negative controls) were retained for further analysis. Comparative analyses between HFrEF patients and controls were performed using appropriate statistical tests, with adjustment for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.

Exerkine selection Exerkines were selected a priori based on human studies demonstrating responsiveness to exercise and/or established roles in metabolic regulation, inflammation, and inter-organ signaling. Proteins were included if they met at least two of the following criteria: (1) modulation with acute or chronic exercise in humans; (2) involvement in skeletal muscle biology (growth, regeneration, or catabolism); (3) roles in adipose signaling, energy metabolism, or insulin sensitivity; or (4) relevance to cardiovascular disease, inflammation, or metabolic stress pathways pertinent to HFrEF.

The final panel comprised a functionally diverse set of circulating factors, including inflammatory mediators (e.g., IL-6, TNF), metabolic and stress-response proteins (e.g., GDF15, FGF21), muscle-related factors (e.g., myostatin, BDNF), and extracellular matrix/remodeling proteins (e.g., decorin, cathepsin B).

As many of these proteins are not exclusively muscle-derived, the term “exerkines” is used to denote exercise-responsive circulating factors rather than direct measures of exercise-induced secretion. This targeted, hypothesis-driven approach enabled biologically interpretable integration with proteomic, functional, and body composition data while limiting multiple testing. However, it may introduce selection bias; findings should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating and require external validation. Importantly, the study assesses baseline circulating levels rather than acute exercise responses, reflecting potential chronic alterations in inter-organ signaling in HFrEF.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on distribution. Normality was assessed visually using histograms and Q–Q plots and formally tested with the Shapiro–Wilk test.

For comparisons between two groups with normally distributed data, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was applied. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate.

Correlations between variables were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To prevent power loss from missing clinical data, missing values were handled using Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE). Associations between clinical parameters were assessed using partial Spearman's rank correlations to strictly control for the confounding effect of age. To correct for multiple hypothesis testing, the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied, with statistical significance set at a False Discovery Rate (FDR) < 0.05. Correlation networks were constructed based on pairwise associations between exerkines. Edges were defined using FDR < 0.05. Network metrics, including number of edges, network density, and average degree, were calculated to quantify network complexity. Detailed information regarding sample sizes, statistical tests used, and exact p values is provided in the figure legends.

Differential protein abundance between healthy controls and HFrEF patients was evaluated for selected proteins using age-adjusted linear regression models. The regression coefficient for the HFrEF group was extracted as the age-adjusted log2 fold change, and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated as estimate ± 1.96 × standard error. Multiple testing correction was performed across all tested proteins using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR method.

Associations between circulating protein levels and NT-proBNP in HFrEF patients were assessed using correlation analysis. For each protein, the correlation coefficient (Pearson’s r) and corresponding 95% CI were obtained. Multiple testing correction across all proteins was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR method.

Associations between circulating protein levels and clinical variables in HFrEF patients were evaluated using age- and plasma creatinine concentration-adjusted linear regression models. Protein expression levels were derived from Olink proteomic assays and expressed as NPX. The following continuous clinical variables were included in the analysis: body mass index (BMI), 6MWT, frailty index, HGT, sarcopenia index, PMI, PMD, CSA, QMT, EAT volume and EAT density, iVAT volume and VAT density, and iSAT volume and SAT density. Only variables available in the merged analytical dataset were included in the final analysis. For each protein—clinical variable pair, a linear model was fitted with the standardized clinical variable as the dependent variable and standardized protein expression as the independent variable, adjusting for standardized age and plasma creatinine concentration. All variables were standardized (z-scored) prior to modeling. The resulting regression coefficient for the protein term represents the fully standardized beta coefficient. Analyses were performed separately for each protein measured in the dataset. Observations with missing or non-finite values were excluded prior to model fitting. Multiple testing correction across all protein—clinical variable associations was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR procedure.

Variance partitioning analysis was performed using the variancePartition R package to assess the contribution of comorbidities and medications to protein expression variability (NPX). A multivariable linear model including all variables was fitted for each protein, and the fraction of variance explained by each factor was estimated using fitExtractVarPartModel with pairwise handling of missing data. All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio (Posit, Boston, MA, USA) and GraphPad Prism (Dotmatics, Boston, MA, USA).

Adiposity phenotypes, skeletal muscle, and sarcopenia

Body composition analysis revealed substantial heterogeneity in adiposity phenotypes (Fig. 2A). Based on BMI, 70% of patients with HFrEF were overweight (34%) or obese (36%), whereas only two patients had a BMI < 20 kg/m2. BMI and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) showed a moderate correlation (r = 0.68), with few individuals demonstrating discordant patterns suggestive of predominantly central or peripheral fat distribution. Indexed visceral adipose tissue (iVAT) correlated moderately with BMI (r = 0.54, p < 0.001) and WHR (r = 0.48, p < 0.001); however, these anthropometric measures explained only 33% and 24% of VAT variability, respectively, indicating their limited ability to capture visceral adiposity in advanced HFrEF. The VAT/SAT ratio varied considerably across patients, with 38% demonstrating visceral fat predominance (VAT/SAT ratio > 1) and the remainder subcutaneous fat predominance (VAT/SAT ratio < 1; Fig. 2B).

Measures of skeletal muscle also showed marked interindividual variability. Muscle strength (handgrip test, HGT), muscle quantity (psoas muscle index, PMI; diaphragm crus cross-sectional area, CSA; quadriceps muscle thickness, QMT), and muscle quality (psoas muscle density, PMD) differed substantially across the cohort. Both PMI and PMD were significantly but weakly associated with functional performance, as reflected by HGT and 6-min walk test distance (6MWT) (Fig. 2C–E). In contrast, PMI and PMD were not significantly correlated with each other (Fig. 2F). The sarcopenia index (SI), integrating muscle quantity (PMI), muscle quality (PMD), and strength (HGT), showed a moderate association with 6MWT but was not related to adiposity indices (Fig. 2G–H).

Correlation matrix analysis further demonstrated a clear separation between muscle-related and adiposity-related domains (Fig. 2I). Muscle quantity (PMI, QMT, CSA), muscle quality (PMD), and functional measures (HGT, 6MWT) formed a coherent muscle–function cluster, with muscle strength generally showing stronger associations than exercise capacity. In contrast, adiposity markers clustered separately. BMI correlated strongly with both indexed VAT and SAT, and iVAT was highly correlated with iSAT. EAT showed moderate positive associations with other fat depots. Fat density parameters were inversely related to fat volumes and showed weak-to-moderate negative associations with muscle and functional measures. Fat volume indices were moderately correlated with measures of muscle quantity but not with muscle quality or functional performance. BMI exhibited a similar pattern, although with stronger correlations.

Overall, these analyses indicate a clear segregation between adiposity-related and muscle-related domains in advanced HFrEF, with limited cross-domain interactions.

Circulating exerkine profiles in HFrEF versus healthy controls

Circulating exerkine profiling revealed pronounced alterations in patients with HFrEF compared with healthy controls (Fig. 3A). Pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including IL-6, TNF-α, CCL2, and CXCL8, and stress- and metabolism-associated factors such as GDF15, FGF21, ANGPTL4, CTSB, DCN, RENT and FST were upregulated, reflecting heightened systemic inflammation and metabolic stress. Conversely, proteins linked to muscle integrity, regenerative and neurotrophic signaling myostatin (MSTN), BDNF, IL-7, SPARC were significantly reduced, suggesting impaired anabolic and regenerative signaling within skeletal muscle. Leptin and IL-15 levels did not differ between groups. Together, these patterns indicate a maladaptive adipose–muscle–inflammatory-metabolic stress profile in HFrEF, characterized by enhanced catabolic and inflammatory signaling alongside reduced trophic support. Exerkines and the adiposity–muscle–function network in HFrEF

In patients with HFrEF, circulating exerkines demonstrated biologically coherent associations with adiposity, muscle quality, and functional performance (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Table 1). Leptin showed the strongest relationships with adiposity, correlating positively with BMI, VAT, SAT, and EAT volumes, and inversely with adipose tissue density, defining a leptin–adiposity axis. Catabolic and metabolic stress mediators including GDF15, IL-15, FGF21, and CTSB were inversely associated with psoas muscle density and functional measures (HGT and sarcopenia index), forming a muscle quality–stress/catabolic axis. Myostatin correlated positively with psoas muscle density and functional measures, particularly the sarcopenia index, HGT, and 6MWT, establishing a muscle quality/function–myostatin axis. Neurotrophic signaling, reflected by BDNF, was inversely related to frailty, defining a neurotrophic–frailty axis. Importantly, classical pro-inflammatory exerkines (IL-6, TNF-α, CCL2, CXCL8) were not significantly associated with muscle or adipose parameters or functional measures. Discussion

This study provides integrated imaging, functional, and proteomic evidence supporting the existence of a maladaptive adiposity–muscle–function axis in advanced HFrEF. Several key observations emerge: (1) Adiposity in end-stage HFrEF is driven primarily by fat distribution rather than total body mass, (2) Muscle quantity and quality are strongly linked to functional capacity, (3) Visceral adiposity associates with muscle mass but not muscle quality or exercise performance, (4) Circulating exerkines define distinct biologically coherent signaling axes connecting adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, and functional performance. Together, these findings suggest that predominantly metabolic signaling rather than inflammatory or obesity alone, underlies skeletal muscle dysfunction in HFrEF.

Adiposity phenotype in HFrEF: beyond BMI

The obesity landscape is evolving rapidly, not only in the general population but also among patients with HFrEF. Early studies reported a prevalence of obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) in HFrEF of approximately 15% [20], whereas in our cohort it exceeded 30%. While obesity plays a well-established role in the pathogenesis of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, its contribution to the progression of HFrEF and its interaction with skeletal muscle remain poorly understood.

In our cohort, 70% of patients were overweight or obese (BMI > 25 kg/m2), consistent with findings from the EMPHASIS-HF trial [21] and population-based data [22]. Importantly, central obesity was not predominant: BMI correlated strongly with waist-to-hip ratio, and only about one-third of patients demonstrated visceral fat predominance based on the VAT/SAT ratio.

All fat depots were interrelated in terms of both volume and density, suggesting shared systemic regulation. VAT and SAT were closely linked, whereas epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) appeared more autonomous, likely influenced by paracrine and endocrine signals from the failing heart [15, 16].

Muscle quantity and quality as determinants of functional capacity: role of adiposity

We performed a comprehensive assessment of skeletal musculature, combining CT-based evaluation of central and core muscles—psoas major (PMI) and the crus of the diaphragm (CSA)—with ultrasound-derived measurement of the largest peripheral muscle, quadriceps muscle thickness (QMT), to obtain both broad and nuanced insight into different muscle depots. Muscle quality was assessed using psoas muscle density (PMD), as reduced CT attenuation reflects intramuscular fat infiltration (myosteatosis) and structural degeneration, which are associated with impaired function and adverse clinical outcomes [23]. Muscle strength and functional performance were measured using standardized handgrip strength (HGT) and the 6-min walk test (6MWT), respectively. To integrate these dimensions, we calculated a sarcopenia index combining muscle quantity (PMI), quality (PMD), and strength (HGT), excluding 6MWT as it predominantly reflects cardiac rather than peripheral muscle performance in our end-stage HFrEF cohort.

Our findings indicate that (1) measures of muscle quantity were generally interrelated, (2) muscle quantity was largely independent of muscle quality (PMD), and (3) functional performance—including strength, exercise capacity, and the sarcopenia index—was strongly associated with both muscle quantity and quality. These data support the notion that in HFrEF, functional performance is determined not only by muscle mass but also by muscle quality, with PMD serving as a valuable and independent marker of muscle integrity [24].

Interestingly, BMI as well as visceral and subcutaneous adipose tissue (VAT and SAT) correlated with skeletal muscle mass but not with muscle quality or functional performance. This suggests that greater adiposity may coexist with preserved or even increased muscle bulk, reflecting the mechanical and metabolic coupling between total body mass and lean tissue. However, excess adipose tissue—particularly visceral fat—may impair muscle composition via lipid infiltration, inflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to reduced muscle quality and metabolic efficiency [25]. Accordingly, sarcopenic obesity in HFrEF appears to be driven more by deterioration in muscle quality and function than by absolute muscle loss [26].

Exerkine signatures

Proteomic profiling in our HFrEF cohort revealed four putative exerkine axes: (1) metabolic stress response axis, reflecting activation of mitochondrial and systemic energy-sensing pathways; (2) myostatin-related axis, indicative of muscle quality and catabolic signaling; (3) leptin signaling axis, associated with adipose–muscle cross-talk and energy homeostasis; and (4) neurotrophic axis, encompassing factors involved in muscle–nerve interactions and neuromuscular maintenance. However, this classification should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive, given the pleiotropic effects of individual exerkines and the substantial overlap and interconnectivity among their biological pathways.

Growth Differentiation Factor 15 (GDF15), Fibroblast Growth Factor 21 (FGF21), Cathepsin B (CTSB), and Interleukin-15 (IL-15) were all upregulated in HFrEF patients and all were inversely associated with muscle density and muscle strength. These factors reflect a systemic metabolic stress response linking skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, and cardiometabolic regulation. GDF15 is induced by mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress and serves as a marker of metabolic distress in cardiovascular disease [27]. It is also is released by forceful contraction of skeletal muscles [28] and rises when fatty acid availability exceeds mitochondrial capacity to process them [29], indicating the risk of cellular lipid accumulation. FGF21 regulates lipid and glucose metabolism and is upregulated under metabolic stress to promote fatty-acid oxidation [30]. CTSB is a lysosomal protease released during muscle remodeling and proteolysis [31], while IL-15 is produced by skeletal muscle in response to exercise [32] or pro-inflammatory cytokines [33], and acts to reduce white adipose tissue deposition, serving as a mediator in a muscle-to-fat axis, promoting lipid oxidation and anabolic signaling. Their inverse correlation with PMD and 6MWT suggests that these elevations may reflect skeletal muscle deterioration. In HFrEF, this pattern is consistent with skeletal myopathy, where qualitative muscle impairment—myosteatosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and metabolic inefficiency—predominates over absolute muscle loss, highlighting these exerkines as potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets in HFrEF related sarcopenia.

Notably, none of the pro-inflammatory exerkines elevated in HFrEF patients—including IL-6, TNF-α, CCL2, and CXCL8—showed significant negative correlations with muscle mass, muscle quality, or physical performance, suggesting that systemic inflammation is unlikely to be a primary driver of sarcopenia in this population. Moreover, none of the pro-inflammatory exerkines correlated with fat parameters, making adipose tissue, either visceral or subcutaneous, an unlikely inducer of systemic inflammation in HFrEF.

In our HFrEF cohort myostatin levels were (1) reduced compared with healthy controls, (2) inversely correlated with NT-proBNP, (3) positively associated with parameters of skeletal muscle quality (PMD), but not quantity (PMI, CSA, QMT), (4) positively correlated with muscle strength (HGT) and functional capacity (6MWT), (5) negatively correlated with metabolic stress mediators (GDF15, IL-15, FGF21, CTSB) and inflammatory markers (TNF-alpha, CXCL8). Myostatin is a well-established negative regulator of skeletal muscle mass [34, 35], predominantly expressed in skeletal muscle, with lower expression in cardiac and adipose tissue [36]. While we previously demonstrated upregulation of myostatin gene expression in cardiac tissue from HFrEF patients [37] and animal models suggest increased circulating levels of cardiac origin with beneficial effects of its inhibition on muscle mass [38], clinical studies have yielded inconsistent results, reporting both increased [39, 40] and decreased [13, 41] circulating levels. This variability suggests context dependence, potentially related to disease stage. Notably, our study provides a comprehensive characterization of myostatin in relation to HFrEF severity, body composition, functional parameters, and circulating exerkines. These findings caution against interpreting myostatin as a simple biomarker of cachexia or therapeutic target [42] in HFrEF, particularly in advanced disease, where its inhibition may be ineffective or even detrimental. Instead, our data suggest that in end-stage HFrEF, reduced circulating myostatin potentially reflects impaired skeletal muscle quality and/or metabolic integrity. Further studies are needed to better elucidate these complex relationships.

Leptin demonstrated strong age-independent correlations with VAT, SAT, and EAT volume, as well as adipose tissue density, confirming preserved leptin–fat mass coupling even in advanced HFrEF. However, leptin was unrelated to muscle mass, muscle quality, or functional performance, highlighting that adipose-derived leptin reflects fat burden rather than protection against sarcopenia. Interestingly, leptin was positively correlated with myostatin, a relationship absent in healthy controls, suggesting potential crosstalk between adipose signaling and muscle-preserving pathways that warrants further study.

Finally, the neurotrophic–frailty axis, represented by BDNF, emerged as a distinct feature. BDNF was markedly downregulated in HFrEF and inversely associated with frailty index, but not with adiposity, muscle mass, muscle quality, or functional performance. This dissociation suggests that neurotrophic signaling primarily influences systemic resilience and vulnerability rather than localized musculoskeletal parameters. Mechanistically, BDNF supports neuromuscular junction integrity, motor neuron survival, and mitochondrial function in skeletal muscle, thereby influencing functional reserve and capacity to withstand physiological stress. In HFrEF, reduced BDNF may contribute to frailty, poor exercise tolerance, and heightened vulnerability, independent of absolute muscle mass or adiposity. This finding aligns with emerging evidence linking neurotrophic pathways to frailty and physical resilience in aging and chronic disease populations [43]. Circulating BDNF levels have been linked to sarcopenia and frailty in older adults and in chronic disease populations [44], suggesting that lower neurotrophic support may contribute to reduced physiological reserve and vulnerability. Together, these data suggest that neurotrophic signaling influences systemic resilience and functional reserve in HFrEF, potentially independent of direct effects on muscle mass or adiposity, and that BDNF may serve as a marker or mediator of frailty in this population.

Conclusions

In advanced HFrEF, body composition and functional capacity are shaped by a maladaptive adiposity–muscle–function axis. Fat distribution, rather than BMI alone, drives adiposity, with visceral and subcutaneous depots correlating with muscle mass but not muscle quality or performance. Skeletal muscle quality, assessed by PMD, independently predicts strength and exercise capacity, highlighting qualitative impairment as a key contributor to sarcopenic obesity. Circulating exerkines define distinct axes linking metabolism, muscle preservation, adiposity, and frailty: metabolic stress markers reflect muscle deterioration, myostatin parallels preserved muscle quality and functional measures, leptin mirrors adiposity, and BDNF tracks systemic resilience and frailty. Classical inflammatory markers are largely dissociated from muscle or fat parameters, suggesting that sarcopenia in HFrEF may be more closely linked to metabolic dysregulation than to systemic inflammation. However, given the cross-sectional design, these findings should be interpreted as associative rather than indicative of causality.. Integrating imaging, functional, and proteomic profiling may improve identification of patients at risk and highlight therapeutic targets to enhance muscle quality, resilience, and functional outcomes.

These findings highlight the potential of exerkine profiling to not only improve risk stratification but also to identify mechanistic pathways amenable to intervention, including metabolic stress mediators (GDF15, FGF21, IL-15), myostatin, leptin, and BDNF. Future studies should prospectively validate these candidate biomarkers in longitudinal cohorts and explore targeted interventions—such as exercise training, nutritional modulation, or pharmacologic manipulation of myostatin, metabolic, or neurotrophic pathways—to assess their utility as predictive markers and therapeutic targets in HFrEF.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cohort consisted exclusively of white male patients, precluding assessment of sex-specific effects. Second, the cross-sectional design prevents inference of causality. Third, findings are limited to end-stage HFrEF patients undergoing LVAD implantation and may not generalize to earlier disease stages. Finally, although multiple testing correction and adjustments were applied, residual confounding cannot be excluded.
