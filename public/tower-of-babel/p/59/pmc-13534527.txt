Chromosome-level de novo assembly of the nuclear and mitochondrial genomes of Arcopilus aureus, a filamentous fungus with multifaceted ecological and economic roles
Authors: Mengmeng Yang, Feiyu Yan, Qingzhou Ma, Yanling Zhang, Bingye Yang, Yashuang Guo, Meng Zhang, Taibo Liang, Chao Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The filamentous fungus Arcopilus aureus (Sordariale: Chaetomiaceae) is notable for its multi-domain significance across agriculture, medicine, and industry. In this study, we generated a chromosome-level nuclear genome and a complete circular mitogenome for A. aureus by integrating data from next-generation sequencing, PacBio HiFi, and Hi-C technologies. The final nuclear genome assembly spans 33.77 Mb (GC content: 57.67%), and was organized into seven chromosomal-sized scaffolds (only one gap) with an N50 size of 5.09 Mb and BUSCO completeness of 95.91%. A total of 10,282 protein-coding genes, 228 non-coding RNAs, and ~1.77 Mb of repetitive elements were predicted in the nuclear genome. By contrast, the mitogenome of A. aureus is 33,820 bp in length, with a GC content of 25.96%. It harbors 15 typical mitochondrial protein-coding genes, one unidentified ORF, two rRNAs (small subunit rns and large subunit rnl), and 28 tRNAs. This high-quality genome assembly provides a valuable resource for understanding the ecology, genetics, and evolution of A. aureus, which facilitates elucidating its mechanisms of biocontrol, infection, and metabolite synthesis.

Background & Summary

Arcopilus aureus (Chivers) X. Wei Wang & Samson is a filamentous ascomycete fungus of the family Chaetomiaceae (Sordariale, Sordariomycetes), predominantly inhabiting soil and plant-associated environments1–6. The fungal species was first described by Chivers (1912) under the Latin name Chaetomium aureum Chivers7. In 2016, Wang et al. (2016) revised the classification of Chaetomiaceae based on multi-locus phylogeny and morphological data, and transferred several members of Chaetomium (including C. aureum) to a newly proposed genera Arcopilus, with A. aureus (≡ C. aureum) as the type species8. A. aureus exhibits a broad geographic distribution, having been documented in multiple countries including China, Morocco, Spain, Brazil, India, and México2,3,9–12. Nevertheless, this fungus had long remained inconspicuous until recent studies recognized it as a plant-beneficial microorganism showing remarkable biocontrol capabilities. To date, A. aureus (both endophytic and rhizospheric) and its bioactive extracts have demonstrated potent antifungal activities against a range of phytopathogens, including Magnaporthe grisea and Rhizoctonia solani2, Fusarium circinatum10, Phytophthora infestans and F. culmorum13, F. fujikuroi5, as well as F. graminearum, Bipolaris maydis, and Botrytis cinerea14. Notably, the application of A. aureus as a plant biocontrol agent necessitates thorough risk assessment, given confirmed cases of leaf spot diseases caused by this fungus in Pseudostellaria heterophylla, Cucumis melo, and Nicotiana tabacum6,15,16.

It is well established that endophytic fungi, through co-evolution and potential horizontal gene transfer, can biosynthesize host metabolites or other putative phytochemicals. The endophytic A. aureus isolates, derived from Thymelaea lythroides (a medicinal plant) and Vitis vinifera, are notable for producing pharmaceutically valuable compounds sclerotiorin and resveratrol, respectively9,11,12,17,18. Sclerotiorin has been reported to not only exhibit antimicrobial activity but also function as an inhibitor of the Hsp90 chaperone machine, a therapeutic target in cancer treatment9. Resveratrol, a naturally occurring phytoalexin, has been linked to numerous health benefits including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-aging properties, alleviation of cardiovascular and neurological diseases, and preventing obesity and diabetes19. Additionally, A. aureus isolated from grapevines produced a stable, non-toxic yellow pigment consisting of cochlioquinol II and riboflavin, which could be considered for use in food processing20. Therefore, the endophytic A. aureus poses as a promising new source of natural bioactive substances with potential for utilization in medical and industrial applications.

Despite the ecological and economic significance, no genomic map (even draft sequences) of this fungus has been produced so far, impeding research into the molecular mechanisms underlying its biocontrol activity, pathogenesis, and secondary metabolism. Here, we finished assembling the first chromosomal-scale nuclear and mitochondrial genomes of A. aureus by integrating its next-generation sequencing (NGS), long-read sequencing, and high-through chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) data. The final assembly yielded a 33.77-Mb nuclear genome organized into seven chromosomal-sized scaffolds (containing only one gap; N50 = 5.09 Mb), along with a complete circular mitogenome of 33,820 bp. Quality of the nuclear genome assembly was assessed through Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Ortholog (BUSCO) analysis, demonstrating a high level of completeness (95.91%). Furthermore, annotation of the A. aureus nuclear genome revealed 10,282 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 228 non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), and ~1.77 Mb of repetitive sequences (5.24% of the total assembly). By contrast, only 16 PCGs and 30 ncRNAs (28 tRNAs and two rRNAs) were identified in the mitogenome. The high-quality genome assembly and annotation of A. aureus would provide foundations for precisely characterizing its genomic function, regulation and evolution, thereby elucidating the mechanisms of biocontrol, infection, and synthesis of metabolites.

Methods

Sampling, DNA preparation and genomic sequencing

In 2023, multiple A. aureus isolates associated with tobacco leaf gray spot were collected from a greenhouse located in Zhengzhou, Henan province of China6. One (designated MB-11) of these A. aureus strains was randomly selected for subsequent whole-genome sequencing. The mycelia of A. aureus MB-11 harvested from 10-day-old PDA (potato dextrose agar) culture at 25 °C were used for genomic DNA extraction following the modified CTAB method21. Quality and concentration of the extracted gDNA were examined using a NanoDrop ND2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

For genome survey and correction, a paired-end (PE 150 bp) library was constructed using the VAHTS Universal Plus DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina V2 (Vazyme Biotech, China), and subsequently sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (MGI Tech, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. This yielded ~4.62 Gb of raw reads, covering about 137× of the estimated A. aureus genome (Table 1), which were then filtered with the software fastp v0.23.4 to produce ~4.42 Gb of clean reads (without adapters, bases below Q20 < 30%, and no ambiguous bases “N”)22. For high-standard assembling of genome, an SMRTbell library (15 kb in insert size) was generated using the SMRTbell prep kit 3.0 (Pacific Biosciences, USA). PacBio HiFi sequencing was then performed using the PacBio Revio system (Pacific Biosciences, USA), producing approximately 4.94 Gb (~146× coverage) of circular consensus sequencing (CCS) reads with an N50 value of 19.21 kb (Table 1). To determine the order and orientation of the contigs, an Hi-C library was prepared using the cross-linked chromatins (formaldehyde crosslinking) derived from the A. aureus mycelia. The constructed Hi-C library was sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform, generating a total of 24.17 Gb raw data, which provided ~716× coverage of the genome (Table 1). Genome estimation and chromosome-level nuclear genome assembly

Prior to the actual genome assembly, a genome survey was conducted using the filtered MGI short reads to assess the size, homozygosity, and duplication of the A. aureus genome. K-mer sets were created using the software Jellyfish v2.3.023. Genome evaluation was performed using GenomeScope 2.0 based on the k-mer (k = 17) frequency distribution with k-mer number/k-mer depth24. The genome size of A. aureus was finally estimated as ~33.13 Mb with a heterozygous ratio of 0.39% and a duplication rate of 3.8% (Fig. 1).Fig. 1GenomeScope k-mer (k = 17) profile plot of Arcopilus aureus based on its 150 bp paired-end sequencing data. len, estimated haploid genome size; aa, homozygosity rate; ab, heterozygosity rate; dup, duplication rate.

The PacBio HiFi reads were used to perform de novo genome assembly by using hifiasm v0.19.6 with default parameters25. To mitigate the high error rate inherent in third-generation sequencing, the preliminary assembly was polished using Racon (with long reads) and Pilon (with short reads) successively, to correct small indels and substitutions, hence improving the overall assembly quality26,27. Additionally, a tool named Purge_haplotigs was employed to eliminate the heterozygous regions from the above error-corrected genome28. This resulted in a relatively high-quality genome assembly of ~33.94 Mb (containing mitogenomic fragments), comprising 11 contigs with an N50 value of 4,707,493 bp. Finally, the filtered Hi-C reads were mapped to the contig-level assembly using BWA v0.7.17-r1188 with default parameters29, followed by processing with Juicer v1.630. The output from the Juicer v1.6 was then taken as input for 3D-DNA v180419 with the parameter “-r 0” to anchor the assembled contigs into pseudochromosomes31. A thorough manual inspection and correction of any potential errors was done using Juicebox v1.11.0832. Moreover, the Hi-C data were visualized using HiCExplorer v3.6 to analyze positional relationships and interaction intensities among the contigs33 (Fig. 2). We ultimately obtained a chromosomal-level nuclear genome assembly of ~33.77 Mb, which is highly consistent with the previously estimated genome size. This assembly was consisted of seven chromosomes, numbered from Chr1 to Chr7 in descending order of their sizes (6.14 Mb to 4.05 Mb), with an N50 length of 5.09 Mb (Table 2, Supplementary Table S1). The software BUSCO v5.4.4 was employed to assess the integrity and quality of the A. aureus nuclear genome assembly based on the fungal core dataset fungi_odb1034. RNA sequencing and analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the mycelia of A. aureus grown for 10 days on PDA using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Prior to library preparation, polyA selection was performed on the total RNA using oligo(dT) magnetic beads to enrich for mRNA. The enriched mRNA was then subjected to library construction using the VAHTS Universal V10 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit (Vazyme Biotech), and sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform to generate ~7.45 Gb of 150 bp paired-end reads (Table 1). The raw RNA-seq data were quality-enhanced using fastp v0.23.4 through adapter trimming and the removal of low-quality reads22. As a result, ~6.57 Gb of clean RNA-seq data were obtained, of which 95.12% were successfully mapped to our assembled A. aureus nuclear genome using HISAT2 v2.2.1 under default parameters35.

Prediction of repeat elements and ncRNAs in the nuclear genome

Repetitive elements in the chromosomal-scale nuclear genome were identified by using an integrated approach that combined de novo prediction and homology-based alignment. An ab initio repeat library was firstly constructed using RepeatModeler v2.14.0 based on the intrinsic sequence characteristics of the A. aureus nuclear genome36. Subsequently, RepeatMasker v4.1.6 was applied to detect repetitive sequences using the custom repeat library37. For homology-based searches, RepeatMasker v4.1.6 was used to perform repeat annotation based on the latest Repbase fungal library (http://www.girinst.org/repbase)38. Upon merging of the above prediction results, a total of 1,770,936 bp were finally identified as repetitive sequences, representing 5.24% of the entire nuclear genome. Of these repetitive elements, DNA transposons, retroelements, unclassified repeats, simple repeats, and low complexity regions accounted for 1.23%, 1.02%, 1.04%, 1.65%, and 0.31% of the nuclear genome, respectively (Table 3). The software tRNAscan-SE v2.0.12 was used to predict tRNAs across the nuclear genome39. Other ncRNAs, such as rRNAs and small RNAs (sRNAs), were predicted using INFERNAL v1.1.5 through searches against the Rfam database v15.040,41. Gene prediction and functional annotation of the nuclear genome

The repeat-masked genome assembly was used for gene prediction to avoid artifacts caused by repetitive sequences. Gene models were predicted by integrating evidences from the following three distinct approaches: (a) ab initio prediction, (b) homology-based prediction, and (c) transcriptome-based prediction. The ab initio gene prediction was performed by using Augustus v3.5.0 and GeneMark v4.30 under default settings42,43. For homology-based gene prediction, protein sequences from the species closely related to A. aureus were obtained (e.g., C. strumarium, GCF_033439665.1) and subsequently employed as input for GALBA v1.0.11 to construct training datasets44. For transcriptome-based gene prediction, reference-guided assembly of transcripts was performed using StringTie v2.2.3 based on the RNA-seq alignments previously generated by HISAT2 v2.2.135,45. The resulting transcripts were processed with CD-HIT v4.8.1 to remove redundancies46. TansDecoder v5.7.1 was then used to predict the open reading frames (ORFs) based on the assembled transcripts47. Finally, the three aforementioned gene prediction models were integrated using EvidenceModeler v2.1.0 to generate a non-redundant and more comprehensive gene set48. This integration resulted in the prediction of 10,282 PCGs distributed across the nuclear genome, with a mean CDS length of 1,463 bp (Table 2). Functional annotation was performed by BLAST alignment (e-value < 10−5) of the predicted genes against public functional databases, including NCBI-nr49, Swiss-Prot50, GO51, KEGG52, eggNOG53, and Pfam54 (Supplementary Fig. S1-S3). The comprehensive annotation process successfully assigned functional terms to 10,085 genes, representing 98.08% of all PCGs (Table 4). Additionally, Circos v0.69-9 was employed to create a comprehensive visual representation of the A. aureus genome55. Circular mitogenome assembly, annotation, and phylogeny

Cleaned MGI short reads were de novo assembled using the software SPAdes v3.15.5 with kmers of 21, 33, 55, 77, 99, and 12756. Mitochondria-related contigs were identified and retrieved by conducting a local BLASTN search using the reference mitogenome of Madurella mycetomatis (GenBank: NC_018359.1), a species closely related to A. aureus57. Then, we filled gaps between these contigs by using MITObim v1.9 to build the closed-circular mtDNA macromolecule of A. aureus58. Initial annotation of the complete mitogenome was performed with the online tool MFannot based on the genetic code 459. All mitochondrial components including typical protein-coding genes, unidentified open reading frames (uORFs), rRNAs, and tRNAs were predicted and named according to their usual nomenclature60. Gene boundaries were adjusted by performing BLASTN searches against the NCBI nr database. The exon–intron borders of protein-coding genes were corrected by comparing them with the corresponding intron-free genes of closely related species. Subsequently, ORFs were modified or predicted using the NCBI Open Reading Frame Finder (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/orffinder), followed by functional annotation through BLASTP searches (e-value < 1e-5) against the NCBI nr database. Graphical map of the mitogenome was drawn with the online program OGDraw v 1.3.161. As a result, the A. aureus mitogenome harbored 15 typical mitochondrial protein-coding genes, one uORF, two mitochondrial rRNAs (small subunit rns and large subunit rnl), and 28 tRNAs (Table 5, Supplementary Table S2, Fig. 3B).

To investigate the taxonomic position of A. aureus, we performed a phylogenetic analysis involving 13 species of Sordariomycetes based on a concatenated alignment of 13 mitochondrial conserved proteins (nad1-6, nad4L, cox1-3, cob, atp6, and atp8) (Supplementary Table S3). Individual mitochondrial protein sequences were first aligned using MAFFT v7.475 with auto strategy62. The resulting alignments were subsequently concatenated into a supermatrix using SequenceMatrix v1.7.863. Phylogenetic inference was constructed using the maximum likelihood (ML) method with 1,000 bootstrap replications in MEGA v12.0.1164. Tree rendering was carried out using the FigTree v1.4.4 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/). This tree topology revealed that all the 13 fungal species were assigned into six well-supported clades, corresponding to Sordariales, Magnaporthales, Diaporthales, Ophiostomatales, Glomerellales, and Hypocreales (Fig. 3C). In the order Sordariales, A. aureus was clustered tightly with Madurella mycetomatis, suggesting their close evolutionary relationship.

Technical Validation

Genome assembly assessment

Two independent approaches were used to validate the quality of the assembled nuclear genome of A. aureus. Firstly, BUSCO assessment was performed on the chromosome-level nuclear genome assembly using the BUSCO v5.4.4 and the lineage dataset fungi_odb10 (released on 2024-01-08). The result showed that a substantial majority (95.9%) of the essential and conserved genes (fungal core proteins) were successfully captured, suggesting a high level of completeness of the nuclear genome assembly. Secondly, the NGS short paired-end reads employed for genome survey and correction were mapped to the final nuclear genome assembly using HISAT2 v2.2.1 with default settings. The alignment of the short reads to the genome assembly resulted in a mapping rate of 95.26% and a coverage of 99.52%. Of the remaining unmapped short reads, ~56.48% could be aligned to the A. aureus mitochondrial genome, covering 100% of the circular mitogenome.

Assessment of the gene prediction

The predicted and integrated protein-coding genes were also evaluated using BUSCO v5.4.4 with the lineage dataset fungi_odb10. Briefly, of the 758 BUSCOs analyzed, 94.72% (including 716 single-copy and 2 duplicated genes) were identified as complete, while only 15 were fragmented and 25 were missing (Table 2).
