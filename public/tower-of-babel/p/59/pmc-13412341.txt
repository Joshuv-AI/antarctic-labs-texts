Artificial intelligence for battlefield triage in large-scale combat operations: Opportunities, limits, and ethical considerations
Authors: Quentin Mathais, Pierre-Julien Cungi, Antoine Lamblin, Olivier Dubourg, Julien Bordes, Salah Boussen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT:Large-scale combat operations (LSCOs) impose major constraints on battlefield medical systems, combining sustained casualty inflow, degraded communications, prolonged evacuation timelines, and limited opportunities for repeated clinical reassessment. Under such conditions, conventional triage frameworks—designed for episodic assessment and rapid evacuation—become insufficient. This narrative review examines how artificial intelligence (AI) could support battlefield triage in LSCO, not as a replacement for clinical judgement, but to preserve situational awareness and prioritization over time when human vigilance alone is insufficient. Based on military medical, technological, and doctrinal literature, we analyze AI through three operational functions: extending caregiver perception, sustaining cognition under pressure, and enabling anticipatory and personalized decision-making. Near-term deployable capabilities include wearable physiological sensors, early warning systems, digital casualty documentation, and unmanned platforms supporting remote assessment, resupply, and evacuation coordination. Mid-term developments may integrate multimodal data fusion, predictive decision support, augmented reality–assisted guidance, and partial automation of prioritization. Longer-term conceptual frameworks, such as digital twins, envision fully predictive and individualized triage and resource allocation but remain at the research stage. We further examine the engineering, human, doctrinal, ethical and strategic constraints that govern AI deployment in LSCO, including DDIL environments, data quality, cognitive and ergonomic risks, automation bias, survivability concerns in a transparent battlefield and requirements for robust governance. Overall, the value of AI for triage in LSCO lies in human–machine teaming that sustains vigilance, coordination, and anticipation under extreme operational constraints, provided deployment remains disciplined, ethically governed, and operationally grounded. INTRODUCTION

Large-scale combat operations (LSCO) are high-intensity, multidomain military engagements conducted against near-peer adversaries, characterized by simultaneity, depth, duration, and deliberate targeting of command-and-control, logistics, and support functions.

Within this context, military medical systems are exposed to pressures that exceed the design assumptions of classical prehospital care. High casualty volumes, heterogeneous injury mechanisms, degraded communications, prolonged evacuation timelines, and recurrent mass-casualty events combine to create operational conditions in which traditional triage frameworks show clear limitations.1–3 Although tools such as START, SALT, or NATO T1–T4 categorization remain operationally useful, they were developed for settings in which rapid assessment, prompt evacuation, and a functional medical chain of care could be assumed—conditions that are no longer consistently preserved in contemporary high-intensity conflict.4,5

Beyond casualty volume, LSCO is defined by a transparent battlespace. Persistent surveillance, ubiquitous sensing, and precision fires reduce previously protected rear areas, disrupt evacuation, and fragment the medical chain.6–8

At the same time, modern battlefields have become increasingly data rich. Wearable sensors, unmanned systems, digital medical devices, and command-and-control platforms generate continuous physiological and contextual information.9,10 Similar data-driven transformations in civilian emergency and critical care have reshaped early warning and triage paradigms. Artificial intelligence (AI)—encompassing machine learning, deep learning, computer vision, and large language models—offers the possibility of converting these heterogeneous data streams into structured insight that could support or augment triage decisions.

Yet battlefield triage is not merely a technical task. It involves value-laden decisions conducted under extreme uncertainty, where ethical, doctrinal, and operational constraints must remain central.5,11 Military triage explicitly integrates utilitarian objectives, operational priorities, and force preservation considerations, complicating the introduction of algorithmic decision support. Although doctrinally dynamic and iterative, triage in LSCO is often reduced in practice to episodic assessments constrained by cognitive load and operational tempo. The mismatch between continuously evolving trauma physiology and discontinuous human reassessment becomes a central vulnerability of battlefield care.

The objective of this narrative review is to provide a balanced, operationally grounded synthesis of what AI may offer—and what it cannot offer—for battlefield triage in LSCO, intended for a broad readership beyond technical specialists in AI technologies.

METHODS

This article is a narrative review based on a targeted examination of military medical, technological, and doctrinal literature. For indexed medical literature, a structured PubMed/MEDLINE search was performed using a PICO-informed approach, where the population of interest included war casualties, wartime settings, or military medicine; the intervention was AI; and outcomes included triage, management, or mortality. The search strategy combined terms as follows: (“war casualty” OR “wartime” OR “military medicine”) AND “artificial intelligence” AND (“triage” OR “management” OR “mortality”).

Given the operational focus of the topic, this search was complemented by nonindexed sources, including institutional reports, military doctrinal publications, and technical documents relevant to LSCO contexts. Sources were selected based on relevance to battlefield triage, AI-enabled decision support, and constraints specific to high-intensity conflict. This review does not aim to provide a systematic or exhaustive synthesis but rather an operationally grounded narrative analysis.

WHY CONSIDER AI FOR BATTLEFIELD TRIAGE IN LSCO

The Inherent Limits of Human Triage Under Combat Conditions

Triage is fundamentally a cognitive task performed under uncertainty. Caregivers must rapidly integrate physiological measurements, injury patterns, environmental context, and resource availability with incomplete or conflicting information. In LSCO, these demands occur under conditions of fatigue, darkness, environmental noise, sleep deprivation, and threat exposure, all of which degrade situational awareness and cognitive performance.12,13

Studies from both civilian and military contexts consistently show substantial intraoperator and interoperator variability in triage decisions, with significant rates of over-triage and under-triage even among experienced clinicians.14–16 These discrepancies reflect the limits of human cognitive processing in high-stress, information-dense environments.

Cognitive science and human-factors research further demonstrate that stress, time pressure, and working-memory saturation impair the integration of multiple competing cues, reinforcing these limitations in combat conditions.17,18 Many life-threatening conditions—such as internal hemorrhage, traumatic brain injury, or respiratory failure—may evolve silently in the minutes following injury, presenting initially with deceptively normal vital signs.13,19 A casualty may appear initially stable while undergoing physiologic deterioration, making early recognition difficult. This creates a fundamental mismatch between continuously evolving trauma physiology and episodic human reassessment. Although triage is doctrinally dynamic, sustained reassessment becomes operationally unattainable when casualty volume, environmental threat, and evacuation delays converge.

Moreover, in LSCO, where evacuation delays may extend for hours to days due to contested airspace, electronic warfare, or logistical saturation, misjudging these trajectories becomes increasingly consequential.1,10

Real-time Data Exploitation in LSCO

For decades, major improvements in combat casualty care have emerged from retrospective data analysis.20–22 LSCO increasingly demand near–real-time data exploitation to support dynamic clinical and operational decisions. Recent analyses of large-scale conflicts emphasize the need for rapid innovation and adaptation, driven by compressed decision timelines and evolving operational constraints.6

In this environment, medical systems must transition from retrospective learning to continuous situational awareness across the continuum of care. AI becomes relevant not because it replaces clinicians, but because LSCOs generate more data than any human can continuously interpret while delivering care.1

Emerging Medical Challenges in LSCO

LSCO introduces medical challenges beyond classical mass-casualty paradigms. Prolonged evacuation timelines and contested access to higher echelons of care increase the likelihood of prolonged field care, requiring sustained monitoring and anticipatory decision-making.

In parallel, casualty flow may be continuous rather than episodic, demanding dynamic prioritization over time rather than one-time categorization. These conditions amplify risks such as sepsis related to delayed definitive care and ischemia-reperfusion injury associated with prolonged tourniquet use.

AI-enabled monitoring and predictive modeling may therefore assist in identifying deterioration trajectories, optimizing timing of interventions, and adapting triage to resource constraints.

HOW AI COULD SUPPORT BATTLEFIELD TRIAGE: TOWARD A DATA-ENABLED BATTLEFIELD

Across these domains, AI is not primarily a tool for faster classification, but for temporal continuity. By enabling persistent sensing, automated interpretation, and continuous risk reassessment, AI addresses one of the central vulnerabilities of battlefield triage in LSCO: the impossibility for humans to continuously monitor and reassess multiple casualties over time. In this sense, AI does not replace clinical judgment but extends it across temporal and cognitive dimensions that are otherwise inaccessible to human operators alone. Programs such as the DARPA Triage Challenge illustrate this direction, advancing sensing platforms and AI-enabled algorithms for remote physiologic assessment and prioritization.23

AI relevance to battlefield triage is best understood through the three operational functions it may augment: extending perception, sustaining cognition under pressure, and enabling anticipatory and personalized decision-making.

Seeing Earlier and Farther: Extending the Caregiver’s Perception

Before triage can occur, casualties must first be detected, located, and physiologically characterized. In LSCO, this initial function is increasingly dissociated from direct physical contact, as access to casualties may be delayed or denied. AI-enabled sensing technologies, therefore, extend the caregiver’s perceptual reach in space and time.

Wearable sensors: growing maturity and operational promise

Physiological monitoring has progressed from conceptual prototypes to systems approaching deployment readiness. Wearable sensors capable of capturing heart rate, SpO₂, respiratory rate, or movements using photoplethysmography or accelerometry have been evaluated under demanding conditions.24–26 While not yet universally deployed, NATO and US Army programs anticipate integration into next-generation soldier systems.27–29

Two trends are particularly relevant: miniaturization supporting austere deployment, and network integration enabling transmission of physiological data to caregivers or command elements. Continuous sensing provides the data foundation for alerting and predictive systems.

Computer vision for multimodal assessment

Computer vision and noncontact sensing demonstrate that physiological information can be extracted from video and remote signals. Deep learning and image-processing methods can estimate respiratory rate and heart rate from thermal or optical video streams with growing accuracy.30–33

These approaches support upstream risk stratification when direct access is delayed, with triage beginning from observation rather than physical examination.

Drones and autonomous ground platforms for remote triage

Unmanned aerial systems (UAS) and autonomous ground vehicles (AGV) extend remote sensing under threat conditions. UAS can locate casualties, provide overwatch, assess environmental hazards, and relay physiologic information prior to medic arrival. Radar-equipped UAS have demonstrated the ability to detect respiratory activity remotely.34 AGV have been explored for casualty extraction, delivery of triage tags or medical supplies, and camera-guided assessment in high-risk environments.35

Together, these platforms enable distributed sensing and prioritization while preserving human oversight.

The Cognitive Exoskeleton: Organizing Information Under Pressure

Once casualties are detected and monitored, the primary limitation becomes cognitive rather than sensory. Caregivers must interpret continuous data streams under time pressure, fatigue, and operational stress. In this context, AI functions as a cognitive support layer, structuring data to preserve situational awareness when continuous human vigilance is unattainable.

AI-enabled alerting systems for dynamic battlefield triage

The transition from automated data acquisition to meaningful clinical impact depends on effective alerting systems. AI-enabled alerting transforms continuous physiological inputs into prioritized, actionable signals.

Early warning systems provide a foundational layer.36 By aggregating physiological parameters, these systems detect deterioration before it becomes clinically obvious and trigger reassessment. Rule-based models already identify deviations in basic vital signs, providing a first level of decision support. Machine learning approaches extend this by recognizing multidimensional patterns of shock or respiratory compromise that may precede overt instability. For clinical relevance, alerting must be embedded within operational workflows. Digital platforms that aggregate vital signs, structure documentation, and support handover create the infrastructure necessary for actionable rather than overwhelming alerts.

Several systems illustrate AI-enabled alerting without full autonomy. The Battlefield Assisted Trauma Distributed Observation Kit (BATDOK) provides real-time aggregation of physiologic data and structured documentation along the evacuation chain.37–39 Combat Medical Early Warning Systems (CMEWS) focus on continuous physiological monitoring and early detection of deterioration trajectories.36 The APPRAISE-HRI framework explores supervised human-robot interaction, supporting hemorrhage risk stratification while preserving human oversight.40,41 The Smart Medical Evacuation Support System integrates monitoring, decision support, and evacuation planning.42

Collectively, these systems illustrate a shift from episodic assessment toward continuous, data-informed prioritization without removing clinician oversight.

Augmented reality and cognitive guidance

Augmented reality (AR) technologies represent a complementary form of cognitive augmentation rather than automation. In military contexts, AR head-mounted displays can provide hands-free procedural guidance and visual overlays of Tactical Combat Casualty Care (TCCC) algorithms within the medic’s field of view.

Programmatic initiatives such as the French RAPACE program (Augmented Reality for Expert Surgical Assistance) explore immersive tele-assistance through three-dimensional annotations and remote expert guidance in austere environments.43–45 Systematic reviews in civilian healthcare similarly report improved procedural accuracy and reduced cognitive load in acute care settings.46,47

When coupled with AI-driven analysis, AR platforms may deliver real-time contextual cues—such as highlighting deteriorating oxygen saturation trends or rising shock indices—thereby supporting dynamic triage and early intervention without removing clinician oversight.

Speech recognition and language processing tools

Speech-recognition and natural language processing tools offer a complementary means of reducing documentation burden by enabling hands-free data capture and structured triage tagging. In civilian trauma care, voice-assisted systems have demonstrated potential to streamline workflow and support clinical documentation in time-critical environments.48

In LSCO, such tools may reduce cognitive load while preserving data continuity across evacuation echelons.

Looking Forward: Predicting and Personalizing Care and Resource Needs

Beyond extending perception and sustaining cognition, AI introduces a third function: anticipatory and personalized decision-making. Rather than responding to static physiological states, AI-enabled systems may estimate clinical evolution, treatment response, and resource requirements, enabling adaptation to individual trajectories and operational constraints.

Smart devices and automated interventions

In addition to supporting triage decisions, AI may influence the execution of selected life-saving interventions. Emerging concepts include smart tourniquets capable of automated tightening based on pressure or flow metrics, closed-loop infusion systems guided by shock prediction algorithms, automated ventilatory support devices, and adaptive occlusive dressings incorporating embedded sensing.49–52

Most of these technologies remain at early developmental stages and are not yet operationally deployable. However, they illustrate a possible trajectory in which AI contributes not only to prioritization but also to partial automation or optimization of specific therapeutic actions in resource-constrained environments.

Digital twins, predictive triage and individualized care as a conceptual end state

A more ambitious and largely conceptual development involves the creation of digital twins—computational representations of individual casualties built from continuous physiological monitoring, structured clinical inputs, injury mechanisms, and operational data.53 As wearable sensors and digital documentation become more widespread, the accumulation of high-frequency, multimodal datasets may enable modeling of individualized physiological trajectories over time.

Rather than relying solely on single-point categorization, predictive models could continuously estimate the probability of deterioration or response to treatment. Early trauma research programs illustrate the feasibility of this approach. The ShockMatrix study demonstrated that machine learning models could identify trauma patients requiring hemorrhage control resuscitation with performance comparable to clinician prediction.54 Similarly, AI-driven frameworks have been proposed to support individualized resource allocation and treatment strategies in hemorrhage management.55

In LSCO, such predictive and personalized approaches remain predominantly research stage. Nonetheless, they suggest a shift from reactive triage toward dynamic risk estimation aligned with anticipated clinical evolution and operational feasibility.

Medical command-and-control and medical logistics

The implications of anticipatory AI extend beyond point-of-care decisions. Integration of continuous physiological monitoring with digital casualty records can generate a shared, near–real-time medical picture, supporting anticipatory medical command-and-control.56 At higher echelons, predictive models may assist in evacuation sequencing, allocation of surgical capacity, and identification of impending system saturation.

AI-enabled logistics similarly expands this anticipatory dimension. UAS have demonstrated feasibility for the delivery of blood products and critical supplies in contested environments.57 Predictive analytics may further support consumable stock management, anticipate casualty surges, optimize resupply scheduling, and enable predictive maintenance of medical equipment and evacuation assets.9

Taken together, these developments illustrate how AI could extend triage from a point-of-care classification task toward a system-level anticipatory function. However, while selected components—such as predictive alerting and logistics optimization—are approaching operational maturity, fully personalized and digitally twinned triage remains conceptual and dependent on robust data ecosystems.

LIMITATIONS AND OPERATIONAL CONSTRAINTS

The limitations of AI-enabled triage in LSCO are heterogeneous. They include technical constraints, human and doctrinal risks, strategic survivability considerations, and challenges related to evaluation, governance, and accountability in high-stakes operational contexts.

Engineering Constraints

Technical fragility and DDIL environments

AI systems depend on sensors, communications, and computational infrastructure. In LSCO, electromagnetic interference, cyberattack, hardware failure, and extreme environmental conditions can disrupt these components. Such denied, degraded, intermittent, and limited (DDIL) environments—characterized by sporadic connectivity and constrained bandwidth—challenge data availability and model reliability under operational stress.

Computer vision and deep learning approaches are computationally and energetically demanding compared with simpler sensing modalities. Estimating respiratory rate from video streams using neural networks may require substantially more processing power and energy than simpler impedance or electrode-derived measurements for comparable clinical information. In LSCO, where power, computing resources, and emissions must be minimized, such trade-offs become operationally significant, and computer vision may be best reserved for scenarios where contact-based sensing is unavailable or unsafe.

Data quality and the military training data challenge

Models trained on civilian populations may poorly generalize to military cohorts, whose physiology, gear, and injury mechanisms differ substantially. AI-enabled triage is dependent on large, high-quality, operationally relevant datasets. In military medicine, the scarcity of prospective, real-time casualty data remains a major limitation for model development and validation, underscoring the need for robust medical data ecosystems.56

Model lifecycle, retraining, and operational robustness

Operational validity of AI-enabled triage systems cannot be assumed to persist. Injury patterns, equipment, operational practices, and evacuation timelines evolve, requiring periodic retraining and revalidation. Without such mechanisms, performance degradation and confirmation loops may emerge, whereby biased training data may progressively reinforce decision patterns over time. Robust registries, standardized data governance, and independent oversight are therefore necessary to ensure that AI systems remain aligned with operational reality rather than historical data artefacts.

Human and Doctrinal Risks

Risk of over-reliance and deskilling

High-performing tools may inadvertently erode foundational triage skills or create dangerous dependency patterns. Automation bias—the tendency to over-trust algorithmic recommendations, particularly under stress or fatigue—has been consistently described in clinical AI use and may be exacerbated in LSCO environments.58

This risk is particularly salient for junior or isolated medics operating under hierarchical or time pressure, where algorithmic outputs may be perceived as authoritative rather than advisory.11,59

AI-enabled systems must therefore be designed to support, not replace, clinician judgment, and training must explicitly address appropriate override and uncertainty management. Systems must also allow graceful degradation when data streams, connectivity, or algorithms are unavailable.

Mediation of clinical perception through interfaces

Beyond loss of skills, the introduction of digital interfaces between caregivers and casualties may inadvertently attenuate direct clinical perception. Reliance on sensor-derived data or decision-support displays can reduce hands-on examination and contextual appraisal, occasionally leading to missed obvious clinical signs. Interfaces may narrow attention to displayed variables at the expense of holistic assessment under stress.60,61

AI “hallucination” phenomenon

The use of generative AI systems, particularly large language models, introduces the risk of so-called “hallucinations,” in which outputs may appear coherent and authoritative while being factually incorrect.62 Such errors are problematic when AI-generated summaries or recommendations are accepted without verification. This risk reflects the probabilistic nature of generative models and may be amplified under stress and time pressure. Mitigation, therefore, relies less on algorithmic perfection than on strict role definition, transparency of uncertainty, and maintaining human responsibility for interpretation and decision-making.

Doctrinal, ergonomic, and training integration

AI must integrate seamlessly into TCCC workflows, complementing rather than disrupting task sequences. Poorly designed interfaces risk increasing cognitive burden instead of reducing it. Interoperability with existing command and control and medical documentation systems remains a practical barrier.

A related operational risk is alarm fatigue. Poorly calibrated or overly sensitive alerting systems may saturate caregivers with nonactionable signals, degrading attention and trust rather than enhancing decision-making, and paradoxically worsening performance.63 This underscores the need for calibrated, hierarchical alerting strategies and continuous evaluation of alert relevance.

Finally, successful implementation depends on structured training addressing system capabilities, limitations, and appropriate modes of use; without such preparation, decision-support tools risk being misunderstood, misused, or rejected.

Strategic and Survivability Constraints

Beyond technical reliability, wearable sensing and wireless transmission raise operational survivability concerns in LSCO. Even low-power or intermittent emissions may be detected, geolocated, and exploited through communications intelligence or electronic support measures, potentially exposing medical personnel or casualty collection points to targeting. This dynamic imposes constraints on sensor placement, transmission frequency, and system architecture, reinforcing the need for ultra-low-emission modes, local processing, delayed transmission, or offline functionality in high-threat environments.

Unlike purely engineering challenges, these vulnerabilities cannot be resolved solely through improved algorithms or additional data. They require explicit doctrinal decisions regarding acceptable risk, operational trade-offs, and the conditions under which AI-enabled triage may be employed or restricted.

Evaluation, Accountability, and Governance

Beyond technical feasibility, AI-enabled triage systems must demonstrate measurable clinical and operational benefit. Assessment should extend beyond algorithmic accuracy and include outcomes relevant to LSCO, such as mortality, preventable deaths, over-triage and under-triage rates, time-to-intervention, caregiver cognitive workload, and system performance under DDIL constraints. Operational testing should also examine resilience, graceful degradation, and interoperability across echelons of care. Evaluation should not be limited to predictive performance but also encompass usability, robustness, and overall system effectiveness.

Responsibility for triage decisions must remain clearly defined. AI systems may structure information, generate alerts, or provide probabilistic risk estimates, but final authority must rest with designated medical personnel and, where applicable, operational command leadership. Clear override mechanisms, decision logging, and audit trails are essential, particularly when AI recommendations conflict with clinical judgment or operational priorities.

Governance frameworks must therefore address performance monitoring, bias auditing, retraining protocols, and mechanisms for postevent review. In high-stakes environments where triage decisions may later be scrutinized, transparency and traceability are not optional safeguards but prerequisites for operational legitimacy.

ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS

Ethical concerns are not external to operational constraints; they shape what is permissible, acceptable, and sustainable in battlefield medicine. Triage in LSCO is an inherently utilitarian task that must balance humanitarian obligations with operational necessity. AI does not remove this tension; it may formalize and amplify it.4,5

Normative Stability: the Ethical Promise of AI-enabled Triage?

AI-enabled triage may reduce interoperator variability, provide objective early warning, and accelerate time-critical decisions, while remote triage supported by unmanned systems may limit unnecessary exposure of medics. Rather than comparing algorithmic performance solely to individual clinician judgment, a more appropriate benchmark may be alignment with predefined clinical, humanitarian, and operational standards that should not fluctuate with individual judgment, fatigue, cultural background, or stress.64

In this perspective, the potential ethical value of AI does not primarily lie in outperforming humans, but in enforcing stability, proportionality, and transparency in decision-making under conditions where human variability is known to be substantial. When supported by robust data and clearly defined criteria, AI-enabled triage may enhance consistency without displacing human moral responsibility.

This does not eliminate moral responsibility, which must remain human, but it suggests that, under well-defined governance and oversight, AI-enabled triage could be viewed as ethically preferable to ad hoc human judgment in extreme mass-casualty contexts.

Algorithmic Opacity, Trust, and Responsibility

Deep learning models often lack transparent reasoning pathways, complicating trust and adoption in life-critical contexts. In triage, noninterpretable outputs may be clinically or ethically unacceptable.11 Systems should incorporate mechanisms, such as post hoc explainability techniques, that enhance transparency, traceability, and user understanding, particularly when influencing life-critical decisions.65,66

AI also may erode clinical autonomy or subtly pressure medics to follow algorithmic recommendations, potentially generating moral injury when outputs conflict with intuition.67 Responsibility becomes diffuse when decisions are influenced by opaque models.68 Clear doctrinal guidance is therefore required to define authority in cases where AI recommendations conflict with clinical judgment or operational priorities. AI must function as advisory decision support, with final authority resting with designated medical personnel and, where applicable, command leadership. Transparent logging and explicit chains of responsibility are essential to prevent diffusion of responsibility, particularly in anticipation of postevent investigation, peer review, or liability assessment. Future doctrinal and legal frameworks may need to clarify liability and review standards specific to AI-enabled triage decisions. In parallel, remote or autonomous triage risks depersonalizing care, and continuous physiological monitoring raises concerns regarding privacy, consent, and data governance.

Fairness, Bias, and Distributive Justice

Fairness considerations deserve particular attention in AI-enabled triage. While algorithmic systems may reduce random variability, they also risk formalizing inequities if trained on unrepresentative or biased datasets. In contrast to civilian settings, military triage involves populations with heterogeneous physiological profiles, training levels, and exposure patterns, making fairness a dynamic and context-dependent concept rather than a fixed metric. Moreover, without rigorous dataset curation and validation, AI risks embedding structural biases and generating inequitable prioritization.5 Ethical deployment therefore requires explicit governance choices regarding acceptable trade-offs between sensitivity, specificity, and distributive justice.11

Recent work on fairness in clinical AI highlights that no single model can simultaneously satisfy all fairness criteria—such as calibration, equal opportunity, demographic parity, or equalized odds—when the baseline risk differs across groups. Attempts to enforce one notion of fairness often degrade other metrics or overall calibration.69–71

In LSCO, where AI systems may operate across multiple triage points in real time, distributive justice acquires a system-level dimension. Networked decision-support systems could, in principle, aggregate data across collection points, account for resource availability and evacuation distance, and support more consistent force-wide triage standards. Rapid second opinions may be obtained through distributed systems, potentially informing adoption of expectant care when survivability estimates are extremely low and resources are constrained.

Military triage is also explicitly utilitarian by design. The ethical objective is not equal access to care, but maximization of survival or operational effectiveness under resource constraints. AI does not introduce this logic; it formalizes it. However, formalizing survivability modeling within algorithmic systems requires predefined thresholds, doctrinal endorsement, and transparency of criteria to maintain ethical legitimacy.

Ethical Governance as Operational Necessity

Frameworks such as the US Department of War and NATO AI Principles, as well as GREAT PLEA (Governability, Reliability, Equity, Accountability, Traceability, Privacy, Lawfulness, Empathy, and Eutonomy) for IA in healthcare, provide guidance on transparency, accountability, and human oversight.11,72 These principles are operational prerequisites for legitimacy, trust, and sustained adoption in LSCO.

CONCLUSIONS

AI has the potential to meaningfully enhance triage in LSCO by improving early detection, augmenting situational awareness, and enabling dynamic prioritization. The evolution toward a data-enabled battlefield—characterized by continuous physiological sensing, multimodal data streams, and predictive modeling—creates opportunities for more anticipatory and resilient medical systems.

However, these opportunities are inseparable from technical, human, and strategic constraints. The central challenge is not technological feasibility alone, but disciplined integration under operational, ethical, and doctrinal conditions that are more demanding than in any civilian context.

Within this framework, AI research and deployment should be considered strategic priorities for military health services—not as ends in themselves, but as tools to reduce cognitive burden and sustain vigilance under fire and over time.

If designed with robustness, transparency, and ethical integrity, AI-enabled systems may provide medics with a cognitive edge in LSCO. Yet this potential will only be realized through rigorous evaluation, continuous performance monitoring, bias auditing, and clearly defined chains of responsibility. In LSCO, governance is not an abstract safeguard but a prerequisite for operational legitimacy and sustained adoption.
