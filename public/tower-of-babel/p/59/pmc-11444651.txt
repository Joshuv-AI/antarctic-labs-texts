Role of immunotherapy in the overall survival of gastrointestinal cancer patients with liver metastases
Authors: Taoyuan M Beninato, Saber A Amin, Michael J Baine, Chi Lin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAim: To investigate the role of immunotherapy in the overall survival (OS) of gastrointestinal cancer patients who have liver metastases at the time of the primary site cancer diagnosis.Materials & methods: Survival outcome was compared between groups with immunotherapy and groups without immunotherapy.Results: Chemoimmunotherapy was associated with improved OS (hazard ratio [HR] 0.768; 95% CI 0.739–0.800; p < 0.001) compared with chemotherapy alone. Radiotherapy to the primary site plus chemoimmunotherapy was also associated with improved OS (HR 0.796; 95% CI 0.705–0.898; p < 0.001) compared to chemoradiation. Chemoimmunotherapy with radiotherapy to metastatic sites (except liver) was associated with improved OS (HR 0.771; 95% CI 0.706–0.842; p < 0.001) compared to chemoradiation.Conclusion: Immunotherapy plus chemotherapy or chemoradiation was associated with improved OS compared with chemotherapy or chemoradiation without immunotherapy.Keywords: : gastrointestinal cancer, immunotherapy, liver metastases, GI primary, National Cancer Database, overall survival

Introduction

It is well known that the liver is a common site of metastases [1–4]. The liver’s complex and abundant blood supply, direct drainage from the gastrointestinal (GI) system and immune properties are a few reasons for the high frequency of liver metastasis in GI cancers [1,5,6]. In a study published in 2020 by Horn et al., 5.14% of patients diagnosed with primary cancers also had liver metastasis [7]. In addition, for men ages 20–50, colon cancer was the most common diagnosis, along with liver metastases, followed by rectal, lung and pancreatic cancer. When stratified, most primary cancers with concurrent liver metastases came from GI cancers [7]. In many studies, the top three primary cancer sites for liver metastases are the pancreatic, colorectal (CRC) and pulmonary [2,7]. In CRC alone, the rate of liver metastasis can reach up to 30% at the first diagnosis [8]. The median survival of patients with GI cancer with liver-only metastases is 2–13 months [7]. Patients with hepatic versus non-hepatic metastasis had a worse 1-year survival outcome of 15.1% versus 24%, respectively [7].

The high frequency of GI cancers with liver metastases, with significant mortality, leads to a convincing reason to more effective and aggressive treatments. Currently, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network treatment guidelines for liver metastases from stage IV GI cancers involve a multitude of therapies, supported by clinical trials and studies. Treatments for this population of patients include different combinations of chemotherapy (CT), resection of the metastatic tumors with or without perioperative CT, resection of the primary tumor with/without preoperative CT or chemoradiation (CRT) to the primary cancer, radiotherapy (RT)/interventional radiation to the liver lesions, targeted therapy and the newer addition of immunotherapy [9–12]. Despite disease progression and variable treatment regimens, the outcomes are poor in many patients. The introduction of immunotherapy in treatment plans can be a changing mode of treatment to improve outcomes.

Immunotherapy has changed the treatment landscape of cancer patients. Ipilimumab (anti-CTLA) was approved in 2011 [13] and pembrolizumab (anti-PD1) was approved in 2014 [14] for metastatic melanoma. Since then, it has been approved for many cancers, including cancers of the GI tract, such as esophageal, gastroesophageal junction (GEJ), gastric, colorectal and liver [14,15]. Immunotherapy can be used with CT, RT, or CRT, as it may synergize with standard-of-care cancer treatments [16–21]. Although the use and approval of immunotherapy have been mainly in the metastatic setting, there is a lack of research on the efficacy of immunotherapy in real-world clinical settings for patients diagnosed with advanced GI cancers who have liver metastases at the time of diagnosis of primary cancer. The present study aimed to use the National Cancer Database (NCDB) and investigate the association of immunotherapy combined with CT or CRT with the overall survival (OS) of patients diagnosed with advanced cancers of the esophagus, stomach, small intestine, colorectal and rectosigmoid who also had liver metastases at the time of their primary cancer diagnosis. We only focused on these cancers because the treatments for these cancers are similar, which will aid in the interpretation of the results.

Methods

Data source: The data used for this analysis were extracted from the NCDB, a collaboration between the Commission on Cancer of the American College of Surgeons and the American Cancer Society. Nearly 70 percent of newly diagnosed cancer cases in the United States have been reported in NCDB. This study also used the de-identified files in the NCDB, so it was exempt from the Institutional Review Board.

Study population

The current study included patients with stage IV GI cancer who had liver metastases during their primary cancer diagnosis and were diagnosed between 2010 and 2016. None of the patients underwent definitive surgery for the primary tumor. The year 2010 was the first year when the NCDB started collecting information regarding liver metastases. Patients with missing data on treatments, such as surgery, RT, chemotherapy and immunotherapy, were excluded.

End point

The primary end point in this study was the OS of the patients, which was calculated from the date of diagnosis to the date of death or the last follow-up. Those who were alive or lost to follow-up were censored. The odds ratios for identifying the factors associated with receiving immunotherapy in the multivariable logistic analysis were also calculated.

Explanatory variables

The independent variables analyzed for OS were immunotherapy, immunotherapy plus CT and immunotherapy plus CRT. Immunotherapy plus RT was not analyzed because of the small sample size (n = 15). Age at diagnosis, race, sex, education, income, place of residence, hospital type, insurance status, year of diagnosis, Charlson/Deyo score and treatment type without immunotherapy were covariables.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive characteristics of immunotherapy were calculated as mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and frequency and percentage for categorical variables. Odds ratios (OR) from the multivariable logistic analysis were used to identify the likelihood of factors associated with receiving immunotherapy (OR>1 is more likely to receive immunotherapy). OS was analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared between the groups using log-rank statistics. Survival intervals were measured in months from the date of diagnosis to the date of death or last follow-up. The Cox proportional hazards model was used to determine the significant factors associated with OS and to estimate the hazard ratio of death along with the 95% CI. The multivariable analysis was adjusted for age, sex, race, neighborhood education level, neighborhood income level, place of residence (urban/rural), hospital type (academic/community), insurance status, Charleston/Deyo score, receipt of CT, immunotherapy or RT, histology, type of primary GI cancer, histology and year of diagnosis. All tests were two-sided, and P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

Results

In total, 42,097 patients with metastatic disease to the liver from GI primary cancers who did not receive definitive surgery for the primary tumor were included. In total, 5,987 (14.2%) patients received immunotherapy (Table 1). Among the patients who received CT (39,771) and RT (9,781), 14.79% (5,884/39,771) received CT plus immunotherapy and 8.67% (838/9,781) received RT plus immunotherapy (Table 1). Of the 9,781 patients who received CRT, 58.6%, 3.85% and 37.5% received RT to the primary sites, liver metastases and metastatic sites other than liver, respectively. It was observed that 8.02% (460/5,733), 25.7% (97/377) and 7.65% (281/3,671) of patients received immunotherapy plus CRT at the primary sites, liver metastases and metastatic sites other than liver, respectively (Table 1).

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, younger age at diagnosis, male sex, higher education level, having health insurance, receiving chemotherapy, diagnosis with squamous cell histology, adenocarcinoma histology (compared with other histology types), liver metastases-directed RT and no RT (compared with RT to metastatic sites other than liver or primary cancer site), were positively associated with the receipt of immunotherapy. The multivariable analysis was adjusted for age at diagnosis, sex, race, income, education, treatment facility, comorbidity score, year of diagnosis, chemotherapy, RT, primary cancer type and year of diagnosis. Patients living in a neighborhood with a low-education level were on average 10% less likely to receive immunotherapy than their counterparts living in a higher-education level area (OR: 0.904, 95% CI: 0.848–0.962; p = 0.002). Patients without health insurance were 18% less likely to receive immunotherapy compared with patients who had health insurance (OR: 0.816, 95% CI: 0.708–0.940; p = 0.005). Patients who did not receive chemotherapy were 49% less likely to receive immunotherapy than those who received chemotherapy (OR: 0.532, 95% CI: 0.424–0.667, p < 0.001). Compared with other histology types, patients diagnosed with adenocarcinoma were 1.52-times more likely (OR: 1.528, 95% CI: 1.372–1.701; p < 0.001), while patients with squamous cell carcinoma were 85% less likely (OR: 0.138, 95% CI: 0.075–0.253; p < 0.001) to receive immunotherapy. Compared with patients diagnosed with rectosigmoid colon cancer, patients with esophageal cancer, stomach cancer or small intestine cancer were less likely to receive immunotherapy (OR: 0.421, 95% CI: 0.361–0.491; p < 0.001, OR: 0.381, 95% CI: 0.329–0.442; p < 0.001 and OR: 0.303, 95% CI: 0.232–0.396; p < 0.001). The OR of the factors associated with immunotherapy is provided in (Table 2).

Based on Kaplan Meier curves, patients who received immunotherapy had significantly improved survival than those who did not, with a median OS benefit of 6.97 months [17.58 vs 10.61 months; 95% CI, p < 0.0001] in the entire study population (Figure 1). Patients who received CT plus immunotherapy had a significantly improved survival with median OS benefit of 6.12 months [17.68 vs 11.56 months; 95% CI, p < 0.0001] (Figure 2) compared with the group without immunotherapy. In patients who received liver-directed RT plus chemotherapy, there was no significantly improved survival seen with a median OS difference of 5.26 months [21.85 vs 16.59 months; p = 0.1078] between patients with or without immunotherapy (Figure 3A). In patients who received primary cancer site-directed RT plus chemotherapy, the addition of immunotherapy was associated with a significant improvement in survival, with a median OS benefit of 7.19 months [18.66 vs 11.47 months; 95% CI, p < 0.0001] (Figure 3B).

In the multivariable Cox proportional analysis, patients treated with immunotherapy had significantly better OS than those who did not receive immunotherapy (HR, 0.749; 95% CI 0.723–0.777; p < 0.001). Other factors associated with improved OS were younger age, non-white non-black race, primary rectal cancer and lower education (Table 3). Esophageal and stomach primary cancers, lack of chemotherapy and lower income were associated with worse survival (Table 3). Subset analyses revealed that chemotherapy plus immunotherapy was associated with a significantly improved OS (HR 0.768; 95% CI 0.739–0.800; p < 0.001) compared with CT alone (Table 4). RT to the primary site plus CT and immunotherapy was also associated with a significantly improved OS (HR 0.796; 95% CI 0.705–0.898; p < 0.001) compared with RT to the primary site with chemotherapy (Table 4). Chemotherapy with RT to metastatic sites except liver plus immunotherapy was associated with improved OS (HR 0.771; 95% CI 0.706–0.842; p < 0.001) compared with its counterpart without immunotherapy (Table 4). Interestingly, liver-directed RT plus CT and immunotherapy was not associated with improved OS compared with their counterparts without immunotherapy (HR 1.038; 95% CI 0.705–1.528; P = 0.85) (Table 4). None of the patients underwent RT without chemotherapy.

Discussion

Our study is the first to investigate the role of immunotherapy in combination with other cancer treatments in the OS of patients with GI cancer and liver metastases at the time of primary cancer diagnosis. We found that immunotherapy combined with CT or CRT was associated with improved OS in patients with GI liver metastases without definitive surgery at the primasite. RT to the primary site plus chemoimmunotherapy was also associated with improved OS compared with CRT alone. Chemoimmunotherapy with RT to metastatic sites (other than the liver) was also associated with improved OS.

The improved OS associated with CT plus immunotherapy and CRT plus immunotherapy may be due to the synergistic interaction between immunotherapy and these treatments. It has been shown that chemotherapy can recruit dendritic cell and reduce Treg cells [22]. CT can also induce immunogenic cell death, which promotes antigen presentation and leads to the priming of tumor-specific T cells [23]. This function can convert noninflammatory tumors to inflammatory cancers and may lead to synergy with checkpoint inhibitors, especially PD-1 and PD-L1 [16,23–25]. CT and RT also cause the release of neoantigens and cytokines that trigger T-cell responses to attack tumor cells. Through the abscopal effect, RT causes the release of tumor-associated antigens, which can stimulate a tumor-specific immune response, allowing immune cells (T cells) to recognize and attack both the primary tumor and metastatic disease in autovaccination [19–21]. Through these mechanisms, immunotherapy could have great efficacy when combined with chemotherapy, RT, or both.

Our findings are similar to those reported for immunotherapy plus CT in clinical trials for advanced GI cancers [15,26,27]. In a randomized Phase III trial, 970 patients with previously untreated, unresected advanced, recurrent, or metastatic esophageal squamous cell carcinoma were treated with nivolumab (PD-1 inhibitor) plus CT, nivolumab (PD-1 inhibitor) plus ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4 inhibitor), or CT alone. The results showed that both first-line nivolumab plus CT and nivolumab plus ipilimumab improved OS compared with the CT alone arm [26]. Similar findings were observed in the Checkmate 649 trial looking at first-line immunotherapy plus CT, immunotherapy plus ipilimumab and CT alone in patients with advanced gastric, GEJ and esophageal adenocarcinoma [15]. Pembrolizumab (PD-1 inhibitor) with CT has also shown its benefits over CT alone in esophageal cancers in the Keynote 590 clinical trial [27]. These studies were foundational to the approval of CT plus immunotherapy for the treatment of advanced esophageal cancers.

Only a few clinical studies have focused on chemoimmunotherapy for treating GI liver metastases. Nevertheless, we can postulate these outcomes by examining how advanced GI cancers react to immunotherapy combination treatments. A preclinical trial showed improved colorectal liver metastasis (CRCLM) outcomes after chemoimmunotherapy in CRCLM mice 24. Nano-Folox/5-Fu was assessed with anti-PD-L1 mAb in mice, which showed increased treatment activity and improved survival. It is thought that the efficacy is due to turning the CRCLM from a non-immune response to a responsive response with the help of CT. A reduction in liver metastasis and longer survival were observed in mice [24]. A retrospective cohort study of gastric cancer patients with synchronous liver metastases found that compared to chemotherapy alone group, the immunochemotherapy group had a higher overall response rate and a higher disease control rate (59.4 vs. 44.4%; p = 0.029 and 71.9 vs. 49.2%; p = 0.036) respectively. In addition, patients who received chemoimmunotherapy had better OS and PFS compared with those who received chemotherapy alone (HR: 0.23, 95% CI: 0.09–0.62; p = 0.003 and HR: 0.46, 95% CI: 0.25–0.86; p = 0.015) [28]. These findings can augment our findings of improved OS with chemoimmunotherapy in the target patient population.

A combination of RT and immunotherapy is being utilized. RT has been theorized to increase antigen presentation, release cytokines, act synergistically with immunotherapy and can activate an abscopal effect, leading to its therapeutic goals in metastatic GI cancers [17–21,29,30]. These results have been shown to be important in preclinical and clinical trials testing RT with immunotherapy in advanced GI cancers. This strategy of synergizing immunotherapy with RT is like that of chemoimmunotherapy. Our data showed that liver-directed RT plus chemotherapy was not associated with an improved OS. However, liver-directed RT alone was associated with significantly improved OS in the multivariate COX regression analysis in our study. Studies with similar results have hypothesized that the lack of improved OS is secondary to the lack of tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte activity [29], failure of the abscopal effect [20], or less responsive GI cancers (microsatellite stability vs. high microsatellite instability). These reasons can explain why liver-directed RT with chemoimmunotherapy showed no OS improvement in this study and other studies. These are barriers that require further research to optimize combined immunotherapy.

Based on the results of this study, multiple directions could be pursued. First, prospective clinical trials and analyses of immunotherapy treatment in metastatic GI cancers for patients who did not undergo primary resection of their cancer must be conducted to validate our findings. Furthermore, our analysis cannot adequately explain why liver-directed RT combined with chemoimmunotherapy was the only combination not significantly associated with improved OS. This finding should be explored further using other databases and preclinical models. As immunotherapy continues to expand, it would also be helpful to analyze more updated NCDB data between 2016 and 2022 once this becomes publicly available.

The current study has the advantage of a large sample size, which allowed for adjustments of key patient and tumor characteristics in the analysis. Additionally, the patients were stratified by the absence of definitive surgery. However, the study is not without limitations, which include a lack of information about the cause of death, limited information about the use of multi-agent CT regimens, and absence of data on the type and combination of immunotherapy used. Additionally, the database does not include information on the microsatellite instability status of patients who are more likely to respond to immunotherapy. Based on our knowledge and clinical experience, most of the patients will receive multi-agent chemotherapy. Also, among patients who received immunotherapy, the majority will receive immune checkpoint inhibitors. These two limitations are still worthy of further investigation. Nevertheless, our study is the first comprehensive analysis investigating the role of immunotherapy combined with CT and CRT in the survival of GI cancer patients with liver metastases at the time of primary cancer diagnosis.

Conclusion

In the current comprehensive analysis of NCDB, we found that immunotherapy was associated with improved OS compared with no immunotherapy. CT plus immunotherapy was associated with improved OS compared with CT alone. RT to the primary cancer site plus chemotherapy and immunotherapy improved survival compared with chemoradiation without immunotherapy. RT to metastatic sites other than liver metastases plus chemotherapy and immunotherapy was associated with improved OS compared with chemoradiation without immunotherapy. This study can encourage physicians to add immunotherapy to increase survival in one of the highest-mortality cancer groups and suggest that clinical trials should be conducted to examine the effects of combining immunotherapy with standard treatments in patients with advanced GI cancers.
