In vitro multi-organ invasion model for exploring cardiac resistance to cancer metastasis
Authors: Amid Shakeri, Dhana Abdo, Matthew Ho Cheong Lei, Sargol Okhovatian, Garrett F. Beeghly, Anna Maria Popovic, Richard Jiang, Chuan Liu, Karl T. Wagner, Daniel Vosoughi, Jennifer Kieda, Jason E. Fish, Milica Radisic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite the systemic spread of cancer, the ventricular myocardium is one of the least common sites of metastasis—a phenomenon that remains poorly understood. To examine this, we develop the Multi-organ Invasion Device (MInD), an organ-on-a-chip platform that enables multi-organ culture under flow. Organ compartments are connected in MInD using PermeoTubes—3D-printed porous conduits that support cancer cell intravasation, migration, and extravasation. In dual-organ devices, where highly aggressive breast cancer cells are co-cultured with either hepatic or cardiac tissue, invasion into cardiac tissue is significantly suppressed relative to hepatic co-culture. Importantly, in cardiac–hepatic–cancer tri-culture, the presence of cardiac tissue reduces overall invasion, with cancer cells preferentially migrating toward hepatic compartments. Cytokine profiling and RNA sequencing reveal that cardiac co-culture suppresses cell metastasis and invasion, while inducing immune activation. Overall, this platform presents an approach for uncovering organ-specific drivers of metastasis, accelerating future discovery of metastasis-inhibiting therapies.

Introduction

Metastasis, the spread of cancer cells from a primary tumor to distant organs, is responsible for over 90% of cancer-related deaths1. Metastasis involves cancer cell extravasation from the primary site, intravasation into the vasculature, and eventual colonization of distant tissues. A critical early step in metastasis is cell-extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, where cancer-associated fibroblasts and tumor cells alter the stromal architecture, increasing porosity and forming aligned collagen tracks that guide invasion2. In triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), which often originates in the terminal duct lobular units (TDLUs) of the breast, cancer cells are initially confined within the ductal epithelium3. As the tumor progresses, the surrounding ECM becomes highly disorganized and exhibits fractal-like porosity, which becomes more pronounced in later-stage tumors4. This dynamic and heterogeneous ECM topology facilitates interstitial migration of cancer cells5.

While many organs, such as the liver6, lungs7, and bones8, are frequently affected by metastasis, the ventricular myocardium exhibits an unusual resistance to metastatic colonization9. This resistance, observed across various cancer types10, suggests the existence of unique microenvironmental11, mechanical12, or biochemical factors13 within cardiac tissue that inhibit metastatic spread. Understanding these mechanisms could pave the way for novel therapeutic approaches to the prevention and treatment of metastasis. Despite its clinical significance, cardiac resistance to metastasis remains one of the least explored areas in cancer research. Notably, metastasis studies often focus on organs that are highly susceptible to secondary tumors14, leaving the mechanisms underlying cardiac resistance largely unexplored.

Traditional metastasis models, including animal studies and 2D cell cultures14–16, are limited in their ability to replicate the dynamic interplay of biological and mechanical forces found in the human heart. While in vivo models allow for systemic interactions17, fundamental biological differences between mice and humans, such as differences in organ-specific gene expression, immune systems, metabolism, tumor kinetics and tumor size, limit their suitability for accurately modeling human cancer metastasis18. Moreover, 2D in vitro cultures fail to replicate the three-dimensional architecture and biochemical gradients present in tissues, further limiting their applicability19. Recently, advances in organ-on-chip (OOC)20 and 3D bioprinting technologies21 have enabled more sophisticated in vitro platforms capable of replicating key physiological features, including shear stress, dynamic fluid flow, multicellular 3D architecture, and ECM interactions19–22. However, most of these systems are designed to model more common sites of metastasis, such as the liver23 or lungs24 and are not designed to study the unique properties of cardiac tissue.

An ideal in vitro metastasis model should capture the key steps of the metastatic cascade, including the emergence of heterogeneous, fractal-like porosity of cancer-associated ECM. To recapitulate these processes, organ-on-a-chip systems require inter-compartmental structures that replicate the structural and mechanical complexity of native invasion pathways.

In this study, we developed a Multi-organ Invasion Device (MInD), a versatile open-well plate platform designed to model the tumor metastatic environment and investigate cardiac resistance to cancer metastasis (Fig. 1a). We set out to create a tubular structure to connect individual organ compartments in MInD with the following properties: 1) multi-scale porosity, as in the native ECM, 2) permeability, and 3) mechanical stability with physiologically relevant properties. An extrusion-based, 3D-printed viscoelastic conduit made from granular polymeric ink, termed PermeoTube, was engineered to provide a tunable pathway for cell migration and biochemical communication between tissue compartments. Using this setup, a central compartment cultured with breast cancer cells was connected to a cardiac compartment or a hepatic compartment, a common site of breast cancer metastasis, used as a control6. We performed cytokine analysis and RNA sequencing and found that in co-cultures of cancer cells with cardiac tissue, pro-metastatic and immunosuppressive cytokines were broadly downregulated, whereas immune-activating signals, antigen-presentation pathways via MHC class II, and pro-apoptotic gene programs were upregulated, relative to co-cultures of cancer cells with hepatic tissue. Together, these findings underscore how our tunable, biomimetic multi-tissue platform, MInD, can recapitulate tissue-specific metastasis under controlled conditions. Results

MInD fabrication using granular polymer 3D printing to produce viscoelastic tubes with multi-scale porosity

The Multi-organ Invasion Device (MInD) was designed to support the culture and enable interactions between several different tissue types, such as cardiac, breast cancer, and hepatic, within the same system, enabling the study of cancer metastasis to different tissues (Fig. 1a). When placed on a rocker, the device generates gravity-driven flow across the tubes, mimicking interstitial fluid dynamics found in vivo (Fig. 1b). This configuration facilitates key steps of the metastatic cascade: cancer cells seeded in the central well can intravasate into the PermeoTube, migrate through its multi-scale porous structure, and extravasate into adjacent tissue wells.

The PermeoTube was fabricated from poly(octamethylene maleate (anhydride) citrate) (POMaC), a biodegradable, biocompatible polyester derived from citric acid, maleic anhydride, and 1,8-octanediol that offers tunable mechanical properties25–27 (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 1a). Proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy identified distinct peaks that align with the expected chemical groups in the POMaC pre-polymer prepared via a one-pot polycondensation reaction, confirming that the correct POMaC pre-polymer was obtained (Fig. 1d).

Multi-scale porosity in PermeoTube was achieved using extrusion 3D printing of a granular pre-polymer ink. The ink was generated by emulsifying the pre-polymer with a porogen, surfactant, and photoinitiator. (Fig. 1e). The size distribution of POMaC granules was influenced by the stirring rate during emulsification (Fig. 1f, g), with increasing stirring speeds from 400 to 1200 rpm producing smaller granule diameters and varying polydispersity. UV-crosslinked flat membranes were used to investigate multi-scale porosity resulting from different granules (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig. 1b, c). The granules fabricated at 1000 rpm produced a controlled size distribution within the 2-14 µm range (Fig. 1f) and yielded finer and more densely packed pore networks (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d). Fractality of pore structure was evident in all membrane conditions, with a representative fractal dimension (Df) of ~1.76 for membranes produced from granules emulsified at 1000 rpm (Supplementary Fig. 1e). This stirring condition was selected for all the subsequent characterizations and MInD fabrication.

Cultivation of dermal fibroblasts on the multi-scale porous structures created from POMaC granules, non-porous bulk POMaC, and standard tissue culture wells as controls, demonstrated a comparable cell density (Supplementary Fig. 2a) and cell structure (Supplementary Fig. 2b) confirming the biocompatibility of the multi-scale porous structures.

To fabricate porous tubular structures, we employed a coaxial extrusion approach (Fig. 1i, j). Flow rates of Pluronic and granular POMaC inks at the inner and outer gauges were systematically varied (Supplementary Table 1), with 900–950 µL h−1 (POMaC) and 650 µL h−1 (Pluronic) identified as optimal for producing well-formed PermeoTubes (Fig. 1k). Fluorescence imaging further highlighted the porous architecture formed by the fused granules in PermeoTubes (Fig. 1l). To improve fabrication scalability, PermeoTubes can also be fabricated through automated 3D printing using a modified coaxial extrusion setup with an in situ curing system (Fig. 1m and Supplementary Fig. 3a). This setup enables reproducible fabrication in both simple and complex geometries (Supplementary Fig. 3b, c and Supplementary Movie 1).

The MInD platform was fabricated by polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) soft-lithography using custom 3D-printed molds to generate a five-well base structure. Pre-fabricated PermeoTubes were inserted into designated grooves, and polycarbonate (PC) walls were subsequently bonded to the PDMS scaffold and sealed using semi-cured PDMS. The final MInD platform was comprised of five wells connected solely by a PermeoTube running along the base of the device (Fig. 1n and Supplementary Fig. 4a–d). To support 3D tissue formation in MInD, smaller wells (referred to as compartments) were embedded within well 2–4 in the design (Fig. 1n and Supplementary Fig. 4b). The surrounding larger wells served as media reservoirs to maintain appropriate culture conditions.

Biomimetic mechanical properties of permeoTubes

Flow sweep measurements were performed to assess both viscosity and shear stress as functions of shear rate of the granular POMaC ink and its bulk POMaC pre-polymer ink control (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 5a). Bulk POMaC showed stable viscosity with limited shear-thinning, while granular ink exhibited pronounced shear-thinning and lower shear stress, indicating reduced flow resistance and suitability for extrusion-based 3D printing. Frequency sweep tests were conducted to evaluate the viscoelastic properties of POMaC in bulk and granular forms (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 5b). Both formulations exhibited predominantly viscous behavior (G″ > G′), but granular POMaC showed a greater frequency-dependent increase in elasticity than bulk POMaC, narrowing the G′-G″ gap at higher frequencies. Amplitude sweep measurements further highlighted the mechanical differences between these conditions (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 5c). SEM examination of the PermeoTubes revealed a rough outer surface and interconnected pores across the wall (Fig. 2d–g), resulting in permeability of the tubes (Fig. 2h). Larger pores often contained smaller secondary pores, indicating fractal organization (Fig. 2f) with a Df of 1.60 ± 0.13, consistent with values reported for breast tumor ECM at early to intermediate progression stages4. Pore sizes were skewed toward 2–4 µm, with occasional pores >30 µm (Fig. 2i). Cross-sectional imaging showed a well-defined lumen and ~55 µm wall thickness (Fig. 2g). A FITC-dextran (70 kDa) diffusion assay, with the dye introduced into the middle well, illustrated a rapid permeability of PermeoTubes, with fluorescence dye signal appearing in adjacent wells within 5 minutes and distribution across all wells by 60 min after dye introduction (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Movie 2). Degradation assessment also showed that the PermeoTubes were relatively stable under physiological conditions (PBS at 37 °C) (Fig. 2j). Gravity-driven flow within the PermeoTube under rocking conditions was quantified by measuring the volume in each well at the maximum left and maximum right tilt positions. The corresponding volume shifts across the five wells at these angles are shown in Supplementary Fig. 6. Based on the inlet and outlet displacements, the average oscillatory flow rate was 4.5 ± 2.6 µL min⁻¹ (mean ± SD), indicating reproducible bidirectional fluid transport across devices.

From the linear region of the stress-strain curves obtained during the uniaxial tensile testing (Fig. 2k), the apparent Young’s modulus of the PermeoTube was calculated to be approximately 87 kPa. This value falls within the physiological range of native human blood vessels (20–200 kPa)28. Tube elasticity is further visualized in Supplementary Movie 3. Under compression, the tubes displayed a clear hysteresis loop in the loading-unloading cycles (Fig. 2l), indicative of energy dissipation and viscoelastic behavior (Supplementary Movie 4).

Cardiac secretome inhibits cancer cell metastasis in MInD dual-organ co-culture

We first validated MInD device functionality using a simplified 3-well configuration, in which either GFP+ breast cancer cells or GFP+ dermal fibroblasts (as a negative control) were seeded in the central compartment (well 2 in the reduced layout) (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). Over 4 days, cancer cells proliferated and invaded adjacent wells (Supplementary Fig. 7c), although migration was occasionally asymmetric between inlet and outlet compartments, consistent with stochastic invasion dynamics under gravity-driven flow (Supplementary Fig. 7d). In contrast, fibroblasts remained confined to the central well in all devices, confirming effective compartmental isolation and absence of inter-well leakage (Supplementary Fig. 7e, f).

We next evaluated three culture conditions in the MInD device: 1) cancer monoculture in a 4-well setup, 2) cancer–hepatic co-culture, and 3) cancer–cardiac co-culture, both in the 5-well configuration, with an empty fibrin gel in the remaining middle wells (Supplementary Fig. 8 and Fig. 3a, b). A common medium (i3M) was selected to accommodate all cell types, prioritizing the sensitivity of cardiomyocytes. With the culture media validated, we monitored cancer cell migration in the MInD devices. Fluorescence imaging showed progressive proliferation and compaction of cancer tissue across all configurations. In the monoculture, few cells entered the acellular fibrin compartment by day 6, increasing markedly by day 10 (Supplementary Fig. 8). Cancer–hepatic co-cultures exhibited greater invasion, with numerous cells in hepatic and fibrin compartments by day 6 and extensive spread by day 10 (Fig. 3a). In contrast, cancer–cardiac co-cultures showed negligible invasion throughout the 10-day culture (Fig. 3b, c). Metastatic incidence differed across conditions on day 10, with the lowest frequency observed in cancer–cardiac devices (Fig. 3d). While cardiac tissue area decreased by 43% throughout the experiment, beating rate remained stable, indicating preserved functional activity in these devices (Supplementary Fig. 10 and Supplementary Movie 5).

Cancer cell invasion was further visualized, and tissue identity was confirmed within the MInD platform by confocal fluorescence imaging (Fig. 3e–h). Cancer cells were observed surrounding the PermeoTube structure, indicating their confinement and active migration into the porous matrix (Fig. 3e, f). Cryosectioning of the PermeoTube followed by fluorescence imaging of the cancer compartment further confirmed the presence of breast cancer cells within the porous wall and inside the lumen, demonstrating that inter-compartmental migration occured through the PermeoTubes (Supplementary Fig. 11).

The inhibitory effect of the cardiac secretome was evaluated using a scratch migration assay with GFP+ breast cancer cells (Fig. 3i). Exposure to cardiomyocyte-conditioned medium resulted in a significant reduction in gap closure compared to fresh medium at both 18 and 24 h (Fig. 3j). Moreover, a clear and a significant dose-dependent response was observed at 24 h, in the range from 0 to 25% of conditioned medium. Above 25%, no further inhibition was observed, indicating that the effect was at its maximum (Fig. 3k and Supplementary Fig. 12).

To confirm that the cardiac secretome inhibitory effect extends beyond a single breast cancer cell line, we also tested its effects on lung carcinoma (Supplementary Fig. 13) and colon adenocarcinoma (Supplementary Fig. 14), cancer types known to metastasize in humans. Treatment of lung carcinoma with 100% conditioned medium resulted in a marked reduction in migration into the scratch area compared to fresh medium controls (Supplementary Fig. 13b). In addition, a significant decrease in total cell number and Ki67+ proliferating cells under conditioned medium exposure was observed (Supplementary Fig. 13c), indicating that the cardiac secretome suppressed both migratory and proliferative responses in lung carcinoma cells. Similarly, scratch assays were performed using colon adenocarcinoma cells (Supplementary Fig. 14a). Owing to their relatively slow migratory behavior, gap closure could not be reliably quantified. However, exposure to 100% conditioned medium led to noticeable cell detachment at 48 h (Supplementary Fig. 14b). At 48 h, immunostaining showed that cardiac conditioned media significantly reduced total cell number, and thus absolute Ki67+ cell number, relative to fresh media, while the fraction of Ki67+ cells remained similar between groups.

To further validate these findings in a more physiologically relevant setting, an ex vivo co-culture model was established using murine ventricular explants positioned in transwell inserts above scratched breast cancer monolayers (Supplementary Fig. 15a). In both conditions, the scratch gap was nearly completely closed by 18 h, likely due to the presence of serum in the medium to support the ventricular explants (Supplementary Fig. 15b). However, quantitative analysis of DAPI and Ki67 staining at 48 h showed significant differences in cell numbers (Supplementary Fig. 15c). Cultures without cardiac tissue exhibited higher total cell numbers and as a result Ki67⁺ proliferating cells, forming densely packed, multilayered regions, whereas co-culture with ventricular explants resulted in reduced cell density and a more uniform monolayer.

Cytokine profiling of the media collected from MInDs identified 143 differentially expressed cytokines in cancer-hepatic compared to cancer monoculture, with 120 downregulated and 23 upregulated (Supplementary Fig. 16). Of the 20 most significantly regulated cytokines, 18 were downregulated in the cancer-hepatic group, including inflammatory and immune-related factors, such as IL-3Rα/IL-3, MMP-7, Granzyme A, and IL-1 receptors, while proteins, such as Nidogen-129 and IGFBP-130 were upregulated, implicated in supporting ECM stability and cell survival29. Comparing cancer–cardiac to cancer monoculture conditions showed 80 differentially expressed cytokines (Fig. 3l, m). Among the 65 downregulated cytokines, 15 were interleukins, including top pro-inflammatory factors IL-17C31, IL-332, and IL-10 Rβ33. Upregulated cytokines included a mix of anti-metastatic (LIF Rα34, CRIM135) and pro-metastatic (GHR36, MIP-1α37) signals, as well as angiogenesis-regulating Angiopoietin-238.

Cardiac microenvironment deters cancer invasion in cardiac-cancer-hepatic tri-culture

To assess cardiac-mediated suppression of metastasis in the presence of hepatic tissue, we established a tri-tissue model incorporating cardiac, hepatic, and cancer tissues in MInD (Fig. 4a). Confocal imaging verified breast cancer cell localization adjacent to the PermeoTube and confirmed cardiac (cTnT + ) and hepatic (albumin + ) tissue identity (Fig. 4b–d). By day 10, cancer invasion was significantly lower in the tri-culture compared to cancer-hepatic devices, with no significant increase over the cancer culture period (Fig. 4e). Although not statistically significant, preferential cancer cell migration to the hepatic over the cardiac compartment was evident in the same devices (Fig. 4a, f). This trend was further supported by quantification of metastatic incidence within the same devices, which showed a higher percentage of colonization events in the hepatic compared to the cardiac compartment (Fig. 4g). Cytokine profiling of tri-culture relative to cancer-hepatic devices revealed 54 significantly upregulated cytokines with none significantly downregulated (Fig. 4h, i). Upregulated factors in the tri-culture included tumor cell adhesion/trafficking mediators (CXCR3, CCR9, CNTF)39–41, ECM/vascular remodeling proteins (MMP-2, MMP-25, Angiopoietin-1)39–41, and immunomodulatory cytokines (GASP-1, MFG-E8, Activin A)42,43. These findings indicate distinct secretome shifts associated with the presence of cardiac tissue.

Comparing the tri-culture system with cancer-cardiac devices identified 107 significantly upregulated and 15 significantly downregulated differentially expressed cytokines (Fig. 4j). Heatmap visualization (Fig. 4k) showed broad cytokine upregulation in tri-culture. Notably, chemotactic factors associated with metastatic behavior (Eotaxin-3, Eotaxin-2, CCL28, CXCL13, CXCR3, and CXCR6)44–48 were elevated, along with activation of TGF-β superfamily (Gremlin-1, Activin A, and BMP-8) known to facilitate epithelial-to-mesenchymal (EMT) and stromal adaptation49–51. IGF-I, Insulin receptor, FGF-13-1B and FGF-7 were also upregulated, which are all implicated in PI3K-AKT/MAPK signaling, metabolic reprogramming and cytoskeletal stability52–54. The tri-culture secretome contained increased levels of proinflammatory cytokines including IL-6, TNF-α, IL-13, IL-4R, and the IL-17B/IL-17BR pair55–57, compared to cancer-cardiac co-culture. These cytokines have been shown to activate STAT3/NF-κB pathways and enhance survival, invasive capacity and matrix-remodeling58–60. These results are consistent with an increased trend in metastasis in the tri-culture devices relative to the cancer-cardiac co-culture (Supplementary Fig. 17c).

Cytokine profiling of cancer–cardiac versus cancer–hepatic co-cultures in the MInD platform revealed significant downregulation of pro-angiogenic molecules Angiogenin68, Endoglin69, and VEGF70, alongside upregulation of angiogenic inhibitors Robo471 and Angiopoietin-2, which promote vessel regression and endothelial cell death in the absence of VEGF72 (Fig. 5i,j). However, a marked increase in pro-angiogenic receptors TIE-1 and VEGF R2 was also observed. Among the most strongly downregulated factors in cancer–cardiac co-culture were Nidogen-1, IGFBP-1, IL-1 R3, and Endothelin, proteins associated with epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, matrix invasion, and other pro-metastatic programs29,73–75. Concurrently with the RNA sequencing data, secretome analysis also revealed upregulation of immune-activating and recruitment factors including BAFF-R and CD40L (B cell survival/antigen-specific activation)76, MIP-1α, MIP-2, CXCR3, and CCR8 (macrophage, neutrophil, and effector T cell recruitment))77–79, M-CSF (monocyte–macrophage differentiation)80, and TNF-R1/R2 (sensitized response to TNF-α)81 in cancer-cardiac cocultures. Adhesion molecules ICAM-2 and ALCAM, which facilitate leukocyte binding and transmigration, were also elevated82,83, although their correlation with cancer cell invasiveness remains unclear in breast cancer cell lines84. Downregulated factors included IL-17B and IFN-γ (pro-inflammatory amplification)85,86, and the growth-supportive signals FGF-7 and IGFBP-1, which promote epithelial cell proliferation, migration, and survival73,87.

Transcriptomic comparison of cancer–cardiac relative to cancer monoculture tissues revealed 11,724 overlapping mRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 18c). GO analysis showed reduced oxidative metabolism and substrate binding, alongside increased humoral immune response, complement activation, and enrichment of blood microparticles, platelet alpha granule, ECM structures, cytokine, and chemokine activity (Supplementary Fig. 18d, e). Exposure of cancer tissues to the cardiac microenvironment led to 166 significantly upregulated and 25 downregulated genes compared to cancer monoculture (Supplementary Fig. 18f). Upregulated genes included angiogenesis-related (VEGFA, VWF, TIE1, TGFBI, ITGB3), ECM remodeling (MMP1, MMP10, COL11A2, CXCL1-3), and immune/stress response genes (C1R, C4A, NFKBIZ, GSDMB) (Supplementary Fig. 18g).

Finally, transcriptomic comparison of cancer–hepatic versus cancer monoculture resulted in 11,623 shared mRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 19c). GO analysis showed reduced metabolic and hormonal reprogramming, downregulated immune processes, such as MHC class II assembly and antigen binding, and enrichment of angiogenesis, extracellular structure organization, and endothelial proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 19d, e). ECM-related terms were present in both upregulated and downregulated GO categories. However, these terms were more frequently enriched among the upregulated genes, reflecting a noticeable distribution pattern in the dataset. Hepatic co-culture induced upregulation of angiogenesis-related genes (MDK, ITGB3, SLIT2), complement components (C4A, CFB), and downregulation of migration inhibitors (DUSP1, PIK3R3) and immune recruitment genes (CX3CL1, HLA-DRA, TLR2) (Supplementary Fig. 19f, g).

To further delineate the mechanism, we turned our attention to highly upregulated genes in cancer cells in the presence of cardiac co-culture vs hepatic co-culture (Fig. 5e). One of these was ADAMTS9, a zinc-dependent, secreted enzyme that breaks down ECM components like versican. ADAMTS9 is also found in the pathways associated with enrichment of negative regulation of migration (Fig. 6a, b). To investigate whether ADAMTS9 contributes functionally to altered adhesive behavior in the cardiac microenvironment, siRNA-mediated knockdown of ADAMTS9 was performed in breast cancer cells and verified with qPCR (Fig. 6d). Cancer cells were subsequently exposed to cardiomyocyte conditioned or fresh media (Fig. 6e), and early cell attachment was quantified. In scrambled control cells, conditioned medium significantly increased cell adhesion compared to fresh media (Fig. 6f). In contrast, ADAMTS9 knockdown significantly reduced cell attachment in the conditioned medium relative to scrambled controls. Cell adhesion in fresh media was also slightly reduced after ADAMTS9 knockdown. These findings indicate that ADAMTS9 expression contributes to cardiac-mediated enhancement of cancer cell adhesion.

Discussion

Low incidence of cancer metastasis to the ventricular myocardium could offer mechanistic insight for development of new therapies, yet it remains incredibly challenging to study in vivo due to complex systemic interactions. This study introduces the Multi-organ Invasion Device (MInD), a metastasis-on-a-chip platform that enables multi-tissue co-culture under gravity-driven flow. Central to this platform are the PermeoTubes, biomimetic tubular structures with multi-scale porosity that interconnect tissue compartments to enable cancer cell migration to distal tissues to simulate the metastatic process. The PermeoTubes exhibit elasticity and viscoelastic properties that approximate native soft tissues and blood vessels28. Quantification of their porous architecture showed a fractal dimension comparable to that of breast tumor ECM, indicating that the tubes recapitulate the hierarchical complexity of remodeled cancer ECM4 and enable physiologically relevant molecular transport and invasion dynamics88.

These distinctive physical properties arise from the granular polyester POMaC ink used to 3D print the PermeoTubes, where UV crosslinking binds adjacent granules together while leaving interconnected voids. This architecture could enable viscoelastic behavior, with granules contributing to elastic energy storage and the intervening voids facilitating internal rearrangement and stress dissipation.

Controlled granular materials have been produced and 3D printed from a number of hydrogels, but 3D printing polyester granules is more challenging89–92. Moreover, the intrinsic elasticity of POMaC provides sufficient structural stability to maintain tubular integrity, while its modulus remains within the range of native soft tissues—properties that are difficult to achieve with hydrogels93. Several groups have investigated mechanical modifications, such as covalent inter-particle bonding, to increase the mechanical strength of granular hydrogel materials; however, the resulting moduli are generally still lower than those we obtain with polymer granules94. In addition, we used bulk emulsification to create the granular POMaC, offering significantly higher throughput and an easily tunable range of granule sizes compared to droplet microfluidic techniques95.

Using this engineered platform, we demonstrated that breast cancer invasion differs markedly depending on the multi-organ microenvironment. We intentionally employed the highly metastatic TNBC cell line MDA-MB-231 to challenge the cardiac compartment under aggressive metastatic conditions. An endothelial lining was intentionally omitted, noting that the tube was designed to function as a perfusable, multi-scale porous conduit modeling cancer-associated ECM rather than vascular intravasation.

Cancer cells were found to preferentially migrate into hepatic tissues, while consistently failing to invade cardiac tissues. Even when cultured with both cardiac and hepatic tissue in a cancer-cardiac-hepatic tri-culture system, cancer metastasis was significantly reduced relative to cancer-hepatic co-culture and appeared to exhibit a directional preference toward hepatic tissue within the device. In 2D assays, cardiac-conditioned media significantly reduced proliferation in breast cancer, lung carcinoma, and colon adenocarcinoma cells. A dose-dependent effect was observed across 0–25% conditioned media, although migration was not fully suppressed at any dose, indicating room for further improvement through additional mechanisms. Ex vivo assays using left ventricular slices likewise suppressed breast cancer cell proliferation in 2D. The consistent lack of cancer cell translocation in the presence of cardiac tissue in MInD, together with reduced proliferation in 2D experiments, suggests that cardiac tissue may secrete bioactive molecules—such as exosomes, cytokines, or ECM proteins—that actively inhibit cancer cell migration.

Secretome analysis revealed that both cardiac and hepatic co-cultures exerted immunomodulatory effects compared to cancer monoculture, including downregulation of immune-activating and pro-inflammatory cytokines. In the hepatic condition, this was accompanied by increased ECM remodeling and survival signals, consistent with a more permissive metastatic niche. In contrast, cardiac co-culture induced a mixed profile, with simultaneous upregulation of pro- and anti-metastatic factors. This juxtaposition suggests that cardiac-derived cues may exhibit growth-inhibitory pressures while the tumor counter-responds with factors that preserve its invasive potential. Notably, expression of vasculature-de-stabilizing Angiopoietin-2 was increased with cardiac tissue, which may impair angiogenesis with VEGF downregulation, as observed in the cancer-cardiac condition, further supporting the possibility of a less permissive microenvironment for metastatic outgrowth.

In the tri-culture devices, the elevated pro-metastatic and immunosuppressive cytokines relative to the cancer-hepatic co-culture may reflect a delayed metastatic onset caused by cardiac tissue; the secretome at day 10 may be capturing an early invasive phase. The cancer-hepatic condition, however, likely represents a later, more stabilized metastatic state. Crucially, despite this profile, the cardiac compartment remained largely free of cancer cells, demonstrating active suppression. Compared to cancer-cardiac devices, the tri-culture secretome exhibited significant enrichment in chemotactic, invasive and growth-signaling pathways, reflecting a potentially pro-invasive and pro-survival signaling environment induced by hepatic tissue. These results are consistent with an increased trend in metastasis in the tri-culture devices relative to the overall metastasis in the cancer-cardiac co-culture.

Transcriptomic analysis further validated these findings. Compared to hepatic co-culture, cancer cells exposed to cardiac tissue showed downregulated genes linked to immune escape and tumor progression while upregulating pathways associated with metastatic suppression and immune signaling, including cytokine and interleukin pathways. Notably, this immune-related activation occurred despite the absence of immune cells, suggesting that cardiac cues may intrinsically enhance tumor immune visibility. Moreover, several GO terms involving antigen presentation via the MHC class II pathway were upregulated; previous studies have shown that MHC II pathway expression in TNBC supports an antitumor immune response via tumor antigen presentation and recruitment of tumor infiltrating lymphocytes, ultimately leading to better patient prognosis96. Furthermore, gene set enrichment analyses revealed elevated pro-apoptotic signaling and enhanced substrate adhesion, implying increased susceptibility to cancer cell death and reduced migratory potential in cancer-cardiac versus cancer-hepatic conditions.

Compared to cancer monoculture, both cardiac and hepatic co-cultures upregulated genes related to angiogenesis and ECM remodeling. However, cardiac co-culture uniquely induced strong immune-related GO processes—including humoral immune response, complement activation, and stress-response pathways—along with suppressed oxidative metabolism. In contrast, the hepatic microenvironment downregulated immune-associated programs, such as MHC class II assembly, antigen binding, immune recruitment signals, and migration inhibitors.

Direct comparison of the cancer-cardiac and cancer-hepatic secretomes reinforced these findings, with cardiac co-culture showing enhanced cytokine signatures associated with immune activation and recruitment, including pathways supporting B cell survival and antigen-specific activation76 and macrophage, neutrophil and effector T cell recruitment77,78,80. In addition to the immune-related changes, the cancer-cardiac secretome predominantly exhibited attenuation of angiogenic activity relative to the cancer-hepatic secretome, with downregulation of pro-angiogenic signaling molecules68–70, and upregulation of angiogenic inhibitors71,72. The concurrent increase in some pro-angiogenic receptor levels may reflect a compensatory response to reduced ligand (e.g. VEGF) availability72. Furthermore, suppression of key mediators of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, matrix invasion, and other pro-metastatic programs reinforces the notion that the cardiac microenvironment is inherently less permissive to metastatic colonization. Moreover, downregulation of the growth-supportive factors FGF-7 and IGFBP-1 in the cardiac microenvironment suggests restricted proliferative capacity of metastatic cells attempting to establish secondary growths in cardiac tissue73,87,97.

Collectively, these findings suggest that the cardiac microenvironment may limit pro-metastatic signaling, creating conditions that are inherently unfavorable for the colonization and outgrowth of secondary tumors in cardiac tissue.

The availability of co-culture RNA sequencing data allows us to build a hypothesis on the possible mechanisms of action preventing cancer metastasis towards cardiac tissue. Among the adhesion-associated transcripts enriched in the cancer–cardiac condition, ADAMTS9 emerged as a notable candidate. ADAMTS9 is a metalloproteinase with tumor suppressor properties98 that regulates cell migration through the cleavage of versican, an anti-adhesive proteoglycan that mediates cell focal adhesion99. We hypothesized that upregulated ADAMTS9 expression in cancer tissue promotes cell attachment, inhibiting cancer cell detachment and migration to distal tissues in the MInD device. Our functional knockdown studies further support a role for ADAMTS9 in reducing the adhesive response to cardiomyocyte-conditioned medium, as suppression of ADAMTS9 attenuated cardiac-induced cell attachment.

How does the cardiac secretome enhance expression of ADAMTS9? We present the following possibility. Canonically, ADAMTS9 expression is transcriptionally regulated through NF-κB responsive promoter elements and inducible by pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1ß100. Using a cytokine antibody array, we identified a significant reduction in soluble IL-1ß receptor, IL-1 R3 (Fig. 5j), which may reduce decoy-mediated ligand sequestration101 and is consistent with enhanced IL-1ß signaling in the context of driving ADAMTS9 expression in cancer-cardiac co-cultures. Notably, this target is a promising candidate for future investigation into its role in regulation of ADAMTS9 expression and any mechanisms potentially leading to a cardiac resistance to cancer metastasis.

Overall, the MInD platform provides a physiologically relevant system for modeling organ-specific cancer invasion under dynamic conditions. Using this approach, we demonstrate that cardiac tissue may suppress breast cancer metastasis, potentially through enhanced immune visibility, pro-apoptotic signaling, anti-EMT, and strengthened adhesion programs, including ADAMTS9-mediated regulation of cell detachment as one of plausible mechanisms.

While this work presents a valuable model for studying organ-specific cancer metastasis, there remain areas for improvement. Many of the observed changes in secretome and transcriptome are immunological in nature; however, without any immune cells in our model, we were unable to explore these in more detail. Moreover, the PermeoTubes used in this model were not endothelialized, as we sought to mimic migration through ECM. Incorporating an endothelial lining within the PermeoTubes in future studies would better mimic native vasculature and thereby more accurately recapitulate cancer cell metastasis as it occurs in vivo. The use of cell lines for both the cancer cells and the hepatic tissue somewhat limits the relevance of these findings. With increased access to patient-derived cancer cells and iPSCs, this model could be adapted to be completely isogenic, with cancer cells and iPSC-derived cardiac and hepatic tissues derived from the same donor, demonstrating the system’s potential for personalized medicine. Finally, advanced maturation of the cardiac tissue via mechanical or electrical signals might be necessary to capture the effects of mechanical environment on metastiatic cells102. Ultimately, such advances could inform new strategies for engineering metastasis-resistant niches and developing anti-metastasis therapies.

POMaC synthesis and characterization

POMaC pre-polymer (pre-POMaC) was synthesized via polycondensation of citric acid, maleic anhydride, and 1,8-octanediol in a 5:1:4 molar ratio through a one-pot reaction using the setup demonstrated in Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1a as described104–106. The monomers were weighed according to the 5:1:4 molar ratio, combined, and melted at 160 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere with constant stirring. The temperature was then reduced to 140 °C, and the reaction was allowed to proceed for 3 h under continuous nitrogen flow. The resulting pre-polymer was purified by dissolving in 1,4-dioxane and precipitating dropwise into deionized water under stirring. The precipitate was collected and air-dried for 48 h to remove residual solvent. The final product was a clear, viscous pre-polymer, which was stored at 4 °C in the dark until use. A required amount of pre-POMaC was mixed with PEGDME (40% w/w) and 2-Hydroxy-4′-(2-hydroxyethoxy)-2-methylpropiophenone photoinitiator (5% w/w). The mixture was heated on a hot plate at 100 °C for 1 h while covered with aluminum foil. The polymer blend was then transferred into a syringe and protected from light.

The chemical structure of the POMaC pre-polymer was analyzed using 1H NMR spectroscopy on a 500 MHz Agilent DD2 spectrometer. Samples were prepared by dissolving POMaC pre-polymer in deuterated DMSO-d₆ at a concentration of 10 mg mL-1. Chemical shifts were referenced to the proton signal of DMSO. Peak integration corresponding to specific monomer protons was performed with the aid of predictive analysis tools (Cambridge Soft ChemDraw Ultra) to evaluate the relative composition of the monomer units in the pre-polymer.

POMaC granule fabrication

POMaC granules were fabricated by emulsifying the polymer in mineral oil containing 1% Tween 20 as a surfactant (Fig. 1c). Briefly, mineral oil and Tween 20 were added to a 15 mL beaker and stirred at 400, 800, 1000 or 1200 rpm. POMaC granules used to form PermeoTubes were formed using a stirring speed of 1000 rpm. POMaC pre-polymer (0.2 v/v relative to oil) was then added dropwise under continuous stirring. The emulsion was immediately transferred to a Falcon tube, allowed to settle for 15 min, and the resulting pellet was collected into a syringe. Excess oil was removed by inverting the syringe and gently pressing the plunger to expel the overlying oil. The final product was stored at 4 °C in the dark.

Rheology

The viscoelastic properties of POMaC polymer were characterized in both granular and bulk forms using a Discovery HR-2 rheometer (TA Instruments) equipped with a 40 mm, 1.998° cone plate and stainless-steel Peltier plate. All measurements were performed at room temperature. A flow sweep test was conducted using a logarithmic shear rate sweep from 0.01 to 100 s⁻¹, with an equilibration time of 5 s, and an averaging time of 30 s, to assess shear-thinning behavior. Frequency sweep measurements were carried out at a constant strain of 1% over a frequency range of 0.1–50 Hz, to determine the storage and loss moduli. Amplitude sweep tests were performed at a fixed frequency of 1 Hz using a logarithmic strain range from 0.05% to 5%, to identify the linear viscoelastic region.

Membrane fabrication

Flat POMaC membranes were fabricated by spreading the granular ink onto a ~ 1 cm2 glass mold to achieve a thickness of approximately 0.5 mm. The mold was pre-wetted with 10% Pluronic prior to adding the granular ink. The membrane was then crosslinked under UV light (90,000 μW cm-2 at 38 cm; Labino UVG3 2.0 UV LED Flashlight, spotlight, Sweden). During curing, the flashlight was positioned 15 mm above the membrane surface for 30 min, after which the cured membrane was gently peeled off the substrate (Supplementary Fig. 1b).

PermeoTube printing

POMaC tubes were fabricated using a coaxial extrusion setup with 25% Pluronic as a sacrificial template. Pluronic F127 solutions at 25 and 10% (w/v) were prepared in deionized water. Prior to printing, the 10% Pluronic and granular POMaC ink were brought to room temperature. A syringe (5–10 mL) containing 25% Pluronic was connected to the inner gauge (28 G) of a coaxial needle and pre-heated at 70 °C for 20 min to lower its viscosity. The syringe containing POMaC granules was connect to the outer gauge (21 G) of the coaxial needle, and the assembly was mounted above a petri dish containing 10% Pluronic at room temperature (Fig. 1g). During printing, the syringe containing 25% Pluronic was maintained at 37 °C using an electrically heated wrap to preserve its high temperature and viscosity. The flow rate of the inner gauge with 25% Pluronic was 650 µL h-1 (Fisherbrand™ 780100I syringe pump), and the granular POMaC ink through the outer gauge was perfused at 900-950 µL h-1 (Aladdin Single-Syringe infusion Pump AL-300, WPI). As the materials were extruded, the UV flashlight (90,000 μW cm-2 at 38 cm; Labino UVG3 2.0 UV LED Flashlight, spotlight, Sweden) was positioned near the coaxial needle ( ~ 50 mm from the needle tip), and the construct was crosslinked in real time during printing. Simultaneously, the petri dish beneath the needle was moved to guide the extrusion path. The petri dish containing the tube was then exposed to UV flashlight (distance of 15 mm), while wrapped in aluminum foil, for an additional 30 min, with the setup wrapped in aluminum foil to enhance crosslinking from both the top and bottom.

3D printing

A 3D printer (RegenHu Discovery 3D Bioprinter) was modified to incorporate a coaxial extrusion print head, enabling the fabrication of PermeoTube structures (Fig. 1n, Supplementary Movie 1). The granular POMaC ink and Pluronic gel were loaded into 3D-printing syringes configured for coaxial flow (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Precise control of flow rates was maintained; with the outer granular POMaC ink flow rate set at 900 µL h-1 and the inner Pluronic flow rate set at 650 µL h-1. The print speed was maintained at 3 mm s-1 to ensure uniform deposition and consistent formation of the outer POMaC layer.

Mechanical properties, porosity and degradation of permeotubes

The tensile properties of PermeoTubes were characterized using uniaxial tensile testing on a mechanical testing system (ElectroForce 5200, Bose). Samples were stored in water prior to testing, and immediately transferred to the instrument to minimize dehydration and better replicate physiological conditions. All tests were conducted at room temperature.

The compressive behavior of PermeoTubes was assessed using a MicroSquisher system (CellScale). A circular tungsten microbeam (0.4064 mm diameter) affixed to a 2 × 2 mm stainless steel plate was used to apply the load. Tubes were submerged in PBS during testing to maintain hydration. The microbeam was carefully lowered until the plate rested atop the tube. The compression protocol included a ramp to 150 µm, a 20-second load phase, a 5-second hold, and a 20-second recovery period. Additionally, time-lapse videos were generated using Microsoft Clipchamp by stitching together sequential images captured during the fabrication process.

Investigation of the degradation rate of an entire PermeoTube was performed in hydrolytic conditions (pH 7.4 at 37 °C) to analyze the device’s stability. The dry weights of the PermeoTubes were used throughout. Small sections of each tube were weight and placed in a 20 mL scintillation vial with 2 mL of PBS in an incubated shaker under physiological conditions. Weights of each tube were measured at 7 and 14 days to analyze for stability.

Fractal analysis

To assess the fractal characteristics of the printed structures, brightfield images of flat POMaC membranes and fluorescence images of PermeoTubes were analyzed to calculate their fractal dimensions (Dfs). Image analysis was performed using ImageJ (Fiji) following the same method as described in our previous publication107.

Device fabrication

Custom molds for PDMS scaffolds were designed using Autodesk Inventor, with dimensions shown in Supplementary Fig. 4a. The designs were exported as G-code using PrusaSlicer-2.6.1, and 3D printed (Original Prusa MINI + 0.25 nozzle) using polycarbonate (PC) filament. After printing, molds were cleaned and polished. To fabricate the base layer of the device, 6 g of degassed PDMS was poured into a 60 mm petri dish and cured at 75 °C overnight. The printed mold was then placed onto the cured slab, secured with clips, and additional degassed PDMS was poured to fill the dish around the mold. This assembly was cured at 75 °C for 40 min. Once fully cured, the PDMS scaffold was removed from the petri dish, and the mold was carefully extracted (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Smaller wells (referred to as “compartments”) were embedded within the central wells (wells 2–4) of the device to confine the seeded gels and support tissue formation. Notably, the geometry of these tissue compartments did not remain precisely rectangular as depicted in the schematic, due to resolution limitations of the 3D-printed molds and the PDMS casting process. As a result, the edges appeared slightly angled after casting. For each device, four PC walls were 3D printed (dimensions shown in Supplementary Fig. 4c) and bonded to the scaffold. PermeoTubes were cut to the desired length. The tubes were inserted into the grooves and secured by semi-cured PDMS at the wall interfaces. The PC walls were then positioned and bonded with the main PDMS platform using semi-cured PDMS. The platform was placed in a humidity chamber and cured at 75 °C for 40 minutes. The addition of semi-cured PDMS at the wall interfaces and beneath the walls, where the tube is positioned, ensured complete sealing between adjacent wells. This design confined fluid flow exclusively through the pores of the PermeoTube, eliminating the possibility of leakage between wells (Supplementary Fig. 4d). During assembly, the sacrificial Pluronic inside the tubes helped maintain their shape and prevent collapse. To wash off Pluronic afterward and render the tubes hollow and perfusable, the devices were placed at 4 °C overnight or at −20 °C for a few minutes to dissolve Pluronic. All devices were first inspected for inter-well leakage by adding water to each individual well and observing for any rapid fluid transfer to adjacent wells; only devices that showed no such leakage were used in subsequent experiments. Tube perfusability was confirmed by introducing a visible blue dye into the inlet and monitoring its progression through the tube and connected wells over time. Completed devices were filled with water and stored in a humidity chamber at 4 °C until use.

Permeability and flow measurements

To evaluate the permeability of the PermeoTubes (Fig. 2h), 70 kDa FITC-dextran was diluted at 0.2 mg mL-1 in deionized water. Each device was filled with 200 µL of water in all wells, with the center well containing the FITC-dextran solution. Devices were placed on a rocker and incubated for 5, 15, 30, or 60 min. After incubation, the solutions from each well were collected and transferred to a 96-well plate along with a standard curve of FITC-dextran dilutions (at 0.2, 0.1, 0.05, 0.025, 0.0125, 0.00625, 0.003125 mg mL-1). Fluorescence intensity was measured using a plate reader (Cytation 5) to quantify molecular diffusion across the tube.

To quantify fluid transport within the PermeoTube under conditions consistent with the cell experiments, flow measurements were performed in the MInD platform using basal DMEM. Each of the five wells was filled with 200 µL of medium, and the devices were placed in a closed Petri dish and briefly equilibrated at 37 °C. They were then transferred to the rocker platform and positioned flat at room temperature for flow measurements. The rocker continuously moves the devices to the maximum right tilt (11.5°) and left tilt (-11.5°). The volume in each well was measured twice: at the first maximum left tilt (V1), and again half a cycle later at the subsequent maximum right tilt (V2). The initial right-tilt measurement was excluded because the volumes had not yet stabilized. Volume measurements were conducted by carefully collecting and weighing medium from each well. Measured mass was converted to volume (V) assuming a density of 1.00 g/mL for DMEM. The volume displacements at the inlet (well 1) and outlet (well 5) were calculated for each device by subtracting the volume measured at maximum right tilt (V2) from that measured at maximum left tilt (V1) (i.e., ΔVinlet = V2well1 ‒ V1well1, ΔVoutlet = V2well5 ‒ V1well5). The flow rates were then calculated as Q = ΔV/T, where T = 5 min corresponds to the time required to move from maximum left to maximum right tilt. The average rocker flow rate (oscillatory flow) was calculated by averaging the absolute inlet and outlet flow rates (i.e., Qavg = (|Qinlet | +|Qoutlet | )/2).

Cell line expansion

Breast cancer cells, lung carcinoma cells, colon adenocarcinoma cells, dermal fibroblasts, lung fibroblasts, cardiac fibroblasts, and HepG2 cells were cultured under standard conditions at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Cells were thawed rapidly in a 37 °C water bath, diluted dropwise into pre-warmed culture medium, centrifuged to remove cryoprotectants, and resuspended in fresh medium before plating in T-flasks. Media were changed every other day, and cells were passaged upon reaching 70–90% confluency using enzymatic dissociation with trypsin-EDTA. Following PBS rinses, trypsin was added and incubated until detachment, after which cells were neutralized with complete medium and centrifuged prior to reseeding. Each cell line was maintained in its specific growth medium: breast cancer cells in DMEM-C supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 1X GlutaMAX, and 1X Non-Essential Amino Acids (NAA); lung carcinoma and colon adenocarcinoma cells in F-12K supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin; cardiac fibroblasts in FGM-3 supplemented medium (see Materials); dermal and lung fibroblasts were cultured in DMEM-F supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 1X GlutaMAX, and 10 mM HEPES; and HepG2 hepatocytes in EMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Cell counting was performed manually using a hemocytometer and trypan blue exclusion to assess viability and determine cell concentration.

Cardiomyocytes differentiation

Cardiomyocytes were differentiated using previous established protocols108. Briefly, BJ1D iPSCs were lifted using Accutase or TrypLE™ Express at 60–70% confluency and replated in a 12-well plate at a concentration of 500,000 cells per well in mTeSR+ until they reached 100% confluence (1–2 days). For the first 7 days of the differentiation, cells were maintained in RPMI medium supplemented with 2% B27 minus insulin (Differentiation Medium I). On day 0, cell medium was switched from mTeSR+ to Differentiation Medium I supplemented with 8 µM CHIR99021. After exactly 24 h, CHIR was removed from the medium. After two days of culture, 2 µM C59 was added to the media for two days, then removed. On day 7, cells were switching to RPMI medium supplemented with 2% B27 with insulin (Differentiation Medium II). Cells were kept in Differentiation Medium II, changed every 3–4 days, until they were used in the devices.

Differentiated cardiomyocytes were dissociated using 10X TrypLE™ Select supplemented with DNase (1:100) prior to seeding into the devices. Following dissociation, cells were cultured in i3M medium containing complete StemPro™, supplemented with 1% GlutaMAX, 0.15 mg mL-1 transferrin, 213 µg mL-1 ascorbic acid, 20 mM HEPES, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin.

Cell metabolic activity

To assess the compatibility of i3M medium with hepatic and cardiac cells, dome-shaped fibrin gels containing either HepG2 cells or cardiomyocytes were prepared following the same fibrin composition and seeding protocol as described previously. Lung fibroblasts were omitted from the hepatic condition. The domes were formed directly in non-tissue culture-treated well plates. Each cell type was cultured in its standard growth medium or in i3M for comparison. At defined time points, 200 µL of 10% (v/v) alamarBlue solution in DMEM was added to both cell-containing and blank control wells and incubated at 37 °C for 35–40 min. Subsequently, 100 µL of the supernatant was collected, and fluorescence was measured using a plate reader to evaluate cell viability. The averaged blank fluorescence value was subtracted from each measurement, and each measurement was subsequently normalized to the average fluorescence value of the tissue in its standard growth medium at day 0.

Cell seeding into devices

Prior to cell seeding, devices were sterilized by sequentially washing the wells with PBS followed by 70% ethanol and repeated three times. For the final ethanol wash, devices were fully submerged in filter-sterilized 70% ethanol for 1 h under aseptic conditions in a biosafety cabinet. Following sterilization, devices were rinsed with sterile PBS and filled with complete cancer cell culture medium. Devices were incubated at 37 °C for 1–3 days with media changes every 1–2 days prior to seeding to equilibrate the environment and promote surface conditioning.

Devices were pretreated by adding 0.3% (w/v) gelatin solution—250 µL into the inlet, 100 µL into the central wells, and 20 µL into the outlet wells—and incubated at 37 °C for 1 hour. Meanwhile, fibrinogen (33 mg mL-1), thrombin (25 U mL-1), and aprotinin (5 mg mL-1) were thawed on ice. Cells were dissociated and resuspended in serum-free RPMI 1640 medium. For hepatic tissue constructs, 500,000 HepG2 cells (P#4-5) were mixed with 10,000 lung fibroblasts (P#7-8) (2%); for cardiac constructs, 500,000 iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes were combined with 10,000 cardiac fibroblasts (P#4-7) (2%). Differentiated cardiomyocytes were filtered through a 40 µm cell strainer before use. After centrifugation, the supernatant was removed to obtain a dry pellet. Following gelatin removal from the devices, the cell pellets were resuspended in a fibrinogen-thrombin mixture (4:1, v/v), and 8.5 µL of the resulting cell-laden fibrin gels was seeded into the tissue compartments in well 2 or 4 (Supplementary Fig. 4b) in dome-shaped deposits. To prevent cell leakage into adjacent wells, neighboring wells were filled with medium to pressurize the device and direct flow toward the seeding well. Acellular control gels were prepared by forming fibrin domes without cells. The gels were allowed to crosslink at 37 °C for 10 min. Next, 200 µL of i3M medium supplemented with 5 µg mL-1 aprotinin was added to each well. Devices were maintained in petri dishes alongside PBS-filled containers to minimize evaporation and incubated overnight under static conditions (day 0). The following day, devices were placed on a rocker at 0.1 cycles per minute, and the media was refreshed every two days. Aprotinin was included in the first media change, after which only i3M was used.

On day 7, after tissue maturation, breast cancer cells (P#5-7) were introduced into the tissue compartment in well 3 using the same fibrin-based seeding protocol. The day of cancer cell seeding was redefined as day 0 for downstream analysis. A total of 100,000 cancer cells were suspended in 11.5 µL of fibrin solution composed of 9 µL fibrinogen and 2.5 µL thrombin and seeded into well 3 of each device. Following seeding, devices were placed on a rocker, and cancer cell migration was monitored by fluorescence imaging on days 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10. Tissues were fixed on day 10 by adding pre-warmed 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) to each well, ensuring complete coverage ( ~ 100 µL per well). Devices were wrapped and stored at 4 °C overnight. Following fixation, PFA was removed, and wells were rinsed with PBS.

In the experiment comparing dermal fibroblast migration to that of cancer cells, 100,000 GFP-labeled dermal fibroblasts were seeded within fibrin gel in the designated well of the device, following the same protocol used for cancer cell seeding. Dermal fibroblasts (P#7) were also seeded onto POMaC-based membranes to evaluate cell interaction with porous versus non-porous structures. Three conditions were tested: porous membranes, non-porous membranes, and control wells without membranes. Membranes ( ~ 5 mm in diameter) were placed inside a well plate and a total of 10,000 cells were added to each well in 200 µL of culture medium. Day 0 was defined as the time of seeding and the media was changed every other day.

Scratch assay

Scratch assays were performed using two-well culture inserts (ibidi cells in focus, WI, USA) placed in a 24-well plate. GFP+ breast cancer cells (P#5-7) were dissociated, counted, and seeded at a density of 1750 cells per insert’s well by adding 70 µL of a 50,000 cells mL-1 suspension in complete cancer media. Cells were cultured for a few days until confluency with regular media changes. Inserts were then removed, and wells were gently washed with PBS to eliminate residual cells and debris. Cells were treated with different concentrations of conditioned medium collected from differentiated cardiomyocytes cultured in 2D well plates (Differentiation Medium II), harvested after two days. To replenish nutrients consumed during cardiomyocyte culture, the conditioned medium was supplemented with fresh B27/insulin, HEPES, and GlutaMAX, to a final concentration of 2% B27/insulin, 25 mM HEPES and 1 mM GlutaMAX. The cardiomyocytes-conditioned medium was applied at final concentrations of 10, 25, 50, 70, and 100%, prepared by diluting the conditioned medium with fresh Differentiation Medium II accordingly. 100% fresh Differentiation Medium II was used as control and labeled as “Fresh”. Wells were imaged at 0, 18, and 24 h to quantify cancer cell migration into the scratch area. The same regions of interest were re-imaged at each time point to ensure consistent assessment of gap closure over time.

Similar scratch assays were performed with lung carcinoma (P#3) and colon adenocarcinoma (P#2) cells using the same protocol described above, unless otherwise stated. Lung carcinoma cells were seeded at 50,000 cells mL⁻¹ and treated with either fresh medium or 100% cardiomyocyte-conditioned medium. Brightfield images were acquired at 0 and 24 h, followed by continued incubation to 45 h. Cells were then fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min, rinsed with PBS, and stained with DAPI and Ki67. Migration into the scratch area was quantified by comparing brightfield images at 0 h with DAPI fluorescence images at 45 h from the same regions of interest. In addition, total cell number (DAPI⁺) and proliferating cells (Ki67⁺) were quantified across the entire 10× field of view.

Colon adenocarcinoma cells were seeded at 100,000 cells mL⁻¹ and treated with fresh or 100% conditioned medium. Due to their clustering growth pattern, cells were not fully confluent at the time of insert removal. Brightfield imaging was performed at 0 and 24 h. Owing to the inherently slow migration of colon cells and partial detachment under conditioned medium exposure, scratch closure could not be reliably quantified. Therefore, cells were fixed and stained at 48 h for DAPI and Ki67, and total as well as Ki67⁺ cell counts were quantified across the entire 10× field of view.

Ex vivo co-culture with ventricular explants

GFP+ breast cancer cells (P#7) were seeded into two-well culture inserts placed at the bottom of 24-well transwell plates as described in the Scratch Assay section. After reaching confluency, the inserts were removed to generate a cell-free gap, and wells were briefly washed prior to initiating the ex vivo co-culture.

Ventricular explants were obtained from C57BL/6NCrl mice (Charles River Laboratories, Canada) at 8–10 weeks of age. Animals were housed at the MARS–Princess Margaret Cancer Research Tower Animal Facility under standard husbandry conditions with ad libitum access to food and water, a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, controlled ambient temperature (20 °C–24 °C), and relative humidity of 40–60%. All animal procedures were approved by the University Health Network Animal Care Committee (Animal Use Protocol AUP 6892.6). Following euthanasia, the left ventricles of mice were removed and placed on ice in 1x Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution supplemented with 2% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin until processing. The resected ventricles were then minced into approximately 1 mm3 explants using sterile long-blade scissors. The resulting explants were washed three times with fresh 1x Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution with 2% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin prior to initiating coculture. Ventricle explants were cultured in the upper chamber of 6.5-mm diameter, 0.4-μm pore size tissue-culture inserts (Corning #07-200-154) in 600 μL of DMEM-F supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and 2% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin. Explants were transferred using wide-bore pipette tips and divided evenly such that four tissue-culture inserts were seeded per resected ventricle.

GFP fluorescence images were acquired at 0, 18, and 24 h following insert removal, with cultures maintained until 48 h. At 48 h, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min, rinsed with PBS, and immunostained for Ki67, with nuclei counterstained using DAPI. Total cell number (DAPI⁺ nuclei) and proliferating cells (Ki67⁺ nuclei) were quantified across the entire 10× field of view.

Immunostaining

Before staining, tissues were washed three times with PBS, then permeabilized with 0.2% Tween20, and blocked with 10% FBS. Cardiomyocytes were immunostained with mouse anti-cardiac troponin T (clone 13-11; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# MA5-12960; 1:200 dilution) and goat anti-mouse IgG1 (cross-adsorbed) Alexa Fluor 647 secondary antibody (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# A21240; 1:400 dilution). HepG2 cells were immunostained with CoraLite® 594-conjugated mouse anti-human albumin monoclonal antibody (Proteintech, clone 4A1C11, Cat# CL594-66051, 1:200). For immunostaining of breast cancer, lung carcinoma, and colon adenocarcinoma cells in ex vivo and scratch assays, they were permeabilized with 0.2% Tween-20 and blocked with 3% BSA. Proliferating cells were labeled using Alexa Fluor® 647-conjugated rabbit anti-Ki-67 monoclonal antibody (clone D3B5, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 12075, 1:300). Nuclear staining was performed with DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:1000) prior to imaging.

Image analysis

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) was performed using the Prisma Environmental SEM (Thermo Scientific) following gold sputter coating of the samples. Fluorescent imaging was conducted using the Olympus IX81 microscope, while immunofluorescence imaging was carried out with the Zeiss LSM710 two-photon microscope. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ (Fiji), with cancer cell migration manually quantified. Unless otherwise specified, for the Cancer-Hepatic, Cancer-Cardiac, and Cancer-Cardiac-Hepatic conditions, migrated cancer cells in tissue/acellular fibrin compartments in both wells 2 and 4 of the devices were manually counted. Cell counts were obtained from two tissue compartments. Within each compartment, counts were first summed and then normalized to the tissue compartment area. For the cancer monoculture condition, only the acellular fibrin compartment was quantified, and cell counts were normalized to the area of this compartment (5 mm²). Scratch closure in breast cancer cells was quantified using GFP fluorescence images. Images were thresholded to define and measure the cell-covered area. Scratch closure (%) was calculated as the percentage reduction in scratch area relative to the initial area at 0 h. For scratch assays using lung carcinoma and colon adenocarcinoma cells, brightfield images were manually analyzed with the assistance of ImageJ to count the number of cells. For fluorescence quantification, DAPI-stained nuclei were segmented in ImageJ following background subtraction and automated thresholding. Binary processing and watershed separation were applied to resolve adjacent nuclei, and particles were analyzed to obtain total cell counts. Ki67 positivity was determined by measuring mean nuclear fluorescence intensity in the Cy5 channel and applying a positivity threshold defined as background mean intensity + 3 * standard deviations. The same protocol was also utilized for ex vivo image analysis.

Statistical analysis and graph generation were completed using GraphPad Prism 11.0.1. Schematic illustrations were created using Autodesk Inventor and BioRender. Brightness and contrast adjustments were applied uniformly across entire images for visualization purposes. Raw images are available in the associated Mendeley Data repository.

MInD cyrosectioning and imaging

MInD devices were fixed with 10% formalin overnight, and washed 3x with PBS. The tube and tissue in the middle cancer tissue compartment of the device were carefully removed, embedded in Optimal Cutting Temperature (Fisher Healthcare Tissue-Plus™ O.C.T. Compound, Cat# 23-730-571, Thermo Fisher Scientific, NY, USA) compound and frozen in isopropanol cooled with dry ice. Samples were stored at −80 °C for at least overnight. 20 µm cryosections were collected and fluorescently imaged.

Cytokine analysis

The medium used for cytokine analysis was collected from all five wells on day 10 and combined; for the cancer monoculture sample, the medium was collected on day 12. Cytokine secretion in the culture medium was measured using the RayBiotech, Human L507 Array Glass Slide, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the sample was purified to remove low molecular weight amine derivatives or unwanted buffer, then biotinylated following the kit instructions. The glass slides were then blocked for 30 min, then incubated with the samples (diluted 10-fold in the blocking buffer) overnight at 4 °C under gentle rocking. The slides were then washed with the provided wash buffers thoroughly before adding Cy3-Conjugated Streptavidin for 1 h at room temperature under gentle rocking. Slides were washed repeatedly again with the provided wash buffers, then in a final ddH2O wash, before being dried gently with a compressed N2 stream, and stored at −20 °C away from light until scanning.

The slides were scanned using the GenePix® Professional 4200 A Microarray Scanner. A 532 nm laser was used, with scanning power at 55%, and standard green filter was used to collect the signal, with photomultiplier tube gain at 370. Images were acquired at a resolution of 10 µm. The signal was post-processed by subtracting background fluorescence from the medium fluorescence at each spot in the array. Further processing to obtain final measures for each cytokine was done using the Excel file provided by the manufacturer. Technical replicates were averaged and normalized to the controls. A two-tailed t-test was used to identify differentially expressed cytokines, and a p-value less than 0.05 was considered significant. Volcano plots were made with GraphPad Prism, and heatmaps were made with SR plot109.

AngioPlateTM seeding

AngioPlates (AngioPlate™384, Organo Biotech, ON, Canada), which consist of interconnected wells grouped in triads via dissolvable sacrificial gels, were used to investigate cardiac-cancer and hepatic-cancer interactions for RNA sequencing. Hepatic, cardiac, and acellular fibrin conditions were established using a seeding approach similar to that used in device experiments, with 100,000 cells per dome seeded into the center well of each triad unit. Lung fibroblasts (P#8), cardiac fibroblasts (P#6), and HepG2 cells (P#4) were used in the respective tissue constructs. The sacrificial gel connecting the wells was allowed to dissolve via media changes every two days. On day 4, the plate was transferred to a rocker to initiate dynamic culture. On day 7, breast cancer cells (P#6) were pre-formed in fibrin gel and introduced into one of the side wells at a concentration of 50,000 cells in 8.5 µL of fibrin solution. The day of cancer cell seeding was redefined as day 0 for downstream analysis. i3M medium supplemented with aprotinin (5 µg mL-1) was added immediately after seeding. Media were changed every two days, with aprotinin included in the first change only. Cancer tissues were collected on day 5 post-cancer seeding for RNA extraction.

RNA extraction

RNA extraction and isolation of the tissues cultured in AngioPlate was performed using the PicoPure™ RNA Isolation Kit (Applied Biosystems, KIT0204) according to manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, the cancer tissue in the middle well of the AngioPlate was removed and placed in a RNase-free microtube, followed by adding 100 µL of extraction buffer, incubating at 42 °C for 30 min, and centrifuging at 3000 g for 2 min to collect the supernatant containing RNA. The RNA extract from each sample was mixed with equal volume of 70% ethanol by pipetting several times until a uniform mixture was formed. The mixture was then run through a preconditioned column to have RNA bound to the column, followed by 3–4 washes according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Lastly, 12 µL of elution buffer was used to elute the RNA from the column. Quantification of isolated RNA was performed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Isolated RNA was stored at −80 °C before sequencing.

Downstream bioinformatic analysis of RNA sequencing data

Read alignment, counting, differential expression analysis, gene ontology (GO), Reactome, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) were performed in-house at Novogene Corporation Inc. Briefly, Hisat2 (v2.2.1) was used to build the index of, and align cleaned reads to, the reference genome (GRCh38). Mapped reads were counted using featureCounts (v2.0.6) then converted to FPKM. The DESeq2 R package (v1.42.0) was used for differential expression analysis. FDR adjusted p-value < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg corrected) was considered significant. The clusterProfiler R package (v4.8.1) was used for downstream biological enrichment analyses with gene length bias corrected, including GO enrichment analysis and Reactome pathway analysis (FDR < 0.05 considered significant for each). For each pairwise GO enrichment comparison between groups, the top 10 most significant upregulated GO terms (Biological Process, Cellular Component, Molecular Factor, FDR < 0.05) were displayed and dimmed if they were not significant (FDR > 0.05) in the list of downregulated GO terms (and vice versa). The Broad Institute web-based tool (v.4.3.2) was used for GSEA (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp; Accessed May 2025) of the GO and Reactome datasets (FDR q-value < 0.25 considered significant).

Principal component analysis of raw counts (from featureCounts) was performed using PCAGO (normalization using DESeq2 rlog transformation) and plotted using GraphPad Prism. Heatmaps were generated using SRPlot109 using rlog normalized counts from DESeq2 analysis performed on the Galaxy platform, with complete Euclidean clustering of rows.

For the custom heatmap of the comparison between the cancer-cardiac co-culture and cancer-hepatic co-culture conditions, plotted genes were selected from differentially expressed genes within the top 10 most enriched GO terms, including up and downregulated genes. For the custom heatmap of the comparisons between the cancer-cardiac co-culture and cancer-hepatic co-culture with cancer monoculture conditions, plotted genes were selected from differentially expressed genes within the top 10 most significantly upregulated GO terms since fewer than 10 GO terms were significantly downregulated in the cancer-cardiac condition. Heatmaps (Fig. 5g, Supplementary Fig. 18g, Supplementary Fig. 19g, and Supplementary Fig. 21a,) were generated using the log2(FPKM + 1) values calculated for each biological replicate of each DEG; these values were used to compute a Z-score and plotted using SR plot109.

Transfection and cell adhesion assay

siRNA transfection of GFP + MDA-MB-231 cells (P#7) with ADAMTS-9 siRNA (h) was performed according to manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were pre-treated in Opti-MEM™ for 1 h at 37 °C prior to transfection. For delivery of siRNA to target cells, Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent was diluted in Opti-MEM™ and incubated with 90 nM of ADAMTS-9 siRNA (h) or Silencer™ Select Negative Control No. 1 siRNA according to manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h before use. Transfected and scrambled-control MDA-MB-231 cells were dissociated, collected, and centrifuged to pellet cells before resuspension in either cardiomyocyte-conditioned medium or fresh Differentiation Medium II. The conditioned medium consisted of 100% medium collected from 2D differentiated cardiomyocytes (Differentiation Medium II) after 2 days of culture and was supplemented with fresh B27/insulin, HEPES, and GlutaMAX to final concentrations of 2% B27/insulin, 25 mM HEPES, and 1 mM GlutaMAX. Cells were seeded into 48-well plates at 25,000 cells per well in 250 µL (1×10^5 cells/mL) and incubated at 37 °C. Cell attachment was assessed by imaging GFP signal after 30 and 60 min of incubation.

cDNA synthesis and qPCR

1 μg of RNA was used for each cDNA synthesis reaction. cDNA synthesis was performed using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Cat #: 4374966) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations with the ProFlex PCR System from Applied Biosystems (Cat # 4483636). Cycle time for cDNA synthesis was as follows: (1) 25 °C for 10 min (1×); (2) 37 °C for 2 h (1×); (3) 85 °C for 5 min (1×); (4) hold at 4 °C. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using the following primers: GAPDH forward (5’-AGGTGAAGGTCGGAGTCAAC-3’) and reverse (5’-GAGGTCAATGAAGGGGTCAT-3’) (Integrated DNA Technologies). Expression of ADAMTS9 was quantified using a pre-validated RT² qPCR Primer Assay (Qiagen, #330001). qPCR was run using the QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR instrument from Applied Biosystems (Cat #: A28135) using default programs (i.e., Comparative Ct with Melt program in QuantStudio 5). The Sybr green reagent used was the PowerTrack™ SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Cat #: A46112).

Data and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10), unless otherwise specified. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and n denotes independent biological replicates in the figure legend. For comparisons between two groups, two-tailed unpaired t-tests were used. For experiments involving multiple groups or factors, one-way, two-way, or repeated-measures ANOVA were applied as appropriate, followed by Tukey’s or Sidak’s multiple comparisons test where indicated. Assumptions of normality were assessed using Prism’s built-in tests. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Detailed statistical information, including exact replicate numbers and post hoc tests, is provided in the corresponding figure captions. Measurements were taken from distinct samples.
