Combined effects of astaxanthin and artichoke leaf powder on growth performance and intestinal morphology of broiler chickens under thermoneutral and heat-stress conditions
Authors: Saad Naji Nasser, Mehran Torki, Fadhil Rasoul Abbas Al-Khafaji, Shahab Ghazi Harsini, Ali Ahmad Alaw Qotbi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effects of dietary astaxanthin (AST) and artichoke leaf powder (ALP) on growth performance and intestinal morphology of broiler chickens reared under thermoneutral or chronic heat-stress conditions. A total of 2,160 mixed-sex one-day-old Ross 308 chicks were distributed into two independent experiments (1,080 birds per environment) using a 3 × 3 factorial arrangement with three AST levels (0, 60, and 120 mg/kg) and three ALP levels (0, 1, and 2 g/kg) from day 1 to 35.A significant AST × ALP interaction was observed for growth performance in both environments (p ≤ 0.05). Under thermoneutral conditions, supplementation with 60 mg/kg AST + 1 g/kg ALP resulted in the highest live body weight (LBW; 2106.79 g) and body weight gain (BWG; 2068.54 g), representing increases of approximately 120 g compared with the control. This treatment also improved feed conversion ratio (FCR; 1.35 vs. 1.42 in control), reduced mortality, and increased the European Production Index (EPI; 442.36 vs. 390.12 in control). Under chronic heat stress, 1 g/kg ALP without AST yielded the greatest LBW (2144.39 g) and BWG (2105.89 g), improving BW by approximately 112 g relative to the heat-stressed control, and achieved the lowest FCR (1.42 vs. 1.49 in control) and highest EPI (425.60 vs. 385.86 in control). Dietary supplementation also markedly improved intestinal morphology (p ≤ 0.05). Under thermoneutral conditions, villus height increased from 960.13 µm in the control to 1109.06 µm with 120 mg/kg AST, while the villus height-to-crypt depth ratio improved from 4.28 (control) to 7.12 with 60 mg/kg AST + 1 g/kg ALP. Under heat stress, villus height increased from 793.13 µm (control) to 1126.92 µm with 120 mg/kg AST + 2 g/kg ALP, accompanied by an increase in villus-to-crypt ratio from 4.23 to 6.31.Overall, AST and ALP supplementation enhanced growth performance and intestinal structural integrity, with moderate inclusion levels optimizing performance under thermoneutral conditions and higher or targeted supplementation conferring protection against heat stress–induced impairment.Keywords: Astaxanthin, Artichoke leaf powder, Broiler performance, Heat stress, Intestinal morphology

Introduction

Broiler meat is a major source of high-quality protein, requiring ongoing improvements in production efficiency and sustainability (Marangoni et al., 2015; Oke et al., 2024). Modern poultry production faces increasing challenges from environmental stressors, particularly heat stress, which is intensified by climate change (Kim et al., 2025). Broilers have limited thermoregulatory capacity due to high metabolic rate and restricted heat dissipation, making them highly susceptible to elevated temperatures (Cartoni Mancinelli et al., 2023). Heat stress commonly reduces feed intake (FI), growth, and feed efficiency while increasing oxidative stress and inflammation (Oke et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2025).

A key consequence of heat stress is excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which can overwhelm antioxidant defenses and damage lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids (Hosseindoust et al., 2020; Oke et al., 2024). This oxidative imbalance is linked to intestinal dysfunction, including villus atrophy, increased crypt depth, reduced villus height-to-crypt depth ratios, and impaired epithelial barrier integrity (Greene et al., 2025). These changes hinder nutrient absorption and increase the risk of bacterial translocation and endotoxemia (Nanto-Hara et al., 2020). Nutritional strategies that strengthen antioxidant defenses and intestinal barriers are therefore essential to support poultry health and performance under heat stress (Nawaz et al., 2021).

Natural antioxidants from plants and microorganisms are gaining attention as sustainable alternatives to antibiotic growth promoters (Kouvedaki et al., 2024). Astaxanthin (AST), a red keto-carotenoid mainly from Haematococcus pluvialis, is a potent antioxidant due to its polar–nonpolar–polar structure, which allows it to integrate into cell membranes and scavenge ROS (Pertiwi et al., 2022). AST protects membrane lipids and mitochondrial integrity, reduces mitochondrial ROS, and improves electron transport chain efficiency, supporting cellular energy metabolism under stress (Kim and Kim, 2018). It also enhances the activity of key antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx), reduces oxidative stress biomarkers such as malondialdehyde (MDA), and modulates redox-sensitive signaling pathways, including nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) and B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2) family proteins, thereby reducing inflammation and apoptosis (Pertiwi et al., 2022).

Dietary AST supplementation has been shown to improve growth performance, intestinal health, and antioxidant status in broilers subjected to heat stress (Awadh and Zangana, 2020; Hosseindoust et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2025).

Similarly, artichoke (Cynara scolymus L.) leaf powder (ALP) is rich in polyphenolic compounds, particularly cynarin and various flavonoids, that exert pronounced antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and hepatoprotective effects (Zaker-Esteghamati et al., 2021). Its prebiotic fiber content also supports gut microbiota balance and intestinal morphology (Ayuso et al., 2024). Previous studies have demonstrated that ALP supplementation can enhance FI, weight gain, and intestinal villus structure in broilers (Rouzmehr et al., 2014; Shokri et al., 2018; Karimi et al., 2020).

While the individual effects of AST and ALP are well documented, their potential synergistic interactions, especially on gastrointestinal development under heat stress, remain largely unexplored. Their combined inclusion may enhance protection against oxidative damage and improve intestinal resilience (Kogure, 2019; Saracila et al., 2022). ALP, rich in polyphenols and inulin-type fructans, may act as a prebiotic, promoting beneficial gut bacteria, while AST reduces intestinal oxidative stress, supporting microbial colonization and barrier integrity (Kim and Kim, 2018; Ayuso et al., 2024). Immunomodulatory effects may be additive, with ALP modulating inflammation and AST inhibiting NF-κB, reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines and preserving mitochondrial function (Zaker-Esteghamati et al., 2021; Pertiwi et al., 2022).

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the individual and combined effects of AST and ALP on growth performance and intestinal morphology of broiler chickens reared under thermoneutral and heat-stress conditions. The findings are expected to provide novel insights into the synergistic potential of natural antioxidants for improving broiler welfare, productivity, and resilience in environmentally challenging production systems.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

All procedures involving animals were reviewed and approved by the Razi University Animal Ethics Committee (Kermanshah, Iran; Approval No. IR.RAZI.REC.1403.078). The experimental protocol adhered to European Union recommendations regarding animal welfare and feed safety throughout the study period.

Experimental design, birds, and husbandry

Two independent experiments were conducted to evaluate the effects of dietary AST and ALP on broiler growth performance and intestinal morphology under thermoneutral and chronic heat-stress conditions. A total of 2,160 mixed-sex, one-day-old Ross 308 broiler chicks (initial BW: 40.20 ± 0.27 g) obtained from Al-Anwar Poultry Company were used, with 1,080 chicks assigned to each environmental condition. In each experiment, birds were randomly allocated to nine dietary treatments. The study followed a 3 × 3 factorial arrangement in a completely randomized design (CRD), consisting of three AST levels (0, 60, and 120 mg/kg diet) and three ALP levels (0, 1, and 2 g/kg diet), selected based on previous studies demonstrating growth, antioxidant, and gut-protective effects in broilers. This arrangement yielded nine treatment combinations, each replicated six times with 20 chicks per pen (n = 120 chicks per treatment per environment). An a priori power analysis (effect size = 0.35; α = 0.05; power = 0.80) guided the determination of sample size using data from prior studies on broiler growth and intestinal morphology.

Birds were housed in an environmentally controlled, closed-sided poultry house without windows, equipped with insulated walls and a mechanical ventilation system to ensure precise environmental regulation. Litter-floor pens (1.0 × 1.5 m) were bedded with paper and wood shavings (3–5 cm depth). Feed and water were provided ad libitum throughout the 35-day trial. Circular feeders were used during the starter phase and replaced with hanging feeders thereafter, while water was supplied via a nipple drinker system. Environmental conditions were controlled according to standard commercial recommendations for broilers. Light was provided using white LED lamps (approximately 2700–3000 K) at an intensity of 30–40 lux during the first week, gradually reduced to 5–10 lux thereafter, under a lighting schedule of 23L:1D during the first week followed by 18L:6D until day 35. Ventilation was mechanically controlled using side-wall exhaust fans and adjustable air inlets to ensure continuous air exchange and uniform temperature distribution throughout the house. Ventilation rates were adjusted according to bird age and BW to maintain air quality and thermal comfort, ranging from 0.7 to 1.0 m³/h/kg BW under minimum ventilation to 6–10 m³/h/kg during heat-stress conditions. Air velocity was maintained at 0.1–0.3 m/s during brooding and increased up to 2.0–3.0 m/s during chronic heat stress to enhance convective cooling. Ammonia concentrations were kept below 10 ppm. Environmental parameters were monitored at multiple locations within the facility to minimize spatial variation and avoid confounding effects among pens. Standard commercial vaccination programs were applied, including vaccines against infectious bronchitis, Newcastle disease, infectious bursal disease, and highly pathogenic avian influenza.

Thermoneutral conditions

Ambient temperature followed Ross 308 management guidelines (Aviagen, 2022a), beginning at 32–33°C at placement and gradually decreasing to 22–23°C by week 5. Relative humidity was maintained at 45–55%. No intentional heat challenge was imposed during this experiment.

Heat-stress protocol

Broilers assigned to the heat-stress experiment were exposed to continuous thermal load from day 1 to day 35. Heat was generated by evenly distributed electric heaters (Heller PTC-5511, 2500 W, China) controlled via thermostats. During peak daytime hours (11:00–19:00 h), ambient temperature was maintained at 35.0–35.8°C (Table 1), deliberately set above the thermoneutral range for Ross 308 broilers to induce chronic heat stress, following the model of Al-Khafaji and Saidi (2019). These temperatures are biologically relevant as they are sufficient to trigger physiological and metabolic responses associated with heat stress, including oxidative stress, reduced feed efficiency, and intestinal impairment. During non-peak hours, heaters were switched off, allowing temperature to gradually decrease from 29°C in week 1 to 26°C by week 5. Relative humidity between 40 and 55% was maintained using digital hygrometers. Temperature was recorded five times daily (11:00, 13:00, 15:00, 17:00, and 19:00 h) using four calibrated digital thermometers placed throughout the facility.

Dietary treatments and feed preparation

AST and ALP were incorporated into a common basal diet according to the experimental design. AST was provided as cracked-cell algal powder derived from Haematococcus pluvialis (AST Algae 5%, Prescribed for Life, USA), while ALP was supplied as a 4:1 powdered extract of Cynara scolymus leaves (Batch No. Lex-5818-H111697, Prescribed for Life, USA). For each treatment, the required quantities of AST and ALP were weighed using a precision digital balance and initially premixed with a small portion of the basal diet at a ratio of 1:10 (w/w) to ensure uniform dispersion. The premix was then incorporated into the remaining feed using a horizontal mechanical mixer and blended for 10 minutes to achieve homogeneity. Inclusion levels were adjusted proportionally to obtain the designated final concentrations in each experimental diet.

Feed was prepared weekly to maintain freshness and nutrient stability. The chemical composition of the experimental diets was determined according to AOAC (2005) procedures.

All birds received crumbled feed formulated to meet the nutritional requirements of Ross 308 broilers (Aviagen, 2022b). A starter diet (1–21 d) containing 23.04% crude protein (CP) and 3021.45 kcal/kg metabolizable energy (ME) and a grower diet (22–35 d) containing 20.06% CP and 3194.92 kcal/kg ME were provided. Productive performance

Productive performance was evaluated at weekly intervals throughout the 35-day experimental period. Live body weight (LBW) was recorded on a pen basis using a calibrated digital platform scale (Defender 3000, OHAUS, Parsippany, NJ; 150 kg, ±10 g), and body weight gain (BWG) was calculated as the week-to-week difference in LBW values. Weekly FI was calculated as the difference between feed offered and feed refused, and then standardized on a per-bird basis. Feed conversion ratio (FCR) was computed as weekly FI divided by weekly BWG. Mortality was monitored twice daily, and all performance parameters were corrected for mortality when necessary. The European Production Index (EPI) was calculated at day 35 to evaluate overall production efficiency using the formula (Kryeziu et al., 2018):

Survival rate (%) = 100 - Mortality (%)

Although weekly measurements were collected, the final analysis and presentation of productive performance are based on the cumulative averages for the entire 1–35 day period.

Intestinal sampling and histomorphology evaluation

After 35 days, two birds were randomly selected from each replicate and euthanized in accordance with approved ethical procedures. Accordingly, 108 birds were sampled per experiment (2 birds × 54 replicates), yielding a total of 216 birds evaluated across both experiments. The abdominal cavity was opened, and the intestines were removed. A 2-cm section of the jejunum, taken from the midpoint between the bile duct entry and Meckel’s diverticulum, was collected, rinsed to remove contents, and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin.

Histological processing and sectioning were performed at the College of Veterinary Medicine, University of Baghdad, following the procedure originally described by Cook and Bird (1973) with the modifications of Bancroft and Gamble (2008). Paraffin-embedded samples were sectioned at a thickness suitable for light microscopy, mounted on glass slides, and stained using routine hematoxylin and eosin techniques.

Morphometric measurements were conducted using a binocular light microscope equipped with an ocular micrometer at 400 × magnification. Villus height was quantified as the distance from the villus tip to the villus–crypt interface, whereas crypt depth was measured from the villus base to the lowest point of the crypt, following the method described by Jessica et al. (2009).

For each sample, five well-oriented villi and crypts were measured in five non-overlapping microscopic fields. The villus-to-crypt depth ratio was subsequently calculated by dividing villus height by crypt depth, providing an index of intestinal absorptive capacity.

Digital micrographs were obtained using a Future Win Joe microscopic imaging system. The final quantification of Villus height and crypt depth was conducted using the Fiji Image Analyzer software (Schindelin et al., 2015) to ensure precise morphometric analysis.

Statistical analysis

A CRD with a 3 × 3 factorial structure was applied to assess the independent and combined effects of dietary AST (0, 60, and 120 mg/kg) and ALP (0, 1, and 2 g/kg). A two-way analysis of variance was performed on all data using the General Linear Model (GLM) procedure in SAS software (v. 9.4; SAS Institute, 2015). When the overall F-test was significant (p ≤ 0.05), treatment means were compared using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test to control for multiple comparisons. Intestinal morphology under normal temperature

The effects of dietary AST and ALP on intestinal morphology under thermo-neutral conditions are presented in Table 3. A significant main effect of AST was observed for villus height, where villus height increased from 960.13 µm in the control (0 mg/kg AST) to 1074.04 µm at 60 mg/kg AST (p = 0.0001). A further numerical increase was recorded at 120 mg/kg AST (1109.06 µm), though not significantly different from 60 mg/kg.

ALP supplementation also produced a progressive and significant rise in villus height (p = 0.0001): 933.35 µm (0 g/kg), 1087.30 µm (1 g/kg), and 1122.57 µm (2 g/kg).

Crypt depth was significantly influenced by the AST × ALP interaction (p = 0.0001). The deepest crypts were found in the control group (188.48 µm), whereas the shallowest, and thus most physiologically favorable, crypts were observed in broilers fed 60 mg/kg AST + 1 g/kg ALP (155.20 µm).

Similarly, the villus height-to-crypt depth ratio, an indicator of intestinal absorptive capacity, was significantly influenced by the interaction (p = 0.0001). The greatest villus height-to-crypt depth ratios were recorded in treatments containing 60 mg/kg AST + 1 g/kg ALP (7.12) and 120 mg/kg AST + 1 g/kg ALP (6.98), while the lowest ratio was observed in the control (4.28).

Consistently, birds fed the diet without supplementation showed the lowest LBW and BWG, measuring 2033.33 g and 1993.24 g, respectively.

A significant AST × ALP interaction was detected for FI (p = 0.0044). The highest FI values were recorded in birds fed 120 mg/kg AST + 1 g/kg ALP (3020.45 g). Conversely, the lowest FI occurred in birds receiving 0 mg/kg AST + 2 g/kg ALP (2917.38 g), closely followed by 60 mg/kg AST + 0 g/kg ALP (2924.92 g).

The AST × ALP interaction also significantly affected FCR (p = 0.0125). The poorest FCR was recorded in the control (1.49 g feed/g BWG). Improved feed efficiency was observed in several supplemented groups, with the most favorable FCRs noted in birds fed 0 mg/kg AST + 1 g/kg ALP (1.42 g feed/g BWG) and 120 mg/kg AST + 0 g/kg ALP (1.42 g feed/g BWG). Moreover, a significant AST × ALP interaction was detected for EPI (p = 0.037; Fig. 1), indicating that the combined effects of the two additives influenced overall production efficiency. The highest EPI was observed in birds receiving 0 mg/kg AST + 1 g/kg ALP (425.60), whereas the lowest value was recorded in the unsupplemented group (0 mg/kg AST + 0 g/kg ALP; 385.86). Under heat-stress conditions, however, neither AST nor ALP supplementation, nor their interaction, had a significant effect on mortality (p > 0.05; Fig. 2).

Intestinal morphology under heat stress

The effects of AST and ALP supplementation on intestinal morphology under heat stress are reported in Table 4. Villus height showed a highly significant AST × ALP interaction (p = 0.0001). The shortest villi, indicating heat stress-induced mucosal atrophy, were observed in the unsupplemented control (793.13 µm). The tallest villi occurred in broilers fed 120 mg/kg AST + 2 g/kg ALP (1126.92 µm), reflecting enhanced mucosal structure.

Crypt depth was also significantly affected by the interaction (p = 0.0001). The deepest crypts were recorded in the control (187.15 µm), whereas the shallowest and most physiologically desirable crypts appeared in birds fed 60 mg/kg AST + 0 g/kg ALP (164.48 µm).

Additionally, the villus height-to-crypt depth ratio, was significantly influenced by the interaction (p = 0.0001). The lowest villus height-to-crypt depth ratio was recorded in the control (4.23), while the highest ratios were detected in birds receiving 120 mg/kg AST + 1 g/kg ALP (6.31).

Discussion

This study demonstrated a significant interaction between AST and ALP on broiler performance under both thermoneutral and heat-stress conditions. Under thermoneutral conditions, all supplemented groups showed improved LBW and BWG compared with the control, indicating that oxidative load associated with rapid growth can impair performance even without environmental stressors (Chen et al., 2022). During heat stress, performance depression was evident in the control group, consistent with known physiological disruptions including hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis activation, corticosterone elevation, and excessive mitochondrial ROS formation (Chen et al., 2022; Oke et al., 2024). Notably, the combinations of 60 mg/kg AST with 0 or 1 g/kg ALP under thermoneutral conditions and 0 mg/kg AST + 1 g/kg ALP and 120 mg/kg AST + 1 g/kg ALP under heat stress produced the highest BWG, highlighting a dose-responsive and complementary effect between the two additives.

AST enhances animal performance by protecting cells from oxidative damage through two primary mechanisms. First, it integrates into mitochondrial membranes and directly scavenges free radicals, preserving mitochondrial integrity, sustaining ATP production, and preventing apoptosis (Kuehu et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2025; Kim and Kim, 2018). Second, AST strengthens endogenous antioxidant defenses by increasing the activity of SOD, CAT, GPx, glutathione S-transferase (GST), and heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) via activation of the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway, thereby reducing oxidative damage to lipids (MDA), proteins (carbonyls), and DNA (8‑hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine) (Kim and Kim, 2018; Kuehu et al., 2024). Under heat stress, these effects help maintain intestinal cell integrity, energy availability, and nutrient absorption.

ALP provides bioactive phenolics such as chlorogenic acid, cynarin, and luteolin derivatives that stimulate bile secretion, improve fat digestibility, and reduce intestinal inflammation by inhibiting NF-κB and pro-inflammatory cytokines (Ayuso et al., 2024). In addition, its inulin and pectin content exerts prebiotic effects by promoting beneficial bacteria, including Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus (Ayuso et al., 2024). Fermentation of these fibers increases short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production, particularly butyrate, which fuels colonocytes, strengthens barrier integrity (Occludin, MUC-1), and reduces inflammation (Holgado et al., 2022; Ayuso et al., 2024). Collectively, AST preserves cellular metabolic efficiency while ALP optimizes gut health and microbial balance, leading to improved nutrient utilization and growth, especially under heat stress.

The beneficial influence of AST on growth performance aligns with previous reports showing increased BWG under heat or oxidative stress at 20–50 mg/kg AST inclusion (Hosseindoust et al., 2020; Ulaiwi and Al-Khafaji, 2020). Under thermoneutral conditions, Jeong and Kim (2014) observed improved growth performance with fermented Phaffia rhodozyma supplying 2.3–4.6 mg/kg dietary AST, supporting the concept that antioxidant support is advantageous even without exogenous stress. However, some studies report minimal effects of AST. Waldenstedt et al. (2003) observed no performance improvement with 7 to 179 mg/kg AST under a Campylobacter jejuni challenge, and Sun et al. (2018) found no growth response at 0 to 80 mg/kg during heat stress. These inconsistencies may reflect differences in experimental design such as challenge type, duration, genotype, and age, as well as AST source and bioavailability, dosage level, and baseline dietary antioxidant status. Environmental severity is also important. Under mild stress, benefits may not be detectable, whereas under severe stress AST may function mainly as a protective agent rather than a growth promoter (Hosseindoust et al., 2020).

Biological variation in intestinal integrity, mitochondrial function, immune activation, and nutrient interactions may further explain the inconsistent outcomes. Because AST efficacy depends on the existing redox balance and endogenous antioxidant capacity, its effects are context dependent. Consistent with this interpretation, Kuehu et al. (2024) showed that although AST alleviated heat stress induced growth suppression, it did not completely restore performance, supporting its role as a physiological stabilizer rather than a direct growth stimulant.

Similarly, literature on ALP supplementation reveals a context-dependent response. Under normal temperature, Rouzmehr et al. (2014) reported improved growth with 100–200 g/ton artichoke premix. In contrast, studies using higher inclusion levels or alternative delivery routes have documented limited responses. Boroumandnia et al. (2015) found no significant effect at 0–500 mg/L artichoke extract in drinking water, and Tajodini et al. (2015) reported no improvements with 0–3% Cynara scolymus powder, suggesting that extract solubility, intestinal exposure time, and palatability may influence efficacy. These discrepancies may reflect differences in dosage form (powder vs. extract), solubility and stability of phenolic compounds, intestinal exposure time, palatability, and variability in the concentration of bioactive constituents such as cynarin and chlorogenic acid. Under heat stress, Effati et al. (2014) demonstrated that even 15–30 g/kg dietary artichoke failed to improve BWG, indicating that the bioactivity of artichoke alone may be insufficient to counteract the profound metabolic and intestinal disruptions induced by thermal challenge. Biologically, ALP efficacy depends on gut microbiota, baseline inflammation, and oxidative status (Zaker-Esteghamati et al., 2021). Thus, inconsistent findings likely reflect differences in stress severity, diet composition, extract standardization, and bird physiology, highlighting the need to evaluate ALP within specific contexts and in combination with complementary antioxidants such as AST.

FI responses varied markedly with both dietary treatment and thermal environment. Under thermoneutral conditions, broilers receiving 0 mg/kg AST + 1 g/kg ALP exhibited the highest FI, suggesting improved feed palatability and gut-mediated appetite stimulation. Conversely, the slight reduction in FI observed with the highest AST dose combined with 1 g/kg ALP (120 mg/kg AST + 1 g/kg ALP) may reflect a physiological plateau in nutrient efficiency, wherein enhanced metabolic optimization and antioxidant protection reduce the biological drive for compensatory intake. Under heat stress, the highest FI values recorded in broilers receiving 1 g/kg ALP with either 60 or 120 mg/kg AST represent a meaningful compensatory adjustment, despite the well-established reduction in FI during heat stress to limit metabolic heat production (Kim et al., 2025). The lowest FI in the 0 mg/kg AST + 2 g/kg ALP group, despite acceptable growth, suggests that excessive ALP may impair palatability or alter appetite-regulating gut–brain signaling.

These patterns likely arise from the distinct but complementary roles of AST and ALP in modulating gut physiology and systemic metabolism. ALP polyphenols (e.g., chlorogenic acid and cynarin) enhance bile secretion, gastrointestinal motility, and microbial fermentation, which can stimulate feed palatability and appetite through improved digestive signaling and increased production of microbial metabolites such as SCFAs (Zaker-Esteghamati et al., 2021; Ayuso et al., 2024). Moderate inclusion (1 g/kg) appears optimal for promoting these effects, whereas excessive supplementation (2 g/kg) may introduce bitterness or alter gut transit rates, dampening feed preference.

AST influences FI more indirectly, primarily through metabolic stabilization. By reducing oxidative stress and preserving mitochondrial efficiency, AST may lower the physiological need for compensatory intake once cellular energy balance is optimized (Pertiwi et al., 2022). Under heat stress, the combined antioxidant and gut-protective effects of AST and ALP likely enhanced thermotolerance and metabolic comfort, enabling birds to maintain higher FI without exacerbating thermal load.

The current results align partially with previous reports on AST and ALP effects on FI. Similar to our findings, Perenlei et al. (2014) and Hosseindoust et al. (2020) observed no significant FI changes at moderate AST levels under normal or heat-stress conditions, while Cao and Wang (2014) and Ohh et al. (2016) reported FI enhancement at higher AST doses or under gastrointestinal challenges, suggesting that AST’s influence on appetite becomes more apparent under oxidative or pathological stress. Ulaiwi and Al-Khafaji (2020) likewise noted increased FI at 40–60 mg/kg AST during oxidative challenge, supporting the notion that antioxidant demand modulates response magnitude.

For ALP, prior studies similarly reveal dose-dependent outcomes. Shokri et al. (2018) reported improved FI with 1–2% ALP, whereas Boroumandnia et al. (2015) and Tajodini et al. (2015) found negligible effects at higher concentrations or via aqueous extracts, likely due to differences in bioactive delivery and palatability.

FCR exhibited the clearest performance enhancement among all measured production traits, reflecting a strong treatment-dependent improvement in feed efficiency. Under thermoneutral conditions, the best FCR was achieved in broilers supplemented with 60 mg/kg AST + 1 g/kg ALP, representing approximately a 7.5% improvement over the least efficient group. Given that feed costs account for the majority of broiler production expenses, this enhancement signifies a meaningful economic gain. Conversely, less favorable FCR values in the 0 mg/kg AST + 1 g/kg ALP and 60 mg/kg AST + 2 g/kg ALP groups indicate that excessive ALP supplementation may compromise energy efficiency, underscoring the importance of maintaining an optimal AST:ALP ratio.

Under heat stress, a significant interaction between AST and ALP confirmed their functional benefits under environmental challenge. Birds receiving either 0 mg/kg AST + 1 g/kg ALP or 120 mg/kg AST + 0 g/kg ALP displayed notably improved FCR compared with heat-stressed controls, demonstrating that each additive can independently enhance nutrient utilization, albeit through distinct physiological pathways.

This improved feed efficiency likely reflects complementary effects of AST and ALP on oxidative balance, intestinal integrity, and nutrient metabolism (Zaker-Esteghamati et al., 2021; Pertiwi et al., 2022).

AST improves feed efficiency primarily by attenuating oxidative stress and preserving enterocyte integrity (Jiang et al., 2025). Its potent radical-scavenging activity reduces the metabolic cost of maintaining redox homeostasis and prevents lipid peroxidation of intestinal membranes, ensuring efficient nutrient absorption (Pertiwi et al., 2022). By maintaining mitochondrial function, AST allows a larger proportion of dietary energy to be channeled into growth rather than repair or maintenance (Jiang et al., 2025).

ALP contributes to FCR improvement through its phenolic compounds, which enhance bile secretion, stimulate lipid emulsification, and strengthen tight junction integrity via upregulation of claudins and occludins (Ayuso et al., 2024). The prebiotic inulin fraction promotes butyrate-producing microbiota, improving colonocyte energy supply and reducing systemic inflammation linked to lipopolysaccharide translocation (Samanta et al., 2013). However, excessive ALP may disrupt microbial energy partitioning, leading to inefficient fermentation and reduced nutrient assimilation.

Under heat stress, these mechanisms are particularly critical: AST mitigates ROS-induced cellular injury and supports intestinal absorptive capacity, while ALP helps maintain gut barrier integrity and microbial stability, together improving the conversion of feed energy into productive tissue accretion.

The present findings show partial consistency with previous research on AST-mediated improvements in feed efficiency. Under thermoneutral conditions, Jeong and Kim (2014) reported enhanced FCR in broilers receiving Phaffia rhodozyma–derived AST (2.3–4.6 mg/kg), while Ohh et al. (2016) observed similar improvements at approximately 100 mg/kg AST, consistent with the current study’s optimal response at 120 mg/kg. Under heat stress, Hosseindoust et al. (2020) found no improvement at lower AST levels (20–40 mg/kg), suggesting that a threshold dose is required to effectively counteract oxidative and metabolic stress.

For ALP, the literature likewise demonstrates variable responses depending on inclusion level, delivery form, and experimental conditions. While Boroumandnia et al. (2015) and Tajodini et al. (2015) observed no improvements with low-dose extract or powder supplementation, Shokri et al. (2018) and Rouzmehr et al. (2014) reported significant FCR enhancement with moderate inclusion levels (1–2% artichoke powder or 100–200 g/ton premix). In contrast, Effati et al. (2014) noted no benefits under severe heat stress, indicating that ALP alone may not fully offset the energetic burden of thermal challenge.

EPI is a measure of overall broiler efficiency, combining growth, feed conversion, and survivability. Higher EPI values indicate better production performance and lower mortality. Under thermoneutral conditions, 60 mg/kg AST combined with 1 g/kg ALP produced the highest EPI and lowest mortality (0.65%), demonstrating clear superiority over the control. The underlying mechanisms of AST and ALP have been discussed in detail in the previous sections; briefly, the response likely reflects the strong antioxidant activity of AST, which enhances growth and oxidative stability (Pertiwi et al., 2022), together with the digestive and antioxidant effects of Cynara scolymus bioactive compounds (cynarin and flavonoids) (Wang et al., 2020).

These findings are consistent with reports that Jerusalem artichoke supplementation (up to 2%) improved EPEF by 4.7–8.9% (Al-Abboodi and Jawad, 2018). In contrast, higher inclusion levels of artichoke leaf meal (2–6%) impaired FCR and EPI (Abdo et al., 2007), likely due to excessive fiber or anti-nutritional factors. The positive response observed at only 0.1% ALP in the present study suggests that low inclusion levels may optimize functional benefits while avoiding adverse effects.

Under heat stress, although overall EPI declined, a significant AST × ALP interaction was detected, with the highest EPI in birds fed ALP alone. This indicates that under thermal challenge, ALP played a more prominent role in maintaining performance, likely through its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties (Wang et al., 2020). Mortality was not significantly affected, suggesting that survival under heat stress was primarily influenced by environmental severity rather than dietary supplementation.

The present study demonstrates that dietary supplementation with AST and ALP, individually or in combination, significantly improved intestinal morphology in broiler chickens. Under thermoneutral conditions, both additives increased villus height and villus-to-crypt ratios while reducing crypt depth, indicating enhanced mucosal development and absorptive capacity. Under heat stress, control birds exhibited shortened villi and deepened crypts, consistent with previous reports showing that heat stress induces enterocyte apoptosis, villus atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, and impaired intestinal barrier function (Nanto-Hara et al., 2020; Kuehu et al., 2024).

Birds receiving 120 mg/kg AST + 2 g/kg ALP developed the tallest villi and one of the highest villus-to-crypt ratios, while even moderate supplementation (60 mg/kg AST + 1 g/kg ALP) markedly improved mucosal morphology relative to the unsupplemented stressed group. Enhanced villus elongation and shallower crypts indicate slower epithelial turnover, improved nutrient absorption, and reduced energetic costs for tissue regeneration (Wang et al., 2025). The beneficial effects observed are closely associated with the antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and gut-protective properties of AST and ALP (Zaker-Esteghamati et al., 2021; Kuehu et al., 2024). Mechanistically, AST directly scavenges ROS and upregulates key antioxidant enzymes, including SOD2, GPx3, and thioredoxin (TXN), thereby mitigating oxidative damage to enterocytes (Pertiwi et al., 2022; Kouvedaki et al., 2024). AST also inhibits NF-κB activation, reducing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and apoptosis, while stabilizing heat shock proteins (e.g., HSP70) to prevent protein misfolding during thermal stress (Kuehu et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2025). Through these coordinated mechanisms, AST helps preserve villus architecture and maintain mucosal barrier integrity.

ALP enhances intestinal integrity through multiple complementary mechanisms. The key polyphenols in ALP, including chlorogenic acid, luteolin glycosides, and cynarin, exert strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting NF-κB signaling, activating Nrf2 pathways, and preventing myosin light chain kinase (MLCK)-mediated disruption of tight junctions (Jiménez-Moreno et al., 2019; Ayuso et al., 2024). Concurrently, inulin-based prebiotics support beneficial microbiota and SCFA formation, particularly butyrate, which energizes enterocytes and promotes mucin production and barrier reinforcement (Samanta et al., 2013; Zaker-Esteghamati et al., 2021). In addition, choleretic actions improve lipid digestion and provide further antimicrobial protection within the intestinal lumen (Rodriguez et al., 2002; Surai, 2014). The superior performance of the combined additives highlights complementary mechanisms: AST mitigates oxidative and inflammatory damage, while ALP improves luminal conditions that favor epithelial renewal and barrier integrity.

The protective effects of AST and ALP may differ between acute and chronic heat stress. In acute stress, rapid oxidative and inflammatory damage predominates (Xie et al., 2015), so AST’s ROS scavenging and ALP’s anti inflammatory actions help limit epithelial apoptosis and tight junction disruption (Pertiwi et al., 2022; Ayuso et al., 2024). In chronic stress, sustained mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative imbalance, and microbial dysbiosis occur (Xie et al., 2015); here, AST preserves mitochondrial function while ALP supports microbiota and barrier integrity, providing cumulative benefits that maintain enterocyte viability and intestinal stability (Pertiwi et al., 2022; Ayuso et al., 2024).

Our findings corroborate previous reports indicating that heat stress induces villus shortening, crypt deepening, and compromised barrier function in broilers (Nanto-Hara et al., 2020; Attia et al., 2023). The improvements recorded with AST supplementation are in agreement with Gao et al. (2025) and Lv et al. (2025), who documented increased villus height and reduced crypt depth in broilers and Pekin ducks fed 20–120 mg/kg AST. Similarly, Lin et al. (2025) showed that 500 mg/L AST enhanced intestinal morphometry in Salmonella-challenged birds. ALP effects observed in the present work also align with Karimi et al. (2020), who reported improved villus morphology following 0.5 g/kg BW artichoke aqueous extract supplementation in broilers.

Conclusions

This study shows that dietary AST and ALP enhance growth, feed efficiency, and intestinal morphology in broilers under both thermoneutral and heat-stress conditions, with a significant AST × ALP interaction suggesting synergistic or complementary effects. Under thermoneutral conditions, birds fed 60 mg/kg AST with 0 or 1 g/kg ALP achieved the highest LBW, BWG, and EPI with optimized FCR, while the unsupplemented control had the lowest values and higher mortality. Under chronic heat stress, peak LBW, BWG, and EPI were observed in birds receiving either 0 mg/kg AST + 1 g/kg ALP or 120 mg/kg AST + 1 g/kg ALP, indicating that moderate ALP or higher AST supplementation can partially mitigate heat-induced performance losses. FI patterns mirrored these trends, and the poorest FCR occurred in the unsupplemented control, whereas several supplemented groups showed substantial improvements in feed efficiency. In both environmental conditions, AST and ALP enhanced intestinal morphology by increasing villus height and villus height-to-crypt depth ratios while reducing crypt depth, indicating improved absorptive capacity and gut integrity.

Collectively, these findings suggest that moderate AST and ALP inclusion is sufficient to optimize growth performance and intestinal health under normal rearing conditions, whereas higher or combined supplementation is required to alleviate heat stress–related impairments. Further research investigating molecular pathways, immune responses, and cost-effectiveness under commercial production systems would strengthen the practical application of these findings.
