Beyond the ischemic brain: Expanding single-cell transcriptomics in stroke research to extracentral nervous system systems, biological contexts, and poststroke complications
Authors: Changqing Mu, Xiaokun Geng, Ashley Schehr, Yuchuan Ding
Published: 2026

Ischemic stroke is the leading cause of death and disability in adults, with a heavy disease burden and a severe prevention and treatment situation.[1] The advent of single-cell and single-nucleus transcriptomics (sc/snRNA-seq) has transformed researchers’ ability to explore cellular heterogeneity at the single-cell level, revealing previously unknown pathophysiological molecular mechanisms in a wide range of diseases.[2] Compared with bulk transcriptomic profiling, sc/snRNA-seq can further resolve the cell types, cell states, and intercellular interactions underlying these transcriptional changes. In ischemic stroke, this technology has been widely applied to uncover diverse and dynamic pathophysiological mechanisms, including immune activation, inflammation, blood–brain barrier (BBB) disruption, angiogenesis, cell death, metabolic dysregulation, neuroregeneration, and remyelination.

The brain is the primary site of injury following ischemic stroke. Accordingly, most sc/snRNA seq studies have focused on the ischemic brain parenchyma following stroke.[3] In recent years, however, alterations in extracerebral tissues and interactions between the brain and peripheral organs after ischemic stroke have gradually attracted attention.[45] Concurrently, there has been growing recognition that stroke biology is shaped by context, such as variations in sex, age, and risk factors.[6] In addition, poststroke complications, including pneumonia, cognitive impairment, and psychiatric disorders, significantly increase mortality and disease burden and have emerged as a research hotspot.[78] These developments have driven sc/snRNA-seq beyond its traditional application in brain tissue or uncomplicated stroke, enabling the exploration of these emerging directions, the construction of single-cell atlases, and the elucidation of previously undefined pathophysiological mechanisms.

Brain-extrinsic Tissues and Systems

Sc/snRNA-seq has now been applied to a variety of extracerebral tissues after ischemic stroke, including peripheral blood, thrombi, meninges, calvarial bone marrow, spleen, gut, and heart. The results indicate that stroke not only affects the central nervous system (CNS) but also induces coordinated cellular responses that extend far beyond the CNS. For example, ScRNA-seq analysis of skull bone marrow has revealed an association between local marrow cell responses and poststroke neuroinflammation, further advancing therapeutic strategies targeting the calvarium meninges interface.[910] In addition, thrombus profiling has uncovered cellular heterogeneity in strokes of different etiologies, providing a potential basis for disease classification and targeted intervention.[11] Studies on the spleen have elucidated the mechanism of the “spleen-brain axis,” through which spleen-borne immune cells mediate secondary brain injury after stroke.[12] Studies on the heart have shown that stroke can induce immune memory, increasing the risk of cardiac dysfunction by a “brain-heart axis.”[1314] Representative studies are summarized in Table 1. These findings indicate that applying sc/snRNA-seq to extracerebral tissues is not only an expansion of sampling sites, but also helps to conceptualize stroke as a multi-organ systemic disease process. This perspective offers important translational value for improved clinical diagnosis, disease stratification, and the development of new therapeutic targets.

Stroke under Specific Biological and Clinical Conditions

A growing body of evidence indicates that the pathophysiological response to stroke is strongly influenced by biological conditions and disease context. Sex, age, and circadian rhythms are each closely associated with stroke risk, disability severity, and mortality. Specifically, female patients have a higher lifetime risk and worse prognosis,[4243] whereas clinical studies have also reported differences in outcomes between genders when receiving endovascular treatment.[43] Elderly patients show significantly poorer functional outcomes compared to younger patients.[44] In addition, stroke onset during the inactive phase is often associated with greater severity and poorer recovery.[45] Sleep duration, as an important behavioral manifestation of circadian rhythm, has also been proven to be independently associated with stroke risk.[46] Moreover, major vascular risk factors, such as hypertension and diabetes, can reshape poststroke immune responses.[7] Further, neonatal stroke and perioperative stroke represent clinical scenarios with distinct pathophysiological features.[4748] However, the precise cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying these macroscopic differences remain unclear.

Sc/snRNA-seq has emerged as a powerful tool used to dissect the heterogeneity of cellular responses after stroke across diverse conditions. Representative studies are summarized in Table 1. Among these, aging-related stroke has been the most extensively characterized, with single-cell transcriptome analyses revealing multiple mechanistic changes in aged-stroke models, including enhanced inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, impaired angiogenic signaling, and alterations in oligodendrocyte lineage.[28293031] In contrast, the findings using sc/snRNA-seq on sex, circadian rhythm, diabetes-associated stroke, neonatal stroke, and perioperative stroke have focused primarily on an immunological perspective.[3233343536] Collectively, these efforts indicate that performing single-cell transcriptome analysis under specific biological or clinical conditions facilitates a comprehensive understanding of the heterogeneity of stroke pathophysiology across different contexts and provides a foundation for precision medicine-oriented prevention and treatment strategies.

Stroke-related Complications

Single-cell transcriptome technology approaches are increasingly applied in the studies of poststroke complications, including poststroke cognitive impairment (PSCI), poststroke depression (PSD), poststroke epilepsy (PSE), and retinal ischemic injury. Representative studies are summarized in Table 1. Poststroke cognitive impairment and emotional problems are highly prevalent and impair stroke prognosis or rehabilitation outcomes.[49] Single-cell analyses of PSCI have revealed region-specific glial and vascular changes, providing new insight into the mechanisms of cognitive decline at the cellular level.[3839] Similarly, studies on PSD, PSE, and retinal ischemia have, respectively, characterized special features of cellular responses.[374041] These findings highlight how sc/snRNA-seq can offer new perspectives for investigating the pathophysiological mechanisms of poststroke complications.

Limitation and Future Direction

Despite rapid progress, current research has limitations in both breadth and depth. In terms of breadth, the application of single-cell technology in extracerebral tissues, specific biological conditions, and stroke complications is still limited. Future efforts should expand systematically to include major vascular risk factors (e.g., weight status,[50] hypertension,[51] hyperlipidemia[52]), different brain regions and infarct locations,[53] futile recanalization,[5455] hemorrhagic transformation,[56] recurrent stroke,[57] brain-peripheral axes (e.g., brain–lung,[58] brain–liver,[59] brain–kidney[60]), and important poststroke complications[8] (e.g., pneumonia, anxiety, fatigue, pain). In terms of depth, existing studies have largely focused on immune and inflammatory mechanisms, with several studies remaining at the level of descriptive cell atlases using sc/snRNA-seq, with limited functional and mechanistic validation. Future research should move beyond the current focus on immune inflammation, expanding to pathophysiological mechanisms, including BBB disruption, angiogenesis, cell death, metabolic dysregulation, neuroregeneration, and remyelination.
