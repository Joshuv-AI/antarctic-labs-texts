Chronic PPI use after anti-reflux surgery: a retrospective observational pilot study
Authors: J W Van Dam, L M Van den Dop, G Van den Brink, M W M De Waal, J C Kiefte-de Jong, M E Numans, W E Hueting
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic Proton Pump Inhibitor (PPI) use after anti-reflux surgery (ARS) remains common. Which patient factors contribute to chronic PPI use after surgery is unclear. The primary aim was to provide a long-term outcome of ARS by evaluating PPI use in the years after surgery and identify determinants of chronic PPI use after surgery.MethodsPatients aged ≥ 18 years who underwent ARS between 2011 and 2020 in a single referral center were eligible. Patient data were retrospectively assessed by medical record review. PPI data were acquired from primary care pharmacies. Chronic PPI use was defined as ≥ 180 Defined Daily Doses (DDD) per year. Patient characteristics predisposing chronic PPI use were identified. Statistical analysis included descriptive statistics and multivariable logistic regressions and a Kaplan-Meier curve.ResultsOnly 45 out of 364 eligible patients (12%) had complete data for analysis. Chronic PPI use was present in 79.5% before surgery compared to 29.5% at 1 and 2 years and 32.0% for 5 years after surgery. A median difference in PPI use of -257 DDD was found for the year after surgery compared to the year before surgery. Male gender was associated with chronic PPI use after surgery but no other determinants were found.ConclusionApproximately a third of the patients use PPIs up to 5 years after surgery. Although ARS is recommended when PPI treatment fails or to avert lifelong PPI use, quality of life and PPI use may not correlate linearly. Adequate patient selection and expectation management before surgery is paramount.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04247-6.Keywords: Anti-reflux surgery, Fundoplication, PPI, GERD

Introduction

Laparoscopic anti-reflux surgery is the gold standard in gastro-esophageal reflux disease when conservative treatment with Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPI) fails [1]. In 2018, just over 2.100 patients received anti-reflux surgery (ARS) in the Netherlands [2]. This number has been increasing every year [1]. Surgical treatment of reflux disease is focused on resolving reflux related complaints without the need for long term PPI use and improvement of quality of life [1, 3].

In the last decades, side effects of PPI use were found in multiple observational studies. Possible side effects of PPI’s consist of: risk for pneumonia, fractures, Clostridium infections, hypomagnesemia, cardiovascular complications with Clopidogrel use because of the PPI’s interaction with liver function and the risk for Vitamin B12 deficiency. These side effects were evaluated in an editorial published in the JAMA Internal Medicine in 2016 [4].

Additional severe side effects such as chronic kidney failure and PPI use were also demonstrated in more recent publication [5]. While new serious side effects of chronic PPI are becoming clearer, an intervention for halting or significantly reducing chronic PPI use appears even more relevant [6].

Effectiveness of ARS is evaluated by follow-up consults with the surgeon, measuring disease-specific complaints and the proportion of patients return to using PPIs [7]. In some cases, about 14% of patients used PPIs chronically 6 years after surgery [8, 9]. However, long-term PPI use is also reported to remain high, reports of up to half of the patients used PPIs 10–15 years after ARS [10]. This raises the question whether improved surgical procedures past years and larger numbers of anti-reflux operations might have led to different outcomes.

The aim of this study was to investigate long-term outcomes of ARS based on PPI use.

More specifically, the primary aim was to assess the proportion of patients that underwent ARS who use PPIs chronically: 1, 2 and 5 years after surgery. Secondary aims were to compare the PPI dose before and after surgery, assess the duration between surgery and restarting PPI, predicting the onset PPI use after surgery by patient characteristics.

Materials and methods

The current study entailed a retrospective observational cohort utilizing routine secondary care data to evaluate the outcome of ARS. This study was reported in accordance with STROBE (STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology) guidelines [11].

Study population

All patients aged 18 years and older that underwent ARS in the Alrijne hospital in the Netherlands between 2011 and 2020 were selected from Electronic Medical Records (EMR). Specifically, laparoscopic fundoplication, open fundoplication and surgical corrections of a hiatal hernia were considered as ARS. Indications for ARS were refractory GERD, or mechanical symptoms due to hiatal hernia. All fundoplications were performed as a 270 degrees Toupet procedure. Postoperatively, some patients continued PPIs for a short period of time in order to prevent rebound effect. Patients were seen two weeks after the procedure, and a telephone contact was conducted after six weeks. Patients were clearly instructed to return to the clinic if complaints reoccurred.

Data collection

The retrieved data concerned gender, age at surgery, type of surgery, date of surgery, body mass index (BMI) and the presence of redo surgery after primary ARS. Secondarily, data concerning PPI use after surgery were obtained from pharmacies: an overview of PPI use concerning prescription dates, alteration dates, dosages and the names of the PPI from 1 year before surgery up until 10 years after surgery (as listed in Supplemental Table 1). PPI use was determined with the following ATC codes: A02BC05 (esomeprazole), A02BC03 (lansoprazole), A02BC01 (omeprazole), A02BC02 (pantoprazole), A02BC04 (rabeprazole). PPI data were considered correct when a complete record of dispensing by the pharmacies with complete dose description resulted in a conclusive summary of PPI use over the extracted period.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was the rate of chronic PPI use after 1, 2 and 5 years after ARS. Chronic PPI was defined as a minimum of 180 defined daily dosages (DDD) a year, which converts to 0.5 DDD a day. Secondary outcomes were dose before and after surgery defined as differences in DDD, duration between surgery and restarting PPI in days, and PPI-free survival in days.

Ethical considerations

The ethical review board of the Alrijne hospital gave consent (NWMO 22 − 12 OAP) for retrieval and pseudonymized analysis of patient data from the medical charts. Since patients did not undergo additional study procedures, no informed consent was needed. However, GDPR was applicable since traceable personal data were processed in order to request PPI data from pharmacies. Therefore, patients were asked to sign an informed consent form to enable linkage. All patients included in the study gave informed consent.

Statistical analysis

Chronic use was determined at 1, 2 and 5 years after surgery and analyzed using descriptive statistics and a 95% confidence interval (CI). The Δ DDD, the difference in DDD’s between the year before and the year after ARS, was stated using a median and interquartile range (IQR). Time-to-restarting a PPI in days has been analyzed with a Kaplan-Meier curve. Predictors associated with chronic PPI use were analyzed using logistic regression analysis. Predictors with a p < 0.15 in the univariate analysis were incorporated in the multivariate analysis. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Chronic PPI use

No PPI use was found in 31.8% (14/44), 50.0% and 44.0% of the patients 1, 2 and 5 years after surgery, respectively. Chronic PPI use was present in 79.5% (95% CI: 65.5–88.8) of the patients in the year before ARS, in 29.5% (95% CI: 18.1–44.2) 1 and 2 years after surgery and in 32.0% (95% CI: 20.2–55.5) 5 years after surgery (Table 2). Additionally, no PPI use (0 DDD) was found in 13.6% of the patients before ARS, these patients underwent ARS for different reasons like dysphagia or incarceration-like symptoms.

Discussion

The present retrospective study demonstrated that a third of the patients chronically used PPIs up to 5 years after anti-reflux surgery, a mean PPI-free survival of 723 days, and a median difference in yearly doses of PPI of −257DDD, comparing the year before surgery to the year after surgery. By using data collected from pharmacies, an alternative route of PPI data collection was investigated. Some of these numbers add strength to previous research; other numbers provide new insights.

In the current study, chronic PPI use was present for approximately a third of the patients up to 5 years after anti-reflux surgery in contrast to almost 80% in the year before surgery. This proportion is higher than various clinical trials reported, but in line with the Danish nation-wide registry study [10, 12, 13]. Reasons for returning to PPI use and who prescribed the PPIs after surgery were not recorded. Possibly, general practitioners were prone to prescribe the medication due to misinterpretation of complaints after surgery. The median difference in PPI use between the year before and after surgery was found to be a decrease of 257 DDD.

The decrease of PPI use as demonstrated by the mean reduction of 257 DDD in this study could hold clinical importance for long-term health and quality of life. Lowering the need for a high dose of PPI with an intervention such as ARS might facilitate the possibility to exchange a PPI for medication with lower risks for side effects such as antacid and H2 receptor antagonists. Especially in patient groups with specific risk factors, for example kidney disease or fall tendency, an alternative for PPI use should be considered [6]. Moreover, when indicating ARS (an elective procedure for a benign condition), one must weigh the anticipated symptom relief and the benefit of reducing long-term PPI use against the operative risks and potential side effects, with the ultimate goal of improving quality of life.

Contrary to existing literature, male gender was a significant risk factor for chronic PPI use after surgery [10, 14]. The small study population might have led to this deviating outcome, although responder analysis showed no significant difference in patient characteristics. Possibly, lifestyle factors such as smoking and alcohol that are more prominent in men than women have contributed to the current found difference [15].

PPI-free survival in this study was considerably worse than stated in preceding studies. The REFLUX trial demonstrated that 38% and 44% of the patients that underwent surgery were taking anti-reflux medication at 1 year and 5 years after surgery respectively, whereas 52% of our responders had a PPI prescription directly after surgery, and 84% at 5 years after surgery [16]. In contrast to the inclusion of all patients that received any form of ARS for GERD and symptomatic hiatal hernia, the REFLUX trial only included GERD patients with PPI maintenance treatment. The decline in the first two years could be described to phasing out PPI use after surgery. The increase in PPI use by five years could be attributed to a combination of relapsing symptoms and the prescription of PPIs as prophylaxis, possibly due to other comorbid conditions becoming apparent.

Evaluation of PPI use in a longer follow-up provides an important evaluation of ARS. It contributes to better expectation management in the process of shared decision making when considering surgical options for the treatment of GERD. Exploring risk factors for chronic PPI use after ARS makes it possible to further specify the expectations of surgical treatment for specific patient characteristics. This is a first step to a nationwide evaluation of the outcome of ARS based on PPI use, that can use the same methods as followed in this study. Next to this, the actual contribution of symptom reduction by restarted PPI treatment after ARS could be evaluated in future research by relating prescription behavior to measured symptoms, comedication such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and focusing on a standardized way to extract data on PPI use after surgery in primary care. It should be stated though, that long term PPI use after ARS not necessarily infers a technical failure of surgery. In some patients, a reduced dosage of PPI in combination with surgery will also increase their quality of life. Diagnostic measures such as a pH metric test or the Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale (GSRS) could be considered for more accurate evaluation of reasons for restarting a PPI after surgery in future research [17]. In larger future study populations, PPI use after surgery could be paired to the specific surgical technique used.

Limitatons

The current study had some limitations involving the retrospective character and low response numbers due to the difficulty of acquiring correct PPI data from the pharmacies and thereby influencing the statistical power. The study’s power was limited by its retrospective design and small sample size, increasing the risk of Type II errors. Negative findings (e.g., non-significant risk factors) should be interpreted cautiously, and future studies with pre-specified power calculations are needed to confirm associations. Possibly, if the full cohort was included, patient factors like age, BMI, and type of hiatal hernia would be statistically significant. Furthermore, some patients who did not use any PPI were less prone to take part in the study and give informed consent, which likely implied responder bias. The current study did not account for an indication for PPI serving as a prophylactic agent, and might confound study outcomes. However, a previous nationwide register study stated that PPI use as a prophylactic agent could not account for the extensive post-surgery use of PPI [10]. While this study assessed BMI > 30 as a potential confounder, other lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking, alcohol use, dietary habits) and comorbidities (e.g., psychiatric disorders with antidepressants, corticosteroid use and NSAID/COX-2 inhibitor use) were not systematically collected. These unmeasured variables may influence PPI use after ARS, perhaps in subgroups such as males, who showed higher chronic PPI use in our cohort. Lastly, prescription of PPIs does not necessarily mean that ingestion of PPIs. However, prescription data is a more objective measure than self-reported data.

Possibly, PPI use as an outcome for ARS could be disputed as symptom relief may have occurred after surgery, while patients continue to rely on PPIs. This could be further investigated in studies examining questionnaires relating to GERD symptoms in combination with registration of PPI dosages [18]. However, restarting a PPI and increasing the dose during the years after surgery could represent insufficient relief of complaints.

Conclusion

This single center study showed that approximately a third of the patients chronically use PPIs up to 5 years after anti-reflux surgery. Mean PPI-free survival was 723 days, the median difference in yearly doses of PPI comparing the year before surgery to the year after surgery was − 257 DDD. This study confirms that ARS can substantially reduce patients’ cumulative PPI exposure, an outcome that is valuable given growing awareness of PPI-related adverse effects. Nonetheless, complete PPI independence is not achieved in a large fraction of cases, underscoring the need for careful pre-operative counseling, especially when PPI-free survival is the main consideration for surgery. Two thirds of patients did not use PPIs chronically, but occasional cannot be ruled out. Current study can be seen as a pilot study for more research to determine prognostic determinants for the outcome of ARS and chronic PPI use after surgery in particular.
