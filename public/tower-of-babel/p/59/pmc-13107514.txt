Impurity-Induced Localized Superhydrophobicity Breakdown
Authors: Youhua Jiang, Baixue Li, Alexander Oron
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A hitherto unknown mechanism for the breakdown of superhydrophobicity, which is the prerequisite for various interface-related applications and phenomena, is reported. When a droplet impacts onto a sphere placed atop a superhydrophobic surface with a droplet-sphere offset, even though the sphere decelerates the droplet, a smaller impact speed than in the case without a sphere impact speed is required for superhydrophobicity breakdown. When the inertia-powered liquid–vapor interface that wraps the sphere with no contact with the sphere surface dominates over the capillarity-driven resistance to interface deformation and the spontaneous spreading of the contact line on the sphere, an air cavity forms near the sphere bottom, and the cavity-collapse-induced jet toward the substrate is revealed to be the reason.

The superhydrophobicity breakdown, , signified by (local) liquid penetration into microstructures, is the transition from the liquid–vapor interfaces suspended between microstructures to the wetted sidewall and bottom of the microstructure. The stability of superhydrophobicity is one of the most important aspects in interface science because it is a prerequisite for various surface functionalities and fundamental research topics, such as self-cleaning, anti/deicing, drag/adhesion reduction, thermal management, droplet manipulation, droplet bouncing/jump, and droplet–substrate contact time. − Although superhydrophobicity breakdown in quasi-static and dynamic conditions have been investigated, those studies were conducted on clean microtextured surfaces free from impurities. −

In practical conditions, impurities pervasively appear atop superhydrophobic substrates, and intuitively speaking, such impurities hinder superhydrophobicity breakdown because the droplet contacts the impurity first and the latter deforms and decelerates the falling droplet (since the droplet–impurity contact area features no-slip), reducing its terminal speed reaching the substrate as compared to the case without impurities. For example, a 10 μL water droplet requires a critical impact speed v c ( =2Hg , where H is the release height) of 0.93 m/s to penetrate the micropillars (the sudden darkening of areas in between the micropillars) with a pillar diameter D pi of 40 μm and center-to-center spacing λ of 50 μm (Figure a and Video S1 use the speed v of 0.99 m/s for a better visibility of the microscopic liquid penetration). The ring-like areas of liquid penetration in Figure a result from the pressure buildup at the falling droplet bottom. − In comparison, when a hydrophobic aluminum sphere (0.5 mm in diameter) is placed atop the same pillared substrate, v c increases to 1.18 m/s for an aligned droplet-on-sphere collision (Figure b uses a v of 1.33 m/s), and the much larger diameter of the ring-like penetration areas results from the radial deformation of the falling droplet, providing evidence that the collision with the sphere decelerates the droplet. Since liquid penetration shown in Figures a and b results from the direct collision between the droplet and the microstructures, it is termed impact-phase liquid penetration.

When the offset distance s between the droplet center and the sphere center exists (the droplet was shifted rightward with respect to the sphere in Figure c-i and Video S2), strikingly, a singular site of liquid penetration appears on the opposite (left) side of the droplet and v c decreases to 0.54 m/s (Figure c adopts the impact speed v of 0.63 m/s for a better demonstration). The above-mentioned results suggest that a sphere placed atop a superhydrophobic surface may hinder or facilitate superhydrophobicity breakdown, depending on the droplet–sphere alignment, which can be quantified by the offset distance s.

As representatives for surface impurities, hydrophobic (treated with a layer of 1H,1H,2H,2H-perfluorodecyltrichlorosilane) and superhydrophilic (decorated with γ-AlOOH nanostructures) aluminum spheres with the diameter d s ranging from 0.5 mm to 1.5 mm and a hydrophobic aluminum wire with the diameter of 0.5 mm were placed atop the pillared surfaces. The sphere may be displaced by the impacting droplet, since it was not firmly adhered to the substrate. The hydrophobized aluminum surface features a θ a of 115° ± 3° and a θ r of 100° ± 3°, and the contact angle (<5°) cannot be effectively measured on the superhydrophilic aluminum as the droplet spontaneously spreads on it to create a film. Superhydrophobic spheres (θ a of 155° ± 3° and θ r of 145° ± 3°) were also prepared by combining the aforementioned nanostructures and chemical coating.

A deionized water droplet of ∼10 μL (droplet diameter D d of ∼2.7 mm, viscosity μ of 1 mPa·s, and ρ of 103 kg/m3) with varying release heights H ( v=2Hg as the impact speed) was allowed to impact onto surfaces with and without a deposited impurity. The droplet–sphere offset s varied from 0 mm (aligned droplet-on-sphere collision) to ∼0.6 mm by laterally adjusting the droplet position. The droplet impact dynamics and the microscopic liquid penetration were visualized simultaneously using two high-speed cameras at 20,000 frames per second from the side and bottom, respectively. The bottom camera was coupled to a reflective interference contrast microscope (RICM). The droplet release height was increased by every 1 mm, and the critical droplet impact speed v c was measured once the local superhydrophobicity breakdown (darkening of areas between micropillars) was observed.

Figure c and Video S2 show that liquid penetration occurs when the droplet base diameter is close to its initial diameter, suggesting that liquid penetration takes place before the droplet starts to spread. This rules out the emergence of a singular jet caused by a cavity in the droplet center for low-speed droplets , and the flow focusing event of a retracting droplet undergoing horizontal-to-vertical momentum redirection ,− from being the reason accounting for that in Figure c because the liquid penetration originating from these two mechanisms takes place after the droplet fully spreads. We conjecture that the sphere-facilitated superhydrophobicity breakdown results from a high-speed liquid jet caused by the coalescence of liquid–vapor interfaces moving from different sides of the sphere (Figure a), where an off-centered droplet-on-sphere collision is the prerequisite. This cavity-collapse-induced jetting is identical to the well-known Worthington jet when a sphere impacts onto a water tank (Figure b), − both of which are water entry problems despite their opposite directions. Specifically, when a droplet impacts onto a sphere with a much smaller size (d s /D d < 1), the inertia-dominated liquid–vapor interfaces move faster than the contact line on the hydrophobic sphere surface (Figure a), causing the liquid–vapor interfaces to wrap the sphere without contacting it, and hence an air cavity forms at the trailing edge of the sphere (the initial droplet–sphere contact point is at the leading edge of the sphere). This cavity collapses when the liquid–vapor interfaces coming from different directions meet, producing two liquid jets with opposite directions (Figure b): one ejects toward the sphere and the other ejects toward the substrate/air. It should be noted that in contrast to the case of a sphere entering a water tank (Figure b), the distorted droplet surface near the sphere prohibits the visualization of jets from the side view.

For the cases without a sphere (half-filled squares), the measured values of the critical droplet impact speed v c are plotted versus the predicted ones using eq for substrates with varying geometrical dimensions in Figure c, showing a good agreement. Strikingly, the cases with a sphere (filled circles) show a universal decrease in v c by up to 44% as compared with those without a sphere. Hence, the filled circles represent the droplet impact speed, and the half-filled squares represent the critical jet speeds that overcome P c that is necessary for liquid penetration of the micropillars. If the jet does not need to overcome P c or P c is negligible compared to the impact pressure of the jet, horizontally aligned filled circles would show. This suggests that the jet speed (half-filled squares) is larger than the droplet impact speed (filled circles) due to the presence of a sphere, and they are proportional to each other, agreeing with prior numerical and experimental studies showing that the jet speed is proportional to and few times larger than the impact speed of an object entering a water tank. , The facilitated superhydrophobicity breakdown with the presence of a sphere universally occurs regardless of surface topography once the jet overcomes the P c . Here, we argue that (1) the formation of a cavity and (2) its successful collapse are prerequisites for the cavity-collapse-induced jet.

The Formation of a Cavity

The cavity will not form (i) if the contact line propagates faster on the sphere surface than the liquid–vapor interface that tends to wrap the sphere, which typically occurs on a superhydrophilic surface, and (ii) if the droplet inertia is insufficient to deform the liquid–vapor interface to the extent that wraps the sphere. To corroborate the former, the off-centered droplet-on-sphere collision shown in Figure c was repeated by using a superhydrophilic sphere of the same size (Figure a and Video S2). As compared to the case of a hydrophobic sphere in Figure c, jet-induced liquid penetration cannot occur at the same droplet impact speed (v = 0.63 m/s). This is because the impact speed is much slower than the average speed of the spontaneously spreading contact line on the superhydrophilic sphere (Figure b), which was measured by allowing a pendent 10 μL droplet to touch a superhydrophilic 0.5 mm-diameter sphere in Figure c. As a result, the liquid–vapor interface cannot wrap the sphere without contacting it, and no cavity near the sphere trailing edge formed. In fact, prior studies about water entry of low-speed hydrophilic spheres also reported the absence of cavities and liquid jets. ,,

To corroborate the latter, the emergence of a jet depends on the competition between the inertia-dominated motion of the liquid–vapor interfaces that tends to wrap the sphere, governed by liquid density ρ, impact speed v, and droplet size D d of the main water droplet, and the capillarity-dominated resistance for the liquid–vapor interface distortion near the sphere, governed by the liquid–vapor interfacial tension γ LV and the sphere diameter d s . The dimensional analysis yields two dimensionless groups; one is the Weber number We = ρv 2 D d /γ LV and the other is the sphere-to-droplet size ratio d s /D d . The outcome of the droplet impact on the hydrophobic sphere for different values of ρv 2 D d /γ LV and d s /D d on the substrate with D pi = 40 μm and λ = 60 μm is shown in Figure d, where the boundary between the occurrence (filled circles) and the absence (hollow triangles) of jet-induced liquid penetration indeed follows ρv 2 D d /γ LV ∼ d s /D d with a slope of 0.04. Intuitively speaking, when the relative size of the sphere increases (d s /D d increases), a larger impact speed is required to force the liquid–vapor interfaces to wrap the sphere.

We also examined the effects of sphere surface wettability, liquid viscosity, and micropillar spacing. The droplet impact outcome does not change significantly for superhydrophobic spheres because once the cavity forms (not in the case of a superhydrophilic sphere), the cavity-to-jet process is irrelevant to sphere wettability. The smallest values of ρv 2 D d /γ LV inducing liquid penetration (the leftmost black dots for each d s /D d in Figure d) for liquids with varying viscosities (4.5- and 10-times viscosity of water with 45 and 60 wt % of glycerol, respectively; with the pillared surface remaining the same) and for micropillars with varying spacings and thus varying resisting capillary pressures P c (D pi = 40 μm; λ = 55 and 80 μm; water were used) are shown in Figure e. The data points are scattered because the viscous dissipation hinders the interface motion, and a larger pillar spacing (a smaller P c ) requires a smaller jet speed (a smaller impact speed) to penetrate micropillars, which is supported by Figure c.

The Collapse of a Cavity

There will be no jet if the cavity fails to collapse or the liquid–vapor interfaces coming from different directions cannot meet. For the aligned droplet-on-sphere collision (s = 0), direct contact between the sphere and the substrate prevents coalescence of the symmetric liquid–vapor interfaces. As a result, liquid penetration is hindered by the presence of spheres (Figure b). On the other hand, when the droplet impacts onto the sphere with an offset s ≈ (D d + d s )sin α/2, the line that connects the initial droplet–sphere contact point and the sphere center makes an angle α with the vertical axis pointing upward (Figure a). Then, starting from the initial droplet–sphere contact point, the liquid–vapor interfaces propagate surrounding the sphere in four directions: counterclockwise, front, back, and clockwise. The liquid–vapor interfaces from the first three directions meet at the location near the angle π–α from the vertical pointing up on the sphere, producing a jet with a speed of v j toward the substrate. This jet results in liquid penetration at the distance L ≈ d s tan α/2 away from the sphere center (Figure c).

The experiment shown in Figure c was repeated using a hydrophobic 0.5 mm-diameter wire placed atop the substrate (Figure a and Video S3). The jet did not occur even when the impact speed was increased to 0.89 m/s (doubled H). This is because starting from the initial droplet–wire contact point, the liquid–vapor interfaces propagate in four directions: back, front, counterclockwise, and clockwise. The first two are along the wire axis, while the last two land on the opposite sides of the wire (Figure a). Therefore, no liquid–vapor interfaces meet near the wire bottom. In contrast with this, when the wire is suspended ∼1 mm above the substrate, the liquid fronts of the 0.89 m/s-speed droplet landing on the opposite sides of the wire spread along the substrates and hence coalesce, leading to liquid penetration right underneath the wire (Figure b and Video S3). In the case with an off-centered impact, the liquid fronts on one side of the wire spread earlier, and hence, the liquid penetration occurs asymmetrically at some distance from the wire central axis (Figure c).

For offset s < 0.25 mm, the release height H leading to impact-phase liquid penetration (the square data points in Figure a) is larger than the case without a sphere (the dashed line) and decreases with an increase in s. This is because the droplet–sphere contact decelerates the falling droplet, and this deceleration decreases with an increase in offset due to a decreasing droplet–sphere overlap. The lowest H taking place near s ≈ 0.3 mm signifies the occurrence of the liquid jet (circular data points), because the offset must be sufficiently large to allow the coalescence of asymmetrically propagating liquid–vapor interfaces. Then, H increases with an increase in s since the effective (vertical) jet speed component for liquid penetration is v j cos α and the horizontal speed component determining where the jet lands is v j sin α. An increase in s leads to an increase in α ≈ arcsin­[2s/(D d + d s )] (up to 90°), thereby a decrease in v j cos α and an increase in v j sin α, which is supported by Figure b: once the jet-induced penetration emerges, the absolute value of the penetration-to-sphere distance L (≈d s tan α/2) increases with an increase in s.

By simultaneously visualizing liquid penetration into micropillars and the droplet impact dynamics, counterintuitively, we found a facilitated superhydrophobicity breakdown when impurities (e.g., a hydrophobic sphere) appeared atop superhydrophobic surfaces. This new mechanism results from the emergence of a liquid jet ejected from the collapse of the air cavity developing near the bottom of the sphere. This jet is akin to the cavity-collapse-induced liquid jet emanating when a hydrophobic sphere enters a liquid tank. Noteworthy, this jet-induced liquid penetration requires the formation of an air cavity near the sphere bottom and the coalescence of the liquid–vapor interfaces moving from different directions, corresponding to two criteria: (i) the dominance of the inertia-empowered liquid–vapor interfaces that wrap the sphere with no contact with the sphere surface over the capillarity that resists interface deformation near the sphere and the spontaneous spreading of the contact line on the sphere and (ii) off-centered droplet-on-sphere collision that enables asymmetric liquid–vapor interfaces to coalesce near the sphere bottom. This study suggests that utilization of superhydrophobic surfaces is more restrictive in real practice than previously perceived when surface impurities are pervasive.
