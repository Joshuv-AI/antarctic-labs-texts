Critical Response to the Comments on the Critical Issue of Infant and Child Mortality in Afghanistan
Authors: Giuseppe Lippi, Camilla Mattiuzzi
Published: 2025

Transparency Statement

The corresponding author, Giuseppe Lippi, affirms that this manuscript is an honest, accurate, and transparent account of the study being reported; that no important aspects of the study have been omitted; and that any discrepancies from the study as planned (and, if relevant, registered) have been explained.

To the Editor,

We read with interest Salvatore Chirumbolo's letter [1], which raises several critiques on the article published by Qamar et al. [2], dealing on the highly critical issue of the high rate of infant and child mortality in Afghanistan. While Chirumbolo seems to offer some constructive feedbacks, there are multiple areas where his arguments are apparently misleading or flawed.

First, Chirumbolo highlights the over‐reliance on secondary literature in Qamar et al.'s study as a key drawback. Although it is generally acknowledged that secondary data analysis carries certain risks, this critique fails to thoughtfully acknowledge the relative value of secondary literature, especially in complex and resource‐limited settings such as Afghanistan. Given the significant challenges associated with conducting primary data collection in underdeveloped, conflict‐affected areas, the use of secondary data is often the most practical and feasible approach. A more balanced argument could have recognized this limitation, while also suggesting that Qamar et al. expand their analysis to include grey literature, reports, or other regional studies available from various sources, and which may have further enriched their findings. Chirumbolo also emphasizes the lack of statistical analysis and novel contributions in Qamar et al.'s paper. However, this critique makes no sense. He criticizes the absence of a multifactorial/multivariate analysis, failing to acknowledge that the article was submitted as a scoping review, a methodological approach that—according to the instructions of the authors of the journal—does not require primary data analysis or advanced statistical evaluation. A scoping review is designed primarily to map existing evidence, identify knowledge gaps, and provide broad understanding of the topic, rather than conducting in‐depth statistical analysis. While Chirumbolo correctly points out the failure to examine success stories and regions with better outcomes, he misses the opportunity to address the challenges of evaluating counterfactual data in a postconflict context. The difficulty of accessing and interpreting such data in regions with instability and limited infrastructure is a key issue that Chirumbolo overlooked. Acknowledging this challenge would have strengthened his critique, as it is understandable why such data might be difficult to obtain or analyze under these circumstances. Chirumbolo also seemingly suggests that the article may be politically biased in its focus on the Taliban's role in healthcare disruption. However, he does not provide evidence to demonstrate how this focus has directly impacted author's conclusions. His critique appears to be influenced by a political stance, which seems to cloud his objectivity. As Chirumbolo is based in Italy and lacks direct experience with the specific political and health‐related challenges in Afghanistan, his perspective might be shaped by external biases rather than a clear understanding of the local context. It is generally prudent to refrain from discussing political issues of countries where accurate and reliable information on the nation's status—especially regarding the real status of the healthcare system—may be difficult to obtain, unless one has direct, firsthand experience of living in the country. For instance, Chirumbolo's recommendation to establish more healthcare facilities in rural areas fails to account for the country's critical economic situation [3], making this suggestion impractical and overly simplistic.

In conclusion, we believe that the critiques raised by Chirumbolo regarding Qamar et al.'s study are mostly unsubstantiated, as the analysis still provides valuable insights to understand the infant and child mortality in Afghanistan, within the context of the complex challenges (conflict, insecurity, and humanitarian crises) that the country faces.
