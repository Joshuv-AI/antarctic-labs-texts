Development of a Binational Clinical Registry for Emergency Care: Data Completeness Across 11 Australian and Aotearoa New Zealand ED Networks
Authors: G M O'Reilly, P Buntine, J Furyk, L Hannon, A Hermiz, P G Jones, A Rogan, E Tan, D Tham, A Wong, L Hackett, M John‐White, N Lowry, M Slaa, J Stella, S Craig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThe primary aim of this project was to test the feasibility of collating valid and useful ED data to inform the future establishment of a binational (i.e., Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand) clinical registry for emergency care. A secondary aim of this project was to utilise multi‐site ED data to assess the crude impact of COVID‐19 restrictions on overall ED presentations.MethodsThis was a retrospective observational study using data routinely collected by EDs. The inclusion criteria were all patients presenting to participating EDs between 2017 and 2020. The primary outcome was data completeness. The secondary outcome was the number of ED presentations. Variables for which there was at least 80% data completeness across all participating sites were eligible for simple demonstrations of multi‐jurisdictional analyses.ResultsThere were data for 11 ED networks and 4,405,762 ED presentations. There were just nine variables which met the 80% completeness cutoff: network and site identification, birthdate, gender, First Nations self‐identification, ED arrival date and time, ED arrival mode, triage category, ED triage date and time. Compared to the expected number of overall annual ED presentations, there was a reduction of 16% (95% CI 14% to 17%) in the actual number of overall ED presentations in 2020.ConclusionsData completeness for routinely collected data collated from multiple sites was satisfactory for just a small number of variables. These findings inform the opportunities and challenges to establishing a binational clinical registry for emergency care.

Background

Across Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand there are over 10 million Emergency Department (ED) presentations annually, which equates to one ED presentation for every three Australasians [1, 2]. Access to high quality emergency care saves lives, minimises harm, and reduces costs [3]. However, unwanted variation in access to quality emergency care occurs every day in EDs across Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand, leading to preventable deaths or harm [4, 5]. A key barrier to addressing this variation is the absence of standardised, routinely collected ED data. Without such data, it is difficult to measure quality of care, benchmark performance, identify safety issues, or design context‐appropriate quality improvement initiatives.

Clinical registries in other settings have been shown to improve clinical outcomes. They are used to capture clinical information [5, 6, 7, 8] and provide a rich data source for provider benchmarking, quality improvement, and comparative effectiveness research. Many health systems still lack dedicated ED registries and robust clinical databases, which limits understanding of patient characteristics, disease patterns, and care outcomes in this high‐throughput setting. The absence of integrated registries also constrains research capacity, hinders evaluation of new policies or models of care, and perpetuates reliance on evidence generated in different health systems that may not share the same resource constraints or epidemiological profiles. Currently, there is no binational, or even national, ED data infrastructure to help understand access to care or variation in care, measure harms, or improve the emergency care delivered in Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand—despite expected feasibility and potential impact [8, 9, 10, 11].

The primary aim of this study was to test and determine the feasibility of the collection of multi‐site ED data to inform the future establishment of a binational (i.e., Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand) clinical registry for emergency care. Specifically, this study, in establishing a large multi‐ED dataset, was intended to measure data quality, specifically the domain of data completeness [12]. A secondary aim of this project was to test the capacity of that routinely collected ED data to determine the crude impact of COVID‐19 restrictions on ED presentation frequencies across multiple jurisdictions [13]. The strong ongoing relationship of the practice of emergency care between the two countries, including the binationally led emergency medicine specialty training program, supports a similarly binational approach to emergency care benchmarking supported by a binational registry for emergency care.

Methods

This was a retrospective observational study using routinely collected ED data. The inclusion criteria were all patients presenting to participating EDs between 1 January 2017 and 31 December 2020. The study relied on routinely collected administrative data which each participating hospital sends to their local health department.

The study used the most recently available data dictionary for each jurisdiction (e.g., for hospitals in Aotearoa New Zealand, the study used the data dictionary for the National Non‐Admitted Patient Collection and the National Minimum Dataset (Hospital Events); for Victorian hospitals the study used the Victorian Emergency Minimum Dataset and the Victorian Admitted Episode Dataset) [14, 15, 16, 17]. On this basis, an informed data dictionary was created to capture variables required to answer a basic secondary research question (date of ED presentation), in addition to other related ED variables of interest for crude descriptive summary analyses. For example, one related ED variable of interest was the Australasian Triage Scale (ATS) category assigned to each patient by the participating sites, the ATS being the five‐level clinical tool used in both Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand to prioritise ED patients based on urgency.

There were 11 participating sites (ED networks) comprising a total of 19 EDs from across metropolitan and regional Victoria (14 EDs) and Sydney, Australia (two EDs) plus Aotearoa New Zealand (three EDs). Specifically, the 11 participating sites (networks) were: Alfred Health (two EDs), Austin Health, Eastern Health (three EDs), Monash Health (three EDs) and Western Health (three EDs; paediatric presentations only) in Melbourne, Australia; Barwon Health and Bendigo Health in regional Victoria, Australia; Camden and Campbelltown Hospitals in Sydney (two EDs), Australia; Auckland, Middlemore (Counties Manukau) and Wellington (Capital and Coast) Health Districts in Aotearoa New Zealand.

Participating sites, who completed their necessary research ethics and governance approval processes, were provided with the data dictionary and the import template. A deidentified sample data set (e.g., up to 1000 cases) was firstly imported into the secure REDCap Project database, where there was an initial determination of data quality. This report was fed back to participating site principal investigators, predominantly emergency physician researchers. Then once the sample dataset met initial data quality requirements for importation according to REDCap's automated preliminary data quality check, the complete dataset was requested. Where sites were unable to provide data on one or more variables but were able to provide data on the variable needed to answer the basic secondary research question (date of ED presentation), this did not preclude them from submitting data for analysis. Once the dataset included data from more than several sites, the storage and functional capacity of REDCap was exceeded. This limitation forced a variation from the initial protocol, such that the data was securely transferred (after initial data quality checks in the REDCap Project database) to the Monash University Secure eResearch Platform (SeRP).

The primary outcome, determined prior to conducting any of the feasible secondary demonstration analyses, was data completeness [12]. In order to analyse the data for this primary outcome (i.e., data completeness), data cleaning procedures were performed. The data cleaning procedure of the datasets submitted from each participating site, in turn, included: consistent coding for missing data (e.g., converting a blank space or “NULL”, “Not specified”, or “Not stated” and so forth, to a single consistent code for missingness); consistent formatting for date and time variables; conversion of implausible data (e.g., age greater than 115 years or age less than zero; non‐existent postcodes) to missing observations; conversion of triage or clinician start times to missing observations if earlier than the ED arrival time. Where, infrequently, ambiguity existed for birth date (i.e., only the last 2 digits of the year provided [e.g., “16” instead of “1916” or “2016”]), the observation was imputed, using a “single imputation” approach, on the basis of the most probable patient age given the patient mix of the study site. Specifically, the assumption for this single imputation approach, applied to less than 0.5% of the whole cohort, was that a patient's age was much more likely to be between 0 and 15 years than between 100 and 115 years [12]. For patients whose disposition from the ED did not include hospital admission, a missing observation for hospital disposition variables (hospital disposition destination, hospital disposition date, hospital diagnosis) was managed by imputing the equivalent ED disposition observation (ED disposition date, ED disposition destination, ED diagnosis), where such an observation existed [12]. Aside from removing implausible observations, accuracy and capture could not be further assessed (e.g., presenting complaint). The data dictionary did not provide an overarching categorical coding framework for the following variables, which were provided as free text: presenting complaint, ED diagnosis, birth country and hospital diagnosis.

The secondary outcome considered, for the “test” secondary research question, was the daily number of ED presentations. The exposure variable used for this simple demonstration analysis was the crude (measured in complete calendar years) time period (i.e., the 2020 year) when COVID‐19 restrictions were imposed; the comparator variable was the time period (i.e., the years from 2017 to 2019) where there were no such restrictions. The measure of association for this secondary research question was the difference between expected and actual daily presentations for the 2020 year. The expected number of daily ED presentations for 2020 was established using negative binomial regression modelling of daily presentations for the years 2017 to 2019. The final dataset used to answer the secondary research question required complete capture of the variable for “date of ED arrival”. Finally, variables for which there was at least 80% data completeness across all participating sites were utilised for simple demonstration summary analyses.

Symmetrical numerical data were summarised using mean (standard deviation, SD); non‐symmetrical numerical data and ordinal data were summarised using median (interquartile range, IQR); nominal data were summarised using frequency (%). A p‐value of 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using Stata (v19.0). Multi‐site ethics approval was obtained via the Monash Health Human Research Ethics Committee for Australian sites (RES‐20‐0000‐473A), and the Health and Disability Ethics Committee for Aotearoa New Zealand sites (20/NTB/293).

Discussion

This study is one of the largest studies to have captured ED presentations across Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand [18]. Specifically, the number of ED presentations for which data was collected, extracted, collated, cleaned and analysed was equivalent to more than 1 in 10 of the total ED presentations in Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand over the same recent 4‐year period.

Firstly, this study demonstrated the variable feasibility of quality data collection across 21 core variables, for which data is routinely collected and regionally reported, across EDs in Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand. A high level of completeness and coding ease was achieved for variables collected on arrival in EDs, in addition to time‐based variables. Conversely, a low level of completeness was the case for variables depicting presenting complaint and ED diagnosis, and similarly for variables related to the post‐ED setting. Secondarily, the simple “test” analysis demonstrated the significant reduction of ED presentations across heterogeneous contexts during 2020, compared to the estimate based on the previous 3 years. Furthermore, this study provided an opportunity to describe the distribution of age, gender, triage category and First Nations identification among ED presentations across Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand.

From this study, there were multiple lessons learned, which, together, can be used to inform efforts to establish and grow a binational clinical registry for emergency care. The major themes can be mapped according to the order of procedures. Firstly, ethics and governance procedures vary widely across sites. A data dictionary (and accompanying data extraction/importation template) is integral to optimising consistent coding, but not foolproof. Such coding needs to exist for missing data in the case of all variables. Notwithstanding the availability of a data dictionary, primary data collection and recording may not match the overarching codes (e.g., postcodes used to define overseas accommodation, the order and format of dates and times, etc.).

Existing automated data quality tests will not easily address all data quality issues; semi‐manual sorting, interrogation and inspection of data are necessary to be confident of data completeness levels until bespoke data quality programs are developed, catering to markedly heterogeneous electronic medical records (EMRs). Even within networks or regions where the EMR vendor is nominally the same, data definitions, coding, extraction and quality vary considerably. Regarding storage, commonly used sources of data importation, storage and data quality checks (e.g., REDCap) may not have the capacity to manage millions of rows of data, even for a small number of variables. And finally, while data completeness, once data is cleaned, can be measurable, data accuracy is much more difficult to verify. While clearly implausible and inconsistent observations can indicate inaccurate data, most data, if plausible, cannot be readily verified for accuracy at a point or time distant to its source. Finally, the duration of each of the steps of ethics, governance, data extraction and importation, plus iterative data quality checks and cleaning are amplified where multiple sites across different jurisdictions are contributing large volumes of data to a central repository.

There remain, of course, additional limitations with the utility of the data, in terms of analysis and interpretation of findings, in such a large volume multi‐site, multi‐jurisdiction database. EDs are not uniform with regard to their accessibility to all people in their geographical realm; they are heterogeneous in terms of their designation, in terms of gender, age groups, severity of illness, and types of presenting concern (e.g., referral centres for major trauma, burns or transplant patients). While intra‐country jurisdictions demonstrated variations in collected data, including completeness, summary measures and major differences in the data between countries were not particularly prominent, notwithstanding the proportion of people presenting to EDs self‐reporting as First Nations people. For example, levels of data completeness and the distribution of demographic and processes of care variables, demonstrated as much variation within each of the two countries as there was variation between them.

Notwithstanding the limitations and lessons learnt from managing large volume, heterogeneous, multi‐site, multi‐jurisdictional data, this study has highlighted, as a quasi‐pilot for a future clinical quality binational (Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand) registry for emergency care, the opportunities, value and challenges of establishing such a database. Building on this foundation and lessons learnt, there is potential to establish a powerful tool for benchmarking and thereby improving the quality of emergency care. Some key next steps to progressing a useful binational clinical quality registry for emergency care include: identify all relevant stakeholders; establish the governance structure and procedures; start with the simplest of datasets (i.e., the core dataset) which will inform initial quality improvement activities and benchmarking; map out a future (extended) dataset representing all quality domains in health, including patient‐centredness; develop the data management protocol, including procedures for extraction and data quality testing; and maximise utilisation (i.e., benchmarking and site‐level reporting) early [19, 20].

Conclusion

Across all participating sites, data completeness and coding ease were satisfactory for a small proportion of the core variables. Notwithstanding the low level of data completeness, this data was able to retrospectively test and confirm the existing impression that the year (2020) in which COVID‐19 restrictions were first imposed in Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand was associated with a crude reduction in presentations to participating EDs. While feasible for EDs across Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand to provide basic raw data regarding ED presentations, there are multiple steps that need to be taken between the first commitment to participate and the timely realisation of report‐generation to inform benchmarking and quality improvement.
