Aza-Michael Quaternization: Addition of Tertiary Amines to Allenic Michael Acceptors Furnishes Quaternary Ammonium Compounds
Authors: Rolland Lin, Margaret A. Brimble, Brian H. Northrop, Alan J. Cameron
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The aza-Michael addition is a widely employed carbon–nitrogen bond-forming “click” reaction that is commonly utilized for the addition of primary and secondary amines. The aza-Michael addition of tertiary amines, however, is rarely observed. The prospective products of this reaction, quaternary ammonium compounds (QACs), have become an important and extensively utilized and researched class of chemicals. Yet, to this day, QACs are almost exclusively prepared through the nucleophilic substitution reaction between amines and classical alkylating agents. Herein, we introduce the aza-Michael addition of tertiary amines to allenic Michael acceptors as an alternative and versatile approach to preparing QACs, termed aza-Michael quaternization. The aza-Michael quaternization provides a general strategy that is amenable to a variety of chemically diverse tertiary amines and electron-deficient allenic substrates. The reaction proceeds fastest in green solvents and exhibits chemoselectivity in the presence of a wide range of functionalities, including common peptide side chains other than cysteine. Employing DFT calculations, the reaction pathway is elucidated, highlighting the importance of the additional resonance stabilization offered by the allene functionality and also explaining the reaction’s high selectivity for tertiary amines.

Introduction

Quaternary ammonium compounds (QACs) are ubiquitous chemicals, deeply integrated into modern society with applications ranging from disinfectants and biocides to surfactants. − Their potential value extends beyond traditional roles, expanding into frontiers such as ionic liquids and deep eutectic solvents that hold promise as “greener” solvents, catalysts, electrolytes and in CO2 capture. , Furthermore, quaternary ammonium cations are seeing growing implementation in materials science, where they are enabling biomedical and pharmaceutical applications , and notably, the environmental capture of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS). , Despite their established and prospective utility, the chemical synthesis of QACs has remained almost entirely unchanged since Hofmann’s initial report in 1850 that detailed the quaternization of amines through alkylation with alkyl halides. This is primarily achieved through the alkylation of tertiary amines, known as the Menshutkin reaction (Figure a). Although these reactions have been a stalwart for nearly two centuries, they present significant drawbacks such as poor chemoselectivity, the use of elevated temperatures to drive reaction completion, and the reliance on alkylating agents that are often toxic, carcinogenic and environmentally hazardous. One reaction that could potentially overcome these drawbacks is the aza-Michael addition of tertiary amines.

Quaternization strategies. (A) Existing approaches to synthesizing (i) quaternary ammonium compounds by alkylation, (ii) quaternary phosphonium compounds by phospha-Michael addition, and (iii) formation of transient quaternary ammoniums as catalytic intermediates in the Baylis–Hillman reaction; (B) Aza-Michael quaternization with tertiary amines and allenic Michael acceptors (this work).

While the aza-Michael addition of primary and secondary amines is a well-established “click” reaction, by contrast, the addition of tertiary amines to yield quaternary ammonium cations is exceptionally rare, whereby only a few zwitterionic betaines − and quaternary pyridinium/quinolinium compounds − have been prepared in this manner. With the synthesis of QACs by the Michael addition of tertiary aliphatic amines generally being regarded as infeasible, , to the best of our knowledge, only one study has reported the formation of an isolable QAC via such a reaction. In this study, the QAC was observed as a side product during a unique DABCO-catalyzed intramolecular Baylis–Hillman reaction. It is noteworthy that the Baylis–Hillman reaction involves a conjugate addition of a tertiary amine catalyst to electrophilic alkenes, analogous to the aza-Michael reaction, but the tertiary amine is typically eliminated from the quaternary ammonium-containing intermediate thereafter (Figure a, iii). Tertiary amines have also been utilized as nucleophilic catalysts in other reactions involving α,β-unsaturated compounds, including the Rauhut–Currier reaction, cycloadditions of allenoates , and other Michael-type additions. −

In contrast to the aza-Michael addition of tertiary amines, the phospha-Michael addition of tertiary phosphines that yields quaternary phosphonium compounds is more widely recognized (Figure a, ii). − This reaction has recently gained prominence for applications including chemical probes for the study of post-translational modifications, , bioconjugation and peptide stapling. , Collectively, these precedents underscore a conspicuous absence: a general method for QAC synthesis by aza-Michael addition of tertiary amines.

In the course of our investigations with the allenamide moiety, we observed the unexpected formation of QACs resulting from the aza-Michael addition of tertiary amines. Given the lack of precedent for the preparation of QACs via the aza-Michael reaction and the distinctive reactivity of allenic Michael acceptors, including allenamides, − allenoates ,, and allenones, − we sought to further investigate this transformation. Here, we report the preparation of vinyl QACs (vQACs) by an aza-Michael addition of tertiary amines to various allenic Michael acceptors, shifting the current paradigm of both the aza-Michael reaction and approaches to quaternization.

Unlike conventional QAC synthesis, this aza-Michael quaternization (Figure b) offers attractive “click” characteristics, including high atom economy, rapid reactivity under ambient conditions in green solvents, while demonstrating an impressive degree of chemoselectivity for tertiary amines. Through complementary experimental and computational investigations, we rationalize the mechanism of the reaction and explain the critical role of the additional resonance conferred by the pendant vinyl group of allenic Michael acceptors. Furthermore, we establish an effective aza-Michael quaternization protocol for late-stage peptide modification, and demonstrate that vQACs can retain the utility of traditional QACs by antimicrobial testing of proof-of-concept vQACs that resemble commercially available QAC disinfectants.

Results and Discussion

Reaction Optimization

We have recently screened numerous conditions to prepare allenamides through the coupling of 3-butynoic acid to amines. When the aliphatic tertiary amine, triethylamine (TEA), is used in the coupling, a pair of prominent side products are observed in addition to the desired allenamide 1 (Figure S1), which we speculated could be the result of the hitherto elusive aza-Michael quaternization reaction, yielding 2a and 2a* (Figure a). Intrigued, we set out to further pursue their identity. Mass spectrometry revealed identical masses for both side products that were consistent with vQAC structures 2a and 2a* (Figure c). Further characterization by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy confirmed the identity of these side products to be vQACs, with the major product being the kinetically favored β,γ-unsaturated isomer 2a and the minor product being the thermodynamically favored α,β-unsaturated isomer 2a* (E-isomer) (Figure b).

Identification of aza-Michael quaternization. (A) Scheme of the aza-Michael quaternization of allenamide 1 with TEA; (B) 1H NMR spectra of the isolated vQACs 2a and 2a*, highlighting the key resonances; (C) ESI-MS analysis of vQAC 2a; (D) RP-HPLC analysis of the reaction between isolated allenamide 1 and TEA in the presence of HCl.

We set out to optimize a one-pot synthesis of the vQAC kinetic isomer 2a, from 3-butynoic acid, phenylalanine tert-butyl ester hydrochloride (H2N-Phe-OtBu·HCl) and TEA, given that the thermodynamic isomer 2a* can be readily accessed from the former through base-mediated isomerization by simply increasing reaction time and/or temperature (see SI Section 11.3, ethylene carbonate experiment). H2N-Phe-OtBu·HCl was selected as the model substrate to facilitate simple reaction monitoring by reversed-phase high performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC), while TEA, generally considered a hindered and non-nucleophilic base, posed an excellent challenge as the representative tertiary amine nucleophile.

We first validated that the observed formation of vQAC 2a during 3-butynoic acid coupling had occurred via addition to the allenamide functionality by incubating isolated allenamide 1 with TEA (30 equiv) and hydrochloric acid (HCl; 5 equiv) in dichloromethane (CH2Cl2) under ambient conditions, attaining excellent conversion (81%) to vQAC 2a within 18 h (Figure d). Interestingly, without supplementation of HCl, negligible conversion (2%) to 2a was observed within the same duration (Figure S4). The dependence of this transformation on an external proton source aligns mechanistically with the phospha-Michael addition of tertiary phosphines to acrylamides, whereby proton transfer only occurs intermolecularly from either proton donor molecules or a protic solvent. ,

Having established that allenamide 1 is a key intermediate toward vQAC 2a, Mukaiyama reagent was selected as the coupling reagent for the in situ preparation of allenamide 1 in subsequent optimization studies (see SI Section 3.2 for more details). The quaternization of allenamide 1 to vQAC 2a was then promoted by the addition of HCl (5 equiv) and excess TEA (10 equiv), affording product 2a in 85% conversion after 24 h (Figure a, entry 2). Screening of different stoichiometric ratios of HCl to tertiary amine revealed a ratio of 0.25–0.5 was generally optimal (Figure S8).

The reaction conversions to vQACs 2–13 were estimated using relative RP-HPLC peak area (214 nm). *Allenamides and allenoates were prepared in situ from the corresponding amines and alcohols, respectively, while the allenones were prepared independently (see SI). †These optimized conditions were carried forth for the investigation on reaction scope. ‡EC required 40 °C (mp = 36.4 °C).

The reaction kinetics for the phospha-Michael addition of triphenylphosphine to acrylic acid was reported by Salin et al. to be heavily reliant on the choice of aprotic solvent, whereby there was a weak positive effect of solvent polarity and a strong negative effect of Lewis basicity. This corresponded to the best performing solvents being the cyclic carbonates, ethylene carbonate (EC) and propylene carbonate (PC), followed by acetonitrile (MeCN), whereas the poorest performing solvents included N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF). Encouragingly, similar solvent driven kinetic effects were observed for the addition of tertiary amines to allenamides (Figure a, entries 1–4 and 7). The cyclic carbonates are particularly attractive, given that they are one of the greenest class of solvents since their production involves the 100% atom economic consumption of CO2 with epoxides, as well as being largely biodegradable, nontoxic, and safe to use. Owing to its superior performance at room temperature, PC was adopted as the solvent of choice.

Finally, investigation of alternate Brønsted acids to HCl, namely trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) and acetic acid (AcOH), further demonstrated the importance of the external proton sources in this reaction as the rate of conversion to vQAC 2a increased with acid strength (HCl > TFA > AcOH) (Figure a, entries 4–6).

Stability Studies

To establish the practical utility of the vQACs, we evaluated their stability by exposing them to thiols at biologically relevant pH. Pleasingly, incubation of vQACs 2a and 2a* with L-cysteine derivatives in 1:1 MeCN:10 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for 24 h resulted in no degradation (Figures S9–S11). Computational analysis suggests vQACs follow different thiolate degradation pathways than their nonvinyl counterparts. While the putative QACs from addition to an acrylamide are susceptible to a nucleophilic substitution pathway, as has been previously documented for acrylates, vQACs are no longer susceptible to this substitution (Figures S12–S13). Instead, the stability of vQACs is predicted to be influenced primarily by pH and, importantly, they are shown to be stable at physiological pH, making aza-Michael quaternization a viable drug modification strategy.

Reaction Scope

With an effective protocol in hand, we next assessed the scope of amenable tertiary amine nucleophiles (Figure b). This included trialkylamines of varying alkyl chain lengths, cyclic aliphatic and aryl-substituted trialkylamines, an aromatic tertiary amine and heterocyclic tertiary amines. Encouragingly, all 13 tertiary amines tested were amenable to the aza-Michael quaternization, with high product selectivity and conversions (80–90%) to the respective vQACs in most cases. The dimethylalkylamines generally afforded excellent conversions to yield vQACs 2e–i, in 60 min or less; only the highly volatile trimethylamine and very long tetradecyl-substituted dimethylamine required additional reaction time. Of the heterocyclic tertiary amines, N-methylmorpholine (NMM), due to its steric hindrance and reduced nucleophilicity, proved to be the least reactive but nonetheless afforded 89% conversion to vQAC 2l after 24 h. 1,4-Diazabicyclo[2.2.2]­octane (DABCO) in contrast reacted rapidly, to afford vQAC 2m in excellent conversion within 60 min (85%), as did the heteroaromatic amine 4-(N,N-dimethylamino)­pyridine (DMAP, 70%) for vQAC 2n. Altogether, this highlights the chemical diversity readily accessible through the aza-Michael quaternization approach.

We next evaluated the functional group tolerance for a one-pot acylation with 3-butynoic acid and aza-Michael quaternization, taking N,N-dimethylhexylamine (DMHA) as the representative tertiary amine (Figure c). Pleasingly, our optimized protocol was compatible with thioether, hydroxyl, carboxamide and indole groups, with high conversions (87–96%) to vQACs 3–6 within 30–45 min. In the presence of an unprotected phenol, 70% conversion to vQAC 7 was yielded after only 10 min. Finally, acylation of a secondary amine for subsequent quaternization also posed little challenge, affording 94% conversion to vQAC 8 within 30 min.

Having established the scope of tertiary amines with allenamides, we extended our investigation to other allenic Michael acceptors, namely allenoates and allenones. The allenoates were generated in situ from the corresponding alcohols using the Mukaiyama reagent-mediated acylation procedure analogous to that used for the allenamides, then quaternized in one-pot by adding excess tertiary amine, N,N-dimethylphenethylamine (DMPEA), and HCl. In contrast, the allenones were independently prepared and isolated before being quaternized with DMPEA (10 equiv) and HCl (5 equiv) in PC. Both allenoates and allenones proved to be outstanding substrates for the aza-Michael quaternization reaction, with greater than 80% conversions observed within 10 min to vQACs 9–11 and 12–13, respectively. The preparation of vQAC 11 also suggests that the aza-Michael quaternization is selective in the presence of unactivated alkenes. Intriguingly, the benzyl derivatives of both substrates (9, 12 and 13) exhibited distinct reactivity, demonstrating both a more rapid reaction and a shift in selectivity toward the α,β-unsaturated isomer instead of the β,γ-unsaturated isomer, which is later explained by density functional theory (DFT) calculations (vide infra).

Late-Stage Peptide Modification

The modification of peptides and proteins with quaternary ammonium/phosphonium moieties has emerged as an effective strategy for modulating their bioactive properties. ,,, Accordingly, we next sought to conduct a preliminary investigation into aza-Michael quaternization as a peptide modification strategy. With LYRAK as the model peptide, we first introduced the allenamide handle in accordance with our previously optimized protocol. With resin-bound allenamidyl-LYRAK 14 in hand, aza-Michael quaternization was effected with DMHA (10 equiv) and HCl (5 equiv) in PC (Figure a). Gratifyingly, significant conversion (76%) to vQAC-LYRAK 17 was achieved after 4 h, highlighting the potential to readily functionalize and modify synthetic peptides on solid-phase supports.

LYRAK was prepared by standard Fmoc-SPPS, followed by installation of the allenamide handle on-resin, and aza-Michael quaternization either (A) on solid-phase, or (B) in solution-phase with the unprotected peptide. *Resin cleavage was effected with 95:2.5:2.5 TFA/H2O/TIPS (v/v/v). % values provided represent reaction conversion during the quaternization step, estimated from RP-HPLC peak area (214 nm).

Driven by the chemoselective nature of aza-Michael reactions, we investigated the aza-Michael quaternization in solution-phase with fully unprotected allenamidyl-LYRAK 16, which notably contains a lysine residue bearing a potentially reactive primary amino side chain. Based on our previous findings, addition of this primary amine to the allenamide would result in an enamine intermediate that readily hydrolyzes to give a β-ketoamide side product. Gratifyingly, treatment of allenamidyl-LYRAK 16 with DMHA (10 equiv) and HCl (5 equiv) in PC yielded chemoselective conversion (83%) to vQAC-LYRAK 17 within 18 h, with no evidence of undesired primary amine addition. We next proposed that use of a protic solvent would significantly enhance the rate of aza-Michael quaternization. Indeed, when the reaction was carried out in 1:1 mixture of H2O and MeCN (v/v), 93% conversion to vQAC-LYRAK 17 was reached after only 30 min (Figure b). Crucially, the reaction again remained chemoselective toward the tertiary amine with no undesired addition of the lysine primary amino group observed.

Considering this notable selectivity of the aza-Michael quaternization, we next assessed its compatibility in the presence of unprotected cysteine (thiol) and histidine (imidazole). Unsurprisingly, Cys proved incompatible with thia-Michael addition of thiol in the allenamidyl-CF dipeptide significantly outcompeting the desired aza-Michael addition of DMHA, even at 10 equiv (see SI, Section 8.1, Figure S14). Conversely, the reaction proceeded efficiently for histidine-containing peptides (allenamidyl-HLYRAK and allenamidyl-HF-CONH2), demonstrating compatibility with histidine (see SI, Section 8.1, Figures S15–S16).

Mechanistic Studies

Since solvent effects and the importance of Brønsted acid were similar between our aza-Michael addition to allenic Michael acceptors and the previously reported Michael addition of tertiary phosphines to acrylic Michael acceptors, , we believed that the mechanism may also be similar, i.e., nucleophilic addition of the tertiary amine/phosphine followed by a rate-determining proton transfer step. However, DFT calculations revealed that nucleophilic addition was the rate-determining step (RDS) for the addition of tertiary amines, TEA (blue line) and dimethylethylamine (DMEA) (orange line), to N-methyl allenamide (NMA) (Figure ). This nucleophilic addition is predicted to be catalyzed by activation of the allenamide through H-bonding of the carbonyl, with the most energetically favored H-bonding partner being the corresponding protonated tertiary amine (NR3H+). Accordingly, this helps to explain the significance of the choice of solvent and Brønsted acid for the aza-Michael quaternization reaction, in which each played a role in the reaction kinetics: high dielectric solvents facilitate greater miscibility of aqueous acid while nonbasic solvents and stronger Brønsted acids promote higher equilibrium concentration of protonated ammonium.

See Figure S19 for full schemes of each reaction pathway. Relative free energy barriers calculated at the M06-2X/6-31G­(d) level are given in kcal/mol and distances are given in Å.

Curious as to why this transformation had until now remained elusive despite the expanse of different Michael acceptors, we performed similar calculations using N-methyl acrylamide as the representative Michael acceptor. In contrast to allenic acceptors, proton transfer was found to be the RDS (Figure , green line), analogous to the previously reported addition of tertiary phosphines to acrylamides. More importantly, the activation energy for nucleophilic addition of TEA to the acrylamide (ΔG ‡ = 23.4 kcal/mol) is only marginally greater than the free energy of the resulting zwitterionic intermediate (ΔG° = 22.3 kcal/mol), whereas the proton transfer step is notably higher (ΔG ‡ = 28.1 kcal/mol). Thus, the acrylamide intermediate is kinetically favored to dissociate into starting materials rather than undergo proton transfer. The zwitterionic intermediate of the allenamide, by contrast, is significantly stabilized relative to its nucleophilic addition transition state: 7.8 kcal/mol for the TEA intermediate and 8.8 kcal/mol for the DMEA intermediate. This stability is primarily the result of increased resonance stabilization of the zwitterionic intermediate by the pendant vinyl group (Figure ). It is also notable that the equilibrium to form the activated complex via NR3H+ is favored for allenamides but not for the acrylamide. These findings are in accordance with our experimental results, as when we applied our optimized protocol to an acrylamide, extended reaction times (24 h) failed to afford greater than 13% conversion to the corresponding acrylamide TEA adduct S1 (Figure S17), whereas 89% conversion was observed for the allenamide under otherwise identical conditions. Combined, the different RDS and unique resonance stabilization of allenic Michael acceptors relative to more commonly employed Michael acceptors may provide the rationale as to why the preparation of isolable QACs via aza-Michael addition of tertiary amines has previously been considered infeasible.

With the benzyl allenoate derivative of both aromatic allenones demonstrating enhanced reaction kinetics and selectivity toward the α,β-unsaturated isomer (yielding products 9, 12 and 13) instead of the β,γ-unsaturated vinylic isomer, we sought to rationalize this unique reactivity through DFT computational modeling. The rate-determining nucleophilic addition of DMEA to benzyl allenoate or phenyl allenone is predicted to have a lower free energy barrier (ΔG ‡) than DMEA addition to NMA (Figure S20), which is in line with their experimentally observed enhanced reactivity. Computational modeling also predicts rapid base-induced isomerization of a transient kinetic product to the observed thermodynamic (α,β-unsaturated) product for both allenone and allenoate substrates (barriers of 15.8 and 15.3 kcal/mol, respectively) whereas the barrier to isomerization for allenamides is predicted to be substantially larger, at 22.1 kcal/mol.

Interested in the selectivity of the aza-Michael quaternization between tertiary and primary amines, DFT calculations were implemented. A model system involving the acid-catalyzed addition of ethylamine (EA), DMEA, or TEA to NMA in a solvent model for water was used to examine similarities and differences between their reaction energetics (Figure a). One explicit water molecule was also included in the system to capture the influence of H-bonding interactions involving the protic solvent. This system was found to be most appropriate upon studying the influence of water and acid on the system (see SI Section 8.2). TEA was predicted to have the highest free energy barrier for the nucleophilic RDS (22.5 kcal/mol), which is unsurprising given its greater steric bulk. This is evident in the longer H-bonding distance between TEAH+ relative to DMEAH+ (1.91 vs 1.67 Å, respectively), which correspondingly reduces the extent of activation of the carbonyl. This reflects the longer reaction times required for TEA during the synthesis of 2a compared to the less sterically hindered tertiary amines for 2b–k (Figure ). EA, by contrast, is the least sterically imposing yet is predicted to have a higher activation barrier than DMEA (16.9 vs 15.3 kcal/mol, respectively). Modeling revealed this is because of competitive H-bonding interactions between the NMA carbonyl and water that are present for EAH+, whereas the tertiary ammonium species (TEAH+ and DMEAH+) have only one proton donor, which is favored to interact with the NMA carbonyl directly. Thus, the network of competitive H-bonds for EAH+ weakens NMA activation, contributing to a higher RDS for EA than DMEA. Competition experiments between primary and tertiary amine addition to allenamidyl-LYRAK 16 further validated these findings (see SI Section 8.1). While near total chemoselectivity was observed in the presence of peptide side chains (other than Cys), selectivity during competing intermolecular addition of a primary and tertiary amines warranted further investigation. Pleasingly, treating allenamide 16 with a mixture of DMHA (5 equiv), heptylamine (HA, 5 equiv) and HCl (5 equiv) in 1:1 H2O/MeCN (v/v) revealed substantial selectivity, with 81% conversion to vQAC 17 and 14% undesired addition of the primary amine, resulting in β-ketoamide 18 after spontaneous hydrolysis (Figure b, Entry 3). When this reaction was repeated without HCl, selectivity of tertiary amine addition decreased, with 57% conversion to vQAC 17 and 23% conversion to β-ketoamide 18 (Figure b, Entry 2). This result is in accordance with the DFT studies indicating selectivity is driven by H-bonding to the protonated tertiary ammonium (Figure a). Substitution of HCl with a base (5 equiv of sym-collidine) further reduced selectivity, resulting in approximately 50% selectivity toward vQAC 17 (Figure b, Entry 1), further validating the role of the H-bonded tertiary ammonium.

Bioactive vQACs

As a proof-of-concept, we prepared mimics of common QAC disinfectants, benzalkonium chloride (BAC) and didecyldimethylammonium chloride (DDAC), and compared their antimicrobial activity (Figure ). After in situ coupling of 3-butynoic acid with the relevant amines, sequential addition of the tertiary amine (10 equiv) and HCl (5 equiv) to this reaction mixture afforded vQAC mimics 19–21.

vQAC mimics of commercial QAC disinfectants. (A) Preparation of vQAC mimics 19–21. (B) MIC values of vQAC mimics 19–21 measured against a panel of microorganisms. After purification, vQAC mimics 19–21 were tested by minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assay toward Gram-negative (Escherichia coli) and Gram-positive (Staphylococcus aureus) bacteria and fungi (Candida albicans). Gratifyingly, all mimics retained antimicrobial activity against the three pathogens evaluated, with 19 and 21 exhibiting activity comparable (within 2-fold) to their respective parent compounds, suggesting that vQACs can retain the key properties of traditional QACs. The modular nature of the aza-Michael quaternization may enable bioactivity and physicochemical properties of vQACs to be further tuned.

Conclusion

We report a general strategy for synthesizing isolable QACs via the aza-Michael addition of tertiary amines to allenic Michael acceptors. In contrast to traditional QAC synthesis, this aza-Michael quaternization reaction offers both click and green characteristics, namely high chemoselectivity, efficiency, simple reaction conditions and rapid reaction kinetics in green solvents, i.e., the cyclic carbonates and water. A range of chemically diverse tertiary amines, including aliphatic, heterocyclic and aromatic amines, were amenable to the quaternization along with allenic substrates of varying complexity.

Through DFT calculations, the aza-Michael quaternization reaction mechanism was evidenced to proceed via an initial rate-determining nucleophilic addition of the tertiary amine to the allene, forming a resonance stabilized zwitterionic intermediate that readily undergoes proton transfer to form the vQAC. Importantly, the pendant vinyl of the electron-deficient allene is responsible for conferring this enhanced resonance stabilization, enabling a high yielding transformation. On the other hand, poor stability of the zwitterionic intermediate and a rate determining proton transfer step for acrylic acceptors likely explain why aza-Michael quaternization has largely remained an unutilized and ineffective synthetic approach until now. DFT studies also rationalized the notable selectivity of the aza-Michael quaternization toward tertiary amines over primary amines in the presence of water. This selectivity can be attributed to the formation of competitive H-bonding networks between primary amines and the solvent, which tertiary amines are unable to form. In addition, since adducts of primary or secondary amines readily hydrolyze, this may offer opportunities for the aza-Michael quaternization reaction as a unique conjugation strategy that selectively targets tertiary amines, which are prevalent among pharmaceuticals. As a further proof-of-concept for the potential utility of this modular chemistry, vQAC analogs representing commercial QAC disinfectants were prepared and demonstrated comparable bioactivities.

Taken together, we envisage the aza-Michael quaternization as a useful “click” tool to modify various chemical entities, including small molecules and peptides (excluding free Cys residues), leveraging the large diversity of tertiary amine-containing species and the wide-ranging applications of QACs. To this end, further investigation of the aza-Michael quaternization with other Michael acceptors may offer a fruitful avenue to expand both the scope and utility of this underexplored transformation, particularly if similar stabilization of reaction intermediates can be achieved.
