Exploring pregnant women’s experiences of a digital self-care intervention for gestational diabetes mellitus in Vietnam: a qualitative study
Authors: Ai Thi Nguyen, Ngoc-Anh Thi Dang, Thi Kim Dung Vu, Thi Minh Phuong Nguyen, Thi Thuy Hanh Nguyen, Ib C Bygbjerg, Tine M Gammeltoft
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPregnant women with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) face complex challenges in managing their condition, especially in low-resource settings with limited healthcare and social support. Digital health interventions show promise, but their real-world applicability in rural contexts remains underexplored.ObjectiveThis study explores the experiences of women using an online intervention for GDM self-care in Thai Binh, Vietnam.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted between February and December 2024 involving 21 pregnant women diagnosed with GDM. Participants were enrolled in a digital self-care program and interviewed in-depth during late pregnancy (29–39 weeks of gestation). The study drew on ethnographic methods, including attention to women’s contexts and the use of fieldnotes. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and thematically analyzed.ResultsParticipants reported improved self-management of GDM through healthier diets, increased physical activity, and regular glucose monitoring. Five main themes emerged: (1) making healthy food choices with family and peer support, (2) learning and practicing blood glucose self-monitoring with guidance, (3) engagement in physical activity encouraged by others, (4) emotional well-being supported by social relationships, and (5) challenges affecting women’s acceptance of the online intervention. Professional support enhanced trust in medical advice, while the peer-support component fostered motivation, companionship, and emotional resilience. The intervention also encouraged family involvement in care.ConclusionsOnline interventions that combine expert guidance with peer and family support can support women to manage the physical and mental aspects of living with GDM. This hybrid model shows promise for broader application in low- and middle-income countries. Further research is recommended to assess long-term impacts and scalability.Trial registrationNCT05744856 (Registration Date 20230215 and Last Update Posted 20240430).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09651-8.Keywords: Gestational diabetes mellitus, Online intervention, Self-care, Vietnam, Qualitative research

Background

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is on the rise globally, particularly in developing countries [1]. The prevalence of GDM in Asia ranges from 1.2% to 49.5%, and this rate is particularly high in Southeast Asia, with 35.0% in Bangladesh and 31.2% in Sri Lanka [2]. The prevalence of GDM in Vietnam has also been increasing in recent years and varies considerably across different settings and diagnostic criteria, with a recent study in northern Vietnam reporting a prevalence of 27.1% [3, 4].

Research increasingly emphasizes that women’s ability to care for themselves is vital in lessening the health and emotional burdens of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) for both them and their families [5–7]. The World Health Organization (WHO) describes self-care as individuals’ and communities’ capacity to maintain health, prevent illness, and cope with disease, whether independently or with professional assistance [8]. In this study, self-care among women with GDM refers to the range of practices and efforts they undertake to manage their condition, including balanced nutrition, regular physical activity, and blood glucose monitoring, as well as how they perceive and experience these processes in their daily lives, rather than the achievement of stable blood glucose levels [9–11].

GDM self-care also depends on supportive relationships. Guidance from healthcare professionals helps women better control their glucose levels and feel more confident in their care, while emotional and practical assistance from families and peers reinforces these efforts [12–14]. In many areas where health resources are limited, informal networks such as family members, friends, and women sharing similar experiences serve as an essential complement to care from health professionals, helping reduce strain on the health system and improving overall well-being [15–18]. At the individual level, these relationships also shape how women practice self-care in their daily lives by providing ongoing encouragement, practical assistance, and guidance that is contextually appropriate. Rather than replicating professional care, such support helps translate clinical recommendations into practical everyday practices, enabling women to adapt medical advice to their own families and living circumstances [19, 20].

The interaction between formal healthcare and everyday social support is particularly important in the context of GDM, where sustained behavioral change is required over time. The combination of professional guidance and support from social relationships creates an enabling environment that helps women maintain effective self-care practices and adapt to the evolving demands of pregnancy [15, 20].

The use of mobile technologies in the delivery and management of healthcare services has increasingly been recognized as an effective approach to supporting chronic disease management in general and GDM in particular [21]. A systematic review indicated that online supportive interventions have a positive impact on enhancing self-care capacity and improving health outcomes among women with GDM [21]. Within these interventions, tailored components such as dietary guidance, physical activity support, and self-monitoring-based feedback have been associated with improvements in glycemic control and self-management. Moreover, telemedicine services within these interventions have been shown to reduce the need for face-to-face clinical visits and improve patient satisfaction [21].

In addition, a systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that such interventions can improve glycemic control, reduce stress and anxiety, and enhance women’s awareness and confidence in self-care practices [22, 23]. Through mobile applications, reminder messages, and online platforms, patients can monitor health indicators, record daily behaviors, and receive timely feedback or counseling from healthcare providers [22, 23]. Furthermore, these digital platforms strengthen the connection between pregnant women and healthcare professionals, facilitating the early detection of abnormalities and the timely adjustment of care plans [22, 24].

Vietnam is a lower-middle-income country [21], and at the primary healthcare level, health systems face challenges in providing continuous monitoring, patient counseling, and follow-up care [22, 23]. In the context of the rapid development of the internet and smartphones in Southeast Asia, particularly in Vietnam, Internet use has increased substantially, rising from 29% in 2010 to 60% in 2020 [25–27]. Online interventions can enhance GDM management by strengthening self-management, promoting engagement with lifestyle recommendations, supporting glucose monitoring, and improving maternal and clinical outcomes [28–30]. Given this situation, this study was conducted in Thai Binh, Vietnam, to explore women’s experiences with an online intervention for GDM self-care, as reported by the women themselves.

Methods

Research setting and sample

The study was conducted in Thai Binh Province, Vietnam, in 2024, from February to December. Thai Binh is located in the northern part of Vietnam, with a population of approximately 2 million people. With the increasing trend of GDM in Vietnam, the prevalence in Thai Binh had risen from 9.2% in 2019 to 28.3%% in 2023 [26, 27]. The burden on the country’s healthcare system is growing, and Thai Binh’s healthcare system is no exception. Similar to the increasing trend in internet coverage, a 2025 study found that 3G network coverage in Thai Binh had reached 100%, comparable to that of major urban areas in Vietnam [27].

This study was conducted under the auspices of a cross-disciplinary collaboration project between Vietnam and Denmark, as part of a larger interdisciplinary project titled “Living Together with Chronic Disease: Informal Support for Diabetes Management in Vietnam - Phase 2: Gestational Diabetes in Vietnam” (VALID II). The online intervention program was designed to enhance the self-care abilities of women with GDM by integrating formal support from healthcare professionals and informal support from family members and peers in GDM management.

This qualitative study used a descriptive approach to explore pregnant women’s experiences with the digital self-care intervention for GDM. As part of a broader ethnographic research project, the present study is a qualitative study informed by ethnographic methods and approaches, with attention to women’s contexts and living conditions. While the present study is primarily based on interview data, fieldnotes were used to support contextual interpretation. Data were collected through semi-structured in-depth interviews and analysed using thematic analysis to identify key themes related to participants’ experiences. We conducted this qualitative study with 21 pregnant women diagnosed with GDM through the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). These participants were recruited from different Zalo-based intervention groups.

Participants were recruited from two sites in Thai Binh province: Thai Binh Maternity Hospital, the only public obstetric hospital in the province, and Kim Ngan Clinic, a private health facility. A list of women diagnosed with GDM who had agreed to participate in the intervention was compiled at the study sites. From this list, participants were randomly selected and contacted by telephone by trained researchers from Thai Binh University of Medicine and Pharmacy. Those who agreed to participate were invited to take part in the qualitative interviews, which were conducted at their homes. Eligible participants were women diagnosed with GDM at the study sites who had participated in the intervention, were willing to take part in the qualitative study, and were able to communicate and share their experiences. Women with severe medical complications were excluded.

The intervention program

This intervention program was implemented based on findings from the ethnographic study and co-creation workshops in Thai Binh [31, 32]. The intervention program combined professional and informal support, including providing educational materials developed by the research team, peer education through the online platform Zalo (which is a widely used messaging application in Vietnam that facilitates both individual and group communication and incorporates social networking features) with support from doctors, and professional coaching and counselling in online meetings via Zoom with doctors. The materials and communication content covered a range of topics, including diet, exercise, blood sugar monitoring, mental health, family support, and postpartum care [31, 32].

Following a diagnosis of GDM at healthcare facilities, pregnant women were provided with general counselling materials on GDM care developed as part of the study, and received counselling from healthcare providers as part of routine antenatal services. In the intervention group, these providers had received additional training on GDM management and counselling through the study. At the same time, they were invited to join a Zalo group; this study established 10 Zalo groups, numbered from 1 to 10. In the Zalo group, participants received counselling materials and were advised by doctors; they also shared experiences with other women diagnosed with GDM who were part of the group. Each Zalo group consisted of approximately 20 members and was managed by a research team member with participation from doctors. These groups operated from the time the pregnant women were diagnosed with GDM until four months postpartum. Zoom meetings were held twice a month with medical experts. The meetings were semi-structured and combined educational content with interactive discussions, covering key topics such as dietary management, blood glucose monitoring, physical activity, and emotional well-being. The Zoom meeting schedule was announced one week in advance in the Zalo groups, with a reminder before each meeting. After sharing and receiving information via the Zalo group, participants may have encountered additional issues that required further discussion. These matters were then brought up/raised during Zoom meetings, where participants had the opportunity to receive direct advice and support from both experts and peers.

Data collection

In-depth, semi-structured interviews were conducted at women’s homes, between 29 and 39 weeks of gestation. The time since GDM diagnosis at the time of interview varied between participants, ranging from approximately 2 weeks (2/21 participants) to more than 4 weeks (19/21 participants). We did not identify any systematic differences in the thematic content by time since diagnosis, and all interviews were analysed within a unified thematic framework. The interviews were conducted using a semi-structured interview guide developed by the researchers and refined iteratively throughout the study based on emerging findings from earlier interviews and field observations, with follow-up interviews conducted when further clarification or deeper exploration was needed. Each interview lasted approximately two hours, and was conducted jointly by Vietnamese and Danish researchers from Thai Binh University of Medicine and Pharmacy, Vietnam, and University of Copenhagen, Denmark, including master ‘s-level researchers and professors with extensive experience in social and medical anthropology. All interviews were conducted in Vietnamese. Throughout the interview, the interviewers worked to establish a comfortable atmosphere, encouraging the pregnant women to share information more openly. All interviews were recorded using audio recorders and transcribed afterward. Additionally, during and after the visits, the researchers took field notes to capture key information, the context of the interview, and other relevant details through listening, observation, and their own reflections. These notes were used to support data interpretation and to provide additional context for participants’ lived experiences.

The transcripts were analysed by members of the research team. Initial coding was conducted through repeated reading of the transcripts and guided by the research question. Codes were developed inductively from the data and discussed among the research team. Through iterative discussions, the coding framework was refined and an agreed-upon coding scheme was developed. Any discrepancies in coding were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. All transcripts were then systematically coded according to this final agreed coding framework, which formed the basis for theme development.

Cross-checking among researchers was conducted during coding and theme development to ensure consistency in interpretation and strengthen analytical rigour. Although a formal reflexivity framework was not applied, the research team regularly discussed and reflected on how their backgrounds and perspectives might influence data interpretation and theme development, and these reflections were integrated into the iterative analytical process to enhance the credibility and trustworthiness of the findings. The final dataset was uploaded to a digital file and stored on an online data file to ensure accessibility for researchers and the security of the information.

Ethical approval

This study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Thai Binh University of Medicine and Pharmacy under Decision No. 1325/HĐĐĐ dated December 1, 2022. All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. It was conducted as part of a larger registered study (VALID II) [33], which is listed at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT05744856; registration date: February 24, 2023). The study was funded by the Danish Ministry of Foreign Affairs. A pre-print of the protocol is publicly available elsewhere [29]. All participants provided informed consent prior to the interviews and were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time without consequence. Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed anonymously, and all names reported in the manuscript are pseudonyms. All data were securely stored and accessible only to the research team to ensure confidentiality.

Making healthy food choices with support from family and peers

For women with GDM, the greatest challenge they faced was related to dietary management: how to stabilize blood glucose levels while ensuring adequate nutrition for the fetus and themselves. After joining the intervention program, participants reported that they found it easier to select appropriate foods upon receiving their diagnosis of GDM. Gaining knowledge about food selection played a crucial role for pregnant women with GDM. Through participation in the intervention program, they learned how to choose suitable foods based on their gestational week and current blood glucose levels; Lanh, a 33-year-old accountant, had this to share: “The information was illustrated in great detail, for example, how to exercise properly, what was included in different fruit groups, which fruits should be consumed more, which ones should be limited, and the appropriate food intake for each stage of pregnancy. I found that everything was written clearly and accompanied by images, which made it much easier to understand… I thought this information was suitable for everyone because it was very detailed. Additionally, issues that arose, such as adjusting the diet to suit blood sugar levels and other concerns, were addressed by doctors and shared by other pregnant women in the group”.

Additionally, women in the intervention program benefited from peer support within the group. When they had questions related to dietary choices, they frequently shared them within the Zalo group and received helpful responses from fellow members. Even if they did not actively ask questions, reading about others’ discussions and experiences helped them learn from their peers. “I listened (on the Zoom meetings) and read (on the Zalo group) to see what issues other pregnant women were experiencing. That way, if I found myself in a similar situation, I knew how to handle it… Most of the mothers shared about their dietary habits and exercise routines, so I also reflected on whether I had been following similar practices. If I hadn’t, I tried to improve and achieve the same level,” said Chang, a 37-year-old preschool teacher.

This collective knowledge allowed them to create diverse meal plans, reducing monotony in food choices while ensuring nutritional adequacy. “Being in the group, I saw that pregnant mothers asked questions even about something as simple as drinking a glass of water, and everyone shared (their thoughts) and advised each other. Occasionally, new information was posted, and that information helped me a lot,” Thanh, 25 years old, shared. Through the knowledge and practical experience gained from the intervention program and peer discussions, women also shared this information with their family members. This, in turn, facilitated family involvement in supporting pregnant women with GDM. When family members understood which foods were suitable for a woman with GDM, they assisted in purchasing and preparing appropriate meals. As Thanh said, “I absolutely loved eating sticky rice, but since that day (when I learned that sticky rice was not good for pregnant women with GDM), my parents stopped cooking it as soon as they heard it was unhealthy for me. I didn’t even see it, so I didn’t crave it (laughs). Over time, I got used to it, and now, even when I see sticky rice at a feast, I feel indifferent to it.” Thu, 31 years old, shared, “I just needed to say that I couldn’t eat something and that I had to do things a certain way, and everyone supported me in doing so. In other words, whatever I wanted, they would follow accordingly.” Thus, the families played an active role in supporting women in implementing dietary recommendations by discouraging unhealthy food choices and fostering a supportive environment.

Learning and practicing blood glucose self-monitoring with guidance and support

Most women in the study acknowledged that discussing questions related to blood glucose levels, monitoring techniques, and frequency of testing within the group helped them better understand the necessity of adhering to healthcare professionals’ recommendations. This knowledge-sharing process not only increased their awareness but also motivated them to comply with medical guidelines. Lanh, 30 years old, a customer service worker, expressed: “Before joining the intervention program, I thought I had to test my blood glucose 9 to 10 times a day. Some online sources and friends even suggested testing more frequently, which made me anxious. I was unsure if I could manage such frequent testing. However, after participating in the program and reading the shared information, I realized that excessive testing was unnecessary. Now, I only test 2–3 times a day, my blood glucose remains stable, and my baby is developing normally. I feel much more reassured now (laughs)”.

Similarly, Thanh, an online salesperson, emphasized that the intervention program helped her understand that blood glucose self-monitoring was not just about testing but also about keeping records to adjust diet and exercise accordingly: “After testing my blood glucose, I record the results to track patterns. If something helps keep my blood sugar stable, I continue eating it. If something spikes my blood sugar, I limit it even if I crave it (laughs)”.

Physical activity and encouragement from others

Physical activity plays a crucial role in managing GDM during pregnancy. However, some participants were unaware of the appropriate exercises that could benefit both maternal and fetal health. Through engagement with the intervention program, participants gained a clearer understanding of the importance of physical activity and identified suitable exercises based on shared experiences and educational materials. Yeu, 27 years old, a housewife, shared: “Before, I didn’t know I needed to exercise. I assumed that pregnancy meant resting more. But after joining the program, I realized the importance of exercise. Now, I make it a habit to walk for at least 15 minutes after meals. If I eat a large meal, I wait at least an hour before walking to avoid discomfort.” This increased understanding, developed through participation in the intervention after their diagnosis with GDM, provided motivation to overcome barriers to exercise. Thanh, 25 years old, added: “I usually walk outside, but if it’s raining or too cold, I walk inside my room, back and forth. Even if I’m sitting, I keep my arms and legs moving to help regulate my blood sugar. If I remain still, my glucose levels spike, so I try to keep moving”.

Moreover, pregnant women with GDM recognized the physical benefits of exercise after participating in the intervention. Anh, 35 years old, a factory worker, noted: “Before exercising, I often had leg pain, even while sleeping. But now that I walk regularly, my legs no longer hurt, and I sleep better. It’s amazing (laughs).” These perceived benefits further motivated women to maintain regular physical activity.

Family support, particularly from husbands, also played a significant role in sustaining an exercise routine. Husbands who gained knowledge about the importance of exercise for women with GDM, either through their wives’ participation in the intervention program, actively participated in their wives’ physical activities. Thanh, a 25-year-old, said, “ I shared all the information I received (from the intervention program) with my husband and other family members, so they understood and could support me as I needed … After dinner, my husband and I went for a walk together. It’s more enjoyable, and I feel safer at night”. This support not only encouraged consistent physical activity but also strengthened emotional bonds between partners.

Emotional well-being through social support: feeling “more comfortable” when “many others are like me”

The story of Thanh

Thanh, 25 years old, worked as an online seller. As this was her first pregnancy, she made great efforts to research and read information online about maternal and fetal health. Since she had married far from her birth home (120 km away from her birth parents) and worked exclusively online, she had almost no social relationships outside of her husband’s family. Therefore, whenever she needed information, she turned to the internet to seek answers. However, upon discovering that she had GDM, she felt “confused and worried” to the extent that on the first day of her diagnosis, she was unable to take her customary afternoon nap. She was aware of the potential complications of GDM, such as preeclampsia, stillbirth, macrosomia, and edema. Her anxiety also affected her husband after she shared with him the possible complications that could affect both mother and baby. However, after joining the intervention program, she learned how to prevent potential complications and manage GDM, and seek advice from professionals as well as support from other pregnant women with the same condition. This helped to ease her fears, making her feel “more comfortable” and “confident” in living with the disease, as she realized that many other pregnant women were experiencing the same situation. Additionally, she received great support from her family, which made her feel that she was not “alone” in her pregnancy journey with GDM.

Like Thanh, the feeling of being “more comfortable” after joining the intervention program was common among most pregnant women in the study. They gained knowledge from “official” sources, which made them feel more confident and reassured when following the guidelines. “At first (before joining the project), I searched for information online. There was so much information, but I didn’t know which was correct and which was wrong because it was all on the internet. I didn’t know what to trust, and at that time, I was confused due to the conflicting information… But now, after joining the program, I feel more reassured and confident with the information provided. It is given by doctors from the medical university, so of course, I trust it… and I just follow the instructions … (laughs),” shared Huong, a 29-year-old primary school teacher.

Additionally, knowing that there were other pregnant women in similar situations made it easier for them to share their thoughts, emotions, and concerns. “I think this project is really great because whenever there is a problem, people pay more attention to you. I feel less isolated… and more comfortable sharing with people like me (who also have GDM),” shared Yeu, a 27-year-old housewife. In particular, whenever pregnant women had questions or faced difficulties, they could share them on Zalo or in Zoom meetings to receive timely advice from doctors and other women with similar conditions. Thuy, 38 years old, stated, “Whenever I have a question, I immediately post it in the group… One time, I was unsure about what to eat for breakfast. I asked in the group, and both the doctor and other women provided advice right away.” This timely support reduced uncertainty and enhanced women’s sense of reassurance and confidence in managing their condition. These timely responses helped pregnant women make appropriate decisions for their situations, ensuring a healthy pregnancy.

Barriers to women’s engagement with the online intervention

From the perspectives of those directly involved - pregnant women with GDM - the intervention was perceived as beneficial and generally satisfactory. However, several challenges of the intervention were identified, reflecting aspects that participants found less satisfactory.

Most of the women in this study, being of working age, often had to bear dual responsibilities - caring for their families and children, and fulfilling their duties in the workplace. This created challenges in finding time to participate in intervention activities or to engage with online materials related to GDM self-care, especially among women who did not live with their parents or had limited family support. Ha, 33 years old, worker, shared, “The Zoom sessions were at 8 p.m., but I usually finished work around 9 p.m., so even though I really wanted to join and listen to others and the doctors, I just couldn’t… It was such a pity.” Some women faced barriers in using new technology, such as “not knowing how to join Zoom.”, “I saw the meeting link posted in the group, but I didn’t know how to join. They also sent some instructions, but honestly, I didn’t understand anything… so I just gave up.” (Vi, 40-year-old, self-employed).

In addition, some participants felt hesitant to share personal information online, particularly with people they did not know. This hesitation extended to avoiding “negative” comments in the group chat, as they feared such messages might affect the emotions of other pregnant women. Many believed that pregnant women should always stay cheerful and optimistic to avoid any potential harm to the baby. For these reasons, some women mainly used the Zalo group to read others’ posts rather than actively contribute, which sometimes reduced the group’s level of engagement. Thanh, 25-year-old, self-employed: “I often read what others post in the group. I only share positive things, like when my blood sugar levels are good or a healthy meal I’ve made. I don’t want to post my worries, I’m afraid it might make other moms anxious too, and that’s not good for the baby.”, “On social media, I’m kind of shy about posting personal things. I just read what others share.” (Thiet, 34-year-old).

These challenges highlight the importance of designing flexible and inclusive digital health interventions that account for women’s work schedules, family support, and digital literacy in low- and middle-income contexts.

Discussion

Vietnamese women with GDM who took part in an online program combining expert and peer support described positive experiences across several aspects, including greater self-care awareness and practices, as well as feelings of comfort, reassurance, and reduced isolation through peer support.

Perceived improvement in women’s self-care awareness and practices for GDM

Healthcare professionals play a crucial role in guiding pregnant women with GDM toward adopting a healthy lifestyle [30]. Similar to traditional intervention programs led by medical experts in GDM management, the women in this study reported positive experiences with managing their dietary habits, physical activity, and at-home blood glucose monitoring among pregnant women with GDM. Proper dietary management and physical exercise are essential for controlling blood glucose levels in pregnant women with GDM. Previous studies had shown that well-structured nutritional interventions can effectively regulate blood glucose levels, thereby reducing pregnancy complications and improving maternal and neonatal outcomes [34].

As a result of using the online program, women reported that they learned how to make appropriate food choices and maintain nutritional balance according to gestational age and current blood glucose levels. This not only alleviated their anxiety about self-managing their diet but also ensured adequate nutritional intake for fetal and their own health development. Additionally, another study has been associated with improved insulin sensitivity and may support blood glucose control regulation, as well as reduce the risk of pregnancy-related complications [35]. Participants in our study gained greater awareness of the importance of exercise and incorporated suitable activities, such as post-meal walking. Other studies have also demonstrated that interventions targeting diet, physical activity, and blood glucose control help pregnant women with GDM achieve better glycemic management [36, 37]. Furthermore, studies have reported that structured interventions such as a couple-based gestational diabetes self-management program and counselling delivered through social applications were associated with improved pregnancy outcomes compared with usual care [37, 38].

The application of digital technology in healthcare, particularly online interventions, has become a crucial trend in managing various health conditions, including GDM. Online platforms facilitate easier access to dietary and physical activity interventions while enabling pregnant women to receive timely feedback from healthcare professionals and peer support groups [39]. Our study found that online interventions enabled pregnant women to quickly share concerns and receive guidance from doctors and experienced individuals, helping them feel more confident in following medical recommendations. Internet-based interventions help alleviate the burden on healthcare systems by reducing the need for frequent in-person visits while enhancing the efficiency of remote consultation and monitoring [40]. Additionally, shared experiences within peer support groups foster empathy, making pregnant women feel less isolated during their GDM journey [13]. Through firsthand experiences, participants exchanged “tips” on food selection, meal preparation, and blood glucose management during pregnancy [39]. These elements play a crucial role in promoting a healthy lifestyle and ensuring effective blood glucose control.

Emotional experiences of feeling supported and less isolated through social support

Pregnant women with GDM are at a higher risk of anxiety, stress, and depression compared to other pregnant populations, due to concerns about the impact of GDM on both maternal and fetal health [41]. Furthermore, the pressure to adhere to dietary adjustments and maintain optimal blood glucose control exacerbates mental health issues in this population [42]. When diagnosed with GDM, pregnant women frequently seek information from various online sources, which can lead to confusion and anxiety regarding the reliability and accuracy of the information obtained [36, 37]. Participation in the program provides them with information from authoritative and verified sources, allowing them to feel more assured and confident in following the recommended guidelines.

Unlike one-on-one consultations with healthcare providers, peer support groups consisting of fellow pregnant women allowed some participants to share their thoughts and emotions more openly, while others reported that they preferred to observe and were hesitant to actively participate in discussions. Studies have shown that peer support can alleviate stress and anxiety in pregnant women with GDM [38]. In our study, many women reported feeling reassured knowing that they were not alone in facing GDM while receiving timely support during difficult situations. Existing research shows that challenges arising during GDM management can cause significant psychological distress [43]. However, sharing experiences within the group enabled participants to receive rapid advice, mitigating anxiety and promoting emotional well-being. This support can be particularly beneficial for those with limited family support or those living away from loved ones [44].

Through disseminating knowledge from pregnant women to their families, women perceived that their family members provided increased support for GDM care, including assistance with dietary and exercise recommendations. Studies have shown that communication and sharing among family members enhance family support in preparing appropriate meals and maintaining a healthy lifestyle [41]. Indeed, in our study, many participants reported that after sharing information about GDM with their families, their cooking habits were adjusted to better align with the women’s dietary needs, making adherence easier. Active family involvement not only supported GDM management but also created a nurturing environment, providing pregnant women with a sense of security during pregnancy [27]. Our study also provides further evidence to support that encouragement from family members, particularly spouses, played a vital role in maintaining exercise routines and blood glucose control [45].

While the intervention was generally well-received, a number of participants also reported less favorable experiences. These were mainly related to time limitations, psychosocial barriers, and challenges in accessing or adapting to digital technologies. Similar obstacles have been observed in previous studies evaluating online-based interventions [21]. Time was a particular challenge, especially for women working and balancing employment with family responsibilities, which often made it difficult for them to attend scheduled online sessions or engage consistently with intervention materials. Some participants also reported difficulties with using digital tools such as Zoom, which limited their ability to fully access the program. In addition, a number of women expressed hesitation in sharing personal experiences in online group settings. Some preferred to observe rather than actively participate, partly due to concerns about privacy and the emotional impact of sharing negative experiences with other pregnant women. Despite some negative experiences, the online intervention was perceived as beneficial overall, helping women improve their self-care awareness and feel more emotionally supported throughout pregnancy with GDM.

Strengths and limitations

This study employed an online intervention model integrating professional healthcare consultations with peer support to enhance access to care for women with GDM. It may be particularly beneficial in contexts where access to healthcare services is limited or where women face time constraints that make in-person visits difficult. The combination of medical expertise and peer sharing contributed to the effectiveness of the intervention by delivering evidence-based information alongside emotional support from individuals with lived experience. Moreover, the female researchers conducting the study had extensive experience in anthropological research, and the initial home visit, together with follow-up interviews conducted when further clarification or additional information was required, helped build a close rapport with participants, enabling them to feel more comfortable sharing their experiences and allowing researchers to clarify and enrich the information collected during subsequent interviews. Furthermore, the intervention method utilized free online platforms, which may be suitable for resource-limited settings while still providing essential consultations to pregnant women. A potential limitation of this study was that the same research team was responsible for both implementing the intervention and collecting the data. This dual role may have introduced courtesy bias, whereby participants might have provided more favorable responses out of politeness or a perceived sense of obligation. The success of the intervention partially depended on participants’ ability to use digital technologies. In our study, some participants experienced difficulties accessing Zoom meetings due to internet connectivity issues, while others were unable to arrange a time to attend scheduled meetings. These challenges highlight barriers related to the usability and accessibility of the online intervention, including difficulties in joining virtual sessions, internet connectivity problems, and time constraints that limited participation. The study was conducted in a rural province in northern Vietnam, and the findings are based solely on women’s perspectives; therefore, the results may not be representative of other regions or broader populations across the country. Therefore, we suggest that future studies should consider providing technical support and offering more flexible schedules, as this may also improve participation, particularly for women with limited digital access or time constraints. Additionally, expanding the research to diverse settings and involving family members could help capture broader experiences and enhance the applicability of the findings.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that online interventions combining expert support and peer support may support not only the management of diet, physical activity, and blood glucose levels but also their mental well-being. The women participating in this study described how involvement in online peer groups fostered a sense of companionship, reduced anxiety, and increased their motivation to adhere to medical guidelines. Additionally, as experienced by the women, the program also improved family involvement, creating a supportive environment for pregnant women managing GDM. This online intervention model combining professional healthcare support and peer support holds great potential for broader application in improving the health outcomes of pregnant women with GDM. Further research is needed to assess the upscaling potential of this integrated intervention approach.
