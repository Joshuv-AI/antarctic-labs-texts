Targeting senescent cells with dipeptidyl peptidase 4-CAR-NK cells alleviates liver fibrosis and reverses metabolic dysfunction
Authors: Rui Hua, Kang Xu, Huan Peng, Kunda Liu, Nan Long, Chenye Zeng, Yida Wang, Yannan Guo, Zhao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractSelective elimination of senescent hepatocytes by DPP4-CAR-NK cells could rescue liver fibrosis; therefore improving liver function and metabolic homeostasis in CCl4-induced liver fibrotic and naturally aged mice.

To the editor:

Eliminating senescent cells has been widely demonstrated to successfully rejuvenate aged tissues and ameliorate age-associated diseases1. Senescent cells contribute to the development and progression of a variety of non-aging-related chronic diseases, such as liver fibrosis and lung injury2. This strongly expands the application scope of senescent cell clearance in disease treatment.

Precise recognition of senescent cells is a prerequisite for their selective clearance, thereby avoiding the common side effects of long-term use in first-generation senolytics for the elderly, such as dasatinib and quercetin, including systemic toxicities, adverse reactions, and off-target effects3,4. Dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (DPP4), a membrane-bound protein, is highly expressed on the surface of senescent cells, and the DPP4-based antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity is able to successfully eliminate senescent cells5,6. Silencing or inhibiting Dpp4 reduces the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) signature and increases cell death in senescent vascular smooth muscle cells, improving vascular disease7. Moreover, Dpp4 gene knockout and DPP4 inhibitor could protect mice from carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)-induced liver fibrosis8. Thus, we hypothesize that eliminating senescent cells with high DPP4 expression in the liver can reverse liver fibrosis and rejuvenate liver function.

The ability of the body's immune system to eliminate senescent cells is decreased with age. New immunotherapies based on chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) technology show great application potential in the field of aging. For example, NKG2D-CAR T cells eliminate senescent cells, improving multiple physiological changes and pathologies, including liver-related biochemical markers in aged mice and nonhuman primates1. Anti-uPAR CAR T cells can efficiently deplete senescent cells in young animals and reverse liver fibrosis9. However, the comprehensive clinical studies on their safety and efficacy in the elderly population have not been conducted. In particular, cytokine release syndrome-like symptoms, the biggest controversy in CAR-T cell therapy, have almost devastating effects on the elderly. NK cell therapy has the advantages of higher safety and feasibility for “off-the-shelf” production, representing a promising anti-aging approach. A chimeric polypeptide, termed E16-uPA24, could label senescent cells with glutamate, thus promoting the clearance of senescent cells via triggering NK cells2. Of note, it relies on activation and sensitization of innate immune cells rather than direct cell killing, which increases the uncertainty and instability of its function in individuals with insufficient immune reserves, such as the elderly2. The potential of CAR-NK therapy for senescence-associated diseases remains to be confirmed.

In this study, we establish the therapeutic potential of DPP4–CAR-NK cells targeting and eliminating senescent hepatocytes in reversing liver fibrosis and restoring metabolic homeostasis in aged and chemical-induced liver fibrotic mouse models.

DPP4 is a specific biomarker of senescent hepatocytes during natural aging or pathology-related liver fibrosis

In order to define the cell type with high expression of Dpp4, we performed a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis of the GSE276063 dataset, the gene expression profile at the single cell level of liver from young (6 months) and old mice (24 months) (Supporting Information Fig. S2A). A total of 15 cell types were identified based on their unique-expression signatures, such as hepatocytes (Cyp2e1, Apoa1, and Hnf4a), hepatic stellate cells (Des, Acta2, and Pdgfrb), endothelial cells (Pecam1, Chd5, and Flt1) (Fig. S2B–S2D). We found that Dpp4 is specifically highly expressed in hepatocytes among 15 cell types from the aged mice (Fig. 1B and Fig. S2E). Moreover, only in hepatocytes, we observed the difference of Dpp4 expression between the young mice and the aged mice, that is, the expression level of Dpp4 in hepatocytes of aged mice was higher than that of young mice (Fig. 1C and D). The expression of Dpp4 was extremely low in other cell types from both groups (Fig. 1C and D). These data strongly suggested that Dpp4 is the specific biomarker of senescent hepatocytes.

It is widely accepted that p21 is mainly expressed in senescent hepatocytes, while p16 is more abundant in non-parenchymal cells10. Therefore, we performed co-immunofluorescence staining for p21 or p16 with DPP4 in livers from the aged mice. DPP4 protein was only distributed on the membrane of p21-positive hepatocytes (large nucleus and loose chromatin) (Fig. 1E). Moreover, DPP4 is not distributed on the membrane of p16-positive non-parenchymal cells (small nucleus and dense chromatin) (Fig. 1E). Masson staining revealed the exacerbated fibrosis in the liver of aged mice (Fig. S1B), accompanied by the activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), which was proved by the expression of αSMA protein (Fig. S1D). However, DPP4 and αSMA were not expressed on the same cell (Fig. 1F); that is, activated HSCs do not express DPP4.

CCl4-induced liver fibrosis in mice (Supporting Information Fig. S3A and S3B) closely mimics aspects of human chromic hepatotoxicity-triggered liver fibrosis. Ablation of senescent cells has been proved to ameliorate CCl4-induced liver fibrosis in mice9. However, whether there exist senescent hepatocytes with high DPP4 expression on membrane remains unknown. Firstly, increased liver senescence (SA-β-Gal staining in Fig. S3C, and SASP factors mRNA expression in Fig. S3D), liver fibrosis (Masson staining in Fig. S3C), systemic inflammation (serum IL-1β and IL-6 concentration in Fig. S3E), and deteriorated liver function (serum AST and ALT level in Fig. S3F) confirmed the successful establishment of the liver fibrosis and aging model. We also found that DPP4 protein is specifically enriched on the surface of p21-positive senescent hepatocytes (but not p16-positive non-parenchymal cells) in the fibrotic liver (Fig. 1G), accompanied by the distinct distribution of DPP4 protein and αSMA protein (Fig. 1H).

We observed that DPP4 is primarily localized on the membrane of p21-positive hepatocytes (Fig. 1E and G), while SA-β-Gal activity is predominantly enriched in the fibrotic peri-lobular regions (Fig. S1B and Fig. S3C). This observation is consistent with the current understanding of the liver senescence cascade. Senescent hepatocytes (p21-positive) serve as the primary source of the SASP, whose secreted factors drive the activation, proliferation, and transformation of HSCs into myofibroblasts. These long-lived, activated myofibroblasts may themselves enter a senescent state under chronic stress, exhibiting strong SA-β-Gal activity. Of note, senescent myofibroblasts still have strong pro-fibrotic ability, which further aggravates progressive fibrosis. Therefore, the localization of SA-β-Gal signal in fibrotic areas reflects a downstream cellular event initiated by senescent hepatocytes.

In summary, these findings support the conclusion that DPP4 is specifically highly expressed on senescent hepatocytes.

To confirm this conclusion, we specifically knocked down Dpp4 of hepatocytes using Adeno-Associated Virus (AAV)-DPP4 RNAi with thyroxine binding globulin (TBG, a specific promoter in hepatocytes) promoter in CCl4-induced liver fibrotic mice (CCl4+AAV-DPP4 RNAi) (Supporting Information Fig. S4A). CCl4-induced liver fibrotic mice injected with AAV-Control were used as the negative control group (CCl4+AAV-Ctrl) (Fig. S4A). The significant decrease (about 160 times) of Dpp4 mRNA in liver but not in other tissues, such as the brain, heart, colon, eWAT, and kidney, indicated the specific knockout efficiency in the liver of CCl4+AAV-DPP4 RNAi group mice (Fig. S4B). The insufficient amount of DPP4 protein in the liver of the CCl4+AAV-DPP4 RNAi mice (Fig. 1I and Fig. S4C), resulting in the inhibition of cell senescence (Fig. 1I, Fig. S4C and S4D). Subsequently, the results showed an alleviated liver fibrosis (Fig. 1I and Fig. S4C), along with the decreased activation of HSCs (Fig. 1J). Reduced serum AST and ALT level (Fig. 1K), and IL-1β and IL-6 concentration (Fig. S4E) in CCl4+AAV-DPP4 RNAi mice proved the importance of DPP4 deficiency in hepatocytes for maintaining liver function.

In summary, our data fully demonstrate that DPP4 in senescent hepatocytes is an effective target for reducing hepatic aging phenotype and reversing liver fibrosis. Benefiting from the characteristic of DPP4 being a membrane-bound protein, targeted elimination of senescent hepatocytes with high DPP4 expression in the liver is expected to become a treatment for age- and pathology-related liver fibrosis and reconstruct global metabolic homeostasis.

DPP4–CAR–NK cells eliminate senescent hepatocytes, thus ameliorating fibrosis and metabolic dysfunction

We constructed a DPP4-specific CAR comprising an anti-mouse DPP4 heavy and light chain variable fragment linked to 2B4 costimulatory and CD3ζ signaling domains, transduced human NK92 cells through genetic lentivirus9 (Fig. 2A). To well characterize the ability of DPP4-CAR-NK cells to target DPP4, DPP4 was introduced into the human hepatocellular carcinoma G2 cell line (named HepG2dpp4 cells). DPP4-CAR-NK cells, but not NK cells, were activated by HepG2dpp4 cells under the co-culture conditions, indicated by increased proportion of CD107a-positive cells and the elevated secretion of IFN-γ in medium (Fig. 2B and C). Moreover, DPP4-CAR-NK cells showed no cytotoxicity towards HepG2 cells, but efficiently eliminated HepG2dpp4 cells (Fig. 2D). We then co-cultured doxorubicin (Dox)-senescent HepG2 cells (Fig. 2E) with either control NK-92 cells or our specific DPP4-CAR-NK cells. DPP4-CAR-NK cells, but not the control NK-92 cells, effectively eliminated the senescent HepG2 cells (Fig. 2E). To study whether DPP4-CAR-NK cells could function as bona fide senolytics in vivo, CCl4-induced liver fibrotic mice were infused with 1 × 106 DPP4-CAR-NK cells (once a week for a total of 4 weeks) through the tail vein (Supporting Information Fig. S5A). DPP4 expression (DPP4-positive cells) was significantly reduced in the liver from DPP4-CAR-NK cell-treated mice (Fig. 2F and Fig. S5B). The decreased SA-β-gal staining positivity rate (Fig. 2F and Fig. S5B) and reduced expression of SASP factors (Fig. S5C) indicated the effective removal of senescent cells from the liver by DPP4-CAR-NK cells. Logically, we also observed the reduced fibrosis level (shown by Masson staining in Fig. 2F and Fig. S5B, and αSMA protein expression in Fig. 2G) and the improvement of regenerative capacity (shown by IHC staining with Ki67 antibody (cell proliferation marker) in Fig. 2F and Fig. S5B, and GS and cyclin B1 protein (cell cycle makers) expression in Fig. 2G). They collectively promoted the recovery of liver function (shown by serum AST and ALT levels in Fig. 2H). Blood biochemical analysis indicated the reduced systemic inflammation level, as shown by decreased pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6 (Fig. S5D). Notably, DPP4-CAR-NK cells restored hypoglycemia in liver fibrotic mice (Fig. S5E), suggesting the improvements in systemic metabolism.

We then proceeded to investigate whether the administration of DPP4-CAR-NK cells could alleviate aging-related liver fibrosis (Supporting Information Fig. S6A). Firstly, we found the absence of DPP4 protein in the liver from DPP4-CAR-NK cell-treated aged mice (Aged + CAR-NK) (Fig. 2I and Fig. S6B), which was accompanied by the reduced liver senescence (Fig. 2I and Fig. S6B and S6C). Subsequently, both liver fibrosis and regeneration were greatly improved (Fig. 2I–J and Fig. S6B), resulting in the recovery of liver function (Fig. 2K). As the largest solid organ and a key metabolic hub, the liver serves as a central node for maintaining systemic homeostasis. Restoring liver function is considered as an effective strategy to improve the systemic metabolic homeostasis in the elderly. DPP4-CAR-NK cells significantly decreased the fasting blood glucose (Fig. S6D), and total cholesterol (TC) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C, a harmful type of cholesterol) of aged mice (Fig. S6E), suggesting the enhancement in blood glucose and lipid regulatory capacity. Subsequently, we performed a glucose intolerance test (GTT) and an insulin resistance test (ITT). Aged + CAR-NK mice metabolized glucose more efficiently than control mice and had improved insulin sensitivity (Fig. 2L). Next, we utilized an indirect calorimetry system to analyze the metabolism health of mice. DPP4-CAR-NK cells obviously rescued the aging-related lower oxygen consumption (VO2), carbon dioxide production (VCO2), and decreased energy expenditure (Fig. 2M). These data supported the contributions of DPP4-CAR-NK cells to systemic metabolism improvement in aged mice.

Adipose tissue plays a role equivalent to that of the liver in the regulation of whole-body energy homeostasis. Therefore, we analyzed whether DPP4-CAR-NK cells affect adipose tissue to identify possible off-target effects outside the liver. We did not observe the difference of cleaved caspase-3 positive adipocytes and the expression of key molecular markers associated with inflammation (TNF-α and IL-6) and energy metabolism (UCP-1 and Cidea) in epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT) between Aged and Aged + DPP4-CAR-NK treated groups (Fig. S6F and S6G). These data suggested that DPP4-CAR-NK cells do not directly target or alter the biology of adipose tissue. The absence of both cellular apoptosis and changes in key functional pathways argues against a direct off-target action in adipose tissue. Therefore, we speculate that the significant improvements in systemic energy expenditure, glucose tolerance, and insulin sensitivity observed in treated aged mice are most likely secondary metabolic adaptations resulting from the clearance of senescent hepatocytes in the liver and the consequent restoration of hepatic metabolic function.

Furthermore, we assessed the safety of DPP4-CAR-NK cells in aged mice. As shown in Supporting Information Fig. S7A, the aged mice treated with DPP4-CAR-NK cells did not show observable signs of weight loss and tissue atrophy. In addition, we did not observe exacerbate spontaneous age-related histological damage in tissues, including eWAT, lung, colon, muscle, heart, and kidney obtained from DPP4-CAR-NK cells-treated mice when compared to age-matched animals without treatment (Fig. S7B).

Conclusions

These results together support the effectiveness and safety of DPP4-CAR-NK cells in targeting and eliminating senescent hepatocytes, and thus reversing liver fibrosis as well as restoring metabolic homeostasis in pathologically fibrotic mice and naturally very aged mice (29 to 30-month-old) (Fig. 2N). Given the limited lifespan of CAR-NK cells in circulation, the risk of adverse effects on normal tissue homeostasis and graft-versus-host disease is comparatively low. Beyond fibrosis, senescence is associated with numerous age-related disorders involving chronic tissue damage, such as heart failure, Alzheimer's disease, and benign prostatic hyperplasia9. Thus, senolytic CAR-NK cells are likely to possess extensive therapeutic potential.
