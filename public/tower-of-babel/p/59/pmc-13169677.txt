Association between triglyceride-glucose index and branch retinal vein occlusion: linking metabolic risk to ocular vascular disease
Authors: Caner Öztürk, Sabriye Bolat, Mustafa Duran, Selim Cevher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo investigate the association between the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and branch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO), and to evaluate its diagnostic performance.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, 49 patients with newly diagnosed BRVO and 50 age- and sex-matched controls were enrolled. All participants underwent a complete ophthalmic examination, and venous blood samples were collected after overnight fasting. Fasting glucose, triglycerides, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein(HDL), and low-density lipoprotein(LDL) levels were measured. The TyG index was calculated as ln [fasting triglycerides (mg/dL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL)/2]. Logistic regression analysis was performed to assess the association between TyG index and BRVO, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to determine diagnostic accuracy.ResultsThe mean age was 64.8 ± 11.2 years in the BRVO group and 66.7 ± 11.5 years in controls (p = 0.396). No significant differences were observed in the prevalence of diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, HDL, or fasting glucose between groups. Patients with BRVO had significantly higher total cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL, and TyG index(p = 0.017, p = 0.004, p = 0.016, and p = 0.025, respectively). Logistic regression identified TyG as an independent risk factor for BRVO(odds ratio: 2.26; 95% CI: 1.08–4.73; p = 0.029). ROC analysis revealed an area under the curve of 0.631, with an optimal cutoff of 8.62 (72% sensitivity, 60% specificity).ConclusionsThe TyG index is independently associated with BRVO and may serve as a simple, inexpensive biomarker for identifying individuals at increased risk, particularly those with concomitant cardiometabolic risk factors.

Introduction

Retinal vein occlusion (RVO) is the second most common retinal vascular disorder after diabetic retinopathy and represents a major cause of unilateral vision loss. Depending on the site of obstruction, RVO is classified into two subtypes: central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO) and branch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO) [1]. BRVO typically occurs at arteriovenous crossing sites, where a retinal artery and vein share a common adventitial sheath. In these regions, a rigid, thick-walled artery may compress the adjacent thinner-walled vein, particularly in the presence of arteriosclerosis [2, 3]. Large-scale epidemiological studies have consistently demonstrated that individuals with systemic atherosclerotic diseases—such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, and dyslipidemia—are at increased risk of BRVO [4, 5].

The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index was first described in 2008 for estimating insulin resistance in healthy subjects [6]. Further studies showed its strong association with several cardiometabolic disorders, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, obesity, metabolic syndrome, and atherosclerosis-related stroke and myocardial infarction [7]. Elevated TyG index levels have also been linked to increased arterial stiffness [8]. The TyG index is regarded as a clinically relevant parameter in the evaluation of both atherosclerosis and microvascular injury [9].

The role of the TyG index in BRVO patients is not yet fully understood. The aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between BRVO and the TyG index, and to assess its diagnostic value in patients with BRVO.

Methods

This cross-sectional study was approved by the institutional board of the Hitit University ethics committee (approval number:2025 − 167) and conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was provided by all participants. A post-hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power version 3.1 (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Germany). Assuming an alpha level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 80%, the required minimum sample size was calculated as 48 participants per group. A total of 49 patients with newly diagnosed BRVO and 50 age- and sex-matched controls were included. Control participants were selected from individuals attending the ophthalmology clinic for cataract surgery who had no history of RVO.

All participants underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic examination, including best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) measurement with Snellen charts, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, Goldmann applanation tonometry, and dilated fundus examination. The diagnosis of BRVO was confirmed by a retina specialist based on the presence of tortuous and dilated retinal vessels, intraretinal hemorrhages, arteriovenous notching, and soft exudates on fundus examination. The presence of macular edema in BRVO patients was assessed using optical coherence tomography (OCT) (Heidelberg Engineering GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany), and the existence of retinal ischaemia was evaluated with fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) (Topcon Medical Systems, Topcon Corp., Tokyo, Japan).

Exclusion criteria included a history of uncontrolled diabetes mellitus or hypertension, severe cardiovascular disease, stroke, cognitive impairment, or ocular pathology other than BRVO. Participants younger than 40 years of age, active smokers, and those with a history of heavy alcohol consumption were also excluded.

Venous blood samples were collected from each participant following an 8-hour overnight fasting. Fasting plasma glucose, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) (mg/dL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL) (mg/dL), and total cholesterol levels (mg/dL) were recorded. The TyG index was calculated using the following formula: ln [fasting triglyceride (mg/dL) × fasting blood glucose (mg/dL)/2] [6].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Normally distributed variables were compared between groups using the independent samples t-test, while non-normally distributed variables were analyzed with the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the TyG index in predicting BRVO. Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical data as frequencies or percentages. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 49 patients with BRVO and 50 control subjects were included in the study. The demographic characteristics, medical history, and laboratory findings of both groups are presented in Table 1. The mean age was 64.75 ± 11.16 years in the BRVO group and 66.70 ± 11.54 years in the control group (p = 0.396). The male-to-female ratio was 27:22 in the BRVO group and 30:20 in the control group (p = 0.622). Similarly, no significant differences were observed between groups with respect to the prevalence of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, or cardiovascular disease (p = 0.143, p = 0.522, and p = 0.085, respectively). High-density lipoprotein and fasting blood glucose levels were comparable between the two groups (p = 0.052 and p = 0.947, respectively). Total cholesterol, triglyceride, LDL, and TyG index were significantly higher in patients with BRVO compared to control subjects (p = 0.017, p = 0.004, p = 0.016, and p = 0.025, respectively).

To further evaluate whether the TyG index was independently associated with BRVO, a multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed including potential systemic risk factors such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, and the TyG index. In this model, the TyG index remained significantly associated with BRVO (odds ratio [OR]: 2.26, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.08–4.73, p = 0.029), whereas the other variables included in the model did not show a statistically significant association with BRVO (Table 2). These findings suggest that the TyG index may provide additional information beyond traditional lipid parameters in relation to BRVO risk.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that patients with BRVO had significantly higher levels of triglycerides, LDL and total cholesterol, as well as higher TyG index values, compared with age- and sex-matched controls. Importantly, logistic regression analysis identified an increased TyG index as an independent risk factor for BRVO, conferring a 2.26-fold higher risk. Furthermore, ROC analysis suggested that a TyG index cutoff value of 8.62 could differentiate BRVO patients from controls with moderate diagnostic accuracy. Collectively, these findings suggest that the TyG index may reflect underlying metabolic and vascular risk associated with BRVO.

The pathogenesis of BRVO is multifactorial, reflecting the interplay between local anatomical factors and systemic vascular health. The mechanical theory emphasizes the importance of arteriovenous crossings, where a shared adventitial sheath allows a rigid, arteriosclerotic artery to compress the adjacent vein. This compression, exacerbated by arterial wall thickening with atherosclerosis, narrows the venous lumen and predisposes to venous stasis [2, 3]. Turbulent blood flow caused by atherosclerotic changes in the artery may result in venous endothelial damage. Such damage can promote the formation of a thrombus within the affected vein. This process reflects Virchow’s triad of elements — venous stasis, endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability — which contribute to the development of BRVO [10].

Previous studies showed that systemic conditions such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, obesity and metabolic syndrome accelerate atherosclerosis, thereby increasing the risk of RVO [11–13]. The Beaver Dam Eye Study identified hypertension and diabetes mellitus as major risk factors for RVO through their contributions to vascular endothelial dysfunction and arteriosclerotic change [14]. Similarly, the Blue Mountains Eye Study reported a significant association between obesity and RVO incidence [15]. A large Korean cohort study further demonstrated that increasing body mass index (BMI) was associated with greater RVO risk [16]. Lim et al. also highlighted that metabolic syndrome components predispose individuals to RVO by exacerbating systemic inflammation, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia [17]. These findings underscore the close link between systemic atherosclerotic burden and RVO, supporting the concept that BRVO represents not only a localized ocular event but also a manifestation of widespread vascular pathology.

Structural retinal changes associated with BRVO have also been demonstrated in imaging-based studies. Previous research has shown that eyes affected by BRVO exhibit significantly increased central macular thickness and mean choroidal thickness compared with fellow eyes at baseline. Following intravitreal anti vascular endothelial growth factor treatment, these parameters were reported to decrease significantly, accompanied by substantial improvement in visual acuity. These findings highlight the important role of macular edema in visual impairment related to BRVO and underscore the clinical relevance of identifying systemic factors that may contribute to the development of this condition. In this context, metabolic markers such as the TyG index may provide additional insight into the systemic vascular mechanisms underlying BRVO [18].

The TyG index, a simple, reproducible, and inexpensive surrogate marker of insulin resistance, has emerged as an integrative indicator of metabolic and vascular risk. Chen et al. reported that a higher TyG index is associated with an increased risk of new-onset diabetes in middle-aged and older adults [19]. Additionally, Wang et al. demonstrated that an increased TyG index was significantly associated with a higher risk of pre-hypertension and hypertension in adults [20]. Recent studies showed that the TyG index can be used as an independent predictor of an increased risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and arterial stiffness [21–23]. As hypertension, diabetes and atherosclerosis play a crucial role in the occurrence and development of BRVO, the TyG index can be a useful predictor of BRVO. Our study extends these findings to BRVO, suggesting that metabolic dysregulation, as reflected by the TyG index, may contribute to the vascular alterations underlying RVO.

There are limited studies have specifically examined the relationship between the TyG index and RVO. Sirakaya et al. reported significantly higher TyG levels in BRVO patients and identified the index as an independent predictor of disease risk, with an optimal cutoff of 8.52 (sensitivity 83%, specificity 70%) [24]. Similarly, Katipoğlu et al. found that an elevated TyG index independently increased the risk of RVO after adjusting for age and comorbidities (OR: 1.45, 95% CI: 1.03–2.02, p = 0.03) [25]. In a recent study, TyG levels were reported to be significantly higher in patients with RVO compared with healthy controls. Moreover, multivariate logistic regression analysis demonstrated a strong independent association between the TyG index and RVO even after adjustment for age and sex (OR = 48.21; 95% CI: 7.19-323.41; p < 0.001). In that study, the TyG index also showed good diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.819 and relatively high sensitivity and specificity values [26]. These findings are consistent with our results, which also demonstrated significantly elevated TyG index levels in patients with BRVO and identified the TyG index as an independent factor associated with the disease. Although the diagnostic accuracy observed in our study was more modest, both studies support the concept that the TyG index may reflect the underlying metabolic and vascular disturbances involved in the pathogenesis of RVO. In the present study, we also performed a multivariable logistic regression analysis including several established systemic risk factors for retinal vascular disease, such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and lipid profile components. After adjustment for these potential confounders, the TyG index remained significantly associated with BRVO, whereas the other variables included in the model were not statistically significant. This finding may suggest that the TyG index captures combined metabolic effects related to insulin resistance and lipid metabolism that are not fully reflected by individual lipid parameters alone. Therefore, the TyG index may serve as an integrated indicator of metabolic and vascular alterations relevant to BRVO.

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and prospective studies are needed to establish temporal associations between elevated TyG index and BRVO development. Second, the relatively limited sample size may reduce the statistical robustness of the findings and may restrict the generalizability of the results to broader populations. Although a post-hoc power analysis indicated that the study had sufficient power to detect group differences, larger multicenter studies with greater sample sizes are needed to confirm and further validate our findings. Third, the control group consisted of patients presenting for cataract surgery, which may introduce a degree of selection bias, as these individuals may not fully represent the general healthy population. However, the control group was carefully matched with the BRVO group in terms of age and sex, and no statistically significant differences were observed between the groups regarding major systemic risk factors such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, or cardiovascular disease. Therefore, the potential impact of selection bias on the study findings may be limited. Finally, another limitation of this study is the lack of information on certain potential confounding factors, such as BMI, smoking status, and medication use (including lipid-lowering or antihypertensive therapies). These variables are known to influence metabolic and cardiovascular risk and may also be associated with retinal vascular diseases. The absence of these parameters in the present analysis may have introduced residual confounding, and future studies incorporating a broader range of systemic and lifestyle-related factors would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the relationship between the TyG index and BRVO.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings indicate that the TyG index is significantly associated with BRVO and may reflect underlying metabolic and vascular alterations related to BRVO. Incorporating the TyG index into clinical practice could facilitate early identification of high-risk individuals, particularly among patients with concomitant cardiovascular or metabolic risk factors, and promote interdisciplinary strategies targeting both ocular and systemic vascular health. Large-scale prospective studies are required to validate these findings and to further define the predictive and prognostic utility of the TyG index in retinal vascular disease.
