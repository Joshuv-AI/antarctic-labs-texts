Predictive analysis of greenhouse gas emissions from electric vehicle charging in the United States
Authors: Mahyar Amirgholy, Faysal A Chowdhoury, Chenyu Wang, S Nikhila Kanigiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectric vehicles (EVs) emit substantially fewer air pollutants than conventional internal combustion engine vehicles. However, the continuous increase in electricity demand from the power grid for EV charging, resulting from the growing adoption and total vehicle miles traveled, leads to higher greenhouse gas emissions from the power sector. This study presents a predictive analysis of energy sector greenhouse gas emissions from EV charging at the regional level across the United States under various projection scenarios of technology costs, fuel prices, demand growth, and electricity sector policies. The predictive modeling of greenhouse gas emissions from EV charging is performed using a machine learning model developed on the Meta Prophet platform designed to capture temporal patterns and seasonality with a high level of precision. Trained on simulation data from the Cambium model, developed by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL), our model provides accurate and continuous predictions of CO2, N2O, and CH4 emission rates from EV charging through 2050 under eight power generation planning scenarios, each outlining different projections for costs, prices, demand, and policy outcomes. Our analysis suggests that, by 2030, total grid emissions of CO2, N2O, and CH4 from EV battery charging in the United States are projected to decline by 52.67%, 65.71%, and 53.65%, respectively, compared to 2025 under the mid-case scenario, despite an overall increase of 152.57% in EV electricity demand. By 2050, emissions are expected to decline further by 80.13%, 94.15%, and 75.62%, respectively, with total EV electricity demand rising by 802.02%. Our scenario analysis results underscore the pivotal role of regional energy mixes and the pace of renewable energy deployment in decarbonizing transportation through electrification, highlighting the urgent need for proactive policy measures to accelerate the adoption of clean and renewable energy technologies in order to meet long-term climate targets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43525-5.Keywords: Electric vehicles, Grid emissions, Machine learning, NREL Cambium, Greenhouse gas, Renewable energy, Electricity sector policiesSubject terms: Engineering, Civil engineering

Introduction

While electric vehicles (EVs) emit substantially fewer air pollutants than conventional internal combustion engine vehicles, the continuous rise in EV charging demand from the power grid also leads to increased greenhouse gas emissions from the power sector. The lower cost of electricity compared to gasoline and the reduced maintenance costs of EVs, coupled with the convenience of driving thanks to advanced driver assistance technologies, encourage users to travel more and further. With the rapid increase in EV adoption, which is expected to accelerate in the coming years due to incentivizing transportation decarbonization policies, the total vehicle miles traveled (VMT) by EVs is projected to significantly rise. The subsequent increase in electrical energy consumption for charging EV batteries will result in higher greenhouse gas emissions from power plants, depending on the power generation technology, distribution network efficiency, load temporal distribution load temporal distribution, and the energy mix of the grid in different states. This research addresses the challenge of predicting the rise in greenhouse gas emissions from the power sector due to the increased EV charging on a large scale using a machine learning approach.

The increasing energy consumption for EV battery charging is placing escalating pressure on the electrical grid, particularly during peak demand periods. Detailed simulations of urban EV charging behavior reveal significant spikes in electricity demand during evening hours, underscoring the urgent need for advanced demand-side management strategies to prevent grid overload1–5. Regional analyses of EV energy consumption also highlight the stress on grids in densely populated areas, where higher concentrations of EVs necessitate the implementation of smart grid technologies to effectively balance supply and demand6–8. Therefore, strategic placement of charging stations and effective control of charging in time and load can help alleviate local grid stress and improve energy efficiency, particularly in urban settings9–12. Comparisons between urban and rural areas show that urban grids, with their higher EV density, may face more immediate challenges, requiring targeted infrastructure improvements. In contrast, rural areas, while currently less impacted, may face future challenges as EV adoption increases. The deployment of decentralized renewable energy systems, such as small-scale wind or solar farms, could provide rural areas with a sustainable source of energy for EV charging, reducing the need for extensive grid expansions13,14. Longitudinal analyses of EV adoption trends project significant increases in electricity consumption, underscoring the urgent need for scalable grid infrastructure to support such growth15–19. On a national scale, integrating renewable energy sources is critical in mitigating the additional load from EVs, particularly when supported by controlled charging strategies that align EV charging demand with periods of high renewable generation, thereby enhancing the synergistic potential between widespread EV adoption and clean energy policies. The integration of renewable energy systems with EV charging infrastructure, particularly through smart charging strategies, can significantly mitigate greenhouse gas emissions from power plants as the energy consumption of EVs from the power grid increases20–23.

Greenhouse gas emissions from power plants, driven by EV charging from the grid, are heavily influenced by the electricity generation mix. In regions where fossil fuels dominate the grid, the expected reduction in greenhouse gas emissions from EVs may be minimal or even negative compared to conventional vehicles running on fossil fuels. In fact, in areas heavily reliant on coal-fired power plants, the life-cycle greenhouse gas emissions from EVs can exceed those of internal combustion engine vehicles due to the high carbon intensity of electricity generation24–26. Conversely, in regions where renewable energy constitutes a significant portion of the electricity mix, EVs can markedly reduce greenhouse gas emissions27–29. The timing of EV charging is also crucial, as grid emissions intensity varies throughout the day. Charging during off-peak hours, when renewable energy generation is higher, can further diminish emissions30–32. Therefore, implementing smart charging strategies that align EV charging with periods of low grid emissions can substantially reduce the carbon footprint of electric vehicles33,34. The widespread adoption of EVs could act as a catalyst for improvements in the electricity grid, potentially accelerating the transition to cleaner energy sources35. However, to accurately assess the energy and environmental impacts of increasing EV adoption, it is critical to analyze greenhouse gas emissions from power plants resulting from EV charging across various scenarios that project technology costs, fuel prices, demand growth, and electricity sector policies.

This research presents a predictive analysis of greenhouse gas emissions from the power generation mix at the regional level with the increase in energy consumption of EVs from the grid across the United States. The predictive analysis of greenhouse gas emissions resulting from EV charging is performed using a machine learning model developed on the Meta Prophet platform, precisely capturing temporal patterns and seasonality. Greenhouse gas emissions from power plants due to the energy consumption of EVs for battery charging are influenced by several critical factors, including power generation technology, distribution network efficiency, load temporal distribution, and the energy mix of the grid. We accurately capture the impact of these factors on greenhouse gas emissions from EV charging by training the proposed machine learning model using simulation results from the Cambium model, developed by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL). The Cambium model projects evolution of power generation and distribution systems, providing a robust framework for simulating the operations of the electric sector across the United States. Trained with simulation results from the Cambium model, our machine learning model enables accurate and continuous predictions of carbon dioxide (CO2), nitrous oxides (N2O), and methane (CH4) emissions from EV charging over the coming months and years. To provide a comprehensive predictive analysis of greenhouse gas emissions resulting from EV charging, we train the model using eight power generation planning scenarios, each outlining different projections of technology costs, fuel prices, demand growth, and electricity sector policies. We then apply our machine learning model to analyze the emissions of CO2, N2O, and CH4 from power generation resulting from increased EV charging across 18 energy sector regions of the United States. Our predictive analysis results indicate that, by 2030, CO2, N2O, and CH4 grid emissions are projected to decrease by 52.67%, 65.71%, and 53.65%, respectively, compared to 2025 under the mid-case scenario, despite an overall increase of 152.57% in EV electricity demand. By 2050, emissions are expected to decrease further by 80.13%, 94.15%, and 75.62%, respectively, with total EV electricity demand rising by 802.02% under the mid-case scenario.

Grid emission model

Greenhouse gas emissions from EV charging exhibit temporal patterns and seasonality due to fluctuations in energy demand and seasonal shifts in the energy mix, particularly in the availability of renewable energy. To accurately capture these dynamics in predicting greenhouse gas emissions from EV charging, we develop a machine learning model on the Meta Prophet platform, enabling the precise capture of temporal patterns and seasonality. The machine learning model is trained using simulation results from the Cambium model, with parameters fine-tuned for optimal performance. Model tuning involved adjusting key hyperparameters within the Prophet framework to balance model flexibility and generalizability. Parameters related to trend dynamics, i.e., growth type and changepoint prior scale, were tuned to accurately capture long-term shifts in emissions patterns. Seasonal dynamics were refined using the Fourier order and seasonality prior scale to represent monthly variability without overfitting. Uncertainty parameters, including interval width and smoothing priors, were calibrated to reflect realistic confidence intervals. Hyperparameter selection was guided by in-sample evaluation metrics, ensuring robust reconstruction of temporal dynamics. Importantly, emissions data were normalized prior to training, allowing the model to focus on learning temporal dynamics consistently rather than absolute magnitude differences.

The primary motivation for developing the proposed machine learning approach is to capture and generalize complex temporal trends and seasonal dynamics in simulated emissions rates at a month-to-month resolution, rather than relying on static interpolation between widely spaced milestone years. Cambium provides emissions rate projections at five-year intervals (2025, 2030, 2035, 2040, 2045, and 2050), which are insufficient for high-resolution analysis of EV charging emissions, particularly given that grid emissions can vary significantly month to month, and electricity demand evolves continuously over time. By training the proposed machine learning model on simulation benchmarks, our approach learns the underlying trends and patterns, enabling accurate and continuous projections of grid emissions from EV charging that are consistent with Cambium’s simulation outputs while effectively filling temporal gaps between benchmark years Simulating grid emissions for every month and year through 2050 using the Cambium model would be computationally expensive and resource-intensive. In contrast, our proposed machine learning approach offers a computationally efficient alternative for generating high-resolution estimates of grid emissions, which is particularly valuable for predictive grid emissions analysis and long-term energy planning at both regional and national scales. Once fully trained, the model provides accurate and continuous predictions of CO2, N2O, and CH4 grid emission rates (i.e., the additional emissions from power plants per 1 MWh increase in EV charging from the grid) through 2050. By bypassing the need for running complex simulations, the proposed machine learning model facilitates the prediction of greenhouse gas emissions from increased EV charging, under various projections of technology costs, fuel prices, demand growth, and electricity sector policies.

Training data

To provide accurate predictions of power plant emission rates through 2050, we train our machine learning model using results from Cambium, an advanced simulation tool developed by NREL. Cambium projects the evolution of power generation and distribution systems, offering a robust framework for simulating the operations of the U.S. electric sector. The Cambium model draws on data from the Regional Energy Deployment System (ReEDS) and PLEXOS, a sophisticated, high-performance tool for energy system simulation, to model both operational dynamics and the long-term evolution of the electric grid under various scenarios37. ReEDS is a least-cost optimization model that projects the structural changes in the electric sector, considering factors such as fuel costs, technology costs, and policies. It determines the optimal mix of generation and transmission assets to minimize system costs. PLEXOS, on the other hand, simulates operations of the electric sector based on the ReEDS projections, capturing the dynamic and operational aspects of the grid. The integration of these two models allows Cambium to provide a comprehensive view of future energy sector.

The Cambium model enables the simulation of operations and the evolution of the electric sector, allowing for the estimation of emissions rates for CO2 (kg/MWh), N2O (g/MWh), CH4 (g/MWh) from EV charging across the United States under various scenarios projecting technology costs, fuel prices, demand growth, and electricity sector policies. The Cambium scenarios represent cost-optimized projections of grid evolution under a wide spectrum of federal and state energy policies. While the modeling framework incorporates mature technologies such as wind, solar, battery storage, and certain forms of nuclear power, it deliberately excludes emerging technologies, such as carbon capture and storage (CCS), hydrogen turbines, enhanced geothermal systems, and small modular reactors, due to prevailing uncertainties regarding their commercial viability, siting feasibility, and cost-performance characteristics. Cambium also does not explicitly capture advanced smart grid functionalities, such as real-time demand response or distributed energy resource coordination. This simplification reflects the current uncertainty around their system-wide deployment and measurable impact. Therefore, the emissions reduction potential associated with grid modernization may be modestly underrepresented in the scenarios. The projected grid emission rates can be then applied to estimate projected emissions from EV charging under a marginal attribution framework, while recognizing that grid electricity is shared across all end uses.

Given the intermittent nature of the simulation data, we normalize the training dataset to ensure consistency and accuracy in the predictive model. Normalization scales the data to a uniform range without distorting the inherent differences, which is essential for effective model training. Normalization standardizes the emission rates, minimizing disparities due to varying magnitudes and ensuring the dataset is cohesive. The resulting standardized training dataset, which includes normalized emission rates across the specified months and years, establishes a reliable foundation for accurate long-term predictions.

With the normalized data in place, we configure and train our machine learning model, designed to handle time series data with periodic, non-periodic, and irregular patterns, as well as missing values, making it a powerful tool for predictive analysis. The proposed model is trained using the normalized emission rates of CO2, N2O, and CH4, incorporating monthly variations across different years. The training process involves fitting the model to capture long-term trends, periodic patterns, and recurring irregularities in the data, ensuring accurate predictions of emission rates. Throughout the training, the model parameters are carefully tuned to align with the normalized dataset. The primary objective of the proposed machine learning model is to accurately capture the underlying trends and patterns in emissions rates based on available simulation data, rather than to perform predictive forecasting beyond the training dataset. Accordingly, the in-sample R² metric serves as a key indicator of the model’s ability to replicate simulation benchmark values, reflecting the model performance and accuracy. Georgia, however, experiences a more gradual reduction in CH4 emissions due to its continued reliance on fossil fuels and slower integration of renewable energy. In all scenarios, most notably Scenarios 8, CH4 emissions are projected to rise after 2035, particularly in Georgia and, to a lesser extent, in California and Washington. This increase is attributed to rising energy demand, coupled with slower renewable energy deployment and a partial return to fossil fuel generation to meet peak demand following the expiration of the IRA’s clean energy provisions. New York, meanwhile, sees moderate reductions in CH4 emissions, benefiting from a balanced energy mix of renewables and natural gas, though it lags behind Washington’s swift transition to hydropower. By 2040, New York surpasses Washington and achieves near-zero CH4 emissions in most scenarios, while Georgia continues to exhibit higher CH4 emission rates due to its reliance on natural gas for peak demand.

Predictive analysis of greenhouse gas emissions from EV charging

The electric energy consumption of EVs from the power grid to charge their batteries results in increased greenhouse gas emissions from power plants, influenced by factors such as power plant technology, distribution network efficiency, load temporal distribution, and the energy mix of the grid. The proposed machine learning approach allows for accurate estimation of greenhouse gas emissions from power plants as EV energy consumption rises under various projections of technology costs, fuel prices, demand growth, and electricity sector policies. Figure 5a–d plot the increase in emissions of CO2, N2O, and CH4 with increasing electricity demand for EV battery charging from 0 to 10 GWh from the power grid under the Mid-case Scenario (Scenario 1) through 2050 in California, Georgia, New York, and Washington.

Projected increase in CO2, CH4, and N2O grid emissions from EV battery charging – mid-case scenario.

In California and Washington, the increase in CO2, N2O, and CH4 emissions is relatively moderate, largely due to their significant reliance on renewable energy sources like solar and hydropower, which substantially reduce the need for natural gas and limit related emissions. California’s early adoption of solar energy and Washington’s vast hydropower capacity allow both states to meet the growing demand for EV charging with minimal dependence on fossil fuels. In contrast, Georgia, with its continued reliance on fossil fuels, particularly natural gas and coal, experiences the sharpest increases in CO2, N2O, and CH4 emissions, reflecting the higher emissions intensity per unit of electricity generated, especially during periods of peak demand. New York exhibits a more moderate increase in emissions across all three pollutants, though these emissions drop sharply by 2040 as the state accelerates its transition from natural gas to offshore wind, nuclear, and other renewable energy sources.

To accurately estimate future increases in grid emissions from EV charging, we utilize projections of EV electricity demand from the power grid, based on projected EV adoption trends modeled in Cambium. Figure 6a plots the projected average trajectory of EV adoption through 2050, along with associated upper and lower bounds, across energy sector regions in the United States37. These projections are based on a range of determinants, including historical EV registration patterns, vehicle fleet turnover, total cost of ownership, and broader macroeconomic trends such as economic and population growth, as well as travel demand. These projections also account for advancements in battery technology, the pace of expansion in both public and private charging infrastructure, and the influence of federal and state-level EV policies, such as purchase incentives and increasingly stringent emissions standards, that directly shape market uptake. The projected increase in electricity demand for EV battery charging is then estimated in each energy sector region based on projected EV adoption rates, average vehicle miles traveled, and the energy intensity of various EV classes (light-, medium-, and heavy-duty) across the United States37. Figure 6b presents the projected growth in electricity demand for EV charging from 2025 to 2050, along with the lower and upper bounds, across all regions of the United States. California is projected to have the largest increase in demand, driven by both a substantial rise in EV adoption and relatively higher average vehicle miles traveled. Other states, such as Georgia, New York, and Washington, follow similar upward trends at a slower pace.

Projected increase in EV adoption and electricity demand for battery charging through 2050.

Utilizing our machine learning model, developed on the Prophet platform and trained with data from Cambium, we estimate greenhouse gas emissions based on the projected rise in EV charging from the grid across the United States. Figure 7a–c plots the projected emissions of CO2, N2O, and CH4 resulting from increased EV charging through 2050 in California, Georgia, New York, and Washington (See Fig. S2a-c in Appendix A for a comparison of grid emissions from EV charging across all states), under eight scenarios that account for various projections of technology costs, fuel prices, demand growth, and electricity sector policies. Overall, projected CO2, N2O, and CH4 emissions resulting from EV charging have declining trends in California, Georgia, New York, and Washington, although the pace and patterns of reduction vary significantly across states and scenarios. At the outset of the projection period starting in 2025, California has the highest grid emissions from EV charging (with the exception of Scenario 7 for N2O emissions), largely due to the high rate of EV adoption and elevated vehicle miles traveled, which together result in greater electricity demand (see Fig. 6b). Despite the high initial levels, California is expected to achieve the most rapid emissions decline among all states (See Fig. S2a-c), reflecting Californios’s ambitious strategy to transition from fossil fuels to renewable energy, particularly solar and wind, within its electricity mix. By 2050, California’s grid emissions from EV charging are projected to be among the lowest nationwide, underscoring the effectiveness of its clean energy policies even as it maintains the highest EV electricity demand in the country.

Predictive analysis of CO2, CH4, and N2O grid emissions from EV charging through 2050 under various energy sector scenarios.

Despite having relatively lower electricity demand for EV charging compared to California, New York, and Washington, Georgia has significantly higher grid emissions of CO2, CH4, and particularly N2O due to Georgia’s continued heavy reliance on fossil fuels, especially natural gas and coal, for electricity generation. At the start of the projection horizon in 2025, grid emissions from EV charging in Georgia are considerably higher than in Washington and New York. The significantly lower grid emissions from EV charging in Washington and New York, despite their higher EV adoption rates and electricity demand, are primarily attributable to their cleaner energy mixes, which already incorporate a greater share of renewable and low-carbon generation sources. Nevertheless, grid emissions in Washington and New York continue to decline over time, even as EV adoption and electricity demand increase (see Fig. 6b), due to the expanding share of renewables in their electricity mix. In Washington, grid emissions from EV charging approach zero after 2030, while New York reaches near-zero emissions around 2040. These trends are driven by significant investments in wind and hydropower in Washington, and in offshore wind and nuclear energy in New York, both of which are replacing fossil fuels in their power grids.

The expiration of the IRA tax credits for renewable energy is expected to negatively impact the pace of emissions reductions after 2035, particularly under Scenarios 3, 5, and 8, where renewable energy and battery costs remain high and/or natural gas prices are projected to stay low. This policy shift is likely to have the greatest adverse effects in states like Georgia, which continue to rely heavily on fossil fuels for electricity generation. In contrast, states such as Washington and New York, which are expected to achieve cleaner energy mixes before the expiration of the IRA incentives, will be less affected.

Overall, analysis results indicate that despite substantial increases projected in EV electricity demand by 2050 compared to 2025 in California, Georgia, New York, and Washington, as shown in Fig. 6b, grid emissions are expected to decline across all three major grid greenhouse gases under the mid-case scenario. Specifically, CO2 emissions are projected to decrease by 80.19%, 81.07%, 95.34%, and 88.68%, N2O by 87.07%, 93.59%, 90.84%, and 97.89%, and CH4 by 81.49%, 77.17%, 98.60%, and 86.58% in California, Georgia, New York, and Washington, respectively, under the mid-case scenario. At the national level, emissions from EV charging are also expected to decline significantly. By 2030, CO2, N2O, and CH4 grid emissions are projected to decrease by 52.67%, 65.71%, and 53.65%, respectively, despite an overall increase of 152.57% in EV electricity demand. By 2050, emissions are expected to decrease by 80.13%, 94.15%, and 75.62%, respectively, with total EV electricity demand rising by 802.02% under the mid-case scenario. The distribution of CO2, N2O, and CH4 emissions from EV charging across the 18 U.S. energy sector regions is detailed in Table S1 in Appendix A.

The results presented in Fig. 7a–c underscore the pivotal role of regional energy mixes and the pace of renewable energy deployment in decarbonizing transportation through electrification. These findings highlight the urgent need for proactive policy measures to accelerate the adoption of clean and renewable energy technologies in order to meet long-term climate targets.

While this study focuses on projecting grid emissions from EV charging under various energy sector scenarios across the United States, accurate prediction of emissions reductions from vehicle electrification also requires accurate modeling of emissions from non-electric vehicles by capturing the complex dynamics and composition of heterogeneous traffic at large scales. In our next research, we will present an integrated modeling framework that simultaneously projects fuel consumption and associated emissions from internal combustion vehicles, alongside electricity demand for EV battery charging and the resulting grid emissions, based on the dynamics and composition of traffic across major cities in the United States.

Conclusion

The increase in electricity demand of EVs from power grids with the rise in their adoption and VMT, is expected to ironically elevate greenhouse gas emissions from the power sector in the coming years in the absence of substantial clean energy upgrades. This research leverages machine learning for predicting greenhouse gas emissions from the power sector due to rise in the electricity demand of EVs from the grid over the coming years. To this end, we have developed a large-scale emission model on the Meta Prophet platform, designed to capture temporal patterns and seasonality with a high level of precision. Our model is trained using the NREL Cambium database, allowing it to accurately predict the increase in CO2, CH4, and N2O emissions from power plants in response to the escalating energy consumption of EVs for charging their batteries across 18 energy sector regions of the United States, under eight projections of pricing, taxation, and development scenarios. By bypassing the need for running complex simulation models, the proposed machine learning model facilitates predicative analysis of greenhouse gas emissions from the energy sector due to the rise in electricity demand of EVs for battery changing.

Our analysis suggests that, by 2030, total grid emissions of CO2, N2O, and CH4 from EV battery charging in the United States are projected to decline by 52.67%, 65.71%, and 53.65%, respectively, compared to 2025 under the mid-case scenario, despite an overall increase of 152.57% in EV electricity demand. By 2050, emissions are expected to decline further by 80.13%, 94.15%, and 75.62%, respectively, with total EV electricity demand rising by 802.02%. Our scenario analysis results underscore the pivotal role of regional energy mixes and the pace of renewable energy deployment in decarbonizing transportation through electrification, highlighting the urgent need for proactive policy measures to accelerate the adoption of clean and renewable energy technologies in order to meet long-term climate targets. In our next research, we will present the results of a comprehensive study on predictive analysis of vehicle and grid emissions, based on the dynamics and composition of heterogenous traffic across major cities in the United States.
