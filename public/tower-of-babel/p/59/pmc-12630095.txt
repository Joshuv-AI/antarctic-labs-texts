Complex perineal degloving injury in a 4-year-old girl: A rare pediatric case managed at tertiary hospital
Authors: Gauthier Bahizire Murhula, Justin Wilson Mwenga, Mathayo Shadrack, Edwin Moringo Mrema, Adelaide Elisamehe Mghase, Laura Pompermaier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPerineal degloving injuries are large extensive avulsion of skin, subcutaneous from their underlying fascia resulting from large shear and rotational force commonly due to road traffic in children.Presentation of caseWe present a 4 years-old girl was rolled over by a large truck and presented a complex perineal degloving wound with associated pelvic and femur fracture, rectovaginal fistula and urethra disruption. She was managed by a multidisciplinary team including anesthesiologists, pediatric surgeons, orthopedists, urologists, gynecologists, plastic surgeons, nutritionist, and physiotherapists. Although persistent slight limping, functional and cosmetic outcomes improved.Clinical discussionThe management of complex perineal degloving injuries requires proper assessment and coordinated efforts of a multidisciplinary team, during the damage control phase. Initial priorities include copious irrigation, meticulous debridement, and diversion of both stool and urine to minimize contamination and infection risk. Secondary wound exploration and the application of advanced dressing are cointegrated with early nutritional support and physiotherapy, all aimed at optimizing the patient's status for definitive surgical reconstruction.ConclusionA multidisciplinary team approach is the mainstay in the management of complex perineal degloving wounds. Timely decision-making regarding wound closure must be guided by both the local wound condition and the patient's overall hemodynamic stability. Early involvement of the plastic surgery team is essential as it significantly enhances the potential for optimal functional and aesthetic outcomes.

Introduction

Perineal degloving injuries are traumatic avulsion of skin and subcutaneous tissue from the underlying fascia, bones, and organs in the perineal region. In infants, these rare but severe injuries are mainly caused by motor vehicle accidents, as highlighted by the Global Report on Road Safety, followed by fall from heights, sexual abuse and blunt injuries [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Their patterns range from minimal contusion to extensive and deep laceration detaching skin, subcutaneous tissue and perineum covering tissue from the underlying structures [5,6].

Severity and extension of these wounds are related to the involvement of rotational shear forces, which cause layers of tissue to slide against each other, disrupting the connection to the underlying blood supply [1,4]. Complex degloving injuries of the perineal region, which involve adjacent structures, such as anus, rectum, vagina, urethra, and pelvic bones, are associated to high risk of morbidity and mortality, primarily due to the potential for severe infections and sepsis resulting from contamination by the digestive and urinary tracts [7].

Surgical management of these complex injuries should involve a multidisciplinary workforce, including general surgeons, gynecologists, urologists, orthopedics, and plastic surgeons. Surgical treatment should include debridement of the wounds and diversion of urine and feces, followed by either primary or delayed wound closure [[8], [9], [10]].

This case report presents the management of a complex perineal degloving injury in a 4-year- old girl, treated at a tertiary care hospital in Tanzania.

Methods

This case report was prepared following the SCARE Guidelines for reporting surgical case reports to ensure completeness and transparency in reporting [11].

Case presentation

Initial presentation

A 4-year-old girl sustained complex perineal injuries following a road traffic accident, in which the tires of a large truck rolled over her right gluteal and lower limb. She was immediately brought by bystanders to the nearby dispensary, where wound dressing was done under analgesia, and after that she was referred to a regional hospital.

At the second level hospital, the treatment actions were based on damaged control surgery principles and were made of resuscitation with one liter of isotonic i.v. fluids and 2 units blood were provided, and after dressing reinforcement.

She was referred to the tertiary care facility located in Dar Es Salaam, Tanzania, for multidisciplinary management, where she was admitted one day after the injury.

On arrival at the emergency department, the patient was awake but pale, with signs of hypovolemic shock: tachycardia (HR 157), hypotension (BP 83/57 mmHg), respiratory rate 25 bpm, SpO₂ 93 % on room air, and capillary refill time > 3 s. Airway and breathing were intact. A thorough physical examination revealed a complex and extensive soft tissue injury extending from the right inguinal region to the lateral aspect of the right knee. The wound involved the anterior and posterior perineal triangles, right thigh, intergluteal cleft, and medial quadrants of the left gluteal region, with exposed sacral and coccygeal bone segments (Fig. 1). Perineal anatomy was severely disrupted. Blood-stained fecal matter was noted from both vaginal and anal orifices, and the urethral meatus was also blood-stained.Fig. 1A complex degloving perineal wound and deep laceration of the right gluteal and thigh on admission (day of the surgery).Fig. 1

Initial resuscitation included 20 ml/kg Ringer's lactate and one unit of whole blood via two 16-gauge antecubital IV lines. All wounds were packed to control bleeding. A multidisciplinary team (MDT) was assembled within 30 min of arrival, including orthopedic surgeons, pediatric surgeons, urologists, and gynecologists. The team developed a coordinated, patient-specific strategy that would not have been feasible within a single-specialty framework. Key decisions included:

Pediatric surgery: A double-barrel colostomy was performed to divert fecal flow, ensuring hygiene and promoting healing of the rectovaginal fistula (RVF).

Urology: Urethro-cystoscopy showed intact bladder wall, and disruption in the distal third portion of the wall. Urethral realignment using 8-French catheter minimized the risk of future stricture.

Gynecology: Conservative management of the RVF was chosen based on anatomical assessment and healing potential, avoiding unnecessary surgical trauma.

Orthopedy: A pelvic binder was applied instead of external fixation due to resource limitations (lack of specific pins).

Given the contamination of the inguinal and perineal wounds, after aggressive debridement, conservative management of the wounds was chosen.

Surgical management and post-operative care

While fecal and urinary diversion, fracture stabilization, and wound management are indeed standard components of care for complex perineal injuries, the added value of the MDT in this case lay in the real-time integration of specialty-specific expertise, which enabled a tailored, function-preserving strategy for a pediatric patient with extensive multi-system trauma.

Specifically:

Pediatric surgeons opted for a double-barrel colostomy rather than a loop colostomy, based on long-term functional outcomes in a growing child. This decision prioritized future continence and minimized the risk of complications associated with fecal contamination of the wound.

Urologists performed immediate urethral realignment using pediatric instrumentation, which is not routinely available or used by general trauma surgeons. This approach reduced the risk of long-term stricture and preserved urinary function.

Gynecologists chose conservative management of the rectovaginal fistula, based on anatomical assessment and healing potential. This avoided unnecessary surgical trauma and reflected a nuanced understanding of pelvic floor anatomy and healing in pediatric patients.

Orthopedic surgeons applied a pelvic binder instead of external fixation due to resource limitations but did so in coordination with the surgical teams to ensure stability without compromising access to the perineal region during debridement and reconstruction.

These decisions were made jointly and dynamically, with each specialty contributing to a comprehensive, patient-specific plan that balanced immediate survival with long-term functional and psychological outcomes. This level of coordination and specialty-specific adaptation goes beyond what a single trauma surgeon could reasonably achieve in isolation.

Following surgery, the patient was admitted to the pediatric ICU for close monitoring. At the ICU, the patient was initially intubated, treated with vasopressor, antibiotics (Ceftriaxone 800 mg twice and Metronidazole 160 mg 8hoursly), analgesia made of paracetamol, proton pump inhibitor, wound dressing initially with normal saline imbibed sterile gauzes was changed daily after irrigation, and oral feeding was started.

On day 4, all inflammatory parameters were elevated, wounds located on the right gluteus and thigh had sloughed and developed some areas of necrosis. A second debridement was performed, and daily dressing with normal saline and honey imbibed sterile gauzes followed. Four weeks after admission, wound beds were covered with clean granulation tissue (Fig. 3). The plastic surgery team was subsequently involved, and gluteal advancement flap combined with split thickness skin graft were planned to after a week of nutritional and metabolic optimization (Fig. 4). Five weeks after admission, a left gluteal maximus random advancement flap was raised to cover bones within the intergluteal cleft, and split thickness skin graft harvested from the left thigh was applied to cover the right thigh (Fig. 5). Follow-up

The patient was discharged home 20 weeks after admission with follow-up arranged through the orthopedic clinic for further evaluation of the pelvic fracture. The femur fracture had already consolidated, and the patient exhibited only a residual limp at 24 weeks' visit. Eight months after the trauma, gynecological examination under anesthesia revealed a complete closure of the RVF and both the rectum and vagina were patent and healed. However, the patient developed a colostomy prolapse, which was subsequently revised. Additionally, an inguino-hypogastric skin contracture was identified and released prior to colostomy closure (Fig. 7). Overall, outcomes after the multidisciplinary approach were successful. All wounds were covered and healed with satisfactory cosmetic aspect (Fig. 8). Urinary and anal sphincters were tonic and functional. All fractures consolidated and only a slight limp remains during walking with mild discomfort likely due to residual pelvic instability or muscle weakness.Fig. 7Wound appearance 8 months post-admission with inguino-hypogastric contracture (Day 140 post first surgery and Day 80 post last surgery).Fig. 7Fig. 8Final wound status after contracture release at the day of colostomy closure (day 170 post first surgery and day 30 post last surgery).Fig. 8

Discussion

Degloving perineal wounds are rare forms of blunt perineal trauma that account for 0,2 to 0,8 % in young girls. Most of these injuries occur before the age of six, as was the case with our patient [1,2]. Degloving perineal injuries are caused by high intensity torsional or rotational forces, such as those from moving vehicles (like motorbikes or car wheels) [5,6].

This led to deep lacerations involving skin, subcutaneous tissue, and sometimes even fascia and muscle, which are separated from the underlying structures. Consequently, perforating arteries are torn, and venous congestion and increased pressure occurs in the sub-dermal plexus leading to compromise the viability of the degloved tissue. Because of this, surgeons often prefer serial debridement and delayed closure rather than immediate repair, in the aim of getting proper demarcation of the viable tissue [8,10].

In complex and polystructural injuries, the current guidelines advocate for damage control surgery principles made of proper resuscitation after primary survey, followed by investigations and a multidisciplinary approach for the first damage control and examination under anesthesia of the perineal region also in unstable patients [4,10,12].

In our case, although the patient arrived at the tertiary hospital within 24 h of the trauma, initial resuscitation was already initiated at the second level hospital, where she was first admitted. This resuscitation was continued upon arrival at the tertiary center, allowing subsequent management steps to proceed without delay. The surgical management of these complex wounds, including debridement, urine and feces diversion, depend on severity and location of the damages [10,[12], [13], [14]]. Many studies are currently advocating for early primary closure in hemodynamically stable patients with mild to moderate contaminated wounds [1,4,6,10]. Unstable patients or patients with severe contaminated wounds are instead taken to the operating room for initial damage control surgery, which can include diversion of urine and stool, and debridement. Delayed closure of the wounds is planned when the patients become hemodynamic stable and their wounds less contaminated [4,8,10]. Different wound coverage options from the reconstructive ladder have been used to cover degloving perineal wounds, ranging from direct closure, and split thickness, to pedicled and free flaps [8,9]. The choice of different surgical options depends upon the local status of defects, available reconstructive options, and local resources. As example, Lau et al. have used pedicled gluteal flap in a complex degloving perineal wound with good cosmetic and functional results [8]. Similarly, Meara et al. has used both pedicled flap and split thickness skin graft after negative pressure wound therapy with good cosmetic and functional results [9]. In our patient, a random gluteal advancement flap was performed to cover the sacral bone that was exposed, and split thickness skin graft were used to cover other granulation tissue area with better functional and cosmetic outcomes.

The literature suggests early involvement of plastic surgeons in the multidisciplinary team that manages degloving perineal wound [1,8,13]. In our case, plastic surgeons were consulted only after four weeks of in-hospital care. Nevertheless, early involvement of surgeons with expertise in reconstructive surgery could suggest more effective dressing options, such as negative pressure wound therapy, and contribute to improve the debridement strategies, reducing unnecessary tissue removal, limiting the defect and decreasing the length of stay [8,9,13]. Involving a plastic surgeon in a multidisciplinary team provides the opportunity to establish a recovery plan and move towards a clearly established treatment process.

Conclusion

A multidisciplinary approach facilitated accurate injury assessment and timely interventions, including immediate urethral realignment and conservative management of RVF. Real-time collaboration between pediatric surgeons, urologists, gynecologists, and orthopedic surgeons was essential in minimizing complications and optimizing both immediate and long-term outcomes for this pediatric patient with a complex perineal degloving injury.
