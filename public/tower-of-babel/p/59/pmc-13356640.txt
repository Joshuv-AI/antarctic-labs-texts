pH-driven interfacial selective assembly of gliadin nanoparticles enables high-performance clean-label whipping creams
Authors: Wenxin Chen, Zhichang Deng, Zhengzhuo Zhao, Jing Li, Wei Gao, Bin Li, Hongshan Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Developing clean-label, plant-based whipping creams without low-molecular-weight emulsifiers requires balancing overrun and stability. Herein, we deciphered the pH-dependent multiscale structure-function relationship of gliadin nanoparticle (GNP)-stabilized whipping creams. Emulsions (pH 5.0) prepared near the isoelectric point (pI) achieved superior firmness and ultra-high overrun. To elucidate the stabilization paradox, whereby a significantly increased interfacial protein mass unexpectedly fails to restrict fat coalescence, we decoupled interfacial and continuous-phase interactions. Quantitative proteomics revealed that although pH 5.0 enhanced overall interfacial GNP adsorption, the cross-linking γ-gliadin subfraction was selectively depleted. This structural vulnerability effectively offset the increased protein load, yielding a constant degree of fat partial coalescence. Consequently, macroscopic firmness was fundamentally driven by the continuous phase. Approaching pI induced profound protein unfolding, driving massive intermolecular disulfide cross-linking and hydrophobic interactions that intensified droplet flocculation and serum-phase aggregation. These molecular insights provide a robust physicochemical framework for rationally engineering clean-label aerated food matrices.

Introduction

The global shift toward clean-label, plant-based food products has driven an urgent industrial demand for synthetic emulsifier-free whipping cream, yet current plant-based formulations free of low-molecular-weight emulsifiers (LMWEs) universally suffer from the critical bottleneck of uncoordinated foaming performance, textural quality, and storage stability. This long-standing challenge fundamentally stems from the lack of a systematic, multi-scale mechanistic understanding of how plant protein-based stabilizers regulate hierarchical network formation in aerated emulsions, which severely limits the rational design and industrial scaling of high-performance clean-label whipping cream.

Whipped cream, a widely used aerated dairy emulsion in beverages, desserts, and baked goods, derives its final quality, including foaming capacity, firmness, and stability, from its underlying microstructure (Han et al., 2018). During whipping, large amounts of air are incorporated and stabilized by a continuous network formed through partially coalesced oil droplets (Zeng et al., 2024). The remaining non-coalesced oil droplets undergo protein-mediated flocculation, strengthening the foam network (W. Chen et al., 2025; Z. Liu et al., 2022). Meanwhile, unadsorbed serum-phase proteins can cross-link with non-coalesced oil droplets to form a continuous-phase emulsion gel network, which significantly contributes to the stability and macroscopic texture of whipped cream (Cao et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2023). These three mechanisms, namely the fat partial coalescence network, the flocculation of non-coalesced oil droplets, and the aggregation of serum proteins, collectively determine the macroscopic mechanical properties and storage stability of whipped cream.

Traditional whipped cream formulations rely on semi-crystalline fats, milk proteins, and low-molecular-weight emulsifiers (LMWEs) to establish this gas-oil-water multiphase system (Xu et al., 2025). However, the growing demand for clean-label products and the expanding use of plant-based proteins have driven the development of novel whipping systems stabilized solely by proteins, without LMWEs (W. Chen et al., 2026; Y. Hu et al., 2025). In this context, gliadin, a plant protein derived from wheat, has attracted considerable interest as a natural alternative to synthetic emulsifiers. In this context, gliadin nanoparticles (GNPs), derived from wheat, have emerged as promising natural emulsifiers due to their surface activity and ability to form viscoelastic interfacial films, making them suitable candidates for LMWEs-free whipped cream (W. Chen et al., 2025; Y.-Q. Hu et al., 2016; Peng et al., 2017; Peng et al., 2018).

In protein-stabilized whipping systems, the physicochemical properties of proteins, are highly sensitive to environmental conditions, particularly pH (Y. Hu et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2023). Variations in pH alter protein conformation and charge distribution, fundamentally influencing adsorption behavior and interfacial composition (Y. Hu et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2023). On one hand, pH influences the amount and composition of proteins adsorbed at the oil-water interface by affecting their charge and structural flexibility ((Li et al., 2022); Shen et al., 2022). This interfacial alteration subsequently regulates the extent of fat partial coalescence, which is a fundamental stabilization mechanism in particle-stabilized foams (Cheng et al., 2020; Bin Zhou et al., 2024; Beibei Zhou et al., 2021). On the other hand, as pH approaches the isoelectric point (pI), the reduced electrostatic repulsion among interfacial proteins triggers dense packing and droplet flocculation (Y.-Q. Hu et al., 2016; Song et al., 2023). While moderate flocculation supports arrest, excessive aggregation may induce gravitational instability (Anjali & Basavaraj, 2017; Ren et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2018). Although our recent empirical study demonstrated the macro-scale feasibility of GNP-stabilized formulations under a specific acidic condition (pH 4.0), a comprehensive molecular-level understanding of how pH drives these multilevel structural transitions remains elusive (W. Chen et al., 2025). Specifically, the dynamic chemical events, such as the selective interfacial adsorption of specific protein subfractions and the evolution of covalent/non-covalent bonds, have yet to be systematically decoupled.

To address the critical knowledge gap, this work systematically investigates the pH-driven multiscale structural evolution of GNP-stabilized LMWE-free whipping cream. We comprehensively deciphered the coupled regulatory mechanisms from three synergistically connected dimensions: fat partial coalescence, droplet flocculation, and continuous-phase protein aggregation. Notably, quantitative proteomic analysis was introduced to unveil the selective partitioning behavior of specific protein subfractions at the oil-water interface. By linking this interfacial compositional shift with film rheology and quantifying the continuous-phase chemical driving forces, we established a complete structure-function relationship. These fundamental mechanistic insights resolve the apparent stabilization paradox in aerated emulsions and provide a facile, scalable pH-regulation strategy for rationally tailoring high-performance, clean-label plant-protein-based food matrices.

Materials and methods

Materials

Wheat gluten (food grade, protein content 77.50%) and shea butter (food grade, purity ≥99.00%) were procured from Hua Feng Powder Co., Ltd. (Fengqiu, Henan, China) and Shanghai PZ Bio-Tech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), respectively. Gliadin (with a purity of 76.38%) was extracted from the wheat gluten using a 70% (v/v) ethanol solution and then freeze-dried and ground to obtain a fine powder. Specific chemicals used throughout the study, including hydrochloric acid (HCl, analytical grade, 36%–38%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, purity ≥96.00%), sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS, purity ≥99.00%) and β-mercaptoethanol (BME, purity ≥99.33%), were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All the experiments were performed using ultrapure water with a resistivity of 18.2 MΩ·cm at 25 °C, generated by a Milli-Q water purification system (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA).

Preparation and basic characterization of GNPs

Production of GNPs with different pH

GNPs were fabricated via an antisolvent precipitation method. Briefly, gliadin powders were dissolved in 70% (v/v) ethanol solution to a final concentration of 10% (w/v). The GNP dispersion was generated by rapidly injecting the gliadin solution into ultrapure water (pre-adjusted to pH 3.0) at a volume ratio of 1:10, followed by continuous stirring for 1 min. The dispersion pH was subsequently adjusted to pH 3.0–9.0 using 0.1 M HCl and 0.1 M NaOH. The particle size and surface charge of the GNPs at different pH levels were determined at 25 °C using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instrument Ltd., U.K.).

Physicochemical characterization of GNPs

Particle size and zeta potential

The particle size and surface charge of GNPs with different pH were determined using a Zetasizer Nano setup (Malvern Instrument Ltd., U.K.).

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy

The GNPs suspensions were diluted to 0.1 mg/mL using deionized water of corresponding pH to the sample solutions. The fluorescence intensity of GNPs suspensions under different pH conditions was measured using a Spark microplate reader (Tecan Trading Co., Ltd., Switzerland). The excitation wavelength (λex) was set at 230 nm, and the emission spectra was collected from 280 to 460 nm, with both excitation and emission slit widths set at 5.0 nm.

Wettability measurement

The oil-water contact angles of GNPs were evaluated using an OCA 20 optical contact angle measuring system (DataPhysics Instruments GmbH, Germany), following a modified procedure based on previous literature (Zhang et al., 2020). (X. Zhang et al., 2020). The freeze-dried GNPs powders with different pH were compressed to thin tablets with thickness of 1 mm and diameter of 13 mm. The tablets were immersed in MCT oil, and a drop of water (3 μL) was deposited to the surface of the tablets. The drop images were recorded, and the oil-water contact angles were calculated using the OCA 20 software.

Preparation of GNP-stabilized whipping creams and whipped creams

Following our established protocol, melted shea butter and GNP dispersions (pre-adjusted to pH 3.0, 4.0, or 5.0) were mixed at 60 °C for 50 min to yield a system containing 30% (w/w) fat and 0.5% (w/w) protein (W. Chen et al., 2025). The mixture was pre-emulsified at 8000 rpm for 5 min and subsequently homogenized twice at 20 MPa using a high-pressure homogenizer (AH-2010, ATS Engineering Ltd., Canada) to obtain the initial oil-in-water (O/W) emulsions (whipping creams).

After aging at 4 °C for 24 h, the emulsion was aerated with an overhead stirrer (DDQ-B01k1, Bear Electric Appliance CO., Ltd., China) at 1200 rpm. To ensure reproducibility and minimize subjective variability, the whipping process was dynamically terminated when the system reached its macroscopic structural arrest point (whipping endpoint). This endpoint was consistently determined by a single experienced operator following a standardized visual criterion: the formation of stiff peaks and stable, non-collapsing ripples on the surface.

Macroscopic properties and microstructure

Overrun

Overrun was determined by weighing a fixed volume of whipping cream emulsion with the same volume of whipped cream using formula (1) (Xu et al., 2022):(1)Overrun%=M1−M2M2×100%where M1 (g) is the mass of the initial, unaerated cream, and M2 (g) is the mass of the aerated cream after the whipping process.

Firmness

The macroscopic firmness of whipped cream was examined using a TA-XT Texture Analyzer (Stable Micro Systems Instruments, UK) equipped with an A/BE back extrusion rig (40 mm disc). The penetration depth was set to 25 mm at a constant test speed of 1.5 mm/s. All measurements were conducted at a controlled room temperature (25 °C). The maximum compression force recorded during the penetration phase was defined as the firmness (g).

Stability

To evaluate the thermal and structural stability, fresh whipped cream samples were incubated at 30 °C and 35 °C for 120 min. The shape retention ability was assessed visually, while the physical stability was quantified by measuring the serum loss (bleeding ratio) over time. The serum loss was calculated using Eq.(2) (Xu et al., 2022):(2)Serum loss%=M1M2×100%

Where M1 (g) is the mass of the melted fluid, M2 (g) is the mass of the freshly prepared whipped cream.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM)

The microstructural organization of the multiphase systems was visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (FV3000, Olympus, Japan). To spatially resolve the dispersed fat phase and the continuous protein network, the samples were fluorescently labeled with 0.01% (w/v) Nile red (dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide) and 0.01% (w/v) fluorescein 5-isothiocyanate (FITC, dissolved in anhydrous ethanol), respectively. FITC was excited at 488 nm with emission collected from 500 to 540 nm, while Nile red was excited at 591 nm with emission collected from 570 to 670 nm.

Characterizing the fat partial coalescence network

Degree of fat partial coalescence

The coalescence degree of the whipped creams was determined according to the method introduced by Zhang et al. (X. Zhang et al., 2025). Briefly, a mixture consisting of 20.00 g whipped cream and 10.00 g Oil Red O solution (0.001 wt% in corn oil) were centrifuged at 1500 ×g for 20 min at 40 °C. This elevated temperature was explicitly selected to completely melt the fat crystals, thereby preventing secondary shear-induced partial coalescence during centrifugation and eliminating the light-scattering interference of solid crystals on the subsequent spectrophotometric absorbance (Petrut et al., 2016). Following centrifugation, the upper red oil layer was carefully separated. Using corn oil without Oil Red O as the control, the absorbance of dye solution was measured at 520 nm. The percentage of oil separated from the partial coalescence droplets in total fat was determined according to Eq. (3):(3)Partial coalescence offat%=Mr×α−1Mc×Ø×100%

Where Mr (g) is the weight of added Oil Red O solution, Mc (g) is the weight of whipped cream, α is the ratio of absorbance of Oil Red O solution before and after centrifugation, and Ø (%) is the oil content.

Interfacial protein load

The serum-phase proteins were extracted by following the method of Li et al. with slight modifications (Li et al., 2021). The emulsion samples were centrifuged for 20 min (1500 ×g, 40 °C), and the serum phase was carefully aspirated with a syringe. Subsequently, the serum phase was subjected to a 0.45 μm aqueous filter to eliminate droplets of lipids. The protein concentration in the filtered serum was determined using a standard Bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit with the absorbance recorded at 562 nm. The percentage of adsorbed protein (AP, %) and the interfacial protein load (Γ, mg/m2) were calculated using Eq. (4) and Eq. (5), respectively:(4)AP%=C0−CsC0×100%(5)Γmg/m2=C0−Cs×D3,2×1−Ø×ρ6Ø

Where D3,2 (μm) is the surface-average diameter of the emulsion (section 2.3.1), Ø (%) is the mass fraction of oil phase, Co (mg/mL) is the initial protein content of the stock protein solution, Cs (mg/mL) is the protein content of the serum phase, and ρ is the density of shea butter (0.8344 g/cm3, 40 °C).

Interfacial adsorption and dilatational rheology

The interfacial adsorption kinetics and dilatational rheological properties of GNPs were characterized using an optical profile analysis tensiometer (TECLIS, France) (Chutinara et al., 2024). A pendent drop of 0.1% (w/v) protein solution was formed at the tip of a needle and dipped into a glass cuvette with MCT oil. Images of the drop were recorded using a charge-coupled device (CCD) camera, and the surface tension (γ) was calculated according to the Young-Laplace equation (Sun et al., 2025). The interfacial adsorption behavior of the proteins was investigated by measuring the change of surface pressure (π) for 2 h, which was calculated according to Eq. (6):(6)π=γo−γt

Where γ0 is the interfacial tension of the clean water-oil interface, and γt is the interfacial tension of the sample at one time-point.

The interfacial dilatation parameters were evaluated in oscillation mode. The procedure involved five sinusoidal cycles of oscillation at a frequency of 0.1 Hz with a relative area amplitude of 10%, separated by two cycles of pause. The complex dilatational viscoelastic modulus (E) was determined as follows (Sun et al., 2025):(7)E=dydA/A=Ed+iEv

Where E is a complex number and consists of the elastic modulus (Ed) and the viscous modulus (Ev).

After 2 h equilibration, sinusoidal oscillatory deformations of the interface were applied using frequency sweeps. In frequency sweeps, the frequency range increased from 0.005 Hz to 0.1 Hz with an amplitude of 10% (within the linear viscoelastic region). Five cycles were performed for each frequency.

Quantitative proteomics (UPLC-ESI-MS/MS)

To elucidate the selective partitioning of protein subfractions, interfacial proteins were isolated from the cream phase via Tween 20 desorption and subsequent surfactant removal using ultrafiltration centrifugal filter units (B. Chen et al., 2023; W. Chen et al., 2026). The purified proteins were reduced, alkylated, and then digested with trypsin at an enzyme-to-protein ratio of 1:50 (w/w). Proteomic profiling was executed on an UltiMate 3000 RSLCnano system coupled to a Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer equipped with a Nanospray Flex ion source (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) (Geisslitz et al., 2022). Data was acquired in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) top20 mode. Raw MS data were processed via MaxQuant (v2.2.0.0) utilizing the Andromeda search engine against the UniProt wheat sequence database. For robust comparative analysis, label-free quantitation (LFQ) intensities were extracted and log2-transformed. The relative abundance of specific subfractions was calculated by normalizing the aggregated LFQ intensities of corresponding protein groups against the total LFQ intensity of all identified interfacial proteins (W. Chen et al., 2026; Schirmer et al., 2023).

Characterizing the continuous-phase emulsion gel network

Decoupling and quantification of droplet flocculation

The particle size distribution of whipping cream and whipped cream was determined using a Malvern Mastersizer 2000 laser diffraction particle size analyzer (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Worcestershire, UK). To decouple the driving forces of flocculation, the samples were dispersed into three different continuous-phase systems at a ratio of 1:9 (v/v): (1) deionized water, (2) 1.0% (w/v) SDS solution (to disrupt non-covalent hydrophobic interactions), and (3) a mixed solution of 1.0% (w/v) SDS and 1.0% (v/v) β-mercaptoethanol (BME) (to simultaneously disrupt hydrophobic interactions and covalent disulfide bonds). The refractive indices of the continuous phase and shea butter were set as 1.330 and 1.451, respectively.

The flocculation degrees (FD) resulting from specific hydrophobic interactions (FDhydrophobic) and disulfide cross-linking (FDdisulfide) were decoupled and calculated according to Eq. (8) and Eq. (9), respectively (Cheng et al., 2021).(8)FDhydrophobic interactions%=D4,3water−D4,3SDSD4,3SDS×100%(9)FDdisulfide bonds%=D4,3SDS−D4,3SDS+BMED4,3SDS+BME×100%

Where D4,3 (water), D4,3 (SDS), and D4,3 (SDS + BME) are the volume-weighted diameter determined in deionized water, 1%SDS solutions, and 1% SDS + 1% BME, respectively.

Protein aggregation in the continuous phase

Non-covalent bonds

To evaluate the contribution of specific non-covalent interactions to continuous-phase aggregation, the serum-phase protein was diluted to 0.2 mg/mL using deionized water adjusted to the corresponding sample pH. The diluted samples were thoroughly mixed in equal volumes with different dissociating buffers targeting specific bonds: 16 mmol/L NaCl (to screen electrostatic interactions), 20 mmol/L urea (to disrupt hydrogen bonds), and 20 mmol/L SDS (to disrupt hydrophobic interactions) (Wang et al., 2019). The intrinsic fluorescence intensity of these mixtures was subsequently measured using a Spark microplate reader (Tecan Group Ltd., Switzerland) under the same instrumental conditions as described in Section 2.3.2.

Disulfide bond

To visualize the covalent disulfide cross-linking, the serum-phase proteins at equivalent concentrations were subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) (Laemmli, 1970). The stacking and resolving gels contained 4.5% and 12.5% acrylamide, respectively. Protein samples were analyzed under both non-reducing (without BME) and reducing (with BME) conditions to explicitly identify cross-linked aggregates. Following electrophoresis, the gels were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue Fast Staining Solution (Beijing LABLEAD Trading Co., Ltd., China) for 30 min and subsequently destained in ultrapure water.

To precisely quantify the covalent cross-linking, the total disulfide bond content in the serum phase was determined utilizing a commercial protein disulfide bond assay kit (BC5795, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in at least triplicate (n = 3), and the error bars in all figures represent the standard deviation (SD) of the replicates. The resulting data were then statistically analyzed, using SPSS software (Version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Differences between sample groups were evaluated using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test. Differences were considered statistically significant at the p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

pH-dependent physicochemical properties and colloidal stability of GNPs

The inherent physicochemical properties and structural integrity of GNPs are highly sensitive to the environmental pH. As the pH of the protein solution increased from acidic to alkaline, the measured zeta potential shifted from positive to negative values, indicating an isoelectric point (pI) near pH 7.0 (Fig. 1A), which is consistent with previous reports (Y.-Q. Hu et al., 2016; X. Zhang et al., 2020). Under acidic conditions (pH 3–6), the strong electrostatic repulsion yielded stable, well-dispersed GNPs, forming transparent to translucent colloidal dispersions. When the pH was adjusted to 7.0 or higher, the neutralization of surface charges severely weakened the repulsive barrier, which triggered pronounced microscale aggregation and macroscopic sedimentation (Fig. 1C) (Y.-Q. Hu et al., 2016). This aggregation behavior under alkaline conditions was further corroborated by particle size analysis, where the hydrodynamic diameter of GNPs peaked drastically at 2282.00 ± 111.41 nm at pH 7.0 (Fig. 1B).Fig. 1Zeta potential (A), particle size (B), and appearance (C) of GNPs with different pHs. Well-dispersed protein particles are essential for emulsion stability (Dickinson, 2019). However, initial emulsification trials revealed that systems prepared at pH 6.0 suffered from rapid gravitational phase separation (Fig. S1, Supporting Information). This macroscopic instability was driven by the weakened repulsive barrier near the pI, which was inadequate to prevent droplet coalescence at a relatively low protein concentration (0.5 wt%) (X. Zhang et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2018). Therefore, to ensure that structural evaluations were performed on initially stable templates, all subsequent experiments exploring multiscale structural evolution were strictly focused on the pH 3.0, 4.0, and 5.0 systems. While pH conditions closer to the pI (e.g., pH 6.0) would be of mechanistic interest, they were excluded from this study due to the rapid gravitational phase separation that precluded the formation of a stable initial emulsion.

pH-driven multiscale structural evolution of aerated emulsions

Macroscopic aeration capacity and multiscale structural properties

The whipping characteristics of whipped cream prepared at different pH values (3.0, 4.0, and 5.0) were first evaluated. As shown in Fig. 2A, whipping time did not differ significantly with increasing pH, whereas the overrun gradually increased. Previous research has indicated that the overrun of LMWEs-free whipped cream depends primarily on the foaming ability of proteins during the initial whipping process (W. Chen et al., 2026). The attenuated electrostatic repulsion near the pI facilitated rapid GNP migration to the dynamically expanding air-water interface, enhancing the foaming capacity (Peng et al., 2018). Remarkably, whipped cream prepared at pH 5 exhibited the highest overrun, significantly exceeding that of commercial cream. To interpret these enhanced macroscopic performance traits, the microstructural organization was visualized via CLSM (Fig. 2E). The improved textural and shape-retention characteristics were microstructurally underpinned by a denser, more aggregated “dual-network” architecture. Specifically, air bubbles were extensively encapsulated and stabilized by a contiguous, partially coalesced fat framework. Concurrently, the continuous phase (interstitial serum spaces) was strongly reinforced by a dense network of flocculated oil droplets and aggregated GNP. As the pH shifted from 3.0 to 5.0, the whipped cream transitioned from exhibiting isolated, well-dispersed droplets to forming a robust, continuous-phase gel network. This structural evolution was driven by the compromised electrostatic boundaries near the pI of the GNPs, which drastically promoted close droplet packing and inter-particle structural order (Y.-Q. Hu et al., 2016).

However, such macroscopic rigidification often masks complex and competitive physicochemical dynamics (W. Chen et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2021). To advance a fundamental understanding of these protein-stabilized whipped cream, the subsequent sections systematically decouple the multiscale events governing this structural assembly. We will thoroughly investigate these mechanisms across two primary structural dimensions: (1) the fat partial coalescence network, integrating interfacial compositional shifts and fat separation kinetics; and (2) the continuous-phase emulsion gel network, driven by droplet flocculation chemistry and protein aggregation pathways.

Degree of fat partial coalescence

To explicitly decouple irreversible partial coalescence from reversible droplet flocculation, samples were dispersed in an SDS and BME mixed solution. SDS and BME can disrupt non-covalent hydrophobic interactions and covalent disulfide bonds, respectively. Since the dissociating medium effectively dismantles protein-mediated aggregates, this dramatic size expansion is exclusively driven by shear-induced fat partial coalescence (Cheng et al., 2021; X. Liu et al., 2017). Under these conditions, pre-aeration emulsions exhibited consistent baseline droplet size (approximately 1.20 μm) across the pH range (Fig. 3A). This uniformity in droplet size distribution was further confirmed by CLSM (Fig. 2E). The degree of fat partial coalescence was further quantitatively evaluated using the dye dilution method. As shown in Fig. 3B, no significant differences were observed in the degree of partial coalescence among whipped cream prepared at different pH levels. This constant coalescence behavior presents a fascinating colloidal paradox. Classical emulsion theory dictates that a higher interfacial protein load should enhance steric hindrance and theoretically suppress droplet coalescence (Cheng et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2018). However, our quantitative analysis revealed that the absolute interfacial protein load increased continuously with rising pH (Fig. 4A). This striking contradiction, where a significantly higher protein mass fails to restrict fat partial coalescence, strongly implies that the shear resistance of these emulsions is not merely dictated by the absolute quantity of adsorbed proteins. Instead, the intrinsic rheological strength and microstructural fragility of the interfacial film must govern its susceptibility to fat crystal piercing. Interfacial properties and proteomic composition

Dynamic interfacial adsorption kinetics

Based on the fascinating coalescence paradox identified above, the fundamental interfacial behavior of GNPs warranted an in-depth dynamic investigation. The dynamic interfacial pressure (π) at the oil-water interface was monitored over time to track the sequential adsorption of proteins. A rapid increase in π within the initial stage indicated the fast diffusion of GNPs (Fig. S2, Supporting Information). The initial diffusion rate (Kdiff) was determined by fitting the π-t1/2 relationship according to a diffusion-controlled model (Fainerman et al., 1994). As demonstrated in Table 1, GNPs at higher pH exhibited faster adsorption to the interface. The diffusion of protein particles toward an interface requires overcoming an energy barrier (Li et al., 2022). The calculated parameters confirm that Kp progressively increases with rising pH, corroborating that near-isoelectric environments promote dense interfacial crowding. Interestingly, the Kr exhibited a starkly inverse trend. This suggests that while near-isoelectric conditions favor rapid particle accumulation, the resulting sterically jammed environment severely restricts the subsequent conformational unfolding and reorganization of the adsorbed proteins.

Interfacial dilatation rheology

To elucidate how this restricted reorganization impacts the mechanical toughness of the oil-water interface, dilatational rheology was performed. All GNP-stabilized interfaces exhibited a predominantly elastic character within the linear viscoelastic region (Fig. 4B). The relationship between the dilatational modulus (E) and surface pressure (π) offers a direct measure of intermolecular interaction strength (Fig. 4C). All systems produced slopes greater than 1, indicating strong intermolecular interactions at the interface (X. Ma, Kong, et al., 2024). Notably, the slope progressively decreased as the pH approached the pI, suggesting a fundamental weakening of the cohesive network at higher pH. This observation aligns perfectly with the Kr identified in the adsorption kinetics. It confirms that the rapid interfacial crowding of neutral particles at pH 5.0 prevents the formation of a highly interconnected and resilient film.

Frequency sweep analysis further corroborated this pH-dependent structural disparity. To quantify interfacial structuring, we extracted the power-law exponent (n) from the frequency dependency of E (Fig. 4D). Lower n values reflect a more highly structured network with superior connectivity (X. Ma et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2025). The noticeably larger n at pH 5.0 confirms that diminished surface charges severely inhibit molecular reorganization, producing a significantly more fragile protein film. These findings elegantly resolve the previously noted paradox. The poorly connected film at pH 5.0 lacks the structural integrity needed to resist fat crystal piercing, explaining why the increased protein mass fails to improve protection against fat partial coalescence.

Label-free quantitation (LFQ) signal intensities further delineated these structural alterations at the subfamily level (Fig. 5B). While the pH 5.0 interface preferentially enriched α/β-gliadins, the relative abundance of γ-gliadins and ω-gliadins experienced a marked decline. This selective depletion of γ-gliadin is likely structurally critical. The γ-gliadins possess a high propensity for forming extensive intermolecular disulfide cross-links (H. Ma, He, et al., 2024). Their severe reduction directly deprives the interface of crucial covalent anchoring points. This compositional shift provides compelling molecular evidence that correlates with our rheological observations. Based on established literature, we deduce that the lack of γ-gliadins-mediated cross-linking highly contributes to the formation of a poorly connected and mechanically fragile interfacial film at higher pH.

These quantitative multiscale findings resolve the apparent stabilization paradox. Approaching the pI thermodynamically drives a massive overall adsorption of GNPs, which undeniably increases the absolute interfacial protein mass. However, this dynamically crowded environment selectively displaces structurally indispensable components like γ-gliadins. The resulting decline in interfacial rheological strength effectively offsets the potential steric hindrance of the higher protein mass. Consequently, the mechanical resistance of the film against fat crystal piercing remains fundamentally constrained. This molecular compensation mechanism provides a plausible explanation for why the degree of partial fat coalescence remains constant across the pH gradient, despite the significant variations in total adsorbed protein.

Droplet flocculation

To systematically decouple the intermolecular forces driving droplet flocculation, the whipped creams were selectively dissociated prior to particle size analysis (Fig. 6A). Samples dispersed in ultrapure water preserved their native interactions and exhibited a bimodal size distribution (Fig. S3, Supporting Information). The addition of SDS, which disrupts non-covalent hydrophobic interactions, induced a marked change in the bimodal profile (Fig. S3, Supporting Information). Specifically, the peak corresponding to large droplet aggregates shifted leftward toward smaller diameters, accompanied by a substantial increase in the intensity (volume fraction) of the smaller-size subpopulation. Upon the further introduction of the disulfide-cleaving agent BME together with SDS, an even more pronounced redistribution of the particle population was observed: the large-aggregate peak continued to shift leftward, and the intensity of the small-droplet peak further increased. These sequential responses unequivocally demonstrate that droplet flocculation is cooperatively mediated by hydrophobic interactions and disulfide cross-linking. Notably, the flocculation degree (FD) driven by disulfide bonds vastly surpasses that of hydrophobic interactions. This confirms that covalent disulfide bridging dictates the primary framework for cross-linking between droplets (Li et al., 2022; X. Furthermore, a gradual but significant increase in the overall FD was observed as the pH increased from 3.0 to 5.0 (Fig. 6B). This pH-dependent trend is intrinsically linked to the spatial packing geometry of the colloidal droplets. As the pH approaches the pI, the suppression of electrostatic repulsion drastically lowers the energy barrier against droplet collision. This charge neutralization promotes a highly compact stacking arrangement, which is visually corroborated by the profound microstructural densification observed in the CLSM imaging at pH 5.0 (Fig. 2E). Such extreme close-proximity packing facilitates intense covalent and non-covalent interactions between adjacent droplets, establishing the primary structural scaffold of the continuous-phase emulsion gel network (X. Liu et al., 2017; J. Zhang et al., 2024).

However, macroscopic gelation in protein-stabilized multiphase systems is rarely a single-component process. To fully construct the robust continuous-phase emulsion gel network capable of compensating for interfacial fragility, the synergistic aggregation of unabsorbed proteins within the interstitial fluid must also be considered.

To explicitly identify the dominant non-covalent interactions driving continuous-phase aggregation, three selective chemical denaturants (NaCl, urea, and SDS) were individually introduced to disrupt electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interactions, respectively (Alrosan et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2019). All three dissociating agents induced an increase in fluorescence. The addition of SDS triggered the most pronounced enhancement (Fig. 7B–D). This differential response proves that hydrophobic interactions dominate protein aggregation in the serum phase (Alrosan et al., 2022). As the pH shifts toward the pI, the tertiary unfolding exposes previously shielded hydrophobic domains. This was independently corroborated by the progressive increase in the trip-phase contact angles, which confirmed the enhanced surface hydrophobicity of the GNPs (Fig. 7E).

Intermolecular disulfide bonds

To determine whether covalent disulfide bond formation contributed to the macroscopic gelation of the serum phase, the unadsorbed proteins were analyzed by SDS-PAGE under both non-reducing and reducing conditions. Under non-reducing conditions, large-scale supramolecular aggregates (>180 kDa) were prominently identified at the top of the resolving gels, indicating the formation of SDS-insoluble cross-linked networks (Fig. 7G) (Grasberger et al., 2024; Song, Guo, & Zhu, 2023). Crucially, these macromolecular assemblies completely dissociate into smaller monomeric subunits under reducing conditions. This electrophoretic mobility shift confirms that covalent disulfide bridging acts as a fundamental structural pillar (X. Liu et al., 2017).

Quantitative analysis further revealed a progressive increase in disulfide bond density with rising pH (Fig. 7F). The pH-induced tertiary unfolding exposes shielded free sulfhydryl groups. Simultaneously, the elevated alkalinity accelerates sulfhydryl–disulfide exchange reactions (Lagrain et al., 2011).

Mechanistic model of dual-network compensatory architecture

Based on the integrated multiscale findings, a comprehensive mechanistic model is proposed to elucidate the pH-driven structural evolution of the clean-label whipped creams (Fig. 8). The macroscopic performance of these GNP-stabilized aerated emulsions is fundamentally governed by two mechanistically distinct yet synergistically coupled networks: the fat partial coalescence network and the continuous-phase emulsion gel network.Fig. 8A proposed mechanism for pH-modulated multiscale network formation.Fig. 8

A proposed mechanism for pH-modulated multiscale network formation.

During the initial aeration process, the rapid adsorption kinetics of GNPs near their isoelectric point (pH 5.0) ensures excellent foaming capacity, yielding an ultra-high macroscopic overrun (Peng et al., 2018). At the microscopic oil-water interface, a fascinating compensatory cancellation effect occurs. While the near-pI conditions thermodynamically drive more proteins to the interface, the dynamically crowded environment selectively depletes cross-linking components (γ-gliadin), which is strongly correlated with the formation of a mechanically fragile interfacial film. These two opposing interfacial effects effectively counterbalance each other. Consequently, the degree of shear-induced fat partial coalescence remains remarkably constant across the pH gradient, providing a uniform baseline scaffold that mechanically supports the initial foam architecture.

With the fat network serving as a constant structural baseline, the profound macroscopic firmness observed at pH 5.0 is decisively driven by the continuous-phase gelation. As the pH approaches the pI, the reduction in surface charge diminishes electrostatic repulsion, allowing residual oil droplets and unadsorbed proteins to pack into an extreme close-proximity packing. Concurrently, the pH-induced tertiary unfolding of serum proteins exposes shielded hydrophobic domains and free sulfhydryl groups. This specific molecular structural shift triggers robust hydrophobic interactions and massive intermolecular disulfide cross-linking. Given this extremely low protein concentration, the serum proteins alone are insufficient to independently form a macroscopic gel (Fig. S4, Supporting Information). Instead, they act synergistically with the dense, uncoalesced oil droplets. These droplets serve as “active fillers” that are physically trapped and chemically cross-linked by the denatured protein aggregates, ultimately constructing an exceptionally robust particulate emulsion gel. Remarkably, this massive extent of aggregation enables the pH 5.0 system to exhibit a significantly stronger macroscopic gel network, despite possessing the lowest content of serum proteins among all evaluated samples (Fig. S4, Supporting Information).

Ultimately, this dual-network compensatory mechanism provides a robust framework to understand the stabilization paradox inherent to protein particle-stabilized multiphase systems. The partial coalescence of fat crystals provides the indispensable structural foundation, while the extensive gelation in the continuous phase acts as the decisive, tunable locus of mechanical reinforcement. By strategically modulating this pH-driven molecular pathway, this study establishes a robust physicochemical framework for the rational design and industrial scaling of high-performance, clean-label plant-based aerated foods.

Conclusions

This study establishes a systematic multiscale framework for rationally designing clean-label, protein-stabilized aerated emulsions. By decoupling the interfacial and continuous-phase dynamics of GNP-stabilized whipping creams, we provided new insights into the stabilization paradox inherent to these complex multiphase systems through a novel dual-network compensatory architecture.

Our quantitative findings highlight that the macroscopic texture is dictated by specific intermolecular interactions and spatial distribution. At the micro-interface, despite the massive protein adsorption near the pI, the selective depletion of cross-linking γ-gliadins is strongly associated with the formation of a functionally fragile film. This structural fragility elegantly offsets the increased steric hindrance, maintaining a consistent, baseline network of fat partial coalescence across the pH gradient.

In addition, the superior macroscopic firmness achieved at pH 5.0 is decisively driven by active continuous-phase gelation. This gelation operates through a synergistic particulate gel mechanism, in which densely packed uncoalesced oil droplets serve as “active fillers” and are cross-linked by pH-denatured serum proteins via hydrophobic interactions and disulfide bonding.

This dual-network perspective, where a constant fat coalescence network provides the structural foundation while tunable continuous-phase gelation dictates mechanical strength, shifts the paradigm of evaluating soft particle-stabilized emulsion foams. These chemical insights offer a highly scalable, theoretically grounded pathway for the industrial optimization of high-performance, sustainable plant-based structured foods.
