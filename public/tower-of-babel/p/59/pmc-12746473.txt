Long-term exposure to PM2.5 components and cardiovascular admissions in medicare patients
Authors: Mahdieh Danesh Yazdi, Yaguang Wei, Allison Sonntag, Heresh Amini, Shuxin Dong, Qian Di, Weeberb J. Requia, Francesca Dominici, Joel D. Schwartz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) has been previously linked to cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). PM2.5 is a mixture of components, each of which has its own toxicity profile which are not yet well understood. This study explores the relationship between long-term exposure to PM2.5 components and hospital admissions with CVDs in the Medicare population.Methods:We analyzed data from U.S. Medicare fee-for-service beneficiaries between 2000 and 2016. Annual levels of fifteen PM2.5 components were derived from hyperlocal spatiotemporal models. Our outcomes included hospital admissions with CVDs, and more specifically with myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, atrial fibrillation, and heart failure. A weighted quantile sum (WQS) approach was used, and stratification analyses examined effect modification by demographic and socioeconomic characteristics. We also restricted the analysis to zip code-years with PM2.5 levels below 8 μg/m3 to assess effects below current US standards.Results:The analyses showed that higher levels of PM2.5 were associated with increased hospital admissions for CVDs, particularly heart failure. Each decile increase in PM2.5 levels increased the rate of CVD admissions by 4.1 % (95 % CI: 4.1 %–4.2 %). Key contributors to these effects included nickel and sulfate. Women and black individuals experienced higher rates of PM-associated CVD admissions, while white and non-Medicaid-eligible individuals had higher rates of stroke, atrial fibrillation, and heart failure admissions.Conclusion:Exposure to PM2.5 components, particularly nickel and sulfate, increased the risk of CVD hospitalizations in the Medicare population, highlighting the potential to reduce CVD risk by controlling sources like fuel combustion, traffic pollution, industrial pollution, and coal use.

Introduction

Air pollution, particularly fine particulate matter (PM2.5), has been extensively linked to an increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD), cardiovascular disease mortality, CVD disability and all-cause mortality (Brook et al., 2010; Krittanawong et al., 2023b; Lederer et al., 2021; Luo et al., 2022; Murray et al., 2020; Wolf et al., 2021), making it a critical public health concern. This is true both of overall CVDs as well as specific cardiovascular conditions.

Among the participants of the National Institutes of Health NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study, higher levels of PM2.5 exposure were associated with higher mortality rates from ischemic heart disease and stroke–even at levels below the current United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) regulatory standards (Hayes et al., 2020). Previous research from the Medicare cohort have also consistently shown adverse cardiovascular effects (Danesh Yazdi et al., 2019, 2022; Klompmaker et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023b, 2024). One study conducted in seven southeastern US states found a 2.6 % higher hazard of first myocardial infarction (MI) hospital admission among Medicare participants due to long-term PM2.5 exposure (Danesh Yazdi et al., 2019), while another study spanning 2002 to 2016 across 10 US states observed a 1.35 % increase in the hospitalization rate for every 1-μg/m3 rise in PM2.5. (Wang et al., 2023b). Overall, PM2.5 exposure has been associated with MI, heart failure (HF), several types of cardiac arrhythmias, acceleration of atherosclerosis and other cardiovascular events (Krittanawong et al., 2023a). These results suggest that even small increases in PM2.5 levels have significant public health implications across populations. In fact, the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) has determined in its latest scientific assessment that there is sufficient evidence to conclude that the relationship between both short-term and long-term exposure to PM2.5 and cardiovascular disease is causal (US EPA, 2022).

While the relationship between PM2.5 exposure and CVD is well-established, the effect of specific variations in PM2.5 chemical composition and the mechanisms of impact remain unclear. Effects at concentrations below the new EPA standards also deserve attention to assess compliance with the Clean Air Act. PM2.5 is a complex mixture of fine solid particles and liquid droplets. Some studies suggest that certain variations may exhibit higher toxicity and thus have a greater impact on health outcomes (Bell et al., 2007; Rice et al., 2023; Sparks and Wagner, 2021; Yu et al., 2024). For instance, in an analysis from 106 U.S. counties (1999–2005) to assess how short-term exposures to PM2.5 components affect hospital admissions, researchers found that for each interquartile range increase in the levels of PM2.5 mass fraction attributable to nickel, the effect of PM2.5 on CVD hospital admissions increased by 14.9 % (95 % CI: 3.4 %–26.4 %) (Bell et al., 2009). In Greater Dhaka, Bangladesh, a time-series study found that short-term exposure to fossil fuel-related PM2.5 had a significantly greater impact on CVD hospitalizations and mortality as compared to biomass burning-related PM2.5 (Rahman et al., 2021). A study in China looking at 184 cities between 2014 and 2017, found an increased risk of hospital admissions with CVDs and CVD sub-types in association with short-term PM2.5 exposure. Researchers found the strongest effect on CVDs to be in association with black carbon in this population (Tian et al., 2024). Despite these findings, there is still limited knowledge about the effects of specific PM2.5 components on CVD, particularly over longer periods of exposure. Given that PM2.5 originates from various pollution sources, identifying which components contribute most to health impacts is essential for informing effective policy measures aimed at regulating the sources.

In this study, we looked at the relationship between long-term exposure to PM2.5 components and hospital admissions with cardiovascular diseases in the Medicare population who used fee-for-service insurance between 2000 and 2016 using methods for the analysis of chemical mixtures and also restricted to the population exposed to levels below the EPA’s current standard.

Methods

Study population

Our study population consisted of a cohort of Medicare fee-for-service beneficiaries 65 years or older, living in the contiguous United States, who were enrolled in Medicare on or after January 1st, 2000, through the end of 2016. Individuals were added to the cohort on January 1st of the year following enrollment. Patients were followed until they developed the outcome of interest, died, were censored, or the end of the study period. Counts of the CVD outcomes were then calculated for each zip code-year. To ensure robust statistical analysis, only zip code-years with at least a population of 100 fee-for-service beneficiaries were included. Individual ZIP codes were updated annually.

Exposure

Our exposures of interest were long-term exposure to fifteen PM2.5 components. The fifteen components included bromine (Br), calcium (Ca), copper (Cu), elemental carbon (EC), iron (Fe), potassium (K), ammonium NH4+, nickel (Ni), nitrate NO3−, organic carbon (OC), lead (Pb), silicon (Si), sulfate SO42−, vanadium (V), zinc (Zn). Of these, most of the mass of PM2.5 can typically be attributed to major components such as EC, OC, NH4+, NO3−, and SO42−. We assigned annual calendar average values for each component using hyperlocal spatio-temporal models that estimated levels of PM2.5 components at a 50-m spatial scale in urban areas and a 1-km scale in non-urban areas. Approximately 80 % of the U.S. population lives in urban areas, so most of the population was captured on this very fine scale(United States Census Bureau, 2022). These spatio-temporal models were developed using machine learning methods, measured values from monitors including the Environmental Protection Agency’s (EPA) speciation network and the National Park Service’s IMPROVE network, several research networks, and over a hundred predictor variables. Predictors included time, location, satellite data, climate variables, emission sources, etc. For non-urban areas, five machine learners were used to generate initial predictions: stochastic Gradient Boosting (GBM), Cubist regression (Cubist), Random Forest (RF), K-nearest Neighbors (KNN), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGB). The predictions from these five learners were then ensembled using seven approaches: Support Vector Machines (SVM), Cubist, GBM, KNN, RF, XGB, and a generalized additive model (GAM). For the urban areas, due to the computational burden, only three machine learners were run in the first stage of model development: GBM, RF, and XGB. Predictions from these models were ensembled using a GAM and the following super learners: SVM, GBM, RF, and XGB. The final model was chosen based on the lowest root mean square error (RMSE) value in the test sets. Results were validated against measured values from approximately 1000 monitors. The R2 values for the annual estimates ranged from 0.91 to 0.98 for major components and 0.79–0.88 for trace elements with the highest predictive values seen for SO42− (Amini et al., 2022a, 2022b, 2023a, 2023b; Danesh Yazdi et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2022a; Wang et al., 2023a).

As a secondary analysis, we included two gaseous components of air pollution in the PM2.5 mixture, namely nitrogen dioxide (NO2) and ozone (O3) to better represent air pollution as an exposure. Similar to the PM2.5 components model, we used high-resolution spatio-temporal models to assign NO2 and O3 levels. These models used hundreds of predictors derived from satellite measurements, chemical transport models, meteorological variables, and land-use terms to predict exposure levels on a 1-km spatial scale and a daily average temporal scale across the contiguous US. The predictors were used as input variables in three machine learners: a GBM, a neural network (NN), and a RF. Predictions generated by these learners were then ensembled in a geographically weighted GAM to generate the final estimated levels. The accuracy of the predictions was validated against measured levels from thousands of monitors across the contiguous US from the EPA’s Air Quality System (AQS) and other local monitoring data using ten-fold cross-validation. The results showed strong validation metrics with R2 of 0.84 and 0.86 for annual levels of NO2 and O3, respectively (Di et al., 2019; Requia et al., 2020).

Residential data was available from the Medicare denominator file for each individual at the zip code-level. As such, we aggregated grid cell values to the zip code level. The estimates for O3 reflect warm-season averages (from April through the end of September of a calendar year). Our main exposure of interest was the annual calendar year pollutant level during the year in which the hospital admissions occurred.

Outcomes

Our primary outcome of interest was first hospital admissions with cardiovascular diseases. This data was obtained from the Medicare Provider Analysis and Review (MEDPAR) database which includes all hospital admissions for Medicare fee-for-service members. We used hospital discharge data and codes from the International Classification of Diseases, 9th and 10th revisions (ICD-9, ICD-10) to define our outcomes. A first case of CVD was defined as having an admission with primary discharge codes: ICD-9 codes 390–459 and ICD-10 codes beginning with “I”. We further looked at subsets of CVDs, namely, myocardial infarction (MIs), ischemic stroke, atrial fibrillation and flutter (AF), and heart failure (HF). MI was defined as ICD-9 codes 410. X0, 410.X1 and ICD-10 code I21. Ischemic stroke was defined as ICD-9 codes 433.X1, 434.X1, 436 and ICD-10 code I163. Atrial fibrillation and flutter were defined as ICD-9 code 427.3 and ICD-10 code I48. Heart failure was defined as ICD-9 code 428 and ICD-10 code I50. For the purposes of analyses, our outcomes were assessed as counts of each outcome in each zip code-year.

Covariates

We had individual-level data on race, sex, age, and Medicaid-eligibility from the Medicare denominator file. These are updated on an annual basis. Since our analysis was at the zip code-year level, we calculated the proportion of Medicare fee-for-service beneficiaries who fell into each category. The categories included were proportion who identified as Black or as a race other than Black or White, proportion who were female, proportion between 65 and 74 years old, proportion between 75 and 84 years old, proportion who were Medicaid-eligible in each zip code-year.

For area-level measures, we used data from the U.S. Decennial Census and the American Community Survey (ACS) which we aggregated to zip code level. The variables we included were: proportion of the population above 65 years of age living below the poverty line, population density, median value of owner-occupied properties, median household income, proportion of housing units occupied by the owner, and proportion of the population above 65 years of age who had not graduated from high school. The lung cancer hospitalization rate in each zip code was used as a proxy for smoking and was derived from MED-PAR. We used the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) to derive zip code-level data on mean body mass index (BMI) and the smoking rate. BRFSS data was collected at the county level and then linked to relevant zip codes. We further obtained zip code-level data on several access-to-care variables among the Medicare population from the Dartmouth Atlas of Health Data: proportion of beneficiaries with at least one Hgb A1c test in a year, proportion of elderly diabetic beneficiaries who had a lipid panel test in a year, proportion of beneficiaries who had an eye examination in a year, proportion of beneficiaries with at least one ambulatory doctor’s visit in a year, proportion of female beneficiaries who had a mammogram over a two-year period. The data was collected at the hospital service area level and linked to the relevant zip code. Missing values for covariates were filled in using linear interpolation. We also included distance to the nearest hospital which was calculated from the centroid of the patient’s residential zip code. Hospital locations across the United States were derived from an ESRI dataset (ESRI Data and Maps; United States Geological Survey, 2010). We adjusted for calendar year cold-season and warm-season temperature by averaging daily values generated by gridMET(Abatzoglou, 2013). Observations with missing exposure or covariate information were assumed to be missing at random and were excluded from further analysis.

Statistical analysis

In order to study the relationship between PM2.5 components and CVD admissions, we used a weighted quantile sum (WQS) approach with random subsets (Carrico et al., 2015; Curtin et al., 2021). This method addresses the issue of multicollinearity in mixtures using dimension reduction and assesses the effect of the mixture components jointly and individually. A WQS approach quantizes the multiple exposures, in this case into deciles, and creates a weighted index that serves as the variable representing the mixture in the outcome regression. In order to estimate the weights, the data is divided into a training set and a validation set. The training data set is then divided into random subsets of the exposure variables. For the purposes of our analyses, we specified that each random subset should have seven (approximately half) of the fifteen mixture components. Weights are calculated in each random subset and then averaged to obtain final weights. Using this method averages weights from de-correlated or less-correlated subsets (Curtin et al., 2021). After the final weights have been determined, the outcome regression is run in the validation dataset to determine the effect estimate of the weighted index. The coefficient for this term represents the change in outcome due to a decile change in the overall mixture. Individual toxicities can then be compared using the weights from the first step which take into consideration the association of the components with the outcome, unlike other methods such as principal components analysis (PCA) which cannot account for the relationship between the individual components and the outcome of interest (Curtin et al., 2021).

Here i is a component of the mixture, n is the total number of components in the mixture, wi is the weight of the ith component, qi is the quantile of the ith component, and ns is a natural cubic spline term for year with three degrees of freedom, which accounts for long-term secular trends in air pollution. We included an offset term for the population in a zip code in a year to calculate the rate of admissions with each outcome. We specified that 40 % of the data should be used for training and 60 % for validation with 250 random subsets. We calculated robust standard errors to account for heteroskedasticity. This model assumes unidirectionality for all the components in the mixture. Given that there is no reason to believe that any component of PM2.5 is beneficial, this assumption should hold in the case of our study.

Additional analyses

We conducted additional analyses using stratification. We restricted our analyses to zip code-years with PM2.5 levels less than 8 μg/m3 to look at the relationship between exposure and CVDs at levels below current federal standards, as previous studies suggest harm even at these lower concentrations (Schwartz et al., 2023). We also looked at effect measure modification by demographic and socioeconomic characteristics such as sex, race, and Medicaid-eligibility.

We conducted several sensitivity analyses using alternative exposure definitions. First, we added two gaseous components of air pollution NO2 and O3 to the mixture to more broadly represent air pollution instead of just PM2.5. Moreover, we used one-year lag calendar year averages and two calendar-year moving averages (current and previous year) of air pollution exposure to look at effects over different time windows.

We used negative exposure control and negative outcome control to test for unmeasured confounding as has been described previously (Wang et al., 2024). We used the future year’s air pollution levels as the negative exposure control and the previous year’s outcome as the negative outcome control as we would not expect next year’s air pollution to be associated with this year’s CVD outcomes and we would not expect this year’s air pollution to be associated with the previous year’s CVD outcomes. For each of our regression results, we also calculated evidence-for-causality values (E-values). E-values represent the strength of the relationship which an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both the exposure and outcome to bias the results to the null (VanderWeele and Ding, 2017).

All data management and analyses were performed in R statistical software (R Core Team, 2022). The WQS model was run using the “gWQS” package (Renzetti et al., 2023). The computations in this paper were run on the FASRC Cannon/FASSE cluster supported by the FAS Division of Science Research Computing Group at Harvard University.

Results

The characteristics of the study population for the CVD dataset can be seen in Table 1. Slightly more than half of the population were female; most were white and about 13 % were eligible for Medicaid. The distribution of exposures can be seen in Table S1. These are consistent with the lower air pollution values observed in the United States. The correlation between the exposures can be seen in Fig. S1. The correlations ranged from weak to moderate to strong. The strongest correlations were observed between sulfate and ammonium which is reasonable as these two components tend to bond together as ammonium sulfate.

The main analysis results can be seen in Table 2. Higher levels of the total PM2.5 mixture were associated with an increase in the rate of hospital admissions with overall CVDs as well as specific conditions such as MI, Stroke, AF, and HF. For overall CVDs, each decile increase in the PM2.5 mixture was associated with a 4.1 % increase in hospital admissions rates (95 % CI: 4.1 %–4.2 %.). The highest effect estimates were seen with heart failure. Each decile increase in the PM2.5 mixture increased the rate of hospital admissions with HF by 6.6 % (95 % CI: 6.5 %- 6.8 %). In zip code-years with levels below 8 μg/m3, these rates of increase were even higher than in the full dataset. In both the main and low exposure analyses, nickel was the most influential component of the mixture (Fig. 1). Other influential components included sulfate and zinc. Nickel is marker of oil combustion and industry, as well as some traffic emissions (Thurston et al., 2011). Zinc is also a marker of industry emissions, particularly the metals industry, as well as non-tailpipe traffic particles (Thurston et al., 2011). Sulfate is generally considered to be a marker of the coal industry (Thurston et al., 2011).

We also conducted several sensitivity analyses which varied the definition of air pollution exposure. The results of these analyses can be seen in Fig. 2. We compared our main exposure definition of fifteen PM2.5 components to one that includes two additional gaseous components, namely NO2 and O3. We also varied the exposure time window to look at PM2.5 component levels from the prior calendar year and 2-year moving averages. For all of these definitions across outcomes, we found harmful effects of air pollution. The results were similar with higher effect sizes for the longer-term definitions. The weights for each component in these models can be found in Fig. S2. These are largely similar across exposure definitions. For CVD and MI, nickel, sulfate, and vanadium contributed most to the toxicity of the mixture. Vanadium, like nickel, is associated with oil combustion (Thurston et al., 2011). For stroke, nickel and zinc had the highest weights, while for AF, nickel and sulfate had the highest weights. For HF, nickel, sulfate, and zinc were generally the most important components of the PM2.5 mixture.

In the double negative control analysis, we found results that were very similar to our main analyses (Table S2). The E-values for the rate ratio suggest that an unmeasured confounder would need to have an association with both exposure and outcome with a rate ratio magnitude of at least 1.2–1.3 in order to bias the results to null (Table 2).

In the effect measure modification analyses, we found women had a higher rate of admissions for overall CVDs and MIs in both the full dataset and the low-exposure dataset. Those who identified as black had higher rates of overall CVD admissions due to PM2.5 than those who identified as white. However, those who identified as white and those who were not Medicaid-eligible had higher rates of admissions with stroke, AF, and HF than those who identified as black and those who were Medicaid-eligible, respectively, in both the full dataset and the low-exposure dataset (Fig. 3). In terms of weights, nickel and sulfate were significant components of the PM2.5 mixture for those who identified as white, while nickel, ammonium, and nitrate were more prominently seen for those who identified as black. Ammonium is generated mainly by chemical reactions between ammonia and nitrogen oxides and sulfur dioxide gases (Amini et al., 2023a; Thurston et al., 2011). Nitrate is a traffic-related air pollutant (Amini et al., 2023a; Thurston et al., 2011). Men and women, and those were eligible for Medicaid and those who were not, were similar in the components that contributed most to toxicity, namely nickel, sulfate, and EC (Table S3). EC is also a traffic-related PM2.5 component (Thurston et al., 2011). There were similar trends in the component weights seen at lower concentrations (Table S4).

Discussion

In this study, we used data from Medicare fee-for-service patients to look at the effect of fine particulate matter, as a mixture of components, on overall and specific CVDs. Our results showed an increase in the risk of all outcomes with increasing overall exposure. These results held in analyses of lower-exposure zip code-years (below US EPA standards) as well as in a number of sensitivity and stratified analyses. This suggests that current regulations may not be sufficient to protect the elderly population against the adverse cardiovascular effects of PM2.5. Notably, elevated PM2.5 levels were linked to higher hospital admissions for CVDs, with each decile increase associated with a 4.1 % (95 % CI: 4.1 %–4.2 %) rise in the rate of CVD admissions and a 6.6 % (95 % CI: 6.5 %–6.8 %) increase in the rate of heart failure admissions. The weights obtained in analyses revealed that nickel, sulfate, and vanadium were most influential for CVD and MI, and nickel and zinc for stroke and heart failure. The inclusion of NO2 and O3 in the mixture did not change the main contributors to the CVD toxicity of air pollution as compared to the main analysis. These elements suggest the importance of sources such as fuel combustion, traffic and industry emissions, and coal production. Policies focusing on mitigating the effects of exposure to air pollution can potentially focus on these sources to reduce the rates of CVD admissions. The double negative control analyses should detect any omitted confounders linearly related, which pose the greatest threats to the relationship between PM2.5 and cardiovascular disease (Schwartz et al., 2023) and found none. Our calculated E-values suggest that an unmeasured confounder would need a moderate relationship of 1.2–1.4 on the ratio scale with both PM2.5 and CVD to nullify our results, which alleviates some of the concern we have regarding potential unmeasured confounding in our study.

In our stratified analyses, women and black individuals had higher CVD admissions, while white and non-Medicaid-eligible individuals had higher rates of stroke, AF, and HF admissions. In terms of the weights of the components for CVD, nickel, ammonium, vanadium, and sulfate were most relevant for those who identified as black and nickel, sulfate, EC, and zinc were most important for those who identified as white. Even though women had higher CVD admissions in association with PM2.5 exposure, the most important components were similar between the two groups. For stroke, sulfate and nickel were the most important components for those who were Medicaid-eligible, nickel and copper were the most important components for those who are black, and nickel and zinc were the most important components for those who were not Medicaid-eligible and white. For AF and HF, nickel and sulfate were the top two most relevant components for all studied sub-groups with the exception of those who identified as black for whom ammonium and iron were most important in the association between PM2.5 and HF. Previous studies have also found some inconsistent results in effect measure modification analyses of air pollution CVDs by race and socioeconomic status (Jin et al., 2022b; Klompmaker et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2024). The similarity in the highest-weighted components and their sources between these demographic and socioeconomic groups suggests that the heterogeneity in the effects of PM2.5 is most likely attributable to other factors rather than varying exposure toxicity to pollution sources among these groups. These could include differences in genetic susceptibility to individual cardiovascular diagnoses. It could also reflect different health behaviors in seeking care. For example, if patients did not or could not adhere to preventive measures, they might be more likely to suffer severe events and die before being hospitalized. Or patients may reside in areas where access to routine medical care is limited and as such had a higher risk of not being diagnosed early enough or receiving appropriate early treatment and hence not surviving incident outcomes or exacerbations of existing conditions. Conversely, it is possible that some sub-groups did have access to early diagnosis and preventive care, and their diseases were treated in an outpatient setting and there was no need for hospitalization. It is moreover possible that these differences are due to residual or unmeasured confounding. Finally, a combination of these factors could be responsible for the results we saw in our stratified analyses.

Previous research on short and long-term exposure to specific PM2.5 components and CVD risks have yielded mixed findings with regards to which components are most important. Among Medicare beneficiaries in Connecticut and Massachusetts between 2000 and 2004, short-term exposure to PM2.5 components such as calcium, black carbon, vanadium, and zinc was linked to an increased risk of cardiovascular hospitalizations among older adults. Positive matrix factorization (PMF) analysis further linked road-dust generated PM2.5 as a risk factor for CVDs (Bell Michelle et al., 2014). A study in Atlanta, Georgia between 1998 and 2002 found short-term PM2.5 exposure attributable to gasoline, diesel, wood-burning, and metal processing to increase the risk of CVD-related emergency department visits. Among specific components, potassium, elemental carbon, organic carbon, and zinc had the highest associations with ED visits (Sarnat Jeremy et al., 2008). A Danish cohort study looking at long-term PM2.5 exposure and CVD-related deaths between 1993 and 2015, found higher CVD mortality risks associated with exposure to primary black and organic carbon as well secondary organic and inorganic aerosols. (Hvidtfeldt et al., 2019). In a Chinese cohort, long-term exposure to total PM2.5 as well as components such as black carbon, nitrate, ammonium, and sulfate were linked to higher risks of total CVD and hypertension, with the highest hazard ratio (HR per IQR increase: 1.721, 95 % CI: 1.517–1.951) observed for sulfate (Liu et al., 2021). In another study of long-term exposure to PM2.5 on atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk in Chinese adults, organic matter and sulfate were identified as the two most important components of the PM2.5 mixture (Li et al., 2023). A study among the participants of the UK Biobank found an increased risk of incident heart failure from exposure to long-term PM2.5 as well as several major components: elemental carbon, organic matter, ammonium, nitrate, and sulfate (Cui et al., 2024). A study in the US among participants in the American Cancer Study (ACS) in 100 metropolitan areas between 1982 and 2004 found that long-term PM2.5 exposure was associated with an increased rate of ischemic heart disease, with sulfur and coal-use representing higher effect estimates than comparable components and sources (Thurston George et al., 2016). In another study conducted among Medicare participants between 2000 and 2009 which examined the relationship between long-term exposure to PM2.5 components and cause-specific mortality, sulfate, organic carbon, nitrate and selenium were found to have the strongest relationship with CVD mortality. Among source-specific exposures, coal, traffic, and soil had the highest associations with CVD mortality (Kazemiparkouhi et al., 2022). Although the statistical methods used across these studies are different, we also identified sulfate as an important contributor to CVD toxicity. Other differences may be due to variations in the components measured and the methods of exposure assessment.

Several biological mechanisms have been suggested to explain the health impacts of PM2.5 and its specific components, including systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, epigenetic changes (Wang et al., 2022, 2023a), alterations in clotting, autonomic dysregulation, and endothelial cell injury (Wang et al., 2015). Mechanisms for the cardiovascular toxicity of PM2.5 components is not fully understood, and existing evidence comes largely from animal toxicology. In mouse models, nickel exposure has been associated with changes in heart rate variability (Lippmann et al., 2006) and increases in levels lipoperoxide and total lipids in cardiac tissue (Novelli et al., 2000). Sulfur dioxide inhalation has been associated with increased levels of inflammatory biomarkers and promoted brain injury (Sang et al., 2010). Animal studies looking at zinc air pollution have found evidence of mitochondrial DNA damage (Kodavanti Urmila et al., 2008).

Our study has several limitations. First, we used spatiotemporal models to assign exposure levels and there might still be some measurement error in exposure assignment. However, our spatiotemporal models had very fine resolution and strong validations metrics. Moreover, any measurement error should be non-differential, and previous studies suggest would bias the results towards the null (Wei et al., 2022). We did not have additional information on indoor exposures or occupational exposures. This combined with the fact that the analyses were conducted at a zip code-year level limit the inferences we can make about individual-level risk. Additionally, like any other observational study, there might be unmeasured or residual confounding. We attempted to mitigate this by including a number of variables that would measure socioeconomic status and access to health care which are the primary confounders of concern. There were numerous strengths to our study. One key strength of this study is the use of a large national cohort, providing statistical power for subgroup analyses. The population includes all fee-for-service Medicare beneficiaries, which increases the generalizability of the results. Furthermore, we used a WQS approach with random subset selection to account for the correlation between the components under study. Moreover, we conducted several sensitivity analyses and assessed potential unmeasured confounding using negative controls and outcomes as well as E-values to strengthen the validity of our inferences. Lastly, we conducted comprehensive effect measure modification analyses across different subgroups. Specifically, we examined potential modifications by demographic and socioeconomic factors such as sex, race, and Medicaid eligibility.

Conclusion

Exposure to PM2.5 components increased the rate of hospital admissions with cardiovascular disease in the Medicare population, even at levels below current federal standards. Nickel and sulfate were particularly identified as components that contributed to the toxic effects of PM2.5 on CVDs. This suggests that controlling sources such as oil combustion, the metals industry, traffic sources and coal production could mitigate the risk of air pollution as a contributing factor to CVDs. The results of this study imply that stricter air quality standards may be necessary to protect vulnerable populations.
