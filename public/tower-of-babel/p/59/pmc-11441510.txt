Activation induces shift in nutrient utilization that differentially impacts cell functions in human neutrophils
Authors: Emily C. Britt, Xin Qing, James A. Votava, Jorgo Lika, Andrew S. Wagner, Simone Shen, Nicholas L. Arp, Hamidullah Khan, Stefan M. Schieke, Christopher D. Fletcher, Anna Huttenlocher, Jing Fan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceAs frontline cells of the innate immune system, neutrophils dynamically reprogram their metabolism over the course of their immune response. However, the contribution of nutrients fueling neutrophil metabolism has not been fully understood, particularly quantitatively. Here we demonstrate that primary human neutrophils (but not neutrophil-like cell line) shift from significantly relying on intracellular glycogen at resting state to primarily using extracellular glucose upon activation. We also show how specific neutrophil functions are impacted differently by the nutrient utilization shift. These findings are important because they reveal the metabolic flexibility across glycogen and glucose utilization that neutrophils may rely on to withstand nutrient fluctuation as well as opportunities to modulate neutrophil functions by targeting specific nutrient metabolism.

Neutrophils, the most abundant circulating leukocytes, provide the first line of defense in innate immunity. In this role, neutrophils are capable of immune functions such as oxidative burst, phagocytosis, and neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) release (1, 2), which are associated with various significant metabolic requirement for energy, redox cofactors, and other biological molecules. It has been long accepted that glycolysis is the main pathway to support the energy demands in mature neutrophils, while neutrophil mitochondria have limited contribution (3, 4). The pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) has been found critical for providing reducing power to enable certain neutrophil functions, particularly oxidative burst (5–8).

These crucial metabolic pathways can be supplied by different nutrient sources, including exogenous glucose and intracellular glycogen storage, both of which feed into the production of glucose-6-phosphate (G6P), the common precursor for glycolysis and PPP activity. Prior research has suggested the importance of both glucose metabolism and glycogenolysis in immunity. For instance, increased glucose uptake is essential for CD4 T cell activation and function (9), while glycogenolysis is essential for fueling CD8 memory T cell recall, the early response of dendritic cell activation, and proinflammatory response in macrophages (10–12). Particularly in neutrophils, extracellular glucose has been traditionally considered as the main nutrient source, and active glucose utilization plays an important role in the response to classical activators or pathogens (13–20). On the other hand, recent studies have revealed neutrophils also possess meaningful glycogen storage, and glycogenolysis is increased by certain stimulations or diseases (4, 21). Moreover, despite neutrophils’ relatively few mitochondria and low mitochondrial energy generation, it has been reported that mitochondrial substrates can support some effector functions such as prolonged oxidative burst (22, 23), and neutrophils express key gluconeogenic enzymes including mitochondrial phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (4), indicating that neutrophils have the capacity to generate glycolytic intermediates or glycogen storage from mitochondrial gluconeogenic substrates.

These recent studies clearly suggest that neutrophils have the capacity to utilize a range of nutrients to support their metabolism, survival, and function, and that neutrophil activation is associated with significant reprogramming of nutrient metabolism. However, two fundamental questions remain unclear. First, what are the quantitative contributions of these possible nutrients under specific conditions? Understanding the relative contribution would bring a complete picture of neutrophils’ nutrient preference in different states. Second, what is the nutrient dependence of various neutrophil effector functions? As the first innate immune cells to be recruited to sites of infection and injury, neutrophils can encounter changing microenvironments with varied nutrient levels. Some metabolic sources, though preferred under optimal conditions, may be compensated for by other alternatives when unavailable, while others may be indispensable for certain effector functions. Determining a) the extent to which neutrophils have the flexibility to use different metabolic sources to support the initiation and sustentation of their critical functions and b) whether altering neutrophils’ nutrient utilization can steer their functions has important implications in health and disease.

Here, we quantified the contribution of major metabolic sources in neutrophils in the resting state and upon activation by a variety of stimuli. In light of growing evidence suggesting that environment can significantly alter metabolism (24), we evaluated the nutrient preference of human neutrophils in three settings: in vivo by isotopic tracing in healthy volunteers to study neutrophils in their physiological environment, ex vivo by immediately assessing freshly insolated human peripheral blood neutrophils in culture condition to study neutrophil metabolism in an experimentally controllable setting, and in vitro in a widely used stable neutrophil-like cell line cultured in media. This study reveals fundamental features of human neutrophil metabolism and elucidates the specific metabolic rewiring induced by different stimulations and conditions. We further quantitatively assessed the dependence of an array of important functions on the two major nutrient sources, extracellular glucose and intracellular glycogen (other sources including glycogenotic substrates were found to have much lower contribution). This examination revealed the differential effects of metabolic manipulations, which prime or reverse the stimulation-induced nutrient preference shift, on different neutrophil functions. These findings have significant health relevance given that dysregulation of neutrophil functions contributes to many infectious diseases and inflammatory conditions and understanding the specific metabolism–function connections carries broad potential therapeutic applications.

Results

Primary Human Neutrophils Use Glycogen Storage as a Major Direct Metabolic Source in the Resting State.

To quantitatively characterize human neutrophils’ nutrient preference under baseline condition, we started with evaluating the contribution of extracellular glucose, which has been traditionally recognized as the main source of neutrophil metabolism. Recent studies have highlighted that cellular metabolism can be significantly impacted not only by cell intrinsic factors but also by nutrient availability and extracellular signals in their environment (24, 25). Consequently, knowledge gained using in vitro or animal models may not always translate into human contexts. Thus, we applied a cutting-edge in vivo isotopic tracing approach to probe human neutrophil metabolism in their physiological environment. Healthy volunteers were intravenously infused with uniformly labeled glucose tracer [U-13C glucose in sterile saline, infused with an initial bolus of 8 g over 10 min, followed by a steady infusion rate of 8 g per hour, following previously developed methods (26–28)], and blood samples were collected at various time points for analysis of metabolites in plasma and in peripheral blood neutrophils (Fig. 1A). In plasma, glucose labeling quickly enriched to ~40% labeled within 10 min and stayed mostly steady up to 60 min (Fig. 1B), while total glucose level maintained a normal range (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). During this time, most labeled glucose remained uniformly labeled, with a small fraction (<5%) of other label forms (M + 1 to M + 5) slowly rising over time (Fig. 1B), indicating metabolic turnover of glucose by tissues via gluconeogenesis. Plasma lactate, which has been recently identified as a major glucose-derived circulating metabolite (29, 30), gradually increased to ~15% 3-labeled (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Other circulating metabolites, including glycerol, which may directly contribute to lower glycolysis, as well as glutamine, glutamate, and alanine, remained minimally labeled in plasma (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 C–F). These are the major circulating metabolites available to neutrophils that can potentially deliver labeling into cells.

In neutrophils, intracellular glycolytic intermediates quickly gained labeling after infusion. The dominant labeling forms, including 3- and 6-labeled fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (FBP) and 3-labeled dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP), reflect direct contribution of circulating glucose via glycolysis. The exact ratio between 3- and 6-labeled FBP, which is more variable across subjects (Fig. 1C), mainly reflects the different degrees of aldolase reversibility, which can join 3-labeled and unlabeled three carbon units to generate 3-labeled FBP. A smaller fraction of other label forms, including 1- or 2-labeled, may have resulted from either scrambling through nonoxidative PPP, which we found is highly reversible in resting neutrophils (5), or to a lesser extent, from gross flux from phosphoenolpyruvate derived from phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase. There was a small fraction of labeling in intracellular TCA cycle intermediates during this time frame (SI Appendix, Fig. S1G). The average labeling enrichments of glycolytic intermediates (on per carbon basis) stayed mostly steady from 30 min to 60 min, and were significantly lower than the average labeling enrichment of plasma glucose (SI Appendix, Fig. S1H), suggesting other nutrients beyond circulating glucose contribute significantly in neutrophils. The average labeling enrichment of intracellular FBP and DHAP normalized to the labeling enrichment of plasma glucose indicates the maximal direct contribution circulating glucose may have to glycolysis (31) (unlike the exact labeling pattern in FBP, the average labeling enrichment on per carbon basis is not sensitive to the reversibility within glycolysis and PPP, but only reflects where the carbon arises from). This ratio stayed around 50% (Fig. 1D). It is a common concern for in vivo tracing that cellular metabolism may be perturbed during sample processing. In addition to applying a neutrophil isolation method that is as fast as possible and with as minimal perturbation as possible to mitigate the effect, we performed a mock isolation experiment to evaluate the potential perturbation. The result showed that during the neutrophil isolation process, labeling in glycolytic intermediates is not lost, but may be slightly increased (SI Appendix, Fig. S1I), indicating the labeling measured in the in vivo tracing experiment reflects an upper bound estimation. Taken together, these results suggested that the direct contribution of plasma glucose was around or slightly less than half.

While the in vivo tracing experiments suggested significant contribution of other nutrients beyond circulating glucose to human neutrophil metabolism, more in-depth investigation in vivo is limited by sample size and limited experimental perturbation feasibility. Therefore, we turned to further assess the nutrient utilization in primary human neutrophils in an ex vivo setting, where freshly isolated peripheral blood neutrophils were assayed in standard culture media (Fig. 1A). Extracellular glucose labeled slightly less than 50% of the glycolytic intermediates across donors (Fig. 1E), with the dominant label forms reflecting the direct contribution via glycolysis (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A), and glucose minimally labeled TCA cycle intermediates (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). These results show the utilization of glucose is similar ex vivo compared to in vivo condition, contributing around 50% or less of glycolysis, with other major metabolic sources directly supplying the metabolism of primary human neutrophils under resting condition.

To examine the other sources, we next tested the contribution of intracellular glycogen, which recent studies have suggested is important for supplying glycolysis across multiple tissues and for neutrophil survival and functions (4, 17, 31). To inhibit neutrophils’ glycogen utilization, because genetic perturbation in primary human neutrophils is unavailable, we treated the cells with two types of glycogen phosphorylase inhibitors (GPi and CP91149). Treatment of both inhibitors consistently increased the labeling of glycolytic intermediates from extracellular glucose in a dose-dependent manner, till nearly complete (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 C and D). Thus, for most of the experiments, we focused on the use of the more potent inhibitor, GPi, at 20 μM, which is the lowest possible dose to consistently achieve a sufficient inhibition across donors. At this dosage, the metabolic effect of GPi is fairly specific, as indicated by G6P being the only significantly decreased compound in metabolomic analysis (SI Appendix, Fig. S2E), and cell viability remaining very high (SI Appendix, Fig. S2F). The contribution of glycogen to generating glycolysis intermediates was quantified by the increase in labeling enrichment when glycogenolysis is blocked (+GPi) compared to baseline (−GPi), and we found the contribution of glycogen was ~50% (Fig. 1F). While glucose and glycogen combined account for most of the direct nutrient sources, other sources may make a minor contribution (<10%). For instance, we observed significantly less labeled glycerol-3-phosphate (G3P) relative to DHAP both with and without GPi (SI Appendix, Fig. S2G). The additional unlabeled G3P can be generated from unlabeled sources such as lipid degradation, which can further incorporate into lower glycolysis.

Unlike primary human neutrophils assessed ex vivo, which showed a ~45:50 mixed nutrient preference toward glucose and glycogen consistent with in vivo results, we found human neutrophil-like cell line HL-60 cultured in vitro primarily depended on extracellular glucose as the direct source for glycolytic intermediates. Extracellular glucose labeled nearly all the glycolytic intermediates and treatment with GPi had minimal effect (Fig. 1G). This observation is consistent with a recent report in T cells (32) that found a freshly isolated ex vivo model, but not in vitro cell models, faithfully reflects in vivo metabolic phenotype, likely due to significant metabolic changes accumulating during prolonged in vitro culturing.

The ability of primary human neutrophils to utilize glycogen storage as a major metabolic source could provide them with higher flexibility in glucose-limited conditions. To directly test whether glycogen can be a meaningful source to maintain the glycolytic rate in neutrophils in the resting state, we measured lactate production of human peripheral blood neutrophils cultured in completely glucose-deplete condition. In glucose-deplete condition, lactate production continued (SI Appendix, Fig. S2H), and remained no less than in glucose-replete condition for at least 5 h (Fig. 1H). After that, lactate production starts to slow down in glucose-deplete condition, likely due to the depletion of glycogen store (Fig. 1H). Consistently, we found that glycogen storage is consumed by ~60% when extracellular glucose is completely absent for 4 h, and by 95% after 12 h (SI Appendix, Fig. S2I). This result shows that glycogen store is at least sufficient to fully maintain glycolysis for a few hours, providing a meaningful metabolic source to neutrophils especially in relevance to their short lifespan (33, 34).

Stimulations Induce Nutrient Preference Shift Toward Primarily Using Extracellular Glucose.

As results above revealed that intracellular glycogen, in addition to extracellular glucose, is a major metabolic source for primary human neutrophils at resting state, we next investigated whether such nutrient preference changes when neutrophils are activated by a variety of stimulation signals. Primary human peripheral blood neutrophils were stimulated ex vivo with phorbol myristate acetate (PMA), zymosan A (Zym), TNFα, or heat-inactivated Pseudomonas aeruginosa (HI PsA; heat inactivation prevents the interference of bacterial metabolism) for 30 min, or LPS for 4 h, and we traced the incorporation of extracellular U-13C-glucose into glycolytic intermediates as an indication of glucose utilization. We observed a sharp increase in the labeling of all glycolytic intermediates from less than 50% to 80–90% upon activation with each of these stimuli (Fig. 2A). Similarly, intermediates of the PPP also showed significantly increased labeling from extracellular glucose (Fig. 2B). These data indicate a substantial nutrient preference shift, with extracellular glucose becoming the dominant direct source for glycolysis and the PPP upon activation.

Mirroring this shift was a substantially reduced contribution of glycogen to glycolysis, as measured by the gap of labeling when cells were treated with GPi compared to no treatment (Fig. 2C), from ~50% to less than 20%. The contribution by other sources beyond glucose and glycogen remained less than 10% in activated neutrophils, as indicated by the fraction of glycolytic intermediates remaining unlabeled from glucose after glycogen degradation was inhibited (Fig. 2D).

While all the investigated stimuli induced a similar nutrient preference shift, the shift occurred with different kinetics. Zymosan induced a very rapid shift, with glucose becoming the dominant source within 15 min after stimulation (Fig. 2E). In contrast, LPS induced such a shift much more slowly, over 2 h (Fig. 2F).

The Shift to Glucose Utilization is Driven by a Substantial Increase in Glucose Uptake.

The stimulation-induced nutrient preference shift could be driven by increased glucose utilization or decreased glycogen utilization (Fig. 3A). Therefore, to understand the mechanism for the shift, we first measured glucose consumption rate. A four- to sixfold increase in total glucose consumption was observed within 1 h after stimulation with PMA, zymosan, and TNFα (Fig. 3B). We also found the level of glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) [one of the main glucose transporters in neutrophils (19, 35)] on the cell surface increased by ~5-fold as soon as 30 min after stimulation (Fig. 3C), which would enable the rapid increase in glucose uptake. It has been reported that GLUT1 can be phosphorylated by PKC, and the phosphorylation enhances its localization to the cell surface (36). Moreover, both zymosan and TNF can activate PKC (37, 38), and PMA is a direct activator of PKC, pointing to a likely mechanism for the rapid increase in GLUT1 level on plasma membrane. Indeed, we found that while the total cellular level of GLUT1 remained mostly constant during this time, GLUT1 phosphorylation was increased in these conditions (Fig. 3D).

Second, as glycogen degradation is regulated largely by the phosphorylation of glycogen phosphorylase (PYGL) (Fig. 3A), we probed the level and phosphorylation of PYGL (Phosphorylated PYGL is the active form). In cells stimulated with PMA, zymosan, and TNFα, there was no significant change in total PYGL level, though PYGL phosphorylation (Ser15) was significantly increased (Fig. 3E). This suggests that notwithstanding the relative preference shift toward less glycogen contribution, the gross flux of glycogenolysis is likely still increased upon stimulation—a more profound increase of glucose uptake can dominate the relative contribution of glycogenolysis.

Glycogen Cycling Is Increased Upon Stimulation.

Intracellular glycogen is often actively turned over by both degradation and synthesis, a process known as glycogen cycling. Although PYGL is increasingly phosphorylated upon neutrophil activation, indicating increased gross glycogen degradation activity, the total cellular glycogen store is slightly higher in zymosan or LPS stimulated condition compared to unstimulated condition (Fig. 3F), suggesting gross glycogen synthesis flux is also increased upon stimulation. To probe glycogen synthesis, we measured the labeling of intracellular glycogen after cells were cultured in media containing U-13C-glucose. Upon stimulation with zymosan, labeling in UDP-glucose, the precursor of glycogen synthesis, increased rapidly and reached plateau within 15 to 30 min. (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A), following the rapid increase in the labeling of its precursor, G6P. The labeling in the glucose moiety of UDP-glucose was then gradually incorporated into glycogen over hours. Such glycogen labeling rate, which reflects gross glycogen synthesis, was much faster in zymosan stimulated condition (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). By 2 h, the glycogen was only <2% labeled in resting state but ~10% labeled in zymosan- or LPS-stimulated state (Fig. 3G). The extent of increase in glycogen labeling exceeded the increase in UDP-glucose labeling (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A), suggesting it cannot be solely explained by increased precursor labeling. These results are consistent with zymosan stimulation increasing glycogen cycling, and the increase in gross glycogen synthesis outweighs the increase in gross glycogen degradation activity, resulting in higher glycogen storage level in stimulated condition, which may help prepare cells for functioning in low glucose microenvironments.

Increased Glucose Utilization Supplies Essential Metabolic Reactions in Activated Neutrophils.

The stimulation of neutrophils triggers a series of immune functions that entail substantial metabolic demands. Greatly increased glucose uptake, along with increased PYGL activity, would increase the capacity of producing G6P, a common metabolite upstream of important biosynthetic and bioenergetic pathways, including glycolysis, PPP, and TCA cycle. For glycolysis, we found the production of its end-product, lactate, increased substantially over 1 h after stimulation with PMA, zymosan, or TNFα (Fig. 4A), indicating increased glycolysis rate. Regarding PPP, consistent with our previous observation that PPP flux is greatly up-regulated in activated neutrophils to support NADPH production and enable effector functions (5), here we observed PPP intermediates accumulated substantially by 30 min. upon stimulation of PMA, zymosan, TNFα, or HI P. aeruginosa. Only LPS stimulation did not induce such change by 4 h (Fig. 4B). To understand how changes in different nutrient utilization support such upregulation of PPP upon activation, we limited the utilization of glucose or glycogen. Withdrawing extracellular glucose significantly reduced the stimulation-induced accumulation of pentose phosphates. In contrast, inhibiting glycogen degradation only had a minor impact on the accumulation of PPP intermediates, and the combination of glucose withdrawal and glycogenolysis inhibition had the strongest effect (Fig. 4C). Consistent with glucose labeling showing that extracellular glucose became the dominant source of PPP upon activation (Fig. 2B), this result shows increased glucose utilization, but not glycogen utilization, is essential to support the upregulation of PPP.

Next, we assessed how the TCA cycle is altered by increased glucose utilization upon neutrophil activation. In resting condition, labeled glucose was incorporated into the TCA cycle slowly, and most TCA cycle intermediates only labeled ~5% by 6 h (Fig. 4D). The low baseline activity of the TCA cycle is consistent with previous reports that neutrophils have few classic mitochondria (3, 39). Upon activation with zymosan, we found a rapid and substantial increase in TCA cycle labeling from glucose, with citrate labeling up to 20% within 1 h (Fig. 4D), which exceeds the extent of increased glycolytic intermediates labeling. This result suggests that upon activation, increased flux was sent through the TCA cycle, supported by increased glucose utilization. Nonetheless, the overall fraction of TCA cycle labeling was relatively small (<20%) within the assessed period, suggesting the TCA cycle is relatively low among the glucose-metabolizing pathways, and there may be other important sources beyond glycolytic products feeding into the TCA cycle in neutrophils. Consistent with this, neither glucose withdrawal nor glycogenolysis inhibition within a short period significantly reduced the level of TCA cycle intermediates (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A).

Cellular energy status was generally well-maintained upon stimulation (Fig. 4E), suggesting the increased glucose utilization, as well as gross glycogen utilization, is sufficient to support the increased bioenergetic demand upon activation. In testing the dependence of cellular energetics on glucose or glycogen, we found that neither inhibiting glycogen utilization nor withdrawing extracellular glucose alone substantially reduced energy charge in stimulated neutrophils, while inhibiting both noticeably reduced energy charge (Fig. 4E and SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). This finding suggests that with increased capacity to utilize both glucose and glycogen, neutrophils can flexibly use either source to maintain their energy status, which can in turn provide the basis for their metabolic flexibility to support cell functions under different nutrient conditions.

Utilization of Extracellular Glucose Is Required for Oxidative Burst.

Results above revealed the shift in nutrient utilization and the downstream changes in metabolic pathways induced by neutrophil stimulation. Reprogramming of cellular metabolism plays a key role in supporting the immune functions that neutrophils perform upon activation. We next investigated how suppressing or priming such activation-induced shift toward glucose utilization impacts neutrophil functions, and examined to what extent various neutrophil functions depend on the major metabolic sources identified here. To do so, we measured neutrophil functions after acutely limiting the utilization of either extracellular glucose, or glycogen, or both.

As an important control, we first examined how such nutrient perturbations impact cell viability, and found that blocking glycogenolysis with glycogen phosphorylation inhibitor, glucose withdrawal, or the combination of both, did not reduce cell viability within a period of a few hours (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). A day after primary neutrophil isolation, a large portion of the cells died across all of these nutrient-perturbed conditions, similar to the nutrient-replete control condition (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). This cell death is consistent with neutrophils’ short-lived nature. Overall, neutrophil survival in a short period is not dependent on either glucose or glycogen. For assaying cell functions, we focused on the first few hours when cells remained highly viable across all nutrient conditions. This is relevant as neutrophils are the first responders in innate immunity and perform their functions rapidly.

Oxidative burst is the production of reactive oxygen species for pathogen killing, which can be induced by PMA or zymosan stimulation. Inhibiting glycogen utilization had minimal impact on the oxidative burst induced by either stimuli, in contrast, inhibiting extracellular glucose utilization by inhibiting hexokinase (using 2DG) or glucose withdrawal greatly suppressed the oxidative burst (Fig. 5A). Limiting glucose utilization not only greatly reduced the overall intensity of the burst (Fig. 5B), but also quickened the decline of the oxidative burst, particularly noticeable for lower dose stimulation, which induces a slower burst (Fig. 5C). This is consistent with neutrophils relying heavily on glycogen utilization at baseline but switching to primarily using glucose soon after stimulation. Thus, when extracellular glucose is absent, oxidative burst is still able to start but soon becomes limited. The effect of inhibiting glucose and glycogen utilization appears to be additive; in the condition with no glucose and with GPi treatment, cells have the lowest oxidative burst (Fig. 5 A and B). Together, these results showed the oxidative burst in activated neutrophils primarily depends on high glucose utilization, with glycogen providing a very minor contribution but unable to replace the role of extracellular glucose. NET Release and Phagocytosis Can be Flexibly Supported by Either Glucose or Glycogen.

NET is a network of decondensed DNA mixed with granule enzymes that neutrophils release upon activation for trapping and killing pathogens. Contrasting with oxidative burst, inhibiting the utilization of either extracellular glucose or intracellular glycogen only slightly decreased NET release, as measured by the amount of extracellular DNA released (Fig. 6 A and B). However, inhibiting both completely abolished NET release (Fig. 6B). This result shows neutrophils have great flexibility to use either nutrient source to support NET release, but at least one of these sources is required and cannot be compensated by alternative sources. Supporting the idea that the ability to utilize both glucose and glycogen provides the flexibility to perform NET release when either source is limited, we found that in HL-60 cells (contrasting with peripheral blood neutrophils), which have very limited utilization of glycogen (Fig. 1G), withdrawal of extracellular glucose greatly suppresses NET release, and inhibition of glycogen utilization has no appreciable effect either in presence or absence of extracellular glucose (SI Appendix, Fig. S5C).

Similar to NET release, phagocytosis of bacteria also showed flexibility in nutrient dependence. Cells could still largely perform phagocytosis when either glucose or glycogen utilization, or both, was limited, although the phagocytosis rate was reduced when either nutrient supply was limited (Fig. 6 C and D). For both NET release and phagocytosis, the dependence on extracellular glucose is slightly higher than glycogen.

Shifting Away from Glycogen Utilization Increases Neutrophil Migration and Fungal Control.

Upon sensing signals associated with infection or inflammation, neutrophils can quickly migrate toward the site. We next examined how the shift from glycogen to glucose utilization impacts neutrophil migration. Migration toward chemoattractant C5a was greatly inhibited by the withdrawal of extracellular glucose, suggesting high dependence of migration on glucose. Conversely, inhibiting glycogen degradation with GPi, which would force the shift to primarily relying on extracellular glucose (Fig. 1E), significantly increased neutrophil migration (Fig. 7A). Interestingly, when glycogenolysis inhibition is combined with glucose withdrawal, we observed an even greater suppression of neutrophil migration than glucose withdrawal alone (Fig. 7A). It is likely that the metabolic shift toward glucose utilization can promote migration; however, cells’ ability to migrate also depends on sufficient cellular energy charge, which is not affected with glycogenolysis inhibition alone, but becomes limited when glycogen utilization is also limited in an environment lacking extracellular glucose.

Given the intriguing observation that inhibition of glycogenolysis promotes neutrophil migration toward chemoattractant, we further assessed the impact of nutrient utilization shift on neutrophil migration and its downstream effects in pathogen control in the setting of fungal infection with A. fumigatus (40). Neutrophils play a significant role in controlling the growth of the invasive pathogen A. fumigatus, and coordinated swarming toward the pathogen enables effective fungal control. In this experiment, to avoid the direct impact of GPi treatment on A. fumigatus, we pretreated primary human neutrophils with GPi for 1 h, then removed the GPi and coincubated the treated neutrophils with fungal germlings in fresh culture media. The effect of GPi in forcing cells to use glucose instead of glycogen is sustained up to 4 h after such pretreatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). Consistently, the effect of GPi on promoting neutrophil migration toward C5a is also sustained after an acute pretreatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). When cocultured with A. fumigatus, neutrophils swarmed toward, and clustered around, fungal pathogens (Fig. 7B). Consistent with the dose-dependent effect of GPi in shifting neutrophils from glycogen utilization toward glucose utilization, the frequency of neutrophil clusters around fungal hyphae was increased when neutrophils were pretreated with GPi in a dose-dependent manner, indicating increased neutrophil migration toward fungal pathogens (Fig. 7C). Further, consistent with the increased neutrophil clustering around developing A. fumigatus hyphae and germlings (Fig. 7 B and C), the fraction of A. fumigatus cells that remained after 4 h coincubation with neutrophils was also decreased in a dose-dependent manner when the neutrophils were pretreated with GPi (Fig. 7D). This result further shows that shifting neutrophil away from glycogen utilization promotes migration and fungal control.

Discussion

This work quantitatively evaluated the contributions of various nutrients in neutrophil metabolism in resting and activated states. Glycogen was found to be a major metabolic source particularly at resting state. Neutrophils can actively turn over glycogen, and such turnover is even up-regulated upon cell activation. Recent work which found that neutrophils express key gluconeogenic enzymes (4) has raised the potential role of gluconeogenesis to supply glycogen storage. We found that at gross flux level, a small fraction of glycolytic intermediates does not directly arise from glucose or glycogen, which may result from gross flux in gluconeogenesis. However, such contribution is minor. At the net flux level, supplying glycogen with gluconeogenesis requires ATP production from nonglycolytic pathways to support bioenergetics. Our observation is that, consistent with previous reports (4, 41), TCA cycle activity in resting neutrophils is very low to support energy production. Therefore, overall, extracellular glucose is the main ultimate source for neutrophil metabolism and energy supply. However, at gross flux level, a large fraction of the glucose is converted to glycogen first, and glycogen acts as a major direct source for neutrophil metabolism at resting state.

The question then becomes why primary neutrophils actively cycle between glucose and glycogen, rather than directly use extracellular glucose like some other cells, or even the neutrophil-like cell line. Cycling glucose with glycogen storage can provide a buffer for fluctuating glucose level. Primary neutrophils need to be actively recruited from circulation to the site of infection, where they perform important functions. Such sites often have low microenvironmental glucose (42), and therefore high capacity to use glycogen storage can provide neutrophils extra metabolic flexibility, even though extracellular glucose is the preferred source for activated neutrophil when available. On the other hand, relying on glycogen cycling rather than just using extracellular glucose directly may also better buffer neutrophils from potential dysregulating effects of high extracellular glucose. For example, hyperglycemia is detrimental during sepsis, and hyperglycemia in diabetes appears to be closely associated with multiple hyperactive phenotypes in neutrophils and altered wound healing (43–45). These effects of high glucose mirror our observation that shifting toward glucose utilization is associated with activation of certain neutrophil functions. The idea that the ability to cycle between glucose and glycogen is critical for neutrophils is further supported by clinical evidence in glycogen storage disease (GSD). Low neutrophil numbers and functional defects in neutrophils are among the most important symptoms of some subtypes of GSD, particularly GSD-Ib (46–49). Interestingly, some other forms, such as GSD-Ia, are not found to show similar neutrophil dysfunction (50). GSD-1b is characterized by a deficiency in G6P transport through the endoplasmic reticulum membrane. This further suggests that not only is general glycogen cycling important for neutrophils, but there may also be subcellular compartment-specific metabolism that allow neutrophils to channel glucose or glycogen utilization differently, which is critical to neutrophil function. The compartment-specific metabolism may also contribute to some of the function-specific dependence on glucose or glycogen we report here, and requires further investigation. Furthermore, there is also likely to be metabolic heterogeneity among neutrophil subpopulations, which can also affect the nutrient preference shift and the function-specific nutrient dependence. Here, we showed that upon stimulation, neutrophils quickly shift to greater extracellular glucose utilization, supported by increased plasma membrane GLUT1 level. Interestingly, in the PMA stimulated condition, it is noticeable that the GLUT1 level appears to be distributed across two peaks, with the smaller subpopulation only having slightly increased GLUT1 level compared to the unstimulated condition, and the bigger subpopulation having further increased GLUT1 level (Fig. 3C). When neutrophils are activated, CD11b level increases. Interestingly, we noticed a correlation that the cells with a higher GLUT1 level tend to be cells with increased CD11b level (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A), pointing to the possibility that the metabolic differences may be associated with neutrophil subpopulations with higher or lower response to stimulation. Metabolic heterogeneity is an important question which merits more in-depth future research.

While in general active cycling between glucose and glycogen can provide more buffering and metabolic flexibility, our results here also reveal that activation prompts neutrophils to utilizing extracellular glucose primarily, and that diverting the nutrient utilization can significantly alter cell functions. In general, suppressing the activation-induced shift toward glucose utilization more significantly suppresses functions performed by activated neutrophils. Intriguingly, priming the nutrient preference shift by treatment with glycogenolysis inhibitor (which we found increased glucose utilization both in relative contribution and in absolute rate) can promote neutrophil migration and fungal control. Furthermore, we found the exact effect of modulating nutrient metabolism on neutrophil functions is function-specific, as different functions vary in their flexibility to use glucose or glycogen to compensate for each other. Notably, the oxidative burst requires the shift to extracellular glucose utilization indispensably, whereas NET release can be very flexibly supported by either glucose or glycogen.

Different functions entail different metabolic demands. The function-specific nutrient dependence likely stems from the specific type, size, and kinetic requirement of each function’s metabolic requirements. Oxidative burst, for instance, requires neutrophils to generate a large amount of NADPH in a short period of time to fuel the production of reactive oxygen species via NADPH oxidase. We have previously shown that this rapid NADPH production depends on substantial upregulation of the PPP (5). Here, we found the PPP upregulation is primarily supported by glucose, which cannot be replaced by glycogen (Fig. 4C). This is likely because activated neutrophils can use extracellular glucose to supply substrates at much higher gross flux than they can utilize glycogen, even though the ability to use both sources is activated. Therefore, glucose, the fast-accessible source, has an irreplaceable role in supporting the high metabolic demands of oxidative burst, and the effects of glucose withdrawal or glycogenolysis inhibition on oxidative burst mirror their effects on PPP (Fig. 5). By contrast, other functions may be associated with metabolic requirements that are not as kinetically demanding [e.g., NET release only needs a low level of reactive oxygen species to signal for its activation (51)], or they may primarily require energy supply. We found that activated neutrophils can largely maintain their energy status with either glucose or glycogen, as long as at least one of them is available (Fig. 4E). Such functions with moderate demands are more likely to have high flexibility to be supported by either nutrient, and such metabolic flexibility is further ensured by neutrophils’ increased ability to use both glucose and glycogen upon activation thanks to the rapid phosphorylation of GLUT1 and PYGL.

These findings of neutrophils’ nutrient metabolism and its impacts on immune functions, have important implications for health, given the crucial role of proper neutrophil functions in a broad range of contexts. While promoting pathogen-eliminating functions is essential for combating against infection, overactivation of functions such as oxidative burst can cause tissue damage, and lowering proinflammation response can alleviate inflammatory conditions. Therefore, understanding neutrophils’ specific metabolism–function connections is fundamental both for discerning how altered nutrient metabolism may contribute to diseases and for targeting nutrient metabolism to modulate specific functions for treatment. Indeed, leveraging specific metabolic manipulation to shape immune response has shown promise in other immune cells. For instance, elevated glucose uptake has been recognized as a hallmark of activated T cells, B cells, and macrophages, and therefore has been probed as a therapeutic target for various diseases such as autoimmunity (52, 53). However, equivalent research in neutrophils had been significantly lagging. Providing an important foundation, this study reveals the quantitative contribution of various nutrients in human neutrophil metabolism, the reprograming of nutrient metabolism upon neutrophil activation, and the impact of altering nutrient utilization on specific neutrophil functions. The findings have therapeutic implications, such as the potential application of glucose transport inhibition to suppress oxidative burst-induced tissue damage without harming neutrophil survival and other functions, or the application of metabolic perturbation to promote fungal clearance. Future research is needed to further understand questions in two areas: First, what exact mechanisms underlie the specific connections between nutrient metabolism and neutrophil functions (e.g., what mechanisms explain why steering neutrophils away from using glycogen promotes migration), and second, whether alteration in nutrient levels in specific biological environments (e.g., hyperglycemia) impacts progression of disease through changing neutrophil metabolism.

Methods

In Vivo Isotopic Tracing.

Healthy adults with no previous history of diabetes received infusions of U13C-glucose after informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the University of Wisconsin Institutional Review Board (2020-1008-CP001). Sterile and endotoxin-free U-13C-glucose was administered as a bolus of 8 gr over 10 min followed by an 8 g/h infusion over 2 h. Peripheral blood samples were collected at various time points into EDTA-coated tubes, and metabolites were extracted from plasma and neutrophils. Plasma was collected by spinning at 1,000×g for 10 min, and plasma metabolites were extracted by adding 50 μL plasma into 200 μL of 40:40:20 acetonitrile/methanol/water (40:40:20 v:v:v). Neutrophils were isolated from peripheral blood collected prior to infusion and at 30 and 60 min following the start of infusion, then metabolites were extracted and analyzed using method specified in SI Appendix.

To assess the possible change in metabolite labeling during the neutrophil isolation process, freshly isolated human peripheral blood neutrophils were labeled in media containing U13C-glucose tracer for 1 h. After that, half of the labeled neutrophil suspension was immediately spun down and extracted for metabolite analysis, and the other half of neutrophil suspension underwent a round of mock isolation process, where it was treated following exactly the same protocol used to isolate neutrophils from in vivo tracing blood samples (as specified in SI Appendix). Following the mock isolation, the metabolites were extracted. The labeling in the immediate extraction samples and the samples following mock isolation were then analyzed using the same method by LC–MS for comparison.

Isolation, Culture, and Stimulation of Human Peripheral Blood Neutrophils.

Human neutrophils were isolated from peripheral blood freshly collected from healthy donors, following the protocol approved by the University of Wisconsin Institutional Review Board (Protocol No. 2019-1031-CP001). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Neutrophils were isolated and studied ex vivo using method as specified in SI Appendix.

Ex Vivo and In Vitro Isotopic Tracing.

Cells were cultured in labeled medium, which is chemically identical to regular culture medium, but with stable isotope tracer U-13C-glucose (Cambridge Isotope, CLM-1396) substituting regular glucose at the same concentration, for indicated time. For labeling experiments of stimulated neutrophils, the cells were switched to labeled medium at the same time stimuli (PMA, Zymosan A, TNF, or HI PsA) were added. The exception was LPS stimulation, neutrophils were stimulated with 1 μg/mL LPS for 3 h in unlabeled glucose media prior to switching to U-13C-gluose media for an additional hour for a total of 4 h of stimulation, but only 1-h labeling, to avoid significant labeling of glycogen due to prolonged culturing in U-13C-gluose, which could complicate result interpretation.

Metabolomics.

Metabolites and isotopic labeling were measured using LC–MS-based methods, following previously established methods, as specified in SI Appendix, Method.

Flow Cytometry and Immunoblotting.

Flow cytometry and immunoblotting following standard protocol was used to measure specific proteins in neutrophils. Specific antibodies and procedure are specified in SI Appendix, Method.

Neutrophil Viability and Neutrophil Function Assays.

Neutrophil viability, NET release, phagocytosis, and migration toward chemoattractant were quantified by imaging-based analysis using Incucyte live cell imager, using methods specified in SI Appendix. Oxidative burst was measured by stimulation-induced oxygen consumption rate (OCR) using a XF-96e extracellular flux analyzer (Agilent) following a previously developed protocol (54), as specified in SI Appendix. Neutrophil clustering around A. fumigatus and fungal control was assessed by imaging analysis as specified in SI Appendix.
