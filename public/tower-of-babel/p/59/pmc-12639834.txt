Chick embryo development assessment and fertility detection using pixel-wise hyperspectral image analysis and deep learning
Authors: Mahdi Ghaderi, Seyed Ahmad Mireei, Aminollah Masoumi, Mohammad Sedghi, Majid Nazeri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate early detection of fertility and embryonic development during incubation is essential for enhancing hatchability efficiency in poultry production. This study employed a line-scan hyperspectral imaging (HSI) system to monitor fertility and embryonic development in white-shelled eggs. Full-transmittance hyperspectral images were acquired at the end of each of the first four incubation days from both fertile and infertile eggs. Classification based on average spectra was performed using soft independent modeling of class analogy (SIMCA), linear discriminant analysis, quadratic discriminant analysis (QDA), and artificial neural networks (ANN). Among these, ANN achieved the highest performance in embryonic stage detection (F1-score: 92.33 %), while SIMCA, ANN, QDA, and ANN provided the best fertility detection on days 1–4, respectively (F1-scores: 77.78–100 %). Pixel-wise classification, incorporating ANN, random forest, deep neural networks (DNN), and convolutional neural networks (CNN), further improved accuracy and enabled spatial visualization. The DNN achieved the highest stage discrimination (F1-score: 94.95 %), while CNN, DNN, ANN, and DNN performed best in fertility detection on days 1–3 (F1-score: 90.00 %) and day 4 (F1-score: 100.00 %), respectively. These findings demonstrate the potential of HSI combined with pixel-wise deep learning classification as a non-destructive and accurate method for real-time fertility assessment in poultry hatcheries.Keywords: Embryo development, Fertility detection, Deep learning classification, Poultry hatchery automation, Spectral-spatial modeling

Introduction

Early detection of fertility and monitoring of embryonic development during incubation provide substantial benefits for both fundamental research and commercial hatchery management. For researchers, these assessments enable evaluation of the effects of external physical factors, as well as chemical or biochemical components, on early embryogenesis. Variations in the rate of early development can influence later stages, including the timing and duration of hatching (Bamelis et al., 2002). From an operational perspective, distinguishing between developmental days allows hatcheries to identify delayed or abnormal development, which may indicate underlying management or biosecurity issues. Furthermore, infertile eggs or those containing non-viable embryos not only represent wasted space and energy but also pose potential microbiological risks to fertile eggs and newly hatched chicks (Karunarathna, et al., 2020). Therefore, early detection and removal of such eggs can significantly improve hatchery efficiency, enhance chick quality, and strengthen biosecurity.

A range of non-invasive techniques have been explored for early fertility assessment, yielding varying levels of accuracy. These include machine vision, which achieved accuracies ranging from 67.6 % to 100 % on days 2–4 (Das and Evans, 1992); visible and near-infrared (Vis–NIR) transmission spectroscopy, which reached 100 % accuracy only on day 4 (Islam, et al., 2017); and hyperspectral imaging (HSI), which reported accuracies of 63–83 % on days 1–3 (Smith et al., 2008) and 74.1–84.1 % on days 1–4 (Liu and Ngadi, 2013). However, these results remain suboptimal for reliable industrial implementation.

For embryogenesis assessment, Vis–NIR transmission spectroscopy has been applied to detect embryonic development from 120 h of incubation onwards (Bamelis, Tona, De Baerdemaeker and Decuypere, 2002), and a NIR sensor has been developed to monitor embryonic growth (Khaliduzzaman, et al., 2020). Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, nondestructive optical techniques capable of characterizing or distinguishing developmental days during the early incubation period (within the first four days) have not yet been reported.

Accurate monitoring chick embryo growth throughout incubation remains challenging due to inherent biological variability in embryonic development. This variability is strongly influenced by factors such as flock age, egg storage conditions, egg size and composition (Iqbal, et al., 2016; Nasri, et al., 2020), alongside incubation temperature (Yalcin, et al., 2022). These parameters collectively contribute to substantial inconsistencies in developmental rates among individual embryos.

Hyperspectral imaging, which provides both spatial and spectral information, has emerged as a promising tool for non-invasive biological assessment. However, given the large data volume inherent to hyperspectral images, efficient extraction of informative spectral and spatial features is crucial. Owing to the minute size of embryos during the early developmental stages, previous studies have primarily relied on spectral analysis for detection of fertility and early embryo development (Liu and Ngadi, 2013). These approaches typically involve averaging spectra from predefined regions of interest, followed by preprocessing and classification. A major challenge in such studies has been the identification of an optimal classification algorithm for fertility detection (Dong, et al., 2019; Park, et al., 2019). However, the rich spectral information contained in hyperspectral images makes them particularly well suited for deep learning applications. A pixel-wise classification strategy that directly leverages the full spectral information can provide valuable spatial context while improving detection accuracy. To date, no study has explored the application of pixel-wise deep learning-based classification for the spatial monitoring of fertility and embryo development.

In this study, we comparatively evaluated the performance of deep learning models and conventional machine learning methods for classifying hyperspectral images of incubated eggs. Initially, various machine learning algorithms were applied to the average spectra extracted from the egg region of interest. Subsequently, both machine learning and deep learning models were implemented using a pixel-wise classification strategy to segment hyperspectral images according to fertility status and developmental stage. The performance of these two approaches was then rigorously compared.

The objectives of this study were (1) to compare the performance of spectral analyses, based on the average spectra of hyperspectral images, with spatial analyses, based on pixel-wise classification, for monitoring fertility and chick embryo development during the initial days of incubation; and (2) to compare the efficacy of conventional machine learning and deep learning models in developing reliable prediction frameworks of these applications.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in full compliance with the animal experimentation protocol approved by the Ethical Committee for Animal Experiments of Isfahan University of Technology (Permit No. 390132) (Ghaderi, et al., 2024). The study involved commercial Leghorn laying breeder hens, which are not classified as endangered or protected species.

Sample preparation for stage-tracking experiment

A flock of 60 Leghorn laying breeder hens (Hy-Line W-36 strain), accompanied by eight roosters, was housed at the experimental poultry facility of Isfahan University of Technology. Eggs were collected daily and the incubated within 24 hours of collection. Prior to incubation, all eggs were gently cleaned, individually numbered, and randomly assigned to either a calibration (training) group (80 % of samples) or a test group (20 % of samples). Eggs were incubated in a commercial incubator maintained at 37.5°C and 60 % relative humidity, with automatic turning once per hour.

Hyperspectral imaging was performed on both the calibration and test eggs at the end of each incubation day (days 1–4). Following each imaging session, test eggs were promptly returned to the incubator, whereas calibration eggs were opened to visually confirm embryonic development. To minimize thermal stress, each egg was removed from the incubator for no longer than three minutes during imaging. After five days of incubation, test eggs were broken out, and their embryonic developmental stage was evaluated based on the morphological characteristics described by Joseph (1997). Only eggs exhibiting normal developmental progression were retained for analysis; infertile or non-developing eggs were excluded.

Sample preparation for fertility assessment experiments

For fertility assessment, we used the dataset originally prepared in our previous study (Ghaderi, Mireei, Masoumi, Sedghi and Nazeri, 2024). Because that work focused on fertility detection of unincubated chicken eggs, only the day 0 (pre-incubation) measurements were reported. That work focused on fertility detection in unincubated chicken eggs (day 0). In the present study, we used hyperspectral data acquired from the same flock during days 1–4 of incubation, following the identical sample preparation protocol. Briefly: (1) The same flock of 60 hens was divided into two groups of 30 each: one group housed without roosters to produce infertile eggs, and the other with four roosters to ensure fertility; (2) both groups were maintained under identical conditions, receiving the same standard diet and management to ensure comparable nutritional and physiological status; (3) eggs were collected daily, numbered, weighed, measured, and incubated under standard conditions (37.5 °C, 60 % relative humidity, turned hourly); (4) on each of days 1–4, racks containing 42 eggs were temporarily transferred to a secondary incubator to avoid disturbing the main incubator environment; (5) eggs were removed from the incubator for imaging and returned within three minutes; and (6) after 5 days of incubation, eggs were broken out, and fertility status was confirmed.

Hyperspectral imaging system

A line-scan visible and near-infrared (Vis-NIR) hyperspectral camera (model V1001, OPTC, Iran) was utilized in this study. The camera operates within 400–1000 nm spectral range with an average spectral resolution of 2 nm. It is capable of simultaneously capturing spatial and spectral information from samples without requiring any relative movement between the sample and the camera. A full-transmittance measurement mode was employed to acquire the hyperspectral images of egg samples. For this setup, a wooden board was positioned between the camera and the light sources. Each egg was placed vertically within a 5 cm diameter hole drilled into the board. The illumination system comprised a centrally located 150 W tungsten-halogen lamp and six additional 50 W lamps arranged circularly to ensure uniform back-illumination. Two cooling fans were installed to dissipate excess heat generated by the light sources.

The camera exposure time was set to 100 ms, and 400 scans were performed to maximize the signal-to-noise ratio and provide complete spatial coverage of each egg’s width. This configuration resulted in a spatial resolution of 400 × 400 pixels per wavelength band. To eliminate ambient light interference, the entire imaging assembly was enclosed within a polyurethane covering. Due to the absorption characteristics of the eggshell (Dong, et al., 2020) and the reduced illumination intensity at shorter wavelengths, spectral data below 500 nm were excluded. Similarly, because the camera detector exhibited a low signal-to-noise ratio above 950 nm, analysis was limited to the 500–950 nm range for fertility and embryo development monitoring. Spectral resolution was reduced by a factor of two by averaging every two consecutive wavelength bands across the selected spectral region to further enhance the signal-to-noise ratio, resulting in a final hypercube of 400 × 400 × 127 (rows × columns × spectral bands).

For spectral calibration, both white and dark reference images were acquired. The white reference image was obtained with the camera set to its minimum exposure time while directly imaging the light sources. The dark image was captured by completely covering the camera lens. To isolate the region of interest (ROI) and remove background noise, the image exhibiting the highest contrast between the egg and the background (identified at of 830 nm) was selected. An edge-detection algorithm was applied to delineate the egg boundary, followed by image binarization. The resulting binary mask was then multiplied with all wavelength images to generate a background-free hyperspectral image cube.

Spectral analysis

Two complementary analytical strategies were employed in this study to monitor fertility and embryonic development from hyperspectral images: (1) spectral analysis and (2) pixel-wise spatial analysis. In the spectral analysis approach, the average spectrum of the ROI was computed and subsequently used to predict either fertility status or embryonic developmental stage. In contrast, the spatial analysis approach involved selecting a representative subset of pixels from each hyperspectral image to develop various machine learning and deep learning models.

To begin the spectral data analysis, principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to the average spectra to provide an initial overview of the data distribution and to identify potential outliers (Martens and Naes, 1992). Samples located outside the Hotelling’s T² ellipse at the 95 % confidence level were flagged as outliers and subsequently removed from further analysis. To enhance spectral quality and minimize unwanted variations, several preprocessing techniques were evaluated, including Savitzky–Golay (SG) smoothing, standard normal variate (SNV), normalization, multiplicative scatter correction (MSC), and SG first- and second-derivatives (Williams and Norris, 1987).

For classifying samples based on fertility status or developmental stage, several well-established machine learning algorithms were implemented: soft independent modeling of class analogy (SIMCA), linear discriminant analysis (LDA), quadratic discriminant analysis (QDA), and nonlinear artificial neural networks (ANNs).

In SIMCA, each class (fertile and infertile eggs, or individual developmental stages corresponding to incubation days) was modeled independently using PCA-based submodels. These class-specific submodels were then utilized to evaluate the class membership of test samples (Esbensen, et al., 2002). LDA constructs linear discriminant functions that identify an optimal linear combination of features to maximize separation between classes. These functions are then used to assign unknown samples to their respective classes (Xanthopoulos, et al., 2013). QDA operates similarly to LDA but employs quadratic discriminant functions that incorporate second-order terms, thus enabling the delineation of class boundaries with quadratic surfaces under assumption of Gaussian-distributed classes (Srivastava, et al., 2007).

Finally, multi-layer feed-forward ANNs were developed to build predictive models for fertility detection and developmental stage classification. Each ANN consisted of an input layer that received the preprocessed spectral data, a single hidden layer, and an output layer. The optimal number of hidden layer nodes (NHL) was determined iteratively through a trial-and-error procedure. For each tested NHL value, three independent networks were trained, and their mean classification accuracy was computed. The optimal NHL and the corresponding network topology were selected based on the relationship between average accuracy and NHL.

Pixel-wise spatial analysis

In addition to spectral analysis, pixel-wise spatial analysis was performed to classify individual pixels within the hyperspectral images into either fertile or infertile categories, or into one of four developmental stages corresponding to days 1, 2, 3, and 4 of incubation. The final class label assigned to each egg was determined based on the most frequently occurring class label within its corresponding image.

To prepare the data for this analysis, the preprocessed hypercube was first unfolded into a two-dimensional (2D) matrix, where each row represented the spectrum of a single pixel and each column corresponded to a specific wavelength. From each training sample, 400 pixels were randomly selected and combined to form a new data matrix.

To mitigate overfitting, a leave-one-sample-out cross-validation (LOSO—CV) strategy was employed during model development. In this approach, each training sample, consisting of 400 pixel data points, was randomly excluded and sequentially presented to the model trained on the remaining samples. The class label for each sample was determined using the majority-vote criterion. This process was repeated until all samples in the training subset had been cross-validated. Based on the cross-validation results, model hyperparameters were optimized, and the final model was trained using all available training samples with the optimized (locked) hyperparameter settings. The fully trained model was subsequently applied to the test hyperspectral images, and the final class labels were again determined using the majority-vote criterion.

For pixel-wise classification, both conventional machine learning algorithms and deep learning approaches were utilized. The conventional methods included ANNs and random forest (RF), whereas the deep learning approaches comprised deep neural networks (DNNs) and convolutional neural networks (CNNs).

Classification by ANN and RF

Consistent with the spectral analysis approach, multi-layer feed-forward ANNs employing the backpropagation learning algorithm were used in this step. Each ANN consisted of an input layer receiving the spectral data from individual pixels, a single hidden layer, and an output layer containing either two nodes for fertility detection or four nodes corresponding to the developmental stages (days 1–4).

The RF model was developed using MATLAB’s TreeBagger ensemble learning function. The ensemble comprised 100 decision trees. To improve generalization and reduce correlation among trees, a random subset of predictors, specifically p predictors where p denotes the total number of input features, was selected at each node split. Out-of-bag (OOB) samples were employed during training to provide on-the-fly estimation of prediction error and to compute variable importance, offering insights into the relative contribution of each spectral feature to overall classification performance.

Classification by DNN and CNN

Fig. 1 illustrates the architectures of the DNN and CNN models used for pixel-level spectral analysis. The DNN architecture (Fig. 1a) consisted of an input layer designed to receive 127-band spectral vectors, followed by three cascaded hidden blocks. Each hidden block was identical and comprised a fully connected layer with 100 neurons for fertility detection and 200 neurons for stage tracking, followed by a batch normalization layer to stabilize training, a ReLU activation function to introduce non-linearity, and a dropout layer with a rate of 0.5 to mitigate overfitting. The network concluded with a fully connected layer containing either two or four neurons for classification, followed by a softmax layer for probabilistic class assignment. The RMSProp optimizer was used was used during training to adapt the learning rate based on the gradient of the loss function. The maximum number of epochs and the mini-batch size were set to 50 and 128, respectively.

Architectures of the deep learning models employed for pixel-wise classification: (a) DNN and (b) CNN. Note: The numbers in parentheses indicate the number of nodes used in the fully connected layers for fertility detection classification.

The CNN architecture (Fig. 1b) also received 127-band spectral vectors as input. These inputs were processed through three sequential convolutional blocks, each comprising a 1D convolutional layer with a 3 × 1 filter across the spectral dimension, followed by batch normalization and a ReLU activation function. The number of filters increased progressively from 32 to 64 and then to 128, enabling hierarchical feature extraction. After each convolutional layer, a max-pooling layer with a 2 × 1 filter and stride was applied to reduce spectral dimensionality. The resulting feature maps were flattened and fed into three fully connected layers. For two-class classification, the first two fully connected layers contained 128 and 64 neurons, whereas for four-class analysis, they contained 256 and 128 neurons. Each fully connected layer incorporated a dropout layer with a rate of 0.5 to reduce overfitting. The final fully connected layer comprised two and four neurons for fertility detection and stage tracking, respectively, and its output was passed through a softmax activation function to produce class probabilities, culminating in the model classification output for prediction.

Once the optimal ANN, RF, DNN, and CNN models were determined, they were applied to hyperspectral test images. The algorithms predicted the class label for each pixel and stored the results in a categorical vector of dimensions (m × n) × 1. A subsequent program reconstructed these predictions into m × n color-coded images, with two or four colors representing fertility status and developmental stages, respectively.

Results and discussion

Overview of spectral data

Fig. 2 presents the mean relative transmission spectra extracted from hyperspectral images of samples collected during first four days of embryo development, as well as from fertile and infertile eggs at different post-incubation days. As incubation progressed, a consistent decrease in transmission values was observed (Fig. 2a), reflecting embryonic growth and increased light absorption. Characteristic absorption patterns were evident across all developmental stages and between fertile and infertile samples, particularly near wavelengths of 580, 634, 665, 730, 780, 830, and 930 nm.

Mean transmission spectra of hyperspectral data: (a) across four days of embryo development, and for fertile and infertile samples on (b) day 1, (c) day 2, (d) day 3, and (e) day 4.

Agro-food products such as eggs generally exhibit complex chemical compositions, resulting in spectral profiles characterized by overlapping absorption bands arising from multiple constituents. This spectral complexity makes it difficult to directly associate individual wavelengths with specific chemical compounds (Luo, et al., 2021). The absorption features observed in pre-incubated eggs have been extensively discussed in our earlier work (Ghaderi et al., 2024). Among these, the spectral region around 730 nm corresponds to the third overtone of O–H stretching vibrations, commonly associated with water and carbohydrate content (Kuroki, et al., 2020). The band near 830 nm is attributed to a combination absorption feature of O–H (Kovacs, et al., 2021), while the peak around 930 nm corresponds to the third overtone of C–H stretching, also related to carbohydrate constituents (Wilson, et al., 2015).

In fertility assessment, the degree of spectral overlap between fertile and infertile samples increased on days 2 and 3 (Figs. 2c and 2d), whereas clearer separation was observed on days 1 (Fig. 2b) and 4 (Fig. 2e) of incubation. The most challenging case occurred on day 3 (Fig. 2c), where, despite substantial overlap, the spectra of fertile and infertile samples diverged around the wavelength bands of 630–690 nm and more prominently within 730–790 nm. The 630–690 nm region is known to be sensitive to blood content (Preuße, et al., 2023), while the 730–790 nm range reflects changes in water and carbohydrate composition (Kuroki et al., 2020), likely associated with embryonic metabolic activity.

Fig. 3 illustrates scatter plots of the first two principal component (PC) scores for samples from the first four days of embryo development and for fertile and infertile samples at different post-incubation days. In stage tracking (Fig. 3a), the PCA score plot indicates that day 4 samples are clearly separated from earlier stages in the PC1–PC2 space. However, considerable overlap among samples from days 1, 2, and 3 suggests that linear methods such as PCA may be insufficient for reliable discrimination, highlighting the need for more advanced chemometric or machine learning techniques to improve class separability. In fertility detection, substantial overlap between fertile and infertile samples was observed across all days, particularly on days 2 (Fig. 3c) and 3 (Fig. 3d). On days 1 (Fig. 3b) and 4 (Fig. 3e), most fertile samples were primarily distributed in the first and second quadrants, whereas infertile samples were concentrated in the third and fourth quadrants. Nonetheless, the considerable overlap between the two groups limited the ability of PCA to clearly distinguish fertile from infertile samples.

A similar pattern of reduced classification performance during early embryonic stages was reported by Liu and Ngadi (2013), who achieved sensitivity values of 78.8 %, 74.1 %, 81.8 %, and 84.1 % for days 1 through 4, respectively, using a hyperspectral imaging system operating in the 900–1700 nm range. Despite employing a more costly NIR system, their overall classification performance was lower than that achieved in the present study. Moreover, our predictive models also outperformed those of Smith et al., 2005, who reported detection accuracies of 54 %, 58 %, and 83 % for days 1 to 3, respectively.

In cross-validation, the ANN achieved the highest overall performance, with a weighted F1-score of 99.50 %. The ANN exhibited consistently strong classification performance across all embryonic stages, yielding F1-scores of 100.00 %, 99.50 %, 98.99 %, and 99.49 % for days 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively.

The DNN and CNN models ranked next, with weighted F1-scores of 89.98 % and 88.26 %, respectively. Both models classified days 1 and 4 with high accuracy (F1-scores: 92.86 % and 91.49 % for DNN, and 91.92 % and 93.12 % for CNN), but their performance decreased for intermediate developmental stages, particularly day 3 (F1-scores: 86.00 % and 83.67 %, respectively).

The RF classifier showed the lowest overall performance, achieving a weighted F1-score of 73.72 %. Although it performed reasonably well on day 4 (F1-score: 91.89 %), its sensitivity on days 1 and 2 was markedly lower (79.00 % and 46.53 %, respectively), indicating a limited ability to capture subtle pixel-level spectral variations associated with early embryonic development.

To evaluate these models in a spatial context, all classifiers were applied to test images excluded from the training process (Table 4). Among the models, the DNN achieved the highest overall performance, with a weighted F1-score of 94.95 %, surpassing the best result obtained from average spectral classification (Table 2; 92.33 %). This finding confirms the advantage of pixel-level analysis, which captures both spectral and spatial variations essential for accurate embryo stage detection. The CNN closely followed, with an F1-score of 94.93 %. The ANN also performed strongly, achieving a weighted F1-score of 92.43 % and maintaining high sensitivity across all stages (95.45 %, 95.65 %, 82.61 %, and 100 % for days 1–4, respectively). In contrast, the RF exhibited the lowest performance, with a weighted F1-score of 88.52 %. Overall, these results underscore the superior capability of deep learning models for hyperspectral embryo classification, particularly when pixel-level spectral information is integrated with architectures capable of learning complex, high-dimensional, and nonlinear features. Among all tested models, the DNN demonstrated the best balance between accuracy and robustness.

Maps from day 1 predominantly showed blue regions, whereas those from days 2, 3, and 4 were progressively dominated by green, yellow, and red areas, respectively. This spatial pattern indicates that the models effectively tracked embryonic progression at the pixel level.

The ANN, CNN, and DNN produced more sharply embryonic boundaries, particularly on days 2 and 3. These models more accurately delineated embryonic regions from the surrounding yolk, highlighting their ability to capture spatial and contextual features. On day 2, when embryos are often imperceptible to the naked eye, ANN, CNN, and DNN identified subtle spectral variations indicative of early embryogenesis, whereas the RF-generated maps appeared more spatially fragmented. By day 3, the CNN and DNN more distinctly outlined the developing embryo with reduced background noise, while RF exhibited misclassifications, especially near the yolk–embryo interface. By day 4, all models achieved near-perfect pixel-level classification, reflecting the strong spectral differentiation characteristic of advanced developmental stages.

In several samples, particularly the first, fourth, sixth, ninth, and tenth, the presence of more developed embryos was evident, and small red (day 4) regions appeared, suggesting accelerated development.

By day 3, embryo growth became more pronounced. On average, 47.25 % of pixels were correctly classified as day 3, with 9.36 %, 23.71 %, and 19.68 % were labeled as days 1, 2, and 4, respectively. Although the model achieved high classification accuracy overall, spatial variation among samples led to some inconsistencies. In two samples, day 3 was misclassified as day 4, while in another, it was misclassified as day 2. These errors likely stemmed from biological variability in embryo development or slight differences in incubation timing.

By day 4, all samples were correctly identified, with red (day 4) as the dominant color. On average, 76.73 % of pixels were correctly classified as day 4, while 0.16 %, 3.37 %, and 19.74 % were misclassified as days 1, 2, and 3, respectively. The more advanced embryo structure substantially altered spectral properties, improving classification performance.

The CNN model (Fig. 6) also demonstrated strong performance in classifying embryo developmental stages. On average, 57.94 %, 67.16 %, 45.16 %, and 77.43 % of pixels were correctly classified for days 1 to 4, respectively, comparable to the results achieved by the DNN model. The number of image-level misclassifications was similarly low, leading to a close overall test performance of 94.93 % (Table 4).

On the first day of incubation, the CNN model outperformed the other classifiers, consistently distinguishing between fertile and infertile samples in both validation schemes, with F1-scores of 87.18 % (cross-validation) and 90.00 % (test validation). Although the ANN achieved a slightly higher F1-score (90.91 %) and superior sensitivity in test validation (100.00 % vs. 90.00 %), its ability to correctly identify infertile samples in both validation methods and fertile samples in cross-validation was inferior to that of the CNN. In fertility detection, false negatives (misclassified fertile samples) are more detrimental than false positives (misclassified infertile samples), given the higher biological and economic value of fertile eggs. This emphasizes the critical importance of the sensitivity metric. The CNN demonstrated higher sensitivity during cross-validation and showed greater capability in detecting infertile samples as well. The DNN and RF models ranked next, achieving test F1-scores of 85.71 % and 76.19 %, respectively.

On the second day of incubation, the DNN model achieved the best overall performance, with F1-scores of 83.33 % and 90.00 % in cross-validation and test validation, respectively. It also exhibited well-balanced sensitivity and specificity across both validation methods, indicating stable and consistent classification. Although the ANN showed comparable cross-validation performance (F1 = 85.88 %), its sensitivity decreased to 80.00 % in test validation, suggesting a diminished ability to correctly identify fertile samples. The RF and CNN models displayed similar trends, achieving moderate accuracy and F1-scores in the 80–82 % range, reflecting lower classification effectiveness compared to the DNN.

By the third day of incubation, nearly all classifiers exhibited a marked improvement in performance, likely due to more pronounced spectral differences between fertile and infertile eggs as incubation advanced. The DNN achieved the highest F1-score (95.59 %) and accuracy (94.78 %) in cross-validation, demonstrating excellent consistency and precision. The ANN and CNN followed closely, each achieving F1-scores above 94 %. In test validation, all models produced comparable results, with the ANN slightly outperforming others (F1 = 90.00 %), indicating high stability and reproducibility in fertility detection using hyperspectral data.

On the fourth day, all classifiers reached their peak performance. The DNN achieved perfect results in test validation, with 100 % sensitivity, specificity, precision, accuracy, and F1-score, indicating flawless classification of fertile and infertile samples. The ANN also achieved perfect test performance, while the RF and CNN models followed closely, attaining high F1-scores of 94.74 % and strong cross-validation results (92.31 % and 94.66 %, respectively). These findings indicate that as embryonic development progresses, fertility-related spectral features become increasingly distinct, enabling near-perfect discrimination by deep learning models. Overall, the CNN demonstrated the best performance under the most challenging early condition (day 1), whereas the DNN excelled on days 2 and 4, and the ANN performed best on day 3.

Overall, all models effectively captured the spatial distribution of fertile and infertile samples across the test images. On day 1 (Fig. 7a), the CNN model predominantly displayed green and blue regions corresponding to fertile and infertile samples, respectively. Only one fertile sample was misclassified as infertile, and one infertile sample as fertile. On average, 62.16 % of pixels from fertile samples were correctly classified as green, while 73.70 % of pixels from infertile samples were accurately labeled as blue.

On day 2 (Fig. 7b), the DNN model effectively identified fertile and infertile samples, with most test images exhibiting distinct and consistent segmentation patterns. Misclassifications were limited to a few boundary regions, likely due to mixed spectral signatures. On average, 62.27 % of pixels from fertile samples were correctly classified as green, and 70.41 % of pixels from infertile samples as brown.

On day 3 (Fig. 7c), the ANN model demonstrated strong classification performance, accurately delineating fertile regions across all test images. Misclassified areas were minimal, primarily occurring near image edges or in low-intensity zones. On average, 72.23 % of pixels from fertile samples were correctly identified as green, and 74.99 % of pixels from infertile samples as pink. These results indicate a clear improvement in spatial classification accuracy compared with earlier days, suggesting that embryo development accentuates spectral differences between fertile and infertile eggs.

By day 4 (Fig. 7d), the DNN model achieved highly accurate and consistent segmentation across all samples, with nearly all fertile regions correctly classified and minimal misclassification. On average, 90.37 % of pixels from fertile samples were labeled as green, while 91.63 % of pixels from infertile samples were correctly classified as gold. This demonstrates that by day 4, the spectral distinction between fertile and infertile eggs becomes pronounced, enabling near-perfect pixel-level discrimination.

Compared with classification based on average spectral data (Table 2), the pixel-level approach markedly improved fertility detection, especially on days 1 and 3. On day 1, the pixel-level CNN model achieved a 13.81 % higher F1-score than the ANN model trained on average spectra (76.19 %). The maximum test sensitivity for the average-spectral ANN model was 80.00 %, whereas pixel-level classification reached 100.00 % (ANN) and 90.00 % (CNN). This improvement indicates that incorporating spatially resolved spectral information allows the model to exploit local fertility-related features and detect subtle heterogeneities in early embryonic development that are lost in average spectra.

On day 2, the performance of both strategies was comparable, with the QDA model (average spectra) and DNN model (pixel spectra) each achieving an F1-score of 90 %. By day 3, the pixel-level approach provided a modest F1-score improvement (1.11 %) but substantially higher sensitivity (90.00 %) compared with classification using average spectra (80.00 %), highlighting its effectiveness in correctly identifying fertile samples and minimizing false negatives. On day 4, both classification strategies achieved near-perfect results, with an F1-score of 100.00 % obtained using the QDA model (average spectra) and DNN model (pixel-level spectra). This demonstrates that as embryonic development progresses, fertility-related spectral features become increasingly distinct and separable, enabling highly accurate discrimination regardless of the classification strategy.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated the effectiveness of HSI combined with machine learning and deep learning techniques for non-destructive monitoring of fertility and embryonic developmental stages in chicken eggs during the first four days of incubation. Spectral analysis revealed that, as incubation progressed, transmission values decreased due to increased light absorption by the developing embryo. While day 4 samples were clearly distinguishable from earlier stages, substantial overlap was observed among the spectral data of days 1 to 3. In fertility assessment, although fertile and infertile samples exhibited spectral overlap on days 2 and 3, clearer separation was achieved on days 1 and 4.

In stage-tracking classification, models based on average spectral data achieved their highest performance using the ANN method, with a weighted F1-score of 92.33 %. However, these models were limited in capturing intra-egg spatial variability and subtle developmental differences. In contrast, pixel-wise classification using the DNN method not only improved overall performance (F1-score of 94.95 %) but also revealed embryonic boundaries, particularly on days 2 and 3. This approach allowed for precise delineation of embryonic regions from the surrounding yolk and effectively captured both spatial and contextual spectral features.

For fertility detection, the pixel-wise approach outperformed classification based on average spectra. On day 1, the pixel-wise CNN model achieved an F1-score that was 13.81 % higher than that of the ANN model trained on average spectra (76.19 %). Although performance was similar on day 2, the pixel-wise approach provided a modest F1-score improvement (1.11 %) and a substantially higher sensitivity (90.00 %) compared with average-spectral classification (80.00 %). By day 4, both approaches achieved near-perfect results, with an F1-score of 100.00 %. These findings indicate that pixel-level classification is particularly advantageous during the early stages of incubation, when spectral differences between fertile and infertile eggs are subtle and spatial context plays a critical role. As fertility-related spectral features became more pronounced, both average-spectral and pixel-level methods achieved comparable performance.

Overall, the pixel-wise classification of hyperspectral images demonstrated strong potential for both accurate classification and spatial visualization of both fertility and embryonic development. This approach provides a valuable tool for non-invasive fertility assessment in hatcheries. Future research could extend this methodology to other poultry species and eggs with varying shell colors, shell thicknesses, and incubator conditions to ensure broader applicability in commercial settings. In addition, its potential for early sex determination in embryos could be further explored.
