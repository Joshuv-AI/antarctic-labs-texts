Production of triacetic acid lactone from oleic acid by engineering the yeast Candida viswanathii
Authors: Saptarshi Ghosh, Alex Hutagalung, Yuqian Gao, Javier E Flores, Yichao Han, Joonhoon Kim, Meagan C Burnet, Young-Mo Kim, Alberto Rodriguez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTriacetic acid lactone (TAL) is a promising platform chemical to produce valuable compounds. The development of engineered microbial hosts to efficiently produce TAL from lipid-containing waste streams could be a cost-effective, sustainable and environmentally friendly approach to meet the industrial demand. In this study, we engineered the yeast Candida viswanathii, possessing robust fatty acid conversion capabilities, to develop an alternative route for TAL production from fatty acids that aims to maximize conversion of the acetyl-CoA pool generated by β-oxidation in the peroxisome. To do so, we inactivated the carnitine acetyltransferase gene to block the transport of acetyl-CoA out of the peroxisome and overexpressed the enzymes methylmalonyl-CoA carboxyltransferase, 2-pyrone synthase and pyruvate carboxylase in the peroxisome to convert acetyl-CoA into TAL. We also performed an adaptive laboratory evolution experiment to obtain mutants with higher growth rate in medium with oleic acid and observed marked differences in central carbon metabolism and organic acid production pathways between the evolved and parental strains. These strains were further engineered by integrating additional copies of TAL biosynthetic genes while reducing competing reactions like ω-oxidation and Lipid biosynthesis, resulting in up to 50-fold increase in titers relative to the initial strain, reaching 280 mg/L. This study contributes to the development of bioprocesses that valorize fatty acids as microbial conversion substrates for the production of valuable compounds.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12934-025-02841-7.

Background

Production of high value chemicals with microbial hosts is a promising way of addressing the growing demand for renewable chemicals and alleviating the dependence on petrochemical resources [1]. Additionally, the conversion of carbon in waste streams into high value bioproducts is one of the major strategies to decarbonize industry [2, 3]. In that regard, mixtures of fatty acids are commonly present in waste streams from vegetable oil industries, paper mills and water treatment plants, which are costly to purify but hold potential as substrates for the production of valuable compounds using engineered microbial strains [4–6].

Oleaginous microorganisms have grown in popularity in the last decade as a way to convert inexpensive carbon sources like sugars to lipids as precursors to produce biofuels and bioproducts [7]. The utility of these organisms also relates to their ability to metabolize lipid-derived molecules and other hydrophobic compounds as substrates [8–10]. In particular, the genus Candida includes yeasts that have demonstrated to be remarkable producers of dicarboxylic acids from alkanes and fatty acids [11], and have emerged as promising biomanufacturing hosts [12].

In this work we engineered the yeast Candida viswanathii to produce triacetic acid lactone (TAL; 4-hydroxy-6-methyl-2-pyrone). TAL, a simple polyketide, is a versatile chemical precursor to compounds with high market values such as caprolactone (polymer precursor), sorbic acid (food preservative), and pogostone (antimicrobial and insecticide) [13–15]. Due to its chemical structure, TAL has become a popular target among synthetic biologists in the last decade and it has been described as a “bioprivileged” molecule [16]. Production of TAL using engineered microbes could become a sustainable, cost-effective and industrially viable alternative to complement the traditional chemical catalysis and plant extraction methods [14, 17].

Several organisms have been engineered to produce TAL, including the model hosts Escherichia coli and Saccharomyces cerevisiae [15] and non-model yeasts like Yarrowia lipolytica, Rhodosporidium toruloides and Pichia pastoris [18–21]. The highest TAL titer reported to date was achieved with Y. lipolytica (35.9 g/L); however, in all instances TAL has been produced from glucose [22]. Considering that fatty acids represent a more direct source of the polyketide metabolic precursor acetyl-CoA, we evaluated the potential of C. viswanathii to produce TAL from oleic acid.

Our strategy involved the inactivation of the carnitine acetyltransferase (CAT2) gene to block the export of acetyl-CoA generated by β-oxidation in the peroxisome coupled to the peroxisomal expression of the enzymes methylmalonyl-CoA carboxyltransferase (MMC), 2-pyrone synthase (PS) and pyruvate carboxylase (PC). These modifications are intended to impose a metabolic state where acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA are confined in the peroxisome to promote TAL production, while pyruvate and oxaloacetate can cross the peroxisomal membrane to maintain the central metabolism (Fig. 1). The engineered strain was then subjected to an adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) experiment to obtain mutants with a higher growth rate in medium with oleic acid and a strain displaying faster growth was identified and characterized by multi-omics approaches. The strains were further modified by performing additional transformation of genes involved in TAL biosynthesis and by blocking competing metabolic pathways. Below we report the results obtained using different strains and cultivation conditions and discuss several ideas to improve the production of TAL in C. viswanathii.

Materials and methods

Strains and cultivation media

Candida viswanathii ATCC 20336 was the wild-type strain used as a host of the genetic modifications that led to the generation of the engineered strain 423B. Strain ALE is a derivative of 423B isolated after an adaptive laboratory evolution campaign, as indicated below.

The inoculation train started by streaking the strains from glycerol stocks onto yeast peptone dextrose (YPD) agar plates and incubating them for 2 days at 30 °C. For precultures, colonies were inoculated into 4 mL of yeast peptone glycerol (YPG) medium in 15 mL culture tubes and incubated overnight at 30 °C and 220 rpm shaking. Unless otherwise indicated, the medium for TAL production campaigns contained yeast nitrogen base (YNB) supplemented with 0.2% ammonium sulfate, 1% oleic acid, 2% glycerol, 1% yeast extract, and 2% peptone. Tergitol (0.1%, v/v) was added as an emulsifier to improve bioavailability. The pH of the medium was adjusted to 6.0 and phosphate buffer [Na2HPO4, KH2PO4] was used for balancing the pH change.

Cultivations for TAL production were performed in 48-well Flower Plates (m2p-labs, Baesweiler, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany) using different media compositions as described above. A starting OD of 0.2 was used by adding 48 μL of preculture in 1,200 μL total culture volume. The plates were covered with a gas-permeable AeraSeal sealing film (Excel Scientific, Victorville, CA, USA) to maintain sterility and reduce evaporation and incubated at 30 °C, 975 rpm and 87.5% humidity for 3 days in a Multitron shaker (Infors HT, Bottmingen, Switzerland).

The cell density was periodically measured by taking OD at 600 nm and the supernatants were collected after 72 h of incubation for TAL analysis. To measure dry cell weight, the cells from 1 mL of culture were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm, washed with sterile water, centrifuged again, and lyophilized for 2 days in pre-tared tubes. Product stability in the culture broth was tested by incubating 0.5 g/L TAL with cells in YNB with 0.2% ammonium sulfate and 2% glycerol for 72 h to compare TAL concentrations before and after the incubation period.

CAT2 knockout and peroxisomal gene expression

The CAT2Δ/CAT2Δ double knockout strain was generated using a targeted approach based on homologous recombination to integrate the selective marker URA3 into the CAT2 loci. The double knockout was confirmed by PCR and by the inability to grow on oleic acid as only carbon source (Supplementary File 1). This modified strain was used as the base strain for peroxisomal expression of the TAL pathway enzymes: methylmalonyl-CoA carboxyltransferase (MMC) from Propionibacterium freudenreichii subsp. shermanii, 2-pyrone synthase (PS) from Gerbera hybrid and pyruvate carboxylase (PC) from Rhizopus oryzae. Two peroxisomal targeting sequences, PTS1 (GRRAKL) and PTS2 (MDRLNQLSGQL), were used in the C-terminal and N-terminal ends of these enzymes [23, 24]. PTS1 and PTS2 were both used for 12 S, PTS1 was used for 5 S and PTS2 was used for 1.3 S. Three dual expression constructs were built, and these constructs were simultaneously integrated into the URA3 locus via homologous recombination.

Inactivation of competing reactions

Genes encoding two cytochrome P450 monooxygenase enzymes (CYP52A13 and CYP52A17), an NADPH: cytochrome P450 oxidoreductase (CPR), and the two lipid biosynthesis pathway genes DGA1 and LRO1 were knocked out with a homologous recombination-based approach. Constructs with a linear cassette having a nourseothricin (NAT) selection marker and 400 bp upstream and downstream homology arms of the target genes were synthesized by Genscript (Piscataway, NJ, USA). The constructs were double digested with restriction enzymes ApaI and NheI to release the linear cassette, which was transformed by electroporation and transformants were selected on NAT (200 µg/mL) plates. The NAT selection marker included loxP sites on both ends for marker recycling and a plasmid containing a CRE recombinase was transformed into knockout strains and selected on hygromycin (HYG) plates. The colonies from the HYG plate were then streaked on NAT plates to confirm loss of the marker. The verified clones were grown for 3–4 generations in YPD medium without the selection pressure to remove the HYG plasmid. This protocol was applied sequentially for each knockout.

Expression of a cytosolic TAL pathway

To perform cytosolic expression of the TAL pathway, a linear cassette containing the same promoters, terminators and genes but without the PTS tags was designed in a carrier vector and synthesized by Genscript (Piscataway, NJ, USA). The Linear cytosolic pathway construct was digested with ApaI and NheI to obtain the Linear cassette which was then transformed into strain 423B by electroporation and plated for selection on HYG plates. Colonies from plates were verified by colony PCR.

Transposon-based genome integration of the TAL pathway

We employed the PiggyBac transposon system that has been previously used in C. albicans [25] to perform an iterative genome integration of the TAL pathway. This system involves a non-replicative plasmid carrying a cassette for scarless integration of cargo DNA and is known to be advantageous to eliminate the false positives and omission of the vector removal step [26]. We constructed a cassette containing the peroxisomal TAL pathway (3 subunits of MMC enzyme and PS along with the PTS tags) using a set of promoters (pTDH1, pACT1 and pPGK1) that were recently characterized in Candida viswanathii [27]. The cassette also included a NAT selection marker and green fluorescent protein (GFP) expression module flanked by the insertion sequence (Supplementary File 2). For easy recycling of NAT markers and GFP, loxP sites were incorporated at both ends of the NAT marker-GFP module. The Cre recombinase expressing plasmid was transformed separately for Cre-loxP mediated removal of the NAT selection marker and GFP expression module. 3 μg of the TAL pathway-transposon construct was transformed into the engineered strains by electroporation and positive clones were isolated after selecting for growth on NAT-YPD plates. A similar protocol was followed for the alternative TAL pathway cassette containing acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) and PS genes. Successful integration events were confirmed by fluorescence microscopy showing GFP expression and colony PCR. The positive colonies were screened for product formation using the TAL production medium described above. The strains and DNA sequences used in this work are Listed in Supplementary File 3.

Product quantification

A previously reported reverse-phase HPLC method [28] was used to quantify TAL in an Agilent Technologies 1260 Infinity series instrument equipped with an Eclipse Plus Phenyl-Hexyl column (250 mm length, 2.6 mm diameter, 5 μm particle size) kept at 50 °C during analysis (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Two mobile phases consisting of 10 mM ammonium acetate in water supplemented with 0.07% formic acid (Solvent A) and 10 mM ammonium acetate in 90% acetonitrile supplemented with and 0.7% formic acid (Solvent B) were used, with a gradient profile as follows: 30% Solvent B, 0.5 mL/min for 12 min, 80% Solvent B, 0.5 mL/min for 0.1 min, 100% Solvent B, 0.5 mL/min for 0.5 min, 100% Solvent B, 1.0 mL/min for 0.2 min, and 30% Solvent B, 1.0 mL/min for 2.8 min. The concentration of TAL in the samples was determined by using a standard curve of analytical grade standard and a UV detector set at 280 nm.

Adaptive laboratory evolution

To perform the adaptive evolution experiment, cells from the engineered strain 423B (expressing the peroxisomal TAL pathway) were serially transferred through YNB medium containing progressively higher oleic acid and lower lactic acid concentrations as carbon sources, starting at 1% lactic acid and 0.5% oleic acid. The evolution experiment was carried out in 125 mL baffled Erlenmeyer flasks with 25 mL culture volume and a starting OD of 0.2 for each passage. The passaging was performed every 3–4 weeks or until the OD reached a value of 0.5. Glycerol stocks were prepared at the end of each passage to preserve the cell population, and the remaining cells were harvested by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm and washed with sterile water before transferring them to the next condition. Individual colonies were isolated from the mixed populations generated in each step by transferring the cells to YPD agar plates after serial dilution. These colonies were compared by inoculating them in YNB medium containing 0.2% ammonium sulfate, 1% oleic acid and 0.5% lactic acid using 48 well plates with a culture volume of 500 µL and a starting OD of 0.2. The growth profiles were captured using a plate reader (DTX 880, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) set at 30 °C with shaking for 3 days.

Intracellular proteomics and extracellular metabolomics

Sample preparation began by growing the WT, 423B and ALE strains in YNB medium with 0.2% ammonium sulfate, 1% oleic acid and 0.5% lactic acid. The wild type and ALE strains were cultivated for 24 h and the 423B strain was cultivated for 72 h due to slower growth. The cells were harvested by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm and washed twice with phosphate buffer saline before transferring them to a pre-tared tube with ~ 50 mg cells per sample. The cell pellets were preserved in 100 µL of cold methanol and stored at −80 ℃. The corresponding supernatant samples were collected from the centrifugation step for extracellular metabolomics analysis.

Sample preparation followed the previously established MPLEx protocol [29]. Cell pellets were fractionated into metabolites, proteins, and Lipids using a mixture of water, cold methanol, and chloroform. For proteomic analysis, the protein pellets were washed with ice-cold methanol and reconstituted in 8 M urea. Protein concentration was determined using the Pierce™ bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Proteins were then reduced with dithiothreitol, alkylated with iodoacetamide, and digested with trypsin (1:50 weight) at 37 °C for 3 h. After digestion, the peptide digests were cleaned using C18 solid-phase extraction (SPE), and peptide concentration was measured using the BCA assay.

Targeted proteomics was conducted using a liquid chromatography (LC) – selected reaction monitoring (SRM) approach, targeting 25 peptides from six specific proteins: (1) methylmalonyl-CoA carboxyltransferase 12 S subunit, (2) methylmalonyl-CoA carboxyltransferase 5 S subunit, (3) methylmalonyl-CoA carboxyltransferase 1.3 S subunit, (4) 2-pyrone synthase, (5) 2-pyrone synthase G2PS2, and (6) pyruvate carboxylase P474N. Peptide candidates were selected based on compatibility with LC-SRM and confirmed for specificity within the organism. For analysis, 500 ng of peptide digest was loaded onto a Waters UHPLC system equipped with a 10-cm, 100-µm i.d. C18 capillary column. The peptides were separated with 78-min gradient elution at 400 nL/min and 45 °C, using a mobile phase of 0.1% formic acid in water (phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (phase B). Mass spectrometry analysis was performed using a TSQ Altis triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) operating in SRM mode. All available y-ion transitions for each peptide were monitored, with collision energies calculated using Skyline software [30]. Data was then imported into Skyline for manual review to ensure precise peak boundary selection and exclude background noise or co-eluting interferences. Quantification was based on the area under the curve (AUC) for the selected transitions of each peptide.

For global proteomics, 500 ng of peptide digests were loaded onto a 30-cm, 75-µm i.d. in-house packed C18 column on a Thermo Dionex Ultimate 3000 nanoUPLC. Peptide separation occurred at 200 nL/min with a room-temperature gradient (0–120 min), using a mobile phase of 0.1% formic acid in water (phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (phase B). Mass spectrometry was conducted on a Q Exactive HF-X Orbitrap in data-dependent acquisition mode with TopN of 12 and dynamic exclusion of 45 s. Protein identifications were performed using MS-GF+ [31] with a 1% FDR threshold. Peptide intensities were extracted with MASIC [32] and log2-transformed, normalized to median abundance, and processed in pmartR [33]. Protein-level quantifications were generated with pmartR. Statistical analyses included Holm-adjusted two-sample t-tests for quantitative comparisons and G-tests with Holm adjustments for qualitative assessments.

The supernatant samples in 2 mL tubes were completely thawed and vortexed, then 50 µL of each was transferred to a glass vial for completely drying under the speed vacuum concentrator. The dried samples were chemically derivatized and analyzed by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry as reported previously [34]. Metabolite identification was confirmed by the in-house version of the metabolite database by matching with two dimensional parameters such as fragmented mass spectra and retention indices. The post-data visualization was done by MetaboAnalyst [35].

Results and discussion

Construction of a peroxisomal TAL pathway in C. viswanathii

Under normal conditions, acetyl-CoA is generated by β-oxidation of fatty acids in the peroxisome and transported into the mitochondria and cytosol by the carnitine shuttle, a process dependent on the enzyme carnitine acetyltransferase (Cat2). Our first strain modification approach involved increasing the availability of acetyl-CoA in the peroxisome by knocking out the CAT2 gene (Fig. 1). The deletion of both copies of CAT2 impairs the growth of this organism on fatty acids as reported previously [36] and confirmed by us (Supplementary File 1). The generated CAT2Δ/CAT2Δ strain was then used as the base strain for building a novel pathway for TAL production, which involves the targeted expression of the methylmalonyl-CoA carboxyltransferase (MMC) enzyme complex and 2-pyrone synthase (PS) in the peroxisome. Specifically, MMC catalyzes the interconversion of acetyl-CoA and oxaloacetate to pyruvate and malonyl-CoA [37], and PS catalyzes the condensation reaction between acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA to synthesize TAL.

The generated strain, named 423B, was cultivated alongside the parental wild-type (WT) strain in presence of glycerol and oleic acid, and the produced cell biomass (reported as dry cell weight, DCW) and TAL levels were measured after 3 days of cultivation. The WT strain reached a cell concentration of 6 mg/mL in medium containing 2% glycerol and 14 mg/mL when 1% oleic acid was supplemented. In contrast, strain 423B reached a cell concentration of 6 mg/mL in both conditions (Fig. 2a). The higher biomass production by the WT strain in presence of oleic acid was expected since the CAT2 deletion in 423B makes it unable to grow on oleic acid as a single carbon source. We observed up to 5 mg/L of TAL being produced by 423B while the WT strain did not produce any detectable TAL (Fig. 2b). In addition, a product stability assay was carried out in the presence of cells was carried out to verify the absence of enzymes that could cause product degradation. We confirmed that there was no conversion of TAL spiked to the culture broth (500 mg/L) after 3 days of incubation with WT or 423B strains and the compound remained highly soluble at these concentrations (Fig. 2c).

Adaptive lab evolution for improved growth on oleic acid

While the presence of TAL in cultures with the recombinant strain confirmed the functionality of the expressed pathway, the reduced growth rate and oleic acid assimilation capability in 423B are possible limiting factors to TAL production. Gain of function through ALE has been a popular strategy for strain improvement since it is known that cells under stress tend to accumulate mutations that allow them to thrive under specific conditions [38]. Therefore, we carried out an ALE experiment to develop a CAT2-deleted strain that can grow faster on oleic acid. To do so, strain 423B was sub-cultured in media with progressively higher oleic acid concentrations and decreasing concentrations of lactic acid as secondary carbon source. Individual colonies isolated from the evolved populations were screened by growing them in medium with 1% oleic acid and 0.5% lactic acid and the results are presented in Fig. 3.

Screening of colonies isolated from mixed population samples collected after adaptive laboratory evolution. Growth profiles for colony 19 (ALE), WT and 423B strains are indicated in red, green and blue color, respectively. All other tested colonies are displayed in different shades of gray

Most of the tested colonies exhibited faster growth and reached higher final optical density (OD) values than both the starting strain 423B and the WT strain under the same conditions (Fig. 3). The colony displaying the highest growth rate and final cell density in this experiment (colony 19) was selected for further studies and will be henceforth referred to as the ALE strain. This strain exhibited a specific growth rate of 0.18 h−1 compared to 0.02 h−1 for strain 423B. The highest OD obtained for ALE was 2.02 whereas the WT and 423B strains reached maximum OD values of 0.97 and 0.69, respectively. A significant reduction in the lag phase was also observed with the ALE strain (1 h) whereas the lag phase time for WT and 423B strains was approximately 4 h and 6 h.

The WT, 423B and ALE strains were further characterized by extracellular metabolomics and global and targeted proteomics. Analysis of the spent medium revealed very different metabolomic profiles for the three strains (Fig. 4). Strain 423B was found to accumulate and secrete the highest amounts of central carbon metabolism intermediates (involved in glycolysis and TCA) compared to the other strains. In contrast, ALE displayed a pattern that is more similar to WT than the pattern displayed by 423B, with relatively low accumulation of malic, fumaric, succinic, citric and pyruvic acids in WT and ALE compared to 423B.

The strains WT and ALE also consumed more oleic acid than 423B since higher amounts of oleic acid were found in cultures with 423B; however, higher consumption of lactic acid was observed only in cultivations with the WT strain (Fig. 4). This suggests that the evolution campaign mainly improved the consumption of oleic acid rather than lactic acid, as we intended, at least under the tested conditions. The metabolomics data is in agreement with the results presented in Fig. 3 and indicate that the CAT2 deletion in 423B significantly disrupted the ability to rapidly consume both oleic and lactic acids, which resulted in a less active metabolism than the WT and caused the accumulation and secretion of metabolic intermediates by 423B. Interestingly, the ALE strain produced the highest amounts of dicarboxylic acids (pimelic, adipic, glutaric), and ω-hydroxy acids (hydroxybutyric and hydroxyvaleric) which are known fatty acid-derived compounds produced by Candida tropicalis [39].

Global proteomics analysis supported the metabolomics data by revealing that several TCA cycle enzymes are upregulated in the ALE strain compared to 423B, including isocitrate lyase (2-fold increase), malate synthase (3-fold increase) an isocitrate dehydrogenase (2.8-fold increase) and malate dehydrogenase (2.2-fold increase) (Supplementary Files 4, 5 and 6). Multiple enzymes involved in Lipid metabolism were also upregulated in ALE compared to 423B such as fatty acid-CoA ligase (3.3-fold increase), acyl-CoA oxidase (3.1-fold increase), 3,2-trans-enoyl-CoA isomerase (3.0-fold increase), 2,4-dienoyl-CoA reductase (3.0-fold increase) and acetyl-CoA C-acyltransferase (2.1-fold increase). In contrast, we observed several downregulated enzymes in ALE relative to 423B that are typically associated with stress responses, including spermidine synthase (4.6-fold decrease), AMP deaminase (2.0-fold decrease), ubiquitin-like protein SMT3 (2.1-fold decrease) and heat shock protein (2.3-fold decrease).

We also performed a targeted proteomics analysis to verify the expression of the TAL pathway enzymes. As expected, all these enzymes were present in the engineered strains and not detected in the WT strain. We also found that these enzymes showed differential expression between the 423B and ALE strains (Supplementary File 7). Taken together, the results indicate that the changes obtained during the evolution campaign significantly altered the central metabolic pathways that produce or consume acetyl-CoA and contributed to faster oleic acid consumption. It should be noted that genome sequencing of the evolved strains was not performed as part of this work, which prevents us from reaching definitive conclusions about the underlying mechanisms driving the observed phenotypes. A full comparison of genomics, metabolomics and proteomics data, as well as genomic validation experiments, would greatly improve our understanding of the changes that occurred during the evolution campaign.

Additional strain engineering efforts

Upon indication that the engineered C. viswanathii strains have a strongly active ω-oxidation pathway to convert fatty acids into dicarboxylic acids, we inactivated three key ω-oxidation genes as a strategy to reduce competition for the precursors of the TAL biosynthetic pathway. These genes include CYP52A13 and CYP52A17 that encode two cytochrome P450 monooxygenases known to be upregulated in presence of oleic acid [11, 40] and a NADPH: cytochrome P450 oxidoreductase (CPR). These enzymes form a hydroxylase complex that converts fatty acids into ω-hydroxy acids as the first step in the ω-oxidation pathway. We also inactivated the genes LRO1 and DGA1 that encode enzymes that utilize malonyl-CoA for lipid biosynthesis.

When comparing the TAL production capabilities of the evolved and engineered strains in medium containing oleic acid, we found that the ALE strain produced almost 7 times more TAL than the parental 423B (Fig. 5). The modifications intended to block the competing pathways proved to be advantageous for TAL production in both genetic backgrounds, resulting in strains 423B_AA and ALE_BB which showed an additional increase in product titers up to 58 mg/L.

The strains 423B_AA and ALE_BB derive from 423B and ALE, respectively, and were obtained by inactivation of three ω-oxidation genes and two lipid biosynthesis genes. Error bars represent the standard deviation of n = 3

Interestingly, the improvement in the 423B background (9.5-fold increase) was much larger than the improvement in the ALE background (38% increase). This could be a consequence of the different partitioning of acetyl-CoA towards ω-oxidation, lipid biosynthesis, TCA, and other pathways between the two strains, as discussed in the previous section, making these improvements less impactful in the ALE background. Notably, enzymes involved in ω-oxidation reactions are upregulated in ALE compared to 423B, such as long-chain alcohol oxidase (3.5-fold), alcohol dehydrogenase (5.1-fold) and fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase (3.2-fold) (Supplementary File 4).

In a parallel effort, we explored if the copy number of the genes in the heterologous TAL pathway could be a limiting factor in the conversion process by applying two different approaches. The first approach involved a transposon-mediated gene integration, a known tool to increase the copy number of recombinant genes, using a vector called PiggyBac [41]. The PiggyBac transposon machinery enabled the multicopy integration of the peroxisomal TAL pathway into the genome of C. viswanathii by creating scarless excision events. An alternative gene expression construct for TAL biosynthesis comprising the native acetyl-CoA-carboxylase and the pyrone synthase (ACC-PS pathway) was also built, integrated and tested.

Colonies from the transformed 423B_AA and ALE_BB strains were screened for TAL production and a high variability among colonies was found. The majority of the colonies produced either similar or lower TAL concentrations than the initial strain, but we identified some colonies that produced higher concentrations of TAL (Fig. 6). The best 423B_AA-derived strains after transposon-mediated integration of either the original pathway or the ACC-PS pathway yielded 102 and 110 mg/L of TAL, respectively, more than doubling the concentrations reached by the parent strain. No improvement in TAL production was found in transformants derived from strain ALE_BB, which may be due to differences at the genomic and metabolic level between the two backgrounds and a relatively smaller number of screened transformants (Supplementary File 8). However, these results should be interpreted with caution since the copy numbers of TAL pathway genes in the transformed strains were not determined. While we focused on screening for changes in product concentrations due to the use of different biosynthetic pathways, strain backgrounds and gene expression methods, we acknowledge that a deeper characterization of the generated library of transformants could help identify opportunities for further strain improvement.

Comparison of TAL production in 423B_AA-derived strains after transposon mediated pathway integration. a Strains transformed with the original TAL pathway; b strains transformed with the alternative ACC-PS pathway

The second approach involved the cytosolic expression of the TAL pathway in addition to the peroxisomal pathway already present in strain 423B and the use of rich medium to provide additional metabolic precursors. The goal was to investigate whether the compartmentalization of enzymes in the peroxisome and potential metabolic limitations could be restricting product titers. Dual cytoplasmic-peroxisomal engineering has proven beneficial to increase titers and yields of non-native bioproducts in yeast through optimization of the availability of acetyl-CoA [42, 43].

The performance of the generated strain was evaluated in YPD rich medium supplemented with oleic acid and sodium acetate, in an attempt to increase the cytosolic acetyl-CoA pool. It has been demonstrated that the supplementation of acetate can lead to higher TAL titers in S. cerevisiae since this molecule can be internalized and directly converted to acetyl-CoA by acetyl-CoA synthase [44]. Our results indicate a strong effect of the cytosolic expression on TAL titers in all medium conditions, and a clear advantage of feeding oleic acid and sodium acetate, resulting in up to 284 mg/L of TAL (Fig. 7). Notably, this enhancement did not occur at the same level in strains expressing only the peroxisomal pathway, indicating that there is a restriction in the peroxisomal availability of acetyl-CoA that cannot be restored by the metabolism of sugars or amino acids present in the rich medium or by directly feeding acetate.

Several insights can be derived from the combined strain engineering results obtained in this work. Although the peroxisomal pathway proved to be functional and an adaptive evolution campaign generated a strain with better performance metrics (which consumes more oleic acid and produces more TAL), further metabolic optimization will be necessary to substantially increase the production to grams per Liter levels. We predict that an imbalance in the peroxisomal pool of pyruvate, oxaloacetate or other key metabolic intermediates is restricting the conversion of oleic acid to TAL in 423B and ALE strains. This is supported by the significant improvements in product titers when the enzymes were expressed in the cytosol and the lack of improvement observed when the strain with only peroxisomal expression was supplemented with glucose and rich media components. Although the generation of an ALE strain was beneficial for TAL production compared to the original CAT2-deleted strain, the expression of a cytosolic TAL pathway in a non-evolved strain and the supplementation of acetate resulted in higher product concentrations. This suggests that the CAT2 deletion is causing multiple metabolic effects that stem from the fact that C. viswanathii has evolved to be dependent on the β-oxidation pathway to assimilate fatty acids as carbon sources. Even if the ALE campaign restored the ability to consume oleic acid, it is possible that the peroxisomal metabolism remains disrupted in CAT2-deleted strains and does not adequately support the high-level production of TAL under the conditions tested in this work. Nevertheless, the observed enhancements to TAL production when presumably increasing the number of peroxisome-located enzymes, when expressing an alternative ACC-PS pathway, and when removing competing reactions indicates that many avenues for improvement still exist. For example, the combined expression of cytosolic and peroxisomal TAL pathways using ALE_BB as the host and the use of engineered strains with a functional or partially attenuated CAT2 gene could be interesting aspects to pursue in future efforts.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the use of a peroxisomal pathway for TAL production and provides the first example of the production of this compound from oleic acid using engineered and evolved C. viswanathii strains. We found evidence that the evolved strains have more active glycolytic and TCA cycle pathways than the parental 423B, resulting in higher TAL production but also accumulation and secretion of many organic acids and metabolic intermediates. Further strain engineering approaches such as blocking competing pathways, performing additional gene transformation campaigns and introducing cytosolic expression of the biosynthetic enzymes resulted in up to 50-fold enhancement in TAL titers. This work highlights the potential of C. viswanathii as a novel microbial conversion host for valorization of fatty acid-containing streams.
