Digital measurement of pupil size, corneal size, and eccentricity in Guinea pigs using python compared with traditional OCT
Authors: Hao Wu, Zhongbao Qiao, Chi Cheng, Wenting Luo, Ting Wan, Na Lu, Tong Qiao, Yue Di
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo develop a Python-based digital technique for accurate measurement of pupil size, corneal size, and eccentricity in guinea pigs, and to validate its efficiency and accuracy against traditional OCT methods in ophthalmology research. Methods: Fourteen healthy guinea pigs were selected, and the eye images were captured using a camera, and the image analysis program was written by Python 3.9. The program integrated edge detection (Canny algorithm), curve fitting (conic curve equation) and pixel-actual distance conversion modules, and designed a graphical user interface (GUI) to visualize the operation. Measured parameters included pupil size, corneal size and eccentricity to compare the difference between in vivo and ex vivo measurements and to verify the accuracy of the method. Results: The Python program clearly identified the guinea pig pupil and corneal limbus, and the fitted curves were in high agreement with the actual contours. Pupil and corneal size measured with an accuracy of 0.01 mm. In vivo and in vitro measurements showed that pupil size increased significantly after ex vivo (p < 0.001), whereas there was no significant difference in eccentricity (p = 0.38). Compared to optical coherence tomography (OCT), the method did not differ significantly in accuracy (p > 0.05), but significantly improved efficiency and reduced reliance on specialized equipment. Conclusion: The Python-based digital measurement technique can effectively and accurately quantify the morphological parameters of the guinea pig eye, providing reliable technical support for non-contact measurements in experimental animals. Keywords: Guinea pig, Pupil size, Cornea size, PythonSubject terms: Medical research, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The guinea pig has become an important animal model for the study of visual development, myopia mechanisms and ocular diseases due to its eye anatomy highly similar to that of humans, inexpensive and easy to manipulate1–3. As key structures of the eye, the pupil and cornea have morphological parameters (e.g., size, eccentricity) that are directly involved in photoregulation, refractive state maintenance, and visual signaling. However, the progression of refractive development in the guinea pig eye has not been adequately studied4, while the dynamic adjustability of the pupil diameter can reflect the functional status of the visual system5. However, the guinea pig cornea is very steep and morphologically complex, which makes it difficult to accurately quantify its subtle variations through conventional measurements, and relatively little research has been conducted in this area. Therefore, the development of high-precision, non-contact measurement techniques is of great significance in revealing the mechanisms of guinea pig visual development and promoting experimental ophthalmic research.

Auto Refractometer (KR-800), optical biometer (IOL MASTER 500), and optical coherence tomography (OCT) can be used to measure human pupil size and corneal size in the clinic6–8, but face challenges in small animal studies: The Auto Refractometer can only measure with an accuracy of 0.25 mm (Fig. 1B), which is not very reliable. Since the Optical Biometer is designed and manufactured based on the size of a human eyeball and the guinea pig eyeball is one-third the size of a human eyeball (Fig. 1A), it is difficult for the Optical Biometer to recognize the guinea pig eyeball to complete the measurement. OCT is a better tool for measuring guinea pig pupil size and corneal size, but it also has some drawbacks. For example, it cannot automatically recognize the guinea pig pupil and corneal limbus, and the operator needs to take the points manually (Fig. 1C), and the results are greatly influenced by the operator’s subjectivity. And its high cost and high operation difficulty also limit the use of researchers. Meanwhile, all three methods need to be considered to be disturbed by motion artifacts and dark-colored periocular hair9–11.

To address the above problems, this study proposes a digital measurement system based on Python programming (Fig. 2A-B). Python is a high-level, interpreted, general-purpose programming language known for its clean, easy-to-read syntax that emphasizes readability and simplicity of code, enabling developers to express complex concepts in less code. This system has the following advantages: (1) It can avoid eye compression during measurement, which can ensure data credibility. (2) It integrates edge detection (Canny algorithm) and curve fitting (conic equation), which can accurately extract complex contours. (3) It supports batch processing through the graphical user interface (GUI) pioneered by Python, which greatly improves the efficiency of data collection. (4) It combines SciPy, OpenCV and other libraries to flexibly optimize algorithms or integrate machine learning modules.

The team previously used MATLAB to implement edge detection and curve fitting for guinea pig corneal curvature and eccentricity12, but there are problems such as complicated operation and reliance on high-contrast images. For example, MATLAB needs to manually write code to remove background noise and is ineffective in recognizing the edge of the eye surrounded by black hair13. Based on this, this study turned to the Python platform to develop an interactive analysis tool, which significantly improved the universality and ease of use of the method by combining manual coordinate point acquisition with automated fitting.

Based on the previous study, this study aims to further explore the use of Python programming techniques to achieve accurate measurements of pupil size, corneal size and eccentricity in guinea pigs. In this study, pupil, corneal size, and eccentricity of guinea pigs from 2 to 12 weeks of age were measured. Comparison of results with OCT measurements to validate method reliability. In addition, we observed the pupil size of guinea pigs under different light intensities and the differences in eye parameters between guinea pigs in vivo and in vitro to study the accommodation of guinea pigs’ eyes.

Results

Pupil and corneal size of guinea pigs measured by Python program, Auto Refractometer and OCT methods

Pupil size in guinea pigs increased from 3.65 ± 0.08 mm at 2 weeks of age to 5.63 ± 0.07 mm at 12 weeks of age, and corneal size increased from 5.23 ± 0.14 mm to 8.06 ± 0.12 mm, as measured using Python. Pupil size in guinea pigs increased from 3.68 ± 0.12 mm at 2 weeks of age to 5.61 ± 0.13 mm at 12 weeks of age, and corneal size increased from 5.25 ± 0.17 mm to 8.04 ± 0.09 mm, as measured using an Auto Refractometer. Given that the data obtained from the Auto Refractometer exhibits a limited precision of 0.25 mm, the subsequent statistical analysis may lack substantive significance. Pupil size in guinea pigs increased from 3.65 ± 0.07 mm at 2 weeks of age to 5.63 ± 0.08 mm at 12 weeks of age, and corneal size increased from 5.21 ± 0.12 mm to 8.05 ± 0.10 mm, as measured using OCT. Pupil size and corneal size of guinea pigs at all weeks of age showed no significant differences when comparing the measurements of the two methods using a paired samples t-test (p > 0.05, Fig. 3A-B; Table 1).

Changes in pupil size of guinea pigs under three light intensities

After light stimulation, the pupil size of both 2-week-old and 12-week-old guinea pigs significantly decreased (p < 0.001, Fig. 4F). A negative correlation was observed between light intensity and pupil size (p < 0.001). Under 100 lx light, the pupil size of 2-week-old guinea pigs decreased to 3.28 ± 0.09 mm, and that of 12-week-old guinea pigs decreased to 5.04 ± 0.1 mm; under 250 lx light, the pupil size of 2-week-old guinea pigs decreased to 2.82 ± 0.1 mm, and that of 12-week-old guinea pigs decreased to 3.96 ± 0.16 mm. The pupil size of guinea pigs was reduced to 2.18 ± 0.08 mm at 2 weeks of age and 2.28 ± 0.09 mm at 12 weeks of age under 500 lx illumination. The reduction in pupil size in 12-week-old guinea pigs was greater than that in 2-week-old guinea pigs (p < 0.05).

Changes in pupil and corneal size and eccentricity of the guinea pig eye in vivo and in vitro

In vivo, there were no significant differences between pupil and corneal eccentricity in guinea pigs (Paired samples t-test, t = −0.48, p = 0.64, Fig. 4A). However, after removal of the eyes, significant differences were found in the pupil and corneal eccentricity in vitro (Paired samples t-test, t = −2.90, p = 0.01, Fig. 4B). Furthermore, the pupil eccentricity in vitro was significantly reduced (Paired samples t-test, t = 4.96, p < 0.001, Fig. 4C), while the corneal eccentricity showed no significant change (Paired samples t-test, t = 0.91, p = 0.38, Fig. 4D). Additionally, it was observed that the pupil size of the guinea pig eyes significantly increased in vitro (p < 0.001, Fig. 4E).

Discussion

Ophthalmic OCT image segmentation algorithms have made significant progress in recent years. Traditional procedural segmentation methods are being replaced by deep learning methods, which have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance in AS-OCT image analysis14-16. In this study, OCT B-scan mode was used, and the results showed that the scanned images could better demonstrate the structure of each part of the anterior segment of the eye. The Python-based digitizing measurement system developed in this study significantly outperforms conventional techniques by integrating edge detection, curve fitting, and pixel-to-actual distance conversion modules. In contrast to optical coherence tomography (OCT), which requires manual labeling of critical points17 and relies on expensive hardware equipment, our method automatically extracts contours through the Canny algorithm and conic curve fitting, achieving comparable accuracy to OCT while attenuating the influence of operator subjectivity on the results. In addition, the method overcomes the limitations of Auto Refractometer and optical biometer, which are designed for the human eye and have insufficient accuracy in small animal studies due to scale mismatch.

A key advantage of the Python program over OCT is its operational efficiency and cost-effectiveness. While OCT remains the gold standard for high-resolution imaging18-20, its reliance on manual parameter labeling and high equipment costs limit its application in large-scale animal studies. Our method enables batch processing through a user-friendly graphical interface (GUI), which greatly improves work efficiency, and requires only a common camera to complete the measurements, making it accessible to laboratories without advanced imaging equipment. In addition, the non-contact design avoids physical contact, reduces motion artifacts, ensures data integrity, and solves a long-standing problem in small animal ophthalmic research.

The present method successfully captures an often overlooked anatomical feature in the guinea pig eye by means of pupil and corneal circle fitting algorithms: a small offset of the pupil center from the corneal geometric center. Measuring this offset often requires equipment with high precision, such as OCT21 and slit-scanning topographers22. Our method achieves similar measurements at a lower cost. Using Python’s SciPy optimization module, we fit a least-squares circle to the pupil and corneal contours separately, accurately calculate the coordinates of their centers, and combined with a pixel-to-actual distance transformation. This offset suggests that there is an asymmetry between the optical center and the anatomical center of the guinea pig eye, which may be related to its wide-field visual adaptation strategy23, and provides a new morphological basis for the subsequent study of its visual signaling mechanism.

Our study validated the pupillary light reflex in guinea pigs Pupil size decreased significantly at high light intensities, and 12-week-old guinea pigs showed greater light sensitivity than younger guinea pigs, suggesting developmental maturation of the iris muscle. Notably, in vitro measurements showed a significant increase in pupil size, which may be due to the loss of parasympathetic tension input24,25, while corneal eccentricity remained stable.These observations are consistent with the guinea pig’s role as a model of human regulatory dysfunction.

Although the present study demonstrated a powerful ability to quantify 2D parameters, some limitations remain. First, the projection-dependent 2D profile cannot analyze the 3D aspheric properties of the cornea. Pérez-Merino. et al.26 investigated a 3-D OCT technique that allows the assessment of the complete 3-D preoptic segment shape and further explores the sources of optical aberration, thus allowing the correlation of morphological and optical quantitative eye modeling in animal models of myopia. However, it focuses more on model construction than data measurement, and in the future, we will combine the 3D data from OCT for a more comprehensive measurement of guinea pig eye parameters. Second, slight head movements occasionally cause image blurring. Finally, current algorithms assume a circular/elliptical geometry, which may oversimplify irregular corneal contours. Future work will explore deep learning-based contour recognition and multimodal data fusion to address these challenges, ultimately advancing the standardized application of this tool in experimental and clinical settings.

The Python-based digital measurement system developed in this study realized the automated, high-precision (accuracy up to 0.01 mm) non-contact measurement of guinea pig pupil size, corneal size, and eccentricity, and significantly improved the efficiency and reduced the equipment dependence by edge detection and batch processing. The system successfully captured the dynamic light response properties of guinea pig pupils, revealing age-dependent photosensitivity. The potential for its application in human models of regulatory disorders is supported. In the future, the design of refractive surgery parameters can be further optimized by integrating the OCT 3D data and real-time analysis module, providing an efficient and standardized tool for experimental and clinical studies.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

All experiments in this study were conducted in full compliance with ARRIVE guidelines. Ethical approval was granted by the Laboratory Animal Management and Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Clinical Centre for Public Health (No. 2022-A009-01). All experiments in this study were conducted in full compliance with the relevant provisions of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) guidelines.

Experimental animals

Fourteen healthy male and female guinea pigs from Danyang City Yichang Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd (SCXK 2021-0002) were housed in a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle. All animals underwent slit-lamp (YZ3, 66 Vision-Tech, China) examinations, and no clinically observable ocular surface diseases were detected. All guinea pigs were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of sodium pentobarbital.

Experimental procedure

Every two weeks, pupil size, corneal size, and eccentricity were measured in all guinea pigs until they reached 12 weeks of age. In addition, the left eyes of guinea pigs were exposed to light intensities of 100 lx, 250 lx, and 500 lx, and the pupillary responses of 2-week-old and 12-week-old guinea pigs to different light intensities were observed. All measurements were performed between 9:00 and 11:00 AM. For each guinea pig, only the left eye was measured. During each measurement, ensure that the guinea pig’s pupil and corneal limbus are fully exposed. All measurements were made under a 50 lx lighting condition to minimize the effect of light on pupil size, with a light meter (DLY-1801 C, DELIXI, China) used to measure the illumination level at the guinea pig’s eye level. After completing the in vivo measurements of the guinea pig eyeballs, the guinea pigs were euthanized with sodium pentobarbital, and the eyeballs were carefully removed and the surrounding fascial and muscular tissues were cleared to ensure that the eyeballs were not punctured in a way that would alter their morphology. The eyeballs were then placed at the center of the platform, and a top-down camera (T201, NETUM, China) was used to capture images of the coronal eye contour to measure the biological parameters of the guinea pig eyes in vitro.

Data measurement

In previous studies, we developed a method for biometric measurement of experimental animal eyes in vitro, utilizing Python 3.9 programming software27 and a camera. We wrote the code in PyCharm Community Edition 2021.1.3 × 64 (Fig. 2A), and imported the Tkinter module to create a graphical user interface (GUI) window (Fig. 2B). The program was packaged into an executable external file along with an installation module to enable visual human-computer interaction. This program was specifically designed for small animal eye measurements and includes features such as image import, line color and thickness customization, conversion coefficient calculation, temporary recording modules, manual point selection for edge coordinates, and curve fitting modules. Finally, the operator can export the generated images’ scale using a designated button to facilitate the calculation of large datasets.

The guinea pig was placed on a specially designed fixture, and gentle handling was applied to keep the animal calm. High-definition cameras (13Pro Max, iPhone, US) were used to capture clear and steady images of the guinea pig’s eyes (Fig. 5B). Colorful objects and sounds were used to direct the guinea pig’s gaze to the left or right, and images were taken when the eye movement was most pronounced, ensuring that both the pupil and corneal edges were subjectively identified. A scale with actual distance reference was fixed in the same plane, and both the left and right eyes were photographed. The images were then input into the compiled program, and the matrix plot module was used to open the image (code: image = Image.open(path + pic, ‘r’)). This process ensured that each point in the image corresponds to a two-dimensional coordinate (x, y).

Pixel-actual distance conversion method

The captured image was imported into Python programming software. The imported image corresponds to a 2D coordinate system with horizontal and vertical coordinates of the pixel length and width values of the image, with each point having a corresponding pixel-valued coordinate. The coordinates in the two-dimensional image are obtained using the “ginput” function. Using geometric principles (Formula: Lmn=), the distance between any two points in the coordinate system, (m1, n1) and (m2, n2), can be calculated. After obtaining the corresponding pixel values for the actual distance in the central region, the actual distance between any two points in the image can be derived through conversion. By repeatedly selecting points on the scale and calculating the average, an accurate conversion factor can be obtained (Fig. 5D).

Eyeballs edge data acquisition

The Canny edge detection algorithm was used to obtain a double thresholded image (Fig. 5A). Points with sharp changes in image brightness were organized into a set of curved segments called edges. The non-target edges are removed and only the eye edges are retained. The image is saved. Use the find Contours function to get information about the coordinates of this edge. When edge detection is not effective, coordinates can also be obtained by manually taking points on the target contour. The data is then saved in a.txt file.

Circle fitting to calculate pupil and corneal size

Assuming that the center of the pupil or cornea forms a circle, circle fitting is applied (Fig. 5B). By using the conversion factor, the actual diameter of the circle can be obtained, which further allows for the calculation of the pupil and corneal area, as reported in previous studies12.

Ellipse fitting fit calculates eccentricity

The pupil and corneal contour resembled an inclined elliptical shape (Fig. 5C). This shape fits well to the conic equation (Ax² + Bxy + Cy² + Dx + Ey + F = 0). Based on the geometry and the least squares principle, the eccentricity of the cornea can then be calculated.

Measurement of guinea pig pupil and corneal size using the Auto Refractometer

The guinea pig was tranquilized, kept quiet and fixed in the detection position of the Auto Refractometer (KR-800, Topcon, Japan). Another operator operates the Auto Refractometer and switches the instrument mode to pupil mode. The parameters and position of the instrument were adjusted so that the image of the guinea pig’s eye was centered in the viewfinder frame, and then photographed after the image had reached its clearest state. After taking the picture, the measuring scale in the image is carefully aligned with the edge of the guinea pig’s pupil or cornea, and then the Auto Refractometer will automatically measure the size of the guinea pig’s pupil or cornea.

Measurement of guinea pig pupil and corneal size using OCT

The guinea pigs were tranquilized by an operator, kept quiet, and immobilized in the detection position of the OCT device (YG-20 W MAX, TowardPi, China). Ensure that it does not move during the inspection process to maintain the stability of the inspection environment. The other operator operates the OCT device, sets the detection mode to anterior segment mode, and installs the anterior segment adapter. The parameters of the device are adjusted so that the guinea pig eye image is centered and in high definition. Images in which the guinea pig pupil and corneal structures were fully exposed were defined as standard images. After image acquisition, the corneal limbus (corneo-scleral transition zone), the pupil margin, and the geometric center were marked manually using the accompanying analysis software, and the values of each parameter were calculated. To ensure the reliability of the data, each measurement was repeated three times by two independent operators, and the mean values were taken for subsequent analysis.

Statistical analysis

The data were imported into Python 3.9 for statistical analysis and graphing. All data in this study, unless otherwise stated, are mean ± standard deviation. ANOVA was used to compare pupil size at different light intensities, and paired-samples t-tests were used to compare differences between Python and OCT measurements. Differences between any two parameters were defined as significant at p < 0.05 and highly significant at p < 0.001.
