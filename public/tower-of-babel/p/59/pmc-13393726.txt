Deep care: a qualitative analysis of black community midwifery
Authors: Kaytura Felix, Chinmayee Balachandra, Alejandro McGhee, Jay Mawuli, Keshia Pollack Porter, Dána-Ain Davis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBlack community midwifery has historically offered holistic, culturally grounded care for Black birthing people, yet remains marginalized within modern maternity systems. As disparities in Black maternal health outcomes persist, this qualitative study explored the values, services, and impacts of Black community midwifery across multiple U.S. regions.MethodsUsing in-depth interviews with five midwives and 53 clients, family members, providers, and community stakeholders, we conducted inductive and deductive coding to identify core themes.ResultsAnalysis revealed 11 primary midwifery services—including family integration, social-emotional support, and dietary counseling—and five guiding values: deep care, patient autonomy, patient safety, lived experience, and spirituality. Deep care, a model that integrates clinical, emotional, cultural, and spiritual dimensions, emerged as the most persistent and defining value across participant narratives. Midwives were described as educators, advocates, and system navigators who fostered client autonomy while balancing clinical safety. Family and spiritual integration were critical components of care, particularly during birth and pregnancy loss. Clients reported greater self-efficacy, trust, and emotional wellbeing compared to conventional healthcare settings.ConclusionOur findings underscore Black community midwifery as a vital strategy for advancing maternal health equity. We theorize that deep care extends beyond contemporary frameworks in the care scholarship, being informed by history, spirituality, and justice. We recommend expanded investment in midwifery education, community-based birth infrastructure, and supportive policy reforms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-026-01961-9.

Background

Midwifery, one of the world’s oldest professions, has held a storied importance for the Black community in the United States. Oral history denotes that the first traditional Black midwife–known later as a “grand midwife” likely arrived as early as 1619 [1]. These midwives utilized a traditional skillset to serve enslaved Black women, poor white women and plantation mistresses in the Antebellum South [2]. For many rural Southern communities, these Black midwives were frequently the only source of pre-, post- and labor care and carried a vast wealth of knowledge and experience [3]. Importantly, the role of Black midwives extended far beyond labor and delivery. These midwives provided “care, nurturance and empowerment to children, other women and their families,” through spiritual community building, ancestral knowledge, and technical skill [3].

Prior to the 1940s, Black midwives attended up to 75% of births in their communities [4]. The advent of modern obstetrics brought an institutional deluge upon grand midwives, who were displaced from their practices over the next several decades [4, 5]. Predominantly white, male obstetricians claimed that grand midwives were “ignorant, untrained, incompetent women,” and insisted on the safety and necessity of universal in-hospital birth [5]. Black midwifery was further marginalized by policy, e.g., the passage of the 1921 Shepphard-Towner Infancy and Protection Act required all midwives to obtain state licensure [4]. This new law imposed significant barriers to traditional Black midwifery overnight and paved the way for the current underrepresentation of modern Black midwives–who make up less than 7% of licensed midwives [6].

Today, while most midwives are certified nurse-midwives (CNMs) in hospital practice, community midwives remain a crucial support for Black birthing people seeking birth experiences out-of-hospital. Community midwives exist in many forms–certified professional midwives (CPMs), certified midwives (CMs), direct-entry midwives, and lay midwives [7]. In the midst of the Black perinatal health crisis and harm sustained within the medical system, Black birthing people and their families are increasingly seeking alternative childbirth experiences that prioritize emotional and physical safety and care [8, 9]. This resurgence of Black midwifery has reanimated these traditions, situating midwives at the forefront of birth justice movements and community health innovation.

We conducted a large scale in-depth qualitative study on Black community midwifery built on previous study findings with community leaders. These experts heralded Black community midwifery as an effective strategy to address the Black maternal health crisis [10]. Recent work on Black-led birth centers highlights this premise, framing these community models as justice-oriented responses to inequitable maternity care [11]. These models uplift community-driven values of safety, love, trust, and justice that operationalize a Black feminist ethic of care [12]. Our study explores how these contemporary Black midwives practice what we term “deep care.” Put simply, deep care is love. It allows for emotional, spiritual, political, and relational engagement with care recipients. Political theorists have established care as a multidimensional moral and political practice that involves where one person engages another to address the latter’s needs. This care literature has also identified the qualities of attentiveness, responsiveness, and respect as essential to the effectiveness of this practice [13, 14]. Indigenous and Black feminist scholars have reconceptualized care both survival strategy and moral response to systemic neglect. It echoes a tradition that views care as a disciplined, political, and loving practice that sustains communities under oppression [15, 16].

Our study sought to describe the distinctive features of this care and to understand the type of support and services that Black midwives provide, their experience providing that care, and its impact on them and clients, clients’ family members, and the community. We also explored the values that midwives carry into their practice and how those inform their work.

Given that Black community midwifery is portrayed as an effective strategy to address the Black maternal health crisis, this paper seeks to describe the provision of care by Black midwives to Black clients and investigate how this care is provided. Specifically, our paper aims to: (1) examine the kinds of services that Black midwives provide and which services are most valued by clients; and (2) explore the values that midwives draw upon to deliver such care and how these values are reflected in their personal narratives. It also focuses on the practice of deep care—a form of caregiving rooted in love defined by trust, commitment, and respect [17]. Deep care captures how Black midwives create conditions for safety and belonging through relationships that affirm the worth and humanity of birthing people.

Methods

Research design

This is a multiple case study of Black community midwifery. Our methodological orientation was grounded in what Dána-Ain Davis describes as Black feminist ethnography- a research practice which pays close attention to gender, race, and power dynamics which deeply impact Black women and birthing peoples’ lives [18]. Following this framework, our study sought to be holistic, attending to all the people involved in the field of community midwifery and foregrounding the expertise of Black midwives themselves. We used open-ended interviewing techniques that allowed midwives to guide the telling of their own stories and did not restrict participation by credential or certification. Additionally, when we realized that low-income clients were missing from the clients’ voices present, we revised our sample to include them, reflecting an iterative and reflexive commitment consistent with Black feminist ethnographic principles [18]. Below, we first describe the overall design of the study and then we describe the design of the sub-study.

Study oversight

The study team established a group of five experts on Black maternal care to act as a study advisory group. The advisory group included an anthropologist researcher, a community midwife with more than thirty years of community-based practice, an obstetrician-gynecologist who chairs a rural hospital OB department and leads hospital-based programming to improve Black perinatal care, an artist, and a public health leader of a West coast program to address Black maternal mortality.

Setting

The study was conducted in cities located in the South, Northeast, Midwest, West, and Pacific West of the United States. For anonymity, states are not named in this current study.

Study sampling

The study included Black community midwives as case participants and the midwives’ clients, clients’ families, collaborating providers, and community stakeholders as key informants.

Inclusion criteria

To be eligible for inclusion in the study, midwives had to self-identify as Black or African American; be at least 18 years of age; be currently practicing within one of the include U.S. states; have a current midwifery license and certificate or was recognized as a traditional midwife; have practiced for at least five years; have clientele that were at least 40% Black; attend to at least 50% of their delivers in the home or in a birth center; had to be willing to provide 10–13 referrals who would serve as key informants; and consent to be audio recorded.

Additionally, client key informants were eligible for inclusion in the study if they identified as Black or African American, had received midwifery care from the case midwife within the previous 3 years. Other key informants were eligible if they were generally knowledgeable of the midwife’s practice or the care a client-key informant had received.

Recruiting of participants, screening of midwife, screening of key informants

We utilized purposeful and snowball sampling for recruitment. First, we recruited midwives primarily through Sista Midwife Directory. Sista Midwife Directory is currently the largest online directory of Black birth workers. The study advisory group also assisted with recruitment. The research team used the eligibility criteria to screen midwives over Zoom.

Key informants were identified during the midwife screening process, where each midwife was asked to provide name and contact information of up to 6 clients, 3 collaborating providers, and 4 community stakeholders. Collaborating providers included were defined as clinicians with whom the midwives worked to provide care to clients in the community. They included midwifery students, massage therapists, imaging technicians, holistic care providers, obstetricians, and other service providers. Community stakeholders are described as nonclinical individuals and organizations that provide direct or indirect support for the midwife’s work in their community.

Consent process

All study participants provided consent to participate in the research as well as to be audio recorded. We used a two-step consent process for key informant interviews. First, the midwife obtained permission for the midwife shared the names of the referrals with the study team. The study team then approached the referral to obtain consent to participate in the study.

Data collection: interviews, observations, and survey

Data collection began in February 2024 and concluded in September 2024. For this study, the team developed interview guides for midwife and key informant interviews (see Additional files 1 and 2). Midwife interviews were framed with open-ended questions and conducted in person by the principal investigator, KF. These sessions were approximately 60–90 min with a total interview time of around 3 h. The use of ethnographic interviewing techniques and minimized interview scripts was employed to build rapport and encourage a Midwife-led conversation. Observations were also made of midwife-client visits by the principal investigator, with one group visit, and multiple one-on-one visits observed. Key informant interviews were conducted using a semi-structured interview guide and conducted over Zoom by all data collection team members, including KF, JM and AM. These interviews lasted for up to 2 h.

The research team conducted a survey of the client key informant immediately before the client’s interview using the MOR & MADM scale. The Mothers Autonomy in Decision Making (MADM) scale is utilized to rate the level of agency and autonomy that a person experiences when participating in decision making conversations with a maternity provider [19]. The MOR scale, Mothers on Respect index, assesses respectful patient-provider interactions. These are both Likert scales. While the observations informed our understanding of community midwifery care, the analysis for this study was limited to case participant and key informant interview data.

Developing codebook based on transcripts

The interviews were first transcribed using Transcribe Me, a human-augmented automated transcription service. The research team read and memo-ed the transcripts iteratively and then defined an initial set of codes that were based on the interview guide. The codebook included deductive codes from the interview guide and inductive codes that emerged from the memos. Thematic analysis was conducted using Dedoose software. Each team member independently coded the same subset of transcripts, and then the analysis team reviewed and discussed their coding processes, decisions, and understanding of the code definitions. This process occurred from June 2024 to February 2025 to achieve conceptual clarity on each code and build consensus and agreement between coders. Once the team reached consensus on coding, independent coding of all transcripts was completed by two members of the data analysis team.

Our paper focuses on the primary codes, Midwifery Services and Values of Midwives, and their subcodes. Midwifery Services was defined as “the specific services that midwives provide to the clients in the community or in the hospital both one-on-one and within group settings.” Values of Midwives was defined as “the values that inform why these people became midwives and guide how they care for their clients.” The associated interview excerpts were extracted from Dedoose. We assessed the prevalence of certain codes across transcripts, as well as the persistence of codes within transcripts. Prevalence is defined as the frequency of codes across transcripts, and persistence is defined as the frequency and depth to which a code is discussed within a transcript. The research team conducted regular analysis meetings where they conducted close readings of the excerpts and identified recurring themes, major points, and overarching patterns. Illustrative quotes from a variety of interviewees were selected to highlight these themes. Quotes are attributed to gendered pseudonyms in this paper.

Results

The age ranges of interviewees were from 26 to 84 years of age. Additionally, there was a diverse representation of religion and spirituality across the interviewees, with interviewees identifying as Catholic, Christian, Muslim, Hoodoo, Ifa, Mormon, Pentecostal, Baptist, and Land-Based faith traditions. Per the study criteria, all case midwives and clients identified as Black or African American, and other participant groups included white, multiracial, and Black interviewees. Of note, while gender-inclusive language was prioritized in our discussions of birthing people and their partners, all interviewed clients identified as cisgender and heterosexual. Below we first describe the midwifery services and values that interviewees described, and we discuss four major archetypes of midwifery care that emerged from our analysis.

Midwifery services

We identified 11 distinct services provided by midwives. They included the following: educating and integrating the family unit, social emotional support, dietary and nutrition counseling, client advocacy, providing access to social networks, childbirth education, medical tests, physical activity and counseling, community education, spiritual support, and physical examination. Overall, the top three mentioned midwifery services were educating and integrating the family unit, social emotional support/counseling, and dietary and nutrition counseling. These services were also persistent within client interviews and were noted to be foundational to the client’s perception of what their midwives did for them over the course of their birth journey. Clients often spoke at length about these aspects of the midwives’ care and provided personal, intimate details about how this impacted their experience.

Values of midwives

We identified 4 primary values or principles that guide midwifery care–deep care, patient autonomy, patient/client safety, and spirituality. Interviewees report on midwifery values in 448 excerpts, with 147 from midwives, 126 from clients, 70 from collaborating providers, 36 from community stakeholders, 19 from midwife families, and 17 from clients’ families. Deep care was mentioned within all participant groups and reflects the foundational value of midwifery care. Client autonomy and safety were the next most prevalent values, with client safety more prevalent within midwife interviews and client autonomy within client narratives. Although spirituality was coded the least within Values of Midwives, it is notably persistent in midwife narratives. Further analysis revealed the following four distinct yet interrelated, overarching themes.

Midwifery as a conscious, love-centered clinical practice

Community midwives are intentional about the values—deep care, client autonomy, safety, and spirituality–they bring to their practice. Community midwives seemed willing to extend and perhaps stretch themselves in service of their clients’ health and wellbeing, deep care was frequently contrasted with the impersonal and often harmful experiences clients had within conventional healthcare systems. Several clients described midwifery care as loving, “feeling like family,” and the sense that midwives were genuinely invested in their emotional, physical, and spiritual wellbeing.Mariah (client), describing her experience of care: “It meant the world to me. It meant so much to me because I felt like I was treated like [myself]….She never rushed my appointment. So, of course, I’m a first-time mom. I had a million questions, and she would be like, “Okay. You want to have the last appointment? That way, I can have a longer time to talk to you?“…it meant so much to me. It made me more comfortable, made me more trustworthy towards her…I felt loved. And I know you don’t feel that a lot by your provider, but literally, I felt loved by her.”

Informed consent was further reflected in practices described within the interviews, such as asking for permission before physical exams, individualized assessments for home birth eligibility, and careful postpartum practices like placental examination.

Midwifery as education, advocacy, and system navigation

Participants described midwives as not only clinicians but also educators and advocates who prepare clients and families to navigate institutional systems. This included childbirth education, nutrition counseling, strategies for advocating within hospitals, and broader efforts to demystify midwifery through community education. Dietary and nutrition counseling was a prevalent service that participants noted, and a fundamental part of Black midwifery care. For example, midwives discussed their approaches to glucose testing and the risks of gestational diabetes, while also balancing client autonomy and intuition:

Maya (midwife): “If a mom is going to tell me, “Hey, I feel this.” I’m not going to take that for granted because women are so intuitive during their pregnancy… Maybe we can explore it a little bit more. Maybe she may not want a certain thing in her pregnancy…I’m okay with that…because some people do not want to do that glucose test. But I also want you to recognize what are the issues with gestational diabetes. Do you really trust that your diet of what you’re eating is low sugar and high protein, healthy fats, and things like that, fruits and vegetables?”

emotional safety. Midwifery as a spiritual, ancestral and emotional practice

Participants expressed that midwifery care is deeply spiritual and emotionally attuned. Social and emotional support was both a prevalent and persistent code within interviews. Spirituality was mainly mentioned by the midwives as a foundational pillar of why and how they do their work. Practices such as building birth altars, using herbs, and creating rituals were described alongside grief support after loss, affirming the midwives’ role in holding sacred space. Clients who had also experienced hospital care noted that this was unique about Black midwifery–that there was priority and attention given to the spiritual needs of birthing parents and families.Samantha (client): But the fact that [Midwife] came and was just like, “Is there any part of the birth…religious or a cultural ritual that we want to do when babies are born in our family that they need to be aware of…Just being asked those questions before experiencing it meant a lot because I was like, “Wow. Actually, I didn’t fully think about it, but now that you asked me, let me make sure. Is there something that I want to make sure I do to honor our family or our beliefs or anything like that?” And just the fact that she asked, that meant a lot to me.”

Discussion

Across interviews, participants consistently described midwifery care as extending beyond clinical support to include education, integration of the family unit, social and emotional counseling, and dietary and nutrition guidance. These patterns suggest that Black community midwifery operates as a holistic, culturally grounded, and spiritually informed model of care, consistent with prior scholarship on community-based and culturally congruent models of care [15, 20, 21].

Participants consistently described midwives as educators, advocates, and system navigators, providing childbirth education, nutrition counseling, and guidance on navigating institutional healthcare systems. Midwives not only conveyed clinical information but also equipped clients with strategies to advocate for themselves, including requesting medical records and seeking clarification of care plans. Midwives also supported holistic health through dietary and physical activity counseling. They communicated this information sensitively and with the client’s individual needs, resources, and emotional well-being in mind. Midwives’ ability to center relationships and tailor interventions was key to supporting behavior change in these contexts. This reflects existing literature on community-based perinatal care models that emphasize patient empowerment, health literacy, and advocacy as core components of care [9, 11]. The findings further underscore the cultural and family-centered nature of Black midwifery care.

Participants also highlighted the importance of integrating partners, children, and extended family into the birthing process, with midwives facilitating inclusive environments while maintaining client comfort and emotional safety. Clients described this as a key distinction from prior hospital-based experiences, where partners were often excluded or minimally acknowledged.

Four core midwifery values identified in this study were client autonomy, client safety, spirituality, and deep care. Participants described how midwives actively balanced client autonomy and safety through practices such as ongoing informed consent, shared decision-making, and individualized counseling. Clients emphasized feeling trusted in their bodily knowledge, while midwives described supporting clients even when they chose alternative care pathways or declined certain interventions. These findings suggest that respect for client autonomy and prioritization of client safety were not treated as opposing principles but rather values that should be harmonized through continuous dialogue and clinical attentiveness.

The central finding of this study was the emergence of deep care as a defining value of Black community midwifery. Deep care was referenced across all participant groups and represented the most foundational value identified in our analysis. Clients frequently described midwifery care as relational, affirming, and continuous, often emphasizing that midwives were deeply invested in their emotional, physical, and spiritual well-being and pointed to the absence of these qualities in conventional medical systems. These empirical findings can be further interpreted through Black feminist and care ethics frameworks, which conceptualize care as both relational and political [16, 22]. In these contexts, care operates not only as clinical support but as an ongoing, relational commitment.

Theorizing deep care

Deep care, as described by participants, can be understood as extending longstanding theories of care as both a relational and political practice [15, 20]. While deep care reflects much of what has been described in the care ethics literature by Fisher and Tronto, Held, and Leininger, our analysis suggests that it extends beyond the contemporary frameworks offered by these scholars: it is informed by history, spirituality, and justice. Additionally, it harmonizes competing client- and midwife-held values and is culturally congruent.

First, interviewees’ descriptions of Black midwifery care map onto the four intertwining phases of Fisher and Tronto’s model: Caring About; Taking Care of; Caregiving; and Care Receiving [23]. The first three phases relate to the care provider and the last relates to the care receiver. But unlike the caregiver in Fisher and Tronto’s model who does not concern themselves with the care recipient’s history, the midwives’ deep care takes a historical and political perspective and contextualizes their work as a repair to the damage done by history and contemporary structures. Midwives appreciate both their clients’ histories of marginalizations as well as the historical disenfranchisement of Black grand midwives in the US.

Second, Fisher and Tronto’s model is silent about the role of spirituality, whereas both Black clients and midwives see spirituality as a central and explicit driver of care [23]. Spiritual care was another cornerstone, with midwives drawing from ancestral practices to create sacred spaces around birth and loss. This is particularly notable given the historical marginalization of Black midwifery, which traces its roots to West African traditions disrupted by the rise of white midwifery and obstetrics [21]. Practices such as building birth altars, incorporating herbs, and facilitating rituals around birth and loss were described alongside grief support and emotional attunement.

Clients emphasized that being asked about spiritual or cultural practices itself constituted meaningful care, particularly in contrast to prior clinical experiences. Rituals around birth, loss and spirituality, situate Black community midwifery within a broader tradition that challenges dominant biomedical paradigms that often divorce birth from emotional, spiritual, and cultural contexts, reinforcing Black midwifery’s critical role in offering care that addresses the full humanity of Black birthing people.

Our analysis shows that Black community midwives made room for their clients’ spirituality and marshaled their own spiritual resources (prayers, extrasensory abilities) in the discharge of their responsibilities to their clients. Midwives talked expansively about the role of spirituality in their practices and how they drew on spiritual practices in providing effective pregnancy, labor, and delivery services.

Third, whereas Fisher and Tronto’s model focuses on prioritization and decision-making as important care activities, the midwives seemed less concerned with decision-making and prioritizing than with harmonizing what could be the competing values of client autonomy and safety [23]. Midwives’ practicing deep care, through listening, making space, engendering familial feelings, were seen as trustworthy and able to balance these competing values. Some participants reported that it was that trust that was developed in their midwife’s care that allowed them to overcome their resistance to go to the hospital when the midwives made that recommendation. They described midwives as “having their back.” Deep care reflects the relational and trust-building dimensions of care described by Held, in which care fosters social bonds, responsiveness, and cooperation [24]. That harmonizing function is important to client safety and well-being. The midwife’s reflection on death of a baby outside the Black community whose mother refused to go to the hospital underscores the importance of this harmonizing function.

Fourth, the midwives’ deep care was culturally congruent and aligned with Leininger’s Culture Care Theory (CCT) in that their care and caring were central, distinct, and unifying within the midwifery practice, and their approach reflected a co-participatory model of care that valued lived experience and incorporated cultural knowledge into care processes [25]. The CCT recognizes the role of religion as an important sociostructural factor of care. But in the CCT, the caregiver’s spirituality is ignored, missing or non-existent [25]. In deep care, spirituality was not merely acknowledged but centered. It acts as a guiding force for both care recipients and care providers. Additionally, it is a force that can be harnessed both individually and collectively.

Fifth, while midwives’ attention to justice ethics included autonomy, it also extended beyond individual rights toward repair. Their orientation toward justice was not abstract but grounded in the lived realities of structural violence and ongoing inequities affecting Black communities [24]. In this way, justice within deep care was relational and reparative, oriented toward healing and restoration rather than solely fairness or autonomy.

As participants described practices such as extended presence, emotional attunement, spiritual support, and culturally grounded care, these findings suggest that care is enacted as “a philosophy of survival in the face of literal and psychic death,” disrupting negative outcomes by centering collective well-being, spiritual connection, and resistance to systemic neglect and harm [16]. In this study, Black midwives embodied this through ancestral knowledge and attention to bodily, emotional, and spiritual wellness—echoing practices like storytelling, spiritual herbalism, and community ritual highlighted in Black feminist healing modalities [22].

In this sense, the practices described by participants reflect what has been theorized as “freedom work”—a form of care labor that transforms caregiving into a collective project of survival and liberation [26]. These acts of care reflect both clinical attentiveness and a sustained ethical stance, i.e., a refusal of disposability and an insistence on interpersonal repair as central to health [15, 20].

This form of care, deep care, resists commodified, institutionalized models of wellness by reclaiming caregiving as sacred and culturally rooted, which is especially critical in a healthcare system that has historically marginalized Black birthing people. Participants’ descriptions of feeling cared for “from a place of love,” alongside experiences of trust, affirmation, and relational continuity, suggest that Black community midwifery operates through a model of care that prioritizes dignity, healing, community, and connection [9, 21]. Here, love emerges not only as an interpersonal experience but as a guiding ethic of care—one grounded in responsibility, respect, and collective well-being [21]. Through this, Black midwifery centers generative relationships that restore dignity, connection, and collective safety.

Implications

Current approaches to addressing the Black maternal health crisis have often focused on hospital-based interventions. However, participants in this study described forms of care that are rooted in sustained relationships, cultural alignment, and holistic support across the perinatal period. These findings suggest that Black community midwifery may address important dimensions of care that are less accessible within institutional models, particularly those related to history, trust, continuity, and emotional and spiritual well-being, which have been identified as critical to improving maternal health experiences [9, 11].

Emerging care models, such as Beloved Birth Black Centering, illustrate how integrating midwives, doulas, and families into care delivery can foster environments that participants associate with safety, empowerment, and support [9, 11]. When considered alongside our findings—particularly the emphasis on education, advocacy, spirituality, and relational care—these models suggest that community-based approaches may play an important role in expanding the range of effective maternal health interventions. From a policy perspective, the findings suggest several potential areas for investment. Expanding Medicaid reimbursement coverage and parity for community midwifery and doula care, supporting Black-led birth centers, and funding group-based and wraparound care models may help sustain and scale the practices described by participants in this study. These approaches align with existing federal and state initiatives, including the Title V Maternal and Child Health Program and the Black Maternal Health Momnibus Act, which emphasize culturally congruent care, diversification of the perinatal workforce, and investment in community-based maternal health organizations [27].

Strengths and limitations

This study is rooted in the personal narratives of Black community midwives and those most directly impacted by their care. Inherently, this is a strength as it offers nuanced and community-led insight—one that might be difficult to access from an institution-led lens. Additionally, this study surveyed and interviewed clients from several regions of the United States—all with different practice regulations. Interviewees were diverse, and this allows for a more inclusive picture of Black community midwifery. Finally, including key informants like collaborating providers, community stakeholders, and family members allows for a wrap-around view of midwifery care—and its ripple effects on family, community, and institutional structures.

This study is somewhat limited by a lack of socioeconomic diversity—which is representative of a larger barrier to care. Typically, clients seeking community midwives or home birth must pay out of pocket, even if they are insured. The cost of a homebirth can be anywhere from 5500–8000$, limiting access to those who can afford it [28]. Many of the clients in this study paid out of pocket, which potentially limits the generalizability of these results to low-income birthing people. Additionally, this study utilized purposeful and snowball sampling. To participate in the study, clients had to be referred by their midwife. This introduced a positive selection bias to our sample where referred clients were those who had positive relationships with their midwife and were more inclined to speak favorably about their experience, potentially impacting the transferability of this study to larger populations. Of note, we interviewed one client who unilaterally terminated her relationship with the midwife around labor. In that interview, the same values emerged.

Future directions

Black community midwifery is a powerful avenue for addressing disparities in maternal health and could be critical to improving outcomes around physical and emotional safety for Black birthing people. Future studies should look towards the barriers that midwives face, as well as the resources they need to ensure this care is accessible for clients and sustainable for birth workers. Future research should also investigate the demand or preference for care attributes identified in this study within the general Black birthing population. Importantly, increasing the number of Black midwives through targeted recruitment and financial support in educational programs will be crucial. Additionally, investing in community-based birth centers and expanding Medicaid coverage to include midwifery services are also vital steps toward creating a more inclusive and effective maternal healthcare system. Importantly, this study highlights the need for care paradigms and payment structures that supports Black birthing people, communities, and midwives—rather than one that lionizes them or overemphasizes their resilience in the face of structural oppression.

Conclusions

This study illuminates the vital role Black community midwifery plays in addressing the multifaceted crisis of Black maternal health. By centering “deep care”—a culturally grounded, emotionally attuned, and spiritually connected model of care—Black midwives provide affirmative support that goes far beyond clinical procedures. Their approach prioritizes education, autonomy, safety, advocacy, and family integration, all while drawing from spirituality, ancestral knowledge, and community wisdom. These values resonate deeply with Black birthing people seeking affirming, respectful, and holistic alternatives to institutionalized maternity care. The findings of this study affirm what generations have long known: Black midwifery is not only a practice of catching babies but one of catching families, communities, and histories. As the U.S. grapples with comparatively poor maternal health and serious disparities in maternal experiences and outcomes, investing in Black midwifery is imperative. Sustained support for education, policy reform, Medicaid expansion, and culturally rooted birthing infrastructure can ensure that the future of maternal health is not only safer, but more joyful and just.
