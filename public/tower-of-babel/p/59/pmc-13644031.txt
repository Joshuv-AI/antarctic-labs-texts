Profile of migraine patients in Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region: a multi-center study
Authors: Mona Hussein, Jasem Al-Hashel, Derya Uludüz, Ahmed Allioueche, Yasmine Kamal, Mohammed Tahir Kurmanji, Diana Khedr, Aynur Ӧzge, Ahmed Dahshan, Hanaa Kedah, Emna Ellouz, Imen Ketata, Aussan Al-Athwari, Wael Alwahchi, Amina Chentouf, Najib Kissani, May M Fayez, Amr Hassan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region presents a unique demographic, cultural, and socioeconomic profile that influence migraine burden and management. Comprehensive multicenter data on patient characteristics, treatment access, and complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) use in this region are limited. The aim of this work was to compare sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of migraine patients in low-/lower-middle-income countries (LICs/LMICs) versus high-/upper-middle-income countries (HICs/UMICs) in the MENA region, and to explore disparities in medication access, treatment adherence, patient satisfaction, and CAM use.MethodsThis cross-sectional multicenter study included 676 adult migraine patients across 12 MENA countries. Data were collected via structured interviews and medical record verification, covering sociodemographic, lifestyle, clinical, and treatment-related variables, as well as knowledge, attitudes, and experiences with CAM.ResultsPatients from LICs/LMICs had significantly longer delays from headache onset to diagnosis compared with those from HICs/UMICs [6 (3–9) vs. 1 (0–3) years; effect size = 1.344, 95% CI: 1.175–1.511, P-value < 0.001]. Chronic migraine was significantly more prevalent in LICs/LMICs (44.3% vs. 20.8%; OR = 0.331, 95% CI: 0.236–0.463, P-value < 0.001), as was medication-overuse headache (38.5% vs. 15.0%; OR = 0.282, 95% CI: 0.195–0.406, P-value < 0.001). Access to medications was more restricted in LICs/LMICs, with 79.4% relying on out-of-pocket payments versus 42.1% in HICs/UMICs (OR = 0.210, 95% CI: 0.138–0.319, P-value < 0.001). Medication adherence was lower in LICs/LMICs (52.0% vs. 78.9%; OR = 3.458, 95% CI: 2.471–4.838, P-value < 0.001). Patient dissatisfaction with healthcare services was markedly higher in LICs/LMICs (35.5% vs. 7.9%, P-value < 0.001). Patients in LICs/LMICs reported greater reliance on traditional healers, religious leaders, and CAM modalities such as cupping, herbal remedies, and spiritual healing.ConclusionThis study highlights substantial disparities in migraine diagnosis and management between patients from LICs/LMICs and HICs/UMICs across the MENA region. Financial constraints and cultural influences may shape treatment adherence and CAM use in LICs/LMICs.Keywords: MENA, Migraine, Low-/lower-middle-income countries, High-/upper-middle-income countries, CAM

Introduction

Headache disorders affect a substantial proportion of the population, with migraine being among the leading causes of disability worldwide. According to the World Health Organization, migraine ranks within the top three neurologic causes of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [1, 2] Despite major advances in understanding migraine pathophysiology and the availability of effective acute and preventive treatments, migraine remains significantly underdiagnosed and undertreated particularly in low- and middle-income regions where awareness, healthcare resources, and access to specialized care may be limited [3].

Studying headache disorders in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region is of particular importance due to the region’s unique demographic profile and cultural context [4]. It is crucial to emphasize that the MENA region encompasses countries with varying socioeconomic conditions, healthcare infrastructures, and levels of access to medical care. Notwithstanding the substantial burden of migraine in this region, comprehensive multicenter data describing the sociodemographic, clinical characteristics, triggers, and comorbidities among migraine patients remain scarce [5, 6]. Furthermore, little is known about potential disparities in patients’ accessibility to medications, adherence to treatment, satisfaction with healthcare services, and perceived migraine-related stigma [7].

Despite the widespread use of complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) among patients with headache disorders [8], comprehensive studies exploring patients’ knowledge and attitude toward CAM use in headache disorders across the MENA region remain limited, highlighting a substantial gap in the literature. In many MENA countries, the use of CAM, including herbal remedies, cupping, relaxation techniques, spiritual therapies, and other traditional practices, is influenced not only by economic factors but also by deeply rooted historical, cultural, and religious traditions, which often encourage their use as complementary or alternative options to conventional medical treatments [9, 10]. Therefore, studying patients’ attitudes toward CAM use in the MENA region may provide important insights into the cultural and socioeconomic factors that shape patients’ motivations to use CAM in headache management.

Addressing these gaps by providing robust, region-specific data is crucial for guiding public health strategies, optimizing patient education, improving diagnostic accuracy, and tailoring migraine management approaches to regional needs and constraints. Ultimately, such efforts may contribute to reducing the clinical, social, and economic burden of migraine across the MENA region.

Therefore, the main objective of this multicenter study was to assess the variations in the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of migraine patients between low-income/lower-middle-income (LICs/LMICs) countries and high income/upper-middle-income (HICs/UMICs) countries in the MENA region. The second objective was to explore the potential disparities between these two groups regarding access to migraine medications, treatment adherence, patient satisfaction, and knowledge, attitudes, and experiences with CAM use.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional multicenter study included 676 adult patients diagnosed with migraine, consecutively recruited from neurology outpatient clinics across 12 countries in the MENA region. The participating countries were selected based on the availability of collaborating neurology centers and investigators who agreed to participate during the study period, in addition to the feasibility of standardized data collection across centers. The included countries were selected to provide representation from different geographic and socioeconomic settings within the MENA region, including both LICs/LMICs and HICs/UMICs according to the World Bank classification. The included countries were classified according to income based on the World Bank income classification system [11] into two groups: LICs/LMICs group which included Egypt, Tunisia, Morocco, and Yemen, and HICs/UMICs group which included Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, United Arab Emirates, Oman, Turkey, Algeria, Iraq, and Libya (Fig. 1). Patient enrollment was conducted over a six-month period, from September 2025 to March 2026.

Eligibility criteria

A total of 676 adult patients with a confirmed diagnosis of migraine, based on the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3) criteria [12], were included. Eligible participants were aged 18 years or older to be able to provide reliable information. Patients with major neurocognitive (e.g., Alzheimer, vascular dementia) or severe psychiatric disorders (e.g., schizophrenia, bipolar disorder) that could impair the reliability of self-reported data, were excluded.

Data collection

Data were collected through a structured, face-to-face interview that was conducted by a trained neurologist to ensure consistency and reliability. Basic sociodemographic data were obtained including age, sex, marital status, and educational level as well as socioeconomic indicators such as level of income. Participants were also queried about some lifestyle factors such as smoking status, alcohol consumption, use of illicit substances, and average daily screen time. Anthropometric measurements, including height and weight, were taken using calibrated equipment and body mass index (BMI) was calculated accordingly.

Clinical data relevant to migraine were also collected during the interview and verified against participants’ medical records to ensure accuracy and completeness. The data included the duration of migraine illness, duration from migraine onset to diagnosis, and primary sources of knowledge about migraine. Migraine subtype was classified as episodic or chronic, and the presence of medication-overuse headache (MOH) was also documented in accordance with (ICHD-3) [12]. Additional information collected encompassed commonly reported migraine triggers and comorbid medical or psychiatric conditions that could influence disease burden or treatment outcomes. Perceived stigma was assessed through direct questioning regarding whether patients felt socially stigmatized because of migraine.

Furthermore, additional data related to patients’ experiences with migraine management were obtained. These data included access to prescribed medications, adherence to treatment regimens and reasons for non-adherence. Medication adherence was assessed through direct patient self-report. Participants were classified as adherent if they reported regular use of prescribed migraine medications according to their physician’s instructions. Patients who reported irregular medication use, frequent missed doses, premature discontinuation, or failure to follow the prescribed treatment regimen were classified as non-adherent.

Patients’ satisfaction with healthcare services was evaluated using predefined categorical responses ranging from very dissatisfied to very satisfied. To further contextualize these ratings, patients were asked to clarify the reasons for any dissatisfaction to capture both barriers to optimal care and areas for potential improvement in clinical management.

Knowledge, attitudes, and experiences with CAM use were also assessed. The included patients were asked whether they had prior knowledge of CAM and to specify their sources of information. They were also asked about the available CAM modalities in their respective countries, practitioners or prescribers of CAM therapies, their reasons for using CAM, and perceived barriers to CAM use. Additional data encompassed personal experiences with CAM use such as perceived effectiveness or any side effects. Patients’ willingness to recommend CAM, disclosure of CAM use to their physicians, preference for physician-led discussions regarding CAM, and attitudes toward integrating CAM with conventional medical care were also assessed.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

Difference in sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of migraine patients between LICs/LMICs and HICs/UMICs in the MENA region.

Secondary outcomes

Variation in medication access, treatment adherence, and patient satisfaction between LICs/LMICs and HICs/UMICs in the MENA region.

Variation in medication access, treatment adherence, and patient satisfaction between LICs/LMICs and HICs/UMICs in the MENA region.

Knowledge, attitudes, and experiences with CAM use in LICs/LMICs versus HICs/UMICs in the MENA region.

Knowledge, attitudes, and experiences with CAM use in LICs/LMICs versus HICs/UMICs in the MENA region.

Ethical consideration

A written informed consent was obtained from each participant before participation in this study. The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was ethically approved by Faculty of Medicine Beni-Suef University Research Ethical Committee (FM-BSU-REC) and the approval number is FMBSUREC/060112026/Hussein.

Sampling

The sample size was calculated using Epi calc 2000, version 1.02, 1997. Based on 10% prevalence rate of migraine [13], a null hypothesis of 15%, and an alpha level of significance of 0.05, a total sample size of at least 469 participants was required to achieve a statistical power of 90%.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS (Statistical Package of Social Science) Version 25 was used to analyze the data. Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to test the normality of data. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. Quantitative variables were expressed as median and inter quartile range (IQR). Comparisons between LICs/LMICs and HICs/UMICs groups in categorical variables were performed using chi-square test. Odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated for categorical variables containing two categories. For categorical variables containing more than two categories, binary logistic regression analysis was performed using an appropriate reference category to estimate Odds ratios and corresponding 95% CI. Comparisons between LICs/LMICs and HICs/UMICs groups in continuous variables were performed using Mann-Whitney U tests. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d, and 95% confidence intervals were also reported to show the strength and precision of the differences. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All tests were two-tailed.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the included patients

This cross-sectional study was conducted on 676 migraine patients: 296 participants from LICs/LMICs and 380 from HICs/UMICs. The median age was comparable between the two groups, being 39 (32–45) years in the LICs/LMICs group and 40 (32–45) years in the HICs/UMICs group (P-value = 0.649). Females constituted the majority of participants in both groups (69.6% vs. 72.9%, P-value = 0.346). Patients from LICs/LMICs had a significantly higher BMI compared with those from HICs/UMICs [26.83 (24.03–31.18) vs. 25.53 (22.68–28.06), P-value < 0.001]. In contrast, years of education were significantly higher in the HICs/UMICs group [16 (12–16) vs. 14 (8–16), P-value = 0.033] (Table 1).

Marital status distribution did not differ significantly between groups (P-value = 0.849). However, residence differed significantly, with a higher proportion of rural residents in the LICs/LMICs group compared with the HICs/UMICs group (29.1% vs. 14.7%, P-value < 0.001). Significant differences were also observed in monthly income, where a greater proportion of participants from LICs/LMICs reported income lower than their expenses (28.4% vs. 12.9%), whereas higher-than-expense income was more common among HICs/UMICs participants (32.6% vs. 14.9%) (P-value < 0.001) (Table 1).

Regarding lifestyle habits, smoking and alcohol consumption were significantly more prevalent among participants from HICs/UMICs (20.8% vs. 12.8%, P-value = 0.007; and 5.8% vs. 0.3%, P-value < 0.001, respectively), whereas illicit drug use did not differ significantly between groups (P-value = 0.474). The median daily screen time was significantly higher in the HICs/UMICs group compared with the LICs/LMICs group (P-value = 0.006) (Table 1).

Regarding sources of knowledge about migraine, treating physicians were the most commonly reported source in both groups, although this was significantly more frequent in the HICs/UMICs group (97.6% vs. 91.2%, P-value < 0.001). Participants from HICs/UMICs were also more likely to report relatives or friends with migraine (61.1% vs. 35.8%, P-value < 0.001), social media (47.9% vs. 34.5%, P-value < 0.001), and medical programs on television (31.6% vs. 23.6%, P-value = 0.023) as information sources. In contrast, online resources and support groups did not differ significantly between groups (Table 2).

A significantly higher proportion of patients from LICs/LMICs had chronic migraine compared with those from HICs/UMICs (44.3% vs. 20.8%), while episodic migraine was more prevalent in the HICs/UMICs group (79.2% vs. 55.7%) (P-value < 0.001). Similarly, MOH was significantly more common among patients from LICs/LMICs (38.5% vs. 15.0%, P-value < 0.001) (Table 2).

With respect to migraine triggers, certain foods were reported more frequently by patients from HICs/UMICs (29.7% vs. 22.6%, p = 0.038). Other triggers, including insomnia or irregular sleep, stress, fatigue, dehydration, hunger or fasting, strong odors, bright lights, and fever, did not differ significantly between the two groups (Table 2).

Regarding associated comorbidities, fibromyalgia (14.5% vs. 9.5%, P-value = 0.049) and anxiety (56.6% vs. 48.3%, P-value = 0.033) were significantly more common among patients from HICs/UMICs. In contrast, hypotension was significantly more prevalent among patients from LICs/LMICs (20.6% vs. 10.8%, P-value < 0.001). Other comorbidities, including IBS, depression, insomnia, OSA, HTN, anemia, stroke, epilepsy, IHD, GERD, and obesity, did not differ significantly between groups (Table 2).

Importantly, perceived stigma related to migraine was significantly more frequently reported among patients from HICs/UMICs compared with those from LICs/LMICs (39.2% vs. 25.3%, P-value < 0.001) (Table 2).

Medication access, treatment adherence, and patient satisfaction

Access to migraine medications differed significantly between the two groups (P-value < 0.001). In the LICs/LMICs group, the majority of patients reported that payment was mandatory to obtain medications (79.4%), whereas this proportion was considerably lower in the HICs/UMICs group (42.1%). In contrast, obtaining medications through health insurance coverage (26.3% vs. 8.1%) or hospital pharmacies (31.6% vs. 12.5%) was more common among patients from HICs/UMICs (Table 3).

Medication adherence was significantly higher among patients from HICs/UMICs compared with those from LICs/LMICs (78.9% vs. 52.0%, P-value < 0.001). Regarding the reasons for non-adherence, financial reasons (64.7% vs. 18.7%), medication side effects (71.6% vs. 28.9%), and lack of response to medications (67.1% vs. 26.7%) were significantly more frequently reported in the LICs/LMICs group (all P-value < 0.001) (Table 3).

Patient satisfaction with healthcare services also differed significantly between groups (P-value < 0.001). Higher levels of dissatisfaction were observed among patients from LICs/LMICs, with 13.5% reporting being very dissatisfied and 22.0% dissatisfied, compared with 1.6% and 6.3% in the HICs/UMICs group, respectively. Conversely, higher levels of satisfaction were reported in the HICs/UMICs group. Several reasons for dissatisfaction were significantly more frequently reported by patients from LICs/LMICs compared with those from HICs/UMICs, including the high cost of care or medications (71.4% vs. 19.1%), lack of insurance coverage (65.7% vs. 20.7%), limited availability of advanced therapies (65.5% vs. 23.7%), ineffective treatment or frequent side effects (74.8% vs. 21.0%), limited access to specialists (66.8% vs. 18.3%), and long waiting times for investigations (65.3% vs. 22.8%) (all P-value < 0.001) (Table 3).

Knowledge, attitudes, and experiences with CAM use

Awareness of CAM was significantly higher among patients from HICs/UMICs compared with those from LICs/LMICs (57.4% vs. 39.9%, P-value < 0.001). Regarding sources of knowledge about CAM, patients from HICs/UMICs more frequently reported friends (38.1% vs. 15.4%) and social media (33.0% vs. 15.4%), whereas patients from LICs/LMICs more commonly cited religious leaders (22.2% vs. 1.8%) and traditional healers (17.1% vs. 2.8%) (P-value < 0.001) (Table 4).

Several CAM modalities showed significant differences between the two groups. Cupping (Hijama) was widely reported in both groups but was more common among LICs/LMICs participants (90.7% vs. 78.4%, P-value = 0.005). Herbal remedies (77.1% vs. 50.2%), acupuncture (58.5% vs. 42.7%), acupressure (42.4% vs. 12.4%), yoga (58.5% vs. 25.5%), hot or cold compresses (75.4% vs. 33.6%), homeopathy (27.1% vs. 12.3%), hydrotherapy (34.7% vs. 17.4%), aromatherapy (27.1% vs. 15.1%), and prayer or spiritual healing (74.6% vs. 26.1%) were significantly more frequently reported among patients from LICs/LMICs (all P-value < 0.05). In contrast, the use of physical therapies, massage and stretching therapy, relaxation exercises, and dietary supplements did not differ significantly between the two groups (Table 4).

Regarding practitioners or prescribers of CAM therapies, physicians were more frequently involved in the HICs/UMICs group (68.3% vs. 39.0%), whereas family members (28.8% vs. 10.6%) and traditional healers (32.2% vs. 21.1%) were more commonly reported in the LICs/LMICs group (P-value < 0.001) (Table 4).

The reasons for CAM use were significantly more frequently reported among patients from LICs/LMICs, including dissatisfaction with conventional treatments (75.4% vs. 31.2%), high cost of medications (68.6% vs. 21.1%), cultural or religious beliefs (64.4% vs. 23.9%), perception of fewer side effects (74.6% vs. 28.0%), accessibility (68.6% vs. 23.9%), and advice from others (72.0% vs. 34.9%) (all P-value < 0.001). On the other hand, perceived barriers to CAM use were more commonly reported among patients from HICs/UMICs, including lack of information (86.7% vs. 45.8%), lack of access (56.9% vs. 28.0%), safety concerns (81.7% vs. 44.1%), and physician advice against CAM use (75.2% vs. 38.1%) (all P-value < 0.001) (Table 4).

Personal use of CAM was significantly higher among patients from LICs/LMICs compared with those from HICs/UMICs (53.4% vs. 39.4%, P-value = 0.014). Among CAM users, patients from LICs/LMICs more frequently reported complete or partial symptom relief, whereas a higher proportion of patients from HICs/UMICs reported no change in symptoms (P-value < 0.001). The occurrence of side effects from CAM did not differ significantly between groups (P-value = 0.296) (Table 4).

Patients from LICs/LMICs were significantly more willing to recommend CAM to others (90.5% vs. 62.8%, P-value < 0.001). However, disclosure of CAM use to physicians, preference for physician discussion of CAM, and preference for integrating CAM with conventional medical care did not differ significantly between the two groups (Table 4).

The impact of educational Level on migraine awareness and treatment behavior

Exploratory analyses demonstrated that participants who reported relatives or friends with migraines as a source of migraine-related knowledge had significantly higher educational levels compared with those who did not (P-value = 0.022). Similarly, patients who identified online resources (P-value < 0.001) or social media (P-value = 0.005) as sources of information also had significantly higher educational level. In contrast, no significant differences in educational level were found between groups when information was obtained from physicians (P-value = 0.508), support groups (P-value = 0.077), or medical programs on TV (P-value = 0.608).

Furthermore, educational level was not significantly associated with medication adherence (P-value = 0.361) or awareness of CAM (P-value = 0.989). Correlation analysis revealed a statistically significant negative correlation between years of education and the duration from headache onset to diagnosis (r = − 0.089, P-value = 0.021).

Discussion

The present study provides valuable insights into the sociodemographic, lifestyle, and clinical characteristics of migraine patients across the MENA countries with differing income levels, with a particular focus on the knowledge, attitudes, and experiences related to CAM use. This contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of migraine burden and treatment behaviors in diverse MENA populations.

Regarding sociodemographic characteristics, Median age was similar across groups [39 (32–45) vs. 40 (32–45) years, respectively], consistent with migraine’s peak prevalence in early to middle adulthood [14, 15]. Female predominance was observed in both groups, in line with established epidemiology [15]. Educational attainment was significantly higher among participants from HICs/UMICs. Higher education is associated with better health literacy, earlier recognition of migraine symptoms, and greater adherence to preventive strategies. The role of education and health literacy as a determinant of health outcomes has been widely discussed by Don Nutbeam [16].

Interestingly, patients from LICs/LMICs in this study had significantly higher BMI compared with those from HICs/UMICs. Obesity has been linked to increased migraine frequency and severity, possibly via inflammatory and metabolic pathways [17]. Socioeconomic constraints in lower-income countries may be associated with higher BMI levels, potentially through limited access to healthy foods, reduced opportunities for physical activity, and increased consumption of inexpensive energy-dense diets [18].

Residence also differed significantly between the two groups, with a larger proportion of participants from LICs/LMICs lived in rural areas, which may limit access to specialized headache care. In many low- and middle-income countries, neurological services are concentrated in major urban centers, which may create barriers to diagnosis and treatment for individuals living in rural areas. Limited access to headache specialists and preventive medications in rural settings has been highlighted in an epidemiological study led by Stovner LJ et al. [19]

Lifestyle factors also varied between the two groups. Smoking and alcohol consumption were significantly more prevalent among participants from HICs/UMICs. Financial factors may partially explain these differences. Both smoking and alcohol have been reported as potential migraine triggers in susceptible individuals [20]. Daily screen time was also found to be significantly higher among participants from HICs/UMICs. Increased screen exposure is an increasingly recognized lifestyle factor associated with headache and migraine exacerbation in modern digital environments [21, 22]. Although smoking, alcohol consumption, and screen exposure were more prevalent among patients from HICs/UMICs, chronic migraine and MOH were significantly more common among patients from LICs/LMICs. This apparent discrepancy suggests that migraine chronification may be more strongly influenced by healthcare accessibility and adequacy of treatment than by lifestyle factors alone. Socioeconomic barriers, including limited access to preventive therapies and specialist care, may be associated with such higher prevalence of chronic migraine in lower-income countries. Additionally, the frequent use of over-the-counter analgesics without adequate medical supervision may be associated with medication overuse and subsequent development of MOH. It is well established that migraine management is closely shaped by healthcare accessibility, health literacy, socioeconomic status, and the financial burden of treatment [23]. Prior data from the Middle East have likewise emphasized that inadequate access to specialist headache care, delayed referral systems, and limited awareness among both patients and healthcare providers remain important barriers that may be associated with migraine chronification [7].

Although disease duration was similar between groups, patients from LICs/LMICs experienced significantly longer delays to diagnosis. Several factors may explain the significantly longer delays from headache onset to diagnosis observed among patients from LICs/LMICs in the present study. Limited access to headache specialists, particularly in rural and underserved areas, may substantially delay appropriate evaluation and diagnosis. In many LICs, referral pathways for headache disorders remain underdeveloped, and primary healthcare providers may have limited training for recognizing and managing migraine. Financial barriers and reliance on out-of-pocket healthcare payments may also discourage early medical consultation. In addition, lower health literacy and cultural perceptions that normalize recurrent headache symptoms may reduce healthcare-seeking behavior and contribute to delayed diagnosis. Patients in low-resource settings often rely on self-medication, traditional remedies, or CAM before seeking specialist care, which may delay accurate diagnosis and preventive treatment [3, 19].

Regarding sources of knowledge about migraine, treating physicians were the most commonly reported source of information in both groups, although this was significantly more frequent in the HICs/UMICs group. Physician-based education plays a crucial role in improving disease awareness and treatment adherence among migraine patients. Lipton RB and Bigal ME [24] have emphasized that physician counseling remains one of the most influential determinants of migraine knowledge. Notably, participants from HICs/UMICs more frequently reported social media and television medical programs as sources of information about migraine. These findings likely reflect the increasing use of digital media and social networks as sources of health information in higher-income countries, where greater access to the internet and higher digital literacy facilitate the use of online platforms for obtaining health-related information [25, 26].

With respect to migraine triggers, certain foods were reported more frequently by patients from HICs/UMICs, whereas other triggers, including sleep disturbance, fatigue, stress, dehydration, and environmental stimuli, did not differ significantly between groups. The similarity in most trigger patterns across income groups suggests that biological and lifestyle factors underlying migraine attacks are largely universal [20].

Regarding associated comorbidities, fibromyalgia and anxiety were significantly more prevalent among patients from HICs/UMICs in this study. The association between migraine and fibromyalgia, has been well documented and may reflect shared mechanisms involving central sensitization and altered pain processing [27, 28]. Similarly, anxiety disorders are among the most common psychiatric comorbidities in migraine patients and are known to worsen disease burden and quality of life [29, 30]. The higher prevalence of fibromyalgia and anxiety among migraine patients in HICs/UMICs is likely multifactorial. Greater access to healthcare specialists in higher-income countries increases the likelihood that these comorbidities are identified. Also, higher health literacy and awareness in HICs/UMICs encourage patients to report symptoms and seek care for chronic pain or mental health issues. Furthermore, in LICs/LMICs, fibromyalgia and psychiatric conditions may be underdiagnosed due to prioritization of acute conditions.

Interestingly, perceived stigma related to migraine was reported more frequently among patients from HICs/UMICs. “Notably, in LICs/LMICs, cultural norms that prioritize tolerance of pain, may mask the psychosocial impact of migraine, leading to lower reporting of stigma even when the condition affects daily life. The consequences of perceived migraine-related stigma have been well documented by Burch RC et al. who highlighted that it imposes a substantial psychosocial burden and negatively affects the quality of life [31].

Regarding medication access, our results revealed significant disparities between patients from LICs/LMICs and those from HICs/UMICs. A considerably higher proportion of patients in the LICs/LMICs group reported paying out-of-pocket for migraine medications, whereas insurance coverage and access through hospital pharmacies were more common in HICs/UMICs. These differences in healthcare financing may influence both treatment access and medication adherence. The substantial financial burden associated with out-of-pocket healthcare expenses in LICs/LMICs may discourage regular follow-up, limit access to preventive therapies and diagnostic investigations, and contribute to treatment discontinuation or irregular medication use. In contrast, broader insurance coverage and healthcare support systems in HICs/UMICs may facilitate more consistent access to migraine care and improve adherence to prescribed therapies. Reports from the World Health Organization have emphasized that financial barriers remain a major obstacle to accessing treatment for chronic disorders, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [32].

Medication adherence was significantly higher among patients from HICs/UMICs. Financial constraints may represent a particularly important barrier in lower-income countries, where patients often bear a substantial proportion of healthcare costs. Additionally, medication side effects and perceived lack of efficacy were frequently reported reasons for non-adherence among patients from LICs/LMICs. This aligns with the observations reported by Lipton RB and Silberstein SD who highlighted that adherence to migraine preventive therapies is frequently suboptimal and strongly influenced by treatment tolerability, perceived effectiveness, and accessibility of healthcare [33].

Patient satisfaction with healthcare services also differed significantly between the two income groups, with an expected higher dissatisfaction among patients from LICs/LMICs. Several factors likely may be associated with this finding, including high treatment costs, lack of insurance coverage, limited availability of advanced therapies, restricted access to specialists, and long waiting lists. These barriers are well documented by Stovner LJ et al. who reported significant global gaps in the availability of headache care and trained specialists between low- and high-income countries [19].

Existing evidence suggests that CAM use is highly prevalent among patients with migraine, with estimates exceeding 40–50% [34, 35]. Nevertheless, data on patients’ motivations, attitudes, and perceptions of CAM effectiveness remain scarce particularly across the culturally diverse and socioeconomically variable countries of the MENA region. The present study provides important insights into the knowledge, attitudes, and experiences related to CAM use among patients with migraine across MENA countries with different income levels. Awareness of CAM was significantly higher among patients from HICs/UMICs compared with those from LICs/LMICs. Higher awareness in high-income settings may reflect higher educational levels, greater access to health information, and wider exposure to digital health resources. Similar findings were highlighted by Bishop FL and Lewith GT who reported that awareness and knowledge of CAM are often higher in populations with greater health literacy and access to internet-based health information [36]. However, despite this higher awareness in HICs/UMICs, actual use of CAM in the present study was more common among patients from LICs/LMICs, suggesting that awareness does not necessarily translate into utilization.

Sources of knowledge about CAM differed markedly between the two groups. Patients from HICs/UMICs more frequently cited friends and social media, whereas those from LICs/LMICs relied more on religious leaders and traditional healers. This difference highlights the influence of sociocultural context on health-seeking behavior. In many low-income settings, traditional healers and religious authorities play an important role in shaping health decisions and remain trusted sources of advice [37].

Several CAM modalities showed significant differences between the two groups. In particular, cupping therapy (Hijama), herbal remedies, acupuncture, yoga, prayer or spiritual healing, and other traditional practices were reported more frequently among participants from LICs/LMICs. The high prevalence of cupping and spiritual healing is consistent with previous studies from the MENA region, where these practices are widely accepted and often linked to religious and cultural traditions [38, 39]. Herbal medicine and traditional remedies also remain common in many low-income countries because of their cultural acceptance, accessibility, and relatively low cost [37].

The present study also revealed notable differences in the individuals involved in recommending CAM therapies. Physicians were more frequently involved among patients from HICs/UMICs, whereas family members and traditional healers were more commonly reported in LICs/LMICs. This finding may reflect greater integration of CAM into healthcare systems in higher-income countries, where some CAM modalities are supervised by healthcare professionals [40]. In contrast, CAM practices in lower-income countries often occur outside the formal healthcare system and are primarily guided by traditional practitioners [37].

The motivations for CAM use were substantially more prominent among patients from LICs/LMICs. Dissatisfaction with conventional treatments, high medication costs, cultural or religious beliefs, perceived safety, accessibility, and advice from others were frequently cited reasons. These findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that patients in low-income countries often turn to CAM because of financial barriers to conventional care, limited healthcare access, and cultural acceptance of traditional medicine [37, 41].

Interestingly, perceived barriers to CAM use were more frequently reported among patients from HICs/UMICs, including lack of information, safety concerns, limited access, and physician advice against CAM use. These findings may reflect stricter regulatory environments and a stronger emphasis on evidence-based practice in higher-income healthcare systems [40]. Consistent with these patterns, personal use of CAM was significantly higher among patients from LICs/LMICs. Moreover, these patients were more likely to report partial or complete symptom relief. While this finding may suggest perceived benefit, it should be interpreted cautiously because subjective improvement may be influenced by placebo effects, expectations, or cultural beliefs regarding the effectiveness of traditional therapies [34]. Importantly, the frequency of reported side effects did not differ significantly between the two groups, suggesting that most CAM modalities used by participants were generally well tolerated.

Patients from LICs/LMICs were also significantly more willing to recommend CAM to others, which may reflect greater cultural acceptance and positive personal experiences. Nevertheless, disclosure of CAM use to physicians and preferences regarding physician involvement in CAM discussions did not differ significantly between the two income groups. Similar observations have been reported by Davis et al., who found that patients often do not routinely inform their physicians about CAM use [42]. This highlights the importance of open patient–physician communication regarding CAM use.

A key strength of this study is its multicenter design, encompassing diverse MENA countries with varying income levels, which allows for meaningful comparisons between LICs/LMICs and HICs/UMICs. The study comprehensively assessed not only sociodemographic and clinical characteristics but also lifestyle factors, migraine triggers, comorbidities, access to treatment, satisfaction with healthcare services, and medication adherence. This multidimensional approach provides a holistic view of migraine burden and management in the region, capturing factors that are often overlooked in single-country studies. Furthermore, by examining patient knowledge, attitudes, and experiences with CAM, the study addresses an important aspect of migraine care that is highly relevant in culturally diverse settings.

However, this study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design precludes establishing temporal or causal relationships between country income category and migraine characteristics, healthcare access, treatment adherence, or CAM use. Second, although the study included multiple countries across the MENA region, the sample was not population-based and may not fully represent all MENA countries or all migraine patients within each country, particularly underserved rural populations and individuals without access to neurology clinics. Third, the reliance on self-reported data may introduce recall bias, which could affect the accuracy of some reported variables. Fourth, the use of non-validated self-reported measures may limit the precision of the performed assessments. Fifth, although the study groups were comparable regarding age and sex, residual confounding from unmeasured or uncontrolled socioeconomic, healthcare-related, and country-specific factors cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Finally, the study population was recruited from neurology outpatient clinics, which may preferentially include patients with more severe migraine or those with greater financial ability to access specialist care. Consequently, migraine patients who are managed in primary care settings, those who rely on self-medication, or those who have limited access to specialists may have been underrepresented in the present study. Therefore, this may introduce a selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. Importantly, in many cultural settings, migraine and other headache disorders remain underreported due to normalization of symptoms, stigma, or limited awareness, which may reduce healthcare-seeking behavior.

Conclusion

The present study revealed several key disparities between migraine patients from LICs/LMICs and HICs/UMICs across the MENA region. Patients from LICs/LMICs had longer delays to diagnosis, greater reliance on CAM, and more frequent out-of-pocket payments for medications, reflecting limited access to healthcare and financial constraints. In contrast, patients from HICs/UMICs demonstrated greater use of smoking and alcohol, longer daily screen time, greater awareness of migraine and CAM, more frequent comorbidities such as fibromyalgia and anxiety, and higher adherence to prescribed medications. Collectively, these disparities highlight the influence of socioeconomic, cultural, and healthcare system factors on migraine burden and treatment behaviors across MENA region.
