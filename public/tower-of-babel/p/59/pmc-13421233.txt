Social Vulnerability, Wildfire Exposure, and Mental Health
Authors: Kyla Thomas, Evan Sandlin, Marco Angrisani, Jiyoon Kim, Margaret Gatz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionUnder what social conditions is wildfire exposure associated with increases in psychological distress?FindingsIn this survey study of 1510 Los Angeles County residents, conducted before and after the January 2025 wildfires, onset of wildfires was associated with a greater increase in psychological distress among evacuation zone residents compared with non–evacuation zone residents, detectable through 11 months of follow-up. This association was concentrated among individuals with household incomes below $100 000, extreme housing cost burden, prefire distress, or limited instrumental support.MeaningThis study suggests that wildfire exposure was associated with increased psychological distress under conditions of psychosocial or economic vulnerability; these vulnerabilities offer a target for policy intervention.

Introduction

Wildfires are an increasingly frequent and destructive climate hazard in the US. Experts project that fire risk will increase 100% to 175% in the coming decades.1,2,3,4 Compounding this risk is population growth into the wildland-urban interface (WUI), where homes and infrastructure intermingle with fire-prone vegetation. Today, roughly 16% of people in the US live in the WUI, a share expected to increase to 21% within 30 years.5 In California, every high-fatality fire in the past decade started in or near the WUI, and wildfire deaths have increased in tandem with WUI growth.6,7

Wildfires have in turn transitioned from infrequent, peripheral events to recurring, population-level hazards, a threat recently underscored by the January 2025 wildfires in Los Angeles, which began on January 7, 2025, and reached containment on January 31, 2025.4 Although the fires were modest in size, their proximity to 2 densely populated WUI neighborhoods made them among the most costly and destructive in California history. Together, they forced the evacuation of more than 200 000 residents, destroyed thousands of homes and businesses, killed at least 31 people, and produced billions of dollars in damage.8,9,10,11 The fires also heightened public concern about the deepening inequality in the region, as their greatest impacts were felt in 2 racially and economically disparate communities: the Pacific Palisades, a predominantly White and wealthy neighborhood, and Altadena, a predominantly Black and socioeconomically mixed neighborhood.12,13

A growing body of evidence links wildfire events with adverse mental health outcomes in affected communities, including elevated rates of posttraumatic stress disorder, depression, anxiety, and suicide.14,15 However, many of these studies rely on cross-sectional designs, limiting causal inference and the ability to assess effect persistence over time.15 Furthermore, research on the social factors associated with wildfire vulnerability remains underdeveloped.16,17,18,19

Social vulnerability refers to the social conditions, positions, and structural arrangements that increase an individual’s sensitivity or susceptibility to harm.17 To date, most social vulnerability research operationalizes these conditions through community-level, sociodemographic measures (traditionally, distributions of age, sex, race and ethnicity, income, poverty, unemployment, language, and educational level). This body of work has been valuable in identifying which communities are at increased risk of disaster exposure and impact. Still, aggregate demographic measures can only approximate the individual-level, and potentially modifiable, resources and risk factors directly shaping an individual’s vulnerability to disaster.16,20,21 In their recent review of the social vulnerability literature, Li et al17 argue for a broader range of individual and institutional indicators and identify housing, physical health, mental health, social connection and isolation, and community infrastructure as worthy of inclusion.

Recent analyses of the 2025 Los Angeles wildfires documented stark community-level disparities in health risk and access to recovery resources.12,22,23,24,25,26 Building on this work, we used longitudinal, individual-level survey data collected before and after the January 2025 wildfires to assess (1) whether wildfire exposure is associated with psychological distress, and the persistence of this association over time, as well as (2) the degree to which individual-level social factors—specifically, housing security, physical and mental health, and social support, in addition to sociodemographic characteristics—modify this association.

Methods

Data Sources

Our primary data source is LABarometer (LAB), a probability-based internet panel of approximately 2000 Los Angeles County adults recruited and refreshed through address-based sampling. LAB is a subpanel of the Understanding America Study (UAS), a national internet panel maintained by the University of Southern California Center for Economic and Social Research. This survey study was reviewed and approved by the Biomedical Research Alliance of New York institutional review board and the University of Southern California institutional review board. Written informed consent was obtained from participants on their recruitment in the UAS, and members without internet access were provided with an internet-enabled tablet and a broadband connection. All UAS surveys are conducted in English and Spanish. This study followed the American Association for Public Opinion Research (AAPOR) reporting guideline.

Monthly mental health measures were drawn from the UAS Monthly Panel Survey, a survey administered on a monthly basis to the entire UAS panel (including LAB), beginning October 1, 2023 (mean response rate, 68.2%). At the time of analysis, 15 survey waves had been completed prior to the onset of the January 2025 wildfires (January 7, 2025), and 12 survey waves had been completed after, through December 31, 2025. Prefire measures of housing security, self-rated health status, psychological distress, social support, and sociodemographic characteristics were drawn from the 2024 LAB Livability & Affordability Survey, fielded from July 24 to October 15, 2024 (response rate, 74.7%). Postfire measures of self-reported wildfire impact were drawn from the LAB Wildfire Survey, fielded 1 day after the fires reached 100% containment: February 1 to March 30, 2025 (response rate, 71.4%). Respondent geocodes were used to link all survey responses with census tract–level measures of proximity to an evacuation warning or order zone. A depiction of our data linkages and inclusion or exclusion criteria can be found in the eFigure in Supplement 1. Together, the linkages resulted in a final analytic sample of 31 632 observations (spanning October 1, 2023, to December 31, 2025), corresponding to 1510 unique respondents.

Measures

Psychological distress was assessed using the 4-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-4), a validated self-report measure of anxiety and depression symptoms scored from 0 to 12 (higher scores indicate greater distress).27 This questionnaire includes the 2-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-2) anxiety subscale (range, 0-6; higher scores indicate greater distress) and the 2-item PHQ-2 depression subscale (range, 0-6; higher scores indicate greater distress), which were also analyzed separately. Wildfire exposure was defined as residence in an evacuation warning or order zone based on each respondent’s residential census tract at wildfire onset. An indicator variable (evacuation zone) was coded as 1 if any portion of the respondent’s census tract intersected a Los Angeles County Maximum Extent evacuation warning or order perimeter and 0 if not. Postwildfire exposure was coded 0 for data collected before January 7, 2025 (wildfire onset), and 1 thereafter. In our main statistical models, the coefficient for postwildfire exposure × evacuation zone reflects the differential change in outcomes after wildfire onset among respondents in evacuation zones compared with those outside evacuation zones, net of stable individual differences and shared temporal trends.

Self-report measures of the wildfire impact were obtained from the LAB Wildfire Survey, which asked respondents, “Did you experience any of the following as a result of the January 2025 wildfires, windstorm, or related pollution?” Respondents were presented a list of 16 items, assessing housing, financial, and health-related impacts, and asked to provide a “yes” or “no” answer to each.

Prefire measures of social vulnerability were obtained from the 2024 LAB Livability & Affordability Survey. These included (1) prefire sociodemographic characteristics: age (18-39 years = 0, 40-64 years = 1, and ≥65 years = 2), gender (female = 0 and male = 1), race and ethnicity (based on self-reported races: Asian [Asian American], Black [African American], Native American [American Indian or Alaska Native], Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, and/or White [Caucasian]; based on self-reported ethnicity: Hispanic or Latino or non-Hispanic or non-Latino; respondents were assigned by our team to 1 of the following categories: Hispanic, non-Hispanic Asian, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, or non-Hispanic other; due to small sample sizes, respondents who identified as only American Indian or Alaska Native, only Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, or >1 race were collapsed into the non-Hispanic other category), household income (<$50 000 = 0, $50 000-$99 999 = 1, and ≥$100 000 = 2), and educational level (high school degree = 0, some college = 1, and bachelor’s degree or higher = 2); (2) prefire housing insecurity measures: extreme cost burden (annual rent or mortgage amount ≤50% household income = 0, annual rent or mortgage amount >50% household income = 1) and maintenance problems (scale of 0-9, coded dichotomously such that ≤2 self-reported maintenance problems = 0 and ≥3 self-reported maintenance problems = 1 [results do not differ when coded as continuous]); (3) prefire fair or poor self-rated health (good, very good, or excellent = 0 and poor or fair = 1); (4) prefire PHQ-4 score (dichotomized at a score of ≥3, the clinical threshold for distress)27; and (5) prefire emotional and instrumental social support, measured using items from the Medical Outcomes Survey.28 Emotional support was assessed by asking respondents how often someone was available to “give you good advice,” “to confide in,” or “who understands your problems.” Instrumental support was assessed by asking how often someone was available to “take you to doctor,” “prepare meals,” or “help with daily chores.” Response options were never, sometimes, often, or always. For both constructs, respondents were coded as having high support (1) if they responded “always” to all 3 items and low support (0) if they responded “sometimes,” “rarely,” or “never” to any item. Race and ethnicity are collected through the UAS demographic survey, which is appended to every UAS survey to characterize the demographic composition of the panel and inform survey weights construction. We include race and ethnicity in our analysis because they are well-documented correlates of wildfire exposure and vulnerability.

Statistical Analysis

Our approach follows a difference-in-differences design, comparing the change in PHQ-4 scores before and after wildfire onset among residents in evacuation warning or order zones with the change in PHQ-4 scores observed among residents outside those zones. To estimate the association between wildfire exposure and mental health, we fit 2-way fixed-effects (TWFE) models, exploiting repeated monthly observations of the same individuals before, during, and after the wildfire period. These models included respondent fixed effects, which control for all time-invariant individual characteristics (observed and unobserved), and month-by-year fixed effects, which absorb common temporal shocks and trends affecting all respondents in a given month-year. The wildfire-attributable change in mental health is identified by the interaction between evacuation zone and postwildfire exposure.

The parallel trends assumption was formally tested by estimating prewildfire interaction terms between evacuation zone residence and each monthly indicator in the prefire period. We assessed temporal persistence of associations by interacting evacuation zone residence with each postfire month. To assess social vulnerability as a moderator, we extended the base TWFE model with 3-way interactions (postwildfire exposure × evacuation zone × vulnerability indicator). To avoid confounding by the 2024 presidential election and start of the second Trump presidency in January 2025, we conducted a sensitivity analysis stratified by political party (Democrat, Republican, and Independent). All models used robust standard errors. Analyses were conducted in Stata, version 18 (StataCorp LLC). All P values were from 2-sided tests and results were deemed statistically significant at P < .05.

Results

Sample Characteristics

The analytic sample included 1510 unique respondents (31 632 person-month observations), with a mean (SD) age of 47.2 (15.5) years. On entry in the UAS Monthly Panel Survey, 924 (61.2%) identified as female and 586 (38.8%) as male, 637 (42.2%) as Hispanic, 225 (14.9%) as non-Hispanic Asian, 111 (7.4%) as non-Hispanic Black, 473 (31.3%) as non-Hispanic White, and 63 (4.2%) as non-Hispanic other. At wildfire onset, 143 (9.5%) were residing in an evacuation zone.

Table 1 characterizes respondents’ social vulnerability in the immediate prefire period and their self-reported wildfire impacts in the immediate postfire period, stratified by evacuation zone residence. Compared with non–evacuation zone residents, evacuation zone residents were older (24.1% [32 of 133] vs 17.4% [222 of 1278] aged ≥65 years), more likely to be non-Hispanic White (59.0% [75 of 134] vs 29.1% [372 of 1279]), and less likely to be Hispanic (19.4% [26 of 134] vs 44.0% [563 of 1279]). Evacuation zone residents were also more likely to have household incomes of $100 000 or more and to have college degrees. Prefire rates of extreme housing cost burden were lower in evacuation zones compared with non–evacuation zones (12.5% [13 of 104] vs 21.2% [195 of 921]). These differences underlie the use of individual fixed effects to isolate wildfire-attributable mental health changes.

As expected, evacuation zone residents reported substantially higher rates of wildfire impact across housing, economic, and health domains. Nearly half (46.4% [58 of 125]) evacuated their homes during the fires compared with 6.2% of non–evacuation zone residents (71 of 1145); 8.8% (11 of 125) reported home damage or loss vs 1.9% of non–evacuation zone residents (22 of 1145) (Table 1). Financial hardship, as measured by reports of wildfire-related income loss or new debt, was roughly twice as common among evacuation zone residents (19.2% [24 of 125] vs 9.4% [108 of 1145]), as were physical or mental health problems associated with the fires (16.8% [21 of 125] vs 11.4% [130 of 1145]). Some non–evacuation zone residents reported housing-related impacts or displacement. This is likely because the LAB Wildfire Survey asked respondents to report impacts related to the wildfire, windstorm, or associated pollution. It also underscores the limits of residential proximity as a wildfire exposure measure. Beyond evacuation zones, some residents may have self-evacuated due to air quality concerns from drifting wildfire smoke or due to concerns about their future safety.

Wildfire Exposure and Psychological Distress

A key assumption of our modeling approach is that, absent the wildfires, mental health trends would have evolved similarly for residents living inside and outside evacuation zones. Figure 1 plots respondents’ mean PHQ-4 scores by month-year separately for individuals residing in evacuation and non–evacuation areas. The red vertical line marks the onset of the January 2025 wildfires. In Figure 1, the mean mental health trajectories of the 2 groups appear to diverge in the postonset period but move in parallel in the preonset period, with no evidence of systematic divergence prior to wildfire onset.

To formally assess the pretrend assumption, we estimated a model interacting evacuation zone residence with a full set of month-year indicators in the prefire period (eTable 1 in Supplement 1). None of the prewildfire interaction terms were statistically significant, indicating no detectable differences in preevent trends between the 2 groups. Both groups exhibited a temporary increase in mean PHQ-4 score in November 2024, which we consider to be associated with the US presidential election, but they do not differ significantly in how much their scores increase.

Table 2 presents results from the primary TWFE models. In model 1 (PHQ-4 total score), the postwildfire exposure × evacuation zone interaction was positive and statistically significant (β [SE] = 0.28 [0.09]; P = .002). On the PHQ-4 scale (range, 0-12), estimated scores increased from 2.11 before the fire to 2.51 after the fire for evacuation zone residents compared with 2.11 before the fire to 2.23 after the fire for non–evacuation zone residents, a differential of 0.28 points, or approximately 12%. This differential corresponds to evacuation zone residents experiencing 1 more symptom of anxiety or depression than non–evacuation zone residents for several days in a given month.

Subscale models suggest that this differential was associated primarily with increases in anxiety (Table 2). In models 2 (anxiety) and 3 (depression), the postwildfire exposure × evacuation zone interaction term was statistically significant for both anxiety (β [SE] = 0.17 [0.05]; P = .002) and depression symptoms (β [SE] = 0.12 [0.05]; P = .02), reflecting a differential change of 14% for anxiety and 12% for depression among evacuation zone residents relative to non–evacuation zone residents after wildfire onset. However, the patterns differed by subscale. For anxiety, estimated scores increased significantly among evacuation zone residents (from 1.09 to 1.40) relative to non–evacuation zone residents (from 1.09 to 1.23). For depression, the interaction term was significant, but estimated scores did not increase significantly in either group (evacuation zone: from 1.02 to 1.11; non–evacuation zone: from 1.02 to 1.00). As a robustness check, we reestimated our models using an interrupted time series design (eTable 2 in Supplement 1); findings are broadly consistent with our TWFE results and interpretation.

The positive and significant postwildfire exposure coefficient in model 2 (anxiety) (β [SE] = 0.14 [0.05]; P = .01) (Table 2) suggests a county-wide increase in anxiety symptoms after wildfire onset, although this cannot be separated from the concurrent start of the Trump administration on January 20, 2025. In sensitivity analyses, we stratified the sample by political party (Democratic, Republican, or Independent) and found that this result was largely associated with Democrats, for whom the estimated association was the largest and only statistically significant one (eTable 3 in Supplement 1). This result suggests that postwildfire exposure may be partially confounded by responses to the Trump presidency. However, postwildfire exposure × evacuation zone—our primary exposure of interest—remained positive and statistically significant across all 3 political groups, supporting our conclusion that the excess distress observed among evacuation zone residents was a response to the wildfire.

To assess the persistence of wildfire-associated distress over time, we estimated month-specific postfire exposure interaction terms (eTable 4 in Supplement 1). None of the postfire exposure interaction terms differed significantly from the January 2025 reference, indicating no significant attenuation of distress over 11 months of postdisaster observation.

Social Vulnerability and Psychological Distress

Table 3 summarizes 3-way interaction results testing social moderators of the association between wildfire exposure and psychological distress. Among the sociodemographic factors tested, the interaction of postwildfire exposure × evacuation zone × household income was negative and significant (β [SE] = −0.05 [0.02]; P = .03), indicating that adults with lower incomes experienced larger increases in wildfire-related distress. Interactions with gender, age, race and ethnicity, and educational level were not statistically significant, although these analyses may be underpowered. The coefficients move in expected directions but have large standard errors, likely due to small subgroup sample sizes within the evacuation zone.

Beyond sociodemographic characteristics, significant interactions were observed for prefire housing security, mental health, and social support. The postwildfire exposure × evacuation zone × cost burden interaction was positive and significant (β [SE] = 1.11 [0.32]; P = .001), indicating greater increases in wildfire-related distress among residents with extreme housing affordability stress (Table 3). Significant interaction effects were observed for prefire measures of mental health as well. Individuals with mild to severe distress prior to the fires (PHQ-4 score ≥3) reported substantially higher levels of postfire distress compared with those without preexisting distress (postwildfire exposure × evacuation zone × prefire distress: β [SE] = 0.77 [0.21]; P < .001). In the case of social support, no significant interaction was observed for high emotional support (β [SE] = −0.25 [0.19]; P = .17). However, a significant interaction was observed for high instrumental support (postwildfire exposure × evacuation zone × high instrumental support: β [SE] = −0.37 [0.18]; P = .04), indicating that access to reliable, practical assistance reduced the association between wildfire exposure and psychological distress.

To better illustrate our significant results, Figure 2 plots the average marginal effect of wildfire onset on psychological distress, comparing individuals living inside vs outside evacuation zones, stratified by prefire measures of household income, housing cost burden, psychological distress, and instrumental social support. Across panels, the association between wildfire exposure and psychological distress is clearly concentrated among individuals with fewer socioeconomic and psychosocial resources prior to the fire. Among respondents with household incomes below $100 000, living in an evacuation zone was associated with increased distress after wildfire onset, while no comparable increase was observed at higher income levels. A similar pattern held for housing security, mental health, and instrumental social support: evacuation zone residence was associated with a postonset increase in distress among individuals who had an extreme housing cost burden, had limited access to instrumental social support, or who reported mild to severe psychological distress symptoms prior to the fires but had little to no association with postonset distress among those without these preexisting vulnerabilities.

Discussion

In this longitudinal survey study of 1510 Los Angeles County residents, we assessed the association between exposure to the January 2025 wildfires and psychological distress. Using a quasi-experimental design with individual and time fixed effects, we found that evacuation zone residents experienced greater increases in psychological distress than non–evacuation zone residents after wildfire onset. This association—detectable through all 11 months of the follow-up period—was due to a postfire increase in anxiety and was concentrated among individuals who reported household incomes below $100 000, extreme housing cost burden, prefire symptoms of psychological distress, or limited access to instrumental social support.

These findings extend prior research in several ways. First, most prior studies of wildfire exposure and mental health use cross-sectional designs that cannot isolate wildfire-attributable mental health associations from preexisting differences between exposed and unexposed populations.15 Our longitudinal design provides evidence that these associations may be causal. After wildfire onset, the sustained association between evacuation zone residence and psychological distress through 11 months of survey follow-up is also consistent with cross-sectional wildfire studies conducted up to a year after disaster.29,30,31,32

The concentration of postfire distress among socially vulnerable evacuation zone residents aligns with theoretical frameworks emphasizing that disaster harm is shaped by preexisting conditions.20 Evacuation zone residence may have been more common among non-Hispanic White residents with a higher income and higher educational level—groups typically considered to be socioeconomically advantaged—but the mental health consequences of wildfire exposure fell most heavily on those already in disadvantaged socioeconomic positions. Emotional support did not emerge as a significant moderator of postfire distress, while instrumental support did. This finding suggests that material stressors such as housing, transit, or financial instability—stressors better served by practical support—were associated with postfire increases in psychological distress more than emotional stress. Together, study results underscore the importance of distinguishing between exposure and susceptibility.16 Even when wildfire risk is geographically concentrated in relatively socioeconomically advantaged areas, it may disproportionately harm those with fewer resources to cope materially, physically, or psychologically.

Limitations

There are important research limitations to consider in generalizing these results. First, wildfire exposure is measured at the census tract level based on residential proximity to an evacuation order or warning perimeter, which may not fully capture other consequential exposures and impacts, including school or work disruptions. Second, while the PHQ-4 provides a validated measure of psychological distress, it captures subclinical symptoms rather than formal diagnoses. Third, the study focuses on a single wildfire event in Los Angeles County. It is unclear if these results will generalize to other wildfire disaster contexts. Fourth, although analyses suggest selective nonresponse was mild, some attrition bias cannot be ruled out. Fifth, the survey was administered in English and Spanish, potentially underrepresenting residents who speak other languages, although this group is expected to be small within the Los Angeles County population. Sixth, the relatively small number of evacuation zone residents in the sample limited statistical power in the social vulnerability analyses, and estimates should be interpreted with caution. Seventh, the Trump presidency coincided with postwildfire survey waves, which may have introduced confounding in estimates of postwildfire distress among non–evacuation zone residents.

Conclusions

In this longitudinal survey study of 1510 Los Angeles County residents, exposure to the January 2025 wildfires was associated with a significantly greater increase in psychological distress among evacuation zone residents than among non–evacuation zone residents, detectable across all 11 months of survey follow-up. This association was concentrated among individuals with household incomes below $100 000, extreme housing cost burden, prefire symptoms of psychological distress, or limited access to instrumental social support. As wildfires increasingly affect urban and suburban populations, the mental health impacts of wildfire exposure should be monitored as a core component of population harm. Furthermore, our results suggest that interventions aimed at mitigating wildfire-related mental health harms may be most effective when they target underlying psychosocial or economic vulnerabilities and when they facilitate access to practical, instrumental forms of support. Policies that bolster housing security and strengthen community-based support networks may play a critical role in promoting wildfire resilience in an era of escalating wildfire risk.
