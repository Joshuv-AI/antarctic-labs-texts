Lung cancer screening by low-dose CT scan in France: final results of the DEP KP80 study after three rounds

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Lung cancer is a major public health problem. It is the leading cause of cancer mortality worldwide. The most powerful prognostic factor for this cancer is its TNM stage classification. While 5-year survival in localised IA stages is 92%, it is less than 10% for metastatic IV stages. Unfortunately, diagnosis of early-stage lung cancer is often by chance, as the disease only develops symptoms later on. Although lung cancer screening is emerging as a new paradigm, its implementation remains a matter of debate. We searched PubMed and the Cochrane Library with combinations of the search terms “lung cancer” and “screening” OR “early detection”, with no language restrictions for articles published before August 15, 2023.

In several randomised controlled trials, lung cancer screening using low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) has proven effective in reducing specific lung cancer mortality and overall mortality in high-risk individuals. However, to this day, despite the recommendations of various learned societies, lung cancer screening has still not been implemented in many countries, including France.

Added value of this study

The aim of our prospective multicentric study was to assess the feasibility and efficacy of a lung cancer screening program in a real-life setting.

The LDCT screening carried out as part of our DEP KP80 study enabled the diagnosis of 71% of localised lung cancers (I-II). Overall, 95% of patients diagnosed with cancer received curative treatment, with a surgical resection rate of 81%.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our real-life study showed that, by adapting to the existing healthcare system, it is now possible to implement effective lung cancer screening using LDCT in high-risk individuals.

Introduction

Despite major therapeutic advances in recent years, lung cancer remains the world's leading cause of cancer-related death, accounting for almost 2 million deaths every year, which is more than deaths related to breast and colon cancer combined.1 The most powerful prognostic factor in lung cancer is its tumour, node, metastasis (TNM) classification, with 5-year survival rates ranging from 92% for Stage IA to 10% for Stage IVA.2,3 Unfortunately, lung cancer is usually diagnosed at an advanced stage. The recent French KBP-2020 study showed that, out of 8999 new lung cancer cases, only 21.5% were diagnosed at Stages I and II compared with 78.5% at Stages III and IV.4

There is now a strong rationale for lung cancer screening using low-dose computed tomography (LDCT). It is particularly based on three positive randomised trials: the U.S. based National Lung Screening Trial (NLST), the Dutch-Belgian lung cancer screening trial (Nederlands–Leuvens Longkanker Screenings Onderzoek [NELSON]), and Multicentric Italian Lung Detection (MILD) studies.5, 6, 7 In the recent Cochrane meta-analysis, this screening reduced lung cancer mortality by 20% and overall mortality by 5%.8

Like many international learned societies,9, 10, 11 a group of French experts recommended lung cancer screening by LDCT for at-risk populations back in 2013, with an update in 2021.12,13 In 2022, the French Health Authority (Haute Autorité de Santé Française, HAS) recognised the efficacy of LDCT in lung cancer screening, and supported the implementation of real-life studies before initiating nationwide organised screening.14

To assess the feasibility and real-life efficacy of a lung cancer screening program using LDCT, we conducted a multicentre prospective cohort study (DEP KP80) consisting of three rounds of annual LDCT screening (T0, T1, and T2) in a high-risk lung cancer population. The study took place in the Somme department, a French administrative territory. The design of the DEP KP80 study, the recruitment procedure, and the intermediate results of the first and second rounds have been reported previously.15, 16, 17 In this article, we present the results of the three study rounds, 3 years after the inclusion of the last participant.

Methods

Screening protocol

Participants in the DEP KP80 study were recruited between March 2016 and February 2020 by the study's investigating general practitioners (GPs) or pulmonologists (PNs). The inclusion criteria were those of the NLST study: men or women, aged between 55 and 74, with a smoking habit of more than 30 packs/year, current smokers or former smokers who quit less than 15 years ago, with no apparent symptoms, informed about possible ways of assisting smoking cessation, agreeing to the screening procedures with accurate information on the results and on the consequences that may result from them (non-invasive explorations: thoracic scanner at 3 months, positron emission tomography (PET), or invasive explorations: CT-guided biopsy, bronchial fibroscopy, surgical exploration, etc.). Exclusion criteria included a history of cancer within the last 5 years, significant impairment of general health, and cardiac or respiratory comorbidities contraindicating thoracic surgery.

All GPs, PNs, and radiologists in the Somme department were invited by letter to be investigators in the study. If they accepted, they were required to attend training meetings designed to present the study and increase their knowledge of bronchopulmonary cancer screening. Public information campaigns were carried out via press articles, radio and TV broadcasts, posters, and leaflets. The study was coordinated by ADEMA 80, the departmental structure that manages organised breast and colon cancer screening.

According to France's National Institute of Statistics and Economics Studies (INSEE), a governmental institution collecting and publishing data pertaining to the French population, the population of the Somme department comprised 572,000 inhabitants at study initiation. Based on these data, we estimated that 14,360 people from the Somme department were eligible for lung cancer screening, depending on the inclusion criteria defined by our study protocol. This estimation was based on a previously published lung cancer screening model.18 The number of subjects required for this feasibility study was calculated based on lung cancer prevalence data in the general population, expected LDCT sensitivity, and desired precision of its 95% confidence interval, considering a bilateral first-order risk of 5%.19 Therefore, while taking as references the sensitivity and prevalence data from the NELSON study,20 which reported a 85% sensitivity and 2.7% lung cancer prevalence, along with a 12% precision of its 95% confidence interval (half-interval amplitude), we needed 1260 subjects, plus 10% to compensate for those lost to follow-up, resulting in a total number of 1386 individuals to be included.

The investigating GP or PN prescribed the low-irradiation thoracic CT scan on a specific 3-part carbonless prescription pad provided by the coordinating centre: one part was a prescription for the patient to book an appointment for the scan, one part was returned to ADEMA 80 in a pre-stamped envelope for traceability, and the last part was for the prescriber. The prescribing physician checked the inclusion and exclusion criteria, explained the screening protocol, and informed the patient of the possibility of discovering an abnormality on the CT scan, as well as the possibility of further explorations. During this consultation, smoking cessation and/or an anti-smoking consultation were suggested.

Recruitment of participants by GPs or PNs limited the selection bias and confounding factors by making sure that subjects complied with the inclusion criteria and did not present any symptomatology suggestive of lung cancer (cough, haemoptysis, general condition alteration, etc.). The expertise of ADEMA 80 regarding screening and their method for collecting and entering information also limited measurement bias, with no missing data for LDCT scan results in the different screening rounds and demographic characteristics of participants and non-participants. ADEMA 80 collected personal data from each participant: age, sex, address, and smoking history using Zeus® software. Of note, this is the management software used by the French regional coordination centres for organised breast and colon cancer screening. Written information was provided on the screening procedures, expected benefits, and possible drawbacks. Informed consent was signed during the consultation. Sex identification was self-reported by study participants. The LDCT scan was performed in the radiological centres participating in the study without contrast injection in volume mode on a multi-slice machine (≥16 slices) with a nominal slice thickness of less than 1.5 mm and iterative reconstructions. Subsequent scans were preferentially performed in the same radiological centre with identical acquisition constants.

A standardised interpretation report (Supplemental Figure S1) was sent by the radiologist to the coordinating centre, who forwarded it to the GP and/or PN who managed the patient according to the predefined algorithms for solid nodules and subsolid or ground-glass nodules (Supplemental Figures S2 and S3). If no suspicious nodule was found, the screening was considered negative and the patient was invited back the following year for a new LDCT, for a period of 2 years. If a nodule measuring more than 10 mm was detected, the screening was positive and the patient's file was presented at a MultiDisciplinary Team (MDT). For nodules between 5 and 10 mm, screening was indeterminate and an intermediate low-irradiation scan was performed 3 months later. Comparison between the initial scan and the second scan was used to calculate the nodule's doubling time. When it was less than 400 days, screening was definitively considered positive, with presentation of the case to the MDT. On the other hand, when it was more than 400 days, screening was negative and the annual follow-up continued. Participants eligible for the second and third rounds received their T0 results by postal mail, with an invitation to consult their GP for the prescription of T1 and T2. Subjects diagnosed with cancer did not participate in subsequent rounds (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Organisation of lung cancer screening in the DEP KP80 study.

Organisation of lung cancer screening in the DEP KP80 study.

Lung cancer diagnosis was obtained by the anatomo-pathological study of biopsy and/or surgical specimens. The World Health Organization classification of lung cancers was used.21 Cancer stage was defined using the TNM classification (8th edition) of the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer Staging Guidelines.3

In order to detect interval cancers, our database was cross-referenced from the first screening scan to the last screening round with that of the Somme Cancer Registry, which exhaustively records all cancers in the department.22 As in the NLST study, we defined interval cancer as a case diagnosed within 1 year after a negative CT scan and before the next annual screen.23

Ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. It has been approved by the French national drug safety agency (Agence Nationale de Sécurité du Médicament Reference: 160048-B81) and the Abbeville Hospital ethics committee (Reference: 23032018). DEP KP80 is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03853967. Written informed consent was obtained during patient consultation.

Statistics

Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages, while quantitative variables were expressed as means with standard deviations or medians and quartiles. The 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) of proportions were calculated using the Clopper-Pearson method.

Sensitivity was estimated by dividing the number of true positive screens by the numbers of true positive and false negative screens, and specificity by dividing the number of true negative screens (negative screens in the absence of lung cancer) by the numbers of true negative and false positive screens (positive screens in the absence of lung cancer). The positive predictive value was estimated by dividing all subjects with a true positive screening by all subjects with positive screening and negative predictive value by dividing all subjects with a true negative screening by all subjects with negative screening.

No adjustment for multiplicity of tests was done. Analyses were performed using R Studio® software version 2023.03.0.

Role of funders

The funders were not involved in study design, data collection, analysis, interpretation of data, preparation of manuscript, or decision to publish.

Results

Results of three screening rounds (T0, T1, and T2), adherence, and demographic characteristics of the participants

The revision of the database for this final report showed that, of the 1254 participants included in the DEP KP80 study, 945 completed their initial scan, (i.e., participation rate of 75.4% at T0) and 309 did not attend their appointment (24.6%). Moreover, 376 and 270 participants completed their second and third rounds, respectively, including twelve participants at T1 who did not participate at T0 or were lost to follow-up at T0 and one participant at T2 who did not participate at T0. Thus, the participation rate was 42.8% at T1 (n = 376/878) and 31.1% at T2 (n = 270/869). The mean age of participants was 62.4 ± 4.9 years, and there were 33.1% of women. During the three rounds, 1591 screening LDCT scans were performed: 5.5% were positive (n = 87), 90.9% were negative (n = 1447), and 3.6% were lost to follow-up (n = 57). There were 12.9% indeterminate screenings (n = 206), finally considered after the 3-month follow-up scan as positive in 6.3% (n = 13) and negative in 66.0% (n = 136) of cases, 27.7% (n = 57) did not attend their 3-month follow-up scan appointment (Fig. 2). There was a reduction in the rate of indeterminate screenings among the different time points (T0: 18.6%, T1: 2.6%, and T2: 7.4%), as well as in the rate of positive screenings (T0: 6.8%, T1:4.8%, and T2: 1.9%). There was no difference in the rate of participation during the different rounds in terms of sex ratio. However, age and screening result during the previous round appeared to be major factors influencing participation. The oldest subjects participated more in the different rounds and the subjects with false positive screening results participated less in the subsequent rounds, especially at the third round (Table 1). Incidence, stage, treatment, and histology of diagnosed cancers

Lung cancer was diagnosed in 42 participants, corresponding to 2.6% over all the CT scans or 4.4% over the total participants (n = 945) having at least one CT scan. The cancers were mostly diagnosed at an early stage: 4.8% carcinoma in situ (n = 2/42), 64.3% stage I (n = 27/42), 7.1% stage II (n = 3/42), 21.4% stage III (n = 9/42), and 2.4% stage IV (n = 1/42) (Fig. 3). The mean age of patients with lung cancer was 63.5 ± 4.8 years. Overall, 64% were men (n = 27/42) and 36% women (n = 15/42). Indeterminate or false positive screening results in previous rounds did not appear to be predictive of lung cancer in subsequent rounds. Most cancers detected at T0 (76.7%) followed an initial positive screening (n = 23/30), and the majority of patients with cancer diagnosed at T1 or T2 (66.6%) had negative screenings in previous rounds (n = 8/12) (Fig. 4).Fig. 3Cancer stages diagnosed in the three rounds (n = 42).Fig. 4Screening results at T0, T1, and T2 for 42 patients with lung cancer. Of the 34 patients who underwent surgery, 79.4% had surgery only (n = 27/34), 5.9% had adjuvant chemotherapy (n = 2/34), and 14.7% had additional radio-chemotherapy (n = 5/34). Thoracic surgery consisted of 79.4% lobectomies (n = 27/34), 11.8% segmentectomies (n = 4/34), and 8.8% pneumonectomies (n = 3/34 including two right pneumonectomies).

A 63-year-old male patient who underwent right pneumonectomy and developed mesenteric ischemia with septic shock died within 60 days of surgery. It was the only procedure associated with death in our study (2.9% surgical mortality). In this patient, an unplanned extended pneumonectomy up to the superior vena cava and carina with sleeve resection was necessary in order to enable a R0 resection of a cT3 neoplastic lesion. The second right pneumonectomy was a total pneumonectomy after R1 lobectomy for a cT1 neoplastic lesion. Arterial sleeve resection was not possible given the postoperative inflammatory response of the pre-tumour tissues. Investigation of false positives

During the three rounds of screening, 45 subjects were screened positive but were not subsequently diagnosed with cancer, giving a false positive rate of 2.8% (n = 45/1591). A total of 72 complementary explorations were performed in these 45 patients.

Most investigations carried out were non-invasive: 37.5% PET scans (n = 27/72), 25% complementary CT scans (n = 18/72), and 1.4% magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans (n = 1/72). Regarding invasive explorations, there were 30.5% flexible endoscopies (n = 22/72), 1.4% rigid bronchoscopies (n = 1/72), 1.4% endobronchial ultrasounds (n = 1/72) and 2.7% video-assisted thoracic surgeries (VATSs) (n = 2/72). Invasive explorations were performed on 55.5% of subjects with false positive screening results (n = 25/45): 48.9% underwent bronchial endoscopy (n = 22/45), 2.2% rigid bronchoscopy and endobronchial ultrasound (n = 1/45) and 4.4% underwent VATS (2/45). The rate of invasive investigations (excluding surgery) in those without lung cancer was 1.9% (n = 24/1212). The two VATS procedures were performed on benign lesions (an adenomatoid cystic malformation and a hamartochondroma) and involved 0.16% of participants without lung cancer (n = 2/1212). These additional investigations did not result in any severe complications. Of note, no diagnostic transthoracic needle biopsy was performed before surgery in our study. The most frequent final diagnoses were infectious pathology (24.4%, n = 11/45), hamartoma (13.3%, n = 6/45), and bronchiectasis (13.3%. n = 6/45) (Table 3). Retrospectively, one participant diagnosed with Stage I lung cancer at T2 had a nodule visible at T1, labelled false positive. By cross-referencing our data with that of the Somme cancer registry, we found no interval cancer. The sensitivity for the three study rounds was 100% (95% CI 92–100), the specificity 97% (95% CI 96–98), the negative predictive value 100% (95% CI 100–100) and the positive predictive value 48% (95% CI 37–59) (Table 4). Discussion

DEP KP80 is a French study evaluating the implementation of organised lung cancer screening on a territorial scale, under real-life conditions.

Lung cancers detected in DEP KP80 were localised in 71.4% of cases (Stage I/II) and 4.8% were carcinoma in situ (CIS). Our results are close to the 68% of localised Stage I/II reported in the NELSON trial and the 70% mentioned in the NLST study.5,6 They are higher than those of the European MILD, DANTE, LUSI, ITALUNG and DLCT studies, in which the rate of Stage I/II varied from 54% to 69%.7,26,27,30,31 Only the UK Lung Cancer Screening (UKLS) study presented a higher rate, with 83% of Stage I/II cases.28

Overall, 80.9% of patients diagnosed with lung cancer underwent thoracic surgery, limited in eight cases out of 10 to a lobectomy. Our high thoracic surgery rate, which remains the standard treatment for localised cancers, is comparable to that of the MILD7 and PanCan studies,32 in which 80% and 75% of patients underwent surgery, respectively. However, it is higher than the 60.6% of thoracic surgery in the NLST study and the 67.7% reported in the NELSON trial.5,6

These results, in terms of diagnostic stage and surgical treatment, confirm the real-life efficacy of lung cancer screening, the main aim of which is to diagnose early stages accessible to curative treatment.

One of the frequently cited drawbacks of cancer screening is overdiagnosis, which corresponds to the detection of indolent pre-cancerous or cancerous lesions which, if undiagnosed, would have remained clinically silent until death from another cause. This overdiagnosis is mostly linked to the discovery of ground-glass lesions corresponding to adenocarcinomas in situ. After being initially estimated at 18.5% in the NLST study,5 this rate was finally re-evaluated at 3% after a follow-up of 11 years.33 The CISNET modelling of the updated USPSTF criteria for lung cancer screening estimates this overdiagnosis at 6%.34 In our study, we found only two in situ cancers (4.8%), both corresponding to ground-glass lesions.

Another potential drawback of lung cancer screening is the false positive rate. In our study, it was estimated at 2.8%. This rate is significantly lower than the 23.3% false positive rate noted in the three rounds of the NLST study, and is close to the 2.6% false positive rate in the first round of the NELSON trial.5,6 The positive predictive value observed in DEP KP80 was 48% (95% CI 37–59), comparable to the 43.5% of the NELSON trial, and better than the 3.8% of NLST study. The concordance of our results with those of the NELSON trial is explained by the use of similar algorithms integrating the concept of indeterminate screening for nodules between 5 and 10 mm, volumetry, and doubling time measurement, and confirms the value of this screening strategy. Moreover, the sensitivity (100% [95% CI 92–100]), specificity (97% [95% CI 96–98]), and negative predictive value (100% [95% CI 100–100]) of the DEP KP80 study were within the average of the corresponding values in previous studies. Jonas DE et al. reported in their literature review for the US Preventive Services Task Force recommendations on lung cancer screening that LDCT sensitivity ranged from 59% to 100%, specificity from 26.4% to 99.7%, negative predictive value from 97.7% to 100%, and positive predictive value from 33% to 43.5%.10 The variability of these results is most probably due to differences among studies regarding inclusion criteria, decision-making algorithms, and screening protocols.

As in the NLST study, most investigations carried out on subjects with false positive screening results consisted of non-invasive explorations, with complementary imaging tests performed in 63.6% of cases. Only two VATS procedures were carried out for benign lesions (an adenomatoid cystic malformation and a hamartochondroma), representing 5.5% of surgical procedures, and involving 4.4% of subjects with false positive screening results. The rate of minor invasive investigations (excluding surgery) in those without lung cancer was only 1.9%. No patient in our study suffered from severe complications following invasive exploration. The rate of thoracic surgery for benign lesions is a quality criterion for lung cancer screening. In our study, it was particularly low. Indeed, in the five main European randomised controlled trials, surgery revealed between 8.9% and 27.2% of benign lesions detected by LDCT7,26,30 and this rate was 24.3% in the NLST study. Despite being low, our rate of futile thoracic surgery could possibly have been optimised by performing transthoracic needle biopsies. The absence of this technique in our study is justified by the fact that most patients with a positive screening result were suspected of having stage I or II cancer and did not require biopsies before surgery, as stated in the guidelines published by the British Thoracic Society and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network.35,36

The lung cancer detection rate in DEP KP80 was 2.6% at the end of three rounds. This rate is in line with the average of the main randomised controlled trials, in which it varies between 2.1% and 5.2%.5,6,26, 27, 28, 29 The decrease in the detection rate observed between the first and third rounds justifies the latest French recommendations proposing LDCT screening 2 years in a row, followed by biennial screening in subjects whose first two screenings were negative in the absence of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or emphysema. Furthermore, the MILD study showed a comparable reduction in lung cancer mortality between annual and biennial screenings, with similar Stage I and surgery rates.7

It should also be noted that most patients with lung cancer screened in the second or third round had tested negative in previous rounds. Therefore, it appears that a false positive or intermediate screening result is not predictive of cancer risk in following rounds, and that, conversely, a negative screening should not be falsely reassuring and should encourage participation in subsequent rounds. The discovery of cancers at each annual screening scan in the NLST study (1% at T0, 0.7% at T1, and 0.9% at T2), the detection of 0.8% cancers in the NELSON trial in the fourth round 5.5 years after initial inclusion, and the results of the MILD study showing a reduction in mortality when screening is continued for 10 years justify the regular continuation of screening regardless of the results of previous rounds.5, 6, 7

We noted an important drop in participation from the first round (75.4%) to the second (42.8%) and third rounds (31%). Participation is the cornerstone of the efficacy of organised screening campaigns. In fact, the relatively low uptake in the second and third rounds is in line with data found in the real-life literature. Indeed, while participation is usually high in randomised controlled trials (over 90% for the four rounds of the NELSON trial and over 95% for the three rounds of the NLST study), the situation is quite different outside clinical trials.

Lopez-Olivo et al., in their systematic review and meta-analysis of participation in lung cancer screening in the USA outside randomised trials, found an overall participation rate of 55% regardless of the follow-up period.37 The participation rate was 30% (95% CI [18%, 44%]) 12 months after the initial scan. Only one study reported a participation rate at 24 and 36 months of 38% and 28%, respectively.38 In this meta-analysis, the barriers to participation were active smoking and race (non-white subjects). Subjects aged between 65 and 73 years and those with a higher level of education had a better participation rate. In our study, we noted that older subjects participated more in the different rounds. In DEP KP80, the absence of a reminder letter for the second and third rounds, as well as the need to consult one's GP to obtain the prescription for the screening scan at T1 and T2, were probably obstacles to participation. Informing and raising awareness among healthcare professionals and the general public regarding the importance of screening and its continuation after the initial scan until non-compliance with the inclusion criteria is essential to optimise participation and the efficacy of lung cancer screening. Particular attention should be paid to raising awareness among the youngest subjects (aged 50–60) and participants with false positive screening results, as these patients seem less likely to attend the various screening rounds. Reminder and re-invitation strategies in the English SUMMIT cohort have proved effective in improving participation.39 Montes U et al. also showed that the availability of a lung cancer screening coordinator is associated with an increased compliance rate, most likely thanks to reminders and follow up contacts with participants.40

While the involvement of GPs in lung cancer screening is considered essential in many countries,41 the organisation of lung cancer screening cannot be left to health professionals alone. The European Respiratory Society and the European Society of Radiology have recently recommended that lung cancer screening should be centralised within identified multidisciplinary structures bringing together PNs, radiologists, GPs, tobaccologists, thoracic surgeons, etc., respecting standardised protocols for nodule interpretation and management, as well as quality criteria for LDCT scans.11 In France, centres managing existing organised screening programs (breast, colon, and cervix) should be integrated into these structures, bringing in their expertise in terms of logistics and database management. Ideally, mobile teams, whose efficacy has been demonstrated in the United Kingdom in particular, should be involved in the system. They enable us to get as close as possible to the most at-risk populations, thus avoiding inequalities in access to screening.42,43

Nevertheless, the DEP KP80 study had some limitations. This observational cohort study was inherently subject to bias, which we reduced by using appropriate statistical methods and models. For this study, we did not collect data from the patients who did not attend their appointments, on ethnicity, smoking status (number of pack-years, current or former smokers), level of experience and number of readings performed by radiologists. However, these missing data were not essential to the main research question and their absence was unlikely to significantly affect our results. The study was non-randomised, uncontrolled, and the sample size was relatively small. However, we did not seek to evaluate the impact of screening on mortality, which has previously been done in the NLST and NELSON trials, but rather to investigate the feasibility and effectiveness of this screening method on a regional scale in France. The strengths of our study resided in its multicentric nature and the fact that existing patient pathways were respected, and screening participants were managed by their usual healthcare professionals (GPs, PNs, radiologists, thoracic surgeons, oncologists, etc.). No dedicated MDT or specific management circuit was set up for this study. The files of participants with positive screening results were presented during existing MDTs. The radiologists participating in DEP KP80 were general radiologists, not necessarily specialised in thoracic imaging, but who had signed a charter of good practice for LDCT and were committed to following the decision-making algorithms for the management of pulmonary nodules.

Our study, based on an existing healthcare system, enabled us to assess the feasibility and efficacy of organised lung cancer screening under real-life conditions, and to draw pragmatic conclusions from the results and any organisational difficulties.

In summary, the DEP KP80 study showed the real-life efficacy of lung cancer screening by LDCT in diagnosing a majority of early tumours that are candidates for surgery.

The design of our study, using existing healthcare circuits, showed the feasibility and possible rapid implementation of this screening. The drop in participation in the second and third rounds highlighted the need to optimise the organisation, particularly through dedicated multidisciplinary structures. In the future, the contribution of artificial intelligence, biomarkers, and risk prediction models may improve the efficiency of healthcare professional resources and thus optimise participation, which remains the cornerstone of any screening program.
