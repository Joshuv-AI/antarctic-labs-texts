A Tight Squeeze – Cardiac Tamponade in a Scleroderma Patient
Authors: Ifrah Nadeem, Charles E. Geringer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Systemic Sclerosis (SSc) is an autoimmune connective tissue disease marked by multiorgan fibrosis, including cutaneous involvement, and vasculopathy. Primary heart involvement in systemic sclerosis (SSc-pHI) includes heart failure, arrhythmia, pericarditis, pericardial effusion, valve disorders, and myocarditis. SSc-pHI remains a leading cause of mortality in this population. Pericardial effusion in SSc is usually associated with heart failure, pulmonary hypertension, and end-stage kidney disease. Cardiac tamponade is a rare initial presentation of SSc-pHI but carries a worse prognosis. Diagnosis is often challenging due to subclinical manifestations and requires advanced imaging modalities such as transthoracic echocardiography with tissue doppler and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging. Further research is necessary to elucidate pathogenic mechanisms and identify biomarkers essential for targeted therapy and improved clinical recommendations for this subgroup. This report presents a patient with SSc who presented with cardiac tamponade leading to cardiac arrest.

Introduction

Systemic sclerosis (SSc) is a connective tissue disease with a global pooled prevalence of 17.6 per 100,000 individuals (95% CI 15.1, 20.5), I2 = 100%.1 Cardiac involvement in SSc (SSc-HI) is relatively common and is associated with a significantly worse prognosis, with a 5-year mortality rate reaching up to 75%.2,3 The reported prevalence of SSc-HI varies widely, from 5% in cases defined by systolic dysfunction or congestive heart failure to as high as 80% when myocardial fibrosis, myocardial inflammation, or contraction band necrosis are included.2 Large pericardial effusion in SSc, particularly when associated with tamponade physiology, is associated with a poor prognosis.4 This report presents a patient with SSc who developed a large pericardial effusion and tamponade, resulting in cardiac arrest.

Case

A 32-year-old female with stage 3b chronic kidney disease, diffuse cutaneous systemic sclerosis (SSc) [ANA 1:1280, nucleolar pattern; sclerodactyly; Raynaud's phenomenon], and scleroderma renal crisis (SRC) presented to the emergency department with a one-week history of dyspnea, chest pain, and palpitations. SSc was diagnosed three years prior, and initial treatment with mycophenolate was discontinued by the patient, leading to disease progression and worsening cutaneous manifestations. Home medications included amlodipine, irbesartan, carvedilol, hydrochlorothiazide, vitamin B12, and folic acid.

On presentation, vital signs were: temperature 37.4 °C (99.4 °F), heart rate 106 bpm, blood pressure 199/140 mmHg, respiratory rate 20/min, and oxygen saturation 97% on room air. Physical examination revealed scleroderma-related skin changes, including salt-and-pepper pigmentation on the chest, abdomen, and arms, as well as sclerodactyly and nailfold capillary dropout (Fig. 1). No digital ulcers or telangiectasias were observed.

Cardiopulmonary examination demonstrated tachycardia without murmurs and clear lung fields. Laboratory results are given in Table 1. An electrocardiogram demonstrated a regular rhythm at 99 bpm, normal axis, left ventricular hypertrophy, and T-wave inversions in leads V4–V6. An elevated D-dimer (1869 ng/mL, N = 0–500 ng/mL) prompted CT pulmonary angiography, which excluded pulmonary embolism but identified a large pericardial effusion (Fig. 2A and B). Echocardiography showed a normal left ventricular ejection fraction, marked concentric left ventricular hypertrophy, moderate diastolic dysfunction, and a large circumferential pericardial effusion (Fig. 2C and D), without evidence of tamponade.

The patient was admitted to the medical intensive care unit. Pericardiocentesis was performed, yielding 1175 cc of clear fluid. Eight hours later, the patient experienced cardiac arrest. Bedside echocardiography confirmed reaccumulation of pericardial effusion. Emergent repeat pericardiocentesis and pericardial drain placement were performed. Vasopressor support was required, and an additional 1.2 L of fluid were drained over the subsequent two days. Pericardial fluid analysis is given in Table 1. Patient was treated with colchicine for pericarditis in combination with mycophenolate for scleroderma crisis at 250 mg twice daily, which was subsequently uptitrated to 500 mg twice daily. Blood pressure was managed with amlodipine and losartan. The patient was successfully extubated and discharged to rehabilitation.

Discussion

SSc-HI may manifest as primary heart involvement (SSc-pHI), characterized by cardiac inflammation that results in myocardial fibrosis, conduction system abnormalities, pericardial effusion, pericarditis, or, less commonly, valve dysfunction. Secondary cardiac abnormalities generally arise as consequences of systemic disorders such as renovascular hypertension or pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH).5

The precise pathophysiology of SSc-pHI remains unclear. Intermittent vasospasm leading to ischemic necrosis and reperfusion injury, which subsequently causes myocardial fibrosis, is considered a major contributing factor. Cytokines such as interleukin-1 and interleukin-6 are important mediators of cardiac inflammation and fibrosis, suggesting potential therapeutic targets in SSc-related cardiovascular inflammation.5 The pathogenesis of SSc-related pericardial effusion is distinct from that observed in rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus. Pericardial fluid in SSc is typically noninflammatory, lacking autoantibodies, immune complexes, or complement depletion.6 Evidence from a small study indicates that SSc pericardial fluid is usually exudative, with lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels exceeding 200 U/L, a fluid-serum LDH ratio greater than 0.6, and a fluid-serum protein ratio above 0.5.7 Histological examination often demonstrates generalized fibrotic thickening, adhesions, and perivascular inflammation.7

SSc-related pericardial disease may present as fibrinous or fibrous pericarditis, adhesions, effusion, and, in rare instances, tamponade or constrictive pericarditis. Pericardial effusion frequently co-occurs with PAH, heart failure, and end-stage kidney disease. The reported incidence varies depending on the diagnostic criteria used. A tertiary center study identified symptomatic pericardial effusion in up to 6.9% of patients with SSc, while cardiac tamponade was rare (0.2%) but more common among patients with atrial fibrillation.8 Although pericardial effusion typically develops later in the disease course, it can occasionally be the initial manifestation.9,10 Therefore, SSc should be considered in cases of unexplained pericardial effusion. Most pericardial effusions in SSc are small, asymptomatic, and not clinically significant, with clinical symptoms present in only 5%–16% of cases.4 However, even small, rapidly accumulating effusions can result in tamponade due to the limited compliance of the fibrotic pericardium. Therefore, ongoing monitoring is crucial, particularly in patients with SSc-pHI. Large, hemodynamically significant effusions may lead to heart failure and kidney dysfunction, most commonly due to renal hypoperfusion and diuretic therapy. The development of pericardial effusion in SSc, particularly in association with the presence of pulmonary hypertension, is associated with high mortality and morbidity. One study demonstrated that, in these patients, the coexistence of PAH with pericardial effusion and cardiac tamponade was associated with increased mortality, with adjusted odds ratios of 2.75 (2.64–2.86, p < 0.0001) and 1.55 (1.30–1.84, p < 0.001), respectively.9

Echocardiography serves as the primary diagnostic modality for pericardial involvement. Exudative pericarditis can be effectively identified, often indicated by electrocardiogram changes such as ST-T abnormalities and low-voltage QRS complexes, or by chest X-ray findings that reveal a globular cardiac silhouette. Modern non-invasive imaging techniques, including cardiac magnetic resonance imaging and echocardiographic strain analysis, enable the detection of subclinical cardiac disease, supporting early intervention and strategies to prevent progression to overt SSc-pHI.

Symptomatic management includes the use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and renal support.11 Corticosteroids generally have limited efficacy but may be beneficial in selected cases.12 Immunosuppressive therapy constitutes the mainstay of treatment for SSc-HI. Diuretics should be used cautiously in heart failure due to the risk of renal complications. Following the diagnosis of pericardial effusion, serial echocardiography is required to monitor progression, assess hemodynamic impact, and evaluate treatment response. Pericardiocentesis is indicated for cardiac tamponade, while a pericardial window or pericardiectomy may be necessary in up to 35% of patients.4 Routine pericardial drainage procedures typically yield low diagnostic value in SSc-associated pericardial effusion, even in cases of large effusions without tamponade or suspected purulent pericarditis, and do not provide clear therapeutic benefit. Clinical outcomes in these cases are determined by the underlying disease and are not improved by routine pericardial fluid drainage. In contrast, cardiac tamponade may be associated with an acute mortality rate of up to 13%, necessitating urgent intervention.4

Conclusion

Pericardial effusion is common in SSc, although it rarely causes symptoms. Cardiac tamponade may develop rapidly due to effusion reaccumulation, irrespective of effusion size. It is associated with high acute mortality, and pericardiocentesis, with or without drain placement or surgical window, is the treatment of choice.
