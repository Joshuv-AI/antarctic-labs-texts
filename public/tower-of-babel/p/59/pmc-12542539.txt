Rapid discrimination of Mycobacterium tuberculosis and non-tuberculous mycobacteria disease via interpretive machine learning analysis of routine laboratory tests
Authors: Jia-Wei Tang, Xue-Song Xiong, Ting-Ting Huang, Yu-Lu Zhang, Lin-Fei Yao, Wen-Wen Zhang, Yun-Yun Xie, Quan-Fa Liang, Zhi-Xuan Tan, Kun Jiang, Xin Liu, Liang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesRapid discrimination of infections caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) and non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) is crucial in clinical settings. Despite overlapping clinical and radiological features, the two require markedly different therapeutic approaches and public health responses. Current laboratory methods are time-consuming and complex, underscoring the urgent need for a simple and efficient diagnostic tool to inform public health decision-making.MethodsDemographic, haematological and biochemical data were collected from two hospitals in Jiangsu province, China, between December 2018 and October 2024. A total of 400 patients were included in the training cohort, with 66 patients used for external validation. Six machine learning models were developed using routine laboratory features, and their performance was evaluated using multiple metrics.ResultsThe random forest (RF) model outperformed others using 49 routine lab features, achieving 82.71% accuracy in the internal cohort and 87.69% in external validation. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) model identified the top 10 critical features influencing model decisions, namely, chloride, sodium, gender, prealbumin, high-density lipoprotein, procalcitonin, albumin, globulin, total protein and creatine. Based on these indicators, an interactive web-based tool was developed (https://mtb-ntm.streamlit.app).DiscussionThe features identified by the model align with established clinical parameters and existing studies. Certain previously underestimated variables, such as Cl and Na, exhibited substantial importance in distinguishing between MTB and NTM, offering valuable insights for the development of decision-support tools.ConclusionRoutine laboratory indicators coupled with the RF model demonstrated potential capacity as an auxiliary diagnostic tool for discriminating MTB and NTM disease, offering effective medical support in resource-limited and remote settings.Keywords: Data Mining, Electronic Data Processing, Medical Informatics

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB), has long been a leading cause of infectious disease-related mortality worldwide.1 MTB is primarily transmitted among humans via the inhalation of aerosolised particles expelled by individuals with active pulmonary disease.2 Studies have shown that regional TB incidence is closely associated with sociodemographic characteristics and socioeconomic inequalities.3 Key determinants underlying the disparity in TB incidence include income, educational attainment, occupational categories and population density.4 Non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM), a group of opportunistic pathogens comprising approximately 180 species not belonging to the MTB complex (MTBC), are capable of causing infections in virtually any part of the human body.5 Epidemiological data indicate a marked rise in the prevalence of NTM diseases across both developed and developing nations.6 In Europe and the USA, the incidence and prevalence of NTM pulmonary disease have increased from 3.3 and 6.8 to 6.0 and 11.7 cases per 100 000 population.7 8 In China, a nationwide prospective study revealed that approximately 1 in 15 patients diagnosed with MTB also harboured an NTM infection.6 This alarming situation underscores the urgent need for more effective anti-TB strategies.

Conventional laboratory methods for diagnosing MTB infection include smear microscopy and mycobacterial culture. Direct smear microscopy remains the most widely used method for TB diagnosis worldwide; however, it suffers from low sensitivity.9 Mycobacterial culture is still regarded as the diagnostic gold standard, although its utility is limited by the prolonged time required for bacterial growth.10 Molecular diagnostic methods offer a reliable and rapid alternative for detecting mycobacteria in clinical laboratories. For instance, the WHO-endorsed Xpert MTB/RIF (Cepheid) assay detects the MTBC as well as key genetic determinants of rifampicin resistance.11 However, the main drawback of Xpert is that it is less sensitive than sputum culture and does not detect NTM infections.12 Currently, the most accurate diagnostic approach involves high-throughput PCR–DNA sequencing targeting the hsp65, rpoB and 16S rRNA genes,13 which provides high specificity but entails significant equipment and operational costs. In resource-limited settings, insufficient laboratory capacity and delayed access to test results pose substantial barriers to effective TB control. Therefore, the development of efficient, cost-effective and accessible diagnostic methods for both MTB and NTM is critical for enabling clinicians to determine the need for initiating anti-TB therapy.

Numerous studies have previously reported the potential clinical value of routine laboratory parameters, such as conventional blood tests, biochemical assays and coagulation profiles, in aiding the diagnosis of infectious diseases.14 However, individual laboratory parameters alone are insufficient to distinguish between infected and non-infected individuals. The integration of these parameters with machine learning (ML) techniques has shown promise in enhancing diagnostic performance. By uncovering complex relationships among clinical features, ML models can enable precise prediction of infection outcomes.15 For instance, Chang et al collected complete blood count, differential count and blood culture parameters to develop a predictive model for bacteremia. The CatBoost model achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve exceeding 0.8 in both prospective and external cohorts and provided clinically valuable discriminatory indicators.16 In the context of TB diagnosis, Luo et al developed a gradient boosting machine (GBM) model based on routine blood test parameters to differentiate active TB from latent TB infection, achieving a sensitivity of 87.63% and specificity of 91.34% in an independent cohort.17 These findings substantiate the feasibility of ML in the diagnosis of infectious diseases. In this study, we aim to develop an interpretable ML model that integrates demographic features and laboratory tests to accurately predict MTB and NTM disease status in patients, thereby optimising clinical management and therapeutic decision-making. Ultimately, we seek to establish a user-friendly online diagnostic platform for MTB and NTM, providing an effective auxiliary diagnostic tool for remote or underserved regions.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective study focused on the derivation and validation of predictive models in patients with mycobacterial disease, including those diagnosed with either MTB or NTM at Xuzhou Infectious Disease Hospital and Huai’an Infectious Disease Hospital between December 2018 and October 2024. The detailed species information of NTM patients from the two hospitals is provided in online supplemental Tables S1 and S2. All cases were tested for MTB and NTM using PCR-reverse dot blot hybridisation (Yaneng BioScience, Shenzhen, China), based on specimens obtained from sputum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid. Clinical and laboratory data encompassed demographic information, complete blood counts and serum biochemical parameters. Extreme outliers, values that were excessively low or high, were flagged for further examination. Gender data were binarised and encoded as 0 and 1. Patients infected with mycobacteria other than MTB or NTM, such as Mycobacterium leprae, Mycobacterium lepromatosis and other species, including Mycobacterium bovis and Mycobacterium africanum, were excluded. Cases with substantial missing clinical data were also omitted.

Model development and comparison

The training data were split into an internal training set (70%) and an internal validation set (30%). Scikit-learn (V.1.3.0) was employed for hyperparameter tuning and model construction. Six ML algorithms, adaptive boosting, decision tree, light GBM, random forest (RF), support vector machine and eXtreme Gradient Boosting, were selected to predict MTB and NTM disease. GridSearchCV was applied to optimise model performance and identify the optimal combination of hyperparameters. The parameter search space and the best-performing combinations are detailed in online supplemental table 3. Model performance was evaluated using several commonly adopted metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, specificity and the ROC curve. Fivefold cross-validation was conducted to assess model robustness further. Additionally, calibration plots were used to evaluate the agreement between predicted probabilities and actual outcomes across different models. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was employed to assess the potential clinical utility of the models, illustrating the net benefit across a range of decision thresholds. Furthermore, as multicollinearity may affect the predictive accuracy of the model, this study employed the corr function to test correlation coefficient thresholds ranging from 0.6 to 1.0 in increments of 0.5, constructed correlation matrices and evaluated model performance under each threshold. We followed the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis statement.

External validation and interpretability analysis

The external validation data from two independent hospitals were collected using the same inclusion and exclusion criteria as the training cohort, aiming to evaluate the model’s performance on previously unknown data. The number of features collected is identical to that after multicollinearity processing. Given that the purpose of external validation is to assess the generalisability of the model rather than to facilitate further model tuning, fivefold cross-validation was not applied to the validation cohort. Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) model is employed to address the ‘black-box’ problem of ML algorithms, the summary_plot visualises the overall feature importance ranking across the model, while the dependence_plot quantifies the contribution of an individual feature to the prediction. Positive SHAP values indicate that the feature increases the predicted value, whereas negative values suggest a decreasing effect.

Webpage tool development

A diagnostic model using the top 10 most important features identified by SHAP in the RF prediction process. Leveraging the open-source Python library Streamlit (V.1.32.0), we deployed the model to the cloud. Users can input the actual values of the required variables to determine the likelihood of MTB or NTM disease. See https://github.com/4forfull/MTB-NTM for the RF model file (.pkl) and the tool interface code.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean±SD or median (IQR). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess whether the data followed a normal distribution. For normally distributed continuous variables, a t-test was applied for comparison, and the results are expressed as mean±SD. For non-normally distributed continuous variables, a Mann-Whitney U test was used, and the results are presented as median (IQR). Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, with results presented as counts and frequencies. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of baseline characteristics between MTB and NTM cohorts

A total of 626 patients diagnosed with either MTB or NTM disease between December 2018 and October 2024 were enrolled. 39 patients with infections other than MTB or NTM were excluded, along with 121 patients with excessive missing data. 22 non-numerical clinical variables and 69 variables with extensive missing values were also removed, resulting in a final set of 49 features retained for model construction. The dataset was split into internal and external cohorts. The internal cohort consisted of 200 patients each for the MTB and NTM groups, while the external cohort included 23 MTB patients and 43 NTM patients. The study workflow is illustrated in figure 1.

Model performance evaluation and feature selection analysis

After parameter optimisation, the performance of six ML models was evaluated on the internal validation set. The results showed that the RF model achieved the highest overall performance, with an accuracy of 82.71%, precision of 82.71%, recall of 82.41%, F1-score of 82.58%, specificity of 75.13%, and a fivefold cross-validation score of 79.32% (figure 2A and online supplemental Table S4). The ROC curve further supported the robust discriminative capacity of the RF model, with an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.8867 (figure 2B). The calibration curve demonstrates satisfactory consistency between predicted risk and observed risk of the RF (figure 2C). The results of the DCA demonstrate that the net benefit of the RF model (red line) consistently exceeds that of the ‘treat-all’ strategy (blue line), indicating that the RF model effectively identifies true positive cases while controlling the number of false positives (figure 2D). This highlights the practical utility of the method in real-world decision-making. To further enhance its performance, we analysed the correlations among the initial raw variables and removed redundant features (figure 2E). When the correlation threshold was set at 0.75, the number of features was reduced from 49 to 45, excluding total bilirubin, triglycerides, platelet count and lymphocyte ratio. Under this condition, the RF model achieved an accuracy of 85.57%, a precision of 85.57%, a recall of 85.20% and an F1-score of 85.41%, with a substantial improvement in model performance.

External validation and interpretability analysis of the diagnostic model

The external validation results from the two hospitals indicated that RF achieved scores exceeding 80% across all five metrics, with accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score and specificity recorded at 87.69%, 87.69%, 86.26%, 87.69% and 81.82%, respectively, indicating superior accuracy and robustness in predicting MTB and NTM tasks. The AUC Score for RF was 0.9091. The confusion matrix revealed an average prediction accuracy of 83%, with 18% of MTB and 16% of NTM cases mispredicted (figure 3A–C and online supplemental Table S5). 45 features retained after multicollinearity filtering were used for model interpretability. As shown in SHAP summary plots (figure 3D), the top 10 contributors are chloride (Cl), sodium (Na), gender, PA, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), PCT, ALB, globulin, TP and creatine. Additionally, SHAP dependence plots were employed to elucidate further how individual features influence model output (figure 3E). SHAP values greater than zero indicate a prediction toward the positive class (NTM). For example, Cl<104 mmol/L and Na<136 mmol/L drive the model’s prediction toward the NTM class.

Discussion

In this study, we conducted a retrospective analysis of the clinical characteristics of patients infected with MTB and NTM and developed six machine-learning models. Multiple evaluation methods and metrics demonstrated that the RF algorithm exhibited strong discriminative power and clinical utility. Subsequently, we applied Pearson correlation coefficients to eliminate multicollinearity among features and assessed the performance of the RF model in external validation. In addition, we employed SHAP to visualise the predictive model, facilitating the interpretation of key features and their threshold values in the model’s decision-making process. Finally, the constructed RF model was deployed to the cloud, and a user-friendly decision-support system was developed.

Current diagnostic approaches for TB primarily rely on microbiological methods, such as sputum smear microscopy and culture of mycobacteria on liquid or solid media.17 While smear microscopy is rapid and cost-effective, its sensitivity is limited, particularly in cases without cavitary lesions. Culture, regarded as the diagnostic gold standard due to its higher sensitivity, typically requires 2–6 weeks to yield results.5 Several omics-based approaches have identified novel biomarkers for distinguishing MTB and NTM. Pathway analyses of the proteomic and lipidomic profiles revealed significant regulation of the TB pathway, sphingolipid signalling and adiponectin signalling. Integration of immune-related protein and lipid biomarkers markedly enhanced the diagnostic specificity and sensitivity in distinguishing NTM from MTB.18 Targeted metabolomics further identified specific metabolites, such as meso-hydroxyheme and itaconate anhydride, that robustly discriminated between pre-extensively and extensively drug-resistant TB strains,19 yet these biomarkers lack rigorous and reliable external validation. ML approaches that integrate epidemiological features, risk factors and laboratory diagnostic parameters have also been employed to transform complex datasets into actionable diagnostic models. For instance, Takeuchi et al leveraged immune cell profiles, inflammatory mediators, chemokines and tissue injury to guide the management and treatment of bacterial infections in peritoneal dialysis patients.20 However, the mode’s applicability is constrained by the need for comprehensive and high-resolution clinical data, limiting its implementation to well-equipped hospital settings. In contrast, models developed using routine laboratory parameters offer several practical advantages. In this study, we highlight the potential of routine laboratory parameters in differentiating between MTB and NTM disease. A total of 49 clinical features from 466 patients were collected to construct ML models. Among various evaluation metrics, the RF algorithm demonstrated the best performance, achieving accuracies of 82.71% and 87.69% in internal and external validations, respectively, with robust sensitivity and specificity. Given that an excessive number of features may hinder its clinical applicability and compromise its accuracy,21 we conducted a Pearson correlation analysis to identify highly correlated features. The results showed that setting the correlation coefficient threshold to 0.75 yielded the best performance for the RF model, achieving an accuracy of 85.57%.

However, the acceptability of clinical diagnostic models by both clinicians and patients, as well as their integration into shared decision-making processes, constitutes a fundamental prerequisite for translating such models into clinical practice.22 We used DCA to evaluate the clinical utility of all models. Among them, the optimal diagnostic model, RF, demonstrated a net benefit in identifying patients requiring treatment for either MTB or NTM disease within a probability threshold range of 0.51–0.9. This finding suggests that, in scenarios where minimising missed NTM disease is a priority, a lower threshold may be preferred. To enhance clinicians’ trust in ML-based decisions and improve model transparency, the SHAP method was employed to provide both global and local interpretability.23 Contribution analyses for each feature in the model revealed findings that align with previous reports. Prior research, accounting for environmental, geographical and lifestyle factors, identified gender as the only retained variable in statistical models, with female patients exhibiting a higher susceptibility to NTM infection.24 Regarding HDL, patients with NTM pulmonary disease presented higher HDL levels relative to those with drug-resistant NTM.25 The procalcitonin biomarker demonstrated high specificity before and after MTB treatment.26 For ALB, evidence indicates that patients with NTM infection often display compromised nutritional status, with levels frequently falling below the normal range (40.00–55.00 g/L); in this study, the identified ALB cut-off was <39.00 g/L, closely matching clinical observations.27 Intriguingly, Cl and Na emerged as the two most important predictors in this study. While there is currently no prior evidence supporting these variables as independent discriminators between MTB and NTM, variations in their measured values appear to be associated with infection by either pathogen. Ultimately, to maximise generalisability and facilitate the translation of this research into real-world clinical practice, we developed a free webpage tool for clinicians and patients. Users can input the 10 real indicators to calculate the predicted probability of MTB or NTM.

Several limitations remain to be addressed. First, this study was conducted at only two hospitals with a relatively small sample size, lacking comprehensive multicentre validation and potentially introducing selection bias. Robust cross-validation and external validation indicate that future studies would benefit from multicentre, large-sample, randomised controlled trials to provide sufficient statistical power for the development of models with high accuracy and strong generalisability. Second, clinical data on immunodeficiency, such as HIV infection, organ transplantation or immunosuppressive therapy, were not available. As immunosuppression is a known risk factor for NTM disease, this may have introduced unmeasured confounding and limited the generalisability of the model across clinical populations. Third, all variables in this study were derived from routine laboratory tests, while many emerging biomarkers with promising diagnostic potential warrant inclusion in future models to enhance diagnostic performance. Fourth, samples with negative results on PCR-reverse dot blot hybridisation or smear microscopy were not included in model development, which limits the applicability of the model to patients with confirmed mycobacterial disease. Moreover, this study focused solely on distinguishing between MTB and NTM, without further subclassification of specific species such as Mycobacterium avium complex and Mycobacterium kansasii, and was also unable to differentiate between infected and non-infected individuals or identify cases of NTM–MTB coinfection. With continued experimental efforts and data accumulation, a more comprehensive diagnostic model for TB could be developed.

Conclusion

This study confirms that ML diagnostic models based on routine laboratory parameters hold promise as a tool to advance the development of diagnostic approaches for MTB and NTM. Our model offers a more accessible and cost-effective clinical application for the diagnosis and management of MTB and NTM, particularly in resource-limited settings.
