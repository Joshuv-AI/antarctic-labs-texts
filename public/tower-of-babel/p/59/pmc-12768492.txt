Studying the molecular mechanisms of tef (Eragrostis tef) antioxidant properties in human THP-1 monocytes
Authors: Anastasia V. Romanov, Tyler N. Graf, Warren Vidar, Dereje D. Jima, Nicholas H. Oberlies, Zhenquan Jia, Ayalew Ligaba-Osena
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Brown tef (Eragrostis tef) grain extracts increase cellular glutathione (GSH) in THP-1 monocytes, however, the molecular mechanisms remained unclear. In this study, we found that the tef extracts induced luciferase activity by 19-fold in HEK293 cells stably expressing the luciferase gene under the control of the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)–antioxidant response element, indicating the activation of the antioxidant pathway. RNA-seq analysis revealed that the tef extract enhanced the expression of 87 genes, including some regulated by the Nrf2 transcription factor. Differentially expressed genes included ferritin heavy chain pseudogenes, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, and solute carrier genes. Using LC-MS, we identified compounds that were differentially enriched in the active tef fraction as compared to the inactive fraction, including vernolic and α-linolenic acid, which were enhanced by over 60-fold. In summary, our findings indicate that tef grains contain phytochemicals that enhance the antioxidant levels through the Nrf2-signaling pathway.

Introduction

Oxidative stress is characterized by an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) and antioxidant defense mechanisms (Shankar & Mehendale, 2014). Reactive oxygen species (ROS), commonly referred to as free radicals, are highly reactive compounds formed from diatomic oxygen, water, and hydrogen peroxide. Maintaining a delicate equilibrium between ROS and antioxidants is vital for proper physiological functioning and metabolic processes. These free radicals are typically generated as by-products of cellular aerobic metabolism, with hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), hydroxyl radical (OH), and superoxide (O2•−) radicals being well-known examples. Various stress conditions, including metabolic disorders and dietary factors, can induce oxidative stress (Migliore & Coppedè, 2009). Environmental stressors such as ionizing and UV radiation, pollutants, xenobiotics, and drugs can significantly amplify ROS production (Pizzino et al., 2017). Similarly, acrolein has been identified as an oxidative stress contributor and ROS generator (Sun et al., 2014). The consequences of oxidative stress encompass damage to lipids, proteins, and deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) through oxidative chain reactions (Özcan, Erdal, Çakırca, & Yönden, 2015), leading to inflammation. Numerous studies underscore oxidative stress as a pivotal factor in the development of various pathological diseases, both chronic and degenerative (Pizzino et al., 2017). It has also been implicated in accelerating the aging process and contributing to the onset of acute conditions such as stroke (Liguori et al., 2018). While the issue is often associated with low antioxidant levels, the supplementation of antioxidants alone may not provide a comprehensive solution. Consequently, oxidative stress emerges as a multifaceted and overarching concern for overall health.

Our body’s defense mechanisms neutralize the detrimental effects of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and nitrogen species (NOS) (Sachdev, Ansari, Ansari, Fujita, & Hasanuzzaman, 2021). These defense mechanisms consist of two intricate systems: enzymatic and non-enzymatic. Enzymatic mechanisms involve enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase, catalase, and reductase. Nonenzymatic mechanisms encompass antioxidant molecules like glutathione (GSH), free radical scavengers, albumins, and ascorbic acid (Kleczkowski, Kluciński, Sikora, Zdanowicz, & Dziekan, 2003). Naturally occurring antioxidants are found in fruits, vegetables, seeds, and whole grains. Evaluating the levels of oxidants and antioxidants provides valuable insights into their impact on diseases.

Glutathione is present in most cells and is a tripeptide that is biosynthesized from glutamate, cysteine, and glycine (Aoyama, Watabe, & Nakaki, 2012). It is predominantly found in its reduced form (GSH) and at a minimal level in its fully oxidized form (GSSG) (Pisoschi et al., 2021), effectively counteracting oxidative stress by hydrophilically scavenging radicals and electrophiles. As the most abundant endogenous antioxidant, GSH plays a crucial role in shielding cells against oxidative stress. Additionally, GSH acts as a key determinant in redox signaling, influencing processes such as detoxification, apoptosis, and immune function (Lu, 2013). Depletion of GSH has been linked to the initiation of apoptosis, accompanied by increased ROS production (Deponte, 2017; Teskey et al., 2018). Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that glutathione supplements can prevent ROS-induced apoptosis (Purohit et al., 2007).

GSH accumulates in the cells through three mechanisms. The first involves a two-step enzymatic process, whereby γ-glutamylcysteine is formed from glutamate and cysteine as catalyzed by the enzyme glutamate cysteine ligase (GCL), comprising a catalytic (GCLC) and a modifier (GCLM) subunit. The second step, which is catalyzed by glutathione synthase, leads to the formation of GSH from γ-glutamylcysteine and glycine (Lu, 2013). The second mechanism entails the regeneration of oxidized GSSG to reduced GSH, facilitated by glutathione reductase and NADPH. The third mechanism involves the recycling of cysteine by γ-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGTP). The antioxidant role of GSH is primarily attributed to the reaction catalyzed by GPx. In these reactions, GSH is oxidized to GSSG while reducing hydrogen peroxide and lipid peroxide. The redox cycle involves the subsequent reduction of GSSG back to GSH by GSSG reductase. Glutathione peroxidase demonstrates the capacity to scavenge free radicals and detoxify hydrogen peroxide, contributing to its antioxidative function (Pizzorno, 2014).

The signaling pathway responsible for the upregulation of glutathione (GSH) is well-established. The protective properties of GSH are mediated by the activation of the Nrf2-heme oxygenase (HO) signaling pathway as documented by Kwon et al. (2019). The Nrf2 regulates hundreds of genes involved in many cellular functions, such as detoxification and the anti-inflammatory responses (He, Ru, & Wen, 2020). Under normal conditions, the Nrf2 is bound to dimeric Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1) in the cytoplasm. However, in response to reactive oxygen species (ROS), electrophiles, and many phytochemicals (Krajka-Ku’zniak & Baer-Dubowska, 2021; Suzuki & Yamamoto, 2015; Baird & Yamamoto, 2020), Nrf2 migrates to the nucleus and binds to a cis-acting DNA promoter sequence termed the antioxidant response element (ARE), which allows the transactivation of hundreds of cytoprotective genes (He et al., 2020; Zhang, 2006; Kensler, Wakabayashi, & Biswal, 2007), including the GSH biosynthetic genes. Additionally, it has been demonstrated that the constitutive activity of the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) contributes to the promotion of GSH biosynthesis (Meng et al., 2010). NF-κB plays a role in inducing the expression of proinflammatory genes associated with inflammation, cancer, and stress responses (Meng et al., 2010).

Numerous studies provide compelling evidence supporting the utilization of plants as dietary supplements to enhance human health. The consumption of fruits, vegetables, and grains has been associated with a reduced risk of chronic diseases (Boeing et al., 2012). Phytochemicals derived from diverse plant species have demonstrated antioxidant activities, as evidenced by in vitro studies. For example, extracts from Indian lettuce (Lactuca indica) exhibited significant free radical scavenging activity and demonstrated the reduction of oxidative stress and chemopreventive therapeutic properties in human promyelocytic leukemia HL-60 cells (Wang et al., 2003). Several antioxidants, including lutein, zeaxanthin, and β-carotene, have been identified in ethanol extracts of wheat varieties (Zhou, Su, & Yu, 2004). Similarly, analysis of the methanolic extract of Cymbopogon citratus leaves unveiled the presence of tannins, flavonoids, and other phenolic compounds with antioxidant activity (Unuigbe et al., 2019). Antioxidant activity was also observed in phytochemicals derived from 13 medicinal grasses, with high concentrations of phenolics and flavonoids found in Cymbopogon spp., Cymbopogon nardus, and Cenchrus ciliaris methanolic root extracts (Gebashe, Aremu, Gruz, Finnie, & Van Staden, 2020). The abundance of phytochemicals in various plants suggests a promising avenue for exploring their therapeutic potential.

Tef is an annual grass originating from Ethiopia and Eritrea. Remarkably, among the 350 Eragrostis species, it is the only one that is cultivated and boasts a rich history that spans >6000 years (Habtegebrial, Singh, & Haile, 2007; Tadele & Hibistu, 2021). Tef holds deep cultural significance, traditionally utilized in the preparation of the bread ‘injera,’ making it a vital staple crop in Ethiopia. Its prominence extends to being the leading cereal crop in terms of land use in the country (Cannarozzi et al., 2018; Ligaba-Osena et al., 2021; Minten, Tamru, Engida, & Kuma, 2016), coupled with economic superiority over other cereal crops (Lee, 2018). While globally recognized primarily as a forage crop, tef is cultivated in various countries, including the United States, Canada, Australia, and Switzerland (Flynn, 2019). Its grains boast high mineral and crude fiber content, a low glycemic index, elevated levels of essential amino acids, and an extended shelf life in comparison to other grains (Gebru, Sbhatu, & Kim, 2020). Additionally, tef grains are gluten-free, contributing to their appeal for individuals with dietary restrictions (Spaenij-Dekking, Kooy-Winkelaar, & Koning, 2005), such as those suffering from celiac diseases. In recent times, there has been a growing interest in tef grains owing to their perceived nutritional and health benefits, underscoring their multifaceted significance.

The potential of Eragrostis species as valuable sources of natural health supplements, antioxidants, and neuroprotectants has garnered attention in various studies. Tef varieties contain phenolics and flavonoids and as such have antioxidant potential (Ravisankar, Abegaz, & Awika, 2018; Reta et al., 2022). Several studies have reported the antioxidant potential of tef based on in vitro free radical scavenging assays such as 2,2′-azino-bis-(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic) acid ABTS and 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) to ascertain the correlation between the antioxidant activity and the presence of free and bound flavonoids and phenolics (Kotásková, Sumczynski, Mlček, & Valášek, 2016). However, the assessment of the extracts’ antioxidant properties using physiologically relevant mammalian cell model THP-1 monocytes has only recently been undertaken by our group (Cotter et al., 2023). Grain extracts, particularly from a brown variety, increase cellular GSH levels. Although the precise molecular mechanism underlying these antioxidant properties remains unclear, the observed increase in glutathione (GSH) was hypothesized to be linked to the antioxidant pathway (Cotter et al., 2023). The objective of this study was to elucidate the molecular and signaling mechanisms regulating elevated GSH levels in THP-1 monocytes treated with tef extracts. This investigation focused on studying the antioxidant properties of organic, hexane, and aqueous fractions obtained from the methanol-chloroform extraction. To our knowledge, this is the first report on the molecular mechanisms regulating the antioxidant activity of tef grain extract in a mammalian cell model.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and materials

Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium, fetal bovine serum (FBS), and penicillin/streptomycin were purchased from ThermoFisher Scientific (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). GSH, o-phthalaldehyde (OPT), NADPH, 3 -(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT), and Calbiochem bovine serum albumin (BSA) (MilliPore Sigma. Burlington, MA) and Coomassie Bradford protein assay reagent were purchased from (ThermoFisher Scientific). Tissue culture flasks and 24-well plates were purchased from Corning (Corning, NY). DNase I amplification grade, high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit, and PowerUp® SYBR™ Green master mix were purchased from ThermoFisher Scientific. Primers were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technology (Coralville, IA).

Tef grains and biomass

Brown and ivory tef seeds were purchased from the Teff Company (Boise, IO). The seeds were finely ground to powder using a Waring spice grinder (WEBStaurantStore, Lancaster, PA).

Extraction

Tef seed extracts were prepared as shown in Fig. 1. Brown tef seeds weighing 2 lbs. were ground for 3 min using a spice grinder. The resulting powder was suspended in 1.5 L of CHCl3:MeOH (1:1) and allowed to stand overnight at room temperature. Afterward, the solution underwent filtration and subsequent drying utilizing a rotary evaporator. The tef marc, constituting the insoluble residue, was then resuspended in 1.0 L of CHCl3 for an hour before filtering and incorporating it into the initial extraction solution for resuspension. The resultant solution was transferred to a separatory funnel to which 250 mL of MeOH and 1250 mL of H2O were added, whereupon a biphasic mixture formed. The organic (i.e., bottom) layer was separated and collected into a round-bottom flask, followed by evaporation to dryness. The resulting dried organic extract was reconstituted in 2 L of hexane:MeOH:MeCN (2:1:1) solution. This biphasic solution was vigorously shaken before being transferred to a separatory funnel. The organic layer (MeOH: MeCN) and the hexane layer were separated and collected into round-bottom flasks, followed by evaporation to dryness under vacuum. Subsequently, the extracts were allowed to dry under nitrogen for 24 h. Aliquots of the dried fractions were dissolved in DMSO to achieve a stock concentration of 50 mg/mL before being utilized for treating the THP-1 monocytes. An identical extraction protocol was applied for the ivory seeds.

Fractionation via automated flash chromatography

For further separation, the brown organic fraction was dissolved in CHCl3 and adsorbed onto Celite 545 (ThermoFisher Scientific). This mixture underwent chromatographic separation via normal-phase flash chromatography utilizing a Teledyne ISCO Combiflash Rf 200 system, monitored by both an evaporative light scattering detector (ELSD) and a photodiode array (PDA) detector. The separation process employed a gradient of hexane-CHCl3-MeOH at a flow rate of 35 mL/min, utilizing a RediSep Silica 24 g Gold column. This separation produced 7 fractions labeled 1 through 7 as in Fig. 2B. The Fraction 4 which triggered the highest GSH level was further separated in a second round of normal-phase flash chromatography utilizing a gradient of hexane-ethyl acetate-MeOH at a flow rate of 18 mL/min, utilizing a RediSep Silica 4 g Gold column. This secondary separation also produced 7 fractions, which in this case were labeled Fraction 4–1 through 4–7. Five of the fractions 4–1, 4–2, 4–4, 4–5, and 4–6 were tested in the THP-1 monocytes as in Fig. 2C.

LC-MS analysis of tef extracts and fractions

Dried extracts and flash column chromatography fractions of tef were reconstituted in MeOH prior to LC-MS analysis. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate using a Thermo Fisher Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher Scientific) coupled to a Waters Acquity UPLC system (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA). A 5 μL injection volume was loaded onto the system, operating at a constant flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. Separation was carried out on an Acquity BEH C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 mm × 50 mm) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile), applied in a gradient elution over 10 min: beginning at 15% B, linearly ramped to 100% B over 8 min, held for 1 min, and re-equilibrated to starting conditions over the remaining 1 min. Eluents were introduced to the mass spectrometer via a heated electrospray ionization (HESI) source. Data were acquired in positive ionization mode using full scan and data-dependent acquisition (DDA) settings. Full MS scans were recorded at a resolution of 35,000 with an AGC target of 1 × 106, a maximum injection time of 100 ms, and a scan range of m/z 100–1500. In DDA mode, the three most intense ions per cycle were isolated and fragmented. MS/MS spectra were acquired at 17,500 resolution with an AGC target of 1 × 105, a maximum injection time of 50 ms, and an isolation width of 1.0 m/z. Fragmentation was induced using collision energy of 35 eV. Additional DDA settings included a minimum AGC threshold of 8 × 103 for MS/MS triggering, an intensity threshold of 1.6 × 105, dynamic exclusion of 5.0 s, and exclusion of isotopes.

Metabolomics data processing and statistical analysis using MZmine

The LC-MS raw data files were converted to *.mzML format and subsequently processed in MZmine 4.4.3. A batch file configured for non-targeted analysis was loaded into the program, incorporating the following modules: mass detection, extracted ion chromatogram (XIC) building, smoothing, deconvolution (resolving), isotope filtering, alignment, gap filling, and post-processing filters such as duplicate and MS/MS filtering. The Blank Subtraction module was applied to remove features present in blank samples or arising from background signals. The final aligned and filtered feature list was exported as a *.csv file containing the feature ID, m/z, retention time, and peak area of each feature (as rows) across all samples (as columns). An *.mgf file was also generated for feature annotation using SIRIUS 5.8.5. Additionally, the Statistics Dashboard module in MZmine was used to perform statistical analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA) and volcano plot analysis. Differential features were identified using unpaired statistical testing with a p-value threshold of 0.05 and a log2 fold change cutoff of ±1 (corresponding to a fold change of ≥2 or ≤0.5).

Cell culture and viability assay

THP-1 monocytes (a human leukemia monocytic cell line) were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) (ATCC, Manassas, Virginia). These monocytes were cultured in 25 cm2 tissue culture flasks and regularly maintained in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, within a controlled environment of 5% CO2, humidified atmosphere, and a temperature of 37 °C. Cell subculturing was performed approximately twice a week, upon reaching 70–80% confluence.

Cell viability was evaluated by using the Trypan blue dye staining assay. Equal volumes of cell suspensions and 0.4% Trypan blue dye were combined in a microcentrifuge tube and thoroughly mixed by vortexing. Following a one-minute incubation period, the suspension was transferred to a hemacytometer well. Using a stereoscope, the number of viable cells (trypan blue negative) and dead cells (trypan blue positive) were enumerated. Cell viability was calculated by dividing the count of unstained cells by the total cell count. It is noteworthy that a cell confluency of 90% or higher was maintained for all assays.

Treatment of cells with tef extracts

THP-1 monocytes were exposed to 50 μg/mL tef extracts in 10 mL of RPMI media for a duration of 24 h. To ensure data reproducibility for all assays, three biological replicates and nine technical replicates were performed. Following the treatments, cells were harvested and suspended in 1 mL of 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by sonication. The sonicated cells were then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min. The resulting supernatants were collected and maintained on ice for subsequent quantification of total protein and glutathione (GSH).

Assay of protein content

Total protein was quantified using the Coomassie protein assay kit (Bradford, 1976). The standard solution comprised 790 μL of Coomassie protein assay reagent and 10 μL of a 1.48 mg/mL BSA standard. Subsequently, 3 μL of each sample was added to a microcentrifuge tube containing 790 μL of Coomassie protein assay reagent. Upon addition of the extracted protein, the tubes were promptly vortexed and then allowed to incubate at room temperature for 10 min. Following the incubation period, 200 μL of the sample was transferred to 96-well plates, and the absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a microplate reader.

Assay of GSH content

The total cellular GSH content was determined using the fluorometric method developed by Hissin and Hilf’s (1976), employing a GSH standard curve. In this assay, extracted protein (10 μL) was incubated with 37 μL of 0.1 mol/L sodium phosphate buffer containing 5 mM EDTA, and 12.5 μL of 25% metaphosphoric acid (HPO3). Following a 10-min incubation on ice, the samples were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm at 4 °C for 5 min. The resulting supernatant (45 μL) was then incubated with 1.89 mL of 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer containing 5 mM EDTA and 100 μL of o-phthalaldehyde (OPT) solution in glass tubes. The standard sample consisted of 100 μL of 5 μg/mL GSH standard, 1.8 mL of 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer containing 5 mM EDTA, and 100 μL of OPT solution. Following a 15-min incubation in the dark at room temperature, 200 μL of the solution was transferred to 96-well plates, and the fluorescence was measured at excitation 360 nm and emission 460 nm using a microplate reader.

Luciferase assay

The Nrf2/ARE Luciferase Reporter human embryonic kidney 293 (HEK293) Stable Cell Line (P/N SL-0042) was purchased from Signosis, Inc. (Santa Clara, California). The HEK293 were cultured in a complete growth medium. To investigate the potential activation of the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway by tef extracts, Nrf2/ARE reporter cells were treated with an active fraction of tef extracts for 24 h in a 96-well plate. Following the 24-h incubation period, the media was aspirated using a multichannel pipette, and 40 μL of lysis buffer was added to each well. The plate was shaken for 15 min, and subsequently, 20 μL of the solution from each well was transferred to wells of an opaque 96-well solid bottom plate. Immediately, 100 μL of luciferase substrate was added to each well, and the plate was promptly read using a Bio-Tek Synergy™ plate reader (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA). The luminescence levels measured are indicative of the transcription factor activation level based on manufacturer’s protocol.

RNA-sequencing and data analysis

To investigate the impact of tef extract on the cellular transcriptome, RNA sequencing was conducted. THP-1 monocytes were subjected to treatment with or without active brown tef fraction 4. Following a 24-h incubation period, RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent and treated with Amplification grade DNase I (ThermoFisher) to remove any traces of genomic DNA. The quantity and purity of RNA were assessed spectrophotometrically. Subsequently, DNA library preparation was carried out according to the protocol outlined in the Illumina NEBNext® Ultra™ II RNA Library Prep Kit (E7770, E7775) (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA). Post-gel electrophoresis, the DNA bands were excised and purified, and the DNA was quantified using Qubit (ThermoFisher Scientific). The samples were then pooled and submitted for sequencing.

Data analysis was performed in consultation with the Bioinformatics Core of the NCSU Center for Human Health and Environment. An average of approximately 25.3 million raw paired end RNAseq data was generated for each replicate. The quality assessment of the sequenced data was performed using the fastqc application, following which 12 poor-quality bases were trimmed from the 5′-end. Subsequently, the remaining high-quality reads were aligned with the Human reference genome (hg39 version 100) obtained from the Ensembl database using the STAR aligner (Dobin et al., 2013). Per-gene counts of uniquely mapped reads for each replicate were computed using the htseq-count script from the HTSeq python package (Anders et al., 2025). The resulting count matrix was imported into the R statistical computing environment for further analysis. Initially, genes with no count in the majority of replicate samples were discarded. The remaining count data were normalized for sequencing depth and distortion, and dispersion was estimated using the DESeq2 Bioconductor package (Love et al., 2014) in the R statistical computing environment (R Core Team, 2022). A linear model was fitted using the treatment levels, and differentially expressed genes were identified following multiple testing corrections using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). The final significant genes were determined using a padj threshold of less than 0.05.

The Human Hallmark gene list was acquired from the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis website (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp, version 2022). The ranking process involved multiplying the log2 Fold Change obtained from the DESeq2 analysis by 1/p-value for all genes that passed the filtering criteria. Subsequently, the fgsea algorithm with 1000 permutations was used to identify significant mouse Hallmark pathways by comparing them with the ranked gene list (Korotkevich, Sukhov, & Sergushichev, 2019). After sorting the output according to the normalized enrichment score, the top 20 pathways were visualized through a bar graph.

qPCR analysis

To investigate the effect of tef extracts on the expression of oxidative stress pathway genes, THP-1 monocytes were exposed to 50 μg/mL of active brown tef fraction 4. Following a 24-h incubation period, total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent from ThermoFisher Sci. Subsequently, the RNA underwent DNase I treatment, and 1 μg of RNA from each sample was reverse transcribed into cDNA utilizing the high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit purchased from ThermoFisher Sci. following the manufacturer’s instructions. The sequence of forward and reverse primers employed in quantitative reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) for genes associated with Nrf2, NF-κB, IKKβ, NQO-1, HO-1, glutathione reductase (GR), glutathione synthase (GSS), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), GCLC, and GCLM were detailed in Table S1. Relative expression levels, expressed as fold changes in comparison to the vehicle control, were determined using the ΔΔCT method (Livak & Schmittgen, 2001) available in QuantStudio3 software (Applied Biosystems), utilizing GAPDH gene as an internal control.

Statistical analysis

All data underwent analysis of variance (ANOVA) utilizing the PROC GLM procedure within the SAS statistical package (Lee, 1987). Following significant F-tests, Tukey’s multiple comparisons (Keselman & Rogan, 1977) were used to distinguish between means (P < 0.05). The data were presented as mean ± SE derived from a minimum of three independent experiments except otherwise indicated. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied across all datasets.

Results

Cell viability in response to tef extracts

Immortalized THP-1 monocytes are widely utilized in immunology and toxicology research due to their consistency, resulting in minimal variability in cell phenotype. These monocytes serve as an ideal model system for investigating biological processes under controlled and reproducible conditions. Previous research has confirmed that THP-1 monocytes exhibit normal cell viability characteristics expected in standard in vitro settings. Our recent study indicated that 50 μg/mL tef extract dissolved in DMSO did not induce toxicity (Cotter et al., 2023). Consequently, DMSO was employed as the negative control in subsequent experiments. Each treatment was administered to six million cells.

The extraction and partitioning of tef grains yielded three fractions: organic-soluble, hexane-soluble, and aqueous-soluble. An emulsion fraction was exclusively present in the brown tef. The THP-1 monocytes were cultured with 50 μg/mL brown and ivory fractions for 24 h in RPMI media. Tef extracts were dissolved in DMSO at a stock concentration of 50 mg/mL. Trypan blue was utilized to assess cell viability. A small variation was observed (Fig. S1) in cell viability across the treatments: Brown (B) organic (96.5%), ivory (I) organic (96.9%), B hexane (99.0%), I hexane (97.7%), B aqueous (98.4%), I aqueous (97.3%), and B emulsion (96.5%), compared to the control (V) (95.1%). Although small, the difference between control (V) and B hexane or B aqueous was statistically significant while there was no significant difference between the control and other treatments.

Cellular GSH in response to tef fractions

Quantification of GSH level can be a useful measure of antioxidant response. GSH deficiency or low GSH/GSSG ratio is associated with several chronic illnesses. In vitro assays have shown that tef grains have antioxidant properties (Cotter et al., 2023). We determined the protein content in the THP-1 monocytes treated with tef fractions using the Bradford protein assay kit to normalize the level of GSH to the quantity of protein. Only minor fluctuations were observed in protein concentration among the different treatments that were not statistically significant (Fig. S1). The protein concentration ranged from 0.90 mg/mL for the brown aqueous fraction to 1.021 mg for the ivory aqueous fraction.

Gluthatione levels were quantified according to Hissin and Hilf (1976). After treatment with tef fractions for 24 h, a significant increase in GSH levels was observed in cells treated with the brown organic fraction as compared to the control (Vehicle) and other treatments, reaching 28.8% increase while there was no marked increase in GSH levels for the other fractions such as the ivory fractions (Fig. 2A).

To examine the positive results observed with the brown organic fraction, it was further purified via flash chromatography to generate seven fractions, brown fractions 1–7. the THP-1 monocytes were then treated with all the seven fractions. Following a 24-h treatment, cellular GSH were measured. A notable increase in GSH levels was observed (Fig. 2B) in Fractions 4 and 5. These two active fractions demonstrated an increase in GSH levels of 234.6% and 194.3%, respectively, suggesting that bioactive phytochemicals were concentrated therein. Although the crude brown organic fraction also exhibited elevated GSH levels, they were not as pronounced as those observed in the aforementioned fractions. The remaining fractions induced a smaller increase in GSH levels (Fig. 3A) that were not statistically significant.

Fraction 4 was subjected to second round of separation which were labeled Fractions 4–1 to 4–7 (Fig. 2C). This secondary separation produced 7 fractions labeled Fraction 4–1 through 4–7. Fraction 4–2 showed about 2.8-fold increase in GSH followed by Fraction 4–4, while Fraction 4–6 was not different from the vehicle control.

Cellular GSH in response to increasing dose of tef fractions

To ascertain the effect of tef phytochemicals on cellular GSH levels, we performed a dose-response study by exposing the cells to varying concentrations of the most active tef fraction (i.e., fraction 4) in concentrations of 0, 12.5, 25, 37.5, and 100 μg/mL for 24 h. A linear relationship between the relative increase in GSH to the amount of the active fraction 4 was observed (Fig. 3). Nevertheless, analysis of cell viability decreased following treatment with 100 μg/mL, indicating its cytotoxic effects with increasing concentration of the sample. This does not appear to be due to the increase in the amount of DMSO used to dissolve the extract because cell viability was not affected by DMSO even at 200 μL/mL, but likely due to the phytochemicals in the tef extracts.

Activation of Nrf2 Signaling pathways by tef grain extract

Our studies have indicated that tef grain extracts trigger an increase in cellular GSH levels within THP-1 monocytes due to the presence of phytochemicals that have antioxidant properties. However, the underlying molecular mechanism responsible for the observed elevation in glutathione (GSH) levels by the tef samples remained unclear. To delineate the specific signaling pathways involved in the increase in cellular GSH, Nrf2/ARE luciferase reporter HEK193 cell lines were exposed to active brown tef Fraction 4 at a concentration of 50 μg/mL for 24 h. This sample prompted a significant activation (19.1-fold) of the Nrf2/ARE signaling pathway (Fig. 4). The heightened activation of the Nrf2/ARE antioxidant signaling pathway induced by the phytochemicals of tef could explain the relative increase in GSH levels.

Transcriptome analysis of THP-1 monocytes treated with tef grain extract

To understand the overall change in gene expression in response to tef phytochemicals, we performed RNA-sequencing of THP-1 cells under control conditions and those treated with tef extracts. Principal component analysis was employed to assess the consistency across the samples. PCA1 accounted for approximately 94% of the variation observed between the libraries of the two treatment groups, whereas PCA2 elucidated only about 3% of the variation (Fig. S3). Notably, libraries derived from cells treated with the brown tef fraction 4 exhibited a distinct transcriptome profile compared to the control (Vehicle), with the exception of one replicate, S58_TEF (Fig. S4). This discrepancy in the S58_TEF treatment could potentially be attributed to experimental artifacts. Conversely, minimal variation was observed among libraries generated from control untreated cells.

Characteristics of transcriptome data

Complementary DNA libraries were generated from three independent biological replicates and sequenced using the paired-end sequencing using Illumina Hiseq 4000. A total of 151.8 million raw reads with an average of 21.8 million reads (control) and 28.8 million reads (tef extract treated) were obtained for each sample (Table S2). The total clean reads of the six libraries were aligned to the human genome sequence. On average 65.84% (control) and 57.86% (tef extract treats) of the reads were uniquely aligned to the reference genome.

Heatmap of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Our transcriptome analysis revealed distinct differences in transcriptome profiles between control samples and two of the tef-treated samples. However, it should be noted that the transcriptome profile of one of the treated samples, TEF-R1, exhibited similarities with the control samples, which could potentially be attributed to experimental artifacts as mentioned in Section 3.5. We identified the top twenty-five up and downregulated differentially expressed (DE) genes between the Brown Fraction 4 tef treated and the Vehicle control samples (Fig. 5A and B). Specifically, in tef-treated samples, ferritin heavy chain 1 pseudogene 10 (FTH1P10) and FTH1P7, and tumor necrotic factor (TNF), a gene regulating cellular morphology and organization of the cytoskeleton (FMNL2), and immune response and inflammation (NKG7) were the top of the list of upregulated genes. Other genes that were upregulated include FTH1P11 and FTH1P20, the solute carrier genes such as SLC11A1, SLC45A3, SLC12A7, SLC7A7, and SLC43A2, among others. Another prominently expressed gene is heme oxygenase 1 (HMOX1), encoding the HO-1 enzyme, and glutathione-disulfide reductase (GSR), and glutathione reductase (GR) genes, could have also contributed to the observed increase in GSH levels observed in the GSH level. In contrast, the top five downregulated genes include SIM bHLH transcription factor 1 (SIM1), leukocyte receptor tyrosine kinase (LTK), calcium voltage-gated channel auxiliary subunit beta 1 (CACNB1), mitochondrially encoded ATP synthase membrane subunit 8 (MT-ATP8), and transcription factor 23 (TCF23). The majority of these genes are known to be involved in cellular homeostasis, development, and physiological processes.

Gene set enrichment analysis

To assess the enrichment of various signaling pathways by the tef extracts, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the Hallmark database. This analysis compared the normalized counts of all genes expressed in THP-1 monocytes treated with 50 μg/mL of Brown Fraction 4 tef extract to those expressed in the control samples. Our findings revealed 24 gene sets that were significantly enriched in the THP-1 monocytes treated with Brown Fraction 4, while 6 gene sets were significantly enriched in the control samples. Notably, the top three enriched pathways in the treated THP-1 monocytes, according to the GSEA analysis, were the TNF-α signaling via NF-κB, the inflammatory response, and the reactive oxygen species (ROS) pathways. Other enriched pathways included the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), TGFβ, angiogenesis, p53 signaling pathways, etc. Conversely, certain pathways such as E2F targets, G2M Checkpoint, and spermatogenesis exhibited greater enrichment in the control samples compared to the tef-treated ones (Fig. 6).

Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs

The DEGs that were significantly upregulated (p < 0.05) were subjected to PANTHER classification for functional enrichment analysis. This analysis categorized the genes into two main categories: molecular function and biological process. Unclassified genes were filtered out of the analysis. The analysis revealed 13 molecular functions that were positively enriched in THP-1 monocytes treated with the active brown tef fraction 4. Predominantly, these functions were associated with binding (32.0%), catalytic activity (24.3%), and transcription regulator activity (8.4%) (Fig. S4A). As depicted in Fig. S4B, the top three components of biological processes were the cellular process (45.2%), biological regulation (34.9%), and metabolic process (23.2%), with detoxification accounting for 0.10%. These findings suggest that tef extracts may modulate various regulatory processes post-transcription, thereby influencing other biological functions.

Validation of Nrf2 regulated gene expression

As previously outlined, GSH accumulates within cells firstly by enzymatic biosynthesis involving GCLC, GCLM, and GSS glutathione synthase. Secondly, GSH accumulation relies on GR and the cofactor NADPH. Lastly, cysteine recycling occurs via gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGTP). The transcription factors Nrf2, acting via the antioxidant response element (ARE), and NF-κB play pivotal roles in regulating the expression of these genes.

Our findings indicate that tef grain extracts augment the activation of the Nrf2 signaling pathway, as evidenced by luciferase activity in the Nrf2/ARE luciferase reporter cell line. To further elucidate the effects of the extract on the expression of Nrf2 signaling pathway genes, THP-1 monocytes were treated with brown fraction 4 for 24 h. Subsequently, total RNA was extracted from the monocytes, and gene expression was assessed via qPCR. In our investigation, we examined the expression of selected genes regulated by Nrf2 (Nrf2, NQO-1, HO-1, GR, GSS, GPx, GCLC, and GCLM) and NF-κB (Keap-1, IKKβ).

As depicted in Fig. 7, treatment of the THP-1 cells with the active tef extract Brown Fraction 4 led to a significant elevation in the levels of Keap-1, IKKβ, NQO-1, HO-1, GR, GSS, GCLC, and GCLM transcripts compared to the control V (Fig. 7B-F, H, I and J). Notably, the NQO-1 transcript level increased by over 5-fold, while the HO-1 transcript level was upregulated by over 28-fold (Fig. 7D & E). The transcript level of the Nrf2, a key player in the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway showed a slight decrease with the Brown Fraction 4 treatment, which was not statistically significant (Fig. 7A). Conversely, the transcript of its inhibitor, Keap-1, exhibited a marginal increase of 1.2-fold (Fig. 7B). Similarly, the transcript of IKKβ, a gene involved in the NF-κB proinflammatory pathway and an inhibitor of NF-κB, displayed a slight increase of 1.2-fold (Fig. 7C). Moreover, the transcript of GR increased by 1.9-fold (Fig. 7F), while the transcripts of GSH peroxidase (GPx) were not affected (Fig. 7G). Similarly, transcripts of key genes involved in GSH biosynthesis, including GCLC and GCLM, demonstrated an increase of 1.7- and 2.4-fold, respectively (Fig. 7I & J). However, the transcript levels of GSH synthetase (GSS), another gene implicated in GSH biosynthesis, exhibited a slight decrease by 0.8-fold which was statistically significant (Fig. 7H).

Identification of compounds with antioxidant activity

Our findings reveal that fractions of tef extracts vary in their antioxidant activity. To identify compounds that were likely responsible for the observed activity, the active and inactive fractions were subjected to LC-MS analysis. Fig. 8A shows that PCA1 explains 89% of the variation between the two fractions, and samp8 shows differentially enriched compounds in the active and inactive fractions. Interestingly, we found several compounds that were enriched by more than two-fold in the active Fraction 4–2 as compared to inactive Fraction 4–6, while in Fraction 4–6, we have not detected any compound that was enhanced by two-fold as compared to Fraction 4–2. Annotation of the compounds that were more abundantly enriched in the active fraction identified fatty acids, alkaloids, polyketides and terpenoids (Table S3). The most abundant compounds in the active fraction relative to the inactive fraction were fatty acid such as vernolic acid (C18H32O3), α-linolenic acid (C18H30O2) and 12-oxo-phytodienoic acid (C18H28O3).

Discussion

Previous research on tef seed extracts has highlighted their antioxidant properties, especially for varieties with brown-seed coat than ivory/white ones (Cotter et al., 2023), yet the precise mechanism remains to be well understood. This investigation explores the molecular mechanisms of tef extract’s antioxidant properties using THP-1 monocytes. The THP-1 cell line serves as a widely acknowledged model for studying macrophage-associated physiological phenomena. This study provides various lines of evidence regarding the antioxidant properties of the tef phytochemicals. Maintaining a balance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and antioxidant defenses is crucial for proper physiological functions. Excessive ROS can lead to oxidative stress, implicated in various pathologies and the onset of degenerative diseases (Poljsak, Šuput, & Milisav, 2013). Glutathione (GSH), often termed the master antioxidant, stands as a potent defender against oxidative stress. GSH is the most prevalent thiol which plays a pivotal role in safeguarding cells against oxidative damage, complementing their array of antioxidants and anti-inflammatory defense mechanisms.

Our findings consistently indicate that tef extracts, particularly the brown organic fraction, contain phytochemicals endowed with antioxidative capabilities. Phytochemicals from the most active brown tef fraction increase cellular GSH in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3). We investigated the cellular mechanisms governing the observed antioxidant properties of tef phytochemicals, particularly their influence on the activation of the antioxidant Nrf2/ARE pathway. By employing luciferase assays (Lai, Jiang, & Li, 2006) with stably transfected cell lines for Nrf2/ARE activation, we ensured a high degree of specificity in our assessments. Our findings revealed an over 19-fold increase in the activity of LUC enzyme (Fig. 4), which is a marker for enhanced Nrf2 nuclear translocation. This means that increased levels of antioxidants are intricately modulated by the Nrf2 antioxidant signaling pathways. This research offers pioneering insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the enhanced antioxidant activity facilitated by tef phytochemicals, thereby revealing their potential therapeutic efficacy in disease treatment and prevention.

To comprehensively investigate the gene expression patterns within THP-1 monocytes treated with the active brown tef fraction (Brown Fraction 4), we conducted both RNA-sequencing analysis and quantitative PCR (qPCR) (Fig. 7). The findings revealed that the expression of Nrf2 and Keap-1 genes were mildly affected by treatment with tef extracts. This suggests that the activation of these genes may not be contingent upon transcript abundance but rather on posttranscriptional protein modifications, such as protein phosphorylation, which is known to activate the dissociation of Nrf2 from its inhibitor Keap-1 (Liu, Lv, Zhao, & Jiang, 2021). Similarly, the expression of the IKKβ gene, an inhibitor of NF-κB, did not increase following treatment with the active Brown Fraction 4. Prior research has demonstrated that Nrf2 activation triggers the expression of genes encoding glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic subunit (GCLC) and modifier subunit (GCLM) (Zhang, Liu, Zhou, Yuen, & Forman, 2017). Our findings corroborate with that of Zhang et al. (2017) as a notable upregulation in the expression of GCLC and GCLM genes was observed. This increase in gene expression levels of GCLC and GCLM aligns well with the elevated cellular GSH in the THP-1 cells. It is conceivable that other genes involved in GSH biosynthesis, such as those encoding cysteine and sulfur amino acid precursors, may also be implicated. Moreover, the expression levels of cryoprotective genes, heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) and NAD(P)H quinone dehydrogenase 1 (NQO-1), exhibited a significant increase. Nrf2 activation has been known to enhance the expression of HO-1 and NQO-1 genes (Zou et al., 2012). Heme oxygenase 1 (HO-1) plays a pivotal role in heme catabolism and is instrumental in immune regulation, as well as safeguarding cells and tissues against diverse stressors. The notable increase in HO-1 transcript levels, along with a slight elevation in GR expression observed in qPCR data, may be attributed to the increased expression of heme oxigenase 1 (HMOX1) gene. Additionally, there was a slight augmentation in the expression of GR, suggesting a potential mechanism for the reduction of GSSG to GSH. Our data provide compelling evidence supporting the induction of Nrf2 target genes (GCLC, GCLM, HO-1, NQO-1) and reduction of GSH from GSSG via GR. These findings collectively suggest that tef phytochemicals enhance GSH levels via the activation of the Nrf2-pathways.

We previously reported that in vitro digested tef grains increase cellular ferritin which is an established marker for iron bioavailability in human CaCo2 (Caucasian colon adenocarcinoma) cells, surpassing the levels observed for reference cereals wheat and rice (Ligaba-Osena et al., 2021). Consequently, tef holds promise for addressing iron-deficient conditions, as evidenced by its potential therapeutic efficacy in mitigating iron deficiency-related anemia (Habte et al., 2022). Our transcriptome analysis revealed that tef extracts induce the transcripts of the ferritin heavy chain pseudogenes FTH1P7, FTH1P10, FTH1P11, and FTH1P20. Although pseudogenes are typically non-coding, they may exert regulatory influence on ferritin biosynthesis and potentially contribute to iron delivery to cells. It has also been reported that ferritin heavy chain (FTH) demonstrates notable anti-growth effects within breast cancer cells through the inhibition of c-MYC expression (Ali et al., 2021). Our transcriptome analysis also revealed upregulation of the solute carrier genes, including SLC11A1, SLC45A3, SLC12A7, SLC7A7, and SLC43A2. The solute-carrier gene (SLC) superfamily encodes membrane-bound transporters, which are implicated in the transport of a range of organic and inorganic molecules (He et al., 2020). Overexpression of SLC22A10 and SLC22A15 has been associated with increased GSH transport (Nayak et al., 2022). The SLC genes may also play a role in the transport of phytochemicals in tef extracts into the cells.

The GSEA in this study revealed a significant enrichment of E2F targets, G2M checkpoint, and spermatogenesis signaling pathways in the control group (Fig. 6) while for cells treated with tef extracts, enrichment in several pathways was observed, including TNF-α signaling via NF-κB, inflammatory response pathways, and pathways related to reactive oxygen species (ROS). How tef phytochemicals are involved in the inflammatory pathway is the focus of an ongoing investigation. Furthermore, PANTHER classification (Fig. S4) suggests that tef extracts may modulate various regulatory processes at the post-transcriptional level, thereby impacting other biological functions. Nevertheless, further investigations are warranted to unravel the intricate interplay between the antioxidant properties of tef extract and the regulation of antioxidant and proinflammatory pathways. These results collectively shed light on the impact of tef extracts on various signaling pathways in THP-1 monocytes, providing valuable insights into their potential mechanisms of action. This study potentially unveils the initial molecular mechanism underlying the augmented antioxidant activity elicited by tef phytochemicals, highlighting their therapeutic and preventive potential against specific diseases. Moreover, subsequent separation and purification of the active tef extract revealed diminished antioxidants potentially due to either the absence of dominant pure compounds or the partitioning of active components into distinct fractions (data not shown). Enhanced GSH levels may stem from the synergistic action of multiple compounds.

To identify compounds that increased GSH levels and modulate gene expression, including oxidative stress responsive genes in the THP1 monocytes, we performed LS-MS analysis of the active and inactive tef fractions. We identified several compounds which were highly enriched in the active fraction as compared to the inactive fractions, including long-chain fatty acids vernolic acid, α-linolenic acid and 12-oxo-phytodienoic acid (Fig. 8B, Table 1). Vernolic acid is a long-chain and mono-unsaturated fatty which is stored in large quantities in seeds of Asteraceae and Euphorbiaceae species (Cahoon, Ripp, Hall, & McGonigle, 2002). However, vernolic acid is not generally detected in cereals, including tef. Interestingly in this study, over 60-fold higher vernolic acid level was detected in active Fraction 4–2 that possessed higher antioxidant activity in THP1 monocytes as compared to the inactive Fraction 4–6, suggesting that the observed antioxidant activity in Fraction 4–2 could be attributed to higher vernolic acid content. Vernolic acid has been reported to have antioxidant and cholesterol lowering activities as observed in in vitro studies using tombili (Caesalpinia bonducella) seed extracts (Musa et al., 2023). Vernolic acid possesses distinct epoxide functionalities which allow diverse chemical modifications, enhancing its utility in various applications. Similarily, over 60-fold higher α-linolenic acid was detected in the active Fraction 4–2 as compared to the inactive Fraction 4–6. Alpha-linolenic acid is one of the dominant fatty acids detected in tef grains (Yisak, Yaya, Chandravanshi, & Redi-Abshiro, 2022; Reta, Atlabachew, Mehari, Hilawea, & Asmellash, 2024). It has been reported to exhibit antioxidant effects in mice by inducing the expression of Nrf2 (Alam et al., 2021) and activating antioxidant enzymes (İstifli, Demir, Kaplan, Ateş, & Doran, 2019). In this study, although, Nrf2 gene expression is not induced by the active tef extract (Fig. 6A), the Nrf2/ARE signaling pathway is induced by 19-fold as compared to the negative control via the activation of Nrf2 translocation from the cytosol to the nucleus in Nrf2/ARE luciferase reporter HEK193 cell lines (Fig. 4). Translocation of the Nrf2/ARE to the nucleus activates the antioxidant signaling pathway, including the activation of GSH biosynthetic genes (Fig. 7F, I, J). Therefore, α-linolenic acid might have also contributed to the increased antioxidant activity by the active tef fraction in the THP1 monocytes. As shown in the volcano plot (Fig. 8B), the level of 12-oxo-phytodienoic acid was also elevated by about 30-fold in the active tef faction compared to the inactive fraction. This compound is a primary precursor of jasmonic acid (JA) that regulates plant defense responses and growth processes (Liu & Park, 2021). The biological activity of tef 12-oxo-phytodienoic acid has not been studied, however, in human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cells, it has been shown to suppress H2O2-induced cytotoxicity, inhibit the H2O2-induced ROS increase, induce the nuclear translocation of Nrf2 and increase intracellular GSH level and the expression of the Nrf2-regulated phase II antioxidant enzymes hemeoxygenase-1, NADPH quinoneoxidoreductase1, and glutathionereductase (Taki-Nakano, Ohzeki, Kotera, & Ohta, 2014). Taken together, variation in antioxidant activity between the tef fractions is likely due to the elevation of biologically active phytochemiclas such as vernolic acid, α-linolenic acid and 12-oxo-phytodienoic acid, however, an in-depth investigation is imperative to identify all the tef biologically active phytochemicals and elucidate their mode of action. Further investigation is warranted to comprehensively elucidate the correlation between the antioxidant properties of tef extract and the expression of proinflammatory genes.

Conclusion

In our investigation, we discovered that tef extracts do not exert cytotoxic effects. Particularly, extracts derived from brown tef varieties notably elevate cellular glutathione (GSH) levels in THP-1 monocytes. Leveraging cell lines with stable expression of a luciferase gene, our findings suggest that tef extracts primarily activate the Nrf2 signaling pathway. Furthermore, transcriptome analysis using RNA-seq and qPCR unveiled the upregulation of various gene families by tef extracts, including TNF, GSR, ferritin heavy chain pseudogenes (FTH1P), solute carrier (SLC) gene family, zinc finger proteins (ZNF), and heat shock proteins (HSP). Conversely, genes such as MT-ATP8, NAPEPLD, AKAP91P5, EEF1A, UBTD2, and ST3GAL3 were downregulated by the tef extracts. A deeper understanding of these genes may reveal targets of tef phytochemicals for preventing oxidative stress related diseases such as diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and cancer. We found that extracts from brown tef have higher antioxidant properties than the ivory/white varieties. Brown tef has higher nutritional value and stronger flavor than ivory tef, yet ivory varieties have generally higher consumer preference because of their visual appearance. Our study also showed that brown tef extracts comprise a plethora of compounds, including some that are differentially enriched in the active fraction as compared to the inactive faction, such as vernolic acid, α-linolenic acid and 12-oxo-phytodienoic acid. Further research is needed to introduce the biosynthetic pathways of phytochemicals identified in brown varieties into ivory varieties to improve their health benefits. Taken together, brown tef varieties with their bioactive phytochemicals and nutritional quality hold promise for potential therapeutic efficacy in disease treatment and prevention.
