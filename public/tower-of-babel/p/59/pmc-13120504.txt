A Robust Artificial Intelligence Method for Detecting Near–Non-Reactive Non-Stress Test Patterns: What Should We Expect?
Authors: Haitian Chen, Caixia Zhu, Zhuyu Li, Xietong Wang, Bin Xu, Xiaohui Guo, Jingwan Huang, Bin Liu, Hongyan Li, Yan Kong, Xiaobo Yang, Jingyu Du, Zilian Wang
Published: 2026

To editor,

Electronic fetal monitoring (EFM) plays an essential role in ensuring maternal and fetal safety. The prenatal non-stress test (NST) is a commonly used assessment tool for evaluating fetal well-being. In 1997, the National Institute of Child Health and Human Development introduced standardized guidelines for interpreting fetal heart rate (FHR) patterns,1 which were later incorporated into a three-tier classification system of reactive, atypical, and non-reactive NST patterns.2–4 Conventionally, NST tracings are printed on paper and visually interpreted by obstetricians to detect abnormalities. Accurate interpretation of NST patterns has been associated with improved neonatal outcomes and reduced unnecessary cesarean deliveries.

To improve clinical decision-making, several adjunctive tools have been explored, including fetal electrocardiography5 and artificial intelligence (AI). Recently, AI-assisted interpretation of NST tracings has emerged as a promising approach. However, previous studies suggest that AI systems perform best when identifying typical FHR patterns and may be less reliable when tracings are ambiguous or atypical.6

With the growing use of remote fetal monitoring systems, large volumes of NST tracing are generated in routine clinical practice. While these systems provide advantages in convenience, real-time monitoring, and accessibility, they also increase the workload for healthcare professionals who must review and interpret the recordings. The rapid development of AI-based healthcare technologies offers potential solutions for automated interpretation of EFM data, which may help reduce delays in physician review caused by staffing shortages.

Given that the application of AI in prenatal monitoring remains relatively new, further validation studies are warranted. The present study evaluated agreement between an AI system and experienced obstetricians in interpreting prenatal NST tracings.

Materials and methods

We analyzed 1000 NST records collected between June 1, 2020, and December 31, 2021, from the remote FHR monitoring system of four tertiary hospitals: The First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, Zhongshan Boai Hospital, Shandong Provincial Hospital, and Shandong Women and Children Hospital (250 records each).

Records from pregnancies < 32 weeks of gestation or with a monitoring duration of < 20 min were excluded. All included cases were randomly selected. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen University (Approval No. 2024-858).

All 1000 cases were independently interpreted by five obstetricians from different hospitals. In parallel, each tracing was analyzed by an AI system. All interpretations were classified into one of three categories: reactive NST, atypical NST, or non-reactive NST. If disagreements occurred among the five obstetricians, the case was reviewed by an expert panel of three professors, whose judgment served as the final reference standard for evaluating AI performance. All obstetricians and reviewers were blinded to each other’s interpretations, the AI results, and pregnancy outcomes.

Cases were stratified into two groups: Group A comprised cases with unanimous agreement on the NST category, while Group B included cases with any discrepancies. Random sampling was employed in Group A for expert review to verify the consistency of interpretation.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.2. Agreement between observers was evaluated using the proportion of agreement (Pa), with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s kappa statistic, which measures agreement beyond chance. The following thresholds were used to interpret kappa values: 0–0.20, poor agreement; 0.21–0.40, fair agreement; 0.41–0.60, moderate agreement; 0.61–0.80, substantial agreement; and 0.81–1.00, excellent agreement.

AI performance was further evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV) by Confusion Matrix Method, assuming the obstetricians’ interpretations are correct.

Results

Consistency in tracing interpretation

Of the initial 1000 NST records, 29 were excluded due to gestational age < 32 weeks, and seven because the monitoring duration was < 20 min. The final analysis included 964 NST tracings.

Among these, 485 cases (50.3%) showed complete agreement among all five obstetricians (Group A), while the remaining 479 cases (49.7%) exhibited discrepancies in interpretation (Group B).

Agreement between AI and obstetricians

In group A, the AI system demonstrated strong agreement with obstetrician interpretations. The proportion of agreement was high (94.02%), with a kappa value of 0.75, indicating substantial agreement. In contrast, AI performance was lower in Group B, where obstetricians themselves disagreed in their interpretations. In this group, the AI accuracy was 43.42%, and the kappa value was 0.15, indicating only slight agreement. In all cases, the overall agreement between AI and obstetricians was 68.89%, with a kappa value of 0.42, indicating moderate agreement (Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/MFM/A126).

Diagnostic performance of the AI system

The AI system identified 575 cases as reactive NST, 188 as non-reactive NST, and 201 as atypical NST (Supplementary Table 2, http://links.lww.com/MFM/A126).

For reactive NST patterns, the AI system achieved an accuracy of 83.0%, with a sensitivity of 82.8% and a specificity of 83.4%. The PPV for reactive NST patterns was 90.3% and NPV was 72.2%.

For non-reactive NST patterns, AI performance was higher, with an accuracy of 83.6% with a sensitivity of 91.7% and a specificity of 83.3%. Furthermore, the PPV was 17.6% and the NPV was 99.6%. However, AI performance for atypical NST patterns was lower, with an accuracy of 71.2%, and a sensitivity of 37.2% and a specificity of 87.6% (Supplementary Table 3, http://links.lww.com/MFM/A126).

Discussion

The NST serves as a critical tool for fetal surveillance during the antepartum period. This represents the first study to systematically evaluate the agreement between an AI-based system and experienced obstetricians in interpreting prenatal NST tracings. The results demonstrate that AI achieved excellent agreement with obstetricians in identifying non-reactive NST patterns.

In the present study, we identified moderate agreement between the AI system and human observers in NST pattern interpretation, with a kappa of 0.42. Devena et al.7 reported moderate agreement among obstetricians for identifying accelerations and decelerations, while Caning et al.8 demonstrated similar variability in cardiotocography interpretation. Furthermore, a meta-analysis by Balayla et al.9 reported moderate agreement between clinicians and AI systems in intrapartum FHR interpretation.

Our results are consistent with these findings and suggest that AI systems may provide useful support for NST interpretation, particularly in cases with clear, typical patterns. When a non-reactive NST pattern was detected by AI, the pregnant woman was asked to repeat NST or visit the clinic.

Owing to the delayed interpretation of NST patterns and discrepancies among obstetricians, analysis of prenatal NST patterns using an AI system is important.10 AI-assisted interpretation could help obstetricians rapidly assess fetal well-being during the antepartum period, potentially reducing workload and improving efficiency in prenatal care.

In the atypical NST category, sensitivity was low, potentially reflecting instability in the clinical definition. In addition, the low PPV and high NPV observed for non-reactive NST may be influenced by the low prevalence of this category, limiting their clinical significance.

The primary strength of our study lies in its utilization of a large-scale, real-world dataset to evaluate the performance of an AI system in interpreting prenatal NST patterns. However, several limitations should be noted. First, the AI system demonstrated a lower accuracy for atypical NST patterns, potentially leading to unnecessary clinical interventions or repeat testing. Second, validation of the system’s accuracy and agreement was confined to the antepartum setting; although results are promising, larger prospective studies are required to replicate and confirm these findings. Third, the study population was limited to pregnancies from 32 weeks of gestation to term; future investigations should include women from 28 weeks onward to assess the system’s applicability across a broader prenatal timeline. Furthermore, the grouping design may constitute a post hoc stratification. Group A likely represents inherently easier-to-interpret cases, while Group B represents ambiguous or borderline cases. Therefore, differences in AI performance between these groups are expected and do not necessarily reflect true differences in clinical reliability.

Conclusion

AI shows potential as an adjunct tool for the interpretation of prenatal NST patterns, particularly for identifying non-reactive patterns when clinician agreement is high. However, AI should not be regarded as a standalone diagnostic system. Its application is most reliable in settings where tracings are unambiguous, and clinician agreement is high. Instead, it may serve as a supportive technology to assist clinicians in fetal monitoring and improve workflow efficiency.

Further research is needed to improve the robustness of AI algorithms and to validate their utility in prospective, outcome-based studies.
