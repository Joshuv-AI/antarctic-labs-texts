Organic fertilizers increase microbial community diversity and stability slowing down the transformation process of nutrient cycling
Authors: Xueli Chen, Xingzhu Ma, Zhuxiu Liu, Haidong Gu, Hairui Fang, Zongzhuan Shen, Huibo Zhang, Shuming Wan, Weiqun Li, Xiaoyu Hao, Nicholas John Clarke, Junjie Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSoil microbes play a central role in nutrient recycling in soils: however, the genetic mechanisms governing their responses to long-term fertilization remain poorly understood. While the agronomic benefits of long-term fertilization are well-documented, the genetic mechanisms and ecological processes underlying microbial community responses to different fertilization regimes remain poorly understood, particularly in unique soil systems such as black soils (Mollisols), which are critical for global food security. A deeper insight into how organic and inorganic fertilizers influence microbial assembly, functional potential, and community stability is essential for developing sustainable soil management practices.ResultsThis study deciphers microbial assembly mechanisms, functional gene dynamics, and community restructuring in black soils subjected to 44 years of chemical fertilizer (CF), manure amendment (M), and integrated chemical fertilizer with manure (CFM) treatments. Results revealed that CF significantly enhances functional gene abundance related to carbon (C) degradation (e.g., starch, cellulose, chitin and lignin) and nitrification, accelerating the conversion of recalcitrant C to labile C pools and ammonium to nitrate. Conversely, M and CFM treatments promote microbial diversity and stability while decelerating nutrient transformation processes. In addition, microbial assembly mechanisms shift from stochastic to deterministic processes with long-term fertilizer application in CF. The structural equation modeling (SEM) indicated that soil chemical properties shape both the diversity and composition of taxonomic and functional gene communities which subsequently regulate microbial -mediated nutrient cycling processes and crop yield.ConclusionsOur findings highlight the trade-offs between microbial functional potential and community stability under contrasting fertilization strategies, emphasizing the need to integrate microbial metrics into sustainable land management frameworks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40793-025-00791-6.Keywords: Black soil, Microbial community, Functional genes, Fertilization, GeoChip, High-throughput sequencing

Introduction

Fertilization is a cornerstone of modern agriculture, underpinning global food security by enhancing crop productivity through improved plant biomass accumulation and optimized soil nutrient cycling [1–3]. While chemical fertilizers (CF) have played a pivotal role in achieving high-yield targets, their long-term application often disrupts soil ecosystem stability and health [4, 5]. In contrast, organic fertilizers (OF) are widely recognized for their capacity to mitigate soil degradation, enhance microbial diversity, and promote sustainable agroecosystems [6, 7]. The fertile black soils of northeastern China have been increasingly over-fertilised, and it is expected that green production and achieving a balance between high yield and protection of soil resources will continue to be a challenge [4, 5].

Recent decades have seen extensive research on the interplay between fertilization practices and soil microbial communities across diverse agroecosystems and climatic zones [8–11]. A substantial body of research has been conducted on the diversity and composition of soil microbes in relation to different fertilisation practices across a range of agricultural production systems, encompassing diverse soil types climate zones, and global scales [12]. For instance, long-term CF applications have been shown to reduce microbial diversity while enriching copiotrophic taxa such as Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria [13, 14]. Paradoxically, CF also increases the prevalence of pathogenic fungi, exacerbating soil-borne diseases [15]. On the other hand, CFM regimes have demonstrated synergistic benefits, stabilizing microbial communities and improving nutrient use efficiency [16, 17]. However, soil microbial communities are disproportionately influenced by a subset of “keystone taxa” (e.g., Cyanobacteria and Glomeromycota) rather than dominant high-abundance groups (e.g., Proteobacteria, Ascomycota) [18]. These keystone species play disproportionate roles in regulating ecosystem functions such as nutrient cycling, organic matter decomposition, and pathogen suppression [19]. While previous studies have primarily focused on the effects of fertilization on microbial biomass and activity [16, 20, 21], emerging evidence highlights the need to integrate functional gene expression and assembly processes into agronomic assessments [22, 23]. Specifically, elucidating how fertilization regimes shape taxonomic and functional diversity and their feedback on soil carbon and nitrogen dynamics remains critical for optimizing sustainable land management practices [24].

In the fertile black soils of northeastern China, a global hotspot for intensive cereal production, decades of excessive fertilization have led to critical challenges, including soil acidification, organic carbon depletion, and structural degradation [8, 11]. Black soils in this region typically exhibit carbon and phosphorus limitation under natural conditions [25]. CF exacerbates these limitations by promoting rapid microbial turnover and carbon mineralization [26, 27]. In contrast, OF inputs enhance soil carbon sequestration through physical stabilization and biochemical recalcitrance to microbial decomposition [28]. Strategic substitution of organic nitrogen for chemical nitrogen has been proposed as a means to alleviate microbial carbon limitation while reducing phosphorus constraints [25]. Notably, bacterial communities exhibit heightened responsiveness to OF amendments, manifesting as increased diversity and compositional stability [17]. However, long-term CF selectively enriches acidophilic bacteria and fungi, driving shifts in community structure that may compromise ecosystem resilience [15, 29].

The composition of soil microbial communities in black soil arable lands exhibits significant variation in response to fertilization; beyond cataloging taxonomic shifts, a critical yet less understood aspect is how these fertilization regimes alter the ecological assembly processes that govern microbial community composition. Community assembly is theorized to be governed by a balance of deterministic (niche-based) processes, where environmental filtering (e.g., pH, nutrient availability) and biotic interactions select for specific taxa, and stochastic (neutral) processes, including probabilistic dispersal, ecological drift, and random birth–death events [30–32]. Understanding this balance is pivotal to mechanistically predict the trajectory and stability of microbial communities under agricultural intensification. It remains unclear whether long-term fertilization imposes strong deterministic selection, overriding stochasticity, or alternatively, if organic inputs foster a more stochastic assembly by increasing habitat heterogeneity and reducing competitive exclusion. Specifically, how different fertilization regimes influence microbial community assembly processes rather than merely identifying taxonomic changes has not been systematically investigated. Furthermore, existing studies often focus on taxonomic diversity while neglecting functional traits, particularly functional pathways associated with soil carbon (C) and nitrogen (N) cycling. Integrating both perspectives is critical for advancing our understanding of fertilization's impact on soil ecosystem functioning in agricultural contexts. Thus our central hypotheses were that: (1) fertilization-induced alterations in soil physicochemical properties would differentially modulate microbial community composition and metabolic potentials, thereby influencing crop productivity and agroecosystem sustainability, and (2) long-term chemical fertilization (CF) would enhance deterministic processes, driven by strong environmental filtering (e.g., soil acidification and nutrient imbalance), while combined chemical and organic (manure) fertilization (CFM) would promote stochastic assembly processes by increasing resource heterogeneity and buffering against harsh selective pressures.

Methods

Location, plant materials and treatments

Soil samples were collected from a long-term fertilization experiment established in 1980 at the Scientific Observing and Experiment Station of Arable Land Conservation and Agriculture Environment (Heilongjiang), Ministry of Agriculture (N45°20′-46°20′, E126°15′-127°30′), located in Harbin city of Heilongjiang province, China. The soil type is black soil (Mollisols, USDA classification). The region has a temperate continental monsoon climate with an annual average rainfall of 550 mm. A wheat–maize-soybeans rotation system has been conducted every three years since 1980. The experiment is arranged in a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications. Four fertilization treatments were selected for this study: no fertilizer (without any fertilization, NoF), manure amendment (horse manure application, M), chemical fertilizer (nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium(K) fertilization, CF), integrated chemical fertilizer with manure (a combination of the CF and M treatments, CFM). Each treatment had three replicated plots, 4 m × 9 m (36 m2) for each plot. A schematic diagram of the experimental design is provided in Supplementary Figure S1. The fertilization regime has been consistently applied since 1980. For the M and CFM treatments, horse manure was applied at the rate of 75 kg N ha−1 (equivalent to 18,600 kg manure ha−1) once per full rotation cycle (every three years after the harvest season of wheat) in the autumn and immediately incorporated into the soil. Chemical fertilizers were applied annually to the respective crops within the rotation. For wheat and maize, 150 kg N ha−1, 75 kg P2O5 ha−1, and 75 kg K2O ha−1. For soybean, 75 kg N ha−1, 150 kg P2O5 ha−1, and 75 kg K2O ha−1. Nitrogen was applied as urea, phosphorus as calcium superphosphate and potassium as potassium sulfate.

Bulk soil samples in this study were collected in September 2023, after the harvest of the soybean crop. Within each plot, five random subsamples were collected from the 0–15 cm depth using a stainless-steel soil auger. The five subsamples from the same plot were thoroughly homogenized to form one composite sample. This process was repeated for all three replicates of each treatment, resulting in a total of 12 composite soil samples (4 treatments × 3 replicates). Samples were immediately placed on ice, transported to the laboratory, sieved through a 2 mm mesh, and subdivided into aliquots for molecular biological analysis (stored at -80 °C) and physicochemical analysis (air-dried).

Soil properties and crop yield measurements

Soil pH was measured after shaking the soil: water (1:2.5 w/v) suspension for 30 min. Soil microbial biomass carbon (MBC) and nitrogen (MBN) were measured using the chloroform-fumigation-extraction method [33]. Correction factors for C and N (kEC = 0.38 and kEN = 0.45) were taken from [34]. The soil total nitrogen (TN) and total carbon (TC) contents were determined using an elemental analyzer (VarioEL ILL, Elementar Analysensysteme, Hanau, Germany). Soil total phosphorus (TP), and contents were measured using a flow injection autoanalyzer (SKALAR, San + + , Netherlands). Soil total inorganic phosphorus (TIP) and total organic phosphorus (TOP) were analyzed by the cauterization method, and contents of available potassium (AK) and total organic carbon (TOC) were determined using methods described by Lu [35].

DNA extraction and quantification

Soil DNA was extracted from 0.5 g of frozen soil with a Fast DNA® SPIN Kit for Soil (Qbiogene Inc., Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted DNA was diluted in TE (10 mM TriseHCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0) buffer. The quality and concentration of soil DNA were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). All samples exhibited optimal absorbance ratios, with 260/280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0 (indicating no protein contamination) and 260/230 ratios greater than 1.0 (indicating minimal contamination from humic acids, salts, or carbohydrates). This consistent high-quality profile across all samples gave us confidence that the extraction process was not significantly compromised by co-extracted contaminants [36].

Real-time quantitative PCR

The copy numbers of bacterial 16S rRNA gene were quantified by real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) on a LightCycler 480 II instrument (Roche). The V3-V4 hypervariable region was amplified using primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) [37]. The 20 μL reaction mixture consisted of 10 μL of 2X ChamQ SYBR Color qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), 0.8 μL of each forward and reverse primer (5 μM), 2 μL of template soil DNA, 0.4 μL of 50 X ROX Reference Dye 1 and 6.0 μL of sterilized MilliQ water. The thermal cycling protocol was as follows: initial procedure with 95 °C for 3 min, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 58 °C for 30 s, a final extension at 72 °C for 1 min.

For absolute quantification, the standard was prepared from a recombinant pMD18-T plasmid containing a cloned 16S rRNA gene insert from Escherichia coli strain DH5α, which was amplified using primers 338F and 806R targeting the V3-V4 hypervariable region. The plasmid copy number in the stock solution was calculated based on its concentration (measured by NanoDrop 2000) and molecular weight, and a tenfold serial dilution series (10⁻2 to 10⁻⁸) was used to generate the standard curve for absolute quantification.

16S rRNA amplicon sequencing and raw data processing

The DNA extracted from each soil sample was subjected to PCR amplification with primers used as described above for qPCR. PCR amplifications were performed in 20 μL mixtures containing 0.8 μL of each primer at 5 μM, 10 ng of template DNA, 4 μL of 5 × FastPfu Buffer, 2 μL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.4 μL of FastPfu Polymerase, 0.2 μL of BSA, and adding ddH2O to 20 μL. To monitor for contamination during the PCR setup, a negative control (using nuclease-free water as template) was included in every amplification run. The following thermal program was used for amplification by ABI GeneAmp 9700. First, the samples were heated to 95 °C for 3 min (1 cycle), followed by 27 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s, with a final extension of 72 °C for 10 min and 10 °C until use. Each aliquot was amplified in triplicate. The negative control yielded no visible amplicon on an agarose gel, confirming the absence of exogenous DNA contamination in the PCR reagents. The PCR products from triplicate reactions were pooled and purified using the EZNA Gel Extraction kit (Omega, USA). An equal amount of the PCR product from each subsample was combined in a single tube and prepared for 2 × 250 bp paired-end sequencing with Illumina Hiseq 2500 platform. The raw reads data were filtered by Trimmomatic (V0.33, http://www.usadellab.org/cms/?page=trimmomatic) for quality filter. The N-containing reads, the reads with quality value less than 20 and the filtered reads with sequence length less than 100 bp were filtered. At the same time, according to the barcode and primer information at the beginning and end of the sequence, using the Mothur software (v1.35.1, http://www.mothur.org), the barcode and primer were removed to obtain the paired end clean reads after quality control. The paired end clean reads were combined with FLASH (v1.2.11, https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/FLASH/) into full length sequences. Raw tag sequences were quality filtered using Mothur software to get effective splicing fragments (clean tags). The trimmed and unique sequences were used to detect operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at sequence divergences of 3%, i.e. different units share less than 97% sequence similarity. To ensure valid comparisons of alpha diversity across samples with unequal sequencing depth, all libraries were rarefied (subsampled without replacement) to an even depth of 33,030 sequences per sample, which was the minimum sequence count obtained in any sample. We constructed rarefaction curves for bacterial communities across all soil samples (Figure S2). The results showed that all of the curves eventually reached their asymptotes, indicating that the data generated in this study were enough for analyzing microbial diversity. Following rarefaction, a total of 7946 OTUs were obtained across all samples. The average number of observed OTUs per treatment was 2,019 for NoF, 2,035 for M, 1,828 for CF, and 2,064 for CFM. Alpha diversity metrics, including the number of Observed OTUs, the Shannon index (estimating species richness and evenness), Ace index (estimating species richness) and the Chao1 index (estimating species richness), were calculated on the rarefied OTU table using the ‘diversity’ function within the QIIME 2 pipeline. Beta diversity was analyzed based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrices. The raw sequences of the 16S rRNA were deposited into the NCBI database under the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) number PRJNA948152.

Co-occurrence analysis

Co-occurrence network analysis was conducted based on a SparCC correlation matrix (|r|> 0.6 and p < 0.01) using the WGCNA package in R [38]. The core microbiota was defined as the set of bacterial OTUs that were both (i) present in at least 80% of all soil samples and (ii) had a mean relative abundance greater than 0.01% across the dataset. This stringent definition ensured that only ubiquitous and abundant taxa, which represent the central and stable members of the community, were included in the subsequent network construction to improve its reliability and ecological relevance. Network properties (e.g., modularity, degree, betweenness centrality) were calculated by using the R igraph package [39]. Network figures were generated using Cytoscape software.

Community assembly process

To uncover the assembly mechanism of the bacterial community, we performed the null model following the framework described by Stegen [40] and Ning [41] in the “picante” package. Beta nearest taxon index (βNTI) and Bray–Curtis based Raup-Crick (RC bray) were calculated to estimate the variations in phylogenetic and taxonomic diversity. A value of βNTI > 2 represents the community assembly governed primarily by heterogeneous selection, while βNTI < -2 indicates homogeneous selection. |βNTI|< 2 represents the dominance of stochastic processes. Additionally, RC bray values were used to further partition the stochastic processes. RC bray < -0.95 represents homogenizing dispersal, and RC bray > 0.95 represents dispersal limitation. If |βNTI|< 2 and |RC bray|< 0.95, undominated assembly, consisting of weak selection, weak dispersal, diversification, and/or drift, governs the community assembly.

GeoChip hybridization and raw data processing

The composition and density of functional genes in soil were determined by GeoChip 5.0 as described previously [42]. The extracted DNA was labeled with Cy3 using random priming, cleaned using a QIAquick purification kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and then dried. Labeled DNA suspended in hybridization solution containing 10% formamide was pipetted into the center of a gasket slide well (Agilent), covered with an array slide, sealed using a SureHyb chamber, placed into the hybridization oven, and hybridized at 67℃ for 24 h. After hybridization, sliders were rinsed and imaged with a NimbleGen MS200 microarray scanner (Roche NimbleGen, Madison, WI, USA).

Raw GeoChip data downloaded from the scanner were standardized before being analyzed as follows: (a) the signal to noise ratio (SNR) of spots less than 2.0 were removed as poor-quality signal spots; (b) log-transformation of the signal intensity for each gene, and dividing them by the mean intensity in the sample were conducted; and (c) only one signal was detected in three replications of the treatment as unique probe was removed [43].

Statistical analyses

Three nonparametric dissimilarity tests (multi-response permutation procedure [MRPP], analysis of similarities [ANOSIM] and nonparametric multivariate analysis of variance [adonis] in the vegan package in R were used to compare differences of microbial community composition. The stability of the bacterial community was calculated by the ratio of the average value to the standard deviation of Bray–Curtis distances in the treatment [44]. Structural equation models (SEM) were calculated using soil properties, composition of the core bacterial community and functional genes and crop yield by AMOS. Soil properties in SEM included soil pH, soil total phosphorus (TP), soil nitrate concentration (NO3−-N), and crop yield (i.e. average yield of crops in one rotation period, maize-soybean-wheat). Overall fit of piecewise SEM was evaluated using Shipley’s test of d-separation, with Fisher’s C statistics (if p < 0.05, then no paths are missing, and the model is a good fit) and Akaike information criterion (AIC). Indicator bacterial communities for crop yield were analyzed based on the relationship between core bacterial communities and average yield of three crops (maize-soybean-wheat) in one rotation system by software of R 4.1.1. Indicator bacterial species for fertilization were calculated by the multipatt function in R using the indicspecies library based OTU table [45]. Species with a high indicator value (IndVal), based on a Monte Carlo permutation with 1000 replicates, were important indicators in the community [46].

Results

Influence of fertilization on crop yield and soil nutrient contents

After over forty years of fertilization, crop yield increased by 75%, 176% and 186% for the M, CF and CFM treatments, respectively, compared with NoF (p < 0.05) (Table 1). CF resulted in a decrease in soil pH value of approximately 0.8, while CF resulted in a 6.2–120% increase in soil nitrate, a 14.9–25.1% increase in available potassium, and a 71.1–109.6% increase in total phosphorus (p < 0.05, Table 1). Furthermore, the levels of MBC and MBN were found to be higher in M compared to other treatments (p < 0.05, Table 1).

Structures of bacterial communities and functional genes under long-term fertilization

It was observed that M and CF significantly increased and decreased bacterial community abundance, respectively, compared to NoF (Fig. 1). Functional gene number detected by GeoChip 5.0 revealed a contrasting pattern in the abundance of bacterial communities. The continuous input of fertilizer was found to result in a substantial increase in functional gene numbers, particularly in the case of CF (Fig. 1). The α-diversity of the bacterial community showed that CF significantly decreased the diversity of bacterial communities, while alpha-diversities of functional genes were significantly increased by CF compared with NoF and M (Table S1).

Principal Co-ordinates Analysis (PCoA) (R = 0.7690, P = 0.001) revealed that there was a distinct difference in bacterial communities between chemical fertilizer input (CF and CFM) and no chemical fertilizer input (NoF and M) (p < 0.05). PCoA analysis of functional genes (R = 0.9410, P = 0.001) showed that fertilizer application (M, CF and CFM) significantly altered distribution (p < 0.05), and manure application resulted in similar composition in M and CFM (Fig. S3; Table S2). The degree of bacterial community differentiation based on the Bray–Curtis distance revealed that long-term chemical fertilization led to higher bacterial community differentiation compared to other treatments (Fig. S4A), while CF significantly decreased bacterial community stability in contrast to other treatments (Fig. S4B).

Changes in taxonomic composition of bacterial communities and functional genes by fertilization

The compositions of the bacterial community and functional genes were significantly altered by fertilization using three nonparametric statistical analyses (ANOSIM, Adonis, and MRPP, p ≤ 0.018; Table 2), while more than eighty unique OTUs were detected in each treatment, and proportions of thirteen phyla of bacteria varied with long-term fertilization (Fig. S5). In brief, CF significantly enriched members of Proteobacteria, Gemmatimonadota, Patescibacteria, WPS-2, Dependentiae, and SAR324_cladeMarine_group_B, whereas NoF and M treatments showed higher abundances of Entotheonellaeota, Latescibacterota, Desulfobacterota, Methylomirabilota, WS2, NB1-j, and GAL 15 (P < 0.05). At finer taxonomic levels, relative abundances of 123 genera including Acetivibrio, Acidiphilium, Acidipila among others exhibited significant shifts in relative abundance (Table S3).

Assembly processes and coexistence in soil microbiome

We calculated βNTI values across samples to quantify the relative contributions of stochasticity versus determinism to community structure (Fig. 4A). The average βNTI value in CF samples was 2.01, while other treatments exhibited βNTI values < 2. This suggested that the microbial assembly in CF was primarily driven by heterogeneous selection (58.3%), followed by dispersal limitation (33.3%), and undominated processes (8.3%). Notably, manure application (CFM) reduced βNTI values and shifted assembly processes toward stochasticity, as dispersal limitation's contribution increased to 66.7–76.2% (Fig. 4B).

A co-occurrence network constructed from 1055 dominant bacterial OTUs identified 55 core nodes defined as core microbiota accounting for 0.47% of total soil bacterial reads. These core nodes comprised 7 phyla, including Acidobacteriota, Actinobacteriota, Chloroflexi, Firmicutes, Gemmatimonadota, Nitrospirota and Proteobacteria (Fig. 4C, Table S7). Significantly enriched OTUs were identified across treatments, with 242 in NoF, 92 in M, 36 in CFM, and 211 in CF, respectively. In addition, Node-level topological features varied significantly among treatments. The NoF-, M-, and CF-enriched OTUs formed independent modules and exhibited as more closed than CFM-enriched OTUs (Fig. 4D). Indicator bacterial communities for fertilization and crop yield

We identified bacterial indicator species and used them as ecological proxies for soil habitats under different long-term fertilization regimes. Discussion

Decoupling of taxonomic and functional responses to fertilization unveils ecosystem functional resilience

Our study reveals a central paradox in long-term fertilized black soils, that chemical fertilization (CF) significantly reduced bacterial taxonomic diversity yet concurrently enhanced the genetic potential for nutrient cycling, as evidenced by the increased abundance and diversity of functional genes (Table S1, Figs. 2, 3). This finding challenges the conventional paradigm that taxonomic erosion invariably leads to functional impairment [20, 47], highlighting a critical knowledge gap in our understanding of microbiome response to agricultural pressures. This apparent contradiction aligns with the concept of functional redundancy [49, 50], a fundamental principle in microbial ecology wherein multiple taxa perform similar ecological functions, thus buffering ecosystem processes against biodiversity loss.

We propose that this divergence can be explained by two non-exclusive mechanisms: functional redundancy and environmental selection. The observed functional enrichment despite taxonomic loss suggests that the remaining taxa possess a broad genetic capacity to maintain key biogeochemical processes [49, 51]. More importantly, CF creates a strong selective environment characterized by acidification and resource limitation (low SOM and microbial biomass C). This selective pressure likely favors the dominance of a few highly adapted K-strategist taxa [52, 53] that are genetically equipped for rapid nutrient acquisition from complex substrates (e.g., cellulose, chitin) and nitrification. This is consistent with the theory of environmental filtering [54, 55], which predicts that harsh abiotic conditions select for a phylogenetically clustered set of traits. Thus, the elevated functional gene diversity in CF may not represent a healthier ecosystem, but rather a stressed community channeling its resources into survival and competition for limited resources, explaining the accelerated C and N transformations we observed.

Keystone taxa as indicators of management driven shifts

Beyond broad community patterns, we identified specific bacterial phyla (Chloroflexi, Acidobacteriota, Actinobacteriota, Proteobacteria) as key indicators of fertilization regimes and crop yield (Table 2), positioning them as potential keystone taxa modulating agroecosystem functioning [56, 57]. The concept of keystone species, which have a disproportionate impact on their environment relative to their abundance, is well established in ecology [58] and is increasingly applied to microbial systems [56].

The trajectories of these keystone taxa reveal the distinct selective landscapes imposed by management. In CF, the enrichment of oligotrophic specialists like Candidatus_Solibacter (Acidobacteriota) and Nocardioides (Actinobacteriota) reflects a community adapted to resource limited, acidic conditions [59, 60]. These taxa, functionally linked to carbon degradation and metal homeostasis, represent a specialized consortium that thrives under high environmental filtering. Conversely, manure amendments (M, CFM) fostered a different dynamic. The proliferation of r-strategists within Acidobacteriota and Proteobacteria [52, 53], such as cellulose-degraders like Acidothermus [61], demonstrates how organic inputs shift the selective landscape from stress tolerance to resource opportunism. By providing labile carbon, manure alleviates resource limitation and supports a broader, more functionally engaged microbial network, a phenomenon supported by studies in diverse agricultural systems [62, 63]. Notably, the identification of these keystone taxa provides tangible biological indicators for soil health and a mechanistic understanding of how organic fertilization directly supports microbial groups that drive nutrient cycling.

Manure amendment modulates community assembly to enhance stability

Our findings provide a mechanistic understanding of how fertilization alters the fundamental rules of community assembly, moving beyond descriptive composition changes. The shift towards deterministic processes (heterogeneous selection) in CF (Fig. 4) confirms that chemical fertilization acts as a strong environmental filter, consistently selecting for a narrow, stress tolerant microbiome over time [64]. This deterministic winnowing mechanistically explains the observed loss of taxonomic diversity and is a common response to strong anthropogenic selection [54, 55].

Critically, CFM mitigated this effect, shifting the balance towards stochastic processes (dispersal limitation). This implies that organic matter incorporation increases spatial heterogeneity and resource availability [65], creating a wider array of ecological niches that allow for more random assembly and greater taxonomic variability. This shift from deterministic to stochastic assembly is a key mechanism through which manure enhances community stability and functional resilience [66, 67]. The intermediate disturbance hypothesis [68], which posits that moderate levels of disturbance maximize diversity, may explain the superior diversity and stability in CFM treatments. A stochastically assembled community, with higher phylogenetic diversity, is likely more adaptable to environmental fluctuations, thereby underpinning the sustainable productivity observed in CFM treatments [69].

Synthesis and implications for sustainable intensification

Our findings collectively demonstrate that long-term fertilization regimes reorganize the soil microbiome through two interlinked pathways: (1) physicochemical alterations (e.g., acidification, SOM depletion) that impose deterministic filters, and (2) resource-mediated shifts in community assembly processes (Fig. 4A).

The practical implication is clear: while chemical fertilization alone maintains functional potential at the cost of taxonomic diversity and pushes the system toward a deterministic, potentially fragile state, integrated organic amendment is indispensable for sustaining both microbial diversity and function. By promoting stochastic assembly and supporting keystone taxa, manure sustains a more complex and stable ecological network that is likely more resilient to perturbation, aligning with the portfolio effect theory where ecosystem stability arises from diverse asynchronous responses of species to environmental changes [70].

Therefore, we propose that sustainable agricultural management in black soil regions should move beyond a purely chemical-based paradigm. Instead, strategies should aim to harness the power of organic inputs to engineer a robust soil microbiome, an approach supported by the microbial engineering principle [71]. This involves fostering stochastic assembly processes and supporting keystone functional groups, ultimately leading to ecosystems that are not only productive but also resilient and sustainable in the long term. Our SEM model (Fig. 5), which directly links microbial functional gene diversity to crop yield, provides a quantitative framework for evaluating the success of such management practices and underscores the critical role of microbial-mediated nutrient cycling in agroecosystem productivity [72, 73].

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study moves beyond cataloging microbial population shifts to reveal the fundamental ecological mechanisms through which long-term fertilization reorganizes the black soil microbiome. Our key novel finding is that chemical fertilization promotes deterministic assembly via environmental filtering (e.g., acidification), while manure amendment facilitates stochastic processes by enhancing habitat heterogeneity and buffering against harsh selection. This mechanistic shift from selection to dispersal limitation is critical, as it underlies the enhanced functional gene diversity, community stability, and ecosystem multifunctionality observed under manure treatment.

Notably, we demonstrate that these microbial properties are not merely academic metrics but are positively correlated with crop yield, providing a direct link between management practices, microbial ecology, and agricultural productivity. The identified core taxa (Acidobacteriota, Actinobacteriota, Chloroflexi, and Proteobacteria) serve as key biological indicators for soil health and yield potential.

Therefore, we propose that partial substitution of chemical fertilizers with manure is a superior strategy for the sustainable intensification of agriculture in black soil regions. This practice effectively mitigates the adverse effects of chemical fertilization, fosters a more diverse and stable microbiome through distinct assembly rules, and ultimately supports higher crop yields by enhancing microbial nutrient cycling functionality.
