Association of biological age acceleration with mortality in adults with diabetes or prediabetes: a mediating role of physical activity
Authors: Nan Wu, Jianfang Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe sought to investigate the association between biological age (BA) acceleration and mortality among adults with diabetes or prediabetes and to assess whether physical activity mediates this association.MethodsWe included 7,337 participants with diabetes or prediabetes from the 1999–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. BA was estimated using the Klemera–Doubal method (KDM). BA acceleration was defined as the residual difference between the estimated BA and chronological age. Cox proportional hazards models were used to examine the association between KDM-BA acceleration and mortality. The mediating effect of physical activity were evaluated to investigate the potential mechanism of the associations between BA acceleration and mortality.ResultsOver a median follow-up period of 94 months, 1,445 deaths occurred. Participants in the high tertile of KDM-BA acceleration had a 35% higher risk of all-cause mortality compared to those in the low tertile (HR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.18–1.54, P < 0.0001). Participants engaging in ≥ 600 MET-minutes/week of physical activity had a lower risk of all-cause mortality compared to those with < 600 (HR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.77–0.95, P = 0.0047). Mediation analysis indicated that physical activity accounted for 17.53% (P < 0.0001) of the association between KDM-BA acceleration and all-cause mortality. Similar patterns were observed for cardiovascular mortality.ConclusionElevated BA acceleration is associated with an increased risk of mortality in adults with diabetes or prediabetes. Higher levels of physical activity are linked to reduced mortality risk and partially mediate the relationship between BA acceleration and mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40520-025-03204-3.

Introduction

Over recent decades, diabetes has become a major global public health challenge. Patients with diabetes demonstrate significantly elevated risks of both cardiovascular and all-cause mortality [1, 2]. It is estimated that approximately 536.6 million adults are affected worldwide, accounting for about 10.5% of the adult population [1]. Prediabetes is even more widespread, tends to progress to diabetes, and increases cardiovascular risk [3]. These adverse outcomes suggest that individuals with dysglycemia may experience accelerated health decline, potentially reflecting an increased biological age (BA) relative to their chronological age.

BA quantifies the cumulative physiological deterioration of the body’s systems in contrast to chronological age [4]. Unlike chronological age, BA can vary among individuals of the same age owing to differences in genetics, lifestyle, and exposure, and a higher BA is thought to indicate greater underlying biological damage or aging [5, 6]. Studies have shown that when an individual’s BA exceeds their chronological age, a state often termed accelerated biological aging, there is an increased risk of age-related diseases and premature mortality [7–9]. Indeed, accelerated biological aging has been associated with a higher incidence of chronic conditions (e.g. cardiovascular disease, diabetes) and all-cause mortality in diverse populations [10, 11]. Multiple methods for estimating BA have been developed. One widely used approach is the Klemera–Doubal method (KDM), which computes the composite BA by optimally weighting biomarkers to match the age distribution of a reference population [12, 13].

In the context of diabetes, accelerated biological aging is a particularly pertinent concern. Chronic hyperglycemia and metabolic dysfunction in diabetes can drive processes such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and vascular damage, which may hasten the deterioration of organ systems. Recent evidence suggests that individuals with diabetes tend to have increased BA relative to their non-diabetic peers. For instance, Cortez et al. [14] found that individuals with diabetes exhibit a significantly higher BA than their chronological age, indicating accelerated biological aging. Accelerated biological aging may contribute to the observed increased risk of complications and mortality associated with diabetes. However, studies specifically examining BA acceleration in diabetic or pre-diabetic populations and its relationship with mortality outcomes are limited.

Another important consideration is the role of lifestyle factors in modulating accelerated biological aging and their impact on health. Physical activity is a well-known determinant of health and longevity. Regular exercise is associated with improved glycemic control, blood pressure, lipid profiles, and lower mortality rates [15–17]. Emerging evidence suggests that physical activity may slow certain aspects of the aging process at the molecular level. For example, higher activity levels have been linked to slower epigenetic aging and longer telomeres, indicative of a more “youthful” biological state [18–20]. A recent report noted that physically active individuals tend to exhibit slower epigenetic acceleration and better age-related health outcomes [21]. These findings suggest that physical activity attenuates the risks associated with accelerated biological aging. To the best of our knowledge, few studies have examined whether physical activity mediates the association between BA acceleration and mortality.

In light of these gaps, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of adults with diabetes or prediabetes in the US. Our objectives were to (1) examine the association between BA acceleration and the risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in this population and (2) evaluate whether physical activity mediates the relationship between BA acceleration and mortality.

Methods

Study population and data source

We used data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 1999–2018, which is a series of surveys designed to assess the health and nutritional status of the US population. We analyzed the NHANES data from 1999 to 2018, focusing on eligible participants with diabetes or prediabetes. As shown in Fig. 1, a final analytic sample of 7,337 participants was obtained after applying the following exclusion criteria: (1) individuals younger than 18 years of age (n = 42,112); (2) participants without diabetes or prediabetes (n = 46,222); and (3) participants with missing BA data (n = 1,295), missing physical activity data (n = 4,338), or missing mortality information (n = 12). The National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Ethics Review Board approved the NHANES research protocol and all participants provided written informed consent.

Definition of diabetes and prediabetes

Diabetes was defined as meeting any of the following criteria: (1) self-reported physician diagnosis of diabetes; (2) laboratory evidence including glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%, fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, random plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, or 2-hour plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L during oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT); or (3) current use of antidiabetic medications. Prediabetes was defined as impaired fasting glucose (IFG) (FPG ≥ 6.1 to < 7.0 mmol/L) or impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) (2-hour OGTT glucose ≥ 7.8 to < 11.1 mmol/L).

Measurement of BA acceleration

BA was measured using the KDM [12, 22]. The KDM calculates BA based on eight biomarkers: Ln-C-reactive protein (Ln-CRP), serum creatinine, glycosylated hemoglobin, serum albumin, serum total cholesterol, serum urea nitrogen, serum alkaline phosphatase, and systolic blood pressure.

BA acceleration, referred to as KDM-BA acceleration, was defined as the residual of regressing the estimated BA on chronological age. A positive residual value indicates accelerated biological aging, whereas a negative value indicates decelerated aging.

Outcomes

The primary outcome measure was all-cause mortality. Mortality status and cause of death were determined via linkage to the National Death Index (NDI) through December 31, 2019. Data from each NHANES participant were linked to corresponding NDI records using unique identifiers and their vital status was ascertained. The secondary outcome was cardiovascular mortality. The cause of death was classified according to the underlying cause from the death certificate and coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10). Cardiovascular-related deaths were identified based on ICD-10 codes I00–I09, I11, I13, I20–I51, and I60–I69, covering a broad range of cardiac and cerebrovascular conditions [23].

Covariates

We included a range of baseline covariates known as potential confounders or important participant characteristics. Demographic variables included age (years), sex (male or female), and ethnicity (categorized as non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, Mexican American, other Hispanic, or other ethnicities). Socioeconomic status was assessed using the poverty to income ratio (PIR), a continuous measure of household income relative to the federal poverty threshold. PIR was further categorized as poor (< 1.0), near-poor (1.0–1.9), middle income (2.0–3.9), and high income (≥ 4.0). Educational level was classified as less than high school, high school graduate, or higher. Smoking status was defined as never smoked, former smoker, or current smoker. Alcohol consumption was categorized based on lifetime intake and average daily consumption as follows: never drinkers (< 12 drinks in lifetime), former drinkers (≥ 12 drinks in lifetime but abstinent for > 1 year), mild drinkers (≤ 1 drink/day for women or ≤ 2 drinks/day for men), moderate drinkers (1–3 drinks/day for women or 2–4 drinks/day for men), and heavy drinkers (≥ 4 drinks/day for women or ≥ 5 drinks/day for men). Stroke was defined by patients’ self-reported diagnosis from a physician during face-to-face interviews. Parkinson’s disease was defined by self-reported use of antiparkinsonian medications (including benztropine, levodopa, carbidopa, methyldopa, ropinirole, entacapone, and amantadine).

Physical activity was examined both as an independent exposure and as a potential mediator. The NHANES collected self-reported data regarding the frequency and duration of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) across occupational, transportation, and recreational domains using structured questionnaires. These activities were converted to total weekly metabolic equivalent task minutes (MET-minutes/week) according to the NHANES analytic guidelines. In accord with public health recommendations, we dichotomized physical activity into meeting (≥ 600 MET-minutes/week) or not meeting (< 600 MET-minutes/week) the minimum recommended activity level—equivalent to approximately 150 min of moderate-intensity exercise per week. This threshold corresponded to the physical activity guidelines of the US Department of Health and Human Services [24]. We examined the association between physical activity and mortality and assessed its role as a potential mediator in the relationship between BA acceleration and mortality.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics, with continuous variables presented as means ± standard deviation(SD) and categorical variables as proportions. Differences among the KDM-BA acceleration tertiles were evaluated using one-way ANOVA for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables.

Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate the associations between KDM-BA acceleration and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. The findings are displayed as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression models were further used to evaluate the dose–response relationship between KDM-BA acceleration and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality.

Additionally, Cox regression analysis was performed to determine the association between total physical activity and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. “Mediation” package in R 4.2.0. was used to perform mediation analysis to assess the mediating effects of total physical activity on the association between KDM-BA acceleration and mortality, adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, poverty-to-income ratio, education, smoking, drinking, stroke, and Parkinson’s disease. A mediating effect was defined as satisfying all of the following conditions: a significant indirect effect, a significant total effect, and a positive proportion of the mediator effect.

To address the complex sampling design of the NHANES, we performed sensitivity analyses using NHANES-recommended sampling weights for core regression models. Weighted HRs were compared with unweighted estimates to evaluate consistency.

All statistical analyses were performed using the EmpowerStats software (www.empowerstats.com; X&Y Solutions, Inc. Boston, MA) and R software v.4.2.0 (http://www.r-project.org). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value < 0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics stratified by tertiles of KDM-BA acceleration in the study population of 7,337 participants. The groups were defined as follows: Tertile 1 (− 13.68 ± 4.65), Tertile 2 (− 2.03 ± 3.31), and Tertile 3 (15.71 ± 9.50). Significant differences (all P < 0.05) were observed across the tertiles for key demographic and metabolic variables including age, sex distribution, ethnicity, poverty-to-income ratio, education, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity levels. Participants in Tertile 3 exhibited the highest prevalence of diabetes mellitus (74.45%) and the lowest proportion of impaired fasting glycemia (14.39%) and impaired glucose tolerance (11.16%).

During a median follow-up of 94 months, 1,445 participants died from all causes (cumulative incidence, 19.7%). Among them, 477 (6.5%) died of cardiovascular causes.

Association of BA acceleration with mortality

As shown in Table 2, KDM-BA acceleration was significantly associated with increased risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among US adults with diabetes or prediabetes. In the fully adjusted model (model 2), each 5-year increase in KDM-BA acceleration was associated with a 5% higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR = 1.05, 95% CI: 1.03–1.07; P < 0.0001). When participants were categorized by tertiles of KDM-BA acceleration, those in the middle and high tertiles showed significantly increased risks of all-cause mortality compared to the low tertile group, with HRs of 1.28 (95% CI: 1.12–1.46; P = 0.0003) and 1.35 (95% CI: 1.18–1.54; P < 0.0001), respectively. A significant dose-response relationship was observed (P for trend < 0.0001).

Similarly, for cardiovascular mortality, each 5-year increase in KDM-BA acceleration was associated with a 7% higher risk (HR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.04–1.11; P < 0.0001) in model 2. The middle and high tertile groups had HRs of 1.39 (95% CI: 1.11–1.75; P = 0.0047) and 1.53 (95% CI: 1.21–1.92; P = 0.0003), respectively, compared to the low tertile group, with a significant trend across tertiles (P for trend = 0.0003). The dose-response relationships are visually presented in Figures S1 and S2.

Associations of physical activity with mortality

As shown in Table 3, when comparing total physical activity levels ≥ 600 versus < 600 MET minutes/week, a statistically significant reduction in all-cause mortality was observed. In model 1, which was adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, poverty to income ratio, and education, individuals with physical activity ≥ 600 MET-minutes/week had a 15% lower risk of all-cause mortality (HR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.76–0.94, P = 0.0023). This association remained statistically significant after further adjustment in model 2 (HR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.77–0.95, P = 0.0047). For cardiovascular mortality, although a lower HR was observed in participants with physical activity ≥ 600 MET-minutes/week compared to those with < 600 MET-minutes/week, the associations were not statistically significant in either model 1 (HR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.74–1.07, P = 0.2259) or model 2 (HR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.76–1.09, P = 0.3150). These findings suggest that increased physical activity is associated with reduced all-cause mortality in adults with diabetes or prediabetes.

Mediating role of physical activity

As illustrated in Fig. 2, total physical activity partially mediated the association between KDM-BA acceleration and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. For all-cause mortality, the total effect of KDM-BA acceleration was 0.019 (P < 0.0001), with an indirect effect through total physical activity of 0.003 (95% CI: 0.002–0.005, P < 0.0001), accounting for 17.53% of the total effect. The direct effect remained significant (0.022, 95% CI: 0.012–0.032, P < 0.0001). Similarly, the total effect on cardiovascular mortality was 0.012 (P < 0.0001), with an indirect effect of 0.001 (95% CI: 0.001–0.002, P < 0.0001), representing a mediation proportion of 11.11%. The direct effect was also statistically significant (0.013, 95% CI: 0.006–0.020, P < 0.0001). These results suggest that total physical activity serves as a significant but partial mediator of the relationship between KDM-BA acceleration and mortality outcomes.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted additional sensitivity analyses using NHANES-provided sampling weights to assess the robustness of our core findings. These results were consistent with those of the original (unweighted) analysis. Specifically, the association between BA acceleration and mortality, as well as between physical activity and mortality, remained statistically significant and directionally consistent after applying sampling weights. These weighted results are included in Supplementary Table S1 and Table S2.

Discussion

In this cohort of American adults with diabetes or prediabetes, we found that higher KDM-BA acceleration was significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Moreover, higher levels of physical activity were associated with lower mortality risk and partially mediated the relationship between KDM-BA acceleration and mortality. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the association between KDM-BA acceleration and mortality, and the mediating role of physical activity in adults with diabetes or prediabetes.

In our study, we demonstrated that participants in the highest tertile of BA acceleration had a 35% higher risk of all-cause mortality and a 53% higher risk of cardiovascular mortality than those in the lowest tertile after adjusting for conventional risk factors. These findings are supported by Sabbatinelli et al. [25], who analyzed a well-matched cohort of Italian patients with diabetes over a 16-year follow-up period. Using epigenetic clocks, specifically DNAm PhenoAge and Dunedin PoAm, they found that BA was significantly associated with a higher mortality risk. DNAm PhenoAge reflected a HR of 1.16 (95% CI: 1.05–1.28), and DunedinPoAm yielded a stronger association (HR = 3.65, 95% CI: 1.43–9.35), independent of traditional clinical predictors.

Multiple studies in populations with various chronic diseases have demonstrated a significant positive association between BA acceleration and mortality risk, which supports the main findings of our study. For example, Liu et al. [26] reported that among 2,643 cancer survivors, a one-year increase in phenotypic age acceleration was associated with a 4% higher risk of all-cause mortality. Similarly, in a prospective cohort study of 2, 199 participants with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, Wang et al. [27] found that each 1-SD increase in KDM-BA acceleration was associated with a 3% higher risk of all-cause mortality and a 3% higher risk of cardiovascular mortality. Jiang et al. [28] used data from 341,159 participants in the UK Biobank and found that each 1-SD increase in KDM-BA acceleration was associated with a 3.5% higher risk of mortality, further supporting a robust association between BA acceleration and mortality. These consistent findings across diverse populations and aging measures strongly agree with our results.

Our results highlighted the role of BA acceleration as an integrative measure of health status. Traditional risk factors (blood pressure, glucose, etc.) are known to predict mortality. However, the KDM-BA combines these factors into a single metric that reflects the cumulative deviation from expected “normal” aging [12, 29]. Essentially, KDM-BA acceleration can be viewed as a summary measure of multisystem dysregulation or “wear and tear” on the body. Higher KDM-BA acceleration likely signifies subclinical organ damage or accelerated pathophysiological changes. In individuals with diabetes, chronic hyperglycemia and insulin resistance may accelerate damage to the cardiovascular system, kidneys, eyes, and nerves [30, 31], which is reflected by abnormal clinical biomarkers (e.g., higher blood pressure, worse lipid profiles, and renal function decline). Thus, it is plausible that individuals with higher BA acceleration in our study had more severe or longstanding metabolic perturbations, putting them at risk of complications and death.

Our findings provide insights into the potential mechanisms linking BA acceleration to mortality in adults with diabetes or prediabetes, particularly regarding the mediating role of physical activity. Participants who met the physical activity recommendations exhibited significantly lower all-cause mortality, which is consistent with the results of previous studies surrounding the benefits of regular exercise. Biologically, physical activity is associated with improved cardiovascular function, enhanced insulin sensitivity, reduced systemic inflammation, and better vascular health [32–34], all of which may help counteract the adverse physiological effects of accelerated biological aging. Although molecular markers of aging were not assessed in this study, previous studies have demonstrated that higher levels of physical activity are associated with slower epigenetic aging and more favorable biomarker profiles. For example, Radák et al. [35] reported that elite athletes exhibit delayed epigenetic aging compared to non-athletes. While our population differed substantially, these findings support a broader biological gradient in which greater physical activity is linked to healthier aging trajectories.

Our mediation analysis showed that approximately 17% of the association between BA acceleration and mortality was mediated by physical activity. This suggests that individuals with higher BA acceleration may be less physically active, possibly because of underlying frailty, comorbidities, or behavioral factors, and that inactivity contributes to increased mortality risk. Conversely, promoting physical activity could potentially mitigate excess mortality associated with accelerated aging. However, most associations between BA acceleration and mortality remained direct (i.e., not explained by measured physical activity), suggesting that even optimal levels of exercise may not fully offset the biological burden of accelerated aging. This residual effect likely reflects additional mechanisms such as intrinsic cellular aging processes, organ system dysfunction, or unmeasured behavioral and environmental factors, including poor dietary habits and psychosocial stress [36–39]. Nevertheless, the identification of physical activity as a partial mediator highlights its relevance as a modifiable behavioral factor and supports the potential value of lifestyle-based interventions, particularly for individuals exhibiting BA in excess of their chronological age.

Our findings concerning cardiovascular mortality are broadly similar to those for all-cause mortality, which is to be expected given that cardiovascular disease is a leading cause of death among individuals with diabetes [40]. The observed association between BA acceleration and cardiovascular mortality may reflect underlying physiological processes such as atherosclerosis, arterial stiffness, and cardiac dysfunction, which are related to cardiovascular risk [41, 42]. This association persisted even after adjusting for conventional cardiovascular disease risk factors (captured indirectly using the BA algorithm and covariates), suggesting that BA acceleration may reflect additional aspects of cardiovascular health status.

This study was a hypothesis-driven association analysis, rather than a nationally representative prevalence estimate. Therefore, primary analyses were conducted without weightings, which is consistent with prior methodological recommendations. Nonetheless, sensitivity analyses using the NHANES sampling weights confirmed the robustness of our findings.

This study had several notable strengths. First, it involved a large cohort of US adults with diabetes or prediabetes: a population at elevated risk for adverse health outcomes. Second, by incorporating mediation analysis, we explored the potential role of physical activity in the pathway linking BA acceleration to mortality. The observed partial mediation highlights physical activity as a potentially modifiable factor that may attenuate the negative health effects of accelerated biological aging in this high-risk population.

This study had some limitations. First, physical activity was self-reported, making it prone to measurement errors. Second, residual confounding by unmeasured factors could potentially influence the associations. Nevertheless, we addressed this concern by demonstrating consistent results across two models with sequential adjustments, which strengthens the robustness of our findings. Finally, the observational nature of the NHANES precludes causal inferences between BA acceleration and health outcomes. It is important to note that the mediation analysis was based on an assumed causal pathway, wherein BA acceleration contributes to lower physical activity, which, in turn, is associated with increased mortality. However, the relationship between biological aging and physical activity is likely bidirectional and complex; declining health may reduce activity levels, whereas sedentary behavior may further exacerbate biological aging and adverse health outcomes. Therefore, our mediation findings should be interpreted as descriptive, reflecting the shared variance between variables, rather than as evidence of a definitive causal mechanism. Future randomized controlled trials are needed to evaluate whether increased physical activity among individuals with high BA acceleration can lead to improved survival outcomes.

Conclusions

BA acceleration is positively associated with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality risk among US adults with diabetes or prediabetes. Higher levels of physical activity are linked to reduced mortality risk and partially mediate the relationship between BA acceleration and mortality.
