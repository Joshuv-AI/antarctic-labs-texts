Laser-Assisted OPN Technique for Recalcitrant Multilayer In-Stent Restenosis
Authors: Omar Majwal, Vu Hung Quan, Robert Avram, Mohamed Nosair
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultilayer in-stent restenosis (ISR) with severe stent underexpansion represents a challenging clinical scenario with limited effective treatment options when conventional interventions fail, despite all our current advances in interventional cardiology.Case SummaryWe present a 65-year-old man with recurrent angina despite multiple prior right coronary artery interventions, including intravascular lithotripsy and drug-eluting stent implantation. Optical coherence tomography revealed triple-layer stent restenosis with severe underexpansion refractory to noncompliant balloon and OPN super-high-pressure balloon dilation. The patient was referred to a specialized center, where contrast-enhanced excimer laser coronary atherectomy (ELCA) combined with OPN balloon angioplasty (L-OPN technique) was performed. Intravascular ultrasound–guided ELCA (1.4-mm catheter, 60 mJ/mm2 × 60 Hz, 6 runs with contrast) followed by OPN balloon at 40 atm achieved significant improvement in minimum lumen diameter (from 1.8 × 1.9 mm to 2.6 × 3.1 mm) and minimum lumen area (6.7 mm2). The procedure was completed with drug-eluting balloon therapy and ostial stenting.DiscussionThis case illustrates the systematic L-OPN approach for treating complex multilayer ISR when conventional methods have failed. Contrast-enhanced ELCA generates rapidly expanding microbubbles creating acoustic pressures that can disrupt calcified and fibrotic tissue surrounding underexpanded stents, facilitating subsequent balloon expansion. Intravascular ultrasound guidance was critical for assessing baseline underexpansion and confirming an adequate final result.Take-Home MessageWhen conventional balloon angioplasty and intravascular lithotripsy fail to achieve adequate stent expansion in multilayer ISR, the combination of contrast-enhanced ELCA followed by OPN super-high-pressure balloon (L-OPN technique) guided by intravascular imaging offers a viable therapeutic option.Key words: excimer laser coronary atherectomy, in-stent restenosis, optical coherence tomography, stent underexpansion, super-high-pressure balloon

History of Presentation

A 65-year-old man presented with Canadian Cardiovascular Society class III angina despite optimal medical therapy. His interventional history was notable for multiple prior right coronary artery (RCA) interventions spanning several years:

•2021: The patient was initially treated for in-stent restenosis (ISR) involving 2 layers of stents in the mid and distal RCA, using resting flow ratio–guided percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). Cutting balloon dilation followed by drug-coated balloon achieved a satisfactory physiological result.

•2024: The patient re-presented with recurrent restenosis. The lesion was treated with cutting balloon dilatation and intravascular lithotripsy followed by implantation of an additional stent layer, thus totaling 3 layers of stent.

This history underscores the critical importance of intravascular imaging during complex repeat PCI to optimize stent expansion and guide therapy selection, particularly in recalcitrant ISR.

Past Medical History

The patient's cardiovascular risk factors included diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. His extensive coronary intervention history included PCI to the left anterior descending artery (stent patent), circumflex artery (stent patent), and multiple RCA interventions dating back before 2021, when drug-eluting stents and drug-coated balloon therapy were performed for ISR. The pattern of recurrent RCA restenosis despite multiple interventions with increasingly aggressive approaches suggested significant fibrocalcific tissue resistant to conventional therapies.

Differential Diagnosis

Given the history of multiple prior interventions and recurrent symptoms, the differential diagnosis included neoatherosclerosis within the previously placed stents, neointimal hyperplasia, and mechanical stent underexpansion owing to underlying calcified or fibrotic tissue. Intravascular imaging was critical for distinguishing between these mechanisms to guide appropriate treatment strategy.

Investigations

Coronary angiography via left radial access revealed right coronary dominance with patent stents in the left anterior descending and circumflex arteries. The RCA demonstrated severe midvessel (90%) and distal (80%) restenosis within the previously stented segments, with TIMI flow grade 2.

OCT was performed at the referring center using a 6-F JR-4 guide catheter and provided critical diagnostic information. The imaging revealed 3 layers of stent struts in the mid-RCA, with severe underexpansion. The minimum stent diameter measured only 1.6 mm against a reference vessel diameter exceeding 3 mm, confirming significant mechanical underexpansion as the primary mechanism of restenosis. Calcified neointimal tissue was evident surrounding the underexpanded stent segments (Figure 1).

Management

Initial intervention attempt

A systematic approach to lesion modification was undertaken. Predilation with a noncompliant 3.0 × 20 mm balloon was performed, followed by cutting balloon angioplasty (3.0 × 10 mm at 18 atm). Despite achieving cutting balloon deployment, repeat OCT demonstrated persistent severe underexpansion, with minimal improvement.

Escalation to noncompliant balloon (3.0 mm at 20 atm) followed by OPN super-high-pressure balloon (3.0 × 10 mm at 30 atm; SIS Medical) was performed. Even with these aggressive measures, postdilation OCT confirmed persistent calcified ISR with severe underexpansion of the triple-layer stent segment, indicating failure of conventional balloon-based strategies.

Planned L-OPN technique

Given the refractory nature of the lesion, the decision was made to refer the patient to Montreal Heart Institute for contrast-enhanced excimer laser coronary atherectomy (ELCA) combined with OPN super-high-pressure balloon angioplasty—what we term the L-OPN (laser-assisted OPN) technique.

L-OPN procedure at Montreal Heart Institute

Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) (Avvigo+ multi-modality guidance system, Boston Scientific) was performed to assess the lesion, revealing severe underexpansion with minimum lumen dimensions of 1.8 × 1.9 mm. The L-OPN technique was then executed systematically:

Outcome and Follow-Up

The L-OPN procedure achieved excellent angiographic and intravascular imaging results (Figure 4). IVUS demonstrated significant improvement in lumen dimensions, from a baseline MLD of 1.8 × 1.9 mm to a final MLD of 2.6 × 3.1 mm, with an MLA of 6.7 mm2—representing a >3-fold increase in cross-sectional area (Figure 5). The procedure was completed with 2 drug-eluting balloons (Pantera Lux 3.0 × 20 mm) to the treated segment and ostial stent placement (Xience Pro 3.5 × 12 mm).

Total contrast volume was 120 mL, radiation dose was 62.5 cGy⋅cm2, and fluoroscopy time was 18.4 minutes. There were no intraprocedural complications such as dissection, perforation, slow flow, or distal embolization. The patient tolerated the procedure well and was discharged on dual antiplatelet therapy.

At the 4-week clinical follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic, with complete resolution of anginal symptoms. There were no major adverse cardiovascular events, including myocardial infarction, target lesion revascularization, or stent thrombosis. The patient continued dual antiplatelet therapy without bleeding or ischemic complications and returned to normal daily activities.

Discussion

Stent underexpansion is a well-established predictor of stent thrombosis and ISR, reported in 20% to 30% of PCI cases when assessed by intravascular imaging. Persistently underexpanded stents carry a markedly higher risk of adverse events, and this problem is amplified when multiple stent layers are present. In such cases, resistant fibrocalcific tissue limits the effectiveness of conventional postdilation.2

Our case illustrates a systematic approach to managing recalcitrant multilayer ISR. Despite aggressive therapy at the referring center, including intravascular lithotripsy, cutting balloon, and OPN super-high-pressure balloon at 30 atm, the calcified fibrotic tissue surrounding the underexpanded stents prevented adequate expansion. This pattern is consistent with published literature demonstrating that the amount of resistant scar and fibrotic tissue, rather than calcium alone, can cause failure of conventional therapies.3, 4, 5, 6, 7

ELCA offers a unique mechanism for modifying resistant tissue. The laser uses photochemical, photothermal, and photomechanical energy to ablate plaque and modify vessel compliance.8 When performed with contrast injection (off-label), ELCA generates rapidly expanding microbubbles, creating acoustic pressures that may exceed 100 atm, which can disrupt calcium and fibrotic tissue that resist conventional balloon angioplasty.9 In our case, using a 1.4-mm catheter at 60 mJ/mm2 and 60 Hz with 6 contrast-enhanced runs provided sufficient tissue modification to allow adequate OPN balloon expansion to 40 atm.

The L-OPN technique combines the tissue-modifying capabilities of contrast-enhanced ELCA with the high-pressure dilation force of an OPN balloon. Published data from the ELLEMENT registry8 demonstrated high success rates using laser for underexpanded stents, defined as achieving an increase in minimum stent area of ≥1 mm2. A recent series of 16 patients treated with coronary laser and simultaneous contrast injection showed an average increase in minimum stent area of 2.34 ± 1.57 mm2, with no technique-related complications.6 Our results—achieving a final MLA of 5 mm2 from a severely underexpanded baseline of approximately 1.4 mm2 (calculated from an MLD of 1.5 × 1.2 mm)—are consistent with these published outcomes and demonstrate the efficacy of this approach.

Intravascular imaging played an essential role in this case. OCT and IVUS confirmed the severity of baseline underexpansion, explained the mechanism, and documented the significant improvement in lumen dimensions after L-OPN—findings that would have been angiographically unclear. While rotational or orbital atherectomy may be considered for calcified single-stent underexpansion through an ablative technique, multilayer stent architecture limits their effectiveness and increases entrapment risk, making contrast-enhanced ELCA the preferred approach in this clinical scenario.

Alternative management strategies warrant consideration. The patient presented with Canadian Cardiovascular Society class III angina despite optimal medical therapy. Coronary artery bypass grafting was less favorable given the localized RCA disease without left main or extensive multivessel involvement. Intracoronary brachytherapy remains an option for recurrent ISR but has limited availability; in this case, the successful L-OPN result with drug-eluting balloon therapy provided effective treatment without requiring brachytherapy referral.

Conclusions

The present case highlights the challenges of managing multilayer ISR with severe stent underexpansion refractory to conventional interventions. The systematic L-OPN approach—combining contrast-enhanced excimer laser atherectomy with super-high-pressure balloon angioplasty, guided by intravascular imaging—represents an effective strategy for treating these complex challenging lesions when other options have been proven ineffective.
