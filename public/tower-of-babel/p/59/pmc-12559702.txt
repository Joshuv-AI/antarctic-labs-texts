Exergoeconomic and enviroeconomic evaluation of double pass solar air heater with black coated porous wire mesh and galvanized iron packed bed
Authors: Md. Maruf Tahmid, Md. Abdul Monem, Md. Mukul Uddin, Md. Sejan Hossain, Almostasim Mahmud, Mohammad Nurul Islam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study focused on developing a renewable energy-based thermal storage system using black porous wire mesh and galvanized iron (GI) packed bed, combined with multilayer porous insulation to reduce heat loss. The construction functioned as a Double Pass Solar Air Heater (DPSAH) utilizing solar energy to heat air. The system was optimized based on thermal, environmental and economic aspects, including energy, exergy, entropy, economic and environmental (5E) analyses. With three different air mass flow rates (0.021 kg/s, 0.028 kg/s, and 0.0323 kg/s), thermal assessment showed that a higher mass flow rate improved energy and exergy efficiency, whereas it reduced the temperature rise and entropy generation. The current design of the DPSAH system could mitigate CO2 by more than 2 kg per hour with the environmental cost around 3¢/hr at higher mass flow rates. Technoeconomic analysis indicated an energy payback period of less than 2 years and an exergy payback period of over 30 years, where the two payback periods varied inversely with changes in mass flow rate. The results from experimental and theoretical comprehensive assessment processes indicated that the design showed improved performance compared to the conventional SAH systems using simple wire mesh.

Introduction

Energy produced from conventional energy resources like fossil fuels has negative impacts on the environment, leading to global warming and climate change, primarily due to carbon dioxide emissions. Additionally, primary energy sources are diminishing day by day. Therefore, alternative renewable energy sources must be developed1 before conventional sources become extinct. Solar energy can be a promising solution to the problem as it is the most abundant, inexpensive, readily available, clean, and reliable renewable energy source2,3. According to the International Energy Agency, the consumption of solar energy is expected to increase by 45% over the next five years in various ways1. Most significantly, solar energy has the potential to be easily accessible since heat can be harvested by concentrating solar radiation onto a collector. The most common type of solar collector is solar air heaters (SAHs), which effectively capture both direct and diffuse radiation simultaneously. Additionally, SAH systems are known for several beneficial features such as their ability to be installed on top of buildings, easier maintenance, greater durability, lower production costs, and simpler construction. The SAH system is often used in space heating using hot air3, as well as in drying products for preservation, especially agricultural products and seafood4. However, the SAH system has low overall efficiency due to a low heat transfer rate between the flowing air and the heated absorber surface, which has led to many modifications of the SAH system5.

The study of thermodynamic performance is the main focus of all types of investigations in the literature on solar air heaters. The key parameters in thermodynamic studies are temperature rise and thermal efficiency. Most research concentrates on improving thermal performance by increasing turbulence in airflow systems, aiming to enhance convective heat transfer through greater contact between the fluid and the heated surface. Different modifications of solar air heaters were examined by Ravi et al.6, who reported that double-pass heaters are 10–15% more efficient than single-pass ones. Thermal performance was improved by increasing the flow rate up to a certain limit. Notably, they found that using porous absorbing material increased thermal performance by at least 25%. In a study by El-Khawajah et al.7, it was shown that the (Double Pass Solar Air Heater) DPSAH with porous wire mesh achieved higher thermal performance compared to a nonporous medium. Pramanik et al.8 achieved a 94 °C temperature rise and 69% thermal efficiency using longitudinal fins as turbulators on a coated aluminum absorber plate. Abdullah et al.9 used aluminum cans as turbulators and reflectors to concentrate solar heat, further improving the efficiency of the solar air heater. Solar intensity, ambient temperature, and mass flow rate significantly impacted DPSAH efficiency, ranging from 20% to 41%, as noted by Raj et al.10. Abo-Elfadl et al.11 indicated that aluminum tube absorbers (TSAH) were more efficient than flat plates. Salih et al.12 and Singh et al.13 confirmed that porous media and rough surfaces with ribs greatly increased thermal efficiency, although frictional losses also rose. Hassan et al.14 enhanced performance by incorporating transverse plates with lateral gaps and central holes, while El-Said et al.15 improved thermal efficiency through the use of corrugated plates and external reflectors. Singh et al.16 utilized the benefits of porous layers by employing wire mesh packed beds and turbulators, achieving around 80% thermo-hydraulic efficiency. Madhukeshwara et al.17 studied the performance of SAH systems with different surface coatings and concluded that black paint is the most absorptive and least reflective coating. The present study was mainly motivated by these previous works documented in the literature.

Exergy analysis is essential in the optimization of solar air heaters (SAHs) since practical irreversibility has adverse effects on the efficiency of the solar air heater. Velmurugan et al.18 found triple-pass SAH to have the highest thermal and exergy efficiencies. Also, mesh in the double-pass SAH demonstrated superior performance compared to the single-pass ones19. This statement was also supported by Tiwary et al.20, who achieved additional energy and exergy efficiencies of nearly 15% and 2%, respectively, with a double-ended system. Another study of Tiwary et al.21 reported that the introduction of Phase Change Material as thermal storage for SAH design improved the performance further in terms of thermal and environmental aspects. Hedayatizadeh et al.22 achieved a maximum exergy efficiency of 6.27%, with 63.67% exergy destruction of V-corrugated DPSAH. Fahmi et al.23 showed that adding fins improved both energy and exergy efficiencies with minimal deviation from previous results. Farhan et al.24 observed that metal foam fins showed better performance compared with solid fins, with corrugated foam fins being the most efficient. Kumar et al.25 achieved up to 62.8% energy efficiency and 5.85% exergy efficiency with zig-zag copper absorbers. V-shaped transverse fins improved exergy efficiency by 20%26. Prokash et al.27 noted that forced convection with a reflector gave the best results, supporting the use of finned surfaces for performance improvement. Hassan et al.28 found that a tubular SAH with a Nebla-shaped configuration outperformed conventional designs. Bhuvad et al.29 achieved 82.2% energy and 4.14% exergy efficiencies with apex-up discrete arc rib roughened SAH, despite increased pressure loss.

Recent studies on solar air heaters (SAH) that focus on entropy analysis are limited; however, they are crucial for assessing performance beyond energy and exergy metrics for design optimization. Naphon et al.30 observed that the entropy generation rate increased with mass flow rate, while the entropy number decreased due to improved energy performance, making mass flow rate less of a concern. Phu et al.32 found that in roughened SAH systems with conic curve ribs, entropy generation from heat transfer outweighed that from viscous dissipation, with the increased conic effect enhancing energy performance and reducing the entropy number. Another study by Phu et al.30 indicated that a third pass in triple-pass SAH systems significantly affected thermal performance and entropy generation more than a second pass. Nidhul et al.33 compared V-ribbed triangular ducts with smooth plates and found that ribbed surfaces achieved better performance with minimum entropy generation and maximum exergy at a 45° V angle.

Cost and environmental impacts are vital when evaluating SAH systems, leading to “4E” analyses: energy, exergy, economic, and ecological. Tuncer et al.34 identified V-grooved SAH as the most efficient, with enviro-economic costs ranging from $4.22 to $7.66 per year. Additionally, the use of aluminum fins enhanced the exergy performance by more than 6% and provided around 13% more CO2 mitigation per year, found from another study of Tuncer et al.35. Abo-Elfadl et al.36,37 showed that pin-finned and double-pass SAH systems achieved higher efficiencies, shorter payback periods, and better CO2 reduction compared to flat plate and single-pass systems. Hassan et al.38 found that perforated v-corrugated SAH improved economic performance by 24% over flat plate SAH. Hassan et al.39 reported an energy payback time of 0.78 years for V-grooved DPSAH, while Gurel et al.40 indicated it was 1.35 years with a zigzag finned plate. This study obtained an exergo-economic parameter of 0.1931 kWh/$, and the exergy payback time was 45.9 years. Carbon mitigation was calculated at 1.04 kg/h, with an environmental cost of 1.508 ¢/h. Hagde et al.41 examined cross-flow SAH, which benefits from a laminar sublayer during airflow. Results showed carbon mitigation of 1.12 tons annually, with an environmental cost of $16.31 and an energy cost of $0.049 per kWh. The energy and exergy payback periods were 5 months and 22 years, respectively. Fattoum et al.42 conducted a detailed study on energy, exergy, environmental, and economic parameters, noting that the use of mesh increased thermal efficiency but decreased exergy efficiency, with CO2 mitigation reaching 1370.21 tonnes per hour.

Based on the literature review, no studies have specifically explored the use of black-coated wire mesh combined with black galvanized iron as a packed bed featuring multilayer insulation in double-pass SAH systems. Additionally, a comprehensive analysis using 5Es, including energy, exergy, entropy, exergo-economic, and enviro-economic evaluations, enhances the appeal of this innovative SAH system development. Methodology

Experimental setup

In this study, a packed bed solar collector was built using a wooden box, glass cover, glass wool, collector sheets, and other components. This setup covered a basement area of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1575\:\times\:840\:$$\end{document}square millimeters. The basement of the wooden box was sturdy enough to support the weight of all the components placed on it. An insulating layer was created with multiple layers of materials over the wooden box to minimize heat loss. The first layer of insulation was composed of glass wool, which was placed on the wooden box of 1.27 cm thickness, followed by a foam layer of 1.27 cm thickness. Next, a 1.27 cm thick cork sheet layer was placed over it. The thermal conductivity of these materials is very low, resulting in comparatively much lower equivalent conductivity, incorporating the contact resistance. The high thermal resistance at the bottom of the collector would likely minimize the heat loss and increase the thermal transport to the flowing air. Hence, the insulation layer increased the effectiveness of the design. The thermal conductivities of glass wool, foam and cork sheet are 0.065 W/m− 1K− 1,0.03 W/m− 1K− 1, and 0.05 W/m− 1K− 1, respectively43, which indicates that the heat loss was negligible compared to the available energy. The packed bed collector was constructed with a 1 mm galvanized iron sheet serving as the absorbing plate and a black-coated wire mesh. The wire mesh layer measured 4.5 cm in thickness, 1.5 m in length, and 0.7874 m in width, positioned on top of the GI sheet, acting as a porous layer. Next, two transparent glass plates, each 3.5 mm thick, were installed with a 21 mm distance between them. The two-pass system was created with a U-shaped aluminum component that helps reduce heat loss to the surroundings. The sides of the collector bed were made from 25 mm-thick wood, providing additional insulation. Air inlet and outlet vents were constructed with two GI pipes, each 3.5 cm in diameter. A centrifugal blower was used in the inlet pipe to draw ambient air into the collector. The entire collector assembly was mounted on a frame made from MS iron angle bars for support. The working principle of a solar air heater is to absorb solar energy and convert it into heat, which is then transferred by airflow. In this experimental setup, when sunlight strikes the upper glass plate, it heats the absorber layer. Then the heat passes through the middle glass plate and is concentrated on the lower absorber plate, which is well insulated. Therefore, the upper pass is less heated than the lower pass in this double-pass SAH system. As shown in Fig. 2, the diagram of the DPSAH system, the setup is positioned at a fixed tilting angle to optimize sunlight exposure. Air is drawn in through the narrow inlet port by a blower, moving through the upper pass to absorb heat. The upper pass serves as a preheater for the air. After making a U-turn, the air flows over the absorber layer of the lower pass, transferring most of the heat during this process.

The mass flow rate is measured at the blower’s discharge point using an anemometer. The anemometer recorded the velocity, which is then used to manually calculate the mass flow rate based on air density and cross-sectional area. The air density is considered at ambient conditions.

Method of data collection

This experimental study on the DPSAH system was conducted from April 28 to May 10. Since most days were sunny, the climate of Rajshahi is favorable for solar energy devices. Rajshahi city is located at a latitude of 24.71060 N and a longitude of 88,94140 E. The entire study was carried out for three mass flow rates: 0.023 kg/s, 0.028 kg/s, and 0.0323 kg/s. Solar intensity and temperature are the key parameters in this SAH system investigation. Solar intensity was recorded every 30 min between 9:00 and 16:00 h, serving as the primary input parameter. The air inlet temperature was assumed to be at ambient temperature, while the output temperature was measured at the absorber exit point. It is important to note that data were collected under the least cloudy conditions.

In an experimental study, the measurement process introduces errors to the output values. To minimize the unusual errors, the thermocouples were primarily calibrated and a steady state condition was ensured during the measurement. However, it is typical to account for the error level of the measuring device, which depends on the sensitivity of the particular instrument. This consideration is important when the system optimization is carried out, as all the parameters, such as Energy and Exergy. This study involved four measuring devices with an acceptable accuracy level, as Table 2 shows that the individual uncertainty is not high. Method of data analysis

In this study, after collecting experimental data such as solar intensity, temperature increase, air flow rate, etc., a post-analysis is performed to assess the performance and effectiveness of the DPSAH system. During the evaluation process, the first law analysis measures heat transfer from the collector to the flowing air and calculates energy efficiency based on the temperature increase. Exergy analysis provides information on available useful energy and exergy efficiency. Entropy generation is examined through second law analysis, also known as entropy analysis, which focuses on determining the entropy number. The following sections are dedicated to techno-economic, exergo-economic, and enviro-economic analyses, where energy and exergy payback times, energy costs, exergo-economic parameters, carbon costs, and carbon mitigation are evaluated.

Economic analysis

Economic analysis is a crucial aspect of SAH systems, as they are considered alternatives to traditional electrical heating. This analysis examines costs related to the energy extraction process, cost recovery as a sustainable option, salvage value, and other factors to assess economic viability. To evaluate economic performance, several parameters must be considered, including the Annual Operating Cost (AOC)48, which covers all operating expenses each year, primarily caused by blower operation, depending on the pressure drop ΔP, operating time, and electricity cost CE. The Capital Recovery Factor (CRF)49, representing the rate at which initial costs are recovered over the system’s 20-year service life50, denoted by n, and an assumed interest rate of 10%51, is also included. The First Annual Cost (FAC), calculated from the Total Cost Invested (TCI) and Capital recovery factor (CRF), reflects initial investment. The Annual Salvage Value (ASV), derived from the Sinking Fund Factor (SFF) and Salvage Value (SV), accounts for residual value. Additionally, the Annual Maintenance Cost (AMC) is considered to be 10% of the first annual cost (FAC)52. Ultimately, the Annual Cost (AC) is the total cost that encompasses all elements, including initial investment, operating costs, maintenance costs, and salvage value.

Results & discussion

In this experimental investigation and subsequent theoretical study of a newly designed SAH system, a comprehensive evaluation process was conducted, covering thermodynamic, economic, and environmental aspects. Solar energy availability throughout the days and the corresponding temperature rises at three different flow rates (0.021 kg/s, 0.028 kg/s, and 0.0323 kg/s) were measured during the experiment, along with considerations of ambient temperature and humidity of air. The air flow system used in the experiment was multi-speed blower. From the available air flow speed measured with the speed meter and the fixed cross section of the flow inlet, the mass flow rates were obtained. The flow rates were also in good agreement with the range used in the literature16. Based on the experimental data, performance parameters were assessed in terms of energy absorbed, thermal efficiency, second law efficiency or exergy efficiency, entropy, and an economic and environmental analysis was performed to reach a conclusive decision. All parameters were evaluated at three mass flow rates over 7 h (9:00 to 16:00) on different sunny days in April and May.

Solar intensity and temperature gain

Solar radiation from the sun, measured in terms of solar intensity, is the primary source of solar energy. Therefore, in this solar energy study, it was essential to measure the available energy from the sun. Figure 3(a) shows the solar intensity distribution over 7 h, from 9:00 AM to 4:00 PM, for ten different sunny days. The solar intensity ranged from 970 W/m² to 990 W/m² in the morning, i.e., at 9:00 AM, and then increased over time, with a reasonable fluctuation range (1.5% to 2.5%), as solar intensity on sunny days tends to fluctuate less, forming a narrow band shape over time. However, the rate of increase varied day to day, influenced by factors such as clouds and sun angle, making it unpredictable. The maximum solar intensity variation across different days was slightly larger, typically occurring when the sun was overhead around 12:30 PM, with negligible cloud influence and a closer to maximum view factor for solar radiation. Most days showed maximum intensity within a band of 1130 to 1140 W/m², though only two days recorded peaks beyond 1150 W/m². After midday, solar intensity declined at a similar rate, but in the opposite direction, as the sun provided less energy due to a reduced view angle. Consequently, the solar intensity graph followed a pattern of rising and falling over the course of the day. The study indicates that available solar energy did not fluctuate significantly from day to day and followed a consistent pattern, aiding in the more effective evaluation of the SAH performance.

In this experimental study, air at ambient temperature was passed through the packed bed twice to be heated by solar energy converged on the solar collector. At any specific flow rate, the temperature rise, ΔT, of the airflow indicates the amount of energy transferred to the air and the energy collected by the SAH system from the sun. From Fig. 3(b), which shows the temperature increase of the flowing air based on the experimental results, it is clear that the temperature rise followed an increasing-to-decreasing pattern, similar to the solar intensity distribution throughout the day. It is important to note that the temperature rise decreased with higher mass flow rates of air. This is because of the reduced time available for heat transfer from the bed surface to the moving air. For a flow rate of 0.021 kg/s, the temperature rise was about 40 °C at 9 AM, reaching a maximum of around 50 °C at midday. After noon, the temperature rise declined again, dropping to nearly 40 °C by 4 PM. When the mass flow rate was increased to 0.028 kg/s, an additional 0.007 kg/s, the temperature rise decreased by nearly 3 °C, regardless of the time of day. With an extra flow rate of 0.0043 kg/s, making the total flow rate 0.0323 kg/s, the temperature rise in the morning was approximately 34 °C, about 3 °C less than at 0.028 kg/s. This difference in temperature rise slightly lessened until midday, then increased again to 3 °C in the afternoon. From this analysis, it can be concluded that even with a smaller increase in flow rate, the decrease in temperature rise was comparable to that seen at higher flow rates.

Optimization through second law analysis

Second law analysis is essential for optimizing the SAH system, balancing energy gain and energy loss due to irreversibility. The quality of energy can be assessed through the useful energy parameter, exergy, entropy and the irreversibility parameter. The second law efficiency, or exergy efficiency, differs from energy efficiency in terms of energy format because it measures the ratio of useful energy output to input; thus, it reflects the actual energy conditions based on availability. From Fig. 5(a), it is clear that the trend of the exergy efficiency curve is the same as the energy efficiency curve. It increases to a peak at midday and then drops back close to the initial value. However, exergy efficiency values are quite low, typically between 2 and 4%, which is common for a SAH system. Notably, unlike thermal energy efficiency, exergy efficiency decreases with increasing mass flow rate. This indicates that, although increasing the mass flow rate may seem to enhance energy performance, it could actually reduce useful output energy because higher irreversibility occurs at higher flow rates. Therefore, a higher exergy efficiency signifies a more effective system. This study shows that the exergy efficiency performance was improved and satisfactory compared to other SAH systems. As the data were recorded during very hot summer days, the sun radiation was open to incident on the surface with view factor close to unity. However, the design was not capable of capturing all the available incident energy, which resulted in comparatively lower exergy efficiencies. Improvement of the absorbance layer can further enhance the exergy performance. Meanwhile, the entropy number, or entropy generation number, is a dimensionless measure representing the ratio of entropy generation and the energy received from the source by the SAH system. A higher entropy number indicates poorer performance, indicating higher energy loss relative to energy gain, and vice versa. Figure 5(b) illustrates how the entropy number varies with local time for three different mass flow rates. Throughout the study, the entropy number remained below one; it reached a minimum at midday when thermal and exergy efficiencies peaked, then increased again as time progressed. Interestingly, a higher mass flow rate resulted in a lower entropy number, even within a variation of 0.1%. This suggests that, despite a possible increase in entropy due to greater irreversibility with the increasing energy transfer at higher mass flow rates, it does not negate the improved heat transfer resulting in the reduction of the entropy-to-energy ratio, known as the entropy number. That means even if the entropy increases, the entropy number does not increase in the current condition due to dominating heat transfer enhancement, which is beneficial for the system optimization.

Conclusion

In this study, energy, exergy, entropy, exergo-economic, and enviro-economic analyses were conducted to evaluate the performance of the double pass solar air heater (DPSAH) with a black-coated porous mesh packed bed, based on experimental data such as solar intensity and airflow temperature increase. The average solar intensity measured on April 15 was 1065 W/m², and the daily variation did not exceed 3% during the observation period. From the first law analysis, it was observed that a higher rate of heat transfer was achieved with this design. The average temperature rise exceeded 40 degrees, with average thermal efficiency and second law efficiency surpassing 65% and 2.88%, respectively, particularly at peak midday conditions. The exergo-economic parameter Rg, ex averaged 14.5 kWh/$, while the energy cost ranged from 0.0035 to 0.0026 $/kWh with increasing mass flow rate, according to economic analysis. Most importantly, as a sustainable energy storage device, this construction can reduce CO2 generation by more than 1.5 kg/hr for every mass flow rate considered, which is a remarkable achievement over previous design. Moreover, the environmental cost was 2.7 to 3.7 ¢/hr. Finally, the technoeconomic parameters, Energy and exergy back pay-back time, were determined to be 1.39 to 1.9 and 32.54 to 41.39 years, respectively. Thus, this new packed bed construction with comprehensive evaluation helped to develop a well-performed thermal storage system.
