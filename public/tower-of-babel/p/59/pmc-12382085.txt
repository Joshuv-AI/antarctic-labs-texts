Radical surgery versus definitive radiotherapy in treatment of locoregional upper esophageal squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Huan Zhang, Bowen Zhang, Qi Jiang, Bingjie Wu, Liyang Zhu, Mei Kang, Ying Dai, Shiyu Wang, Wanli Xia, Ningning Kang, Yu Chen, Yichun Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundsCurrent consensus guidelines lack robust evidence to advocate the optimal treatment approach for locoregional upper esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (L-UESCC). The purpose of this study was to conduct an in-depth investigation of the treatment patterns, outcomes, and prognostic factors among patients with L-UESCC.MethodsConsecutive patients diagnosed with L-UESCC who underwent curative-intent treatment at two cancer centers from January 2013 to December 2018 were enrolled in this study. To compare overall survival (OS) and conduct prognostic analysis, we employed the Kaplan-Meier method, Cox regression models, and propensity score matching (PSM).ResultsIn the entire cohort, 247 patients (64.2%) received definitive radiotherapy, whereas 138 patients (35.8%) underwent radical surgery. Multivariable Cox analysis revealed that T stage, N stage, and tumor length were independent predictors of OS. The Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated comparable median OS between the surgical and radiotherapy groups both before and after PSM adjustment. Before PSM, the median OS was 27.0 months in the surgical group and 29.0 months in the radiotherapy group (P = 0.48); after PSM, it was 23.0 and 30.0 months respectively (P = 0.38). Notably, the combination of chemotherapy with radiotherapy was associated with a significant survival benefit over radiotherapy monotherapy (median OS 36.0 versus 20.0 months, P = 0.005). In chemotherapy-naïve subgroups, surgery conferred a significant survival advantage over radiotherapy (P = 0.016), particularly in stage I-II (P = 0.003), but not in stage III (P = 0.54).ConclusionsIn patients with L-UESCC, surgical resection and definitive radiotherapy yielded similar OS results. Introduction

Esophageal carcinoma (EC) ranked eleventh globally in cancer incidence and seventh in cancer mortality during 2022, with epidemiological estimates indicating 510,000 new diagnoses and 445,000 deaths [1]. Histologically, the two predominant subtypes are adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). Distinct biologically from adenocarcinoma, esophageal SCC (ESCC) predominates in the upper/middle esophagus and shares molecular profiles with other SCCs [2]. Upper esophageal cancers (ECs), including those affecting the cervical and upper thoracic regions, account for a smaller proportion compared to middle/lower ECs [3, 4]. The intricate anatomical constraints of the cervico-upper mediastinal region, characterized by proximity to vital neurovascular structures and complex lymphatic drainage pattens, pose significant therapeutic challenges of upper EC [5].

The controversies on management of the upper ESCC (UESCC) have existed for many decades. Current clinical practice acknowledges both surgical resection and radiotherapy as potentially curative options for locoregional diseases. Notably, many clinical practice guidelines stratify management approaches based on primary anatomical subsites of tumor: definitive radiotherapy (with or without chemotherapy) is advocated for non-metastasis cervical ESCC (CESCC), whereas resectable upper thoracic ESCC (UTESCC) cases are recommended to undergo esophagectomy with lymphadenectomy, often combined with neoadjuvant/adjuvant therapies. Nevertheless, these recommendations are currently supported by limited high-level evidence, primarily due to three key factors: firstly, clinical decision-making predominantly relies on evidence from retrospective cohort studies; secondly, the small sample sizes hinder the conduct of adequately powered clinical trials, particularly those involving meaningful subgroup analyses [6, 7]; and thirdly, there is a lack of prospective, randomized comparisons between surgical and non-surgical treatment approaches.

Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of curative-intent treatment for non-metastatic EC. Retrospective analyses have suggested superior overall survival (OS) for upper EC patients undergoing radical esophagectomy compared to radiotherapy [8, 9]. However, significant selection biases persist in these observational studies, where surgical cohorts predominantly comprise younger patients, earlier stages, and more upper thoracic tumors. Additionally, the technical complexity of proximal esophageal resections carries substantial morbidity risks and poor quality of life. These perioperative challenges contribute to non-surgical approaches like radiotherapy and definitive chemoradiotherapy being employed in the majority of patients with non-metastatic upper EC [8–10]. Emerging therapeutic advancements have the potential to redefine the treatment approach. Minimally invasive techniques and novel precision radiotherapy technologies have shown promising outcomes, including improved survival rates and reduced complication incidences [11, 12]. Therefore, critical knowledge gaps persist regarding the optimal treatment modality selection, necessitating large-scale data analysis studies to establish robust evidence for informed decision-making of UESCC.

The objective of this study was to investigate the treatment patterns and outcomes of locoregional UESCC (L-UESCC). Specifically, it entailed the following aspects: (1) an analysis of trends in treatment modalities; (2) a comparative analysis of the oncological outcomes between radical surgery and definitive radiotherapy; and (3) the utilization of Cox regression analysis to identify clinicopathological predictors for treatment selection. By integrating real-world data, this study has the potential to provide valuable insights for developing an evidence-based decision framework for management of UESCC patients.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective cohort study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (Approval No. PJ 2025-01-50), with informed consent waiver granted under the Declaration of Helsinki for retrospective analyses of anonymized clinical data. Between January 2013 and December 2018, 688 non-metastatic upper EC patients were identified through our institutional cancer registry and the Anhui Provincial Chest Hospital. Anatomically, upper EC is rigorously defined as a tumor located between the cricopharyngeus and the azygos vein arch, which is divided into cervical EC and upper thoracic EC by the thoracic inlet. Location is defined by the position of the epicenter of the tumor in the esophagus.

Endoscopy with biopsy and histologic examination was performed before treatments. All patients should perform barium esophagography and computed tomography (CT) of the neck and chest. CT scan of the abdomen was not mandatory if there was abdominal ultrasound without signs of metastasis. For inclusion in our analysis, patients should meet the following criteria: (1) histologically confirmed SCC; (2) accessible baseline CT images before treatment; (3) locoregional disease (M0) and those with supraclavicular lymph node metastasis (SCLNM); (4) curative-intent treatment strategies; (5) adequate renal, hepatic, and bone marrow function; and (6) complete follow-up records. Patients were excluded if they had secondary malignancies; middle/lower EC; distant metastasis (M1) except SCLNM; palliative interventions; non-SCC histology; missing key parameters (comorbidity profiles, standardized physical assessments, and therapeutic modality specifications detailing radiation dosimetry, chemotherapeutic regimens and surgical approaches) or follow-up records; and survival less than one month.

Staging

The eighth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control/American Joint Committee on Cancer (UICC/AJCC) staging criteria was applied for TNM classification of ESCC. For non-surgical patients, the clinical T and N staging was generally determined through CT, positron emission tomography/CT (PET/CT), endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS) or their combination. Due to limitations in CT imaging resolution for differentiating early T-stage lesions, clinical T stages were categorized as T1-2 (grouped), T3, and T4. For clinical N staging, the diagnostic criteria for lymph node (LN) metastasis included: (1) LNs demonstrating a short-axis diameter ≥ 10 mm (≥ 5 mm for nodes in the tracheoesophageal groove); (2) LNs exhibiting morphological abnormalities (e.g., fusion, necrosis) or interval enlargement on serial imaging; and (3) metabolic activity with maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) ≥ 2.4 on PET/CT. SCLNM was classified as M1 according to current staging guidelines. All staging assessments were conducted by two independent investigators (H.Z. and B.W.Z.), with discordant cases adjudicated through consensus review by Y.C.W.

Treatments

The standard surgical protocol comprised en bloc tumor resection with systematic lymphadenectomy. Surgical approaches included open esophagectomy, minimally invasive esophagectomy, or hybrid approaches combining both techniques. All patients underwent radiotherapy delivered via three-dimensional conformal radiation therapy (3D-CRT) or intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT). Radiation fields encompassed either elective nodal basins or involved-field irradiation, with definitive radiotherapy requiring a total dose of ≥ 50 Gy (for those undergoing chemotherapy) or ≥ 60 Gy (for those not receiving chemotherapy), delivered in 1.8–2.0 Gy per fraction. The systemic chemotherapeutic agents include taxanes, platinum agents, and fluoropyrimidines, primarily administered in two-drug combination regimens. The treatment protocols for patients in this study were determined by the discretion of medical surgical specialists, radiation oncologists, and oncologists. These protocols are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Based on therapeutic modalities, patients were stratified into two groups. The surgical group (S group) included patients who underwent surgery alone (SA), surgery followed by adjuvant chemotherapy (S + CT), surgery followed by adjuvant radiotherapy (S + RT), or surgery followed by both adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy (S + CRT). The radiotherapy group (R group) consisted of patients who received radiotherapy alone (RT) or chemoradiotherapy (CRT), which was administered either concurrently or sequentially.

Data collection and surveillance

Comprehensive clinicopathological data were retrospectively extracted from institutional medical archives, encompassing demographic parameters (age, sex), tumor clinicopathological profiles (primary anatomic location, longitudinal dimension, and TNM staging), therapeutic interventions (surgical approach, systemic therapy, and radiation parameters), comorbidity profiles, and follow-up outcomes.

Patients were followed-up every 3 months for the first 2 years; every 6 months for years 3 to 5; and annually thereafter. The prespecified study termination date was December 31, 2024, with all outcome measures censored at this protocol-defined cutoff. The primary endpoint was overall survival (OS), which was calculated from the start of diagnosis to the date of death or censoring at the last confirmed follow-up. Death information were obtained from medical records, telephone interviews, or the central registry of Chinese Bureau of Population Statistics.

Statical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (v27.0.1; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and R (v4.4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Categorical variables were analyzed with Pearson’s χ² test or Fisher’s exact test (applied for expected cell counts < 5). Continuous variables underwent Student’s t-test for normally distributed data or Mann-Whitney U test for non-parametric distributions. Survival curves were generated using Kaplan-Meier methodology with the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression modeling was employed to evaluate prognostic factors. Variables demonstrating significant association (P < 0.05) in univariate analysis were incorporated into multivariate models. A significance level of two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To address treatment selection bias, propensity score matching (PSM) was implemented to compare the survival between the S group and the R group. The balanced clinicopathological variables consist of age, gender (male/female), tumor location (cervical/upper thoracic), tumor length, T category (T1-2/T3/T4), N category (N0/N1/N2-3), chemotherapy administration (yes/no), comorbidity burden (yes/no), and SCLNM status (yes/no). The matching protocol was executed through the MatchIt package in R software. One-to-one nearest-neighbor matching without replacement was performed on the logit of the propensity score. Caliper restriction set at 0.2 times of the standard deviation of the logit propensity score was used. A standardized mean difference (SMD) of less than 0.2 for all covariates after matching suggested a good performance of the propensity score.

Results

Patient characteristics

Following rigorous eligibility screening, a total of 385 UESCC patients were enrolled in this study. The median age was 67 years (range 40–89) and the median tumor length was 3.5 cm (range 0.6–11.0). In terms of patient characteristics, the majority of the cohort were male patients, accounting for 68.3% (n = 263). Moreover, 82.3% (n = 317) of the patients had UTESCC. When stratified according to the AJCC Cancer Staging Manual (8th edition), the distribution of the disease stages was as follows: stage I - II patients constituted 59.7% (n = 230), stage III patients made up 29.6% (n = 114), and stage IV patients accounted for 10.6% (n = 41). SCLNM was detected in 6.0% (n = 23) of all the patients. Comorbidities were present in 14.5% (n = 56) of the cohort, and 61.8% (n = 238) of the patients received chemotherapy. Notably, significant differences were observed between the S group and the R group in terms of age distribution, tumor location, T stage, N stage, TNM stage tumor length distribution, chemotherapy administration, comorbidity burden, and SCLNM status (P < 0.05, Table 1). After PSM, 160 patients (80 per group) achieved optimal intergroup balance, with SMD < 0.2 for all baseline covariates. Post-matching analysis confirmed homogeneity between the S group and the R group (P > 0.05, Table 1).

Treatment patterns

Definitive radiotherapy with or without chemotherapy (RT and CRT) was administered to 64.2% (247/385) patients. CESCC patients demonstrated a 19.1% (21/110) surgical resection rate, whereas UTESCC cases exhibited a 39.4% (125/317) operative rate. The therapeutic decision-making trends, incorporating tumor location, TNM staging, age stratification, and tumor length, are comprehensively illustrated in Figure 1.

Treatment modality selection patterns. CESCC, cervical esophageal squamous cell cancer; UTESCC, upper thoracic esophageal squamous cell cancer; SA, surgery alone; S + CT surgery with adjuvant chemotherapy; S + RT, surgery with adjuvant radiotherapy, S + CRT, surgery with both adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy; RT, definitive radiotherapy; CRT, definitive radiotherapy and chemotherapy

Surgical intervention was predominantly employed for patients with stage I–III diseases, accounting for 97.8% (135/138), while it was rarely applied in stage IV cases, making up only 2.2% (3/138). Age-stratified analysis revealed a clear downward trend in the utilization of surgery as age advanced. Among patients ≤ 70 years, the surgical utilization rate was 45.8% (120/262). This rate dropped to 18.0% (18/100) for those at 71–80 years, and 0% (0/23) in octogenarians. There was also an inverse relationship between tumor length and the likelihood of surgical intervention. For lesions ≤3.0 cm, the resection rate was 45.9% (73/159). In contrast, the resection rate was 30.4% (51/168) in 3.1-5.0cm lesions and 24.1% (14/58) in >5.0 cm lesions. As a result, radiotherapy was more frequently selected as the treatment option for larger tumors.

In the S group (n=118), treatment distribution was as follows: SA in 44.9% (n=53), S + RT in 18.1% (n=21), S + CT in 16.7% (n=20), and S + CRT in 20.3% (n=24). Patients receiving adjuvant therapy generally presented with more advanced disease stages compared to the SA (Supplementary Table S2). Conversely, the R group (n=247) showed a higher frequency of chemotherapy administration among patients with younger age (Supplementary Table S3).

Survival

Treatment outcomes and prognostic factors

For the entire cohort, the median OS was 28.0 months (95% CI: 22.3–33.7), with 3-year, 5-year, and 10-year OS rates of 43.9% (39.0%–49.4%), 34.2% (29.5%–39.6%), and 19.0% (14.7%–24.5%), respectively.

Median OS for patients receiving RT, CRT, SA, S+CT, S+RT, and S+CRT was 20.0 (15.2–24.8) months, 36.0 (25.5–46.5) months, 35.0 (0.5–69.5) months, 29.0 (2.9–55.1) months, 20.0 (13.2–26.8) months, and 25.0 (15.5–34.5) months, respectively. In the S group (Figure 2A), postoperative adjuvant therapies did not improve OS compared to SA. In the R group, CRT demonstrated superior survival over RT in patients with UESCC (Figure 2B). No significant survival difference was observed between patients treated with SA and CRT (P = 0.388). The P-value for pairwise comparisons of survival outcomes between different treatment regimens were shown in Supplementary Table S4.

Univariable Cox regression analysis identified T stage, N stage, tumor length, and SCLM status as significant risk factors for OS (all P < 0.05, Figure 3). However, in the multivariable analysis, only T stage, N stage (N2-3 vs. N0), and tumor length (≥5.0 cm vs. ≤3.0 cm) remained independent prognostic factors for OS (all P< 0.05, Figure 3).

Treatment efficacy of surgery versus radiotherapy

The median OS was comparable between the S group and the R group (27.0 months, 95% CI: 21.2–32.8 vs. 29.0 months, 95% CI: 21.7–36.3; P = 0.48). The 3-year OS rates were 42.4% (95% CI: 34.6–52.0%) for the S group and 44.7% (95% CI: 38.6–51.7%) for the R group, while the 5-year OS rates were 37.4% (95% CI: 29.8–47.0%) and 32.3% (95% CI: 26.6–39.1%), respectively. At 10 years, OS rates further declined to 21.4% (95% CI: 14.7–31.3%) in the S group and 16.9% (95% CI: 11.5–24.7%) in the R group. No statistically significant difference in OS was observed between the two groups (HR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.71–1.18; P = 0.48) (Figure 4A).

OS difference between the S group and the R group. PSM, propensity score matching; R, the R group; S, the S group

After PSM, 80 patients were successfully matched in each group. The median OS was 23.0 months (95% CI: 17.6–28.4) in the S group and 30.0 months (95% CI: 16.4–43.6) in the R group. The 3-year OS rates were 35.9% (95% CI: 26.2%–49.0%) and 45.0% (95% CI: 34.9%–58.0%) for the S group and the R group, respectively. The 5-year OS rates stood at 28.4% (95% CI: 19.5%–41.2%) in the S group and 36.3% (95% CI: 26.7%–49.3%) in the R group. At the 10-year mark, the OS rates were 15.8% (95% CI: 9.0%–27.7%) and 21.3% (95% CI: 13.3%–34.3%) for the S group and the R group, correspondingly. Although patients in the R group tended to have better survival compared to those in the S group, there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups (HR= 1.18, 95% CI: 0.82 - 1.70; P = 0.38, Figure 4B).

Subgroup stratification analysis assessed the efficacy of radical surgery versus definitive radiotherapy across different patient characteristics (Figure 5). No significant differences were observed between the two treatment modalities in terms of age, gender, tumor location, T stage, N stage, TNM stage, tumor length, comorbidity status, or negative SCLNM (P >0.05). However, patients who did not receive chemotherapy exhibited a significant survival benefit with radical surgery (P = 0.016), particularly in stage I-II (Figure 6A), but not in stage III (Figure 6B).

Discussion

UESCC is associated with poor survival outcomes, with reported 5-year OS rates of approximately 30% for patients undergoing curative therapy [8, 9, 13, 14] and 34.2% in the present study. Although surgery remains the gold standard for curative treatment in many malignancies, proximal esophageal resection poses unique challenges, including achieving adequate margins, complete lymph node dissection, and complex gastrointestinal reconstruction due to the intricate surrounding anatomy. Additionally, the high perioperative morbidity and mortality associated with esophagectomy further limit its widespread use. Consequently, the majority of UESCC patients (64.2% in this study) receive definitive radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy, aligning with findings from prior research [8–10].

Due to the low incidence of UESCC and the absence of large-scale randomized controlled trials, no consensus exists regarding the optimal local treatment approach. Previous retrospective studies comparing surgery and radiotherapy have shown comparable outcomes [8, 15]. Our study further revealed that surgical candidates were typically younger, had earlier-stage disease, shorter tumor lengths, more upper thoracic involvement, and better baseline performance status (fewer comorbidities). To minimize selection bias, we compared treatment efficacy using PSM. Results demonstrated no significant survival differences between surgery and radiotherapy before or after PSM, as well as in Cox regression analyses. Similarly, a National Cancer Database study reported no significant difference in 5-year OS between chemoradiation (34.0%, 95% CI 27.6–42.0%) and esophagectomy (41.2%, 95% CI 34.6–49.1%, p = 0.224) after PSM [9]. Additionally, Cox regression analyses confirmed no survival benefit for surgery in either CESCC (HR= 1.19, 95% CI 0.59–2.40) or UTESCC (HR = 0.88, 95% CI 0.67–1.16).

Radiotherapy offers advantages such as lower mortality, reduced morbidity, and organ preservation. However, both our study and prior retrospective analyses have demonstrated that radiotherapy alone yields poor survival outcomes [8, 16, 17]. Chemotherapy not only targets micrometastases but also serves as a radiosensitizer, enhancing radiotherapy efficacy. The landmark RTOG 85-01 trial established that CRT significantly improves overall survival compared to radiotherapy alone in locally advanced EC [18]. Subsequent studies further support CRT as a standard treatment for unresectable EC [17, 19]. The present study also found that adding chemotherapy to radiotherapy significantly improved survival. Nevertheless, the role of chemotherapy in early-stage UESCC remains uncertain and warrants further investigation [8].

Although chemoradiotherapy has been widely employed in the treatment of UESCC, its survival benefits have not shown significant improvement over the past decades. A substantial proportion of patients experience recurrence during follow-up, predominantly locoregional [13, 16, 20]. Given these findings, surgical intervention remains a viable therapeutic option, particularly for patients with chemo-radioresistant disease phenotypes. For early-stage UESCC, surgery has demonstrated superior survival outcomes compared to non-surgical treatments [8]. In the present study, surgery alone also yielded better survival than radiotherapy alone in stage I-II patients who did not receive chemotherapy. Early-stage UESCCs exhibit a lower propensity for regional lymph node or distant metastasis. This characteristic underscores the importance of definitive local treatment, particularly surgery, to address the primary lesion and prevent recurrence. Moreover, surgery is recommended for patients who do not achieve a complete response or experience recurrence following chemoradiotherapy [21]. Therefore, accurate assessment of treatment efficacy, prediction of therapeutic response, and rigorous follow-up are critical for patients undergoing radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy.

Local recurrence remains a predominant failure pattern in UESCC, regardless of whether surgery or radiotherapy is employed, particularly in locally advanced disease. Neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy followed by surgery has shown promising results for locally advanced thoracic EC [6, 7], but the role of adjuvant therapy after upfront surgery remains controversial. In selected patients undergoing upfront surgery, adjuvant therapies have been shown to improve survival, as demonstrated by prior studies [22]. The present study found no significant survival benefit with postoperative therapies compared to surgery alone. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously due to significant differences in patient selection. Surgery alone was mainly used for early-stage disease, while adjuvant therapy was more commonly used for locally advanced cases. In our study, adjuvant therapy was generally recommended for stage II–IV patients (T3–4 or node-positive). These findings also highlight the need for well-designed clinical trials to clarify the role of adjuvant treatments in UESCC. Moreover, recent progress in immune checkpoint inhibitors has demonstrated promising efficacy in EC [23], indicating their potential integration into multimodal treatment strategies for UESCC, which warrants further investigation.

The retrospective design of this study had inherent limitations. Selection bias in treatment choice was inevitable in clinical practice. Clinical staging relied primarily on CT imaging of esophageal lesions, but distinguishing T stages, particularly between T1 and T2, was challenging. This limitation precluded separate analyses for T1 and T2 UESCC patients, potentially affecting our understanding of early-stage prognosis. Additionally, CT's limited accuracy in lymph node assessment may have led to discrepancies between clinical and pathological staging, which could influence treatment decisions and survival predictions. Furthermore, the absence of strict guidelines for radiation dosimetry, chemotherapy regimens, and surgical approaches introduced potential bias. Lastly, while SCLNM demonstrates similar survival rates to regional lymph node metastasis in UESCC [24], its classification as M1 disease in the AJCC 8th edition may cause inconsistencies with other studies. Despite the previously mentioned limitations, the study has several notable strengths. First, it was a large-sample study in two high-volume centers with long-term follow-up, enhancing the reliability and generalizability of our findings. Second, we conducted a thorough analysis of real-world treatment selection patterns, which further enriched our insights into clinical practice. Third, analyzing both CESCC and UTESCC provided a more comprehensive understanding of the disease spectrum. Moreover, we used PSM analysis and stratified subgroup Cox regression analyses to compare survival outcomes, thereby providing a more comprehensive and precise evaluation of treatment effects across different subgroups.

Conclusions

In the current study, we found that definitive radiotherapy-based therapies remained the most commonly adopted treatment for patients with L-UESCC, particularly those with locally advanced disease and CESCC. Radical surgery yielded comparable survival outcomes to definitive radiotherapy. Notably, the addition of chemotherapy could improve the survival rates in radiotherapy-treated patients. Moreover, in early stage (I-II) patients without chemotherapy, radical surgery conferred a significant survival advantage over radiotherapy. Given these findings, personalized treatment decisions should be made after a thorough evaluation of each patient's condition. Further research is needed to develop more effective treatment strategies to improve survival outcomes for these patients.
