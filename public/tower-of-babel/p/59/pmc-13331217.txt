Construction of an ultrasound feature-based diagnostic model for predicting triple-negative breast cancer using 108 machine learning algorithm combinations
Authors: Xin Zhang, Xiaomin Zhang, Shan Zhu, Ningxia Ao, Yali Cao, Guangfei Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is an aggressive subtype of breast cancer lacking estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, and human epidermal growth factor R expression. This study aimed to identify ultrasound features associated with TNBC and construct a robust diagnostic model using machine learning techniques.Material and Methods:A total of 433 BC patients (47 TNBC and 386 non-TNBC) with complete clinical and ultrasound data were retrospectively analyzed. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses identified independent ultrasound predictors of TNBC. Subsequently, 108 combinations of feature selection methods and machine learning classifiers were systematically evaluated to identify the optimal diagnostic model. Model performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic curves, decision curve analysis (DCA), and calibration plots in both training and validation cohorts. The ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board, and the requirement for individual written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.Results:Multivariate analysis identified posterior acho, margins, calcification, and aspect ratio as independent predictors of TNBC (p < 0.05). Among all model combinations, the Stepwise Generalized Linear Model combined with Random Forest model achieved the best performance, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 1.000 in the training set and 0.913 in the validation set. Subgroup analysis revealed higher model accuracy in patients aged ≤50 years (AUC = 0.911) compared to those >50 years (AUC = 0.706). DCA and calibration plots indicated high clinical utility and excellent calibration performance.Conclusion:Specific ultrasound features can effectively distinguish TNBC from non-TNBC. A machine learning-based diagnostic model that integrates multiple algorithmic combinations demonstrates strong generalizability and may serve as a valuable clinical tool, particularly for younger patients.

INTRODUCTION

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is a subtype of breast cancer (BC) characterized by the absence of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression, accounting for approximately 15–20% of all BC cases.[1] TNBC is more commonly observed in young women, individuals of African descent, and carriers of BRCA1 mutations. It exhibits highly aggressive clinical behavior and a high risk of recurrence, typically presenting with high histological grade, frequent lymphovascular invasion, and early distant metastasis.[2-4]

Due to the pronounced heterogeneity of TNBC and the lack of hormone receptor and HER2 targets, present treatment strategies mainly rely on a combination of chemotherapy, surgery, and radiotherapy.[5,6] TNBC is often diagnosed at an advanced stage, contributing to a poorer prognosis compared to other BC subtypes. The 5-year survival rate for TNBC is approximately 65%.[7] Although initial treatment responses may be favorable, the lack of effective non-invasive diagnostic tools and challenges in patient screening result in many TNBC patients already presenting with metastasis at diagnosis.[8] Therefore, early and accurate identification, along with risk assessment, is critical for optimizing treatment decisions and improving patient survival.

Breast ultrasound is a widely used diagnostic tool in BC evaluation due to its advantages of being non-invasive, free of ionizing radiation, real-time, user-friendly, and cost-effective.[9] Particularly in individuals with dense breast tissue, ultrasound effectively compensates for the limitations of mammography, significantly improving the detection rate of early-stage BC.[10,11] Beyond merely detecting and localizing breast masses, ultrasound provides multidimensional structural and functional information regarding lesion morphology, margins, internal echogenicity, posterior acoustic features, calcifications, and vascular signals. These imaging characteristics have been closely linked to the biological behavior of various molecular subtypes of BC.[12] Therefore, breast ultrasound plays an important role in pre-operative assessment and clinical decision-making. However, ultrasound interpretation is highly dependent on radiologists’ subjective judgment, which introduces variability and limits diagnostic consistency and accuracy, posing challenges to the advancement of precision medicine.[13]

In recent years, the emergence of artificial intelligence has brought new perspectives and technical platforms to ultrasound-based diagnosis. In addition to conventional ultrasound, advanced ultrasound modalities, such as elastography (which assesses tissue stiffness) and ultrasound tomography (enabling three-dimensional structural reconstruction) have shown promising prospects in BC subtype differentiation. However, their application in TNBC-specific prediction remains underdeveloped, and conventional ultrasound still serves as the primary imaging basis for clinical screening and evaluation due to its widespread accessibility and standardized operation.[14,15] Machine learning algorithms, capable of handling high-dimensional and unstructured data, can identify the most predictive features for specific diseases from large sets of ultrasound variables and construct generalizable classification models. While previous studies have explored the application of machine learning in breast ultrasound diagnosis,[14-16] most have focused on limited combinations of feature selection methods and classifiers, lacking comprehensive comparisons of algorithmic performance and clinical utility across diverse modeling schemes. There is still a lack of systematic evaluation of feature selection methods and classifier combinations specifically for TNBC prediction. Comparative analyses of algorithm performance and their clinical utility remain limited. Therefore, developing more generalizable, interpretable, and accurate ultrasound-based diagnostic models is essential for the early identification of TNBC and for informing individualized treatment planning.

Based on this background, the present study aimed to develop a robust and generalizable predictive model for TNBC using ultrasound-derived features. We first employed univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses to identify key predictive variables. Subsequently, we systematically evaluated 108 integrated algorithmic combinations of feature selection methods and classification algorithms, training and validating the models across both the training and testing sets. This study not only identified ultrasound features closely associated with TNBC but also proposed a modeling strategy that integrates the strengths of various machine learning algorithms, thus addressing the research gap of insufficient comprehensive algorithmic comparison in existing studies.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study population

This study included a total of 552 patients diagnosed with BC who received treatment at the hospital between January 2020 and February 2024. All patients underwent ultrasound examination at the time of admission and were subsequently confirmed to have BC by histopathological analysis. To clarify the cohort definition and minimize potential selection bias, the following strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were explicitly defined: Inclusion criteria: (1) Pathologically confirmed primary BC after surgical resection; (2) Complete pre-operative ultrasound examination data with assessable key lesion features; (3) Complete immunohistochemical (IHC) results of post-operative surgical specimens for ER, PR, and HER2, enabling definitive molecular subtype classification; (4) No prior history of breast-related surgery, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy before ultrasound examination; (5) Complete clinical baseline data and follow-up information.Exclusion criteria: (1) Incomplete ultrasound imaging data (e.g., missing key features, such as lesion size, margins, or calcifications) or unreadable image quality; (2) Missing or ambiguous IHC results for ER, PR, or HER2, precluding molecular subtype determination; (3) Concurrent malignant tumors in other organs; (4) Pregnancy or lactation during the study period; (5) Obvious errors or logical inconsistencies in clinical or ultrasound data that cannot be corrected.

Based on the above criteria, a total of 119 patients were excluded due to incomplete ultrasound data or clinical information, resulting in a final cohort of 433 patients included in the analysis. Molecular subtypes of all patients were determined based on IHC analysis of post-operative surgical specimens.

Variable collection and definition

Demographic data collected included age, categorized as >50 years and ≤50 years. All ultrasound examinations were performed using two identical ultrasound systems (Feinon Ultimus 9E, Feinon Technology (Changsha) Co., Ltd.) equipped with a linear array transducer (L12-5 MHz). The acquisition parameters were standardized to ensure consistency: Frequency range = 5–12 MHz, imaging depth = 4–6 cm, Gain = 60–70 dB, dynamic range = 60–70 dB, and focus position adjusted to the center of the target lesion. Ultrasound images of all patients were independently reviewed by two ultrasound specialists, each with more than 10 years of clinical experience. Tumor features were documented according to the standardized criteria of the Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS). Other collected variables included tumor transverse and longitudinal diameter, echo intensity (low, medium, high, none, or unknown), lesion structure (cystic, cystic-solid, solid, or unknown), margin characteristics (smooth or non-smooth), boundary clarity (clear, unclear, or unknown), tumor shape (regular, irregular, or unknown), internal echo pattern (homogeneous or heterogeneous), posterior echo (increased, decreased, or unknown), aspect ratio (ratio of longitudinal to transverse diameter, classified as >1, ≤1, or unknown), calcification (coarse, marginal, microcalcification, none, or unknown), blood perfusion (no, yes, or unknown), and BI-RADS classification (ranging from category 2–6, or unknown), in the above variables, “Unknown” was assigned when the original ultrasound report did not contain a clear description of the feature or when the image quality was insufficient for a confident assessment by the reviewing radiologists. These “Unknown” values were not imputed. Instead, they were treated as a separate nominal category within each variable during model construction, allowing the model to learn whether the absence of a definitive feature description carries any predictive value. All variables were collected and categorized according to standardized definitions to ensure consistency and reliability. In cases of disagreement between the two reviewers, a final consensus was reached through discussion.

Logistic regression analysis

To identify ultrasound features significantly associated with TNBC, we treated the TNBC subtype as a binary dependent variable (TNBC = 1 and non-TNBC = 0). After converting the aforementioned ultrasound features into numeric variables, logistic regression modeling was performed using the generalized linear model (glm) function from the stats package in R. Univariate analysis was initially conducted to evaluate the association of each variable with TNBC risk. Variables with a p < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were then included in a multivariate logistic regression model. Stepwise regression was used to control for potential confounding factors and to identify independent predictors of TNBC. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were extracted for interpretation.

Construction and validation of machine learning-based TNBC diagnostic models

The significant variables identified in the multivariate logistic regression were used as input features to construct multiple machine learning models. A total of 16 commonly used machine learning algorithms and their corresponding feature selection strategies were combined, resulting in 108 binary classification modeling schemes. These combinations included feature processing methods, such as stepwise logistic regression (stepglm), partial least squares regression (plsRglm), glmBoost, LogitBoost, Elastic Net, and Lasso, as well as classifiers, such as random forest (RF), Gradient Boosting Machine, K-Nearest Neighbors, Neural Network, Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), Naive Bayes, Stepwise LDA, Stepwise Quadratic Discriminant Analysis, and support vector machines with radial and linear kernels (svmRadial, svmLinear). The full cohort of 433 patients was randomly split into a training set (n = 260, 29 TNBC cases, 11.2%) and a testing set (n = 173, 18 TNBC cases, 10.4%). The distribution of TNBC cases between the two sets was consistent with the overall cohort (p = 0.872, Chi-square test), ensuring no selection bias in subtype distribution. It should be clarified that the random split was performed without explicit stratified sampling. The balanced distribution of TNBC proportions between the two sets was achieved by chance, as confirmed by the Chi-square test. Class imbalance (overall TNBC proportion 10.9%) was not addressed in the present analysis; no oversampling, undersampling, or class weighting techniques were applied. Furthermore, no fixed random seed was set for the random split or for any stochastic algorithms (e.g., RF). Therefore, the results may not be exactly reproducible in a different random draw. Future studies should consider using a fixed random seed and stratified sampling to enhance reproducibility and balance. All 108 models were trained on the training set, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (area under the curve [AUC]) was calculated in the training set, testing set, as well as in stratified age subgroups (≤50 years and >50 years). The rationale for age stratification is explicitly clarified as follows: (1) Epidemiological evidence consistently indicates that TNBC is more prevalent in younger women (≤50 years) and exhibits more aggressive biological behaviors (e.g., higher proliferation rate and early metastasis) in this population; (2) age-related differences in breast tissue composition (e.g., higher breast density in younger patients) may affect the presentation of ultrasound features and the diagnostic performance of imaging-based models; (3) stratifying by age enables the evaluation of the model’s applicability across distinct risk subgroups, which is critical for its clinical translation, especially in identifying high-risk young populations who may benefit more from early TNBC screening. The model with the highest average AUC was selected as the optimal model, with additional consideration given to the number of features and clinical interpretability. Specifically, among the 108 combinations, Stepglm[forward]+rf achieved the highest average AUC (0.883 across training and testing sets) and the highest testing AUC (0.913); it was therefore chosen as the final model. No other performance metrics (e.g., sensitivity, specificity) were used in the model selection process. The contribution of each feature to the model was also analyzed to understand the distribution of model weights. To assess the clinical applicability and predictive reliability of the model, decision curve analysis (DCA) and calibration curve analysis were conducted. DCA was performed using the rmda package to compute standardized net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities, thereby evaluating the model’s practical value in clinical decision-making. Calibration curves were plotted using the rms package to assess the agreement between predicted probabilities and actual outcomes, reflecting the model’s goodness-of-fit.

It should be noted that hyperparameter tuning, crossvalidation, and bootstrapping were not performed in the present study. All models were trained directly on the training set without internal resamplingbased validation. Therefore, the perfect training AUC of 1.000 may carry a risk of overfitting, particularly given the small number of TNBC cases. The testing set AUC of 0.913 provides some evidence of generalizability, but future studies should incorporate rigorous internal validation strategies, such as repeated crossvalidation or bootstrapping to further assess model robustness.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.5.0. Comparisons of clinical and ultrasound variables were conducted using the autoReg package. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test, while continuous variables were compared using the t-test or Mann–Whitney U test as appropriate. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. ROC curves and AUC calculations were performed using the pROC package.

RESULTS

Comparison of ultrasound characteristics between TNBC and non-TNBC patients

A total of 433 BC patients were included in this study, of whom 47 (10.9%) were classified as having TNBC, and the remaining 386 (89.1%) were non-TNBC. A comparison of clinical and ultrasound characteristics between the two groups is presented in Table 1. There was no statistically significant difference in age distribution (p = 0.504); the proportion of patients aged ≤50 years was 55.3% in the TNBC group, similar to 49.0% in the non-TNBC group. Tumor transverse diameter (p = 0.568) and longitudinal diameter (p = 0.225) also showed no significant differences. Regarding echo intensity, most lesions in both groups appeared hypoechoic, with no statistical difference between groups (p = 0.687). In terms of lesion structure, solid masses were predominant in both TNBC (80.9%) and non-TNBC (75.4%) groups, without a significant difference (p = 0.517). However, several ultrasound features exhibited statistically significant differences in TNBC patients. In terms of margin characteristics, smooth margins were more frequently observed in the TNBC group (34.0% vs. 16.8%), while non-smooth margins were more common in the non-TNBC group (69.9% vs. 55.3%), showing a significant difference (p = 0.017). Posterior echo enhancement was significantly more common in TNBC lesions (87.2% vs. 68.7%, p = 0.030). The difference in aspect ratio was particularly notable: The proportion of lesions with an aspect ratio >1 was significantly higher in the TNBC group compared to the non-TNBC group (61.7% vs. 17.6%, p < 0.001). In addition, calcification patterns differed significantly between the two groups (p = 0.003). TNBC lesions were more likely to show no calcification (63.8% vs. 36.3%), whereas microcalcifications were more frequently observed in non-TNBC patients (52.6% vs. 29.8%). Other ultrasound features, including boundary clarity (p = 0.434), tumor shape (p = 0.131), internal echo pattern (p = 0.174), and blood perfusion (p = 0.183), did not differ significantly between the two groups. Similarly, BI-RADS classification showed no statistically significant difference (p = 0.414). These findings suggest that TNBC lesions are more likely to exhibit smooth margins, posterior echo, a higher aspect ratio (>1), and absence of calcification on ultrasound.

Analysis of ultrasound features predictive of TNBC

To further evaluate the association between individual ultrasound features and the risk of TNBC, both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted. As shown in Table 2, the univariate logistic regression analysis indicated that posterior echo (OR = 1.587, 95% CI: 1.017–1.798, p = 0.026), boundary (OR = 0.684, 95% CI: 0.376–0.857, p = 0.021), margins (OR = 1.248, 95% CI: 1.015–1.837, p = 0.027), calcification (OR = 1.759, 95% CI: 1.159–2.763, p = 0.011), and aspect ratio (OR = 1.703, 95% CI: 1.154–2.488, p = 0.006) were significantly associated with TNBC (p < 0.05). Other factors, including age, transverse and longitudinal tumor diameter, internal echo pattern, blood perfusion, and BI-RADS classification, did not reach statistical significance in the univariate analysis (p > 0.05). Subsequently, the variables that showed significance in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate logistic regression model. The results revealed that posterior echo (OR = 1.371, 95% CI: 1.182–1.761, p = 0.006), margins (OR = 1.234, 95% CI: 1.091–1.896, p = 0.034), calcification (OR = 1.847, 95% CI: 1.208–2.961, p = 0.007), and aspect ratio (OR = 2.938, 95% CI: 1.682–5.233, p < 0.001) were independent risk factors for TNBC.

Construction and validation of the TNBC diagnostic model

The four ultrasound features identified as statistically significant in the previous multivariate logistic regression analysis – posterior echo, margins, calcification, and aspect ratio – were used to construct the TNBC diagnostic model. Based on the training set data, we systematically evaluated the performance of 108 binary classification models composed of different combinations of feature selection methods and classifiers, to identify the model with the best generalization ability. As shown in Figure 1a, the combination of Stepglm[forward]+rf yielded the best overall performance across all datasets, with an average AUC of 0.883, and was therefore selected as the optimal model. Further analysis revealed the order of importance of the four input features in this model: calcification, aspect ratio, posterior echo, and margins [Figure 1b].

DISCUSSION

In the context of precision medicine, increasing attention has been paid to the relationship between BC radiomic phenotypes and molecular subtypes.[17,18] Previous studies have demonstrated the diagnostic value of ultrasound features in differentiating between benign and malignant breast lesions.[19-21] The distinct sonographic characteristics of TNBC and non-TNBC provide opportunities for developing predictive models for subtype classification.[16,22] In this study, we systematically evaluated the differences in ultrasound features between TNBC and non-TNBC patients and identified posterior echo, margins, calcification, and aspect ratio as independent predictors of TNBC. Based on these features, we constructed a machine learning model with high diagnostic performance. Our findings are consistent with previous literature and provide new evidence-based support for the non-invasive imaging-based diagnosis of TNBC.

Our results showed that the proportion of lesions with an aspect ratio >1 was significantly higher in the TNBC group, suggesting stronger vertical growth capacity and indicating a higher invasive potential. This may be related to the biological behavior of TNBC, which tends to penetrate the basement membrane and invade deeper tissues, manifesting radiologically as enhanced vertical growth.[23-25] Prior studies have also reported that TNBC patients with an aspect ratio ≥2 are more likely to have lymph node metastasis.[26,27] Our findings further validate the aspect ratio as a useful indicator for identifying highly aggressive BC, with important clinical implications. In addition, we observed a significantly higher prevalence of non-calcified lesions in the TNBC group compared with the non-TNBC group, consistent with earlier findings.[28,29] Histologically, TNBC typically lacks ductal structures and is primarily composed of solid or sheet-like tumor cell clusters, making it less likely to form microcalcifications. This differs markedly from the calcification patterns commonly seen in ductal carcinoma in situ. Consequently, typical calcification signals are rarely observed on ultrasound images of TNBC.[30] This feature not only reflects the unique histological architecture of TNBC but also serves as a clinical alert: The absence of calcification in a suspicious lesion warrants consideration of TNBC as a potential diagnosis.

Previous studies have reported that TNBC may exhibit sonographic features resembling benign lesions, such as smooth margins and posterior echo.[31-33] Our study also found that the incidence of posterior echo was significantly higher in the TNBC group than in the non-TNBC group, consistent with findings by Huang et al., who reported the lowest rate of posterior attenuation in TNBC cases.[34] From a pathological perspective, TNBC is characterized by high cellular density and low stromal content, with dense clusters of tumor cells and lymphocytes often forming extensive areas of necrosis. These tissue characteristics enhance the transmission of ultrasound waves, resulting in the observed posterior acoustic enhancement.[35] Moreover, margin is a key ultrasound parameter for distinguishing benign from malignant BC. While conventional wisdom suggests that malignant tumors typically present with spiculated or irregular margins, TNBC often displays relatively smooth or well-defined borders despite its aggressive nature. Our findings are in line with those of Su et al.[21] TNBC commonly follows a rapid proliferative growth pattern with minimal stromal reaction, leading to compression of surrounding tissues without eliciting significant fibrosis or desmoplastic response. This results in well-circumscribed, microlobulated margins.[12,36] Such a deceptively “benign” appearance may confound imaging-based diagnoses, underscoring the importance of integrating multiple features in the comprehensive assessment of breast lesions.

In this study, multivariate logistic regression analysis further confirmed that the four ultrasound features mentioned above were independent predictors of TNBC. Based on this, we developed a systematic machine learning modeling framework that integrated 108 combinations of feature selection methods and classification algorithms. The optimal model, Stepglm+rf, demonstrated an excellent performance, with an AUC of 1.000 in the training set and 0.913 in the testing set, indicating strong model fit, generalizability, and robustness. Compared with the TNBC prediction model based on ultrasound features proposed by Li et al. (AUC = 0.78),[12] our model achieved higher predictive accuracy. Moreover, this systematic approach effectively overcame the limitations of traditional single-algorithm models, such as the tendency toward overfitting or underfitting, further highlighting the powerful potential of machine learning in constructing diagnostic models based on ultrasound features. From a clinical decision-making perspective, DCA showed that the model yielded a higher standardized net benefit when the predicted probability was below 0.20, suggesting its practical utility in early TNBC risk screening. The calibration curve also demonstrated good agreement between predicted probabilities and actual disease incidence, indicating the model’s stable probabilistic interpretability and strong clinical applicability. Notably, age-stratified analysis revealed that the model performed better in patients younger than 50 years (AUC = 0.911), significantly outperforming its performance in those aged 50 years and older (AUC = 0.706). This finding may be attributed to the higher prevalence of TNBC in younger patients, the more aggressive biological behavior of TNBC in this population, and the greater likelihood of typical ultrasound features in younger individuals.[37-40] These results further enhance the clinical application potential of the model, especially in high-risk subgroups, such as younger patients.

This study has several notable strengths. First, it included a relatively large sample size of 433 BC patients, ensuring adequate statistical power. Second, a systematic comparison of various machine learning model combinations was conducted, ensuring the robustness and generalizability of the final model. Finally, the model maintained high performance in an independent validation cohort, and the integration of DCA and calibration curve analysis provided solid evidence to support its clinical translational value.

However, some limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the study was a retrospective single-center analysis, which may introduce selection bias; thus, future multicenter prospective studies are needed for further validation. Second, ultrasound feature annotation may involve a certain degree of subjectivity. Third, the relatively small number of TNBC cases may affect the calibration of the model; future studies should aim to expand the TNBC sample size. Fourth, the training set AUC of 1.000 raises a concern for possible overfitting. While the independent testing set AUC of 0.913 suggests reasonable generalization, we did not perform internal resampling techniques such as crossvalidation or bootstrapping to quantify the optimism of the training performance. Future studies with larger sample sizes and more rigorous internal validation are needed to confirm the model’s robustness and to better calibrate its predictive probabilities.

CONCLUSION

This study identified significant differences in ultrasound imaging features between TNBC and non-TNBC BC patients. Based on four independent predictors – posterior echo, margins, calcification, and aspect ratio – a high-performance machine learning diagnostic model was developed. This model demonstrated a strong fit and predictive capability in both the training and testing cohorts, showing superior performance, particularly among younger patients, and highlighting its promising potential for clinical translation.
