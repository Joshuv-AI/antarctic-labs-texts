Network analysis of social support and anxiety symptoms among college students during the COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Tingwei Feng, Lei Ren, Guimin Zhang, Yanqin Hou, Buyao Wang, Lin Wu, Mingdi Mi, Hui Wang, Xiuchao Wang, Daqing Huang, Xufeng Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecent research suggests that focusing on specific symptoms, rather than general levels of impairment, may offer a more nuanced understanding of psychopathology. Given the unique social and psychological challenges posed by the COVID-19 pandemic, this study aims to explore the relationships between the three dimensions of perceived social support family support, friend support, and other support—and individual anxiety symptoms among college students. The pandemic context provides an opportunity to examine how different forms of social support may influence specific anxiety symptoms in a time of heightened stress and restricted social interactions.MethodsWe conducted a network analysis of anxiety symptoms and perceived social support in a sample of 4105 college students. The network structures and bridge effects between social support and anxiety symptoms were examined at two time points (T1 and T2). A network comparison test (NCT) was conducted to assess differences in the network structures and bridge centrality indices across the two time points.ResultsIn this sample, perceived social support was significantly associated with anxiety symptoms. Bridge centrality analysis indicated that, across both networks, family support generally correlated with lower levels of certain anxiety symptoms, serving as a protective factor. In contrast, friend support showed a mixed pattern—positively associated with some symptoms (e.g., irritability) but negatively associated with others (e.g., uncontrollable worry). These findings suggest that different forms of social support may have distinct effects on specific anxiety symptoms.ConclusionsNetwork analysis revealed that family support consistently served as a key protective factor against anxiety symptoms, while the influence of friend and other support weakened over time. These findings highlight the dynamic nature of support systems and provide implications for tailored mental health interventions in college students during and after the pandemic.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07215-2.

Background

Perceived social support refers to an individual's subjective emotional experience of feeling supported, respected, and understood in their social interactions [1]. Various aspects of social support, including family and friend support, hold significant significance for college students [2, 3]. Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) is a mental disorder characterized by excessive anxiety and worry about numerous activities and events [4–7]. Along with the academic, personal, and employment challenges inherent in the college experience, individuals often manifest negative emotional experiences such as fear, unease, and apprehension [8–10], leading to cognitive, behavioral, and physiological changes [11, 12].

Research by Sullivan et al. [13] demonstrates that social support significantly predicts anxiety and depressive symptoms among college students, with family and friend support playing particularly critical roles. These findings underscore the buffering effect of social support in alleviating anxiety among college students, especially when facing academic and psychological stressors. Bedaso et al. [14] systematic review and meta-analysis further confirm a negative correlation between social support and anxiety and depression among pregnant women. The study suggests that an increase in social support is associated with a reduction in anxiety and depressive symptoms, indicating that social support may serve as an effective intervention target to reduce anxiety issues in college students. Similarly, Shao et al. [15] found a significant relationship between social support and anxiety and depressive symptoms in medical students. In their study, inadequate social support was linked to higher anxiety and depressive symptoms, while strong social support correlated with improved mental health outcomes.While extensive research during the COVID-19 pandemic examined the link between social support and anxiety among college students worldwide, there is still a need for studies that focus on specific dimensions of social support and their distinct impacts on individual anxiety symptoms. Our study addresses this gap by exploring these nuanced relationships in a Chinese college student population.

Previous research has shown relatively limited attention to the relationship between social support and anxiety [16, 17], leaving scholars with a less comprehensive understanding of their interplay. Prior studies have often employed mediation analyses [18] to explore this relationship, potentially overlooking a more nuanced comprehension of their interaction at the symptom level. Emerging research indicates a significant negative correlation between perceived social support and employment anxiety among college students [19]. The more social support college students receive at the societal level, the lower their levels of anxiety [20]. Studies suggest that positive social evaluations exhibit distinct associations with individual anxiety symptoms [21]. Previous research has consistently demonstrated that antecedents of anxiety exert differential effects on specific anxiety symptoms. For instance, intolerance of uncertainty (IU), emotion regulation (ER), and the Big Five personality traits are each uniquely associated with distinct patterns of anxiety symptoms [22–24]. Neglecting the heterogeneity of anxiety symptoms may obscure differential relationships between various clinical symptoms and levels of social support, potentially leading to inconsistent findings across studies.

To address the challenges encountered in the aforementioned research, we have employed a symptom-based analytical approach, namely network analysis. From a network analysis perspective, mental disorders stem from relationships among interacting variables, which include both symptomatic and non-symptomatic factors, rather than being mere consequences of an underlying common cause [25]. With this method, perceived social support and individual anxiety symptoms, such as worry, serve as nodes, and the potential pathways connecting different symptoms are depicted as edges. In network analysis, nodes represent individual variables—in our case, specific anxiety symptoms and types of social support. Edges are the connections between these nodes, indicating the relationships or associations between the variables. Thus, the emphasis of network analysis lies in identifying specific pathways linking social support to individual symptoms of anxiety. This stands out from latent variable models that focus on how varying degrees of social support may affect latent psychopathological structures. In addition to describing the social support-symptom pathways, network analysis also provides empirical indices, such as bridge expected influence [26]. The concept of bridge expected influence in network analysis represents the extent to which a specific variable, such as family or friend support, influences symptoms in another community, like anxiety symptoms. A higher bridge expected influence indicates a stronger capacity of the variable to impact symptoms across communities [27]. These novel metrics enable researchers to quantify to what extent family support, friend support, and other support may convey positive/negative influences (both risk and protective capabilities) to clusters of symptoms.

This study investigates the relationship between three dimensions of social support family support, friend support, and other support and anxiety disorders among college students. College students, facing significant academic, interpersonal, and career pressures, often experience elevated anxiety, which can be mitigated through social support.

(1) Bridge effects exist in the associations between different types of social support and anxiety symptoms. (2) The network structures at two key time points (T1 and T2) during the COVID-19 pandemic were compared to identify potential similarities and differences in network characteristics. By testing these hypotheses, we aim to enrich the existing theoretical framework concerning the temporal dynamics of the relationships between social support and anxiety symptoms. Furthermore, this study seeks to provide empirical evidence to inform the development of targeted screening, prevention, and intervention strategies for anxiety across different stages of the pandemic.

In conclusion, this study aims to explore the nuanced relationship between different forms of perceived social support (family, friend, and other support) and anxiety symptoms in college students, particularly in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic. By employing network analysis, we provide a detailed examination of how these support dimensions interact with specific anxiety symptoms, offering new insights beyond general correlations.

Methods

Participants and ethical statement

This study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, which necessitated specific adjustments to ensure participant safety and data integrity. Data were collected remotely through Wenjuanxing (Questionnaire Star), an online survey platform, between January 16, 2022, and April 18, 2022. This method allowed us to reach participants while minimizing health risks associated with in-person contact. A total of 4,142 college students from Xijing University in Shaanxi Province, China, initially participated. After excluding 38 responses due to incomplete demographic information or failure to pass honesty-check items, the final sample included 4,105 participants, with 1,956 males and 2,149 females, averaging 22.74 years old (SD = 5.37). Participants were informed of the study’s purpose and provided informed consent electronically. The survey included quality control questions, and responses were collected anonymously to encourage honesty. Data were then carefully monitored and checked for completeness and consistency before analysis. The baseline assessment at T1 was conducted between January and April 2022, corresponding to the early stage of the COVID-19 pandemic. The follow-up assessment at T2 was conducted between July and October 2023, during the later phase of the pandemic as it was approaching its end. The follow-up targeted the same group of participants and was conducted via the online survey platform Wenjuanxing.

At T2, a total of 2,052 participants did not respond to repeated follow-up invitations and were excluded from the study. Ultimately, 2,052 participants were retained for analysis, resulting in a follow-up rate of 49.9% (see Table 1). Measures

The Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) [28, 29] is a widely used instrument developed to assess an individual's self-perceived level of social support from different sources within their social environment. The scale comprises 12 items, distributed across three dimensions: Family Support (items 3, 4, 8, and 11), Friend Support (items 6, 7, 9, and 12), and Other Support (items 1, 2, 5, and 10), which captures support from individuals other than family or friends. Rather than measuring specific supportive behaviors, the MSPSS focuses on general perceptions of support. For instance, items such as “I feel supported by my friends” and “I believe my family values me” reflect the respondent’s subjective belief in being supported, instead of quantifying the frequency or content of such support.

Responses are rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“completely disagree”) to 7 (“completely agree”), with higher scores indicating greater perceived social support. In the present study, the MSPSS demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.90).

The Chinese version of the MSPSS was translated by Chou using a rigorous forward and back-translation procedure, and has been widely applied in psychological research among Chinese populations. Its three-factor structure, internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α > 0.85), and construct validity have been confirmed in multiple studies [30, 31]. These findings support the cross-cultural applicability of the MSPSS and justify its use in the current Chinese sample.

We employed the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item (GAD-7) scale to assess participants'anxiety symptoms [4]. The GAD-7 consists of seven items measuring the severity of core anxiety symptoms. Participants were instructed to report how often they experienced each symptom over the past two weeks, using a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”). Higher scores indicate more severe anxiety symptoms. In the present study, individual item scores were used as the primary variables of interest in subsequent analyses. The scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.90).

The Chinese version of the GAD-7 has been psychometrically validated in multiple studies involving Chinese college student populations. These studies confirmed its strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α > 0.85), robust factor structure, and satisfactory convergent validity [32]. These findings support the use of the GAD-7 as a reliable and valid instrument for measuring anxiety symptoms in Chinese samples.

Network analysis

This study constructed and analyzed the same network structure at two different time points to explore its temporal dynamics and potential differences. Specifically, the focus was on the Perceived Social Support–Anxiety network. The network structure of the two undirected networks was estimated using graphical LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) methodology [33]. In each network, edges represent the partial correlations between two nodes when adjusting for the effects from all other remaining nodes. The networks in this study were based on Spearman partial correlations [33, 34]. The graphical LASSO technique was employed to regularize the partial correlations within the represented network. By penalizing very small partial correlation coefficients to zero, this technique aids in removing spurious edges, resulting in a more stable and sparse network [35]. The Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC) hyperparameter γ was set to 0.5 to balance sensitivity and specificity [36]. The networks presented herein were constructed using the Fruchterman-Reingold algorithm [37]. The qgraph package in R was utilized to compute the proposed networks [38].

In this study, two communities were predefined, namely the Perceived Social Support community (comprising family support, friend support, and other support) and the Symptom community (anxiety symptoms). Bridge expected influence, which calculates the sum of edge weights connecting a given node to all nodes in the other community, was computed to identify bridge nodes connecting these communities [26]. Bridge expected influence was computed to identify nodes that connect variables across the two communities (e.g., social support and anxiety symptoms), thereby assessing the influence each variable has on the other community [39, 40]. Bridge expected influence was calculated using the network tool package in R [26].

The accuracy of edge weights was assessed by plotting 95% confidence intervals for each edge using 2,000 bootstrap samples. To evaluate the stability of bridge expected influence, the Correlation Stability (CS) coefficient was calculated using the bootstrapping method with 2,000 bootstrap samples. Following recommendations [41], an ideal CS coefficient is higher than 0.5 and should not be less than 0.25. Further, bootstrapped difference tests were conducted for edge weights and bridge expected influence with 2,000 bootstrap samples. These procedures were carried out using the R software package bootnet [41].

Discussion

Applying network analysis, we aim to explore the fine-grained relations between various symptoms of perceived social support and anxiety. It is showed that various symptoms of anxiety are commonly but differentially connected with components of MSPSS.

This study explores perceived social support and anxiety disorders among Chinese university students, highlighting the long-term psychological effects of different types of social support on their mental health. Using network analysis, we examined how individual differences in social support can mitigate the risk of anxiety symptoms. Consistent with previous research [42, 43], our findings reveal that family support, friend support, and other forms of support play distinct roles in specific anxiety symptoms, with family support acting as a protective factor and friend support, along with other support, exhibiting detrimental effects.

Notably, family support was found to be negatively correlated with four anxiety symptoms: irritability (G6), difficulty relaxing (G4), excessive worry (G3), and worry about things happening (G7). In the era of artificial intelligence, college students often face a disconnect between their academic and career expectations and reality. With increasing numbers of students prioritizing theoretical learning over rapidly applying theory into practice, they may find themselves in high-pressure and anxiety-inducing situations [44, 45]. In contemporary educational settings, AI-driven learning tools and personalized learning methods are becoming mainstream, and family support plays an important role in helping students adapt to these new technologies and manage the associated pressures. We are not suggesting that family support helps students learn AI tools directly, but rather that AI, as a non-human interface, may replace traditional support systems such as classrooms and study groups. This shift makes students more reliant on family and peer support, especially when facing academic and career challenges. Strong family support can help students manage the pressures associated with adapting to AI learning tools, fostering stability and confidence amid academic challenges, and thereby alleviating anxiety symptoms as measured by the GAD-7 scale. Existing research suggests that family support can help college students manage academic and career pressures, boost their self-efficacy, and further reduce anxiety symptoms [46, 47].

Our network analysis also found a significant negative relationship between family support and irritability (G6), suggesting that family support may buffer against irritability, a common anxiety symptom. Family interactions that promote emotional stability—such as open communication and emotional validation—can reduce stress and frustration, thereby alleviating anxiety. Previous studies have consistently shown that good family support enhances emotional expression and reduces negative emotions, further supporting the protective role of family support in alleviating anxiety and depression [48].

Given that college students face multiple sources of stress—academic demands, career uncertainties, and interpersonal challenges—family support is particularly important for this population. Our findings underscore the significant protective role of family support in mitigating anxiety symptoms, especially in the context of the unique pressures faced during the college years. However, our sample was limited to college students, which may affect the generalizability of the results. Future research should include a more diverse sample across various age groups and cultural contexts to further explore the dynamic relationship between social support and anxiety.

In this study, our findings indicate a significant association between perceived social support and anxiety symptoms. Norris and Kaniasty [49] social support deterioration deterrence model provides a theoretical foundation for understanding this relationship. The model suggests that following major stressful events, a reduction in perceived social support may lead to increased psychological distress. However, if adequate support resources are mobilized during this period, the deterioration in perceived support can be effectively counteracted, thereby alleviating psychological distress. Based on this theory, social support plays a crucial protective role in mitigating anxiety and stress. For college students, a unique population under high levels of uncertainty and pressure, perceived social support may serve as an important buffer against psychological distress. The negative correlation between family support and anxiety symptoms observed in this study supports the idea of social support functioning as a protective mechanism against anxiety.

In the social support-anxiety network, we found that friend support was positively related to G6'irritable'and G1'tense or anxious,'and negatively related to G2'uncontrollable worry,'G7'worry about things happening,'and G5'restlessness.'This finding can be explained in several ways. For instance, during the pandemic, being disconnected from offline environments and daily communication for an extended period may require college students to engage in academic and employment activities in an isolated and high-pressure environment (potentially involving ongoing stressful events). Intensive study until graduation in the face of uncertainty may objectively increase the perceived difficulty of events [50, 51]. In this scenario, college students'support or communication with peers or classmates may make them more sensitive or excessively concerned about the uncertainty of the future, given the uncertainty of external information [52, 53]. Given the unique social conditions during the COVID-19 pandemic, including prolonged social distancing and limited in-person interactions, it is possible that the associations between social support and anxiety symptoms observed in this study are specific to this context. Under normal circumstances, students may have more access to diverse support systems, such as campus resources, extracurricular activities, and peer groups, which could influence the nature and effectiveness of social support. Future studies are needed to replicate this research in a post-pandemic setting to determine if these relationships hold or if social support functions differently when students are able to engage in typical in-person interactions and social activities. It is worth noting that family support may be more effective in alleviating somatic anxiety symptoms than friend support and other support. Future research needs to test these two hypotheses and determine the direction of these pathways.

Our findings suggest an association between friend support and certain anxiety symptoms. This raises interesting questions about the nature of friend support—whether it might sometimes contribute to heightened anxiety symptoms through mechanisms such as co-rumination, or conversely, if students with higher anxiety may be more inclined to seek friend support. Future studies could explore how students perceive the outcomes of friend support, for example, assessing whether talking to friends provides relief or inadvertently intensifies worry.

These cross-sectional network results at T1 and T2 offer valuable insights into the evolving relationship between perceived social support and anxiety symptoms over the course of the pandemic. Notably, the overall reduction in the number of non-zero edges and the weakening of bridge centrality values from T1 to T2 suggest a potential shift in the functional role of social support in buffering against anxiety. This pattern may reflect an adaptation process, in which individuals gradually develop alternative coping strategies or experience support fatigue as the pandemic progresses.

The consistently strong bridge centrality of family support across both time points highlights its robust and enduring role as a protective factor, particularly in times of prolonged stress. In contrast, the diminishing connectivity of friend and other forms of support—especially evident at T2—may point to reduced accessibility or perceived effectiveness of these support sources during the later stages of the pandemic. These findings echo prior literature indicating that family often serves as a more stable and immediate source of emotional and instrumental support in crises, while peer-based and community resources may fluctuate based on context and duration.

Moreover, the observed reduction in network density and bridge effects at T2 could imply a general attenuation in the impact of external resources on internal emotional states, potentially due to pandemic-related desensitization or emotional exhaustion. These findings underscore the importance of tailoring mental health interventions to the dynamic nature of support systems and highlight the need for sustained reinforcement of family-based support channels during extended periods of collective stress.

Bridge analysis revealed that family support consistently showed the strongest negative bridge effect across both time points, underscoring its central protective role in mitigating anxiety symptoms, followed by other support and friend support.

In terms of edge patterns, the T1 network demonstrated broader and stronger negative connections between support types and anxiety symptoms—especially for family support—whereas the T2 network showed a reduction in both the number and strength of these associations, with friend support displaying notably fewer connections. These temporal differences may suggest that as the pandemic progressed, individuals'reliance on social support structures shifted. While family support remained a stable protective factor, the influence of other forms of support appeared to diminish over time.

To quantify the protective or risk capacity of social support on anxiety symptoms through network analysis, we calculated the bridge expected influence for each network separately. In the social support-anxiety network, family support had a negative bridge expected influence value, while friend support and other support had positive bridge expected influence values, suggesting that family support may act as a protective factor, whereas friend support and other support may function as risk factors for anxiety in college students. These findings align with previous research that has considered family support and friend support as protective and risk factors for anxiety [54].

The current research findings hold several significant implications for the development of early detection and intervention strategies aimed at safeguarding the psychological well-being of college students. First and foremost, by identifying the pathways between social support and anxiety, our study results suggest that friend support might be prioritized as a target for early detection and prevention. Specifically, when employing symptom-based questionnaires as screening tools, addressing the stigma around mental illnesses (e.g., the fear of repercussions from employers, health regulatory authorities, or medical boards) might introduce self-report biases. Conversely, items related to other support are more neutral and are less likely to raise concerns such as fear of retaliation during screening. Moreover, given that other support may exist independently of symptoms, measuring self-expression inhibition may help identify high-risk individuals and offer timely support before symptoms manifest. Therefore, stakeholders may consider incorporating the Perceived Social Support Scale into existing occupational health assessments.

Our findings should not be interpreted as suggesting that any form of social support is inherently harmful. Rather, they reflect the complexity and potential bidirectionality of support-symptom relationships. Social support from friends, family, and others remains essential, particularly during times of distress. Our results suggest that psychological education programs emphasizing the benefits of family support, while encouraging balanced reliance on friend and other forms of support, may be beneficial. Given the high prevalence of anxiety symptoms among college students, there is an urgent need for such programs to mitigate both short- and long-term psychological impacts. Finally, by elucidating the differential relationships between various sources of social support and specific psychological symptoms, our findings may offer a theoretical foundation for personalized prevention and intervention strategies targeting symptom heterogeneity.

While the current findings are novel, there are also some limitations that should be considered. Firstly, the use of cross-sectional data restricts our ability to determine the direction of causal relationships. It remains unclear whether social support is influenced by anxiety symptoms, or if external circumstances simultaneously induce symptoms and affect social support. Secondly, the observed network structures in this study may be specific to the questionnaires we employed and may not be replicable when different measures are used to assess these structures. However, all the measures employed in this study have been widely adopted and validated in various contexts. Thirdly, the social support-symptom network at the group level reflects effects between individuals, which may not capture intra-individual processes. Diary network analysis using dense longitudinal data can assist in understanding the social support-symptom relationship within individuals. Fourthly, considering the differences in healthcare systems between China and other countries, future research should investigate university students from various countries to test the generalizability of the reported study results.

Conclusions

This study revealed dynamic and time-sensitive associations between perceived social support and anxiety symptoms across two critical stages of the COVID-19 pandemic. Through network analysis, we found that family support consistently played a central and protective bridging role in mitigating anxiety symptoms at both time points. In contrast, the connections between friend support, other support, and anxiety symptoms weakened over time, suggesting shifting support dynamics as the pandemic progressed. These findings underscore the importance of considering not only the type of social support but also the temporal context in which it operates. By identifying specific bridge connections between support types and individual anxiety symptoms, this study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of how support networks function under prolonged stress. These insights offer empirical guidance for the development of stage-specific mental health interventions and early identification strategies targeting college students during and beyond pandemic conditions.
