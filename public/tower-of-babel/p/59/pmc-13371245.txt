Prognostic value of vascular lesions in immunoglobulin a nephropathy: a meta-analysis
Authors: Zhihan Lei, Ye Xu, Ling Guo, Ye Liu, Jingjing Zeng, Jing Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeImmunoglobulin A nephropathy (IgAN) stands as the most frequently observed primary glomerular disease globally and a major contributor to end-stage renal disease (ESRD). The aim of this study is to investigate the prognostic value of vascular lesions in IgAN through meta-analysis.MethodsFour databases, comprising Embase, Cochrane Library, PubMed, and Web of Science, were comprehensively searched from the commencement of each database to November 8, 2025. The quality of all eligible studies was evaluated utilizing the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (NOS). Statistical analysis was implemented using Stata (version 18.0). The association between vascular lesions in IgAN and adverse kidney outcomes was assessed by pooling hazard ratios (HRs) along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsTotally 16 studies from 6 countries, involving 8,862 patients, were included. Among patients with IgAN, arteriosclerotic lesions increased the risk of poor kidney outcomes (HR = 1.95; 95%CI: 1.47, 2.60; P < 0.001). Similarly, microangiopathy (MA) triggered an elevated risk of adverse kidney outcomes (HR = 1.98; 95%CI: 1.54, 2.56; P < 0.001). Vascular lesions significantly raised this risk (HR = 2.27; 95%CI: 1.78, 2.91; P < 0.001). After adjustment for confounders, HRs remained statistically significant, indicating that both MA and arterial lesions were associated with adverse outcomes in IgAN.ConclusionIndividuals suffering from IgAN with vascular lesions, including arteriosclerotic lesions and/or microangiopathy, are confronted with a pronouncedly elevated risk of experiencing poor kidney outcomes. Introduction

Immunoglobulin A nephropathy (IgAN) stands as a primary glomerular disease primarily featuring IgA or IgA deposits in the mesangial region of the glomeruli. IgAN is the most frequently observed primary glomerular disease worldwide and a substantial contributor to end-stage renal disease (ESRD), representing a grave threat to human kidney health [1, 2]. The incidence of IgAN varies by region, and globally, the annual incidence rate is at least 2.5 cases per 100,000 individuals [3]. Among patients with primary glomerulonephritis confirmed by renal biopsy, IgAN accounts for 40–50% of cases in East Asia, approximately 20% in Europe, 10–20% in North America, and below 5% in Africa [3]. Previous studies have indicated that the kidney survival rate within 10 years for IgAN is merely 54%, with roughly 20–40% of individuals advancing to ESRD within 2 decades following IgAN diagnosis [4–6].

IgAN has an insidious onset, and the prognosis of IgAN varies significantly among individuals. While some patients maintain stable disease for extended periods, others experience rapid progression. Early and accurate identification of factors associated with poor prognosis is crucial for optimizing treatment strategies and delaying adverse kidney outcomes. Currently, recognized prognostic factors for poor outcomes in IgAN include proteinuria level, early renal impairment, and pathological alterations (e.g., interstitial fibrosis and glomerulosclerosis) [5, 7–11]. Multiple studies have also begun to focus on the high incidence rate of vascular lesions in IgAN and their association with established prognostic factors of adverse kidney outcomes, such as hypertension, proteinuria level [12], hyperuricemia [13], glomerulosclerosis [14], as well as interstitial blood flow and hypoxia [15]. Nevertheless, vascular lesions were not included in the Oxford classification, since the significance of vascular lesions in renal prognosis was not discovered in the in the Oxford cohort [16]. Conversely, a study by Soares, J et al. [17] suggests that vascular lesions should be integrated into the MEST-C framework to enhance risk stratification models for IgAN and improve the predictive accuracy of relevant models. The role and predictive value of vascular lesions, as a significant component of pathological injuries of the kidney, in IgAN progression urgently require to be clarified. Currently, there is still no universally accepted standardized definition for vascular lesions in IgA nephropathy. Referring to the published literature and KDIGO guidelines, Vascular lesions in IgAN are primarily categorized into arteriosclerotic lesions and microangiopathy (MA) [18, 19]. Arteriosclerotic lesions mainly manifest as varying degrees of intimal fibrosis (thickening of arteria-arteriola walls) and arteriolar hyalinosis. MA refers to pathologic features that represent tissue responses to endothelial injury, covering lesions with and without actual thrombi. Specifically, microangiopathic lesions characterized by endothelial cell swelling, subintimal edema, arteriolar thrombosis, and/or fibrinoid necrosis are classified as acute, while those presenting with arterial “onion-skin” lesions are defined as chronic [20].Typically, the acute microangiopathic lesions are also termed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).In summary, vascular lesions in IgAN pathologically encompass a spectrum of pathological changes from acute endothelial injury to chronic fibrosclerotic alterations. Accurately identifying and classifying these vascular lesions is crucial for evaluating and predicting kidney outcomes among patients with IgAN.

The association of vascular lesions in IgAN with adverse kidney outcomes remains controversial. Therefore, this study endeavors to systematically search and integrate published original research on the prognostic power of vascular lesions in IgAN. By employing meta-analysis to synthesize data from relevant studies, it seeks to illuminate the strength of association between vascular lesions in IgAN and adverse kidney outcomes, and to verify whether vascular lesions can be treated as a potential pathological indicator for evaluating the prognosis of IgAN. This study aims to provide evidence-based support for the early identification of risk factors for IgAN progression and the assessment of disease prognosis.

Methods

This study was performed adhering to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [21]. The study protocol was registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (Registration number: CRD420251235592).

Literature search

Four databases, comprising Cochrane Library, Embase, PubMed, and Web of Science, were systematically searched from the commencement of each database until November 8, 2025. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms with free-text terms. The MeSH terms employed in the literature search were Glomerulonephritis, IGA; Thrombotic Microangiopathy; Arteriosclerosis, Nephrosclerosis, Kidney Failure. Additionally, grey literature and reference lists from other relevant literature were manually examined to identify potentially eligible studies. The search strategy is detailed in Table S1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies that met the criteria below were included: (i) Participant: patients diagnosed with IgAN confirmed by renal biopsy; (ii) Exposure: a presence of vascular lesions (including arteriosclerotic lesions and MA); Comparison: an absence of vascular lesions; (iii) Study Design: observational studies; (iv) Outcome: poor kidney outcomes, such as death, progression to ESRD, or ≥ 30% decrease in estimated glomerular filtration rate.

Studies were excluded provided that they were: (i) scientific experiment plans, case reports, commentaries, animal or cell experiments, editorials, letters, or conference abstracts; (ii) publications with serious errors or missing data; (iii) duplicate publications; (iv) studies from which the full texts were unavailable; or (v) studies involving overlapping participants.

Literature screening and data extraction

The retrieved records were all imported into EndNote. The titles and abstracts were independently assessed by two researchers (Lei ZH and Xu Y). Subsequently, the full texts of potentially eligible studies were reviewed for a second screening. Any disagreement was reconciled through discussion or via consultation with a third investigator (Lv J). Data extraction was conducted utilizing a pre-designed spreadsheet. The extracted data consisted of publication year, country, first author, sample size, study design, sex, and outcome indicator.

Quality assessment

Two investigators (Lei ZH and Xu Y) independently assessed the quality of the included studies leveraging the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (NOS) [22]. This tool evaluates studies across 8 items classified into 3 domains: selection, exposure, and comparability. NOS score varies between 0 and 9. Studies achieving an NOS score of ≥ 6 were deemed high-quality, while those scoring ≤ 5 were judged to be low-quality.

Statistical analysis

Each statistical analysis was implemented utilizing Stata (version 18.0). The association of vascular lesions in IgAN with adverse kidney outcomes was evaluated by calculating pooled hazard ratios (HRs) alongside their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Heterogeneity among studies was evaluated leveraging the Cochrane I² statistic. P < 0.10 and I2 > 50% signified significant heterogeneity. A random-effects model (Restricted Maximum Likelihood method) was adopted in cases of pronounced heterogeneity; otherwise, a fixed-effects model (Inverse-variance method) was selected [23]. The robustness of the results was examined through sensitivity analysis. Subgroup analysis (based on study region) and meta-regression were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Publication bias was assessed via funnel plots and quantitatively identified with Egger’s test. P < 0.05 indicated significant bias.

Results

Literature search and screening

Totally 3,352 records were initially identified. After the removal of 578 duplicates, 2,736 records were removed in light of their titles and abstracts. Afterwards, the full texts of the remaining studies were reviewed strictly. Ultimately, 16 studies were selected. The screening process is detailed in Fig. 1.

Basic characteristics and quality assessment of included studies

The 16 included studies [12, 20, 24–37] were performed across 7 countries (China, Brazil, Japan, South Korea, Netherlands, France and Thailand), involving 8,862 patients. The cohort comprised 4,253 males and 4,609 females, and their ages varied between 14 and 75 years. All included studies were retrospective cohort studies. IgAN diagnosis was ascertained by renal biopsy among all included patients, and the presence of arteriosclerotic lesions and/or MA was determined based on pathological results. Meta-analysis

Pooled results for each outcome

Outcome 1: Arteriosclerotic lesions

Ten studies involving 6,928 patients reported arteriosclerotic lesions. The heterogeneity analysis revealed: I²=58.86%, P < 0.001; hence, a random-effects model was adopted. The pooled results indicated that among individuals suffering from IgAN, the risk of poor kidney outcomes was pronouncedly higher in the group with arteriosclerotic lesions in comparison with the control group (HR = 1.95; 95% CI: 1.47, 2.60; P < 0.001), as shown in Fig. 2.

Forest plot for arteriosclerotic lesions

Subgroup analysis

A subgroup analysis was performed according to whether the included studies adjusted for confounders. The vast majority of studies had controlled for confounding factors, and the adjusted HR remained statistically significant. This suggested that arteriosclerotic lesions possibly served as an prognostic factor for poor kidney outcomes in IgAN. The results are presented in Fig. 3.

Subgroup analysis of arterial lesions (adjusted for confounding factors)

Outcome 2: MA

Data on MA were provided in seven studies involving 2,392 patients. The heterogeneity was: I²=1.15%, P = 0.42, and a fixed-effects model was adopted. The pooled results demonstrated that among patients suffering from IgAN, the risk of adverse kidney outcomes was pronouncedly higher in the MA group in comparison to the control group (HR = 1.98; 95% CI: 1.54, 2.56; P < 0.001), as shown in Fig. 4.

Forest plot for MA

Subgroup analysis

A subgroup analysis was implemented according to whether confounders were adjusted for. Most studies had adjusted for confounders, and the adjusted HR retained statistical significance, indicating that MA was possibly an prognostic factor for poor kidney outcomes in IgAN (Fig. 5). Moreover, a subgroup analysis by study region was conducted. Heterogeneity was comparatively low among studies published in Asia, while it increased among those from non-Asian regions (Fig. 6).

Subgroup analysis of MA (adjusted for confounding factors)

Subgroup analysis of MA (by region of publication)

Outcome 3: Vascular lesions

This outcome was reported in three studies involving 1,076 patients. The heterogeneity analysis revealed: I²=0.00%, P = 0.67; hence, a fixed-effects model was employed. The pooled results showed that among individuals with IgAN, the risk of adverse kidney outcomes was markedly higher in the group with vascular lesions compared with the control group (HR = 2.27; 95% CI: 1.78, 2.91; P < 0.001), as illustrated in Fig. 7.

Forest plot for vascular lesions

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was implemented to appraise the stability of the meta-analysis findings. The result for the group with vascular lesions was not robust (Fig. 8C). By contrast, the results for the other groups remained stable (Fig. 8A and B). All results are presented in Fig. 8.

Sensitivity analysis of (A) arteriosclerotic lesions; (B) MA; (C) any vascular lesion

Publication bias

To evaluate latent publication bias in the meta-analysis, Egger’s test and funnel plots were employed for outcome groups that were reported in ten or more studies. For the group with arteriosclerotic lesions, the funnel plot was asymmetrical. In combination with the Egger’s test result (P < 0.001), publication bias might be observed (Fig. 9A). The stability of the results of publication bias was examined utilizing the trim-and-fill method. It was revealed that, after adjustment, arteriosclerotic lesions in IgAN remained an influencing factor for adverse kidney outcomes (Fig. 9B), suggesting that the observed publication bias did not alter robustness.

Funnel plot for (A) arteriosclerotic lesions; (B) arteriosclerotic lesions (after trim-and-fill)

Meta-regression

Meta-regression was conducted based on several variables (sample size, publication year, mean age, and sex proportion) to investigate possible sources of the observed heterogeneity. The meta-regression revealed that for arteriosclerotic lesions, the regression result for sex proportion reached statistical significance (P = 0.001), suggesting that the proportion of males/females might be a source of the noted heterogeneity. Regarding all analyzed factors for MA and the other examined factors for arteriosclerotic lesions, none of the regression results were statistically significant (P > 0.05).

Discussion

Through a systematic literature search and meta-analysis, the present study ultimately included 16 high-quality cohort studies across 6 countries, encompassing a total of 8,862 IgAN patients, and for the first time systematically evaluated the impact of intrarenal vascular lesions (including arteriosclerotic lesions, microangiopathy, or any vascular lesions) on adverse kidney outcomes in IgAN patients. The findings demonstrated that arteriosclerotic lesions, microangiopathy, and any vascular lesions all significantly increased the risk of adverse kidney outcomes in IgAN patients. The vast majority of the included studies had adjusted for confounding factors, and the pooled effect remained statistically significant, indicating that vascular lesions are associated with adverse kidney outcomes in IgAN.

This study identified arteriosclerotic lesions as an risk factor for adverse kidney outcomes in IgAN (HR = 1.95; 95%CI: 1.47, 2.60; P < 0.001), which was consistent with the findings of multiple included original studies. Subgroup analysis confirmed that after adjusting for confounding factors including age, blood pressure, proteinuria, baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and MEST-C score, the adjusted HR remained statistically significant, suggesting that the prognostic impact of arteriosclerotic lesions is independent of traditional clinical indicators and the MEST-C score. Notably, studies by Huang et al. [34] and Ohashi et al. [38] both found that moderate-to-severe arteriosclerosis remained an independent risk factor for end-stage renal disease (ESRD) in the normotensive IgAN subgroup. This suggests that intrarenal arteriosclerosis may not merely be a secondary change of hypertension, but itself is involved in the progression of IgAN. However, the conclusions of this study were inconsistent with those of Cai et al. [24], who reported that renal arteriosclerosis was not a risk factor for IgAN progression. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in baseline characteristics of the study populations, pathological diagnostic criteria for arteriosclerotic lesions, and follow-up durations across studies. Furthermore, meta-regression analysis in the present study revealed that sex proportion was a significant source of heterogeneity in arteriosclerotic lesion studies, and the underlying mechanism may be associated with gender-based differences in hormone levels, vascular endothelial function, and comorbidity distribution among patients, which warrants further investigation.

Microangiopathy (MA) was also identified as an risk factor for adverse kidney outcomes in IgAN (HR = 1.98; 95%CI: 1.54, 2.56). Subgroup analysis showed that the HR remained statistically significant after confounding factor adjustment, indicating that the prognostic effect of MA is also independent of clinical indicators and the MEST-C score. Heterogeneity was relatively low among relevant studies conducted in Asia, but increased in non-Asian regions, which may be related to regional differences in genetic background, clinical diagnosis and treatment strategies for IgAN, and the level of risk factor control among populations. In addition, no obvious publication bias was observed for microangiopathy in this study, suggesting that the prognostic impact of MA on IgAN may be more stable, and its clinical value as a prognostic biomarker for IgAN deserves further exploration.

Since some included studies did not clearly distinguish the types of intrarenal vascular lesions, we also conducted an analysis of any vascular lesions. The results showed that any vascular lesion was associated with adverse kidney outcomes in IgAN (HR = 2.27; 95%CI: 1.78, 2.91). This finding suggests that IgAN patients with any vascular lesion identified by pathological examination should be classified as a high-risk population, and more aggressive interventional measures should be promptly implemented. However, sensitivity analysis indicated that this result lacked robustness, which was mainly limited by the small number of included studies (only 3 studies, all conducted in Asia). The robustness of this conclusion thus requires verification by more large-sample studies.

From a pathological mechanism perspective, atherosclerotic lesions and MA exacerbate glomerulosclerosis and interstitial fibrosis by collectively altering renal hemodynamics, mediating the damage to endothelial cells, activating the complement system, and inciting inflammation. This synergistic action ultimately accelerates the progression of kidney dysfunction and the onset of ESRD [39–41]. The occurrence of IgAN-arteriosclerosis (IgAN-AS) is possibly associated with factors such as complement system activation, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammatory cell infiltration [40–42]. Thickening of the blood vessel wall and stenosis of the lumen directly reduce blood perfusion and oxygen supply to the tubulointerstitial area, creating a localized ischemic or hypoxic microenvironment that promotes cell apoptosis and tubulointerstitial fibrosis [40]. Stenosis of arteries and arterioles raises afferent arteriolar resistance and reduces glomerular perfusion, leading to elevated intraglomerular capillary pressure and a focal hyperfiltration state, which in turn impairs renal autoregulation, causes persistent podocyte damage and accelerates glomerulosclerosis [30, 40, 43]. The occurrence of IgAN-microangiopathy (IgAN-MA) is primarily associated with endothelial cell injury mediated by immune complexes, inflammatory responses, and abnormal activation of the complement system. Circulating galactose-deficient IgA1 (Gd-IgA1) immune complexes deposit along the walls of glomerular capillaries, which directly induces damage to vascular endothelial cells and triggers inflammatory responses. This leads to mesangial cell proliferation, extracellular matrix accumulation, and crescent formation, thereby accelerating glomerulosclerosis [44–46]. The deposition of Gd-IgA1-containing circulating complexes also causes aberrant activation of the complement system [39], compromising the integrity of the walls of glomerular capillaries. This results in widening of the subendothelial space, capillary occlusion, coagulation disorders, and promotes platelet aggregation and microthrombus formation, exacerbating renal ischemia and nephron loss [46].

Certain limitations should be clarified in this study. First, although the search strategy was not limited to cases with vascular lesions, the inclusion of terms as IgAN and vascular lesion in the same search can potentially lead to a retrieval bias, potentially enriching the influence of studies with emphasis on vascular lesions. Second, Most included studies clearly excluded secondary IgA nephropathy; nevertheless, two studies had ambiguous exclusion criteria [35, 36], and one further enrolled patients with IgA vasculitis [37], which may serve as a potential source of heterogeneity. Third, TMA, acute endothelial injury and chronic onion skin lesions were collectively defined as microangiopathy in the present study. Since the included studies did not separately report the prognostic value of these three subtypes in IgA nephropathy, we could not perform further stratified analysis. The undifferentiated grouping may bring potential heterogeneity, which should be recognized as a limitation of this study. Fourth, heterogeneity was noted in studies reporting arteriosclerotic lesions. Sex composition was a possible source of heterogeneity; however, it could not be ruled out that there were other unidentified factors. Second, publication bias was observed in studies providing data on atherosclerotic lesions. Although after trim-and-fill, this bias failed to influence the robustness of the results, it might reduce the credibility of the conclusions or lead to an overestimation of the prognostic value of atherosclerotic lesions. Fifth, the majority of the included studies were sourced from Asia, which might be related to the high incidence rate of IgAN among Asian individuals. Additional studies are warranted to corroborate the generalizability of the conclusions to populations in other regions such as Europe, America, and Latin America. Sixth, relevant quantitative data were not reported in the included studies, precluding further analysis of the associations between vascular lesions and the MEST-C score as well as clinical parameters (e.g., proteinuria levels, blood pressure levels, uric acid levels), and such analyses remain to be conducted in subsequent research. Seventh, this study did not perform pathological grading and severity stratification of renal vascular lesions. Relevant data regarding the extent of vascular injury were unavailable in the included literature, precluding further quantitative assessment and subgroup analysis. Furthermore, chronic vascular injury in IgA nephropathy is vulnerable to external interference from systemic comorbidities, which may influence the progression of renal vascular lesions. The present study failed to take into account the impacts of chronic systemic diseases on vascular lesions. In summary, the credibility of the findings in this study might be influenced by the aforementioned factors. In the future, additional prospective studies with a large sample size and based on multicenter data are warranted to mitigate the effects of publication bias and heterogeneity.

This study, through extensive literature retrieval, comprehensively and systematically integrated current evidence on the association between vascular lesions and adverse kidney outcomes among individuals suffering from IgAN. The findings clarify the link between vascular lesions and poor kidney prognosis of IgAN, providing a novel pathological biomarker for early assessing the risk stratification of IgAN in clinical settings. For IgAN patients presenting with vascular lesions, early and active targeted intervention strategies should be implemented, with regular monitoring of relevant risk indicators and enhanced comprehensive management to delay IgAN progression.

Conclusion

This study, through a comprehensive search and synthesis of all relevant research, quantitatively evaluates the association between renal vascular lesions for adverse kidney outcomes in IgAN using meta-analysis. The findings indicate that patients suffering from IgAN with vascular lesions are confronted with a heightened risk of experiencing poor kidney outcomes, and After adjusting for available confounding factors, vascular lesions are associated with poor renal prognosis in patients with IgAN. It is hoped that, in the future, more research will concentrate on the function of vascular lesions in IgAN progression. Additional clinical research with a large sample size and based on multicenter data is warranted to further clarify the significance of vascular lesions and their association with poor kidney outcomes.
