Study on the Suppression of Vitrinite Reflectance: A Thermal Simulation Experiment
Authors: Hui Zhang, Weibo Zhao, Huaichang Wang, Huijuan Guo, Ping’an Peng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractVitrinite reflectance is the most widely used parameter for reconstructing the thermal history of sedimentary basins and evaluating the maturation of source rocks. However, suppression of vitrinite reflectance has also been reported, which could affect the accuracy of evaluating the degree of thermal evolution. In this article, the influence of hydrocarbon generation on vitrinite reflectance during thermal evolution is studied based on thermal simulation experiments in a closed system. The results show that hydrocarbon absorption and overpressure can lead to the suppression of vitrinite reflectance. For R0 values between 0.6% and 2.1%, the suppression of vitrinite reflectance is primarily attributed to the impregnation of the telocollinite texture with hydrocarbons generated from type I kerogen. At R0 values exceeding 2.1%, overpressure becomes the dominant cause of the anomalous reflectance. Under closed-system conditions, the retention of volatile products within the pore network of vitrinite hinders the structural reorganization, leading to reflectance suppression.

Introduction

Vitrinite reflectance is the most widely used parameter for reconstructing the thermal history of sedimentary basins and evaluating the maturation of source rocks.1−10 According to extensive literature reviews11,12 temperature and time are considered the main factors influencing the increase in vitrinite reflectance, which follows first-order kinetics.13 However, over the last few decades, many researchers have recognized the suppression of vitrinite reflectance,14−22 where lower-than-expected reflectance values are observed in the profile throughout a stratigraphic sequence.

Three primary explanations for the suppression of vitrinite reflectance have been proposed: (1) high liptinite content in the kerogen or bitumen adsorption into the vitrinite of coal.14,21,23,24 Chen et al. indicated that reflectance suppression is positively correlated with alginite content, liptinite content, H content, and atomic H/C ratio in the lacustrine shales of the Paleogene Shahejie formation of the Dongying Depression in eastern China.21 Hutton and Cook suggested that reflectance was suppressed when alginite content exceeded 10%, with reflectance suppression increasing as alginite content increased.14 Carr found that significant suppression was observed in the presence of high liptinite (>20%) concentrations.25 Murchison et al. found that approximately 20% (by volume) liptinite was required to produce suppression.26 Petersen and Vosegerau showed that initial suppression can be observed in samples with as little as 3% resinite, although significant suppression was noted at 24% (by volume) resinite (total liptinite of 35 vol. %).27 Peters et al. found that the suppression of reflectance was most significant in mixtures with a 90:10 ratio of oil-prone kerogen to coal at comparatively low hydrous pyrolysis temperatures.24 (2) Initial hydrogen-rich of the vitrinitic precursor material.15,28−32 Perhydrous vitrinite forms under anoxic conditions in marine sediments, where anaerobic degradation of the sediment produces vitrinite with high hydrogen contents, which in turn suppresses vitrinite reflectance. Fang and Jianyu and Gentzis and Goodariz attributed the suppression of reflectance in some Chinese and Canadian wells to the higher hydrogen content in vitrinite produced during early diagenesis.28,33 Petersen and Rosenberg studied the suppression of reflectance in Middle Jurassic high-volatile bituminous coals of low liptinite content from the Danish North Sea, and concluded that reflectance suppression was related to the composition of vitrinite.31 (3) Some studies suggested that overpressure retarded maturation.34,35 Fang showed that the vitrinite reflectance in overpressured Neogene sediments in the Yinggehai Basin of the South China Sea was significantly lower than in hydrostatic wells with similar thermal histories.36 Carr suggested that the abnormality caused by overpressure arises from the retention of volatiles within the vitrinite molecular structure, which prevents the molecular reorganization necessary to produce higher reflectance values.37

The works mentioned above focused on analyzing and summarizing the variation in vitrinite reflectance across many samples in a vitrinite reflectance profile. Some researchers have used hydrous pyrolysis experiments to investigate the suppression of vitrinite reflectance in liptinite-rich rocks. Huang heated vitrinite-rich lignite in seawater over a temperature range of 250–500 °C in a confined-pressure system and found that the evolution of vitrinite reflectance mainly depended on the original hydrogen content of the precursor materials, other diagenetic factors such as water pressure, fluid chemistry, fluid flow, the presence of crude oil or type I organic matter and methane or CO2 partial pressures, played only minor roles in the evolution of vitrinite reflectance.38 Peters et al. created artificial rock mixtures composed of silica, Class G Portland cement, vitrinite-rich coal, and liptinite-rich kerogen, which were subjected to hydrous pyrolysis for 72 h at isothermal temperatures of 300, 330, and 350 °C.24 They attributed the suppression of reflectance to free radicals generated during the maturation of liptinite, which slowed the aromatization and rearrangement of the vitrinite structure.24 However, a limitation of these studies is that the experiments were performed at temperatures ranging from 250 to 450 °C, without considering the effects of generated hydrocarbons and overpressure on reflectance values at higher temperatures, from 450 to 600 °C. In this paper, artificial mixture samples were prepared using quartz powders, type I kerogen, and telocollinite. The artificial mixture samples were subjected to a closed-system high-pressure thermal simulation experiment over the temperature range of 250–600 °C to study the influence of bitumen impregnation and volatile matter remaining within the pore network of the vitrinite on vitrinite reflectance.

Samples and Methods

Samples

Coal samples were collected from the Middle Jurassic Xishanyao formation in the Meiyaogou section in the Turpan-Hami basin. Vitrinite is the main maceral in these coal samples, and it was purified through handpicking. Microscopic study shows that vitrinite purity reaches up to 99%. The Jurassic vitrinite used in this study has a low maturity level and relatively high hydrogen content, primarily composed of telocollinite (Tables 1 and 2). Additionally, Cretaceous oil shale with type I kerogen and low maturity was collected from Italy (Tables 2).

The purified telocollinites were crushed to pass through an 80-mesh sieve, while the oil shale was crushed to pass through a 200-mesh sieve. The oil shale was treated with HCL/HF and washed with distilled water to prepare the kerogen. The prepared kerogens were then extracted using a MAB solvent (methanol: acetone: benzene = 1:2.5:2.5). The extracted kerogens were used to prepare the mixtures. These samples consisted of 10–15 mg of type I kerogen and 2030 mg of −telocollinite in a 1:2 ratio (by weight). They were separated from each other by quartz powders of 15–20 mg, with the telocollinite placed at the bottom of gold tubes.

Pyrolysis Experiments

The thermal pyrolysis experiments were conducted at the State Key Laboratory of Organic Geochemistry in the Guangzhou Institute of Geochemistry, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The experiments were performed using a pressurized apparatus. Under an argon gas atmosphere, telocollinite samples (referred to as S-1) weighing 20–30 mg, and the mixture samples (S-2) were sealed into 14 gold tubes (60 mm × 4 mm i.d.). These tubes were separately placed into 14 stainless steel autoclaves, which were then positioned inside a single pyrolysis oven. The heat-circulating fan at the bottom of the oven ensures that the temperature difference among the autoclaves is less than 1 °C. Water is added to the autoclaves through a high-pressure pump to apply pressure to the samples. All autoclaves are connected in parallel, ensuring that the pressure in each autoclave is identical. By adjusting the water samples, all the gold tubes are maintained at a constant confining pressure of 50 MPa, with an error of less than 1 MPa. The autoclaves were rapidly heated to 250 °C within 10 h, and then heated to the target temperatures at two heating rates of 20 and 2 °C/h. Fourteen temperature points were programmed from 288 to 600 °C for each heating rate. Once the designated temperature was reached, the autoclaves were removed from the oven and rapidly cooled to room temperature by being quenched in water.

Telocollinite in residual mixture sample was separated and designated as S-2. Finally, the samples of S-1 and S-2 were divided into two parts: one part was used to measure element content, while the other part was extracted with chloroform to remove hydrocarbons adsorbed on the surface of the kerogens, and then prepared for vitrinite reflectance measurement.

Organic Petrology and Elemental Compositions

Microscopic observations were performed using a Leica DMR XP microscope under both white (reflected and transmitted) and ultraviolet light. Random vitrinite measurements (Rr expressed in %) were conducted following the standard International Commission of Coal Petrology (ICCP) procedures on polished sections of telocollinite using an oil immersion objective (×125).

The EL III element analyzer was used to measure the carbon, hydrogen, and nitrogen contents of the nonextracted parts of samples S-1 and S-2.

The atomic H/C ratios of samples from both the S-1 and S-2 series pyrolyzed under the two heating rates decreased with increasing simulation temperature (Figure 1). Elemental Compositions and R0 Values of the S-1 and S-2 Series Samples

Atom H/C ratios of samples including the S-1 and S-2 series pyrolyzed under heating rates of 2 °C/h (a) and 20 °C/h (b).

During the temperature period of 313–430 °C at a heating rate of 2 °C/h and 335–456 °C at a heating rate of 20 °C/h, the H/C ratio in the S-2 series was higher than the H/C ratio in the S-1 series. During this period, the R0 values ranged between 0.6% and 1.8%, with hydrocarbon products predominantly consisting of C6+.39,40 The C6+ hydrocarbons generated from type I kerogen could impregnate the telocollinite texture in the mixture samples (S-2 series). Coals exhibit an aperture-cavity type of “porosity”.41 The presence of these apertures and cavities can facilitate the impregnation of hydrocarbons generated from type I kerogen.

In the overmature stage, hydrocarbons previously impregnated into the telocollinite texture were subjected to severe secondary cracking, breaking down into smaller molecular hydrocarbons. The amount of light hydrocarbons, especially methane, gradually increases and maybe expelled from the telocollinite. The varying sizes of apertures and cavities in coals can sieve and trap molecules of specific sizes: small molecules (including gases) are sieved by coal micropores with diameters in the 0.4–1.2 nm range.42−46 Under the high pressure system, some products remain in the gas phase within the pore network of the telocollinite or the gold tubes. Given that the typical diameter of a methane molecule is 0.20–0.36 nm,47 which is smaller than the diameter of micropores in coal, light hydrocarbons, especially methane, are expelled from the telocollinite when the gold tubes are pierced. As a result, there is minimal difference in H/C ratios betwen the two sample series.

Vitrinite Reflectances of Pyrolyzed Samples of the S-1 and S-2 Series

The R0 values of pyrolyzed samples of the S-1 and S-2 series are shown in Table 3 and Figure 2. The evolution trends of R0 values for the pyrolyzed samples from both series are similar under the two heating rates, with R0 values increasing as the simulative temperature rises (Figure 2). The R0 values for the S-2 series are lower than those for the S-1 series, with the difference being more significant at a heating rate of 2 °C/h (Figure 2).

Relationship between temperature and vitrinite reflectance (R0) for the S-1 and S-2 series at the heating rates of 2 °C/h (a) and 20 °C/h (b).

As shown in Figure 1, temperatures of 450 °C under the low heating rate and 480 °C under the high heating rate is an interface where differences in atomic H/C ratios between the two samples become apparent. In terms of vitrinite reflectance suppression, 450 °C at the low heating rate and 480 °C at the high heating rate also represent a transition point in the suppressed mechanisms of vitrinite reflectance. In this study, the initial H content of telocollinite is 4.72 wt %, which is lower than that of the vitrinite used by Petersen and Rosenberg31 and results in suppressed reflectance values. Liu found that the yield of liquid hydrocarbons from telocollinite is 32 mg/g, with the pyrogenous products characterized by a low proportion of aliphatic hydrocarbons and a high proportion of hydroxybenzene.48 On the basis of coal-forming environments and the genesis of macerals, Zhao classified the coal in the Turpan-Hami basin into four distinct facies: dry forest swamp, moist forest swamp, running water swamp, and open water swamp.49 The coal with high vitrinite content, predominantly consisting of telinite and telocollinite, is classified as originating from a moist forest swamp, deposited in a weakly oxidizing and weakly reducing environment. The hydrocarbon generation potential and HI value of the Cretaceous oil shale are 52.5 mg/g rock and 709 mg/g TOC, respectively. The H content and H/C ratio of the kerogen sample extracted from the oil shale sample are 6.81 and 1.22, respectively (Table 2), showing excellent hydrocarbon generation potential. Combining this with Figure 1, we suggest that at temperatures below 450 °C with a low heating rate or 480 °C with a high heating rate, vitrinite reflectance suppression is mainly due to liquid hydrocarbons generated from type I kerogen impregnating the telocollinite texture. For example, when the temperature rises from 337.5 to 434.1 °C under the low heating rate, the reflectance suppression of S-2 samples ranges from 0.138% to 0.404%. Lewan observed a reflectance suppression trend in 300–360 °C isothermal 72-h hydrous pyrolysis experiments for oil-prone samples from the Phosphoria, Woodford, Alum, and Mowry formations.50 Peters et al. found that the reflectance for the K90 samples (90:10 kerogen to coal) from 300 to 350 °C is distinct from that of K0 samples (0:100 kerogen to coal), showing a suppression range from 0.14% to 0.22%.24

When the temperature exceeds 450 °C under the low heating rate or 480 °C under the high heating rate, the abnormality in vitrinite reflectance may be related to overpressure. In hydrostatic conditions, the fluids produced by maturation and hydrocarbon generation are expelled from the solid kerogen into the porous systems in both the kerogen and the rock.51 Under closed-system conditions, the amount of light hydrocarbons generated, particularly methane, gradually becomes dominated with increasing maturation. However, under overpressure conditions, volatile products (water/hydrocarbons) escape from the solid vitrinite and still remain in a gas phase either within the pore network of the vitrinite or within the gold tubes. The retention of these volatiles in the vitrinite prevents the reorganization of the vitrinite structure. Without this reorganization, the reflectance cannot increase.52 Unless the volatiles generated by the reaction are removed from the vitrinite, further reactions are restricted, leading to suppressed vitrinite reflectance. Davis and Spackman (1964) found that pyrolysis of cypress wood (a vitrinite precursor) at temperatures and pressures up to 400 °C and 68 000 psi, respectively, resulted in the retention of volatiles within the vitrinite structure.53 Cecil et al. observed that high pressures retarded the maturation of cypress peat.54 Sajgo et al. studied retardation of vitrinite reflectance from bituminous coals pyrolyzed under elevated pressures (maximum 143 000 psi).55

Kinetic Parameters of Vitrinite Reflectance Evolution in the S-1 and S-2 Samples

In this study, the kinetic analysis of vitrinite reflectance evolution was based on the first-order reaction model. Kinetic parameters were calculated using the software KINTICS2000 according to the method described by Liu and Tang.56 The kinetic parameters of vitrinite reflectance evolution are listed in Figure 3.

Kinetic parameters of vitrinite reflectance evolution in samples S-1 (a) and S-2 (b).

A comparison of the kinetic parameters between the two series revealed that both the main activation energy and the corresponding frequency factor are higher in sample S-2 (77 kcal·mol–1 and 1.81 × 1015 s–1) compared to those in S-1 (67 kcal·mol–1 and 5.09 × 1014 s–1), indicating that hydrocarbon generated from type I kerogen in sample S-2 may reduce the reaction rates, thereby hindering the increase in vitrinite reflectance.

Vitrinite Reflectance Evolution in Geological Conditions

Kinetics parameters (activation energy and frequency factor) obtained from kerogen pyrolysis experiments can be leveraged to extrapolate geological conditions, enabling the simulation and reconstruction of kerogens’ generation history over geological time scales.56−61 Based on the kinetic parameters acquired from the experiment of vitrinite reflectance evolution, we can simulate and reproduce the geochemical processes of vitrinite reflectance evolution under geological conditions.

The cumulative conversion rate under a known paleo-geothermal history can be obtained based on the information about the thermal history of the studied strata. Thermal history analysis of the Futan 1 well in the southern Ordos Basin reveals rapid subsidence at the end of the Early Permian (270 Ma), accompanied by a corresponding increase in paleogeothermal temperature. This, coupled with a subsequent Early Cretaceous tectonic thermal event, led to peak paleogeothermal temperatures of approximately 220 °C within the Carboniferous strata by the end of the Early Cretaceous (100 Ma). Since 100 Ma (Early Cretaceous), the Ordos Basin has experienced substantial uplift and denudation, leading to a decline in the geothermal gradient and a consequent decrease in the temperature of the Carboniferous strata to approximately 110 °C (Figure 4). The cumulative conversion rates are calculated on the basis of kinetics parameters of samples S-1 and S-2, and then are converted to R0 values. Based on the above parameters, the evolution characteristic of R0 under geological conditions was proposed (Figure 5). For comparison, EasyRo values are also calculated under the same geological conditions according to Sweeney and Burnham.62 At 128 Ma (R0 = 2.9%), the R0 values estimated using kinetic parameters from sample S-1 closely align with EasyRo values but exhibit a discernible elevation compared to those derived from kinetic parameters of sample S-2 (Figure 5). The difference may result from overpressure. Between 242 Ma and 128 Ma (0.6% ≤ R0 < 2.9%), the evolution trends of the three curves are distinct; the R0 values are the highest in S-1. This is related to the organic maceral composition of the studied samples. Hydrogen-rich samples are prone to generate oil, and finally lead to R0 abnormity. An initial atom H/C ratio of 0.9 was used in EasyRo. According to the equation: wt % C = 1200/[12+(H/C)+ 16(O/C)]–1.5, we can get an initial H content of 4.8 with an initial atom O/C ratio of 0.35 (Burnham and Sweeney, 1989).13 Compared with S-1, these values are higher, which agrees with lower reflectance values. S-2, a mixture sample of type I kerogen and telocollinite, shows that alginite-enriched kerogen tends to produce liquid hydrocarbons, and the the impregnation of these liquid hydrocarbon products into telocollinite results in low reflectance values. As a result, reflectance values in S-2 are lower than those in S-1. Between 242 Ma and 160 Ma (0.6% ≤ R0 < 2.1%), the difference in R0 values between S-2 and EasyRo indicates that the amount of hydrocarbon migrating into telocollinite is limited. Thismigration is controlled by the hydrocarbon generation potential of the source rock and the diffusion efficiency. In this study, the extent of reflection suppression is mainly influenced by the proportion of type I kerogen, thickness, and granularity of quartz. Between 160 Ma and 128 Ma (2.1% ≤ R0 < 2.9%), EasyRo values are close to those from sample S-2, but visibly lower than those from sample S-1. The difference may be due to low hydrocarbon expulsion efficiency, which leads to overpressure, which ultimately results in lower reflectance values.

Conclusions

Pressurized pyrolysis experiments involving vitrinite and/or type I kerogen from oil shale, coupled with vitrinite reflectance measurements, were conducted to elucidate the mechanism of reflectance suppression. The results indicate that hydrocarbon absorption and overpressure are key factors. When R0 falls between 0.6% and 2.1%, suppression is primarily attributed to hydrocarbons generated from type I kerogen impregnating into the telocollinite texture. At higher R0 (>2.1%), overpressure becomes the dominant cause of anomalous R0 values. In a closed system, the retention of volatile products within the pore network of the vitrinite hinders the structural reorganization, leading to reflectance suppression.
