Barriers and facilitators to adherence to home-based pulmonary rehabilitation in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: a mixed-methods systematic review
Authors: Hui Zhou, Changliang Li, Fangyuan Niu, Wenwen Liu, Xing Qiu, Guoli Yang, Honglian Zhang, Jin Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, and home-based pulmonary rehabilitation (HBPR) programs offer a promising non-pharmacological therapy. However, patient adherence to HBPR programs is far from satisfactory. This systematic review aims to synthesize existing evidence on the barriers and facilitators influencing adherence to HBPR programs in COPD patients from the perspective of healthcare professionals, family caregivers, and patients.MethodsA systematic review of quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods studies was undertaken using PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library, and CINAHL. Two independent researchers applied the eligibility criteria and extracted the data onto a predetermined form. The Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool was applied to assess the methodological quality of eligible studies. The data were synthesized using a convergent integration approach and categorized according to the COM-B dimensions (i.e., capability, opportunity, and motivation) and participant types (i.e., healthcare professionals, family caregivers, and COPD patients).ResultsA total of 21 studies (9 qualitative, 8 quantitative, and 4 mixed-methods studies) were included, involving 1444 COPD patients, 153 healthcare professionals, and 17 family caregivers. A total of 76 barriers and 73 facilitators were identified and mapped onto the COM-B model. For COPD patients, the most frequent facilitators were self-management skills (capability), perceived support from healthcare professionals (opportunity), and positive attitudes (motivation). The most frequent barriers were poor use of technical equipment (capability), lack of resources (opportunity), and psychological tensions (motivation). For healthcare professionals, professional competence (capability) and attitudes towards HBPR programs (motivation) were the most common barriers and facilitators, respectively. In the opportunity dimension, resources and collaboration with healthcare professionals were the most common barriers and facilitators, respectively. There was no consistent barrier/facilitator category for family caregivers.ConclusionThe introduction of HBPR programs for COPD patients necessitates a tailored approach that considers the patient’s capability, opportunity, and motivation to improve adherence to HBPR programs. The active engagement of stakeholders is pivotal in guiding the evolution of interventions and ensuring effective clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-03940-x.Keywords: Adherence, Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Home-based, Pulmonary rehabilitation, Systematic review

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a significant global health issue, contributing to over 3 million annual deaths and a rising disease burden, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [1–3]. Pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) is an inseparable component of COPD management that involves a combination of exercise training, education, and self-management intervention [4]. Evidence shows that PR programs help improve symptoms, exercise tolerance, and quality of life [5, 6], while also reducing exacerbations and hospitalizations [7]. However, adherence to PR programs is low, with rates ranging from 10% to 50% [8, 9]. Factors contributing to low adherence include health limitations, social support, transportation, and financial difficulties [10]. Previous interventions to improve PR adherence have included self-management education, physical activity counseling, nutritional supplementation, and gait aid strategies, with varying degrees of success [11]. Despite these efforts, adherence rates remain low.

Home-based pulmonary rehabilitation (HBPR), as a complement to traditional PR, has shown vast potential for application, especially during the COVID-19 pandemic [12]. HBPR programs retain the core elements of traditional PR and, by being implemented in the home setting, effectively address barriers such as transportation and cost [13]. In addition, research evidence suggests that HBPR programs are as effective as traditional PR programs in enhancing exercise tolerance and quality of life compared to usual care [14, 15]. However, HBPR programs pose unique challenges, such as reliance on mobile technology and the need for patient autonomy in managing their care [15, 16]. COPD patients with limited digital literacy or inadequate access to technology may struggle with engagement [17]. In particular, older adults, who comprise the majority of COPD patients, often encounter age-related difficulties in adopting digital tools, while individuals from lower socioeconomic backgrounds may face barriers such as unstable internet connectivity or financial constraints, further complicating adherence. Patients lacking self-management skills may also find it difficult to adhere without regular support from healthcare providers [18]. Furthermore, HBPR adherence is influenced by the interactions of multiple stakeholders, including family caregivers and healthcare professionals. Each of these stakeholders brings different preferences and values to the table, which complicates the issue of adherence [19]. These complexities underscore the urgent need for a comprehensive understanding of barriers and facilitators from multiple perspectives. Studies show that implementing evidence-based practice from a multi-stakeholder perspective can improve continuity and coordination of care, promote positive behavioral change in patients [20, 21]. However, the current understanding of HBPR adherence is fragmented, and a multi-stakeholder, theory-informed analysis of HBPR adherence is needed.

The Capability, Opportunity, Motivation, Behavior (COM-B) model posits that capability, opportunity, and motivation constitute the three essential prerequisites for behavioral occurrence. Behavioral change can only be achieved if an individual possesses the capacity and motivation to leverage opportunities [22]. Applying this model enables the systematic categorization of diverse factors influencing adherence, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding. Mersha et al. employed the COM-B model to identify patient-related factors associated with adherence to nicotine replacement therapy and revealed the significance of motivation and physical capability in facilitating treatment adherence [23]. Similarly, Yang et al. employed COM-B to explore exercise adherence in community-dwelling older adults, identifying 54 factors and proposing eight corresponding intervention strategies [24]. These applications demonstrate the model’s utility in synthesizing complex behavioral determinants and translating them into actionable recommendations. Consistent with the UK Medical Research Council’s (MRC) guidance that intervention design should be theory-driven [25], the COM-B model offers both explanatory power and practical relevance. However, no studies have yet applied the COM-B model to systematically analyze factors influencing adherence to HBPR programs, limiting the translation of review findings into specific clinical interventions.

To address this gap, we conducted a systematic review to identify and synthesize the barriers and facilitators influencing adherence to HBPR programs in COPD patients. Specifically, we examined these factors from the perspectives of healthcare professionals, family caregivers, and patients, and analyzed them within the framework of the COM-B model. Through this integrated perspective, we hope to deepen our understanding of clinical practice and provide a scientific basis for future research and intervention development.

Methods

Study design

This systematic review was conducted following the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology [26] and reported adhering to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement [27]. The protocol was registered within the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO CRD42024525338). In the initial PROSPERO registration, the population was described broadly, with a primary emphasis on patients with COPD. During the review process, it became evident that adherence to HBPR programs is influenced not only by patients but also by healthcare professionals and family caregivers, and several eligible studies specifically addressed these stakeholder groups. Therefore, studies involving patients, healthcare professionals, or family caregivers were included to ensure a comprehensive synthesis of the barriers and facilitators affecting HBPR adherence.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Based on relevant guidelines and previous studies, the inclusion and exclusion criteria were discussed, tested, and refined by the research team before the formal screening process. The PICo (Participants, Interests, and Context) framework was employed to structure the search [28] (Table 1). The systematic review encompassed qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods studies that focused on barriers and/or facilitators of HBPR adherence. Eligible studies were peer-reviewed, published in English, and presented original contributions to the field. HBPR programs were defined as an intervention where patients receive treatment at home, beyond the initial baseline and final evaluations of rehabilitation outcomes, including prescribed, structured exercise and at least one non-exercise component (educational content and/or psychological support), lasting a minimum of six weeks [14, 29]. Adherence was defined broadly as the extent to which patients engaged in HBPR programs, regardless of the specific measurement method.

Search strategy

PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and CINAHL databases were searched. Scopus and Google Scholar were used to identify potentially relevant studies through citation tracking, but did not yield eligible studies. The search strategy consisted of keywords such as, but not limited to, “chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)”, “home-based pulmonary rehabilitation”, “adherence”, “barriers”, and “facilitators”. The complete search strategy can be found in Supplementary Table 1. The search was designed to retrieve articles published from 1990, the year when PR was globally endorsed for the treatment of COPD [30], to June 2024.

Selection procedure

The data was managed using the Covidence software (Veritas Health Innovation, 2021), and a three-stage selection process was conducted. Following deduplication, two researchers (WWL and FYN) independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts for eligibility, thereby improving objectivity and accuracy. Any discrepancies were resolved by a third researcher (HZ) through arbitration.

Data extraction

A customized data extraction form was employed to systematically extract relevant information from the included studies. The data extraction was conducted independently by two researchers (WWL and FYN) to obtain information on the following aspects: the name of the principal author, publication year, study design, participant demographics, and the qualitative and quantitative outcomes presented. For qualitative studies, the extraction encompassed central themes, perceptions, experiences, as well as barriers and facilitators as reported by the participants, alongside any direct citations. Quantitative data extraction focused on descriptions of outcome measures, numerical outcome data presented in tables, and narrative summaries of findings. In the case of mixed-methods studies, the data were extracted separately for the qualitative and quantitative components. Any discrepancies in the data extraction process were adjudicated by a third researcher (HZ), ensuring a reliable and consistent approach.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT, 2018 version) [31], which is designed for evaluating qualitative, quantitative (including randomized controlled trials, non-randomized studies, and quantitative descriptive research), and mixed-methods studies. No additional bias assessment tool was employed, as MMAT minimized inconsistency and redundancy in the quality assessment process. For each study, the relevant MMAT criteria set was applied, which consists of five key questions rated as “Yes,” “No,” or “Can’t tell”. In line with the MMAT developers’ guidance, we did not calculate an overall numeric score. Instead, the quality appraisal results were presented descriptively, highlighting methodological strengths and limitations across studies. Two reviewers (WWL and FYN) independently assessed the methodological quality of the included studies, and any discrepancies were discussed and resolved. All studies underwent inclusion in our review, regardless of their quality assessment.

Data synthesis

A convergent integration approach was adopted, aligning with the methodology recommended by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) [32] and tailored to accommodate the COM-B model as our theoretical framework [22]. The synthesis process commenced with the extraction of quantitative data, which were subsequently translated into narrative descriptions to facilitate integration with qualitative findings. Qualitative data were then subjected to inductive thematic analysis following the guidelines set by Braun and Clarke [33]. The process involved familiarisation with the data, initial coding, and theme development. Then, the finalized themes were deductively mapped onto the COM-B model, including its six sub-dimensions, to systematically interpret how capability, opportunity, and motivation influence adherence behavior. Two reviewers (XQ and GLY) independently coded the data, and disagreements were discussed and resolved by consensus by the whole team throughout the process.

The identified barriers and facilitators were mapped onto the COM-B model to understand the complex interplay of capabilities, opportunities, and motivations that influence adherence behaviors. This process was done in collaboration with the review team; the generated themes were discussed, reflected upon, and refined, and the original articles were referenced where necessary.

Result

Study selection

The initial search identified a total of 6405 potentially relevant studies. Upon removal of 1444 duplicates, the remaining titles and abstracts were screened, resulting in the exclusion of 4722 studies. This process left 239 articles eligible for full-text review, in line with the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. After a thorough evaluation of these texts, 218 articles were further excluded, culminating in a final selection of 21 studies for inclusion in this review [9, 19, 34–52] (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

This review encompassed 21 studies conducted between 2008 and 2023, including 9 qualitative studies, 8 quantitative studies, and 4 mixed-methods studies. Geographically, the studies covered 11 countries, including the USA, Italy, China, France, Norway, the UK, Spain, Australia, India, Denmark, and Iran, illustrating a broad international interest and diverse contextual insights into HBPR programs. The vast majority of the studies (19 out of 21) focused on COPD patients, encompassing a total of 1444 patients. Fewer studies involved healthcare professionals (6 out of 21) and family caregivers (2 out of 21), involving 153 healthcare professionals and 17 family caregivers, respectively. The duration of the HBPR programs reported in these studies varied, from as short as 6 weeks to as long as 48 months, with an average length of approximately 8 weeks. 16 studies reported the methods of delivery of HBPR programs, including telemedicine technology, digital health platforms, written materials, and face-to‐face sessions. Limited studies have reported adherence rates for HBPR programs, with a range of 40.7–90.5% and a median adherence rate of 59.0% (Table 2).

Synthesis of results

There were 76 barriers and 73 facilitators retrieved and synthesized into 6 core themes and 28 subthemes of the COM-B model (Fig. 2; Table 3), which were presented from the perspectives of COPD patients, family caregivers, and healthcare professionals. COPD patients perspective

Capability

19 barriers and 15 facilitators were extracted and categorized into: (1) physical capability, including physical performance; (2) psychological capability, including knowledge, comprehension, use of technical equipment, and self-management skills.

Theme 1 “Physical capability” synthesized data from 7 studies and encompassed the subtheme of “physical performance”. Barriers to HBPR adherence within this theme included advanced age [34], diminished lung function [9, 34], the presence of multiple comorbidities [9, 34, 40, 52], frequent symptoms such as dyspnea and fatigue [9, 51], and the exacerbation of the condition during HBPR programs [9, 41]. On the facilitative side, patients possessing adequate baseline functional status and robust baseline exercise endurance were more inclined to adhere to and complete HBPR programs [34, 38].

Theme 2 “Psychological capability” emerged from 13 studies and was categorized into 3 subthemes: knowledge, comprehension, and use of technical equipment. The most frequently cited barriers were the subtheme “use of technical equipment”, where patients faced challenges operating modern communication devices like iPads and smartphones, as well as accessing relevant websites [36, 37, 41, 46, 47, 52]. Within the “knowledge” subtheme, barriers included a lack of health and disease-related knowledge [9, 34, 46]. The “comprehension” subtheme revealed barriers such as insufficient literacy skills and difficulties in selecting and applying diverse information and services related to rehabilitation [44, 47]. In contrast, the presence of facilitators within Theme 2 was characterized by patients with a higher educational level [48] and robust knowledge reserves [35], which facilitated their enhanced comprehension of the intervention content and modality [41, 46, 50]. The most frequently cited facilitator was self-management skills, with strategies such as encouraging patient participation in decision-making, promoting self-monitoring, and enhancing self-management, significantly enhancing patient adherence [34, 35, 37, 40, 47, 50, 52].

Opportunity

17 barriers and 24 facilitators were extracted and categorized into: (3) physical opportunity, including space and location, resource, time, and competing priorities; (4) social opportunity, including perceived support from healthcare professionals, peers, and family members.

Theme 3 “Physical opportunity”, synthesized from an analysis of 11 studies, identified barriers such as resource, time, and competing priorities. The most frequent barrier was resource and equipment-related issues such as inadequate equipment, unstable connections, and poor audiovisual quality, which negatively affected patients’ rehabilitation experiences [36, 37, 49]. Financial constraints also functioned to impede the availability of resources for adherence [34, 44, 52]. Time limitations and competing priorities presented additional challenges. Participants indicated that they required time to adjust to post-discharge rehabilitation activities and HBPR programs initiation [34, 45, 51], while social engagements, disease management, and pre-existing work commitments largely influenced their decision to participate in the HBPR programs [19, 40, 52]. In contrast, patients consistently asserted that the HBPR programs facilitated their adherence by offering flexible and convenient choices of space and location [40, 46, 49, 51, 52].

In Theme 4, entitled “Social opportunity”, the results of the 10 studies synthesized demonstrated a clear preference for reporting it as a facilitator, with the subthemes of perceived support from healthcare professionals, peers, and family members. The most frequent facilitator was “healthcare professionals support”, where regular communication, personalized guidance, and timely interventions established trust and understanding between patients and professionals during their interactions [35, 37, 40, 41, 46, 47, 49, 51, 52]. Furthermore, positive interactions and experience sharing from peers [34, 35, 40, 49], as well as emotional and material support provided by family members [40, 47], significantly enhanced patients’ motivation and commitment to recovery.

Motivation

17 barriers and 17 facilitators were identified and organized into (5) automatic processes, including psychological tensions, attitude towards HBPR programs, and preference of patients; (6) reflective processes, including action planning and perceived benefit.

Theme 5 “Automatic process” was synthesized from an analysis of 13 studies. The most frequent barrier was psychological tensions. In telerehabilitation, patients often experience psychological distress due to concerns about safety, where anxiety and depression are exacerbated by past negative experiences [9, 19, 42–44, 47]. Feelings of isolation and lack of encouragement further undermine their willingness and motivation to follow a rehabilitation program [51]. Moreover, negative attitudes about rehabilitation outcomes, such as anticipating a cessation of medication or medical visits post-rehabilitation, often lead to disappointment and a sense of failure [9, 19, 47]. The facilitators of this theme were identified as having positive attitudes towards HBPR programs and patient preferences. The most common facilitator was positive patient attitudes towards HBPR programs [46, 48–52]. Additionally, tailoring HBPR programs’ content to patient preferences, including personalized program adaptation, information support, and appropriate supervision [34, 42, 45, 50], also facilitated adherence.

Theme 6 “Reflective process” was analyzed by 7 studies, highlighting the impact of “action planning” and “perceived benefit”. Action planning could be a barrier if patients fail to routinize rehabilitation activities [49, 52]. Conversely, it promoted the formation of rehabilitation habits [34, 35, 40]. In addition, patients’ continued adherence was driven by their perception of symptom improvement; thus, a lack of perceived benefit resulted in reduced adherence [35, 37, 51, 52].

Healthcare professionals perspective

Capability

5 barriers and 4 facilitators were identified as theme 2 “psychological capability”, including knowledge and professional competence. No factors were identified as “physical capability”.

Theme 2 “Psychological capability” was derived from the analysis of 5 studies. Barriers were primarily associated with limitations in professional knowledge and competence [19, 43, 44]. These limitations were evidenced by a lack of practical experience in telerehabilitation, continuous patient assessment, and effective health education [19, 44, 45]. Additionally, patient adherence can be facilitated when healthcare professionals can develop new communication skills, personalize instructions, and refine rehabilitation plans [39, 44, 45].

Opportunity

10 barriers and 8 facilitators were identified and classified into theme 3 “physical opportunity” including resources, time, and competing priorities, and theme 4 “social opportunity” including support from patients and healthcare professionals.

Theme 3 “Physical opportunity” emerged from an analysis of 4 studies. The most frequent barrier was telerehabilitation resources. These included unequal geographic distribution, insufficient equipment, and inadequate staffing [39, 43, 44], but the availability of user-friendly and practical telemedicine systems and devices could enhance the delivery of HBPR programs [47].

Theme 4 “Social opportunity” came from an analysis of 6 studies. Healthcare professionals’ interaction was a barrier, as evidenced by limited time spent supervising, reduced effective feedback, and low frequency of interaction [19, 39, 45]. Conversely, the healthcare professionals’ cordial demeanor and empathetic tone of voice exemplified the role of a facilitator [43, 45, 47]. Collaboration with healthcare professionals was the most frequent facilitator, which ensured consistent and accurate information and education to patients [39, 43–45].

Motivation

4 barriers and 3 facilitators were identified as theme 6 “reflective process” including extrinsic rewards and attitude towards HBPR programs. No factors were identified as “automatic process”.

Theme 6 “Reflective process” derived from 4 studies, revealed that the absence of extrinsic rewards posed a barrier for healthcare professionals [44]. However, the provision of financial incentives and career advancement opportunities significantly facilitated their engagement [47]. Attitude towards HBPR programs functioned as both a barrier and a facilitator. Initially, there were negative perceptions towards HBPR programs [19, 39, 44], but these attitudes shifted positively when healthcare professionals witnessed its benefits to patients and themselves [43, 47].

Family caregivers perspective

Only two studies have addressed the perspectives of caregivers [19, 45], with findings predominantly highlighting barriers. Within theme 2 “psychological capability”, caregivers primarily faced barriers due to the limited availability of disease-related information [19], while the facilitator was more educational initiatives and skill-building training [45]. Theme 3 “physical opportunity” revealed negative impacts on caregivers from “time and competing priorities” [19], with no facilitative factors identified. In theme 4 “social opportunity”, a lack of familial support posed a barrier [19], yet the active involvement of caregivers in medical decision-making emerged as a facilitator [45]. Within the motivation dimension, caregivers were found to bear a considerable psychological burden [19], but no facilitative elements were reported for this dimension.

Discussion

The identified barriers and facilitators to HBPR adherence in COPD patients were complex and multifaceted. Across the capability, opportunity, and motivation dimensions of the COM-B model, for COPD patients, the most frequent facilitators were self-management skills, perceived support from healthcare professionals, and positive attitudes, respectively. The most frequent barriers were poor use of technical equipment, lack of resources, and psychological tensions, respectively. For healthcare professionals, professional competence and attitudes towards HBPR programs were the most common barriers and facilitators, respectively. In the opportunity dimension, resources and collaboration with healthcare professionals were the most common barriers and facilitators, respectively. Notably, the study found a dearth of information on barriers and facilitators from the perspective of family caregivers.

The capability dimension of the COM-B model is a prerequisite for behavior to occur. In the context of HBPR programs, patients’ self-management skills and proficiency in technical equipment operation are crucial for improving adherence. Evidence indicates educational programs significantly enhance self-management skills, improving glycemic control in type 2 diabetes patients [53]. Similarly, self-management support services provided by community pharmacists enhance symptom control, quality of life, and medication adherence in asthma patients [54]. For COPD patients, enhancing self-management skills not only improves HBPR adherence but also enhances overall quality of life and disease management, as supported by findings in this study. Although HBPR programs, like numerous other remotely delivered health interventions, rely on mHealth technology and telemedicine, their efficacy can be significantly diminished by the suboptimal utilization of these technological devices [15, 16]. Elderly patients and those with lower education levels face challenges in operating inhalers and remote monitoring devices, impacting treatment accuracy and adherence [55]. Complex user interfaces and cumbersome operational processes are predominantly responsible for these challenges [56].

Consistent with previous research, the professional competence of healthcare professionals plays a dual role in HBPR adherence. Sami et al. revealed that competence significantly influenced patient attitudes and behaviors, serving as a prerequisite for treatment acceptance and adherence [19]. Proficiency in their field enhances patient confidence in recovery and provides effective guidance. Conversely, deficiencies in expertise may lead to unmet needs, misinformation, and dissatisfaction, indirectly undermining patients’ ability to prevent complications [57].

In the context of HBPR programs for COPD patients, the availability of resources, patient-perceived social support, and collaboration among healthcare professionals constituted the framework of opportunities for patient adherence to HBPR programs. Economic, geographic, and medical resource constraints present the most frequent barriers to HBPR adherence. Financial constraints often prevent patients from acquiring necessary rehabilitation equipment, and when available, usability and ergonomic design issues further hinder their effectiveness [34, 44]. Disparities in healthcare resource allocation lead to inadequate specialized pulmonary rehabilitation services in certain regions, further constraining patients’ access to rehabilitation opportunities [58, 59].

In terms of social opportunities, our findings similarly indicate that perceived support from healthcare professionals is the most common facilitator for patients in adhering to HBPR programs. This support includes both expert medical advice and emotional encouragement. A qualitative study emphasized the importance of a comprehensive support system, individualized exercise prescriptions, and diligent monitoring by healthcare professionals to enhance home exercise adherence [60]. This correlates with previous evidence showing that patients who establish trust with their therapists perceive greater social and emotional support [61]. For healthcare professionals, collaboration with colleagues is a key facilitator. Research on a multidisciplinary treatment illustrated that patients may receive conflicting information from different healthcare professionals [62]. Although multiple insights are necessary, we emphasize the need for greater collaboration and communication between healthcare professionals to provide patients with ordered information [19, 63]. Moreover, interprofessional cooperation can create a supportive work environment and reduce workplace stress [64]. Our findings further reinforce the pivotal role of healthcare professionals not only as clinical experts but also as supportive allies and team collaborators.

Motivation plays a pivotal role in driving patient adherence to rehabilitation plans, with psychological tension and positive attitudes identified as significant factors in this study. Psychological tension, such as anxiety and depression, is a well-documented barrier to HBPR adherence among COPD patients, consistent with existing literature. Turan et al. demonstrated that the presence of depressive symptoms led to decreased treatment adherence in COPD patients [65]. Similarly, Koehorst et al. highlighted the impact of anxiety related to respiratory symptoms on medication adherence [66]. These emotional states not only reduce patients’ willingness to engage in rehabilitation programs but also influence their treatment responses [9, 67].

Conversely, positive attitudes significantly facilitate patient adherence. Both patients and healthcare professionals recognize the benefits of positive attitudes in promoting self-management and treatment adherence. Research on COPD patients showed that optimism and hope were associated with better self-care, promoting engagement in adaptive coping strategies and treatment adherence [68]. Moreover, positive attitudes were associated with higher quality of life [69] and lower healthcare costs [70]. Positive attitudes of healthcare professionals are also important to patient adherence. A meta-ethnographic study showed that patients were more likely to engage actively in rehabilitation programs when healthcare professionals exhibited supportive and optimistic attitudes [71]. Conversely, indifferent or skeptical attitudes may hinder effective patient interactions and diminish patient feedback on treatment concerns [72]. It can be seen that developing positive attitudes involves various dimensions, including the healthcare environment, patient education, and psychological support.

A notable deficiency in the existing literature on HBPR adherence is the lack of the perspective of the family caregiver. This gap may be attributed to an inadequate focus on the family caregiver’s experience and viewpoint. The roles of caregivers extend beyond day-to-day care to emotional support and supervision of rehabilitation programs [73]. Their involvement can greatly influence the patient’s self-management skills, thereby improving patient recovery and treatment adherence [74, 75]. However, family caregivers frequently report a lack of knowledge about disease management and rehabilitation strategies [19], as well as difficulties in balancing personal needs with caring responsibilities [76], which limits their ability to support patients. Furthermore, involvement in caregiver support groups has been demonstrated to enhance coping strategies and emotional well-being, while simultaneously reducing the burden and isolation [77]. Therefore, assessing caregivers’ needs, developing tailored education and training programs, and providing the necessary support (e.g., counseling and respite services) may not only enhance caregivers’ coping skills but also indirectly serve patients’ HBPR adherence.

The included studies showed heterogeneity in the composition, frequency, and duration of HBPR programs, as well as possible differences in the factors that influence HBPR adherence. For example, shorter, more intensive programs may increase physical and psychological demands, while longer programs could face challenges like maintaining motivation or addressing logistical issues over time. Differences in program frequency, such as daily versus weekly sessions, may also affect participation. Higher-frequency programs require more time management and coordination, particularly for patients with comorbidities or caregivers managing multiple responsibilities. These findings suggest the need for tailored strategies to address the specific barriers associated with different HBPR program designs. However, there is currently no consistent evidence provided on which components or models of care are associated with effective interventions [14]. Future research should further investigate how program structures impact adherence behaviors across various populations and settings.

Implications for practice and future research

This review provides a comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing adherence to HBPR programs among COPD patients and offers important implications for nursing practice. Despite the positive attitudes of healthcare professionals and patients toward HBPR programs, gaps remain in their understanding and ability to implement HBPR programs effectively. Nurses are often the primary providers of patient education, follow-up, and psychosocial support. Their unique role enables them to identify individual patient barriers, such as limited digital literacy, low self-management skills, or insufficient family support, and to deliver personalized interventions that balance feasibility, effectiveness, and acceptability. Existing evidence indicates that health coaching based on motivational interviewing can reduce hospital readmissions while enhancing patient engagement and satisfaction [78]. Regular mental health assessments and increased remote interaction with patients can alleviate psychological stress and support sustained engagement in HBPR programs [79]. These intervention approaches underscore the irreplaceable role of nurses in promoting patient self-management and continued engagement. Providing specialized training programs and strengthening nurses’ digital health skills further enhances their capacity to cope with complex adherence issues. Additionally, nurses can act as coordinators within interdisciplinary teams, ensuring continuity of care and facilitating effective communication among patients, caregivers, and other healthcare professionals.

Our study also underscores the diversity in barriers and facilitators perceived by different stakeholders. Best practices and models from the American Thoracic Society emphasize the need for multi-stakeholder collaboration [80]. While healthcare professionals shape patient behaviors, the involvement of family caregivers is crucial in home-based care. Research shows that caregiver-patient interactions significantly influence treatment outcomes [81], and including family caregivers in decision-making enhances disease management [82]. Therefore, patient adherence should be considered a shared responsibility among healthcare professionals, family caregivers, and patients [83]. Future research should increasingly focus on nursing-led interventions, particularly those systematically designed within behavior change frameworks such as COM-B, to enhance the replicability and theoretical rigor of behavior change strategies. In addition, studies should explore innovative, technology-supported, nurse-led HBPR models that integrate family caregiver participation and interdisciplinary collaboration. To address the current lack of caregiver data, family caregivers should also be systematically included in assessment phases, for example, by including caregiver-reported measures of burden, capacity, and support needs.

Strengths and limitations

A notable strength of our study was the systematic categorization of identified barriers and facilitators according to the COM-B model, providing a comprehensive exploration of factors influencing HBPR adherence. Another strength was the inclusion of COPD patients, healthcare professionals, and family caregivers, which distinguishes our study from previous research that often focuses solely on patient perspectives. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the scope of our study may be limited by the exclusion of non-English literature due to language barriers, resource constraints, and the predominance of relevant literature in English, which may affect the generalizability of our findings. Future studies could consider including literature in more languages to validate the findings of this study. Nevertheless, our inclusion of studies from 11 diverse countries enhances the breadth of our results. Secondly, while we comprehensively reviewed barriers and facilitators, we did not quantify their effect sizes or establish their relative significance. The frequency of mentions in the literature may not necessarily reflect the actual importance of these factors. Thirdly, our exploration of caregiver perspectives primarily relied on findings from two studies [19, 45], which may introduce bias when interpreting this perspective. Fourthly, while the convergent integration approach allows for combining quantitative and qualitative data, it can be challenging to translate data across types, potentially affecting consistency and comparability, and may introduce bias due to the subjective judgment required in data interpretation. Finally, there was considerable heterogeneity in HBPR program designs, which may affect the comparability and generalizability of the findings. Future research should further standardize intervention content.

Conclusions

Consistent with the COM-B model, the identified barriers and facilitators to HBPR adherence spanned capability, opportunity, and motivation dimensions and showed their multifaceted and interrelated nature. The inclusion of perspectives from COPD patients, family caregivers, and healthcare professionals deepens our understanding of the complex interactions among stakeholders. Our findings underline the necessity for future HBPR adherence interventions to be co-designed with all three stakeholder groups to effectively minimize barriers and leverage facilitators.
