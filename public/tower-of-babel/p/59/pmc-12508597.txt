Interstitial pregnancy after frozen embryo transfer in a woman with prior bilateral salpingectomy: A case report and literature review
Authors: Caterina Iaquinta, Ginevra Guarrera, Caterina De Luca, Carmela Votino, Alessandro Svelato, Fulvio Zullo, Roberta Venturella
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We describe a rare case of interstitial pregnancy following frozen embryo transfer in a woman with previous bilateral salpingectomy. The 33-year-old patient with a history of recurrent early pregnancy loss conceived after in vitro fertilization. Transvaginal ultrasound revealed a gestational sac with cardiac activity implanted in the interstitial portion of the left uterine cornua, surrounded by a thin myometrial mantle. Medical management with methotrexate was unsuccessful, and serum human chorionic gonadotropin levels continued to rise. The patient underwent laparoscopic cornual wedge resection using local infiltration, monopolar resection, and double-layer closure. The procedure was completed with minimal blood loss, an uneventful postoperative course, and preservation of uterine integrity and future fertility potential. The patient was discharged on the second postoperative day, and follow-up confirmed a steady decline in human chorionic gonadotropin levels. This case highlights that interstitial pregnancy may occur even after bilateral salpingectomy, underlining the importance of early ultrasound surveillance, timely surgical management, and preventive strategies such as complete cornual excision at salpingectomy and single embryo transfer to reduce risks and optimize reproductive outcomes.

Introduction

Interstitial pregnancy is a rare and life-threatening form of ectopic pregnancy in which the gestational sac implants in the interstitial portion of the fallopian tube, embedded within the myometrium of the uterine wall. While it accounts for only 2 %–4 % of all ectopic pregnancies, it is responsible for a disproportionately high percentage of maternal morbidity and mortality due to delayed diagnosis and catastrophic uterine rupture. The condition is particularly challenging to manage in women using assisted reproductive technologies (ART), especially after bilateral salpingectomy, where interstitial implantation may occur despite the surgical removal of fallopian tubes.

In this paper, we present a rare case of interstitial pregnancy after frozen embryo transfer (FET) in a woman with a history of bilateral salpingectomy and recurrent pregnancy loss. We then review the current literature regarding incidence, risk factors, diagnosis, management, and prognosis of interstitial pregnancies post-IVF in salpingectomized patients.

Case Presentation

A 33-year-old Italian woman (G7P0) with a history of five early pregnancy losses and one previous ectopic pregnancy was admitted to a tertiary referral center in southern Italy. Her past medical history was unremarkable, and she denied smoking, alcohol, or drug use. Six years earlier, she had undergone laparoscopic bilateral salpingectomy for a right tubal ectopic pregnancy with severe tubo-peritoneal adhesions involving both tubes. She subsequently underwent two unsuccessful IVF cycles.

Following a frozen embryo transfer (FET), the patient was referred with an initial diagnosis of an ectopic pregnancy at 6 weeks and 1 day. Transvaginal ultrasound revealed a gestational sac (15.5 × 14 mm) with a viable embryo (CRL 3.7 mm, consistent with 6 weeks) located in the interstitial portion of the left fallopian tube, separated from the endometrial cavity and surrounded by a thin myometrial layer (<5 mm), fulfilling Timor-Tritsch diagnostic criteria (Fig. 1). The endometrial thickness was 15 mm and the uterus showed signs of fibromatosis. No free pelvic fluid was observed. The patient was asymptomatic and hemodynamically stable. Given the early gestational age, absence of symptoms, and the patient's fertility desire, a conservative medical approach was initiated. She received intramuscular methotrexate (1 mg/kg) on days 1–3-5, alternating with folinic acid (0.1 mg/kg). However, serial β-hCG measurements revealed a progressive increase: 15295 mIU/mL (day 3), 17663 mIU/mL (day 5), and 18,156 mIU/mL (day 7). Ultrasound findings were unchanged, and cardiac activity persisted.

Due to treatment failure, laparoscopic left cornual resection was performed on day 9. At laparoscopy, the ectopic pregnancy was seen through the serosal surface of the uterus with a large clot over it, which contributed to limiting intraperitoneal bleeding (Fig. 2). The surgical strategy was as follows: (1) peripheral injection around the ectopic tissue of a solution containing 50 mL of saline, 20 mL of 1 % mepivacaine, 10 IU of methylergometrine, and half a vial of adrenaline; (2) monopolar resection of a myometrial wedge including the ectopic pregnancy; (3) double-layer closure of the hysterotomy using a 0 Vicryl suture (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Intraoperative images of left laparoscopic cornuectomy. This surgical approach differs from the one usually used at the center when the interstitial ectopic pregnancy occurs in a patient with an intact ipsilateral fallopian tube. In such cases, the tube is grasped to facilitate the development of a dissection plane, allowing access to the interstitial pregnancy in a less traumatic manner for the myometrium. This technique results in reduced bleeding and enables a single-layer reconstruction.

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 2. Follow-up confirmed a progressive decline in serum β-hCG, and no complications were observed. Counseling was provided regarding reproductive prognosis, emphasizing the recommendation of single embryo transfer in future IVF cycles and cesarean delivery in the event of subsequent pregnancy to reduce the risk of uterine rupture.

Discussion

Interstitial pregnancy is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication, even in women who have undergone bilateral salpingectomy. Residual tubal tissue or embryo migration during embryo transfer may still permit implantation in the interstitial portion, explaining the persistence of risk in this group of patients [1,2]. Clinicians should therefore remain vigilant in monitoring patients after assisted reproduction, particularly those with a history of tubal surgery.

Diagnosis relies on high-resolution transvaginal ultrasound, supported by serum beta-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) trends. Typical sonographic features include an eccentrically located sac, a thin surrounding myometrial mantle, and the interstitial line sign [3,4]. In the present case, these criteria allowed early recognition before rupture occurred. Systematic early ultrasound after embryo transfer is recommended, as symptoms may be absent or nonspecific [2,5].

Management should be individualized. Medical therapy with systemic methotrexate can be considered in selected cases, but its success is limited in the presence of cardiac activity or high β-hCG levels [6]. In the present case, rising β-hCG despite methotrexate confirmed treatment failure. Laparoscopic surgery remains the gold standard, offering definitive treatment while preserving uterine integrity [7,8]. Tailored approaches, including local vasoconstrictor infiltration and meticulous layered closure, help minimize blood loss and maintain reproductive potential [9].

Reproductive prognosis after conservative laparoscopic management is generally favorable, although a small risk of uterine rupture in subsequent pregnancies (2–5 %) has been reported [10]. Careful counseling is therefore essential, including recommendations for delayed conception and cesarean delivery.

Prevention plays a critical role. Complete excision of the cornual portion during salpingectomy is advised to avoid residual tubal tissue [11]. In ART cycles, preventive measures include single embryo transfer, avoidance of deep or high-pressure embryo transfer, and systematic ultrasound follow-up [12]. Establishing standardized ART-specific protocols may further improve safety and outcomes across centers.

In summary, this case underscores the importance of early diagnosis, timely laparoscopic management, and preventive strategies to reduce maternal risk and optimize reproductive outcomes in women with prior salpingectomy (uni-or bilateral) undergoing embryo transfer.
