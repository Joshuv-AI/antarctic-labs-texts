Frequent TCR rearrangements in pediatric B-cell lymphoblastic acute leukemia: genomic and phenotypic features
Authors: Jiajia Zheng, Yiting Wang, Zhiheng Li, Peifang Xiao, Jun Lu, Hu Liu, Yixin Hu, Shuiyan Wu, Bohan Li, Li Gao, Qi Ji, Hailong He, Yi Wang, Jie Li, Nan Chen, Lijun Meng, Yuanyuan Tian, Yizhen Li, Sheng Xiao, Shaoyan Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
T-cell receptor (TCR) loci undergo complex V(D)J rearrangements during T-cell maturation. However, errors in this process can cause TCR promoters and enhancers to aberrantly fuse with oncogenes, leading to their overexpression and the development of T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL). Unexpectedly, in a retrospective study of 97 pediatric B-ALL cases, all diagnosed according to WHO criteria at a single institution, we identified a subgroup of B-ALL harboring TCR fusion. These fusions involved diverse partner genes, including LINC01656, TBC1D10B, TCL1A/TCL1B, CDKN2A, CEBPB/G, and AHI1. Patients with TCR fusions showed a gene expression profile enriched in hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) signatures and frequently carried other adverse genomic markers, such as CDKN2A/B alterations and RAS pathway activation. Clinically, TCR-rearranged B-ALL patients were associated with increased risk (P = 0.005) and had higher minimal residual disease (MRD) at day 19 (P = 0.034), and may benefit from MRD-based risk-directed therapy. Our study highlights frequent TCR fusions in B-ALL, suggesting a stem cell-derived leukemia with potential differentiation into both B-cell and T-cell lineages.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-025-06687-2.

Introduction

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is the most common childhood cancer, with 85% of cases classified as B-cell ALL (B-ALL). Advances in risk classification, incorporating clinical factors such as white blood cell count (WBC) and age at diagnosis, genomic subtyping, and minimal residual disease (MRD) assessment after initial treatment, along with stratified therapies and new drug developments, have significantly improved survival rates for pediatric B-ALL. These advancements make B-ALL one of the significant milestones in the success of precision medicine. Over the past decade, developed countries have reported event-free survival (EFS) rates of 74–87% and overall survival (OS) rates of 85–90%[1].

Gene fusions involving the immunoglobulin (IG) and T-cell receptor (TCR) loci are well-established markers of B-ALL and T-ALL, respectively [2]. In B-ALL, IG rearrangements juxtaposition their potent enhancers to oncogenes such as CRLF2, EPOR, IL3, BCL2, BCL6, MYC, DUX4, and CEBPs, resulting in overexpression [3–6]. These IG rearrangements, depending on their fusion partners, have distinct prognostic and therapeutic implications. For example, IGH::CRLF2 and IGH::EPOR rearrangements, which are associated with the Philadelphia-like (Ph-like) B-ALL subtype, may be sensitive to JAK2 signaling inhibitors [7]. IGH::DUX4 occurs in approximately 5% of pediatric B-ALL cases and is associated with a favorable prognosis [8]. Conversely, IGH::IL3 B-ALL is linked to poor prognosis and often presents with eosinophilia due to IL3 overexpression [6, 9].

TCR, with a gene structure and somatic recombination mechanism similar to IG, is rearranged in approximately one-third of T-ALL cases. Common fusion partners for TCR include TAL1/2, LCK, TLX1/3, TCL1A, MYC, LYL1, LMO1/2, NKX2, BCL11B, NOTCH1, and IRS4 [10–16]. While the oncogenic mechanisms of these TCR rearrangements are mostly defined and serve as effective diagnostic and classification markers, their prognostic and therapeutic values are less well-established compared to IG rearrangements in B-ALL.

Although IG and TCR rearrangements are generally considered specific to B- and T-cell tumors, early studies showed unexpected IGH fusions in myeloid tumors. These IGH rearrangements in myeloid neoplasms lead to the translocation of the IGH enhancers and promoters to oncogenes, such as MECOM and CCNG1, leading to their.

overexpression [17]. There have been occasional case reports of TCR rearrangements in B-ALL, with 7 cases documented to date, involving TRA/D (T-cell receptor alpha and delta) at chromosome 14q11 (Table S1). The fusion partners in these cases include IGH, CDKN2A, and MYC[4, 18–23]. In this study, we identified TRA/D rearrangements in 6 of 97 pediatric B-ALL using a targeted DNA next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel that covered both IG and TCR genes. Our findings reveal that TRA/D rearrangements are more common than previously reported in pediatric B-ALL. These tumors expressed hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) markers and were frequently associated with adverse genomic features, such as CDKN2A/B alterations and RAS signaling mutations. Patients with these alterations may benefit from additional early intensification therapy.

Materials and methods

Patients

This study contained 97 continuous pediatric patients diagnosed with B-ALL at the Children’s Hospital of Soochow University from June 2023 to January 2024. All patients provided informed consent in accordance with institutional guidelines. The details of the diagnosis, risk assessment, and treatment are described in the Online Supplementary Information.

Targeted DNA NGS covering TRA/D loci

DNA from bone marrow samples was isolated using QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kits (Qiagen, Germany). Sequencing libraries were constructed with the Rapid Plus DNA Lib Prep Kit (ABclonal, China) and xGen® Hybridization and Wash Kit (IDT, USA). Briefly, DNA was fragmented, end polished, dA-tailed, and adapter-ligated. Library amplification was performed with initial denaturation at 98℃, followed by 7 cycles of denaturation, annealing, and extension, concluding with a final extension step. Target enrichment utilized biotin-labeled bait oligos targeting 187 leukemia-related genes, including both IG and TCR. Sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (Illumina, US) with 2 × 150-bp bidirectional sequencing. Data analysis for SNVs, inDels, CNVs, IG/TCR gene rearrangement, and variable (V), diversity (D) and joining (J) clonality was performed with custom pipelines developed at Sano Medical Laboratories (China). Variant nomenclature was interpreted following HGVS guidelines (GRCh38/hg38). The capture probe set comprised 5,396 probes, covering 464 kb of exon regions, 136 kb of IG regions, and 48 kb of TCR regions. Reporting criteria included mutations with a variant allele frequency (VAF) of ≥ 3% and at least 3 mutation variant reads. Sensitivity thresholds were established at a VAF of 3% and a sequencing depth of 325x.

Transcriptome and gene set enrichment analysis

RNA was purified from bone marrow specimens using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen). Sequence libraries were constructed with the NEB Next Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs), which included mRNA enrichment, cDNA synthesis, fragmentation, adapter ligation, and amplification. Next-generation sequencing was performed using the NovaSeq 6000 sequencer. Reads count-based differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 R package [24]. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed according to the Developer’s instructions and previous reports [25]. Differential expression was assessed with DESeq2 (v1.42.1), and GSEA was carried out using clusterProfiler (v4.10.1) against gene sets from MSigDB obtained via msigdbr (v7.5.1) and two custom gene sets derived from GSE24759 analyzed with limma (v3.58.1). The four gene sets used were: (1) JAATINEN_HEMATOPOIETIC_STEM_CELL_UP (genes upregulated in CD133 + hematopoietic stem cells versus CD133- cells), (2) EPPERT_HSC_R (genes upregulated in human hematopoietic stem cells versus progenitors/mature cells), (3) genes upregulated (>2‑fold, p < 0.05) in umbilical cord blood HSC compared with mature cells, and (4) genes upregulated (>2‑fold, p < 0.05) in HSC compared with progenitors. Weighted enrichment statistics were calculated on the log2 fold‑change ranked gene list; enrichment was considered significant at FDR < 0.25 and nominal p < 0.05.

Western blot analysis

Bone marrow aspirate was lysed by RIPA lysis buffer. Whole cell lysate was loaded onto polyacrylamide gels and probed by the following primary antibodies: SOCS2 (1:1000, A9190, Abclonal), ITGA6 (1: 2500, A20807, Abclonal), GATA3 (1:1000, 5852, cell signaling technology), and GAPDH (1:5000, AP0063, Bioworld Technology). The secondary antibodies were purchased from Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories. Blots were visualized by the LAS 4010 imaging system.

Data analysis

The results are presented as mean ± standard deviation, median, count, or percentage. Patient follow-up was continued until May 7, 2025. Fisher’s exact test or χ2 test was performed for mutual univariable analysis using SPSS version 2.0 software. A p-value < 0.05 is considered statistically significant.

Results

The fusion partners genes included long intergenic non-protein coding RNA 1656 (LINC01656), TBC1 domain family member 10B (TBC1D10B), T-cell leukemia/lymphoma 1 A/B (TCL1A/B), CCAAT enhancer-binding protein B and G (CEBPB and CEBPG), cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2 A (CDKN2A) and Abelson helper integration site 1 (AHI1). Among them, CDKN2A, AHI1, and TBC1D10B contained intragenic breakpoints and gene truncation, likely leading to loss of function; in contrast, TCL1A/B, CEBPG, and CEBPB had breakpoints outside of genes, preserving the coding regions and likely resulting in a gain of function by overexpression (Figs. 1 and 2; Table 1).

Karyotype analysis revealed that three of these cases had rearrangements involving the TRA/D locus at 14q11, with the following abnormalities: a t (9;14) (p21; p11) resulting in TRA::CDKN2A, a t(6;14)(q23; q21) resulting in TRA::AHI1, and a t(14;20) (q11; q13) resulting in TRA::CEBPB and TRD::CEBPB (Fig. 3A). FISH analysis confirmed TRA/D rearrangement in 4 of the 6 cases, including all 3 with karyotype abnormalities at 14q11 and one case without apparent 14q11 involvement (Fig. 3B). The remaining 2 cases, which were negative by both karyotyping and FISH, were further analyzed using PCR amplification with sequences obtained from the targeted DNA NGS. Sanger sequencing of the PCR products confirmed TRA/D rearrangement in these cases, identifying TRD::LINC01656 and TRA::TBC1D10B, respectively (Fig. 3C). Genomic profiles of pediatric B-ALL with TRA/D rearrangement

A detailed genomic profile was obtained by concurrent transcriptome analysis and the targeted DNA NGS. In addition to the TRA/D rearrangement, these 6 cases also contained known dominant driver fusion transcripts including TAF15::ZNF384,

KMT2A::MLLT10, PAX5::FBRS, SMARCA2::ZNF362, and BCR::ABL1(Table 2; Fig. 3D). Notably, two of these cases contained the same TAF15::ZNF384, and one had SMARCA2::ZNF362, which belonged to the same B-ALL subgroup based on expression profiling [27]. ZNF384 and ZNF362 rearrangements are found in ~ 5% of pediatric B-ALL [28]. In our cohort of 97 cases, 4 had either ZNF384 or ZNF362 rearrangements, and 3 of these 4 also had TRA/D rearrangements (p < 0.001). Other common genomic aberrations included CDKN2A/B deletions, detected in 3 of the 6 cases. Including the case with TRA::CDKN2A, which results in CDKN2A truncation, 4 of the 6 cases showed CDKN2A/B alterations, making them the most frequent genomic changes associated with TRA/D rearrangement in B-ALL. Expression signature in pediatric B-ALL with TRA/D rearrangement

The GSEA reported in the manuscript was performed on a carefully selected subset of seven cases to minimise confounding by other B‑ALL subtypes with similar stem‑cell‑like signatures. To identify these cases, we integrated our RNA‑seq data with the microarray dataset GSE79533[28] using the GEDI R package (v1.1) for cross‑platform normalization, followed by UMAP (v0.2.10.0) for dimensionality reduction. Seven cases that clustered with TCF3::PBX1 reference samples were retained for subsequent analysis. The results indicate significant enrichment of HSC genes in B-ALL cases with TRA/D rearrangement, suggesting that these tumors may originate from primitive hematopoietic progenitors (Fig. 3E). Consistently, patients with TRA/D rearrangement showed significantly higher CD34 fluorescence intensity (MFI) compared to other B-ALL cases by flow cytometry (p = 0,002) and expressed typical stem cell markers, including ITGA6, SOCS2, and GATA3, as confirmed by Western blot analysis (Figs. 3F-H).

Clinic features of pediatric B-ALL with TRA/D rearrangement

The clinical and laboratory features of the 6 patients with TRA/D rearrangements are summarized in Tables 2 and 3. The patients’ ages range from 4 months to 14 years at presentation, with a median age of 5 years. The cohort included 5 males and 1 female. Initial WBC counts varied from 19.11 to 202.8 × 109/L, with a median of 30.6 × 109/L. Analysis of cerebrospinal fluid obtained via lumbar puncture revealed no CNS involvement, and none of the patients experienced extramedullary invasion. Bone marrow aspirate morphology showed blasts typical of B-ALL, with medium to large in size, oval or round-shaped, with fine chromatin, often indistinct nucleoli, and minimal cytoplasm. Immunophenotypically, 4 cases showed typical B- ALL immunophenotypes, including positive B-cell markers and early lymphoid progenitor cell marker TdT. Two cases also expressed myeloid markers CD13 and CD33, with one patient additionally expressing MPO (48.7%), satisfying the criteria for mixed phenotype acute leukemia (Table 2, Figure S1). Similar myeloid marker expression was observed in 8 of 91 cases without TRA/D rearrangement in our series (p = 0.055). Although these data suggest that the TRA/D-rearranged patients are more likely to be male and to have additional myeloid markers, statistical significance was not achieved. Future studies with a larger population are needed. Prognostically, 4 of 6 patients (66.7%) were initially classified as intermediate risk (IR) based on various criteria: WBC counts > 50 × 109/L (patient 1), age > 10 years (patient 2), KMT2A::MLLT10 gene fusion (patient 3), and BCR::ABL1 gene fusion (patient 6). Patients 4 and 5 were re-classified as IR due to MRD > 1% at day 19. Four of the 6 patients received an additional cycle of cyclophosphamide, cytarabine, 6-mercaptopurine, vincristine, and pegaspargase from days 50 to 57. Patient 6, with BCR::ABL1, was treated with Dasatinib and achieved MRD < 0.01% by day 19. All 6 patients achieved MRD < 0.01% by day 46, with 5 currently in complete remission. Correlation analysis was performed between TRA/D rearrangements and various clinical parameters, including age at initial diagnosis, sex, peripheral blood WBC count, extramedullary infiltration, risk stratification, and MRD levels on days 19 and 46 of treatment. Patients with TRA/D rearrangements had higher MRD levels on day 19 (P = 0.034). Initial risk classification did not differ significantly between the two groups (P = 0.081). However, when integrating MRD response into the risk classification algorithm, patients with TRA/D rearrangements were more frequently classified as IR ALL (P = 0.005). No significant differences were observed in other clinical parameters between the groups (Table 3).

Discussion

Leukemias originating from hematopoietic cells can exhibit varying degrees of differentiation into downstream lineages[29]. This includes mixed phenotype acute leukemia (MPAL), where cells can show both myeloid and lymphoid characteristics[30], and chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), which can progress to either a myeloid blast crisis or a lymphoid blast crisis [31]. Our study identified TCRrearrangements in 6.2% of pediatric B-ALL. B-ALL most commonly arises from pre-B cells, with some originating from pro-B cells, an earlier stage in B-cell development[32]. Our analysis of TCR-rearranged B-ALL patients revealed an mRNA expression profile enriched in HSC markers, consistent with stem cell leukemia characteristics. Although TCRrearrangement suggests potential T-cell differentiation[33], the tumor cells did not express T-cell markers by flow cytometry, likely due to limited T-cell development. TCR V(D)J gene segment rearrangement occurs at the double-negative (DN) stage of T-cell development, when specific T-cell markers are not yet expressed.

The impact of TCR rearrangements on tumorigenesis in these B-ALL remains unclear. In T-ALL, potent enhancers from TCR genes can drive overexpression when fused with oncogenes [34]. A common fusion partner is TCL1A/B, which, when overexpressed, promotes the proliferation of early T-cell progenitors by activating the PI3K/AKT pathway[35]. One of our patients (patient 3) showed a TRA::TCL1A/B fusion, suggesting it may share similar mechanisms of tumorigenesis. Additionally, TRA::CDKN2A was identified in 1 of the 6 patients studied. The CDKN2A breakpoint occurs within intron 1, leading to truncation of the p16ink4a tumor suppressor protein. Interestingly, an identical TRA/D::CDKN2A fusion was found in another B-ALL case (Table 1), indicating a potentially recurrent event [36]. CDKN2A inactivation is a common genomic alteration in ALL, occurring in approximately 20% of B-ALL cases and 50% of T-ALL cases [37, 38], and is indicative of poor prognosis [39]. In our study, 4 of the 6 cases with TCR rearrangements contained CDKN2A alterations, likely contributing to the increased prognostic risk in this subset of B-ALL. Additionally, Patient 3, who had a KMT2A::MLLT10 rearrangement, a known high-risk marker, had a CNS relapse. Notably, 2 of the 7 B-ALL cases with TCR rearrangements reported in the literature also had KMT2A rearrangements (Table 1), and both of them experienced relapse [20, 23].

Two cases in our series showed rearrangements between TRA/D and two CEBP family members, CEBPB and CEBPG. CEBP family members are known to be rearranged with IGH in B-ALL, leading to their overexpression [40]. The CEBP family of bZIP transcription factors plays a crucial role in regulating hematopoiesis, including stem cell function and the differentiation of myeloid and B lymphoid cells[41–43]. The TRA/D::CEBPB/G fusions likely contribute to leukemogenesis in a manner similar to IGH::CEBPby deregulating their expression[40].The roles of the remaining partner genes, TBC1D10B, AHI1, and LINC01656, are not well defined in B-ALL.

Since all 6 patients with TCR rearrangements harbor known dominant drivers, including TAF15::ZNF384 (2 patients), SMARCA2::ZNF362, BCR::ABL1, KMT2A::MLLT10, and PAX5::FBRS, TCR rearrangement is likely a late event that contributes to tumor progression rather than an initiating event. Expression profiling and GSEA of these TRA/D-rearranged B-ALL cases revealed characteristics of HSC. Notably, 5 of the 6 fusions are associated with stem cell features and multi-lineage potential. For example, B-ALL with ZNF384rearrangement often expresses CD10 dim/-, CD33+, and CD13+[44],and this rearrangement is found in 48% of MPAL cases [29]. ZNF362 fusions have a similar expression profile to ZNF384-rearranged B-ALL and are included in the same subgroup [27]. KMT2Arearrangements are frequently associated with MPAL[45], and Ph + B-ALL often expresses myeloid markers such as CD13 and CD33 [46]. Recent research on Ph + B-ALL has shown that the differentiation stage of leukemia significantly impacts patient prognosis. Specifically, patients with Ph + B-ALL who are differentiated at early progenitor and inter-progenitor stages have poorer OS and EFS rates compared to those whose B-ALL is arrested at the late progenitor stage [47].

The reason for the limited number of previously reported cases of TCR rearrangement in B-ALL, despite an occurrence rate of 6.2% in our series, remains unclear. Two factors may contribute to this discrepancy. First, our study utilized a DNA NGS assay covering all IG and TCR regions, which can detect small genomic rearrangements, including small insertions that might escape detection by karyotyping and FISH. Indeed, 2 of the 6 cases in our series were negative by both karyotyping and FISH. Second, only a portion of B-ALL cases can proliferate in vitro, while others may not divide and only show a normal karyotype from non-neoplastic hematopoietic cells, missing the opportunity to detect chromosome rearrangements, including 14q11 (TRA/D) rearrangements.

In summary, we identified a subgroup of pediatric B-ALL cases with TCR rearrangements. These tumors exhibit expression of HSC genes and frequently harbor other adverse genomic markers, including CDKN2A/B alterations and RAS mutations. In our series of 97 cases, 59 were classified as low risk and 38 as IR, with no cases classified as high risk. Notably, all 6 patients with TCR rearrangement were classified as IR finally, and the proportion of patients with an MRD level higher than 0.1% at day 19 was significantly higher in patients with TRA/D rearrangements than in a comparator group of 11 patients with primary drivers such as BCR::ABL1, KMT2A, PAX5r or ZNF384 fusions but without TRA/D rearrangements (4/5 vs. 2/11; p = 0.0179). These findings suggest that TCR rearrangement may be associated with a poor initial therapeutic response, and these patients could potentially benefit from MRD-based risk-directed therapy. However, further studies are required to validate its role as a prognostic marker for IR. The overall prognostic significance of TCR rearrangement remains unclear due to the short follow-up period. It is noteworthy that 5 of 6 B-ALL cases with TRA/D rearrangement were male; however, the p-value suggests a lack of statistical significance, likely due to the limited sample size. Additionally, 6 of the 7 previously published B-ALL cases with TCR rearrangements were pediatric patients, with the remaining case involving a 20-year-old (Table S1). Whether TCR-rearranged B-ALL is enriched to pediatric cases remains to be determined. Based on these findings, we suggest that TRA/D rearrangement screening could be considered as part of risk assessment algorithms for pediatric B-ALL patients. However, it is important to note that models have yet to be developed to demonstrate its functional role in the poor prognosis of ALL patients. Further research is necessary to explore the biological role of TRA/D rearrangements and better understand their molecular contribution to B-ALL development.
