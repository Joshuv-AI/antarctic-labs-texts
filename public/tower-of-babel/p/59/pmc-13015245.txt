Enzymatically controlled release of proteins and peptides: A promising, alternative secretion approach
Authors: Daniel Ivanusic, Gregor Zipf, Josef Maier, Hubert Bernauer, Norbert Bannert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe biological activity of many proteins is influenced by glycosylation, underscoring the essential role of the glycosylation process. However, no established recombinant technique currently enables the controlled shedding of glycosylated extracellular loops from transmembrane proteins. Here, we describe enzymatically controlled release of proteins and peptides (ENCOREP), a strategy that enables in situ expression at the cell surface and protease-mediated release, followed by collection from the cell culture medium. Using ENCOREP, we achieved the production of glycosylated peptides. These glycopeptides, derived from the large extracellular loop of the highly glycosylated CD63, illustrate the type of targets that are otherwise inaccessible with current methods but can be readily obtained using ENCOREP. Overall, ENCOREP provides a rapid and reliable approach to obtain glycosylated proteins or peptides while bypassing the conventional signal peptide-dependent secretory pathway.Subject areas: Biochemistry, Protein, Cell biology

Introduction

In eukaryotic cells, most plasma membrane proteins and secreted soluble proteins are exported via the secretory pathway, which includes the rough endoplasmic reticulum (ER), the Golgi vesicular stacks, and the trans-Golgi network. Integral membrane proteins are co-translationally inserted into the ER, then trafficked through the Golgi and TGN before being delivered to the plasma membrane, where their luminal regions appear as extracellular ectodomains (Figure 1A, left). Approximately 80% of the secretory proteins undergo glycosylation along this pathway, and the glycan decoration may vary in occupancy and structure depending on the protein’s localization and properties.1,2,3 There is a significant demand for glycosylated recombinant proteins. Post-translational glycosylation of proteins is a complex modification that affects the specific biological activity, structure, and function of proteins. These glycosylated recombinant proteins are predominantly produced in mammalian cells or insect cells, as well as in bacteria or yeast.4 Mammalian cell lines represent the most frequently employed method for biopharmaceutical protein production and are also crucial for conducting basic research on new therapeutic approaches.5,6,7 Protein glycosylation is an important characteristic for recombinant proteins because glycosylation patterns play an essential role in biological efficacy, serum half-life, and antigenicity.8 Especially, human cells are the preferred expression systems to produce therapeutic human-like glycosylated proteins.9,10 Although proteins can be produced in mammalian cells, e.g., murine cells, such proteins have a different glycosylation pattern that may confer them with high immunogenicity and rapid clearance ability in humans.9,11 Protein glycosylation is a multilevel process involving more than 200 genes encoding glycosyltransferases. Their cell type-specific expression and localization determine which proteins become glycosylated. It also dictates their glycan structure and glycosylation site occupancy.12,13 Glycosylation and phosphorylation of proteins are the most prominent protein post-translational modifications (PTMs).1,14 N-Glycosylation dominates the majority of proteome-wide PTMs.15 Membrane-bound proteins localized at the cell surface are usually N-glycosylated, and their glycan trees can have a length of 30 Å, comparable to the size of an immunoglobulin domain.16 Integral membrane proteins are of particular interest because glycosylation can modify protein-protein interactions between ectodomains and their ligands, such as other membrane proteins or secreted proteins.17,18,19,20,21,22 To date, there has been no technique for the production of recombinant native-like glycosylated extracellular loops of membrane proteins that are expressed in situ, released from the surface, and subsequently isolated from the cell culture medium. Hence, we utilized the tANCHOR display system to create a system for enzymatically controlled release of proteins (ENCOREP). The tANCHOR system itself utilizes transmembrane anchors derived from tetraspanin sequences, where the large extracellular loop (LEL) is replaced by the ectodomain of interest. This modification ensures the highly efficient presentation of a peptide or protein of interest on the cell surface of human cells.23,24 Especially, transmembrane anchors derived from CD82 showed the best performance for displaying proteins or peptides on the cell surface of HEK293T or HeLa cells.23 Shedding of extracellular domains (ECDs) can be observed in vivo, regulating the fate and localization of membrane-anchored growth factors.25 The developed ENCOREP system offers the opportunity to acquire post-translationally modified proteins that accumulate into the culture medium, facilitating subsequent experiments. In particular, the expression and isolation of glycosylated tetraspanin LELs, such as those derived from CD63, have so far relied on E. coli expression systems.26,27,28 Unfortunately, such proteins were not glycosylated in unmodified E. coli strains, because the eukaryotic glycosyltransferases are missing, and bacteria with humanized glycosylation are quite inefficient.4 Additionally, the ENCOREP system offers an alternative technology for scaled-up production of soluble proteins released into the medium. In pursuing this approach, modifications were made to the tobacco etch virus protease (TEVp) to restore its protease activity when it travels along the secretory pathway and is secreted, as described previously.29 This facilitates the subsequent release of surface-presented ectodomains by flanked TEVp cleavage sites into the cell culture medium. The application of the highly sequence-specific cysteine protease TEVp allowed us to attain consistent protein yields in a co-expression setup. The primary advantage of TEVp is its remarkable lack of toxicity when expressed in mammalian cell lines. Furthermore, it demonstrates exceptional enzymatic proteolytic activity across a spectrum of temperatures, diverse buffers, and various medium compositions.29,30,31 This co-expression strategy enables efficient and scalable bioprocessing, making it feasible to obtain soluble released proteins in a cost-effective manner compared with the usage of purified recombinant TEVp.

Results

Release of the receptor-binding domain of SARS-CoV-2 from the cell surface

In our experimental approach, we cloned an insert into the tANCHOR system encoding a fusion protein in which the protein of interest is flanked by highly specific TEVp cleavage sites (ENLYFQ|S; P1′ = S, G, or A) (Figure 1B).32 The constructed vectors for this approach are based on the previously developed tANCHOR display system.23 For assessing the feasibility of a TEVp-dependent cleavage from the cell surface, we introduced the coding sequence for the receptor-binding domain (RBD) of SARS-CoV-2 delta variant (B.1.617.2) into the tANCHOR vector by molecular cloning. This type of engineering should allow a TEVp-mediated cleavage, enabling a controlled and targeted release of the RBD from the cell surface (Figure 1A, right). Cell surface localization of the expressed protein was confirmed by confocal microscopy. The tANCHOR membrane-bound fusion protein containing the RBD fused to a V5 epitope tag and a C-terminal hexahistidine tag (His) predominantly localized at the plasma membrane (Figure 1C). To confirm the extracellular accessibility of the RBD-V5-His fused ectodomain protein, we employed a cell-based antibody binding assay specific for the V5 tag, which showed comparable detection of the V5 tag, independent of the presence of the RBD within the extracellular loop on HeLa cells (Figure 1D). The suitability of the SARS-CoV-2 RBD for optimizing experimental parameters is underscored by its favorable characteristics. The protein size of approximately 40 kDa facilitates detection by western blot analysis as a single band on the gel. To set up the protein release experiment, HEK293T cells were seeded into a 6-well plate and were subsequently transfected and incubated for 24 h. When the tANCHORed RBD-V5-His was displayed (typically after 24 h post transfection33), we added recombinant TEVp to the cells. RBD-V5-His released in the supernatants and cell lysates was analyzed by western blot (Figure 1E). We observed that the released RBD-V5-His protein could be detected in the collected supernatant in a TEVp dose-dependent manner (Figure 1F). The RBD-V5-His protein was detected by western blot analysis using the V5-tag epitope, which was C terminally fused to the released RBD protein. As the concentration of TEVp increased, we observed a corresponding decrease in the intensity of protein bands detected for tANCHORed RBD-V5-His in the lysates of cell pellets, which correlated with the rising protein levels of released RBD-V5-His in the supernatant. This indicates that the RBD-V5-His-fused, full-length tANCHORed protein was efficiently cleaved by TEVp, leading to the release and enrichment of the RBD-V5-His ectodomain into the supernatant from the cell surface. Next, we tested if the RBD-V5-His protein is glycosylated and treated the released RBD-V5-His protein with peptide N-glycosidase F (PNGase F) enzyme. The observed shift in molecular size from ∼40 kDa to ∼35 kDa due to PNGase F treatment indicates that the obtained TEVp-released protein is glycosylated (Figure 1G). In summary, these results demonstrate the successful TEVp-controlled release of the RBD-V5-His protein into the medium, achieved through a TEVp dose-dependent cleavage of the flanking TEV sites of the tANCHORed protein on the cell surface.

Quantification of released SARS-CoV-2 RBD-V5-His protein and cytotoxic analysis during treatment with recombinant TEVp

We quantified the released SARS-CoV-2 RBD-V5-His from HEK293T cells by comparing band intensities to a bovine serum albumin (BSA) standard on Coomassie-stained gels (Figure 2A, top). Following staining, the gel was blotted and probed with an anti-V5-HRP antibody to identify the stained RBD-V5-His band, which showed a molecular weight of approximately 40 kDa (Figure 2A, bottom). The results indicated that the amount of released RBD-V5-His protein in band intensity corresponded to 256 ng/mL after a 3-h TEVp incubation and increased 2.8-fold, corresponding to about 728 ng/mL, after 24 h (Figure 2B). This suggests that the full-length tANCHORed RBD-V5-His protein is consistently expressed and cleaved on the cell surface. In addition, we analyzed the protein expression by using the mCherry reporter protein, which was C terminally fused to the tANCHOR chassis. Fluorescence intensity was monitored by fluorescence microscopy in the presence and absence of TEVp (Figure 2C). We observed that the addition of recombinant TEVp reduced the mCherry mean fluorescence intensity. The expression profile validated that the highest protein synthesis rate occurs between 24 and 48 h post-transfection (Figure 2D). Furthermore, after incubation with recombinant TEVp, there was a distinct reduction in the quantity of mCherry-fused tANCHORed RBD-V5-His, a change that is plainly visible in the color of cell pellets used for preparing cell lysates (Figure 2E). Cells subjected to TEVp treatment exhibited a colorless appearance, whereas those without TEVp treatment displayed a noticeable pink color. These experiments indicate that the expression level under TEVp treatment is reduced due to protein degradation of the residual tANCHOR surface protein after shedding of the displayed ectodomain. To rule out the possibility of a cytotoxic effect causing the lowering of the expression, we conducted monitoring of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release during TEVp incubation. First, we monitored LDH release for incubation with increasing TEVp concentrations from 10 U/mL to 1,000 U/mL (Figure 2F). Second, LDH release was monitored over a period of 72 h under continuous TEVp treatment (Figure 2G). The results indicated that a potential cytotoxic effect caused by TEVp treatment was not observable. The elevated LDH release observed at 48 and 72 h can be attributed to dense cell growth, resulting in cell death. Notably, even in the control group without TEVp addition, increased LDH release was observable at these time points. Interestingly, with the addition of TEVp, LDH release was lower. This effect may arise from extended protein degradation, hindering cell growth compared to the control group without the addition of TEVp.

Release of the glycosylated LEL of CD63

For research purposes, peptides derived from the extracellular loop of tetraspanins are typically generated in E. coli.26,27,28 Notably, E. coli lacks the machinery for eukaryotic protein glycosylation, and, therefore, it also does not possess the native-like glycosylation pathways of humans.34 Using yeast cells for protein expression may allow for eukaryotic-type protein glycosylation, but it is essential to note that the glycosylation patterns in yeast cells differ from those in mammalian cells.35,36 Bacterial expression systems with humanized glycosylation are still quite inefficient.4 Furthermore, the same glycoprotein, secreted into the extracellular space, or alternatively expressed as transmembrane ectodomain protein, can differ in glycosylation patterns, as was extensively investigated for the HIV-1 envelope protein and its soluble gp120 and gp140 preparations.37 We, therefore, investigated whether glycosylation patterns on a protein ectodomain, such as the LEL of CD63 (CD63LEL), when it is secreted in soluble form into the extracellular space differ from those when it is expressed as a membrane-anchored ectodomain, using the tANCHOR platform. Subsequently, it was released from the cell surface by TEVp-controlled shedding. For this, we designed protein expression constructs for a TEVp-dependent CD63LEL release based on the tANCHOR vectors and a vector using an N-terminal signal peptide derived from the luciferase gene (Gaussia princeps) for secretion as a soluble protein (Figure 3A). We selected this signal peptide due to its ability to facilitate highly efficient protein synthesis and secretion.24,38,39 CD63, fused with an N-terminal FLAG epitope tag and mCherry at the C terminus, exhibited a characteristic smear band when subjected to western blot analysis using anti-CD63 antibodies (Figure 3B). This observation suggests the presence of different glycosylated proteoforms of CD63.40,41,42 The LEL of CD63 is known to contain three N-linked glycans, which contribute significantly to its additional molecular mass.43,44 For analyzing possible differences in glycosylation of CD63LEL, we compared the TEVp-mediated membrane shedding approach with the Gaussia signal peptide-mediated (SP1) secretion of a soluble form of CD63LEL (Figure 3C), both C terminally fused with a V5 and a 6× His-tag (CD63LEL-V5-His). Our results showed that the TEVp-dependent surface released CD63LEL-V5-His (Figure 3D, left blot, lane 1) and the full-length tANCHORed CD63LEL-V5 (Figure 3D, right blot, lane 1) displayed a smeared band pattern similar to the full-length CD63-mCherry protein (Figure 3B). Thus, the observed migration patterns and PNGase F sensitivity confirm the presence of N-linked glycans of released CD63LEL-V5 (Figure 3D, left blot, lane 1). In contrast, the secreted CD63LEL-V5-His peptide exhibited a smear pattern with three prominent bands and notably lower levels of the fully glycosylated CD63LEL-V5-His proteoform (Figure 3D, left blot, lane 3). The less glycosylated forms corresponding to a molecular weight of approximately 20 kDa were not visible in the blot when CD63LEL-V5-His was released from the cell surface. To test whether highly glycosylated proteoforms depend on the CD63 tetraspanin core of tANCHOR, we compared tANCHOR platforms employing either CD63 or CD82. Our results revealed that even when using a CD82-based tANCHOR, CD63LEL-V5-His with comparable glycosylation patterns was produced, as confirmed by western blot analysis (Figure 3E, left side). To identify residual tANCHORed CD63LEL-V5-His not released by TEVp, we probed the membrane with antibodies targeting the FLAG tag, which was fused with the tANCHOR scaffold at the N terminus. Our observations indicated that the bands at approximately ∼70 kDa originated from the unprocessed tANCHOR fusion protein, as the FLAG tag was fused with the tANCHOR scaffold (Figure 3E, right side). Next, we analyzed by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) the protein localization of CD63 and CD82 tANCHORed CD63LEL-V5-His. These experiments confirmed that both anchored proteins displaying CD63LEL-V5-His were exported to the cell surface (Figure 3F) and were accessible for anti-V5-HRP antibodies (Figure 3G). The diminished binding efficiency to the V5 tag observed when utilizing the tANCHOR based on the CD63 tetraspanin can be attributed to its generally lower abundance, as was demonstrated previously on the surface of HeLa cells, but this effect was not observed for HEK293T cells.23 Taken together, employing the tANCHOR system for peptide release generates a predominant soluble glycosylated proteoform of CD63LEL-V5-His. This pattern is more comparable to CD63LEL expressed in full-length FLAG-CD63-mCherry fusion proteins, which also shows one major proteoform. In contrast, the soluble secreted form of CD63LEL-V5-His exhibits several low-glycosylated proteoforms.

Engineering a secretory-pathway active TEVp variant by eliminating inhibitory PTMs

Adding recombinant TEVp from a supplier to the medium is a practical choice when a small amount of supernatant containing the released protein or peptide is required, but it becomes cost prohibitive for larger-scale production. Therefore, a more cost-efficient choice is to co-produce TEVp with the cultivated production cells themselves. Unfortunately, wild-type TEVp is not active when produced via the secretory pathway. Two major PTMs, cysteine oxidation and glycosylation, significantly influence the correct folding and structure of the TEVp molecule.29 The minimal TEVp substrate recognition motif is encoded by seven amino acids, EXXYXQ-S/G, where X can represent any amino acid. The cleavage site is located between Q and S/G.45 The most efficient TEVp substrate recognition motif is identical to the protease’s natural substrate peptide ENLYFQ-G/S.46 We introduced three mutations in a step-wise manner, like previously shown—N23Q (inactivates N-glycosylation at N23), C130S (avoids cysteine oxidation), and T173G (inactivates N-glycosylation at N171)—into a TEVp coding sequence that already contained the S219V mutation—for enabling TEVp activity within the secretory pathway.29 The S219V mutation was previously shown to disable TEVp inactivation via autoproteolysis near its C terminus.29,47 Additionally, we limited our use to amino acids 1–236, excluding the C-terminal amino acids ELVYSQ. These residues are recognized for their tendency to bind tightly to the active site, thereby reducing TEVp activity due to a known autoinhibitory effect.48 We further modified the C terminus of TEVp by adding a c-Myc tag for western blot analysis and a 6× His-tag for protein isolation (Figure 4A). We addressed inhibition caused by PTMs and generated engineered expression vectors encoding a TEVp variant that remains active when secreted (Figure 4B). To analyze protein expression, we transfected HEK293T cells with the generated TEVp-c-Myc-His vectors and subsequently examined both the supernatant and lysates of washed cells. All TEVp-c-Myc-His variants containing the additional specific point mutations for removing PTMs were secreted at similar levels compared to the TEVp-c-Myc-His with the single mutation S219V (Figure 4C). The TEVp-c-Myc-His with mutations C130S, N23Q, and S219V resulted in a slightly lower apparent molecular mass of the secreted TEVp-c-Myc-His compared to the variant with a single mutation, S219V (∼48 kDa). The TEVp-c-Myc-His containing the mutations N23Q, C130S, and S219V (TEVpN23Q,C130S,S219V-c-Myc-His) corresponded to ∼45 kDa, and TEVp-c-Myc-His containing the mutation sites N23Q, C130S, T173G, and S219V (TEVpN23Q,C130S,T173G,S219V-c-Myc-His) corresponded to ∼40 kDa. Functional activity along the secretory pathway was observed only for the secreted TEVp-c-Myc-His variant with the quadruple N23Q, C130S, T173G, and S219V mutations. (Figure 4D). The slope comparisons showed that only the four-mutation TEVp variant differs significantly from the others, as indicated by non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals (Figure 4E). These results suggest that the designed expression vector is capable of secreting functional TEVp-c-Myc-His into the cell culture medium.

Protein release from the cell surface by co-cultivation or co-expressed secreted TEVp

Following the successful secretion of active TEVp-c-Myc-His, we next investigated the capacity for the release of tANCHORed SARS-CoV-2 RBD-V5-His. There are two potential approaches: one involves co-cultivation of separately transfected TEVp-secreting cells with the cells presenting the RBD-V5-His with flanked TEVp cleavage sites, while the other entails release through co-transfection of the same cell culture with two different plasmids, encoding TEVp-c-Myc-His and the tANCHORed RBD-V5-His (Figure 5A). Cells in the co-cultivation setup were separately transfected and co-cultivated for 24 h, and co-cultivation of TEVp-c-Myc-His-expressing cells and tANCHORed RBD-V5-His-expressing cells was tracked by fluorescence imaging of the cells (Figure 5B, top); after 48 h, the supernatant and cell lysates were analyzed (Figure 5C, top). Alternatively, cells were co-transfected, controlled for tANCHORed RBD-V5-His expression by fluorescence imaging via the mCherry tag on tANCHOR (Figure 5B, bottom), and, after 48 h, analyzed equally as the co-cultivated cells (Figure 5C, bottom). In our experiments, we observed that co-transfection yielded the most efficient release of the RBD-V5-His protein into the medium (Figure 5C). The predicted three-dimensional (3D) structure of the tANCHORed RBD-V5-His and TEVp indicates accessible proteolytic sites for releasing the RBD-V5-His ectodomain protein (Figure 5D). This is true for both tANCHOR versions of the tANCHORed RBD, as the basic topology of CD82 and CD63 was identical in both cases.49,50,51 Comparative modeling of the structure of TEVp and tANCHORed RBD suggests that proteolytic cleavage processing of the protease sites by the modified TEVp is consistent with the experimental findings. The estimated topology-modeling score (TM score)52 of 0.608 for tANCHOR(CD82)-RBD-V5-His (Figure 5D) indicates that the model correctly predicts the topology, because it is above the critical level of 0.5 for usually correctly predicted topologies. The CD63 versions yielded slightly lower values on average, but these were consistently above the critical value of 0.5.49 The modeling of the mutated TEVp-c-Myc-His reliably demonstrated an estimated TM score exceeding 0.9, indicative of an excellent model quality. The structure depicted in Figure 5D exhibited a TM score of 0.931. The membrane embedding procedure yielded the following results: hydrophobic thickness, 32.4 ± 1.2 Å; ΔGtransfer, −50.7 kcal/mol; and an overall tilt angle, 4° ± 2°. For purposes of comparison, the respective parameters for the experimentally obtained crystal structure of CD81 (PDB: 5TCX) are 34.0 ± 1.4 Å, −55.3 kcal mol−1, and 1° ± 1°. This resulted in a more spatially extended representation of the membrane. The fundamental outcome of the embedding algorithm remained unaltered by this, as the TEVp protein lacks transmembrane domains. An additional check with the QMeanBrane method, which is also a modeling of membrane embedding, revealed a topology of the membrane-protein complexes comparable to the PPM (Positioning of Proteins in Membranes) results for both the CD82- and CD63-based constructs.53 Furthermore, it was confirmed by QMeanBrane for both structures that the pseudo-energy output (membrane insertion energy = −83,646 a.u.) of the internally used algorithm for membrane localization falls within the expected range of −220,000 to −30,000 a.u. of a typical transmembrane structure.53

Co-expression of secreted TEVp enables continuous release of glycosylated tANCHORed proteins

Our findings demonstrate the feasibility of co-expressing active TEVp-c-Myc-His in the secretory pathway. However, further experiments are required to analyze the continuous production of tANCHORed RBD-V5-His and its release by cells. To address this question, we compared, in co-transfected HEK293T cell cultures over 96 h, the total amount of released RBD-V5-His protein at 2-h intervals with the quantity of de novo released RBD-V5-His when the supernatant was removed every 24 h and replaced with fresh medium (Figure 6A). The analysis of supernatants revealed that cells were efficiently renewing tANCHORed RBD-V5-His fusion proteins for the continuous release of RBD-V5-His through the ER and Golgi stacks in the secretory pathway and on the cell surface. The de novo RBD-V5-His protein release within a 24-h interval was comparable at 24 and 48 h post-transfection (Figure 6B), and it was still prominent at 72 h. This result indicates that the release of the TEV-site flanked RBD-V5-His protein can be continuously and sustainably executed up to 72 h post-transfection. It is important to note that due to the constraints of using transient transfection and limited space in the cell culture well, our follow-up analysis was restricted to a time frame of 96 h. In the co-transfection experiments conducted, we expressed the optimized TEVp-c-Myc-His protease within the same cell cultures that also expressed the tANCHORed RBD-V5-His with flanked TEVp cleavage sites. Consequently, most cells were co-transfected with both vectors, enabling simultaneous expression of TEVp-c-Myc-His and tANCHORed RBD-V5-His. The question arose whether a TEVp-dependent cleavage could also occur inside the cells along the secretory pathway in co-transfected cells, as was observed for antibody L and H chains linked by a TEV site, which assemble into immunoglobulins after cleavage within the secretory pathway.29 Additionally, our experiments showed that cleavage occurs inside the cells (Figure 5C). However, it remains unclear at what proportion this cleavage takes place. To address this question, we isolated the released RBD-V5-His protein simultaneously from both the supernatant and the corresponding cells under identical binding conditions and volumes. The western blot analysis revealed the presence of RBD-V5-His protein released from the cell surface. Furthermore, the analysis showed the release of the protein inside the cell, as observed in other experiments (Figure 5C), from the synthesized full-length tANCHOR scaffold protein, which had not yet been secreted (Figure 6C). This direct comparison demonstrated that a small fraction of the protein was cleaved inside the cells and was not released from the cell surface. The faint RBD band also indicated the surface adherence of the RBD-V5-His protein, which was detected in low amounts. In contrast, the majority of the cleaved protein was detectable in the supernatant. We quantitatively compared protein release mediated by TEVpN23Q,C130S,T173G,S219V-c-Myc-His for the tANCHORed CD63LEL-V5-His and SARS-CoV-2 RBD-V5-His proteins. From a single 6-well culture plate, we obtained 737 ng/mL of RBD-V5-His and 763 ng/mL of the highly glycosylated CD63LEL-V5-His peptide (Figure 6D). We probed the released delta RBD with biotinylated Sambucus nigra agglutinin (SNA) and detected binding by using streptavidin-HRP (Figure 6E), which further demonstrated that the released protein was glycosylated, consistent with the observed shift after PNGase F treatment (Figure 1G). Lectin probing is a well-established approach to detect glycoproteins by exploiting lectin-carbohydrate recognition of specific glycan epitopes.54 Notably, SNA preferentially recognizes terminal α2,6-linked sialic acid (Neu5Acα2-6Gal/GalNAc); therefore, SNA binding specifically detects the presence of α2,6-sialylated glycans.55 To exclude the possibility that co-expressed TEVpN23Q,C130S,T173G,S219V-c-Myc-His could cause any alterations in protein expression, we compared the expression patterns of CD63mCherry (Figure 6F) and CD82-tANCHORed CD63LEL-V5-His (Figure 6G). In both western blot analyses, we observed similar protein patterns with or without expression of the modified TEVp, indicating that its co-expression does not interfere with protein expression.

Expression of modified TEVp is compatible with sustained cell viability and proliferation

We further analyzed whether the co-expression of TEVpN23Q,C130S,T173G,S219V-c-Myc-His and tANCHORed RBD proteins could cause cytotoxic or anti-proliferative effects. As determined by an LDH release assay (Figure 7A), the background LDH release was comparable to controls expressing only tANCHORed RBD-V5-His or to untransfected cells. We confirmed the LDH assay results by performing additional cytotoxicity monitoring using an MTT assay. The data showed that co-expression of the modified TEVp did not induce cytotoxic effects in cells expressing the TEVp-cleavable tANCHORed protein, and the cells proliferated comparably to those without the modified TEVp (Figure 7B). These results indicate that the expression of the modified TEVp does not affect cell proliferation, suggesting that the cells can tolerate the membrane-anchored tANCHOR backbone after cleavage and efficiently degrade it, while the released protein is secreted into the medium.

ENCOREP-released RBD serves as a functional probe for studying ACE2 interactions

N-linked glycosylation is an essential structural and functional feature of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, influencing protein folding, stability, immune evasion, and receptor engagement. The RBD contains two conserved N-glycosylation sites at N331 and N343, both of which predominantly carry complex-type N-glycans when expressed in mammalian systems.56 Structural and biochemical studies have demonstrated that these glycans, and in particular the glycan at N343, stabilize the RBD conformation required for high-affinity angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) binding.57,58 Mass-spectrometric analyses across viral variants, including the delta lineage, have consistently confirmed that both N331 and N343 remain occupied by complex or partially processed N-glycans.59,60 Enzymatic removal of these glycans has been shown to alter RBD conformation and reduce receptor affinity, underscoring their functional relevance for ACE2 interaction. To experimentally assess the impact of RBD glycosylation on ACE2 binding in the context of the SARS-CoV-2 delta variant, the released RBD carrying a C-terminal V5-His-tag was collected from the culture medium by V5-trap magnetic particles. Soluble ACE2 (amino acids 1–740), produced and secreted as described previously, was used to measure receptor interaction.61 Binding was examined using either glycosylated RBD provided from the ENCOREP system or the RBD subjected to enzymatic deglycosylation with PNGase F (Figure 8A). Bound ACE2 revealed interaction with the glycosylated RBD (control), whereas ACE2 association was significantly reduced for the deglycosylated RBD samples (Figure 8B). The efficiency of deglycosylation was confirmed using western blot analysis, which showed the expected molecular weight shift of the RBD and a corresponding decrease in ACE2 signal intensity, consistent with decreased affinity of the deglycosylated RBD for ACE2 (Figure 8C).

Surface expression and release of small peptides using the ENCOREP system

The tANCHOR/ENCOREP system was used to assess its capability to express and release small extracellular displayed peptides. Constructs encoding the second extracellular loop (EC2) of canine CD20 (cCD20-EC2, 140–189), C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4, 179–199), and the C46 peptide derived from HIV-1 gp41 (628–673) were designed with flanking TEVp cleavage sites and expressed in HEK293T cells along with the TEVpN23Q,C130S,T173G,S219V-c-Myc-His (Figure 9A). Western blot analysis of the culture supernatants confirmed successful TEVp-mediated release of both cCD20-EC2-V5-His, CXCR4-EC2-V5-His and gp41-C46-V5-His peptides (Figure 9B, left side). Following treatment with TEVp, the cell pellets appeared white, in contrast to the pink coloration of the untreated pellets (Figure 9B, right side). Confocal microscopy showed plasma membrane localization of the tANCHORed fusion proteins, with strong mCherry fluorescence indicating correct expression and surface trafficking (Figure 9C). A cell-based ELISA using anti-V5-HRP antibodies demonstrated extracellular accessibility of the anchored peptides, resulting in surface accessibility of anti-V5-HRP antibodies for cCD20-EC2, CXCR4-EC2, gp41-derived C46, and a minimal V5-His peptide compared with the untransfected control (Figure 9D). These findings demonstrated that the ENCOREP system efficiently supports the surface display and TEVp-mediated proteolytic release of small peptides, confirming its flexibility for diverse peptide applications.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate the feasibility of utilizing the tANCHOR system to enzymatically release proteins or peptides from the surface of HEK293T cells. We used the RBD of SARS-CoV-2 to optimize the release and to investigate the efficiency of the ENCOREP system. We demonstrated that the RBD-V5-His fusion protein, flanked by TEVp cleavage sites, could be released into the medium by the addition of purified recombinant TEVp in a dose-dependent manner. We chose the TEVp-controlled release because TEVp is the most reliable and robust endopeptidase for biotechnological applications. We observe no cytotoxic effects when TEVp was used as a protease to cleave the displayed RBD from the cell surface. This is consistent with previous reports showing that TEVp activity, whether extracellular or intracellular, does not cause cytotoxic off-target cleavage in mammalian cells.29,64,65 The successful expression of TEVp in Drosophila melanogaster, coupled with its apparent non-toxic nature, underscores its high suitability for in vivo manipulations.66,67 One reason for this behavior is the strict cleavage-site specificity of TEVp. This feature of TEVp is atypical of other proteases, such as thrombin and factor Xa. These proteases often exhibit low specificity for their cleavage sites, potentially inducing toxic side effects due to proteolysis occurring at unintended sites.68

In order to minimize costs associated with externally added recombinant TEVp, we intended to co-express TEVp directly within the cultured cells. To achieve an active TEVp in the secretory pathway, which is not inhibited by unintended cysteine oxidation or by inactivating N-attached glycans, we introduced three site-specific mutations, N23Q, C130S, and T173G, and an already previously known self-inactivation resistant S219V mutation (TEVpN23Q,C130S,T173G,S219V) in the wild-type TEVp sequence (Figure 4C).29,47 With the introduced mutations, we were able to achieve secretion of active TEVp-c-Myc-His in HEK293T cells and identified the released RBD-V5-His in the supernatant (Figure 5C). Contrary to our expectations, co-cultivation of TEVp-c-Myc-His-secreting HEK293T cells could not efficiently release RBD-V5-His protein from neighboring cells expressing tANCHORed RBD-V5-His on their cell surface. We observed a reliable yield of RBD-V5-His in the supernatant only when active TEVp-c-Myc-His was expressed in the same cells presenting RBD-V5-His on the surface with flanked TEVp cleavage sites (Figure 5C). We suppose that this observation is the result of a proximity cleavage on the cell surface by TEVp-c-Myc-V5 that is de novo translated and secreted from the cell, but it is surprisingly inefficient when a neighboring cell secretes the optimized TEVp-c-Myc-V5. It is crucial for the efficient cleavage of the substrate that the distance between the protease cleavage sites and the membrane is sufficiently large. For the N-terminal TEVp cleavage site, the distance appears, to some extent, limited. However, there is a possibility that the helical structure, which is located at C-terminal of the hinge to TM3, may have a smaller angle relative to the vertical of the membrane surface and, therefore, a more upright position.69,70,71,72,73 This would result in a greater distance between the TEVp cleavage site and the membrane, which would enhance the accessibility of the cleavage site and also reduce the likelihood of steric hindrance by the CD82 small extracellular loop (SEL). The 3D orientation of the protease sites and the resulting local protein structure must be suitable for processing by TEVp. The N-terminal protease cleavage site has a helical structure, but it should be noted that the cleavage of α-helical substrates is not uncommon, even for longer segments.74 This has been identified for various types of proteases,74,75,76,77 particularly cysteine proteases, where it was observed that the local confidence of the structure prediction of corresponding, naturally occurring substrate sequences decreases in the region of the cleavage site.75,78 Our 3D modeling indicated that this is the case for the N-terminal cleavage site, located at the end of a helical segment. Studies with a serine protease have shown that a folded α-helix cannot be proteolytically cleaved, but the target species are the locally unfolded conformations of the native state ensemble.79 We assume this is also the case with our constructs. This was confirmed by our experiments, which showed that the TEVp sites are accessible and that the cleavage by TEVp is possible. The propensity for local unfolding, which may be necessary, was indicated by the observed lower confidence and quality scores at the location of the TEVp cleavage site.75 Proteolytic cleavage at the two sites is likely to occur in a serial manner. This could potentially mean that the (C-terminal) TEVp cleavage site, which appears more favorable in the present figure, is cleaved first, improving the accessibility of the N-terminal TEVp cleavage site. Alternatively, mechanical tensile forces may facilitate unfolding of the helix, thereby increasing accessibility. A comparable phenomenon has been demonstrated for a single octamer of the Ig domain I27ΔTevSite, which contains buried TEVp sites.80 Taken together, the protease sites and the resulting local protein structure must be suitable for processing by TEVp. The 3D model suggests that the secreted TEVp-V5-His must be in proximity to the membrane to bind efficiently to the TEVp cleavage sites. This implies that the co-expression of TEVp-c-Myc-His and tANCHORed RBD-V5-His results in greater proximity of the co-secreted TEVp to the cleavage sites, thereby increasing the probability of binding to these sites. Expectedly, the treatment with recombinant TEVp or expressed TEVpN23Q,C130S,T173G,S219V-c-Myc-His resulted in decreased expression of the tANCHORed RBD-V5-His, which was observed by reduced mCherry fluorescence intensity and tANCHOR-mCherry fusion protein amount (Figures 2D and 2E). The RBD-V5-His protein released from the tANCHOR backbone was found to accumulate in low amounts when the cell lysates were analyzed by western blot analysis and compared directly with the released RBD-V5-His accumulated in the culture medium. There was only a faint band observed that also could be a contamination from extracellular RBD-V5-His release (Figure 6C). This suggests that TEVp can be expressed in the same cells without saturating the secretion pathway with the RBD cleaved along the secretory pathway in the ER or the Golgi stacks. The repeated recovery of the cleaved tANCHORed RBD-V5-His from transiently transfected HEK293T cells indicates the potential for continuous protein release from a membrane-anchored chassis protein. This observation suggests the possibility of generating stable expressing cells that consistently replenish protein for cleavage along the secretory pathway or on the cell surface. HEK293T cells were selected because HEK293/293T cells lack detectable ACE2 expression and have been widely used as ACE2-negative controls in SARS-CoV-2 studies, thereby preventing receptor-mediated internalization of the surface-anchored RBD.81,82,83,84 In contrast, expression in ACE2-positive respiratory epithelial cell lines such as Calu-3 may be more physiologically relevant as a SARS-CoV-2 target cell model, but it is more challenging because RBD-ACE2 interactions can promote endocytosis and reduce surface accumulation.85 Our monitoring of cell cytotoxicity and viability, using complementary LDH release (membrane integrity) and MTT reduction (metabolic activity) assays (Figures 7A and 7B), indicates that co-expression of the modified TEVp does not impair cell viability or metabolic activity. This suggests that the transfected cells can tolerate the membrane-anchored tANCHOR backbone after cleavage, while the released protein is secreted into the medium. To evaluate whether the released proteins produced by the ENCOREP system are functionally active, we examined the well-documented interaction of the released SARS-CoV-2 RBD (delta variant) with its physiological receptor ACE2. Using soluble ACE2 and RBD captured on V5-trap magnetic particles, we observed specific receptor binding that was significantly reduced after enzymatic deglycosylation of RBD with PNGase F (Figure 8B). This experiment demonstrates that RBD protein generated via the ENCOREP system can be directly used to study receptor-ligand interactions and highlights the importance of native-like glycosylation for proper receptor recognition. To further demonstrate the versatility of the ENCOREP system, we applied it to the expression and release of small peptides. Using EC2 of the canine B-lymphocyte antigen CD20 (cCD20-EC2), CXCR4 (CXCR4-EC2), and the C46 peptide of the gp41 protein as examples, we confirmed surface localization and efficient TEVp-mediated release (Figure 9). The gp41-derived C46 peptide is a well-characterized fusion inhibitor of HIV-1, and its successful processing together with cCD20-EC2-V5-His and CXCR4-EC2-V5-His demonstrates that even short peptides (21–50 amino acids) can be effectively displayed and released using the ENCOREP system. These results highlight the versatility of the ENCOREP platform for producing peptides in mammalian cells. These glycosylated peptides can be used for further binding analyses, for example, to map interactions between the CD63 LEL and gp41, as previously reported.44,86,87,88,89 Of special interest is the LEL of tetraspanins, which is essential for the functions of tetraspanin proteins, as most protein-protein interaction sites have been mapped to the LEL so far.90 While members of the tetraspanin superfamily, which carry an LEL ectodomain, may possess one or more potential N-linked glycosylation sites on the LEL, there are limited reports on the functional effects of these PTMs. The biological function of tetraspanin molecules is mainly affected by their glycosylation.91 We demonstrated the in situ production of the LEL of CD63 (CD63 LEL) from the membrane-bound tANCHOR fusion proteins. An assessment of tANCHORed CD63LEL-V5-His released from the cell surface by recombinant TEVp, compared with the secreted, soluble CD63LEL-V5-His variant, showed that the degree of glycosylation differs markedly (Figure 3D). Highly glycosylated proteoforms were found to be more abundant when comparing CD63LEL-V5-His released from a membrane-anchored extracellular loop and that secreted as a soluble protein. The glycosylated tANCHORed CD63LEL-V5-His, exhibiting native-like glycosylation characteristics, which stays attached to the membrane during its travel through the secretory pathway, may, therefore, be functionally superior to the less glycosylated soluble CD63LEL-V5-His domain, which is transported in the lumen of the compartments of the secretory pathway where it is more accessible to soluble glycosidases, resulting in diminished glycosylation.37 Additionally, the sequence of signal peptides may influence glycosylation patterns due to changed translation kinetics, differences in co-translational N-glycosylation occupancy, and differential processing due to changed ER retention times, as was shown for the HIV-1 envelope protein gp120.92,93

Tetraspanins are important proteins in virology, bacteriology, and cancer research.94,95,96 Soluble CD9LEL-derived peptides are potent inhibitors of Staphylococcus aureus on keratinocytes.97 The availability of glycosylated tetraspanin LEL peptides in soluble form, such as those derived from the ENCOREP system, is important for future research to test whether their inhibitory efficacy against S. aureus can be increased. Our aim was not to define the complete glycoform composition but to demonstrate that the ENCOREP approach supports N-glycan processing pathways typical for the mammalian host cells. In future applications, detailed glycan structural analyses can be combined with ENCOREP to assess system-specific glycoform variations. This will be a topic of future investigation. For our proof-of-concept, we clearly demonstrated the ability to produce TEVp-released glycosylated proteins between 15 and 40 kDa at a yield of around 0.7 μg/mL (0.7 mg/L) from a single 6-well cell culture plate (Figure 6D). The production of small secreted proteins and peptides in HEK293 systems often results in substantially lower titers than those reported for large, well-secreted proteins such as antibodies, for which yields of approximately 10–20 mg/L in adherent HEK293T cells and up to ∼140 mg/L in optimized HEK293-6E suspension cultures can be achieved.98 Even with optimized suspension protocols, multiple targets expressed as secreted proteins in HEK293-F have been reported with yields below 1 mg/L, illustrating that low-mg/L productivity is common for challenging secreted cargos.99 Similarly, a panel of 14 secreted soluble proteins or ECDs expressed from stable HEK293S GnTI− pools showed an average expression level of <10 mg/L despite the use of an optimized production system.100 For individual small proteins such as the chemokine CCL8, HEK293 expression yielded approximately 1.5 mg/L of the purified soluble protein.101 These examples indicate that for small or structurally demanding secreted proteins, particularly those containing disulfide bonds and N-glycosylation sites, the typical HEK293 yields are usually in the 1–10 mg/L range, and can even be lower without extensive construct and process optimization. Within this realistic productivity spectrum, the current ENCOREP implementation yields of the released protein from a 6-well HEK293T culture place it squarely at the lower end of the reported HEK293 yields for comparable small secreted proteins yet sufficient for biochemical characterization and functional assays. In the context of upscaled production, the utilization of FreeStyle 293-F or Expi293F suspension cells is advantageous. These cell lines have been adapted from HEK293 to thrive in denser cultures, resulting in higher expression yields.102,103,104 For further optimization, it is advisable to employ these specific cell lines for scaling up protein release. The presented approach aims to optimize ENCOREP protein yield, facilitating subsequent purification steps essential for both research endeavors and potential therapeutic applications.

Limitations of the study

The developed ENCOREP system is highly dependent on a successful display of the protein of interest on cell surfaces. The sequence connected to the TEVp cleavage sites should ideally have a low content of polar amino acids, especially arginine and serine. This optimization is crucial for enhancing the efficiency of displaying the tetraspanin chimeric membrane protein on cell surface.105 If this is not possible, linkers with a low polarity should be introduced. The present study did not include detailed glycoform profiling by mass spectrometry. While our results clearly demonstrate that ENCOREP enables the production of glycosylated proteins and peptides that undergo N-linked glycosylation in mammalian cells, precise glycan compositions were not analyzed. The primary goal was to establish a versatile expression and release platform compatible with native-like glycosylation, rather than to comprehensively characterize individual glycoforms. Future studies integrating ENCOREP with advanced glycoproteomic analyses could further elucidate cell type-specific glycan heterogeneity and site occupancy. In addition, as HEK293T cells were used as the main model system, the resulting glycosylation profiles may not fully reflect those of specialized physiological tissues, and future work in alternative cell types will be valuable to assess system-specific differences.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines and cell culture

HEK293T or HeLa cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Germany, part of Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), 100 IU/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) and 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco) at 37°C, 5% CO2 and a relative humidity of about 95%. Cells were typically cultivated in tissue cell culture flasks T75 (TPP, Trasadingen, Switzerland) and were trypsinized using trypsin-EDTA (Gibco). Cells were tested for mycoplasma contamination using a qPCR-based assay performed by Eurofins Genomics (Ebersberg, Germany) and were routinely monitored by DAPI staining. Cell lines were sourced from in-house stocks and were not independently authenticated. Morphology and growth behavior were routinely monitored and were consistent with those expected for HEK293T and HeLa cells.

Method details

Molecular cloning

The generated vectors for ENCOREP are based on the ptANCHOR-CD63-V5-His-mCherry or ptANCHOR-CD82-V5-His-mCherry vector (ATG:biosynthetics GmbH, Merzhausen, Germany). DNA coding sequence for the released protein is flanked by the DNA coding sequence for the cleavage site ENLYFQG of the tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease. This sequence was fused with the 5′-sided EcoRI (GAATTC) and 3′-sided EcoRV (GATATC) restriction sites. All DNA inserts were optimized for expression in HEK293T and produced by gene synthesis (ATG:biosynthetics GmbH, Merzhausen, Germany). All DNA sequences and accessions are listed in Tables S1 and S2. Inserts were isolated by restriction from the gene synthesis vector with EcoRI/EcoRV enzymes (NEB, New England Biolabs GmbH, Frankfurt, Germany) and ligated into EcoRI/EcoRV digested ptANCHOR-CD63-V5-His-mCherry or ptANCHOR-CD82-V5-His-mCherry vector using T4 ligase (NEB). Ligation was transformed into DH5α E. coli chemocompetent cells (NEB). The same way the gene synthesis fragment coding for the 236 N-terminal amino acids of TEV protease (NCBI protein NP_734212) was fused with a c-Myc-His-tag (EQKLISEEDLHHHHHH) and cloned into the vector FlexMam-Puro (ATG:biosynthetics GmbH, Merzhausen, Germany) using the restriction sites NheI and PmeI. In order to introduce three additional mutation sites (N23Q, C130S, T173G) into the template sequence containing already the S219V mutation site-directed mutagenesis was performed as previously described using Q5 polymerase, DpnI and chemocompetent E. coli DH5α (high efficiency) from NEB.44 Primers containing the desired point mutations are listed in Table S3, and the resulting protein sequence is shown in Table S4. All constructs were verified by restriction analysis and Sanger sequencing. The plasmid pCMV-CD63-mCherry was previously described.107 Plasmid DNA for cell transfection was prepared using a MaxiPrep kit, and plasmid DNA for molecular cloning or site-directed mutagenesis was isolated using QIAprep Spin Miniprep kits (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany).

Cell transfection

Cells were cultured and detached using trypsin–EDTA (Gibco), counted, and seeded at 3.0 × 105 cells per well (6-well plates), 1.5 × 105 cells per well (12-well plates), or 0.2 × 105 cells per well (96-well plates). Cells were transfected at 80–90% confluency with the indicated plasmid DNA using jetPRIME buffer and reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Polyplus, Illkirch, France). Transfection complexes were added to the cells and incubated for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with pre-warmed (37°C) medium containing 2% FBS and recombinant TEV protease (New England Biolabs, Frankfurt, Germany) at the indicated dilution.

Isolation of released His-tagged proteins

Supernatants were collected and centrifuged in order to separate cell debris from the supernatants. Cleared supernatants were adjusted to a pH of 8.0 with 0.1 M NaOH solution (Carl Roth GmbH & Co. KG, Karlsruhe, Germany) and added to 50 μL slurry of His-tag isolation Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) and incubated for 1 h at 4 °C. Dynabeads were washed two times with wash buffer containing 50 mM of Na3PO4 (Carl Roth), 300 mM of NaCl (Carl Roth), 0.01% of Tween-20 (Carl Roth) at pH 8.0 and one time with 1× PBS. After magnetic separation bound His-tagged protein was eluted from Dynabeads with 10 μL of elution buffer at a pH of 8.0 containing 300 mM of imidazole (Carl Roth), 300 mM of NaCl, 50 mM of Na3PO4 and 0.01% of Tween-20. Eluted proteins were mixed with 20 μL of 2× Laemmli buffer containing 10% β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, Steinheim, Germany). His-tagged protein extracts were analyzed by Western blot analysis. To analyze glycosylated proteins based on molecular size shifts, Dynabeads were treated with PNGase F (NEB) under denaturing conditions after binding of the His-tagged protein, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PNGase F enzyme removes N-linked oligosaccharides from glycoproteins. After PNGase F treatment, bound His-tagged protein was eluted from Dynabeads with 10 μL of elution buffer and 20 μL of 2× Laemmli buffer was added.

Western blot analysis

HEK293T cells were lysed for analysis of whole cell protein expression by adding 50 μL (12 well format) or 100 μL (6 well format) of Laemmli buffer 2× (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 250 U/mL Benzonase (Merck Millipore, part of Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) and protease Halt inhibitor 100× (Thermo Fisher). Cell extracts were boiled for 3 min at 90 °C and centrifuged in order to separate cell debris. Prepared protein extracts were separated by SDS-PAGE by using 4-15% Protean TGX gels (Biorad, Munich, Germany). SDS-PAGE buffer contained 144.4 g/L glycine (Carl Roth), 30.3 g/L Tris (tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane) (Carl Roth) and 10 g/L SDS pellets (Carl Roth). Separated proteins were transferred to a 0.2 μm PVDF Mini trans-blot membrane (Bio-Rad, München, Germany) and then blocked for 1 h with 10% low fat milk powder (Carl Roth) diluted in pure water. Proteins were detected by incubating for 1 h with the specific primary antibodies anti-V5-HRP (Invitrogen, part of Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) diluted 1:10,000, anti-mCherry (Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom) diluted 1:1,000, anti-beta-actin-HRP (Thermo Fisher Scientific) diluted 1:10,000, and anti-FLAG-HRP (Sigma-Aldrich, Steinheim, Germany) diluted 1:15,000, respectively. In order to detect CD63 we used the same antibodies as previously described.44 HRP activity was detected by enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) using Pierce ECL Western Blotting substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or Atto SuperSignal substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and imaged with a ChemoCam digital imaging system (INTAS, Göttingen, Germany). To verify the RBD-ACE2 interaction assay by immunoblotting, the TMB reagent was removed and the magnetic particles were washed with 1× PBS. Subsequently, 50 μL of 2× Laemmli sample buffer was added, and the samples were boiled for 3 min at 90 °C. The solubilized proteins were analyzed by SDS-PAGE followed by Western blotting. RBD-V5-His was detected as described above, and ACE2 was detected using the procedure previously described.61 Small peptides derived from cCD20, gp41, and CXCR4 and transferred to a PVDF membrane were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Carl Roth) in 1× PBS for 15 min prior to incubation with antibodies.

Lectin blot (SNA) analysis of protein glycosylation

RBD-V5-His protein captured from the supernatant with V5-Trap magnetic agarose beads, as described above, was separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane, which was blocked as described in the western blot analysis section. After blocking, the membrane was washed and incubated for 2 h with biotinylated Sambucus nigra lectin (SNA, Vector Laboratories, Biozol, Hamburg, Germany) at a concentration of 5 μg/mL in 3% BSA. The membrane was then washed three times with 1× PBS and incubated with streptavidin-HRP (R&D Systems, Bio-Techne, Wiesbaden, Germany) in a dilution of 1:200 in 3% BSA for 45 min. Lectin-bound protein was detected using ECL substrate Atto SuperSignal substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Quantification of released protein

Supernatants were prepared as described for the isolation of released protein by the 6× His-tag. 1 mL of supernatant collected from transfected HEK293T cells (6 well format) with 2 μg of ptANCHOR-CD82-TEVRBD-V5-HisTEV-mCherry was mixed with 4 μL of jetPRIME reagent (Polyplus) according to the manual’s instructions and after 24 h post-transfection cells were treated with TEV protease (800 U/mL, NEB). After 24 h supernatant was adjusted to a pH of 7.2 and 20 μL of nanobody-based V5-Trap slurry (ChromoTek, Planegg-Martinsried, Germany) was added and incubated for 3 h at 4 °C. According to the manufacturer’s specifications, the magnetic agarose exhibits a binding capacity of 15 μg of recombinant V5-tagged protein (∼30 kDa) per 25 μL bead slurry. In case of co-transfection experiments HEK293T cells were co-transfected with 2 μg of ptANCHOR-CD82-TEVRBD-V5-HisTEV-mCherry and 3 μg pTEVpN23Q,C130S,T173G,S219V-c-Myc-His, or with the indicated amount of 2 μg plasmid DNA ptANCHOR-CD82-TEVRBD-V5-HisTEV-mCherry for the control without expression of the TEVpN23Q,C130S,T173G,S219V-c-Myc-His, by using 10 μL of jetPRIME (Polyplus) reagent. 20 μL of V5-Trap slurry are enough to bind 14 μg of V5-tagged protein with a molecular mass of 30 kDa (please refer to the manufacturer’s manual for more detailed information). Magnetic V5-Trap beads were then washed twice with 500 μL of 1× PBS containing 0.05% of Tween-20, were collected subsequently in a new reaction tube and were resuspended in 10 μL of 2× Laemmli buffer. Samples were heated at 95°C for 3 minutes and centrifuged in order to separate cell debris. A protein standard was generated by dilution of 2 mg/mL of bovine serum albumin (BSA, Pierce, Thermo Fisher Scientific) leading to amounts between 100 and 800 ng per lane. Sample and standard curve proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) as described for the Western blot analysis and stained with Coomassie blue (0.1% of brilliant blue R250 (Carl Roth), 50% of methanol (Carl Roth), 10% of glacial acetic acid (Carl Roth), 40% of water) for 1 h. The gel was subsequently destained with 50% of water, 40% of methanol and 10% of glacial acetic acid. Destained gels were imaged by using a Sony NEX-5 camera (Sony Europe B.V., Berlin, Germany). Visualized protein bands were quantified by comparison of the integrated density values of the protein bands of samples and BSA standard processed by the Fiji gel analyzer plugin.108

Quantification of expressed mCherry fusion protein

Expressed mCherry fusion protein was quantified by using the mCherry standard protein from a commercially available mCherry quantification kit (Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom). Cell pellets of transfected HEK293T cells, from a 6 well plate, were lysed in 1.0 mL of mCherry assay buffer and were incubated on ice for 15 min. Samples were centrifuged in order to remove cell debris and 100 μL of the supernatants were transferred to a 96-well optical plate (Greiner Bio-One, Frickenhausen, Germany). In addition, the mCherry standard protein, which was provided with the mCherry quantification kit, was used to generate a standard curve (diluted in mCherry assay buffer to obtain 0, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, 120, 140, 160 and 180 ng per well). This standard curve was used to calculate from the relative light units (RLU) readings of the samples the amount of mCherry protein that was extracted from the cell pellet. All fluorescence values were measured using the GloMax-Multi+ detection system in combination with the filter kit green for red dyes (Promega Corporation, Fitchburg, WI, USA). The mean RLU value measured for wells containing only mCherry assay buffer was subtracted from all well readings.

Cell-based ELISA to assess extracellular accessibility of tANCHORed proteins

All steps for a cell-based ELISA (HeLa) were performed as previously described.33,109,110 Briefly, the tANCHORed protein fused to a V5-His-tag was detected by incubation with a mouse anti-V5-HRP antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a 1:5,000 dilution. A solution of 3% BSA and 2% chicken albumin in 1× PBS containing 10% normal goat serum was used as the blocking and antibody dilution buffer. Plates were sealed with sealing film (Carl Roth) during incubation times.

Monitoring of cell toxicity by LDH release assay

Cell toxicity of the treatment with TEV protease (TEVp) was monitored by measuring the lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release in the cell supernatants for each TEVp concentration. 1 × 104 HEK293T cells were seeded in a 96-well plate and grown until a cell density of 80% was reached. Cells were then transfected with 0.1 μg of plasmid DNA ptANCHOR-CD82-TEVRBD-V5-HisTEV-mCherry by using the transfection reagent jetPRIME (Polyplus) according to the manufacturer’s manual. In co-transfection experiments, cells were additionally transfected with the plasmid pTEVpN23Q,C130S,T173G,S219V-c-Myc-His to express active TEVp in the secretory pathway. As control, cells were transfected with the plasmid ptANCHOR-CD82-TEVRBD-V5-HisTEV-mCherry only. The supernatants were collected at the indicated timepoints and centrifuged in order to remove the cell debris. 50 μL of the supernatant was added to 50 μL of substrate in order to measure the level of LDH release with the Roche Cytotoxicity Detection Kit (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). The optical density was measured at 492 nm with correction at 690 nm using a microplate reader Thermo Multiskan GO (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Vantaa, Finland). As a lytic control, total LDH content of cells treated with 1% IGEPAL CA-630 (Sigma-Aldrich) was measured.

Cell viability and proliferation analysis

Cell viability was assessed by monitoring the reduction of 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) to insoluble formazan crystals by mitochondrial dehydrogenases in viable cells. HEK293T cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells (in 100 μL DMEM) per well and transfected the following day with the indicated plasmids encoding the modified TEVpN23Q,C130S,T173G,S219V-c-Myc-His variant and/or cleavable tANCHORed RBD constructs using jetPRIME (Polyplus) under the same transfection conditions as described for the LDH assay. Control cells were treated with jetPRIME only. After 48 h post-transfection, cell viability was assessed using the MTT reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a concentration of 12 mM (5 mg/mL) in 1× PBS. Briefly, 8 μL of MTT reagent was added to each well and incubated for 3 h at 37 °C. The resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in 50 μL of 0.01 M HCl containing 10% SDS, incubated for 3 h at 37 °C, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader. The viability of untreated control cells was set to 100%, and relative viability was calculated for all transfection conditions.

TEVp activity assay

We conducted fluorometric TEVp activity measurements using the TEV Protease Activity Assay Kit (Abcam). Initially, supernatants obtained from HEK293T cells 48 hours post-transfection (by using 4 μg of plasmid DNA and jetPRIME transfection reagent, as described above) containing the specified TEVp expression constructs were collected and centrifuged to eliminate cell debris. These supernatants were stored at -80°C until use. Prior to commencing measurements, we placed the Promega GloMax-Multi+ detection system equipped with the blue filter kit for green dyes (excitation wavelength: 490 nm, emission wavelength: 510-570 nm) inside a Multitron II incubator (Infors HT, Basel, Switzerland) and prewarmed it for 30 minutes at 34°C. We then prepared the detection setups to ensure immediate measurement of the samples upon addition of the reaction mix. In a 96-well plate, we loaded 50 μL of supernatant along with 50 μL of the reaction mix (containing 48 μL of TEV protease assay buffer and 2 μL of TEV protease substrate), initiating kinetic mode measurements at 2-minute intervals. Fluorescence was monitored for 78 minutes to analyze the influence of mutation sites on TEVp activity, employing a separate protease activity curve for each TEVp variant.

Measuring ACE2 binding to released glycosylated versus deglycosylated SARS-CoV-2 delta RBD

To assess the binding of released SARS-CoV-2 RBD and to evaluate the effect of glycosylation on ACE2 interaction, soluble RBD-V5-His (Delta variant) released from HEK293T cells transfected with the ptANCHOR-CD82-TEVRBD-V5-HISTEV-mCherry construct was collected from conditioned medium as described above. 40 μL of the V5-trap magnetic particles (ChromoTek) was used to capture the released RBD by incubation with 5 mL of culture supernatant for 2 h at room temperature under gentle rotation to ensure saturation of the V5-trap binding sites. According to the manufacturer, V5-trap M-270 magnetic particles have a binding capacity of 0.62 – 1.25 μg of recombinant V5-tagged protein (∼30 kDa) per 25 μL of bead slurry. After washing with 1× PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 to remove unbound material, magnetic particles were centrifuged, resuspended in 200 μL Tris buffer pH 7.8. 100 μL of resuspended loaded magnetic particles carrying immobilized RBD were either left untreated (RBD control) or subjected to enzymatic deglycosylation using 3 μL (30 U) PNGase F (Promega, Walldorf, Germany) in 100 μL Tris buffer pH 7.8 and incubated for 6 h. Magnetic particles V5-trap slurry was washed two times with 1× PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20. For the interaction assay, 200 μL conditioned medium containing soluble, secreted ACE2 (amino acids 1-740) as previously described was added to the RBD-loaded magnetic particles and incubated for 1.5 h at room temperature.61 After incubation, the magnetic V5-trap particles were washed four times with washing buffer between antibody steps. Bound ACE2 was detected using a rabbit anti-ACE2 primary antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a dilution of 1:1000, followed by an HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Dako, Agilent Technologies, Denmark) at a dilution of 1:5000. After the final wash, the magnetic particles were transferred into a new tube, washed again once and 100 μL of TMB substrate was added. The colorimetric reaction was stopped by adding 50 μL of 2 M H2SO4, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm without the magnetic particles.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) and epifluorescence microscopy

1 × 104 HEK293T cells per well were seeded into high glass-bottomed eight-well IBIDI μ-slides (Ibidi, Munich, Germany) and after 24 h cells were transiently transfected with 0.5 μg of plasmid DNA and 1.0 μL of Metafectene transfection reagent (Biontex Laboratories GmbH, Munich, Germany). At 24 h post-transfection, cells were washed once with 1× PBS and fixed with 200 μl of 2% PFA in 1× PBS for 20 min. Cells were washed once with 1 × PBS and left in 200 μl of 1× PBS in the presence of 1 μl of Hoechst 33342 (ImmunoChemistry Technologies, LLC, Davis, CA, USA). All images were acquired using an inverted confocal laser scanning microscope LSM 780 (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Oberkochen, Germany) and a plan-apochromat oil immersion objective (63 ×, numerical aperture 1.4; Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH). Fluorescence signals were detected with the Zeiss ZEN smart setup settings for Hoechst 33342 and mCherry dyes.

Generating 3D structure models for estimation of accessibility of the TEVp cleavage sites

The trRosetta (transform-restrained Rosetta) web server was employed to model the expressed membrane protein structures.111 trRosetta utilizes a deep neural network to predict the geometries between the amino acids. These predictions are transformed into constraints to guide structure prediction using direct energy minimization.112 Additionally, homologous templates can be used as inputs to the network, which enhances prediction accuracy, particularly for fusion proteins composed of native components, which fold independently from each other.111 The standard options (with homologous templates) were used. Subsequently, the PPM (Positioning of Proteins in Membranes) Web Server version 3.0 was employed to ascertain the geometry of the membrane embedding, utilizing the structural model from trRosetta as the input.113 The PPM method achieves this by minimizing the free energy of transfer of a transmembrane protein structure when moved from water to the membrane environment, which is considered to be a liquid anisotropic solvent. The default settings were employed, with the exception of the type of membrane, which was specified as “mammalian plasma membrane”. Furthermore, coordinates of additional dummy atoms that represent the lipid carbonyl groups of the membrane bilayer (visualized as gray bullets in Figure 5D) were added to the model. For purposes of visualization, the PDB files of TEVp-c-Myc-His and the tANCHOR-RBD construct were initially loaded into the molecular-graphics, -modeling and -simulation program YASARA View.114 The structures were provisionally aligned against each other, and the aforementioned embedding procedure was repeated with a merged coordinate file exported from YASARA containing both structures. The local structural quality of transmembrane protein models was evaluated by using statistical potentials with QMEANBrane.53

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD, and the number of independent biological replicates (n) is provided in the corresponding figure legends. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.2.0). All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For comparisons between two independent groups, an unpaired Welch’s t-test was used. For analyses involving two experimental factors, data were analyzed by two-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test (comparisons as specified in the figure legends). For single-factor comparisons among more than two groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s or Dunnett’s multiple comparisons post hoc tests were applied. For normalized data, a one-sample Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare the median to the hypothetical value of 1 (normalized control). Significance levels were defined as: ns, p > 0.05; ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001. Cut-off values were defined as the mean + 3 standard deviations (SDs), calculated from negative control samples.
