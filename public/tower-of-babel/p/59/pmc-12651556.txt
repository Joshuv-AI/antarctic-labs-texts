Integrated Care Models for Hepatitis C: Lessons From Southeast Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa
Authors: Halder J Abozait, Nawfal R Hussein
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatitis C continues to pose a significant global health burden, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, where access to diagnosis and treatment remains limited. This perspective explores how integrated care models, featuring decentralization, task-shifting, and simplified protocols, can accelerate hepatitis C elimination. Drawing on case studies from Egypt, Rwanda, Cambodia, Malaysia, and Nigeria, we highlight key enablers such as political commitment, infrastructure integration, and public engagement. These examples demonstrate that scalable, cost-effective strategies can achieve high treatment uptake and cure rates, even in resource-constrained settings. The paper underscores the importance of adapting successful models to local contexts and calls for broader adoption of World Health Organization–aligned policies to meet 2030 elimination targets.

Hepatitis C is a major global health challenge, with 50 million people carrying hepatitis C virus (HCV) and 240 000 dying from it in 2022 [1]. About 75% of the HCV burden affects low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), highlighting the necessity of targeted public health strategies addressing HCV in these areas [2]. From 2015 to 2022, only 36% of HCV-infected individuals were diagnosed, and 20% received treatment [1]. These are far below the aspirations set by the World Health Organization (WHO), which is eliminating viral hepatitis as a public health threat by diagnosing 90% of HCV-infected individuals and treating 80% by 2030 [1]. We use the term integrated care model (ICM) to describe a structured approach to hepatitis C management that combines decentralization of services, task-shifting to nonspecialist providers, and simplification of treatment protocols within existing health care systems. Although not a universally standardized term in hepatitis C literature, ICM effectively encapsulates the core strategies recommended by the WHO for expanding access to care in resource-limited settings. Similarly, the term “nonadopting countries” refers to those countries that have not yet implemented national hepatitis C elimination programs aligned with WHO guidelines and ICMs.

Understanding Integrated Care Models in Hepatitis C

The traditional model of hepatitis C management with interferon therapy was accompanied by significant side effects, and treatment had to be specialist-delivered [3]. Subsequent discovery of direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) was promising, with higher sustained virologic response (SVR) rates, but the regimens were complex and had to be combined with peginterferon and ribavirin [4]. The side effects from DAAs and the need for sensitive HCV viral load assays to determine treatment success made hepatitis C care more specialist-dependent [3]. The second phase of DAA evolution was the initial availability of all-oral regimens for some genotypes, while others still required specialist care with genotyping and resistance testing [3]. The last phase of DAA evolution was the introduction of highly effective, pangenotypic agents that could be easily administered with shorter treatment courses and fewer side effects [3]. These properties led to the intent to integrate hepatitis C care into a simplified model that can be delivered without intensive supervision. There are 5 key features of ICMs in hepatitis C: first, decentralization of hepatitis C care delivery from tertiary and specialist facilities to lower-level facilities; second, integration of hepatitis C care into the existing services such as harm reduction programs and HIV services; third, task-shifting and capacity-building such as transferring care to primary care physicians or nurses while also providing sufficient training for them; fourth, a simplified care pathway where most of the services are delivered in a single center with minimal interruption of care; fifth, continuous care and follow-up integration into the model. A global-level meta-analysis investigated data from ∼500 000 patients in 142 studies published from 2008 to 2018 [5]. Results showed that decentralization and integration were associated with better access to diagnostic tests, linkage to care, and treatment uptake. Task-shifting to nonspecialists was associated with high cure rates, similar to the specialists. The WHO recommends simplifying HCV care delivery by expanding HCV testing and treatment services, ideally at the same site through decentralization, integration, and task-sharing to appropriately trained nonspecialist doctors and nurses [6]. Follow-up of the patients in the same decentralized convenient centers where they started testing and treatment has been associated with high patient retention and SVR [7]. A study evaluating the effects of telemedicine on decentralized hepatitis C care at drug treatment centers showed that telemedicine is a useful method to re-include patients lost to follow-up for continuing care [8].

Rwanda was the first country in Sub-Saharan Africa to commit to HCV elimination, launching a 5-year national plan in 2018 [10]. The program leveraged existing HIV infrastructure, enabled task-shifting, simplified care pathways, and decentralized services [11]. Negotiated access to low-cost DAAs and district-level accountability further supported scale-up [12]. More than 7 million people were screened, with treatment outcomes showing 94% SVR12 [11]. A 2024 projection suggests that Rwanda could eliminate HCV by 2027, preventing thousands of deaths and saving millions in health care costs [12]. In Cambodia, a rural pilot demonstrated the feasibility of integrating HCV care into primary health centers using nurse-led models. Despite limited resources, patient retention was high, and SVR12 rates reached 94%–98.5% [13]. These results support decentralization and simplification as viable strategies for rural areas [14]. Malaysia tackled underdiagnosis through national screening campaigns and decentralized, nurse-led testing in public clinics. A pilot introduced same-day test-and-treat for rural drug users, resulting in near-universal treatment uptake and SVR12 rates over 92.2% [15]. In Nigeria, where funding limitations persist, Nasarawa State integrated HCV care into existing TB clinics. Utilizing spare capacity on GeneXpert machines allowed for same-day RNA testing and treatment without disrupting TB services [6, 11]. This model demonstrates how existing infrastructure can be adapted for HCV programs, even in resource-constrained settings. Egypt, once home to the highest global HCV prevalence [16], launched a massive, government-led response involving local DAA production, school-based screening, and decentralized treatment. Screening 50 million people and treating >1.6 million, Egypt achieved SVR12 rates above 98% [17]. The program not only met WHO targets but also demonstrated cost savings of $7 billion by 2021, earning WHO Gold-Tier status in 2023 [18].

Policy and Implementation Strategies

The success of ICMs for hepatitis C elimination hinges on a few critical policy and implementation strategies. First, strong political commitment is essential to mobilize resources, negotiate lower drug prices, and sustain national programs. Second, integration of hepatitis C services into existing health care infrastructure, such as HIV, TB, or harm-reduction programs, maximizes efficiency. Third, decentralization of services to primary care settings, combined with task-shifting to trained nonspecialists, increases accessibility in underserved areas. Fourth, simplified protocols and same-day testing and treatment help minimize loss to follow-up. Finally, public engagement through awareness campaigns is vital for improving health-seeking behavior and reducing stigma. Though these strategies require upfront investment, long-term savings and health gains, as demonstrated in countries like Egypt and Rwanda, make them highly cost-effective. For nonadopting countries, adapting these approaches to local contexts can accelerate progress toward meeting the WHO's 2030 hepatitis C elimination targets.

Limitations

This perspective highlights selected case studies to illustrate ICM for hepatitis C but does not offer a comprehensive review or comparative analysis between the models. This may introduce selection bias, and findings may not be generalizable to all settings due to differences in health systems, resources, and political contexts. Despite this, the examples provide valuable insights to inform policy in similar resource-constrained settings.

CONCLUSIONS

Eliminating hepatitis C in LMICs is achievable with the adoption of ICMs that emphasize decentralization, task-shifting, and simplification of services. Case studies from countries like Egypt, Rwanda, and Cambodia demonstrate that political commitment, infrastructure adaptation, and community engagement are key drivers of success. While challenges remain, especially in nonadopting countries, the tools and strategies needed for elimination are well established. Scaling up these proven models offers a realistic path toward achieving the WHO 2030 elimination goals and reducing the global burden of hepatitis C.
