The ‘leaky pipeline’ in orthopaedic surgery: why female trainees drop out?
Authors: Ahmad Furqan Anjum, Muhammad Burhan Anjum, Rahmeen Nauman, Raza ur Rehman Rana
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOrthopaedic surgery remains one of the least gender-diverse medical specialties, with women making up a small fraction of practicing surgeons despite increasing female enrolment in medical schools. The “leaky pipeline” phenomenon describes the progressive loss of female trainees due to systemic barriers such as implicit bias, workplace discrimination, inequitable surgical training opportunities, and inadequate work-life balance policies. Understanding the factors contributing to female trainee attrition is essential for fostering gender equity in orthopaedic surgery.Main bodyFemale orthopaedic trainees face unique challenges, including stereotypes that question their physical capability, limited access to mentorship and sponsorship, and an unsupportive workplace culture. Many report experiencing discrimination, microaggressions, and exclusion from leadership pathways. Additionally, disparities in surgical case assignments limit skill development and career advancement, further discouraging retention. Work-life balance remains a major concern, as rigid training schedules and insufficient parental leave policies force many female trainees to reconsider their career paths. These systemic barriers contribute to higher attrition rates and hinder efforts to diversify the orthopaedic workforce. To address these issues, institutions must implement targeted reforms. Formal mentorship and sponsorship programs can support female trainees in securing leadership roles and competitive fellowships. Equitable case distribution policies are necessary to ensure fair training opportunities. Institutional commitment to creating inclusive environments through bias training and zero-tolerance policies for discrimination can help improve retention. Additionally, introducing family-friendly policies, such as flexible scheduling and structured parental leave, can make orthopaedic surgery a more viable career choice for women. Early outreach initiatives should also be expanded to encourage female interest in the specialty.ConclusionGender disparities in orthopaedic surgery persist due to longstanding systemic challenges. Without targeted interventions, the leaky pipeline will continue to exclude talented female surgeons, limiting diversity, innovation, and patient care. Institutional commitment to mentorship, equitable training opportunities, and supportive workplace policies is essential for retaining female trainees and achieving gender equity in orthopaedics. Addressing these barriers will ensure that success in the field is determined by skill and dedication rather than gender.

Introduction

Orthopaedic surgery remains a gender-disadvantaged medical specialty with women making up less than 10% of practicing orthopaedic surgeons despite increasing numbers of female medical graduates [1]. “The leaky pipeline” is used to describe progressive female losses through various stages of training based on systemic barriers such as implicit bias, lack of mentorship, and challenges in work-life balance [2]. This imbalance has significant workforce diversity implications as diverse surgical teams enhance patient care, improve decision-making, and promote innovation [3]. Resolution of these challenges is required to offer equitable career options in orthopaedics. This correspondence aims to address causes of female trainee attrition and encourage systemic reforms to improve retention and gender equality in orthopaedic surgery.

Attrition trends among female orthopaedic trainees

Despite increasing female enrolment in medical school classes, gender imbalance persists in orthopaedic surgery with female trainees experiencing higher rates of disproportionate attrition. Orthopaedic surgery remains one of the least gender-diverse specialties with women comprising only 6–15% of practicing orthopaedic surgeons globally [4]. Female orthopaedic residents are said to experience a 24% rate of attrition compared to significantly lower rates for men [5]. Regional disparities are also present. Female surgical residency attrition is twice that of men in the United States [6]. An Irish study indicated that female representation in orthopaedic surgery has remained stagnant for 16 years and is reflective of slow progress toward reducing systemic barriers [7]. The persistence of these trends underscores the need for targeted retention strategies. Gender disparities in mentorship, case assignment, and workplace culture are among the reasons for these losses and contribute to the imperative for structural program changes [8]. Systemic barriers leading to attrition in female orthopaedic trainees

Gender bias and stereotypes

Orthopaedic surgery has been stereotypically considered a man’s domain for decades, with implicit biases informing the recruitment and retention of female trainees. Research has identified that women in orthopaedics are subject to subtle and pervasive biases and are perceived to be less competent to perform physically demanding tasks in orthopaedics [10]. Implicit bias is also evident in faculty support and career mentorship, with men receiving more advice and being directed toward leadership positions [11].

Workplace culture and discrimination

An unfriendly work culture is a key cause of female attrition in orthopaedics. Research has identified that 60% of female surgical trainees report gender-based discrimination and that incidents of microaggressions and harassment are prevalent [12]. Male leadership structures reinforce this phenomenon by limiting female role models who can advocate for systemic changes [13].

Work-life balance and family considerations

Orthopaedic surgery schedules are demanding and make work-life balance a key issue for female trainees. Many institutions lack adequate policies for maternity leave, and long and inflexible hours make it difficult for women to balance family and training [14]. Therefore, female trainees are more likely than men to reconsider career choices and transition to less surgically demanding specialties [6].

Fewer mentorship and sponsorship opportunities

Mentorship and sponsorship are key determinants of career progress, yet female trainees are denied access to senior mentors who can advocate for them for advancement [15]. The dominance of men’s networks— often referred to as the “old boys’ club”—confines women in orthopaedics to networking and leadership roles [16].

Disparities in surgical case assignments and skill development

Research shows that female residents are allocated fewer complicated surgical cases, which decreases self-confidence and technical competency [9]. This discrepancy causes delayed career progression, as volume and procedure complexity are key determinants for gaining fellowships and leadership positions.

Thus, all these systemic barriers collectively contribute to higher female orthopaedic trainee attrition. Resolution of these challenges requires institution-wide reforms such as bias training, equitable distribution of cases, and formal mentorship programs to create a more inclusive orthopaedic environment for female trainees.

Call to action: sealing the leaks in the pipeline

The continued gender imbalance in orthopaedic surgery demands wholesale changes in institutions that erase discrimination, offer training equality, and create a culture of inclusion. Unless addressed through deliberate and collective action, the “leaky pipeline” will continue to drain talented female surgeons out of orthopaedic surgery and slowly erode workforce diversity, suppress innovation, and even impact patient care.

Establishing formal mentorship and sponsorship programs

One key to bridging this gap is formal mentorship and sponsorship programs. Mentorship is vital for career development and retention through guidance, self-confidence, and skill development. Sponsorship actively provides support for female trainees for leadership roles, fellowships, and promotions. White et al. [17] discovered that female orthopaedic trainees with strong mentorship relationships are more likely to complete training and secure leadership roles. Nth Dimensions has managed to increase female representation by offering formal mentorship, networking, and clinical exposure [18]. Sponsorship programs would pair female trainees with senior faculty who actively champion them by nominating them for fellowships, leadership roles, and complex surgical cases.

Ensuring equitable surgical case distribution and training opportunities

Ensuring that surgical cases and training are distributed fairly is also a key aspect of gender equality in orthopaedics. One of the greatest challenges for female orthopaedic surgeons is that they are not given access to complex surgical cases. Xu et al. [19] found that female residents perform fewer complex operations than men and are restricted in experience and self-efficacy in seeking competitive fellowship positions. To address this imbalance, faculty must create tracking systems that offer fair distribution of cases and provide equal exposure to significant operations. Formal criteria for case assignment should also replace subjective faculty preferences to minimize bias and provide equal learning for all.

Creating inclusive and supportive work environments

It is necessary to create a work environment that is inclusive and nurturing to reduce female orthopaedic trainees’ attrition. Gender discrimination and bias deeply impact them in mental health, self-esteem, and career choice. Morgan et al. [20] identified that 60% of female surgical trainees are subjected to gender-based discrimination and microaggressions that contribute to higher dropout rates. Institutions must implement mandatory implicit bias training for staff and faculty, enforce zero-tolerance policies against discrimination and harassment with clear reporting mechanisms, and create leadership programs focused on diversity to enhance the representation of female faculty and program directors.

Implementing family-friendly policies

In addition to addressing workplace culture, institutions should create family-friendly policies to facilitate work-life balance for female trainees. Surgical training’s inflexible and demanding schedules and lack of good parental leave policies deter women from entering orthopaedics. Ferrari et al. [15] documented that women in surgical specialties are twice as likely to consider a career switch for work-life conflicts than men. To retain female trainees, institutions should offer flexible scheduling and penalty-free parental leave to careers. On-site childcare support or arrangements with childcare centres and normalizing part-time or modified training tracks can also enhance work-life balance and career viability.

Strengthening the pipeline: early exposure and outreach

There is a need to establish a pipeline by exposing and engaging medical and pre-med students early on and generating interest in orthopaedic surgery. Organizations like the Perry Initiative and the Ruth Jackson Orthopaedic Society have been able to encourage female engagement through early clinical exposure, networking and meeting with role models, and development of skills [21]. Formal mentorship and research exposure should be integrated into medical school curricula by institutions to establish a robust pipeline of female residents for orthopaedic residency programs.

The importance of diversity in patient care and surgical innovation

The benefit of gender diversity in orthopaedic surgery is not just about equity—patient satisfaction and surgical innovation are directly influenced. Studies have identified that female patients are more trusting and satisfied with female physicians, particularly in fields like orthopaedics where patient-surgeon rapport is crucial [19]. Diverse teams of surgeons also improve teamwork, creativity, and decision-making by having multiple viewpoints to apply to complex cases.

Thus, decreasing gender imbalance in orthopaedic surgery requires action based on evidence and long-term commitment by institutions. More studies are required to assess the impact of retention programs, establish data-based solutions for balanced training, and identify best practices in those countries that have managed to improve female representation in orthopaedics. By addressing bias actively, improving mentorship, offering balanced training, and fostering a conducive environment, orthopaedic surgery can overcome gender imbalance and retain future talent.

Limitations

This correspondence is based on a synthesis of existing literature and does not include original data collection. As such, the observations and trends discussed are subject to limitations inherent in the referenced studies, including regional variability, differing methodologies, and potential reporting biases. Additionally, while many of the cited studies are recent and peer-reviewed, their generalizability across all institutions or healthcare systems may be limited. Future research using longitudinal and institution-specific data would help validate these trends and evaluate the effectiveness of proposed interventions.

Conclusion

Gender disparity in orthopaedic surgery continues to reflect longstanding systemic barriers including implicit bias, limited access to equitable training, and a lack of consistent institutional support. Without strategic and sustained reform, female trainees may remain underrepresented, hindering efforts to build a more diverse and inclusive surgical workforce. Interventions such as mentorship programs, equitable case distribution, and anti-discrimination policies have shown promise in enhancing retention. Additionally, family-friendly policies like flexible scheduling and parental leave can help reduce work-life conflict. Early outreach initiatives also play a role in shaping interest and confidence among female medical students. While gender equity enhances representation, it is also associated with broader benefits for patient engagement and clinical innovation. Achieving such equity will require ongoing institutional commitment and evidence-informed approaches to reduce attrition and ensure that opportunities in orthopaedics are determined by merit and motivation rather than gender.
