Complete mitochondrial genomes of Pipistrellus pipistrellus and Pipistrellus nathusii, along with the resolution of the taxonomic positions of Pipistrellus and Nyctalus
Authors: Derya Çetintürk, Emre Barlas, Nuri Yiğit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vespertilionidae is the bat family with the highest number of species and has been studied for years using various methods such as morphology, mitochondrial, and nuclear DNA. However, the taxonomy and systematics of several groups within this family remain controversial, and many relationships are still unresolved. In this study, complete mitochondrial data (15.797–16.719 base pairs) of Pipistrellus pipistrellus and Pipistrellus nathusii were presented with the gene characteristics, and it was also aimed to reveal the phylogenetic relationship between Pipistrellus and Nyctalus. In this context, Eastern Pipistrellus species (P. coromandra, P. abramus, together with Glischropus bucephalus) were located distantly from Western Pipistrellus (P. pipistrellus, P. pygmaeus, P. kuhlii, and P. nathusii) and Nyctalus (N. noctula, N. aviator, and N. plancyi) species, with the mean genetic distance (d) values of 15.9–17.0% between these three lineages. On the other hand, P. nathusii showed a closer relationship with Nyctalus than with Pipistrellus. The genetic distances indicate that Nyctalus, Western Pipistrellus, and the Eastern Pipistrellus lineage (e.g., Alionoctula) may represent three distinct genus-level lineages suggesting that the genus Pipistrellus in its current definition may be paraphyletic. Moreover, the d value of 0.03% between N. noctula and N. plancyi supports the subspecies status of N. plancyi.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46786-2.

Introduction

Vespertilionidae is the second most numerous mammalian group, after rodents, and includes approximately one-third of all living bat species. Over 36 million years, vesper bats have undergone numerous parallel radiation events, resulting in the emergence of more than 500 species1–4. Vespertilioninae Gray 1821, the only member of Vespertilionidae distributed in all zoogeographic regions, is the most populous subfamily with 9 tribes and 240 species described1,5. Traditionally, morphology-based methods have been used to evaluate these species, but similar characters, especially those arising from convergent evolution, cause difficulties in species identification6. Particularly, the taxonomic positions and relationships of the species in the Pipistrellini tribe (Tate 1942) are controversial, and no consensus has yet been reached for many species. For example, Pipistrellus stenopterus Dobson 1875 has been included in the genus Nyctalus Bowditch 1825 in the past because of some similar external morphological and dental characteristics7–9. However, this similarity seems to have occurred through convergent evolution due to a similar feeding style10. Also, the baculum morphology observed in some subgenera of Pipistrellus represents a reduced form of the long-shafted bacular type characteristic of Nyctalus11. Similarly, Hypsugo joffrei Thomas 1915 was classified in Nyctalus and Pipistrellus Kaup 1829 due to its morphological features6,7,9, but was later re-recognized as a member of the genus Hypsugo5. Due to the low discriminatory power of morphological characters, methods utilizing molecular markers have been preferred, but the results obtained contradicted each other. For example, the genera Nyctalus and Pipistrellus are morphologically quite different, including extreme species in terms of body size (Pipistrellus pygmaeus Leach 1825: 4–7.5 g; Nyctalus lasiopterus Schreber 1780: 40–75 g). However, in most genetic studies using mitochondrial and nuclear gene regions, the Western group of Pipistrellus (such as Pipistrellus pipistrellus Schreber 1774, Pipistrellus kuhlii Kuhl 1817, Pipistrellus nathusii Keyserling and Blasius 1839, P. pygmaeus) was closer to the species belonging to Nyctalus than the Eastern group (Pipistrellus coromandra Gray 1838, Pipistrellus abramus Temminck 1838, P. stenopterus etc.)1,10,12–22. In these studies, the proximity/distance relationships of Nyctalus with the Western group species have also yielded divergent results. On the other hand, Roehrs et al.23 concluded that Nyctalus is positioned separately from these two groups. As it could be implied, there is no consensus on the taxonomic position of Pipistrellus and Nyctalus species, as well as on the paraphyletic/polyphyletic status of Pipistrellus. On the other hand, although Glischropus, which includes East Asian species, shows significant morphological differences from Pipistrellus species, cytogenetic comparisons show that Glischropus shares several chromosomal characteristics with Pipistrellus-like bats24. mtDNA and nucDNA studies have also shown that species belonging to this genus are closely related to the Eastern clade of the genus Pipistrellus15,19,20. The majority of these studies are based on a single gene region, which may give a weak phylogenetic signal, so it could be expected that the results of different authors are incongruent. Therefore, in recent years, analyses using the whole mitochondrial DNA have come into prominence. Mitochondrial DNA, which is approximately 16 kb long and consists of 37 gene regions25, lacks recombination and repair mechanisms and introns, and evolves rapidly26–31, and it is frequently preferred thanks to these advantages. Mitogenome data have been provided for a large number of species, including bat species in the Pipistrellini tribe (Nyctalus noctula32, (P. kuhlii1,5, P. coromandra33, P. abramus17,35, Nyctalus plancyi35, Nyctalus aviator18. By employing these sequences, it is possible to elucidate the phylogenetic relationships between species with more reliable results. With this point of view, we aimed to contribute to the systematics of Pipistrellus and Nyctalus with mitogenome analyses by providing the mitogenome characteristics of P. pipistrellus and P. nathusii.

Results

Regarding the mitogenome characteristics, 16.048 (one P. nathusii sample) and 15,797, 16.032, 16.511, and 16.719 (four P. pipistrellus samples) were obtained. Among 37 mitochondrial DNA gene regions (and an additional putative control region), including 13 protein-coding genes, 22 tRNA genes, and 2 rRNA genes, tRNA-Gln, tRNA-Ala, tRNA-Asn, tRNA-Cys, tRNA-Tyr, tRNA-Ser, NADH dehydrogenase subunit 6, tRNA-Glu, and tRNA-Pro regions are on the L strand, and the others are on the H strand. As can be seen in Supplementary Tables S1-S2 and Figs. 1 and 2, overlaps of 1–43 bases occurred between some gene regions in all samples. Besides, GC contents were determined as 37.67% in P. nathusii; 37.50% (PX315794), 37.75% (PX315793), 37.87% (PX315791), and 37.76% (PX315792) in P. pipistrellus.

13.518 bp of mitogenome data were used in the phylogenetic parts of the study. In the Bayesian Inference and Maximum Likelihood trees, most posterior probability (pp) and bootstrap values were highest (pp: 1.00, bs: 97–100); Western Pipistrellus species (P. pipistrellus, P. pygmaeus, P. kuhlii, P. nathusii) were located closer to the genus Nyctalus (N. noctula, N. aviator, and N. plancyi) than Eastern Pipistrellus species (P. abramus, P. coromandra, together with G. bucephalus). Especially, P. nathusii sequences were placed closer to Nyctalus species than Pipistrellus species. Mean genetic distance (d) values were 15.9–17.0% among these three groups (Fig. 3). d values between the species were also determined, and they ranged from 0.3 (N. noctula-N. aviator) to 17.5% (P. nathusii-N. noctula) (Table 1). For all Pipistrellus species examined, 13 haplotypes were identified in a total of 13 samples (haplotype diversity (Hd): 1.000), and the nucleotide diversity (Pi) value was calculated to be 0.124. A total of 4878 mutations were observed, with 4148 polymorphic sites identified, comprising 3358 parsimony-informative sites and 790 singleton sites.

Discussion

Mitogenome data characteristics

The sequences of P. pipistrellus and P. nathusii presented in this study are 15.797 to 16.719 base pairs in length, which is due to length differences (1 to 20) and overlaps in some gene regions. For example, there is a two-base overlap between 16S rRNA and tRNA-Leu in the P. nathusii sample and a three-base overlap in the P. pipistrellus (PX315791) sample. Similarly, the cytochrome c oxidase subunit III (COX3) region in these two samples is 805 and 785 base pairs long, respectively (Figs. 1 and 2). Moreover, GC content (37.50–37.87%) was lower than AT content in both species. In bats, especially in the family Vespertilionidae (except for Corynorhinus rafinesquii), it is known that the AT content is higher in the L strand36–38. In mammals, there is a positive correlation between high GC% and mutation rate, which triggers the evolutionary processes39. AT and GC pairs differ in the number of hydrogen bonds (2 and 3, respectively), and a high percentage of A + T reduces the structural stability of DNA40. In addition, the 12S rRNA and the 16S rRNA genes, as separated by tRNA-Val gene, were placed between the tRNA-Phe and tRNA-Leu genes. These characteristics are seen in all mammals27.

Complete mitochondrial DNA sequences of some bats belonging to the Pipistrellini tribe have been published by various authors. The mitogenomes of P. kuhlii (16.991 bp), P. coromandra (17.153 bp), P. abramus (16.977 and 17236 bp) were reported by different authors15,16,31,32. Also, Lee et al.18, Qian et al.32, and Jiang et al.35 revealed the mitogenomes of N. noctula (17.478 bp), N. plancyi (17.494 bp), and N. aviator (16.863 bp), respectively. A low level of GC% content, which implies the AT-bias, was also observed in these studies Fig. 4.

Phylogenetic implications

Despite the large number of studies, the taxonomic positions and phylogenetic relationships of the genera Pipistrellus and Nyctalus are still controversial. Molecular studies in the literature give incompatible results. For instance, Roehrs et al.23 suggested that Nyctalus is positioned separately from these eastern and western groups of Pipistrellus. Some authors1,12–22 defined that the Eastern group is positioned far from the Western group and Nyctalus. In some of these studies, Nyctalus species were intermingled with Western group species1,12–15,19,23,41, in some studies, Nyctalus and the Western group were completely separated10,17,20–23,41. Similar to the latter studies, these three groups were separated in the Bayesian Inference tree, with the Nyctalus species more closely positioned with the Western group (particularly P. nathusii) (Fig. 3). d values between these 3 groups are close to each other (15.9–17.0%), and Table 1 shows that these values are very close to the d values within the same group. Considering the distance values between Pipistrellus and Nyctalus species obtained from mtDNA gene regions in the literature, Hulva et al.41 found Cyt-b pairwise distance values as 13.8–19.1% and Kruskop et al.10 calculated p-distance values as 15.39–17.01% (Cyt-b) and 16.80–17.25% (COI), and these results are compatible with our results, except for the 0.02% d value between N. noctula and N. plancyi. N. plancyi (Vesperugo plancyi) was previously recognized as a subspecies of N. noctula by Allen42, but Tate6 hypothesized it as a separate species. Mitogenome results seem to support the subspecies level of N. plancyi. However, in our analyses, P. nathusii appeared closer to the genus Nyctalus than to other Pipistrellus species (similar to Koubínová et al.15 and Zhukova et al.20) with the relatively low pp value (0.70), suggesting that this topology may reflect limited phylogenetic signal in the mitochondrial dataset. In addition, G. bucephalus clustered with the Asian Pipistrellus species (P. abramus and P. coromandra) and this pattern is consistent with previous studies suggesting that Glischropus belongs to the “Pipistrellus-like bats” and shares close evolutionary affinities with Pipistrellus and related Vespertilioninae genera15,19,20.

As for the phylogenetic relationships of the genera Pipistrellus and Nyctalus, there are different opinions on whether the genus Pipistrellus is polyphyletic or paraphyletic, and whether they are the same or separate genera1,19–22. As suggested by Zhukova et al.21, Nyctalus and Pipistrellus (West) diverged in the Early Miocene (23.03–15.97 Ma), while the Eastern group of Pipistrellus was the first to split earlier. According to the results of this study based on phylogenetic trees and genetic distances, four scenarios can be considered: One possibility would be to include Nyctalus within Pipistrellus as a subgenus or synonym. A second option would be to recognize two genera, grouping Nyctalus with Western Pipistrellus while assigning the Eastern lineage to a separate genus Alionoctula Kruskop et al., 2018. A third taxonomic solution would be to recognize three distinct genera: Nyctalus, Western Pipistrellus, and an independent Eastern genus, Alionoctula. Finally, the current taxonomy could be retained, leaving Pipistrellus as a potentially paraphyletic genus. However, the Eastern assemblage appears to be paraphyletic due to the position of Glischropus, which complicates a straightforward taxonomic interpretation. However, considering the relatively high genetic distances among the three lineages and the divergence time estimates indicating that the Eastern lineage split first20, the hypothesis recognizing three distinct genera (Nyctalus, Western Pipistrellus, and the Eastern Pipistrellus lineage (e.g., Alionoctula)) appears to be the most consistent hypothesis with the phylogenetic and evolutionary evidence. In order to obtain definitive conclusions on this issue, further mitogenome analyses with larger sample sizes from a larger number of species are needed. Single gene regions may give limited and contradictory results, especially in rapidly evolving animal groups due to saturation43. Indeed, phylogenetic trees derived from Cyt-b data yielded a different topology with low pp and bs values in our study. Therefore, future mitogenome studies may provide more reliable results on the taxonomy and phylogeny of vesper bats. The limited number of complete mitochondrial genomes currently available for vespertilionid bats restricts the resolution of phylogenetic relationships in some lineages. Future studies including mitogenome sequences from a larger number of species and populations, together with nuclear genomic markers, would likely provide a more robust framework for resolving the taxonomy and evolutionary history of vesper bats. In addition, integrating molecular data with morphological characters may further contribute to clarifying the systematic relationships within this diverse group.

Methods

Sampling

Four P. pipistrellus and one P. nathusii specimens were caught from Turkiye as dead specimens and stored in Mammalian Research Collection (AUMAC, mammalia.ankara.edu.tr). Mitogenome sequences of P. nathusii, P. pygmaeus, P. kuhlii, P. abramus, P. coromandra, N. aviator, N. noctula, N. plancyi, Glischropus bucephalus and Hypsugo alaschanicus were also retrieved from GenBank (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank/) for using in the analyses (Table 2).

In addition to the mitogenome analyses, mitochondrial Cyt-b data was also evaluated to compare the results. For this reason, Cyt-b sequences belonging to several Pipistrellus, Nyctalus and Glischropus species were utilized (Supplementary Table S4).

Laboratory studies and genome analyses

Liver tissues of the specimens were used to isolate genomic DNA with the help of DNA extraction kits. Utilizing the NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit, subsequent library preparation was performed, and the paired-end sequencing was optimized with a precise 150-base pair insert size configuration. The high-throughput Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform was employed for sequencing, and the quality of the reads was evaluated. Summary statistical data were produced to evaluate the read quality and sequencing depth. A set of high-quality reads in the “.fastq” or “.fq” format was acquired through a quality filtering process. After that, the genetic composition, profile, and potential functionalities were clarified with quality control of NGS data and filtering, de novo genome assembly, and mitochondrial genome annotation. After the NGS sequencing reaction, the total number of reads, total sequence length, Phred Score (Q); Q20 and Q30 quality scores, and GC contents were defined. Mitochondrial genome annotation was conducted in MitoZ version 3.645. The assembled circular genome and its annotations were viewed in Circos v0.69-846. These processes were performed by Genoks LifeScience Inc. (Turkiye).

Besides the alignment of sequences, mean genetic distance (d) values (p-distance parameter47) between the species were achieved in Mega12 Software48, 13.518 bp mitogenome data was partitioned into the three codon positions (https://bioinformatics.org/sms2/split_codons.html) and the most suitable models to build a Bayesian Inference tree were defined in jModeltest49,50 according to BIC (Bayesian Information Criterion). Accordingly, GTR + I + G (1st codon position), HKY + G (2nd codon position) and HKY + I+ G (3rd codon position)51,52 were determined.

Maximum Likelihood (ML) analysis was performed using IQ-TREE Web Server (http://iqtree.cibiv.univie.ac.at/). The analysis applied a partition scheme defined according to codon positions, and a corresponding model was assigned to each partition. Branching support values ​​were evaluated using 1000 ultrafast bootstrap iterations.

Bayesian Inference (BI) analysis was performed using the MrBayes 3.2.7 program53. Three codon partitions were defined as separate data blocks, and appropriate model parameters were assigned to each. The partitions were separated so that substitution ratios, base frequencies, gamma shape parameter, and invariant site ratio were independent of each other. The analysis was performed with two independent runs and four Markov chains, which were run for 1.000.000 generations, with sampling every 1000 generations. After removing the first 25% as burn-in, consensus trees were constructed from the remaining trees. Using FigTree 1.4 Program (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree), the ML and BI trees were viewed and modified.

The methodology described above was also applied to Cyt-b data with the HKY + G (1st codon)51 and GTR + G (2nd and 3rd codon)52 parameters.
