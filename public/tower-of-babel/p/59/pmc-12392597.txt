Calorie restriction in radiation-exposed mice affects the expression of autophagy-related protein p62
Authors: Takafumi Nakayama, Keiji Suzuki, Norisato Mitsutake
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCalorie restriction (CR) is known to decrease not only spontaneous but also chemical- and radiation-induced carcinogenesis. However, the mechanism of anti-cancer effect by CR is extremely complex and not fully understood. In this study, we focused on changes in autophagy-related proteins (LC3 and p62) as factors related to changes in nutritional status and the suppression of carcinogenesis by CR.MethodB6C3F1 male mice were exposed to 0 Gy or 3.8 Gy X-rays at 1 week of age, and then both groups were fed either a standard diet (95 kcal/week) or a calorie-restricted diet with approximately 30% fewer calories (65 kcal/week) from 7 weeks of age. Mice were sacrificed at several time points, and their livers were collected and analyzed.ResultsCR suppressed the development of liver tumors in the irradiated group during the study period. The expression level of LC3 did not change significantly with or without CR and irradiation. On the other hand, the expression of p62 was significantly and temporarily increased in the irradiated CR group, in which radiation carcinogenesis was suppressed. Using immunofluorescence analysis, we found cells expressed p62 strongly throughout the cytoplasm in the CR group. In some of the p62-strong positive cells, the disappearance of the cell nucleus and a decrease in the nuclear DNA content were observed, and it was not co-localized with the cell proliferation marker, suggesting that cell death had occurred.ConclusionsLong-term CR, which inhibits radiation carcinogenesis, did not significantly affect autophagy activity in the liver as determined by LC3-II levels. On the other hand, increased expression of p62 was observed in the CR group in which the anti-cancer effect was confirmed. In particular, p62 strongly positive cells with smaller or no nuclei were detected exclusively in the CR group, suggesting a novel p62-mediated mechanism in the suppression of radiation-induced liver carcinogenesis by CR.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14771-z.

Introduction

Calorie restriction (CR) suppresses carcinogenesis and increases lifespan in various animals [1, 2]. CR has been reported to suppress not only spontaneous but also chemical- and radiation-induced carcinogenesis [3, 4]. In particular, CR has been reported to significantly suppress solid tumors, including those in the liver, in cases of radiation-induced cancer. CR has been suggested to suppress the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby suppressing DNA damage caused by oxidative stress [5]. It has also been reported that CR can modulate age-associated DNA methylation patterns and mitigate the attenuation of DNA damage repair activity due to aging [6–8]. In addition, it is thought that the suppression of cell proliferation and protein synthesis and the activation of autophagy caused by down-regulating the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway may suppress aging and carcinogenesis [9, 10]. Therefore, the anti-cancer effect of CR is thought to be mediated by a complex interplay of multiple mechanisms. Recently, there has also been progress in the development of CR mimetics, which can provide the benefits of CR without actually undergoing CR [11, 12], and it is important to further clarify the specific molecules and mechanisms that are the targets of the anti-cancer effects of CR.

Autophagy is a physiological response associated with a variety of stresses, including nutrient stress [13]. Macro-autophagy, commonly referred to as"autophagy,"is known to act as a cytoprotective system by selectively eliminating unwanted cytoplasmic material such as abnormal proteins and damaged mitochondria that accumulate within the cell [14]. Therefore, it is thought that it may be involved in suppressing carcinogenesis by preventing the accumulation of abnormal proteins and reactive oxygen species produced by damaged mitochondria, thereby maintaining cellular homeostasis. Indeed, defects in autophagy-related genes in normal tissues have been reported to induce tumors [15, 16].

The autophagy-related protein, microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3), is known to exist in two forms, LC3-I and LC3-II. LC3-I is cytosolic, whereas LC3-II is membrane-bound. LC3-II is formed when phosphatidylethanolamine binds to LC3-I and has been found to play critical roles in autophagy, including autophagosome formation, sequestration of selectively labeled unwanted proteins into autophagosomes, and the degradation and recycling of substrates by lysosomes [14, 17]. The amount of LC3-II is closely correlated with the number of autophagosomes. Therefore, it is widely used as a marker for autophagy. Sequestosome 1 (SQSTM1/p62) is a multifunctional protein that is involved in various signaling pathways, including oxidative stress responses, inflammation, and cell death [18–20]. It binds to ubiquitinated proteins and then binds to LC3 to sequester unwanted proteins in autophagosomes during the process of autophagy, after which it is degraded along with the bound proteins [21]. Therefore, the induction of autophagy has also been reported to reduce the amount of p62 in cells.

Autophagy is sensitive to nutrient stress, and it has been reported that short-term starvation in mice induces transient activation of autophagy in many tissues, including the liver [22]. Therefore, we hypothesized that sustained CR may also protect cells in tissues from oxidative stress and the accumulation of abnormal proteins by increasing autophagy activity, and that this may contribute to the anti-cancer effects of CR. However, few studies have analyzed changes in liver autophagy activity over a long time period using CR. As for the few studies that have looked at the effects of long-term CR on autophagy activity, one study in mice on a 40% CR reported that there was an increase in autophagy activity in the liver [23] as indicated by an increase in the amount of LC3-II, while another study in rats on a 40% CR reported that there was an increase in autophagy activity in the heart but no change in the liver [24], so there is some disagreement about whether long-term CR is effective in increasing autophagy activity.

The purpose of present study is to examine our hypothesis and clarify the mechanism of anti-cancer by CR. Toward this goal, we examined the liver, where calorie restriction has a significant anti-cancer effect. We analyzed the expression levels of autophagy-related proteins, LC3 and p62, and their localization in the liver from the time of radiation exposure to the time of hepatocarcinogenesis using a radiation-induced cancer mouse model, in which CR has been shown to suppress carcinogenesis.

Material and method

Mice

Male B6C3F1 mice were obtained by mating male C3H/HeNCrl mice (KBT Oriental, Saga, Japan) and female C57BL/6NCrl mice (KBT Oriental). Mice were housed three per cage and maintained at a temperature of 23 ± 3℃, humidity of 50 ± 20% and 12:12 light–dark schedule. The mice were weaned at 4 weeks of age and fed a standard diet (CE-2; CREA Japan, Inc., Tokyo, Japan) until 7 weeks of age. The mice were randomly divided into four groups: (i) non-irradiated (0 Gy) with 95 kcal diet, (ii) non-irradiated with 65 kcal diet, (iii) 3.8 Gy-irradiated with 95 kcal diet, and (iv) 3.8 Gy-irradiated with 65 kcal diet. The mice were exposed to radiation at 1 week of age and started CR at 7 weeks of age. The mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation at defined time points (49, 56, 70, 100, 200, 300, and 400 days of age), followed by necropsy. Tissues were preserved in liquid nitrogen and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24–48 h. All of the experimental procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with the institutional guidelines of Nagasaki University. This study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee at Nagasaki University (approval no. 1811261491).

Diet

The diets consisted of two different calorie-controlled regimens: 95 and 65 kcal/week/mouse. The 95 kcal diet is equivalent to ad libitum feeding, and the 65 kcal diet is designed to cut 32% of carbohydrates from the 95 kcal diet and include the same amount of the remaining nutrients (protein, lipids, minerals and vitamins). The diet was fed to the mice on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. Each mouse was weighed weekly until the day of scheduled dissection. Details about the diet composition and feeding strategy have been reported [4, 25].

Irradiation

Mice were exposed to a whole-body dose of 3.8 Gy of X-rays at the 1 week of age. Irradiation was performed using an ISOVOLT TITAN 320 X-ray generator (Toshiba, Tokyo, Japan) at a dose rate of 0.617 Gy/min.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Immunofluorescence staining

A portion of the liver tissue fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin and paraffin-embedded was sectioned into 3 μm thick and stained with H&E.

For immunofluorescence, the 3 μm section was heated in a water bath with Histofine antigen retrieval solution (pH 9.0, Nichirei Biosciences, Tokyo, Japan) for antigen retrieval at 95℃ for 40 min. The sections were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in TBS-T (20 mM Tris–HCL, pH7.6, 137 mM NaCl and 0.1% Tween 20) containing 5% skim milk for 2 h at 37℃, followed by Alexa Fluor-labeled secondary antibodies diluted in TBS-T containing 5% skim milk for 1 h at 37℃. After the sections on slides were washed with PBS, cover glasses were placed on slides together with 10% glycerol/PBS containing 1 μg/mL DAPI.

The primary antibodies used in the experiments were: anti-LC3B antibody (1:200 dilution, NB100-2220, Novus Biologicals, Littleton, CO, USA), anti-p62 antibody (1:100 dilution, GP62-C, PROGEN, Heidelberg, Germany), anti-Keratin K8/K18 antibody (1:200 dilution, GP11, PROGEN), anti-Ki-67 antibody (1:100, 14–5698-82, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and anti-Cytokeratin 8 antibody (1:100, MABT329, Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). The secondary antibodies used were: Alexa Fluor 488-labeled anti-rat IgG (1:200 dilution, A11006, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Alexa Fluor 555-labeled anti-rabbit IgG (1:200 dilution, A21428, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and Alexa Fluor 647-labeled anti-guinea pig IgG (1:200 dilution, A21450, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Images were captured by a BZ-9000 fluorescence microscope (KEYENCE, Osaka, Japan) and analyzed by the BZ-II analyzer software (KEYENCE). Analysis of fluorescence images was also performed using the Image J software. The number of fluorescence-positive cells was counted using the cell counter and analyze particle plugins.

Western blotting

A portion of the frozen liver was soaked in the RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing 1% protease inhibitor (Sigma-Aldrich Japan, Tokyo, Japan) and homogenized using Tissuelyser II (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). Liver lysate was cleared by centrifugation at 15,000 rpm for 3 min at 4℃, and supernatant was used as total protein. Protein concentration was determined by the BCA protein assay (Pierce Chemical Co., Rockford, IL, USA). Protein samples of 15 μg were electrophoresed on SDS–polyacrylamide gel (ATTO Co., Tokyo, Japan) and were transferred to a polyvinyl difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Merck Millipore) in the transfer buffer (100 mM Tris, 192 mM glycine). After incubation with blocking solution (10% skim milk in TBS-T buffer (20 mM Tric-HCl, pH7.6, 137 mM NaCl with 0.1% Tween-20)) for 30 min, the membrane was incubated with the primary antibodies for 1 h followed by the secondary anti-rabbit IgG antibodies conjugated with alkaline phosphatase (BioRad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) for 30 min. Blots were processed with ECL select western blotting detection reagent (GE Healthcare Japan, Tokyo, Japan), and the signal was detected using an LAS-3000 image analyzer (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan).

The primary antibodies used in this study were: anti-LC3A/B antibody (1:500 dilution, #12,741, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-p62 antibody (1:1000 dilution, #23,214, Cell Signaling Technology), and anti-β-Actin (1:500 dilution, Poly6221, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA).

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± S.D for continuous parameters. Expression of autophagy-related proteins were statistically analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and followed by Tukey's post hoc test using R software [26] version 4. 3. 1 with the EZR graphical user interface (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi University, Saitama, Japan). Tumor detection rate was analyzed by the Kaplan–Meier method using the EZR. Fisher's exact test with Bonferroni correction were performed with EZR on the categorical data obtained from immunofluorescence staining. The results were considered significant when p < 0.05.

Results

CR suppressed the development of liver tumors after radiation exposure

The average body weight of the mice during the study period is shown in Fig. 1 and Table 1. The body weights of the 65 kcal group were consistently smaller than those of the 95 kcal group in both irradiated and non-irradiated groups, with a significant difference at 400 days of age. Among the irradiated mice, only 1.25% (1/80) died from acute radiation syndrome between radiation exposure and the start of CR. Table 2 shows the total number of tumors observed throughout the experimental period. Detailed information for tumor detection in time is presented in Supplementary Table 1. A Kaplan–Meier analysis was performed for the entire study period considering lymphoma and solid tumors as an outcome. A significant difference in tumor development rate was observed between the irradiated and non-irradiated groups in the 95 kcal group (p = 0.0293, log-rank test). Furthermore, CR was shown to significantly suppress tumor development in the irradiated group (p = 0.0368, log-rank test) (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 2). Of the tumors collected, immunofluorescence staining for liver tumors were performed using Ki-67, a marker of proliferating cells, and cytokeratin 8/18, which is overexpressed in liver tumors [27], along with H&E staining to confirm liver tumor growth and whether the tumors were primary liver tumors (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 2A-C). The frequency of Ki-67-positive cells in the cytokeratin 8/18-positive tumor region was higher (3–7%) than that in normal liver region (0–0.2%) (Supplementary Table 3 and supplementary Fig. 3), indicating that these tumors were primary liver tumors. The liver tumors were observed in 4 out of 6 mice in the 3.8 Gy-95 kcal group, while no liver tumors were observed in the other 3 groups, including the 3.8 Gy-65 kcal group. CR increased p62 expression in the irradiated group

The expression levels of autophagy-related proteins (p62 and LC3) after CR were analyzed by Western blotting. For LC3 expression, we analyzed the LC3 II/LC3 I ratio, which correlates with autophagic flux and is a reliable indicator of autophagosome formation. The expression of p62 increased significantly in the irradiated group, especially at 3.8 Gy-65 kcal, at 56 days of age, one week after the start of CR (Fig. 3). Thereafter, the expression of p62 gradually decreased in the irradiated group. After 100 days of age, there was no significant difference between the groups, but at 300 and 400 days, both the irradiated and non-irradiated groups showed a trend toward decreased expression in the 65 kcal group. High expression of p62 is associated with the shrinkage and disappearance of the cell nucleus

Next, a more detailed analysis of the p62-positive cells revealed the presence of cells with small nuclei and low nuclear fluorescence intensity, as well as cells in which no nuclei were observed. First, the size of the nuclei was analyzed: Type1 + +, Type1 +, Type2 + +, and Type2 + cells, plus 5–6 hepatocytes around p62-positive cells (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Fig. 5 A). Both Type 1 and Type 2 cells tended to have smaller nuclei in the strongly positive cells. Furthermore, when Type 1 and Type 2 cells were grouped together and compared between p62-strong positive (+ +) and p62-positive (+) cells, the nuclear size of p62-strong positive cells was significantly smaller (Fig. 6B). Discussion

In this study, we analyzed the liver up to the period covering the onset of liver cancer based on previous reports [4], and found that liver tumors were observed in several mice at 400 days of age. Liver tumors were observed only in the 3.8 Gy-95 kcal group but not in the 3.8 Gy-65 kcal group, indicating that CR suppressed radiation-induced carcinogenesis. This result, together with the changes in the body weight of the mice shown in Fig. 1, confirmed that the CR was appropriately performed in this study.

We initially focused on autophagy-related proteins in irradiated mice after CR considering the possibility that CR increases autophagy activity and prevents the accumulation of abnormal proteins and reactive oxygen species produced by damaged mitochondria, thereby contributing to the suppression of carcinogenesis. There are few studies analyzing changes in autophagy activity after long-term CR in tissues, and the results regarding the impact of chronic CR on autophagy activity remain inconsistent [23, 24]. In addition, no studies have analyzed changes in autophagy activity over time after CR at multiple time points. We demonstrated that the expression levels of LC3-II, the most commonly used marker of autophagy, were not affected by X-ray irradiation or CR from 56 to 400 days of age, indicating that autophagy should not be involved in the CR-mediated suppression of liver cancer.

On the other hand, however, the expression of p62 was significantly increased in the irradiated group at 56 days of age, one week after the start of CR, with particularly large increases in the 3.8 Gy-65 kcal group. A significant increase in p62 expression was observed under conditions where radiation carcinogenesis was suppressed, suggesting that p62 may be involved in the carcinogenesis suppressive effect of CR. The p62 is a multifunctional protein. The p62 binds to ubiquitinated proteins and LC3 and then sequesters unnecessary proteins in autophagosomes during the autophagic process, after which they are degraded [21]. Although it is possible that the increase in p62 expression is due to a decrease in autophagy activity, no changes in LC3-II expression were observed at the same time point in this study. Therefore, the increase in p62 expression that occurs after CR is unlikely to be due to a decrease in autophagy activity. Interestingly, the increase in p62 was greatest after CR was greatest in the irradiated group. The liver of young mice has more active cell proliferation than the liver of adult mice, and it has been reported that cell proliferation is significantly increased by radiation exposure [28]. Since cell proliferation requires a lot of energy, it is possible that the irradiated group consumes more energy than the non-irradiated group. In this study, the irradiated group had a significantly lower body weight than the non-irradiated group at 49 days of age at the start of CR, so it is possible that energy expenditure was partially increased (Table 1). With the increase in energy expenditure and the addition of nutritional stress caused by CR, it is possible that the expression of p62, which is involved in autophagy and cell death to maintain tissue, increased significantly in the 3.8 Gy-65 kcal group.

Detailed analysis of p62 expression in tissues by immunofluorescence staining revealed that p62-strong positive cells were observed in the 65 kcal group, especially in the 3.8 Gy-65 kcal group. The p62-strong positive cells were characterized by intense cytoplasmic p62 expression and were extremely rare relative to the total number of hepatocytes. Notably, they were almost never detected in the 95 kcal group, in both the irradiated and non-irradiated groups, and were a distinct characteristic of CR. The p62-strong positive cells tended to have smaller nuclei than the surrounding cells, and the percentage of cells without nuclei was higher than that of normal hepatocytes. Therefore, it is possible that the loss of the nuclear DNA content is progressing in p62-strong positive cells, especially in those that express p62 uniformly throughout the cell.

In the process of cell death, fragmentation and degradation of the cell nucleus and nuclear DNA are observed, and previous studies have reported that p62 interacts with various proteins in the cytoplasm and is involved in the progression of various types of cell death, including apoptosis, necrosis, and parthanatos [20, 29–31]. Therefore, it is suggested that p62-strong positive cells may be capturing the process of cell death in tissues. In this study, the p62 and Ki-67 co-expressing cells were not observed, indicating that p62 is not expressed in proliferative cells. This is consistent with the possibility that cell death occurs in those p62-strong positive cells. Cell death is important in suppressing carcinogenesis, and it is thought that inducing cell death in abnormal cells and eliminating them from the tissue contributes to the suppression of carcinogenesis. In recent years, it has been reported that carcinogenesis is suppressed in the small intestinal epithelium by the elimination of cells with mutations in cancer-related genes through cell competition with normal cells [32]. In addition, it has been reported that the elimination of mutant cells in the small intestine and liver is suppressed in mice fed a high-fat diet [33, 34], suggesting a potential link between nutritional status and carcinogenesis. Therefore, if p62 is highly expressed and p62-mediated cell death after CR is preferentially induced in cancer-initiating cells, such as those with genomic damage, which are eliminated by cell competition, it may contribute to the anti-cancer effects of CR.

The limitations of this study include the inability to clarify the mechanism by which p62-strong positive cells contribute to nuclear reduction and cell death, as well as whether p62-strong positive cells are precancerous.

In conclusion, this study showed that long-term caloric restriction, which inhibits radiation carcinogenesis, did not significantly affect autophagy activity in the liver as determined by LC3-II levels. On the other hand, increased expression of p62 was observed in the CR group in which the anti-cancer effect was confirmed. In particular, p62 strongly positive cells with smaller or no nuclei were detected exclusively in the CR group, suggesting a novel p62-mediated mechanism in the suppression of radiation-induced liver carcinogenesis. Further analysis of the role of p62 in the inhibition of carcinogenesis will provide a more detailed understanding of the mechanism of anti-cancer effect of CR.
