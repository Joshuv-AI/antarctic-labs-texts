FGF21 confers neuroprotection in Parkinson’s disease by activating the FGFR1-sirt1 pathway
Authors: Tingting Liu, Jianshe Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParkinson’s disease (PD) lacks disease-modifying therapies. Fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) is implicated in PD, but its neuroprotective mechanisms via fibroblast growth factor receptor 1 (FGFR1)-sirtuin 1 (Sirt1) remain unclear.MethodsUsing 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-te-trahydropyridine (MPTP)-induced PD mice and lipopolysaccharides (LPS)-stimulated BV2 microglia, this study employed recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV)-mediated FGF21 overexpression (OE). Multi-dimensional analyses (behavior, immunofluorescence, molecular docking, Western blot, PCR, transmission electron microscopy (TEM)) assessed FGF21's effects and mechanisms.ResultsFGF21OE significantly improved motor deficits (gait, rotarod) and non-motor symptoms (depression/anxiety) in PD mice. It repaired the blood–brain barrier (BBB) by upregulating tight junction proteins (claudin, zonula occludens (ZO-1), occludin) and reducing astrocyte activation (glail fibrillary acidicprotein, GFAP). Mechanistically, FGF21 binding to FGFR1 activated Sirt1, enhancing mitochondrial fusion (optic atrophy 1 (OPA1), mitofusin 1 (Mfn1)) and inhibiting fission (dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1), Fission 1 (Fis1)), improving membrane potential and ultrastructure. FGF21 also activated the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway, boosting PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy and inhibiting Casp3/Bax-dependent apoptosis. Furthermore, FGF21 reduced neuroinflammation by suppressing nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB)/NOD-like receptor thermal protein domain associated protein 3 (NLRP3) and shifting microglia from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2. Molecular docking and co-IP confirmed FGF21 enhances direct FGFR1-Sirt1 interaction, synergistically regulating these pathways.ConclusionFGF21 exerts multi-faceted protection in PD via the FGFR1-Sirt1 axis, including BBB repair, mitochondrial homeostasis restoration, microglial polarization towards M2, balancing autophagy and apoptosis, and promoting neuronal survival.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11658-025-00807-6.Keywords: FGF21, Sirt1, Parkinson’s disease, Microglia, Mitochondrial dysfunction, Blood–brain barrier

Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra (SN) and abnormal aggregation of α-synuclein. Its pathological mechanisms involve multiple factors, including mitochondrial dysfunction, neuroinflammation, autophagy dysregulation, and blood–brain barrier (BBB) disruption [1–5]. Although current therapies (e.g., levodopa) can alleviate motor symptoms, they fail to reverse disease progression and show limited efficacy in improving non-motor symptoms (e.g., depression, anxiety) [6–8]. Therefore, exploring novel therapeutic targets to regulate core pathological processes in PD holds significant scientific importance.

Fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21), a metabolic regulator, has recently been found to exhibit potential neuroprotective effects in the central nervous system [9, 10]. Clinical studies reveal significantly reduced serum FGF21 levels in PD patients, suggesting its association with disease progression [11]. FGF21 activates downstream signaling pathways by binding to its receptor FGFR1 and the β-Klotho co-receptor [12]. Sirtuin 1 (Sirt1), an NAD + -dependent deacetylase, plays a critical role in regulating mitochondrial biogenesis, inflammatory responses, and apoptosis [13]. Notably, the synergistic effects of FGF21 and Sirt1 pathways in metabolic diseases have been widely reported [14, 15], but their interaction and specific mechanisms in PD remain unclear.

Mitochondrial dynamic imbalance (fusion/fission dysregulation) is one of the core mechanisms underlying dopaminergic neuronal damage in PD [16]. Additionally, aberrant activation of microglia (polarization toward the M1 pro-inflammatory phenotype) exacerbates neuroinflammation and accelerates neuronal death [17]. Studies suggest that FGF21 may regulate autophagy via the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway and attenuate inflammatory responses by inhibiting nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) signaling [18, 19]. However, whether FGF21 improves PD pathological phenotypes by modulating mitochondrial function and microglial polarization through the fibroblast growth factor receptor 1 (FGFR1)-Sirt1 axis requires systematic validation.

Based on this, our study employs 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-te-trahydropyridine (MPTP)-induced PD mouse models and BV2 microglial inflammatory models, utilizing recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV)-mediated FGF21 overexpression (OE) and FGF21OE plasmid construction to investigate the following scientific questions: (1) Can FGF21 ameliorate behavioral deficits and BBB integrity in PD models? (2) Does FGF21 regulate mitochondrial homeostasis, autophagy, and apoptosis via the FGFR1-Sirt1 pathway? (3) How does FGF21 influence microglial polarization and neuronal survival? Through multidimensional experiments (behavioral, molecular biological, immunofluorescence, and molecular docking), this study aims to elucidate the pleiotropic protective mechanisms of FGF21 in PD, providing a theoretical foundation for developing novel therapeutic strategies targeting the FGF21-FGFR1-Sirt1 axis. Materials and methods

Animal

The study utilized male C57BL/6J mice, aged 6–8 weeks and weighing between 25 and 30 g, sourced from Cyagen Biosciences. The mice were housed in standardized conditions featuring a 12/12-h light/dark cycle, a temperature of 22 ± 2 ℃, and a relative humidity of 55 ± 5%. They had constant access to food and water. All animal procedures adhered to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and received approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Henan University (No. HUSOM2023-285). The experimenters were blinded to the mouse group assignments. Prior to the experiment, the mice were given a two-week acclimation period. They were then randomly assigned to four groups of eight mice each: the Saline group, the MPTP group (Abmole, 25 mg/kg/d, injection for 14 days [20]), the MPTP + rAAV2/8-TBG-EGFP-WPRE-pA (rAAV-null) group (virus titer: 5.8 × 1012 vg/mL) [21], the MPTP + rAAV2/8-TBG-Fgf21-2A-EGFP-WPRE-pA (rAAV-Fgf21) group (virus titer: 6.4 × 1012 vg/mL). The Saline group received an intraperitoneal injection of NaCl in an equivalent volume for 14 days. On day 15, tail vein injection of virus 30 μL. On day 35, at 3 PM, behavioral tests were conducted on all groups of animals. On day 36, at 10 AM, four mice from each group underwent perfusion with 0.9% saline, followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), and their brains and livers were harvested. Observe EGFP fluorescence in the brain and liver. In parallel, four mice from each group had their SN tissues rapidly dissected on ice, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 ℃ for future use.

Behavioral tests

Gait test

To quantify motor coordination deficits by measuring stride length, reflecting bradykinesia and gait impairment in PD. A gray acrylic board with a runway and thickness of 3 mm was used as the testing setup. 200 cm long, 20 cm broad, and 12 cm high make up the runway. On the first day of training, the mice were allowed to become accustomed to the environment. Mice traversed a 200 cm runway. Forepaws/hindpaws were inked with non-toxic dyes. Each mouse underwent 3 consecutive runs; stride length was calculated as the average distance between forepaw and hindpaw footprints per run. A minimum of 6 valid strides per mouse were recorded.

Rotarod test

To assess motor endurance and balance. Each experiment began with the mice being placed in separate isolated lanes. Mice were placed on a rotating rod (4 rpm initial speed, accelerating to 40 rpm over 300 s). Each mouse performed 3 trials (5-min intervals between trials). The recorded metric in each experiment included the average duration in seconds that mice maintained their position on the rod.

Tail suspension test

To evaluate depression-like behavior (stillness indicates behavioral despair). The tail suspension test was used to evaluate depression-like behaviors in animals. The method is based on the observations that when suspended by their tails, mice exhibit immobility, indicative of a state reflecting despair or hopelessness. Mice were suspended 50 cm above ground for 5 min. Immobility (passive hanging without movement) was quantified as total immobility time (s).

Forced swimming

To assess depression-like phenotypes. The forced swimming test was carried out in mice individually forced to swim in an open cylindrical container (diameter 10 cm, height 25 cm), containing 19 cm of water at 25 ± 1 °C. In the test session lasting 5 min, an increase in the immobility duration was considered indicative of an increased depression-related behavior.

Open-field test

To measure anxiety-like behavior and locomotor activity. The device used for the test was based on a square field (50 × 50 × 30 cm). A lamp was located 150 cm above the field, and the illumination of the central area was approximately 100 lx. At the onset of each experiment, the mice were positioned in a central area measuring 15 × 15 cm. Parameters such as the distance moved, the speed of movement, and the time spent in the central area by the mice within a 5 min period were recorded.

Elevated plus maze test

To evaluate anxiety-like behavior (time in open arms reflects reduced anxiety; time in closed arms reflects anxiety/avoidance). The experimental device was positioned at a height of 50 cm from the ground and comprised two opposing open arms (50 × 10 cm), closed arms (50 × 10 × 35 cm), and a central zone (10 × 10 cm). A single 5-min trial was conducted. Mice were placed on the central platform facing an open arm. The time spent by the mice with open and closed arms was recorded, and the ratio of the open time to the total time was analyzed.

Immunohistochemistry

Serial 20-μm-thick coronal brain sections were processed for immunohistochemical staining. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating slides in 1 × Citrate Buffer (98 °C, 10 min), followed by three PBS washes. Permeabilization was achieved with 0.1% Triton X-100 (10 min), and endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched using hydrogen peroxide (20 min, dark), each step followed by triple PBS rinses. Non-specific binding was blocked with 10% normal goat serum, and sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table S1). After washing, sections were incubated with biotinylated secondary antibodies, followed by visualization using a DAB chromogen kit (G1212, Servicebio). Dehydration was performed through graded ethanol series (70%, 80%, 95%, 100%; 3 min each), cleared in xylene-ethanol (1:1) and pure xylene, and coverslipped with neutral balsam. Slides were imaged under a light microscope after drying. Quantitative analysis of immunostaining intensity within predefined threshold ranges was conducted using the IHC profiler plugin for ImageJ software [22].

Evans blue staining

Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 10% chloral hydrate (0.35 mL/kg) or isoflurane inhalation. Once the corneal reflex disappeared, mice were fixed on a mouse board. The tails were soaked in 40 °C warm water for 20 s to dilate blood vessels, followed by slow tail vein injection of 2% Evans blue solution (5 mL/kg). During injection, the engorgement of tail blood vessels was observed. After injection, mice were transferred to a 37 °C thermostatic pad to maintain body temperature and allowed to rest for 1–2 h to ensure full dye circulation. At the predetermined time, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. The skull was quickly opened, the dura mater carefully peeled off, and the brain tissue was completely removed. The brain was gently rinsed in precooled PBS to remove surface blood and debris, and macroscopically observed for the presence of blue-stained areas on its surface.

Observing mitochondrial structure using transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Mouse brain SN tissue was processed for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) as follows: Tissue samples were initially fixed in electron microscopy-grade fixative (G1102, Servicebio, China) for 2 h at room temperature (20–25 °C) to preserve ultrastructural integrity, followed by storage at 4 °C in the dark. Resin-embedded specimens were sectioned into 70–100 nm ultrathin slices using an ultramicrotome, resulting in transparent sections. These sections underwent a dehydration series with gradient ethanol-acetone solutions. Mitochondrial morphology and localization were visualized by negative staining with 2% phosphotungstic acid solution, followed by examination under TEM.

Cell culture

BV2 cell line was purchased from SUNNCELL biological company (Cat. number: SNL-155). The BV2 cells were cultured in high‐glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Gibco Laboratories, Grand Island, NY, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum, penicillin (100 units/mL), and streptomycin, and incubated at 37℃ in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. The culture media was replaced every 2 to 3 days. BV2 cells were seeded in 6‐well plates and divided into the control group, lipopolysaccharides (LPS) (Sigma-Aldrich, EC-297–473-0) group 1 μg/mL [23], FGF21OE or shFgf21 groups, LPS + FGF21OE or LPS + shFgf21 groups after 24 h.

Construction of FGF21OE vector

Based on the FGF21 gene sequence, twelve overlapping primers were synthesized. These primers were combined, and the target DNA fragments were acquired via PCR amplification. Electrophoresis analysis was performed on the PCR products to validate the bands. The products were considered qualified when they exhibited "unique and distinct bands". The qualified PCR products were extracted using gel extraction (DP209, TIANGEN, Beijing, China), and the recovered fragments were applied to the subsequent ligation experiment. The fragments were ligated into the pcDNA3.1-mCherry-C2 vector (with restriction enzyme sites HindIII-XbaI) (R0104S; R0145S; NEB, Beijing, China), followed by screening of positive clone strains with 100% accurate sequences. All constructed plasmids were confirmed by DNA sequencing (Shangya, China). The pcDNA3.1-mCherry-C2-FGF21 plasmid was transfected into BV2 cells using Lipofectamine™ 2000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Construction of shRNA knockdown of Fgf21

The FGF21 nucleotide sequence is shown as: ACCGGgctggagcacggttacaatgtctcgagacattgtaaccgtcctccagcTTTTGAATTC. The target fragments were inserted into the pLKO.1-puro vector (containing SphI-EcoRI restriction enzyme sites) (R3182V; R0101V; NEB, Beijing, China), and positive clone strains with 100% accurate sequences were screened. All constructed plasmids were verified by DNA sequencing (Shangya, China). The sequencing primer used was: TACGATACAAGGCTGTTAGAAG. The Mus-Fgf21-shRNA1-pLKO.1-puro plasmid was transfected into BV2 cells with Lipofectamine™ 2000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's standard protocol.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis

Total RNA extraction was performed with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, San Diego, CA, USA) based on the protocol supplied by the manufacturer, and the SYBR Green reagent was used in qPCR mRNA quantification. The housekeeping gene used was GAPDH, and the comparative ΔΔCT method was used to calculate mRNA relative expression levels. The primer sequence is shown in Table 1.

Western blot

SN and BV2 cell was homogenized with a Polytron in ice-cold RIPA buffer supplemented with PMSF (G2002; G2008, Servicebio, China), sonicated, and cleared by centrifugation (12,000 × g, 10 min, at 4℃). Protein concentration in the supernatant was determined by BCA assay and separated on SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto nitrocellulose membrane (Millipore, IPFL00010, Germany) by electrophoresis. Blots were blocked in 5% nonfat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature (20–25 °C) and probed with primary antibody (Supplementary Table S1) in TBST with 1% nonfat milk overnight at 4℃. After overnight incubation, this was followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody in TBST with 1% nonfat milk for 2 h at room temperature (20–25 °C). The blots were developed using an Enhanced Chemiluminescence assay (BIO-Rad).

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence analyses were performed with 20-μm-thick serial brain sections and BV2 cells. The brain sections and BV2 cells underwent the following series of treatments: treatment with 1xCitrate Antigen Retrieval Solution at 98℃ for 10 min followed by washing three times with PBS, treatment with 0.1% Triton X-100 transparent for 10 min followed by washing three times with PBS, and blocking with 10% goat serum in PBS and overnight incubation with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table S1). After washing thrice with PBS, the brain tissues and BV2 cells were incubated with a mixture of Alexa-fluor 555-conjugated, Alexa-fluor 488-conjugated (1:2000, Invitrogen), and Alexa-fluor 568-conjugated (1:500, abcam) secondary antibodies at room temperature (20–25 °C) for 2 h, and staining of nucleus with DAPI for 10 min. The sections were mounted with VectaShield medium (Vector Laboratories) and subjected to fluorescein detection using a fluorescence microscope (Leica DMI4000B, Wetzlar, Germany). Image J analysis of the mean fluorescence intensity of protein.

Fluorescence detection

Mitochondria were isolated from SN using a Mitochondria Isolation Kit (C3601; C3606, Beyotime). The mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using a JC-1 Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit (G1515, Servicebio). MitoSOX™ indicator (M36008, thermofisher) is used for detecting mitochondrial superoxide (MitoSOX) in BV2 cells. Cell calcium ion (Ca2+) detection was performed using Rhod2-AM (HY-D0989, MCE) fluorescent probe, while cell mitochondrial detection was performed using Mito Tracker Green (C1048, Beyotime) fluorescent probe.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

For BV2 cells in different groups, the DMEM was removed, and the cells were washed once with PBS. Then, 500 μL of cell lysis buffer was added to lyse the cells. After centrifugation at 10,000 rpm/min for 10 min, the supernatant was collected. 0.2 -2 μg of the primary antibody (FGFR1 or Sirt1) for immunoprecipitation was added, and the mixture was slowly shaken at 4 ºC overnight. Subsequently, 40 μL of fully resuspended Protein A + G Agarose (P2179S, Beyotime) was added, and the mixture was slowly shaken at 4 ºC for 2 h. After centrifugation at 2,500 rpm/min for 5 min, the supernatant was carefully removed, and the precipitate was Protein A + G Agarose. The precipitate was washed 5 times with 1 mL of PBS. After the final wash, the supernatant was removed, and the sample was used for SDS-PAGE electrophoresis.

Analysis of FGFR1 and Sirt1 protein interaction

The preprocessed structures of FGFR1 (PDB ID: 5A4C) and Sirt1 (PDB ID: 4ZZH) were imported into the ZDOCK platform as receptor and ligand, respectively. A rigid docking algorithm (e.g., classical fast Fourier transform (FFT) correlation method) was employed to predict their complex conformation ensemble. Following global sampling to generate 2000 initial binding models, the top 10 candidate models ranked by Z-score (reflecting the statistical significance of binding conformations) were selected for in-depth analysis. PyMOL and LigPlot + tools were further utilized to resolve the binding interface between FGFR1 and Sirt1, with a focus on evaluating key amino acid residues.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism, a widely used software for scientific data visualization and statistical testing. Experimental data are presented as either mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) or mean ± standard deviation (SD), providing measures of central tendency and variability to reflect data distribution characteristics. One-Way Analysis of Variance (One-Way ANOVA) applied for comparisons involving three or more independent groups. This test evaluates whether there are statistically significant differences among the means of the groups, followed by post-hoc tests (implied but not explicitly specified here) to identify which specific groups differ from each other. A Pvalue of less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

In vivo experiments

Behavioral testing

Compared with the Saline group, MPTP-induced PD mice model showed motor dysfunction: the MPTP group exhibited a significant reduction in stride length (Fig. 2A) and a marked decrease in latency to fall in the rotarod test (Fig. 2B). In assessments of depressive-like behaviors, the tail suspension test and forced swimming test revealed a significant increase in immobility duration in the MPTP group (Figs. 2C, 2D). Regarding anxious behaviors, the MPTP group showed a significant decrease in total distance traveled in the open field test (Figs. 2E, 2F) and a notable reduction in time spent in the open arms of the elevated plus maze (Figs. 2G, 2H). Notably, compared with the MPTP or MPTP + rAAV-null groups, rAAV-Fgf21 mice in the PD model demonstrated a certain degree of improvement in these behavioral tests, indicating that the FGF21OE can alleviate motor deficits and non-motor symptoms in PD mice.

Pathological indicator detection

Following rAAV tail vein injection, EGFP fluorescence was observed in the liver and SN of both the MPTP + rAAV-null and MPTP + rAAV-Fgf21 groups (Figs. 3A–C). TH and Iba1 immunofluorescence co-localization staining demonstrated that, compared with the Saline group, the MPTP group exhibited reduced mean fluorescence intensity of TH and increased protein fluorescence intensity of Iba1. In contrast, compared with the MPTP group, FGF21OE (MPTP + rAAV-Fgf21 group) significantly enhanced TH fluorescence intensity and markedly diminished Iba1 fluorescence intensity (Figs. 3D, 3E). Furthermore, parallel findings were observed in protein expression and immunohistochemical analyses: MPTP treatment decreased TH protein levels and the number of TH-positive cells, while elevating Iba1 protein expression and Iba1-positive cell counts. Conversely, FGF21OE substantially increased TH protein expression and TH-positive cell numbers, while significantly reducing Iba1 protein levels and Iba1-positive cell counts (Supplementary Figure S1). In the SN, MPTP treatment decreased the fluorescence intensity of Sirt1 compared with the Saline group, whereas FGF21OE significantly elevated Sirt1 levels compared with the MPTP or MPTP + rAAV-null groups (Figs. 3F, 3G).

Analysis of BBB

Evans Blue staining was used to assess BBB integrity. Increased dye leakage into brain tissue indicates compromised BBB permeability. The results suggest that MPTP treatment disrupted the BBB, while rAAV-Fgf21 administration may reduce this leakage, reflecting potential BBB stabilization (Fig. 4A). Compared with the Saline group, the MPTP group downregulated claudin, zonula occludens (ZO-1), and occludin and upregulated glail fibrillary acidicprotein (GFAP), indicating BBB dysfunction and neuroinflammation. In contrast, rAAV-Fgf21 treatment reversed these trends (Figs. 4B, 4C), suggesting therapeutic modulation of BBB integrity and astrocyte activation. Immunofluorescence displays the distribution of ZO-1 in brain tissue. MPTP induced PD mice showed ZO-1 fragmentation or reduced fluorescence intensity, while rAAV-Fgf21 treatment maintained ZO-1 continuity, indicating enhanced integrity of tight junctions (Figs. 4D, 4E). Dual immunofluorescence staining highlighted the spatial relationship between BBB tight junctions and astrocytes. MPTP treatment disrupted occludin localization and increased GFAP-positive astrocytes, reflecting BBB breakdown and neuroinflammation. rAAV-Fgf21 restored occludin organization and reduced GFAP activation (Figs. 4F, 4G).

rAAV-Fgf21 modulates FGFR1-Sirt1 pathway, microglial activation, autophagy, and apoptosis

Compared with the Saline group, MPTP group suppressed FGF21 and PGC-1α and downstream phosphorylated proteins (p-FGFR1, p-Sirt1, p-FOXO1, p-AMPK, p-mTOR); compared with the MPTP or MPTP + rAAV-null group, rAAV-Fgf21 treatment reversed these effects, enhancing FGF21-mediated signaling pathway (AMPK/mTOR) (Figs. 5A, 5B). Pro-inflammatory markers (TLR4, NLRP3, p-NF-κB, IL6, TNFα, IL1β, p-iNOS) were upregulated in MPTP-treated mice, while anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL4, IL10) were downregulated. rAAV-Fgf21 attenuated pro-inflammatory responses and restored anti-inflammatory cytokines levels (Figs. 5C, 5D). MPTP treatment reduced autophagy markers (PINK1, Parkin, LC3A/B, Beclin-1) and increased pro-apoptotic proteins (Casp3, Bax). rAAV-Fgf21 enhanced autophagy (elevated PINK1, Parkin, LC3-II/LC3-I ratio, Beclin-1) and suppressed apoptosis (reduced Casp3, Bax) (Figs. 5E, 5F).

Mitochondrial dysfunction analysis

JC-1 staining revealed that compared with the Saline group, the MPTP group exhibited a reduction in JC-1 aggregates (red) and an increase in JC-1 monomers (green), indicating that MPTP induces a decline in mitochondrial membrane potential and mitochondrial dysfunction. Compared with the MPTP or MPTP + rAAV-null groups, the MPTP + rAAV-Fgf21 group showed a relative increase in JC-1 aggregates and a decrease in JC-1 monomers, suggesting that FGF21OE ameliorates the MPTP-induced reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential (Figs. 6A, 6B). Compared with the Saline group, the MPTP group demonstrated significantly decreased protein expression of optic atrophy 1 (OPA1) and mitofusin 1 (Mfn1), alongside markedly increased expression of dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1) and Fission 1 (Fis1). Discussion

In recent years, FGF21, as a metabolic regulator, has garnered significant attention for its role in central nervous system diseases [24, 25]. This study systematically revealed through in vivo and in vitro experiments the pleiotropic protective mechanisms of FGF21OE against PD models via the FGFR1-Sirt1 pathway. The innovation lies in its integration of multi-dimensional mechanisms, including BBB integrity preservation, mitochondrial dynamics regulation, microglial polarization modulation, and autophagy-apoptosis balance, providing a novel theoretical foundation for PD treatment. Recent advances in the metabolic-neural axis have highlighted FGF21’s neuroprotective potential. For instance, Claflin et al. demonstrated that FGF21 enhances leptin signaling by modulating glutamatergic neurons [10], underscoring its unique advantage in bridging metabolic and neurodegenerative regulation. Additionally, a recent study by Xu et al. showed that FGF21 protects neurons after spinal cord injury by inhibiting ferroptosis [12], further supporting its broad neuroprotective potential.

This study confirmed that FGF21OE significantly alleviates motor dysfunction (abnormal gait, reduced rotarod endurance) and non-motor symptoms (depressive and anxiety-like behaviors) in MPTP-induced PD mice, consistent with Fang et al.’s findings that FGF21 protects dopaminergic neurons by suppressing neuroinflammation [9]. Notably, FGF21OE not only increased the number of TH-positive neurons in the SN pars compacta (SNpc) but also enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis via activation of the Sirt1-PGC-1α axis, thereby ameliorating energy metabolism deficits in dopaminergic neurons. Activation of PGC-1α has been shown to promote mitochondrial DNA repair [26], which may represent a key mechanism underlying FGF21’s improvement of mitochondrial function. Furthermore, the alleviation of non-motor symptoms by FGF21 may involve its modulation of neuroinflammation in the prefrontal cortex and limbic system. For example, Wang et al. reported that FGF21 mitigates depressive-like behaviors by inhibiting NF-κB signaling in the hippocampus [27], suggesting its potential for multi-regional neuroprotective synergy. Intriguingly, FGF21 was also found to influence PD non-motor symptoms by modulating the gut microbiota-brain axis [28], opening new avenues for future research.

Disruption of the BBB is one of the early pathological features of PD) This study revealed that FGF21OE effectively reduces Evans blue leakage and preserves BBB integrity by upregulating tight junction proteins (claudin, ZO-1, occludin) and suppressing abnormal astrocyte activation (downregulated GFAP). These findings align with Gasca-Salas et al.’s proposal that increased BBB permeability accelerates PD progression [2]. Further mechanistic investigations suggest that FGF21 may indirectly protect the BBB by inhibiting the TLR4/NLRP3/NF-κB pathway to mitigate neuroinflammation. The study highlights that BBB repair strategies, such as nanoparticle-delivered miRNA-155 inhibitors, can enhance tight junction protein expression [29], and FGF21 may synergistically reinforce BBB stability through analogous mechanisms. Additionally, whether FGF21’s direct regulatory effects on the BBB involve β-Klotho co-receptor-mediated endothelial cell signaling remains to be explored. Dynamic conformational changes in the β-Klotho/FGFR1 complex may influence FGF21’s transmembrane signaling [30], offering new insights into its molecular mechanisms of BBB regulation.

This study confirmed the physical interaction between FGFR1 and Sirt1 through molecular docking and co-immunoprecipitation, demonstrating that FGF21OE regulates mitochondrial dynamics (promoting fusion proteins OPA1/Mfn1 while suppressing fission proteins Drp1/Fis1) and energy metabolism (via the AMPK/mTOR pathway) through activation of this pathway. As an NAD + -dependent deacetylase, Sirt1 may enhance the transcriptional activity of PGC-1α via deacetylation, thereby promoting mitochondrial fusion and biogenesis [31]. Sirt1 also alleviates oxidative stress by modulating the Nrf2/ARE pathway [32], suggesting that the FGFR1-Sirt1 axis may act synergistically through multiple signaling networks. Furthermore, FGF21OE activates autophagy (increased LC3-II/LC3-I ratio and upregulated Beclin-1) via the AMPK/mTOR pathway, clearing damaged mitochondria (via PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy) while suppressing Casp3/Bax-mediated apoptosis, forming a multi-layered protective network. Notably, subcellular localization shifts of mTORC1 have been shown to affect autophagic flux efficiency [33], warranting future exploration of whether FGF21 enhances autophagy specificity by modulating mTOR spatial distribution.

FGF21OE significantly inhibits microglial polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype (downregulated IL-1β, TNFα, and iNOS) and promotes transition to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype (upregulated IL-10, CD206, and YM-1). This finding aligns with our previous research on how microglial polarization imbalance exacerbates PD neurodegeneration [34]. Mechanistically, FGF21 may suppress NF-κB nuclear translocation via Sirt1, blocking NLRP3 inflammasome activation and reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine release. Studies indicate that Sirt1 also inhibits M1 polarization by deacetylating STAT3 [35], suggesting multi-target regulatory potential. Additionally, FGF21’s promotion of M2 polarization may involve the IL-4/STAT6 signaling pathway [36]. Recent single-cell sequencing applications have identified heterogeneous subpopulations within M2 microglia [37], necessitating future research to determine whether FGF21 selectively regulates specific subsets. Intriguingly, metabolic reprogramming (e.g., glycolysis-to-oxidative phosphorylation shifts) has been shown to drive microglial polarization [38], and FGF21, as a metabolic regulator, may exert its effects through this pathway.

The innovation of this study lies in: (1) systematic demonstration of FGF21’s multi-dimensional effects in integrating mitochondrial function, autophagy, inflammation, and BBB protection via the FGFR1-Sirt1 pathway; (2) elucidating the structural basis of FGFR1-Sirt1 interaction by combining molecular docking with in vivo and in vitro models; (3) proposing a novel mechanism for FGF21 in regulating microglial polarization. However, the MPTP model primarily mimics dopaminergic neuron damage and fails to fully recapitulate core PD pathological features such as α-synuclein aggregation. The α-synuclein fibril injection model developed in this study better aligns with human PD pathology [39], and future work should validate FGF21’s efficacy in this system. Additionally, the long-term safety of FGF21 in humans and its BBB penetration efficiency require further verification. Notably, the FGF21 analog Pegbelfermin has shown good tolerability in clinical trials [40], though its neuroprotective potential remains unconfirmed. Although the LPS model cannot fully replicate all pathological features of PD (such as α-synuclein aggregation), it effectively reproduces key neuroinflammation and microglial polarization abnormalities observed in PD, making it suitable for investigating the role of FGF21 in regulating neuroimmune responses. This study combines the MPTP-induced PD mouse model and the LPS-induced BV2 cell model to elucidate the multi-mechanistic neuroprotective effects of FGF21 across different levels.

Future directions include: exploring the clinical translation potential of FGF21 analogs or small-molecule agonists (e.g., engineering BBB-penetrant variants); investigating synergistic effects of FGF21 with other metabolic regulators (e.g., FGF19, FGF23)—studies suggest FGF19 enhances mitochondrial function via the AMPK pathway [41], hinting at additive benefits of combinatorial approaches; leveraging gene-editing tools (e.g., CRISPR/dCas9) to enhance FGF21 expression, as demonstrated by the AAV-CRISPR system recently applied in Parkinsonian macaques [42]; and validating FGF21’s subtype-specific effects using organoid or humanized PD models, such as patient induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSC)-derived brain organoids that mimic genetic PD pathology [43], to advance personalized therapies.

This study systematically elucidates the multi-mechanistic role of FGF21 in ameliorating PD pathology via the FGFR1-Sirt1 axis, spanning mitochondrial function, neuroinflammation, autophagy-apoptosis balance, and BBB protection, thereby identifying novel therapeutic targets (Fig. 12). Future efforts should prioritize clinical translation and refine individualized treatment strategies through precision medicine. Examples include screening FGF21-responsive populations based on patient genotypes or metabolic profiles, or developing targeted delivery systems to enhance cerebral bioavailability. As research on metabolic-neural crosstalk deepens, FGF21 is poised to emerge as a central molecule in multi-mechanism combination therapies for PD.

The influence of FGF21 on PD through the FGFR1-Sirt1 pathway. The BBB is composed of proteins such as claudin, occludin, and ZO-1, and astrocytes (with GFAP as the marker) are involved in it. When FGF21 crosses the BBB, it binds to FGFR1, which activates the Sirt1 pathway. Once Sirt1 is activated, it further activates PGC-1α. As an important regulatory factor for mitochondrial dynamic balance, PGC-1α can promote the expression of mitochondrial fusion-related proteins Mfn1 and OPA1, enhancing the mitochondrial fusion process. At the same time, it inhibits the activities of mitochondrial fission-related proteins Drp1 and Fis, reducing mitochondrial fission. Meanwhile, the binding of FGF21 to FGFR1 also activates the AMPK pathway. As a key regulatory kinase for cellular energy metabolism, AMPK acts on mTOR on the one hand, regulating the autophagy process. Abnormal mitochondrial autophagy will lead to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria in nerve cells. On the other hand, AMPK can inhibit NF-κB, and then inhibit the activities of apoptosis-related proteins Casp3 and Bax, reducing the apoptosis of nerve cells. The FGF21 can promote the transformation of pro-inflammatory M1-type microglia into anti-inflammatory M2-type microglia. In conclusion, through multiple signaling pathways, FGF21 plays a regulatory role in aspects such as mitochondrial dynamic balance, microglial polarization, autophagy, apoptosis, and the survival of neuronal cells in PD
