A role for vegetation phenology in shaping patterns of ecosystem sensitivity to drought
Authors: Andrew J. Felton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPhenology, as the study of the timing and rhythms of vegetation activity, has traditionally emphasized trends in phenological responses to gradual climate change. Consequently, we lack understanding of phenological responses to concurrent increases in the frequency and intensity of climate extremes. Here, I advance a viewpoint that vegetation phenology is not just altered by drought, but that such alterations frequently generate compensatory dynamics that provide a mechanistic explanation for observed variation in ecosystem sensitivity to drought. I first review a growing body of literature on how vegetation phenology is altered during drought. I then develop hypotheses for how such phenological dynamics during these extremes can underpin and drive patterns of drought sensitivity among ecosystems. Finally, I test predictions of these hypotheses across a large grassland ecoregion, empirically demonstrating that variation in phenological responses during extreme drought can explain cross‐ecosystem variation in drought sensitivity of annual net primary productivity. Deeper consideration of vegetation phenology under drought intensification can improve our understanding and ability to anticipate the ecological consequences of climate change.

Introduction

The study of vegetation phenology has historically emphasized longer‐term trends in climatic changes, such as warming, and the responses of key phenological events, such as leaf‐out or flowering (Richardson et al., 2013; Piao et al., 2019; Collins et al., 2021; Pausas, 2025). From retrospective analyses we have learned, for example, that vegetation across northern hemispheric ecosystems has earlier spring greening under recent warming (Lian et al., 2020). From prospective modeling we have learned, for example, that peak vegetation activity in temperate ecosystems is likely to shift from the summer months to the spring and fall months throughout the 21st century (Hufkens et al., 2016; Teets et al., 2023). These climate‐driven shifts in the timing and rhythms of vegetation activity can have associated costs, namely the loss of vegetation function during periods when peak activity formerly occurred, such as summer (Angert et al., 2005). Yet these costs are not necessarily reflected in vegetation function measured at aggregated annual timescales. In some cases, these climate‐induced shifts in phenological dynamics underlie the apparent impacts of climate change – or lack thereof – to commonly used annual measures of vegetation function (e.g. productivity) (Buermann et al., 2018). Climate‐driven shifts in the rhythms of vegetation activity add nuance toward understanding the impacts of climate change on ecosystem function.

Here, I argue that this nuance should extend to considering phenological dynamics under intensified climate extremes and especially under drought intensification. A growing body of evidence suggests that such phenological dynamics observed in response to longer‐term, more gradual climate change operate similarly under shorter‐term, more abrupt extreme drought (Wolf et al., 2016; Knapp et al., 2020; Miller et al., 2023). For example, during the 2012 North American drought, northeastern forests increased their carbon uptake during the spring, which largely offset summer losses in carbon uptake and stabilized the carbon balance of North America, despite one of the most extreme large‐scale droughts in the prior 100 years (Wolf et al., 2016). Process‐based models largely fail to capture such dynamics (Miller et al., 2023). Thus, we still possess a limited understanding of the response and role of short‐term extreme events in shaping vegetation phenology, which has implications for terrestrial ecosystem processes and land surface feedbacks to the climate system (Richardson et al., 2013; Li et al., 2025). The importance of this limited understanding is heightened amid contemporary increases in climate variability and extremes globally, which includes an intensification of drought (King et al., 2024; L. Chen et al., 2025a; Z. Chen et al., 2025b), heat waves (Barriopedro et al., 2023), and the size of precipitation events (Feldman et al., 2026). These extreme events interact with global trends of rising average temperatures and water limitation (Denissen et al., 2022) and are arguably the primary way that the public experiences contemporary climate change.

I further argue that our limited understanding of the interplay between vegetation phenology and drought undermines our ability to explain the apparent high variability in ecosystem sensitivity to drought (Smith et al., 2024). In this Viewpoint, I briefly summarize research demonstrating how and why vegetation phenology is altered during climatically extreme drought years, with extremity defined as anomalies exceeding (falling below) a target threshold, such as the 1st or 5th percentile of the long‐term climate record. I emphasize drought because such events alter both temperature and water (Williams et al., 2020; Dannenberg et al., 2022), are responsible for a large degree of temporal variation in the global carbon cycle (Zscheischler et al., 2014), and have been of increasing focus in ecological research over the last two decades (Ciais et al., 2005; Schwalm et al., 2017; Z. Chen et al., 2025b) as water limitation rises globally (Jiao et al., 2021; Denissen et al., 2022).

I approach discussing phenology as something akin to what was recently dubbed ‘ecosystem phenology’, which emphasizes continuous ecosystem traits and processes over a historical emphasis on single or seasonal biological events (Chen & Peñuelas, 2025). I specifically focus on the temporal dynamics of ecosystem productivity that are an emergent outcome of physiological (Throop et al., 2012), community (Knapp et al., 2020), and consequently phenological processes that interact during drought (Felton & Smith, 2017). Through literature review and analyses focusing on terrestrial ecosystems with marked seasonal cycles, I show evidence for how vegetation phenology is not just altered by drought, but how these alterations can generate offsetting seasonal responses of vegetation that stabilize ecosystem function at an annualized timescale. Variability in these temporal compensatory dynamics during drought offers an underappreciated mechanism to explain the large spatial variability in drought sensitivity, that is impacts, among ecosystems (Stuart‐Haëntjens et al., 2018; Smith et al., 2024).

Understanding variation in ecosystem sensitivity to drought

It has become a truism that ecosystem responses to climate extremes, namely drought, are variable (Smith, 2011). This truism has been the result of much research focused specifically on understanding both the ecological implications of drought intensification (Cook et al., 2015) and the mechanisms underlying observed differential sensitivity among ecosystems to drought (Stuart‐Haëntjens et al., 2018). It is perhaps surprising, then, that ecologists remain challenged to understand why ecosystem functioning is negatively impacted by drought in some ecosystems and in some seasons, but not in others. Even a globally coordinated drought experiment largely failed to explain the wide variability in grassland and shrubland annual productivity responses to a target 1‐in‐100‐yr experimental drought. In this case, drought intensity – or simply the magnitude of precipitation reductions – was the most relevant explanatory variable amid many explored, explaining just 6% of the variation in drought responses (Smith et al., 2024). This specific result is illustrative of a broader challenge; there remains a surprisingly large amount of unexplained variability in drought sensitivity among terrestrial ecosystems.

Much thinking about what determines site‐to‐site differences, that is spatial variability, in drought sensitivity is framed through resource colimitation theory (Eskelinen & Harrison, 2015). This thinking stems from the logic of Liebig's law of the minimum, which states that plant productivity is limited by the resource least in supply relative to demand. For explaining spatial variability in drought sensitivity, the co‐limitation hypothesis further states that historically drier locations should be more sensitive to drought given a stronger control of water limitation on ecosystem functioning (Stuart‐Haëntjens et al., 2018; Knapp et al., 2024). The logic is that despite the obvious role of water limitation in shaping the evolution of plant functional traits in water‐limited regions (Griffin‐Nolan et al., 2023), what appears more salient for explaining drought sensitivity is that such dryland species must also survive closer to their functional limits, rendering them more vulnerable to drought (Anderegg et al., 2019). By contrast, ecosystem functioning in wetter (or colder), more energy‐limited systems should respond more strongly to changes in radiation, temperature, or soil nutrients than to water (Cunha et al., 2022). Yet even in some energy‐limited tropical ecosystems, dry season productivity is more sensitive to drought than wet season productivity (Tang et al., 2024), implying seasonal differences in water vs energy limitation within an ecosystem (Miller et al., 2023). A specific prediction of the resource co‐limitation hypothesis is that ecosystem sensitivity to drought increases as historical water limitation (e.g. aridity) increases, a prediction that has some, albeit mixed, empirical support (Knapp et al., 2015; Stuart‐Haëntjens et al., 2018; Felton et al., 2021b).

If we view the resource co‐limitation hypothesis from the perspective of vegetation dynamics throughout a year, we can see phenology's potential signal. Using daily canopy greenness data from the PhenoCam Network (Richardson et al., 2018a; for details see Supporting Information), Fig. 1(a) shows divergent intra‐annual dynamics of vegetation greenness during extreme (below fifth percentile of mean annual precipitation) droughts between more energy‐limited, deciduous broadleaf forests and more water‐limited, grasslands (Table S1). Whereas canopy greenness during summer tends to be enhanced in deciduous broadleaf forests during drought years (but see Nardini et al., 2013), it is dramatically reduced in grasslands. This implies a potential, general divide in the intra‐annual dynamics of vegetation responses to drought along the energy‐ vs water‐limitation spectrum. Yet the plot thickens as we move from aggregated, averaged phenological responses among biomes (Fig. 1a) to considering spatial variability in these dynamics within a biome (Fig. 1b,c).

A role for vegetation phenology

A key reason for our limited ability to explain variability in ecosystem sensitivity to drought is an overemphasis on interannual timescales and relationships between vegetation function and climate variability. A consequence of this is an underemphasis on the intra‐annual dynamics that evolve during years of drought. Yet it is these intra‐annual and seasonal phenological dynamics that ultimately shape patterns and processes at annual timescales (Richardson et al., 2009; Wolf et al., 2016; Feldman et al., 2024a). Indeed, it is clear that vegetation functioning is differentially sensitive to temperature and precipitation throughout the year (Xu et al., 2020), may only be sensitive to these drivers during relatively narrow periods within a year (Craine et al., 2012; Holguin et al., 2022; Felton & Goldsmith, 2023), and can be equally as sensitive to daily‐scale climate variability as to interannual‐scale climate variability (Feldman et al., 2024b).

Because seasonality is a primary determinant of vegetation structure (Hajek & Knapp, 2022), it provides a mechanistic basis for understanding how abiotic shifts in the environment during drought may impact vegetation phenology and function. At the crux of this is that drought frequently alters the intra‐annual and seasonal dynamics of, and relationships between, water and energy. For example, in the Great Plains of North America, drought weakens the strong positive coupling between monthly temperature and precipitation primarily by reducing summer precipitation, thus increasing and decreasing the abundance of cool‐season C3 and warm‐season C4 vegetation, respectively (Knapp et al., 2020). This compositional outcome would not be predicted by species physiology alone and instead arises due to how the climatology of drought interacts with the phenology of different species. Specifically, peak water stress during the summer months tends to co‐occur with the timing of peak C4 vegetation production during drought years in the Great Plains (Felton & Goldsmith, 2023), overriding the direct physiological (photorespiratory) advantage of the C4 photosynthetic pathway under warmer and drier conditions (Sage, 2004). Negative correlations between spring temperature and soil moisture across all northern ecosystems (Lian et al., 2020) further imply that warmer springs can amplify reductions in water availability during the summer, resulting in negative soil moisture legacies generated primarily by enhanced evapotranspiration during the spring that increase summer water stress (Wolf et al., 2016).

Although temperature (Ciais et al., 2005; Williams et al., 2022), evapotranspiration (Zhao et al., 2022), and vapor pressure deficit (VPD) (Dannenberg et al., 2022) are all frequently higher during drought, their impacts on vegetation function are unevenly distributed through time. This disparity is reflected in the nonlinear (concave‐up) relationship between air temperature and VPD (Hajek & Knapp, 2022), meaning a 1°C increase during (warmer) summers generates higher VPD than a 1° increase during (cooler) springs. Given this and the typically negative constraint of VPD on photosynthesis (Grossiord et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2025), it should be no surprise that the magnitude of loss in vegetation function due to reduced precipitation inputs during drought tends to be magnified in the warmest months and in the warmest locations in temperate regions, where VPD is spatiotemporally at its highest (Felton & Goldsmith, 2023).

Consistent with expectations of resource co‐limitation theory, the net outcome from these dynamics for annualized metrics of vegetation function may ultimately depend on ‘where’ the ecosystem or ‘when’ the season lands on the water‐to‐energy limitation spectrum (Liu et al., 2025). Some evidence suggests that drought can advance (Li et al., 2023) or stimulate spring vegetation growth, especially in energy‐limited systems (Miller et al., 2023), providing a mechanism to offset later and more common reductions in productivity during summers and thus reducing the apparent net ecosystem impacts of droughts at annual timescales. Perhaps especially salient for shaping these compensatory dynamics is the extant functional – both physiological (Throop et al., 2012) and phenological (Knapp et al., 2020) – diversity (Fig. 1b,c), specifically in terms of the spread of functional types able to take advantage of how drought shapes the seasonal dynamics of temperature and precipitation (Fig. 2).

There is also more direct evidence for seasonal differences in drought impacts. Observational comparisons of seasonal impacts across China revealed consistent reductions in ecosystem‐scale water use efficiency (WUE: GPP/ET) during summer droughts yet widespread increases in WUE during spring droughts (Huang & Zhai, 2024). This is consistent with observations of peak drought impacts to vegetation function during summer drought across a diversity of ecosystem types (Deng et al., 2023) and alludes to seasonal differences in drought impacts on carbon‐water dynamics. Experimental manipulations of precipitation further support seasonal differences in drought sensitivity of ecosystem processes, with observed peak sensitivity in both the spring (Li et al., 2022) and summer in temperate systems (Denton et al., 2017; Hahn et al., 2021) and in the dry seasons in tropical systems (Fisher et al., 2007). Averaging deciduous forest and grassland vegetation greenness responses together hints at differing seasonal drought response dynamics (Fig. 1a inset), but there is high site‐to‐site variability underpinning this pattern, both when separating out grassland vs forest responses (Fig. 1a) and when comparing different locations within a biome (Fig. 1b,c).

The potential importance of this seasonal dynamic for explaining variability in drought sensitivity becomes clarified when considering water‐limited ecosystems that tend to have high sensitivity to climate variability (Maurer et al., 2020; Felton et al., 2021b), span large spatial gradients in water availability (Sala et al., 1988), and are projected to be highly vulnerable to drought intensification (Z. Chen et al., 2025b). In many water‐limited biomes, such as grasslands, climate‐driven fluctuations in water availability can exert as strong – or stronger – a control on vegetation phenology as temperature (Broich et al., 2014; Currier & Sala, 2022; Post et al., 2022). As such, the seasonal cycle of vegetation growth can be dramatically altered during climatically extreme years (Ma et al., 2015, Fig. S1).

An analysis of the phenological dynamics of a single location in the Northern Great Plains of North America (north‐central Montana, USA) – a cooler (MAT = 7.9°C, MAP = 333.9 mm), semi‐arid ecoregion of mixed‐grass prairies co‐dominated by both C3 and C4 grasses – shows an example of how opposing seasonal responses can explain annual‐scale drought impacts (or lack thereof) on grassland productivity (Fig. 1b). Remote sensing productivity estimates (Running et al., 2004) of a location with low observed drought sensitivity (Felton & Goldsmith, 2023) show that during one of the driest years (2017; 49% reduction from mean annual precipitation) of the 21st century for this location, spring (March–May) enhancement of carbon uptake (gross primary productivity; g C m−2) offsets summer reductions in carbon uptake (June–August) (Fig. 1b). As a result, estimated annual net primary productivity during drought was near‐exactly (193 g m−2) the long‐term (23 yr) average of 191 g C m−2 for that location (Fig. 1b top inset). Opposing seasonal responses of carbon uptake were underpinned by both higher temperatures throughout the growing season and lower precipitation (Thornton et al., 2021) that peaked during the summer months (Fig. 2a). This example illustrates one phenological response pathway that partially explains the lack of drought impact to annual‐scale net primary productivity (Fig. 1b).

An analysis of a single location within the Central Great Plains of North America (Felton et al., 2019) (northern Colorado, USA) – a warmer (MAT = 9.1°C, MAP = 338.9 mm) semi‐arid ecoregion dominated by C4 grasses – shows an example of another pathway: zero enhancement of spring carbon uptake coupled with large summer losses. This is despite both highly similar precipitation and temperature dynamics and a drought of similar magnitude (2022; 41% reduction) as the Northern Great Plains (Fig. 2), which in turn led to a near‐elimination of the seasonal phenological cycle (Fig. S1) and a 44% reduction in annual net primary productivity, relative to the long‐term average of 231 g C m−2 (Fig. 1c bottom inset).

Given the similar temperature and precipitation dynamics among the grasslands during their respective drought events (Fig. 2), the observed differences in phenological responses to drought likely reflect their broader ecoregion‐scale differences in biotic attributes such as functional diversity (C3 vs C4 dominance) and baseline water availability. Collectively, the analyses in Fig. 1 show examples of the existing variation in phenological dynamics in response to drought, both among (Fig. 1a) and within biomes (Fig. 1b,c). This phenological variation offers a potential explanation for variation in the sensitivity in annualized measures of vegetation function to drought among not just these specific locations, but potentially across entire regions with high seasonality. Below, I outline a simple hypothesis and test it across an expansive grassland ecoregion in North America using remote sensing data.

The compensation hypothesis

A core premise motivating this Viewpoint is that the combined alterations to water and energy during drought may generate compensatory dynamics in vegetation activity that impact whole‐ecosystem phenology. For drought, the impact of lower precipitation (reduced water inputs) and higher temperatures (and elevated evaporative demand) (Fig. 2) on vegetation phenology (Fig. 1b,c) may differ across spatiotemporal gradients of energy vs water limitation. I draw from observations of vegetation productivity (or proxies thereof) increasing during drought, most prominently in energy‐limited ecosystems and seasons (Chen et al., 2021; Miller et al., 2023). The simplest explanation for this dynamic is the same as for why spring leaf‐out or flowering occurs earlier under gradual warming (Pausas, 2025); dormancy is broken and vegetation productivity is stimulated by warmer – though not physiologically extreme – temperatures, thus advancing the onset and even increasing the magnitude of early growing season productivity, provided sufficient water availability.

The result is a potential stimulation of vegetation productivity that may partially or completely compensate for later and more likely reductions during summer, when water limitation and stress is maximized during droughts in temperate regions with well‐defined seasonal cycles (Felton & Goldsmith, 2023). The balance between this stimulation in spring productivity and compensation for summer losses in productivity during drought, and how this varies among ecosystems, may at least partially explain observed variation in annual‐scale ecosystem sensitivity to drought (Smith et al., 2024). As such, the compensation hypothesis states that warmer spring temperatures during droughts frequently stimulate spring productivity, offsetting later and more consistent losses to productivity during the summer, thus reducing the apparent impacts of drought on an annualized basis (Fig. 3a). A further expectation is that these compensation dynamics should vary spatially – both within and among biomes – according to gradients in energy vs water limitation. A specific prediction is greater spring enhancement of productivity in historically wetter (more energy limited) locations and weaker in historically drier (more water limited) locations (Fig. 3b).

The hypothesis thus secondarily posits the importance of spatial variation in historical constraints of energy vs water limitation, in that more energy‐limited locations experience stronger stimulation of spring vegetation function during drought and that this compensation during spring weakens as historical water‐limitation of site increases. Note that the colors in (a) map on to a limited range of (b), acknowledging that differing combinations of seasonal responses (e.g. more negative or positive spring and summer responses) will map on to lower or higher magnitudes of drought sensitivity.

I tested this hypothesis across the Northern Great Plains of North America, one of the largest remaining intact grassland regions on Earth. As a result, this region is climatically diverse, spanning large temperature (2.5–10.4°C) and precipitation (240–700 mm) gradients, while also containing a high degree of plant functional diversity, being comprised primarily of mixed‐grass prairies. Analysis of spring (March–May) vs summer (June–August) vegetation productivity anomalies (% change from mean) during the driest (lowest annual precipitation) years of the MODIS record (2000–2023) suggests that spring productivity is frequently – though not consistently – increased during extreme drought years across the region (Fig. 4a; spring). By contrast, summer productivity is consistently decreased (Fig. 4a; summer), resulting in a higher spatial variability in spring (mean = 2.5%, SD = 29.2%) vs summer (mean = −38.9%, SD = 17.5%) productivity responses to drought (Fig. 4a). It is this spatial variability in spring responses that the compensation hypothesis posits could help explain spatial variability in annualized drought sensitivity. Indeed, in 50% of locations, spring productivity was increased while summer productivity was decreased during extreme drought years, implying compensation is a common dynamic. In fact, of the possible combinations of spring vs summer productivity responses to drought, this spring compensation dynamic was the most common (Fig. 4b inset). Moreover, 23 drought years were represented across the analysis (no one dry year dominated the analysis; Fig. S2) and a small fraction (7.7%) of those events were preceded by a comparably (within 10% of the magnitude of precipitation reduction) dry year, consistent with the noted rarity of multi‐year extreme droughts (Felton et al., 2021a; Ohlert et al., 2025).

As a first step in testing the spring compensation hypothesis, I focused on those 50% locations that experienced both spring productivity increases and summer productivity decreases during drought. I quantified spring compensation as a unitless ratio that captures the degree to which those spring increases in productivity offset later summer losses and assessed the association with patterns of drought sensitivity, defined as the change in annual net primary productivity (g C m−2) mm−1 reduction in annual precipitation during drought. When analyzed across all 23 drought years, locations with higher spring compensation of summer productivity losses during drought were associated with lower drought sensitivity of annual net primary productivity (Spearman's ρ = −0.74; Fig. 4b). Associations between annual drought sensitivity with mean annual precipitation (ρ = 0.15) or temperature (ρ = 0.33) were weaker and implied higher drought sensitivity at warmer and wetter sites across this ecoregion (Fig. S3). When analyzed within each of the 23 drought years separately (Fig. S2), the average association between annual drought sensitivity with spring compensation remained strikingly consistent (median ρ = −0.71). By contrast, associations shifted to weakly negative for mean annual precipitation (median ρ = −0.28) and disappeared with temperature (median ρ = −0.012; Fig. S4). Consequently, mean annual precipitation – a commonly used proxy for water availability (Huxman et al., 2004; Maurer et al., 2020; Feldman et al., 2026) – was inconsistently associated with drought sensitivity and spring compensation of summer productivity losses (Fig. 4), while spring compensation was more consistently associated with spatial patterns of drought sensitivity.

These patterns suggest that, at least within the specific cases analyzed, spatial variation in drought sensitivity was more closely and consistently associated with spatial variation in phenological dynamics than with spatial variation in climate. This further implies that spatially varying factors other than climate, notably the potential role of cross‐site differences in biotic attributes such as functional diversity (e.g. Fig. 1b,c), may also underpin these spatial patterns and connections between phenology and drought sensitivity (Fig. 4). While not an exhaustive analysis, the resulting patterns indicate partial support for the compensation hypothesis and thus a role for vegetation phenology dynamics in shaping annual patterns of ecosystem sensitivity to drought.

Final comments

I have presented the case for more deeply considering phenological responses to drought. I have specifically shown that the seasonality of vegetation functioning is often altered during drought years, enhancing productivity in some seasons while decreasing it in others. These phenological alternations frequently generate intra‐annual and seasonal compensatory ecosystem dynamics, rendering annual productivity apparently insensitive to drought years in some ecosystems. Variation in these phenological dynamics, I hypothesize and have empirically demonstrated, can provide a mechanistic explanation for observed spatial variation in ecosystem sensitivity to drought.

Ideas not explored in this Viewpoint are best viewed as important avenues for future research on the more general relationship between climate extremes and vegetation phenology. Relevant aspects not fully considered here include autumn phenology (Gallinat et al., 2015), which too is expected to be responsive to climate variability and change (Hufkens et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2020; Teets et al., 2023) and may play into compensatory dynamics during climatically extreme years. Other factors may influence and explain variability in phenological responses to extremes in ways that spatial variation in background climate cannot. Carryover or legacy effects of prior conditions, whether at an interannual or intra‐annual timescale, may factor in as constraints to productivity and the timing and duration of key phenological events (Meng et al., 2024; Goodman & Felton, 2025).

Consideration of nonvegetative phenophases, such as flowering and seed dispersal, may carry further relevance and add critical context given their noted differential responses to climatic variation than vegetative (e.g. green up) phenophases (Collins et al., 2021), and the potential dependence of ecosystem‐scale processes (e.g. productivity and nutrient cycling) on these phases (e.g. pollination) in certain ecosystems (Beard et al., 2019). Other community‐scale dynamics such herbivory may also modify phenological dynamics and responses to extremes by resetting growth stages of vegetation (Geremia et al., 2019). The hypothesis as presented in Fig. 3 likely best applies to biomes that span large spatiotemporal gradients of water limitation with high seasonality, such as grasslands, which I conveniently used as an illustrative example here. But this does not preclude differing combinations of seasonal responses in other systems such as forests, as suggested by Fig. 1(a), from generating spatial variation in drought sensitivity. I have also conspicuously emphasized seasonally temperate ecosystems and drought as tractable examples of a broader view that phenology can play an influential role in shaping ecosystem responses to climate extremes. Yet this does not negate the importance of phenology in other ecosystem types with differing seasonality, including tropical and Mediterranean systems, along with other and more often short‐term climate extremes, including heavy precipitation and heatwaves.

These research avenues can be supported by a variety of existing approaches in our collective research toolkit. While experimental manipulations of climate extremes, and especially drought, in the field setting remain a mainstay (Smith et al., 2024), integrating low‐cost phenological sensors, such as raspberry pies (Jolles, 2021), into experiments (Richardson et al., 2018b) can add value via high temporal resolution measures of vegetation phenology beyond annual productivity responses. Hyperspectral sensors can move the field beyond greenness – which has been emphasized here – to something closer to functional diversity, including community‐, species‐, and leaf‐level dynamics (Schweiger et al., 2018). Combining experiments with these types of observations across scales with proximal and remote sensing and flux‐tower‐based approaches (Pierrat et al., 2025) along with citizen science data (Mora et al., 2024) can provide a well‐rounded empirical understanding of such phenological dynamics. Harmonizing these data and insights can aid in constraining and improving process‐representation of vegetation phenology under drought in models that couple the land and atmosphere, a clear research need given that while we empirically observe that spring photosynthesis is frequently increased during drought (Fig. 4a), model ensembles consistently simulate reductions in photosynthesis, even in wetter, energy‐limited ecosystems such as forests (Le Quéré et al., 2018; Miller et al., 2023). Shifts in vegetation phenology and drought intensification are two of the clearest signatures of contemporary climate change. When considered together, I argue that new insights can be gained regarding ecosystem vulnerability to climate change and that we already have the tools to gain such insight.
