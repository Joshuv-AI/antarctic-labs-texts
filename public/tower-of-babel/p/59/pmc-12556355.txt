Bio-inspired helical pantograph arms for aerodynamic noise suppression in high-speed trains
Authors: Fangying Lou, Xinbiao Xiao, Zongfa Zhang, Jie Zhang, Xinyi Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe pantograph is a major aerodynamic-noise source on high-speed trains, where vortices around pantograph arms and the panhead dominate sound generation. Inspired by seal whiskers and butterfly antennae, this study proposes two bio-inspired helical arm designs—twisted and coiled—implemented on the pantograph. Numerical simulations employ improved delayed detached Eddy simulation (IDDES), the Ffowcs Williams-Hawkings (FW-H) acoustic analogy, and the acoustic perturbation equation (APE) to assess three pantograph configurations: cylindrical, twisted, and coiled. The twisted configuration yields the largest gains, reducing root-mean-square lift fluctuations by 68.9% and lowering mean drag (the coiled design also reduces drag), thereby improving stability and flow quality. Acoustically, the twisted pantograph cuts maximum far-field sound pressure by 4.2 dBA and suppresses near-field noise near the panhead by up to 5.4 dBA, with effectiveness superior to that of the coiled design. These results indicate that biomimetic arm shaping can produce quieter, more efficient pantograph systems.

Introduction

The primary sources of aerodynamic noise generated by high-speed trains include the pantograph, bogie systems, and windshield areas.1 As high-speed train designs increasingly adopt lightweight structures and operational velocities continue to rise, aerodynamic noise has emerged as a critical concern, significantly impacting passenger comfort and environmental noise pollution. While noise originating from bogies and windshields can be mitigated effectively through acoustic barriers and surface coatings, the pantograph presents a greater challenge. Positioned atop the train with an exposed structural configuration, conventional noise suppression methods are often impractical for pantograph-related noise. Consequently, the pantograph is widely recognized as a dominant contributor to aerodynamic noise in high-speed rail systems.2

The pantograph serves as a critical component for current collection atop high-speed trains, featuring a sophisticated structural configuration that exerts a substantial influence on the aerodynamic behavior of the train. At elevated speeds, the airflow surrounding the pantograph exhibits instability, resulting in intricate flow separation and vortex formation along its surface. This phenomenon induces a highly fluctuating pressure field, subsequently converting into aerodynamic noise.3 Consequently, mitigating pantograph-generated noise has emerged as a crucial challenge in advancing high-speed rail technology, prompting extensive experimental and computational investigations by numerous researchers.4,5,6,7 Concerning the aerodynamic noise characteristics and underlying mechanisms in the pantograph region, Zhang et al. explored the generation and propagation dynamics of noise emanating from pantographs. Their study revealed that the panhead, base, and articulation joints constitute the dominant noise sources, with aerodynamic noise propagation adhering to a dipole pattern.4 Siano et al. applied scale-adaptive simulation combined with acoustic boundary element methods to compute pantograph-induced aerodynamic noise at 300 km/h, identifying vortex shedding as the principal mechanism driving noise generation.6 Meanwhile, Lauterbach et al. performed aerodynamic noise experiments on a 1:25 scaled-down model of an intercity high-speed train within an aeroacoustic wind tunnel. Utilizing a 143-microphone array for sound source localization, their findings underscored the pantograph as a dominant noise contributor. Additionally, the study demonstrated that pantograph-generated noise exhibits pronounced tonal characteristics under wind excitation, with far-field radiation noise displaying dependencies on Mach number and Strouhal number (St) similarity.7

Concerning the acoustic emission characteristics of pantographs, Tan et al. conducted numerical simulations to analyze the flow dynamics and far-field noise radiation, with a focus on vortex formation mechanisms, aerodynamic behavior of key structural elements, and their interrelationships. Their findings revealed that the near-field flow was predominantly governed by vortex detachment from rod-shaped elements and wake instability.8 In a separate investigation, Li et al. examined the aerodynamic noise generation of a pantograph operating at 300 km/h, identifying the panhead as a major contributor to far-field noise emissions. Through dimensionality reduction and coherence analysis, they also mapped the flow structures associated with the dominant frequency peaks in the acoustic spectra.9 Further studies by Sun et al. revealed that high-speed flow conditions triggered unsteady flow separation and vortex shedding around critical pantograph components (e.g., panhead and balance arm), generating complex turbulent structures. The dynamic evolution and mutual interference of these vortices produced substantial flow perturbations, constituting the primary mechanism of aeroacoustic noise generation.10 Extensive research has established that pantograph noise sources are spatially concentrated in several regions, including the base frame, insulating elements, balance beam, and articulated arm assemblies. Under high-speed airflow, these components experience periodic vortex alternation, inducing significant surface pressure fluctuations that radiate intense noise both within and outside the rail vehicle. Consequently, optimizing the aerodynamic environment around pantograph subsystems represents a critical strategy for noise mitigation.

Contemporary approaches to reducing aerodynamic noise generated by pantographs are predominantly classified into two groups: active and passive control techniques. Active flow manipulation strategies, such as dielectric barrier discharge actuators, sound wave interference systems, miniature fluidic injectors, and turbulence-inducing elements, have shown significant potential in suppressing noise emissions. Nevertheless, these approaches typically require complex electromechanical arrangements, making passive control measures a more practical and cost-effective solution owing to their energy-independent nature.11,12,13,14,15 Consequently, passive noise mitigation techniques have garnered increased attention in recent scholarly investigations.

Several studies have explored structural simplification of pantographs, particularly by reducing the number of rods, as a means of lowering aerodynamic noise.16 Others have focused on modifying the cross-sectional profiles of pantograph rods to achieve noise suppression. For example, Zheng et al. conducted experimental research on cylinder noise reduction by analyzing flow-field acoustic sources. Their wind tunnel experiments, utilizing time-resolved particle image velocimetry, revealed that dimpled surfaces disrupt vortex generation, delay shedding, and substantially diminish both noise and drag, highlighting the influence of surface morphology on noise mitigation.17 Yu et al. examined the application of a roof-mounted pantograph fairing to reduce aerodynamic noise. Their findings demonstrated that a dual-panel fairing configuration, acting as an acoustic barrier, could achieve a noise reduction of approximately 3 dB.18 In another study, Guo et al. employed the large Eddy simulation (LES) and Ffowcs Williams-Hawkings (FW-H) acoustic analogy to evaluate noise reduction techniques such as perforations, slots, and aerodynamic shaping of pantograph rods. Their results indicated that these modifications effectively weaken vortex strength and acoustic source intensity, with perforated designs and aerodynamic optimization reducing noise levels by 1.1 and 1.2 dBA, respectively, offering viable engineering solutions for high-speed train noise control.19 Additionally, Saito et al. introduced a pantograph head design featuring segmented smooth contours integrated with low-noise supports to enhance aerodynamic performance. Numerical simulations and experimental tests confirmed that this optimized configuration significantly reduces noise by minimizing flow separation and turbulent effects. The study further demonstrated that this design effectively mitigates noise pollution, particularly in tunnel environments, contributing to quieter high-speed rail operations.20 AbdelGawad et al. conducted a comprehensive investigation into the far- and near-field aerodynamic noise emissions of a full-scale pantograph under varying operational scenarios, employing the FW-H and acoustic perturbation equation (APE). Through an analysis of the acoustic pressure and airflow velocity profiles across pantograph components, they developed a structural optimization strategy for the pantograph rods to mitigate noise generation. Their findings revealed that elliptical cross-sectional rod designs significantly attenuate aerodynamic noise.21 In a separate study, Nishimura et al. explored the application of pile fabrics on cylindrical rod surfaces, with wind tunnel experiments confirming their efficacy in noise suppression. Nevertheless, this method introduced increased aerodynamic drag and presented practical limitations in terms of cleanliness and long-term maintenance.22 Qin et al. examined the hydrodynamic and acoustic behaviors of four distinct cavity geometries, including rectangular, rounded-edge, and two variants with asymmetric and symmetric connecting conduits. Their research demonstrated that all three modified cavity designs enhanced aerodynamic efficiency. Positioning the pantograph within these optimized cavities minimized disturbances on lower-region components and suppressed high-intensity vortex formation, thereby refining wake dynamics and rear-surface flow separation. Consequently, the pantograph’s drag coefficient and system-wide resistance decreased by 3.82% and 3.25%, alongside noise reductions of 1.4 and 1.9 dBA, respectively.23

The field of biomimetics has facilitated innovative strategies for minimizing aerodynamic drag and acoustic emissions in mechanical systems. Natural biological structures and adaptive behaviors observed in plants and animals serve as a critical foundation for engineering solutions. In recent years, bio-inspired concepts have been increasingly adopted in engineering design by researchers. For example, Han and Kim performed a comprehensive investigation into aerodynamic noise generation within inter-carriage gaps of high-speed trains, introducing a bio-inspired noise suppression mechanism. By emulating the microstructural attributes of owl plumage, the team engineered a biomimetic interface to enhance airflow dynamics between train compartments. Through computational analyses employing steady-state Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) and LES techniques, the study evaluated the influence of geometric parameters—including feather density and configuration—on turbulence patterns, vorticity, and acoustic output. The optimized bio-inspired interface demonstrated a notable noise attenuation of 3–5 dB relative to conventional designs, accompanied by improvements in both drag coefficients and noise spectra across operational speed ranges.24 In a related study, Gai et al. leveraged an IDDES framework to assess the aerodynamic efficacy of a pantograph fairing modeled after the exoskeletal morphology of beetles. The bio-inspired fairing yielded substantial drag reduction, achieving decreases of 83.88% and 35.29% in retracted and extended pantograph configurations, with overall drag reductions of 10.76% and 4.49%, respectively. Furthermore, the design optimized flow distribution around the pantograph assembly, attenuating turbulence-induced disturbances and flow separation effects.25 These findings highlight the efficacy of biologically derived designs in refining the aerodynamic performance of high-speed rail systems. Expanding on these principles, Foo et al. integrated biomimetic concepts by examining the hydrodynamic and aerodynamic adaptations of species such as kingfishers, falcons, sailfish, and dolphins to refine train nose geometries. Special emphasis was placed on the kingfisher’s beak profile, which was found to substantially diminish drag forces while improving overall aerodynamic efficiency. The study underscores the transformative potential of nature-inspired design in advancing high-speed rail aerodynamics.26

In recent years, research on aerodynamic noise control of pantographs has primarily focused on measures such as fairing optimization (Liu et al.,5 Yao et al.,27 and Zhang et al.28), sound barrier placement (Zhang et al.29 and Lu et al.30), and the use of porous materials (Sueki et al.31 and Ikeda et al.32). While fairings and sound barriers can reduce noise by guiding airflow and suppressing vortices, they often involve complex structures, high costs, and significant spatial requirements, and they may increase aerodynamic drag, thereby degrading aerodynamic performance. Under high-speed operating conditions, these additional structures are also susceptible to flow-field instabilities, and the exposed noise sources on the roof-mounted pantograph remain difficult to shield effectively. Porous materials offer certain noise reduction potential; however, limitations in durability, aerodynamic performance, and structural strength restrict their effectiveness at high speeds. Some biomimetic strategies, such as surface texturing, biomimetic fairings, and biomimetic streamlined designs, have achieved notable progress in noise mitigation, yet they still face constraints in controlling low-frequency noise and lack the ability to actively modulate the flow field.25,33,34 In this study, we propose an innovative approach that optimizes the pantograph arm geometry by drawing inspiration from the coiled antennae of butterflies and the twisted whiskers of seals, replacing conventional cylindrical arms with biomimetic coiled and twisted arms. By altering the three-dimensional arm structure, this design directly modifies flow separation and vortex-shedding modes, thereby fundamentally reshaping vortex structures and noise-source distribution. It avoids the potential instability of microscopic biomimetic textures at high flow speeds, overcomes the limited control of low-frequency vortex-induced noise in streamlined designs, and eliminates the additional aerodynamic drag and structural complexity associated with fairings.

Beyond aerodynamic and acoustic benefits, the biomimetic arm design demonstrates high manufacturability and structural integrity through modern fabrication techniques and appropriate material selection. Advanced manufacturing methods such as 3D printing and computerized numerical control machining enable precise realization of the twisted and coiled geometries, while the use of high-strength alloy materials ensures stability and mechanical robustness. The unique structural features of the bio-inspired arm can enhance its bending resistance and overall stiffness, thereby improving its stability under high-speed aerodynamic loads, with particularly remarkable load-bearing capacity during high-speed operation. Compared with the fabrication of complex fairing and noise barrier structures, the bio-inspired arm offers a significant cost advantage. In addition, the design exhibits a high degree of adaptability, being not only applicable to pantograph systems of high-speed trains but also extendable to other aerodynamics-related fields, such as aerospace and marine engineering, thereby demonstrating broad potential for engineering applications. It remains compatible with existing technologies, is easy to integrate, and can be readily optimized. These advantages highlight its strong potential for practical engineering use and large-scale deployment.

A review of the literature reveals that, although extensive studies have addressed the aerodynamic noise of high-speed train pantographs, most have focused on far-field noise while neglecting an integrated assessment of both near- and far-field acoustic behavior. In addition, the application of widely studied passive control and bio-inspired strategies, such as fairings, acoustic barriers, and bio-inspired surface textures, remains limited in pantographs and requires further improvement. To elucidate the underlying mechanisms of turbulence-induced noise in the pantograph region and to evaluate the effectiveness of the biomimetic arm designs proposed in this study, transient flow simulations were performed using the IDDES approach, coupled with the FW-H equation and the APE for sound propagation analysis. A 1:8 scale pantograph model was subjected to a comprehensive aerodynamic and aeroacoustic evaluation, comparing the flow dynamics, vortex structures, and noise distributions in both the near and far fields for three pantographs equipped with different bio-inspired arms. The findings provide valuable insights for the development of efficient and low-noise pantograph systems.

Methodology

Numerical calculation method

To investigate both near-field and far-field aeroacoustic characteristics of high-speed train pantograph systems, this study adopts a hybrid computational approach that integrates CFD-based flow simulation with the FW-H acoustic analogy and the APE method for aeroacoustic prediction. This strategy offers a reasonable compromise between computational efficiency and accuracy, enabling detailed resolution of complex unsteady flow structures while effectively capturing the key aeroacoustic source mechanisms. The finite volume method combined with a segregated solver is employed to discretize the flow field around the high-speed train pantograph under aerodynamic impact and to solve the governing equations. Considering the operating speed of 350 km/h, the widely used shear stress transport (SST) k-ω-based IDDES turbulence model is adopted.35 In this model, the dissipation rate (ω) equation of the SST k-ω formulation remains unchanged, while the turbulence length-scale function from the DDES approach is introduced into the dissipation term of the turbulent kinetic energy (k) equation. Geometry models and meshing strategy

Throughout evolutionary history, diverse organisms—spanning flora and fauna—have acquired specialized morphological and functional adaptations to thrive in their respective habitats. These biologically optimized traits offer valuable insights for advancing engineering design principles. Illustrative examples include intertidal mollusks, whose sculpted shell geometries and surface micro-patterns mitigate hydrodynamic stresses from cyclic wave exposure. Similarly, the wing morphology of Asio otus (long-eared owl) exhibits serrated leading-edge features and fringed remiges that attenuate aerodynamic noise during prey pursuit. Further biomimetic exemplars comprise the undulating geometry of pinniped vibrissae, which disrupts vortex-induced oscillations in fluid flow, and the helical configuration of lepidopteran antennae, whose streamlined morphology minimizes flow separation while augmenting aerodynamic stability. The pantograph comprises multiple slender rods with intricate geometrical details, making full-scale CFD simulations prohibitively mesh intensive and computationally demanding. Wang et al. demonstrated that the 1:8 scale model reproduces the aerodynamic noise characteristics of the full scale within acceptable deviations, thus proving suitable for both wind tunnel experiments and numerical simulations.43 And the 1:8 scale has become a common choice in pantograph aeroacoustic research (e.g., Qin et al.,23 Shi et al.,35 Tan et al.,44 Li et al.,45 and Sun et al.46). Moreover, comparative studies by Li et al.45 at the 1:8 scale and Xu et al.47 at full scale (1:1) confirmed that pantograph arm optimization can effectively suppress noise, reinforcing not only the feasibility of such optimization strategies but also the validity of conducting investigations at the 1:8 scale. Building on these established practices, the present study also adopts the 1:8 scale to balance accuracy and efficiency while ensuring comparability with prior works. This study aims to comprehensively analyze the impact of pantograph component optimization on the aerodynamic characteristics of the pantograph. Therefore, a rectangular computational domain is constructed around the pantograph body, with its coordinate origin aligned to the geometric center of the pantograph structure. To balance computational cost and minimize boundary-induced interference on the local flow and acoustic fields, the computational domain is defined as L × W × H = 3.4 × 1.7 × 1.1 m, as shown in Figure 3. Based on a comprehensive review of the literature, the computational domain size and pantograph placement adopted in this study not only fall within the empirical ranges established in previous research but also meet the requirements for analyzing the local flow characteristics and near-field noise of the pantograph. It should be noted that, for simplification of the numerical simulation, the train roof is not included in this study. In reality, when the pantograph insulators (with a height of approximately 330 mm) are mounted on the roof, they are almost entirely located within the shear-turbulent region of the boundary layer, as inferred from the relationship between the plate boundary-layer thickness δ and the local Reynolds number (Re): δ = 4.64xRex−1/2, whereas the arms and frame system are outside this region (Li et al.48). Dai et al.49 pointed out that variations in the train body geometry near the pantograph have a relatively minor impact on the flow field above the pantograph, while accounting for these minor changes in computations often significantly increases the mesh count and reduces computational efficiency. Numerous studies have demonstrated that placing the pantograph directly on the ground for aeroacoustic simulations has become a common practice (e.g., Zhang et al.,4 Tan et al.,8 Wang et al.,50 and Shi et al.51). This configuration ensures physical rationality, achieves a sound balance between computational cost and accuracy, and facilitates the investigation of local flow structures around the pantograph. Based on this, the present study neglects the effect of the train roof and employs a ground-mounted pantograph model in order to focus on the key aerodynamic characteristics of the upper pantograph components. It should be noted, however, that, although this approach effectively captures the influence of arm geometry on near-field flow and noise, the results cannot fully replicate the actual conditions of roof-mounted installation. Future work could conduct systematic comparative studies between ground- and roof-mounted configurations and further examine the impact of train body geometry on the aerodynamic and acoustic behavior of the pantograph. Such efforts would contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of pantograph aeroacoustics under realistic operating conditions and provide more targeted guidance for design optimization.

The computational domain was configured with the following boundary conditions: the inflow boundary is set as the velocity inlet condition with 350 km/h. The outflow boundary is set as the pressure outlet condition with a 0 Pa gauge pressure. The right side, left side, and top side are set as symmetry boundaries. The surface of the pantograph is set as a no-slip-wall boundary condition. The ground is set as a slip wall with a speed equal to the running speed of the pantograph. The no-slip wall can faithfully reproduce the ground boundary layer and its influence on aerodynamic loads, which is of significant value for full-train aerodynamic studies or fairing evaluations. However, it also introduces boundary-layer effects and ground reflections, thereby increasing computational complexity and cost. In contrast, the slip wall approximates a frictionless free stream, effectively avoiding ground-induced noise and reflections while eliminating wall friction effects to improve numerical stability. Its limitation lies in neglecting the realistic boundary layer, which may reduce engineering accuracy. Given that the present study focuses on the geometric optimization of pantograph arms and the associated near-field flow disturbances and acoustic characteristics, adopting a slip-wall condition is more suitable for eliminating extraneous interferences, thereby allowing a clearer examination of the effects of arm geometry on local flow and noise. This setup has been demonstrated to be well suited for numerical simulations of independent high-speed train pantographs, as also adopted in the studies of Zhang et al.4 and Shi.49 Future studies may conduct a systematic aerodynamic comparison of the pantograph under slip and no-slip ground conditions to gain deeper insights into the flow differences between the two wall conditions and their impact on aerodynamic characteristics and noise-generation mechanisms. This would further improve the understanding of pantograph aerodynamic behavior in realistic operating environments.

This study selects 350 km/h as the computational condition, representing the typical operating speed of high-speed trains in China. This velocity not only effectively reflects the behavior of the pantograph under strongly unsteady aerodynamic conditions but also aligns with the velocity conditions used in numerous related studies both domestically and internationally. However, aerodynamic flow and acoustic radiation are highly sensitive to the incoming flow velocity, particularly at high Reynolds numbers, where velocity variations can induce nonlinear changes in vortex shedding frequency, flow-acoustic coupling, and far-field radiation efficiency. Therefore, although this study reveals the core physical mechanisms under the selected condition at 350 km/h, its applicability remains limited to a single speed. Future work can extend the analysis to a broader range of speeds to systematically evaluate the flow and acoustic characteristics under different operating conditions, thereby yielding more general and engineering-relevant conclusions.

The computational domain was discretized utilizing the native mesh generation tools integrated within the commercial CFD software STAR-CCM+. A trimmed hexahedral mesh scheme was employed throughout the domain to maintain uniformity in spatial discretization, thereby minimizing potential numerical artifacts arising from mesh inconsistencies across different simulation cases. To enhance resolution accuracy, particularly in capturing vortex dynamics and shear layer phenomena, a prismatic boundary layer mesh was implemented on all model surfaces. The first-layer thickness was carefully calibrated, expanding outward at a controlled growth ratio of 1.1 in the wall-normal direction. This configuration ensures that the dimensionless wall distance (y+) remains below unity (y+ ≤ 1), satisfying the precision criteria of the turbulence model while adequately resolving viscous sublayer effects at low Reynolds numbers. Wake turbulence resolution was further enhanced through multi-level volumetric refinements. Three nested refinement blocks (block 1: 6 mm; block 2: 12 mm; block 3: 24 mm) were deployed concentrically around the pantograph to balance the computational efficiency and flow feature resolution. Figures 4A and 4B respectively show the layout of mesh refinement region and the meshing details applied to the CYL, which aligns with the meshing protocols adopted for other pantograph variants in this study. Numerical setup

In this study, the flow around the pantograph model occurs at low Mach numbers, allowing the airflow to be treated as incompressible. The primary mechanism of flow-induced noise is associated with dipole acoustic sources generated by surface pressure fluctuations on the geometrical boundaries of the pantograph. Numerical simulations are performed using the incompressible unstructured finite volume solver provided by STAR-CCM+, with the flow field governed by the unsteady incompressible Navier-Stokes equations. A second-order implicit scheme is employed for temporal discretization, while spatial discretization is achieved through a central finite difference scheme. For each case, steady-state flow solutions are first obtained within the RANS framework using the SST k-ω turbulence model, with 4,000 iterations performed to ensure numerical convergence. Transient simulations are subsequently carried out using the IDDES approach. A time step of 1 × 10−5 s, together with eight inner iterations per step, is adopted to adequately resolve turbulent and acoustic fluctuations while maintaining a Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy number below unity throughout the domain. After 0.1 s of transient simulation, the flow reaches statistically stationary conditions. To ensure sufficient spectral resolution, an additional 0.4 s of simulation is conducted for acoustic data acquisition, resulting in a total transient simulation time of 0.5 s.

Near-field noise propagates through the train structure, thereby affecting the interior noise level and passenger comfort, whereas far-field noise primarily impacts the external acoustic environment. Accordingly, this study conducts a systematic and comprehensive analysis of both near-field and far-field sound radiation generated by the pantograph system. To estimate far-field noise radiation, the FW-H acoustic analogy is employed, accounting for source motion and solid boundary effects. Near-field noise must be calculated using additional acoustic equations, such as the APE. Because the APE is solved in the time domain, they can account for acoustic effects, including the Doppler shift, wall reflections, and medium refraction, enabling accurate computation of sound pressure. The APE has been applied to problems such as shear flows, rotating vortices, and cylinder wakes. During the simulations, the IDDES equations and the APE are solved throughout the computational domain. When the source weight is set to 1 and the acoustic propagation damping to 0, the APE computes the sound generation in that region; conversely, when the source weight is set to 0 and the propagation damping to 1, the APE performs acoustic propagation calculations in the region. A transition interface exists between the sound source and propagation regions; to prevent signal discontinuities, this interface is assigned a finite thickness, and windowing is applied. The red region shown in Figure 5 is defined as the acoustic perturbation zone.Figure 5Acoustic perturbation region

Acoustic perturbation region

The transition layer not only serves as a buffer zone where the vortex structures that excite the acoustic sources gradually evolve into propagating sound waves but also influences wave attenuation, phase changes, and wavefront transformation, thus acting as a bridge in the overall sound field formation. In the APE near-field acoustic computation, to avoid numerical reflections and high-wavenumber scattering caused by directly truncating the source term between the source and propagation regions, we introduced a smooth transition layer with a thickness of 80 mm. A half-Hanning window function, w(s) = 1/2[1+cos(πs)] (where s 0 ∈ [0,1] is the normalized spatial coordinate), was applied to gradually attenuate the source term from 1 to 0 within the transition layer. This function is continuous in both value and first derivative at the endpoints, effectively suppressing interface reflections. The layer thickness was chosen based on the maximum analyzed frequency fmax, corresponding to 2–4 times the shortest wavelength λmin = c0/fmax, and the layer contains a sufficient number of mesh points (typically no fewer than 10–20) to ensure smooth attenuation so that the sound field in the propagation region contains only physically radiated waves rather than numerical artifacts.

During the steady-state computation, monitors of pressure and velocity components are employed to sample the spatially averaged pressure and velocity, providing fundamental data for the subsequent calculation of APEs. In the unsteady simulation, the acoustic model is activated, and the averaged pressure and velocity fields from the steady computation are imported as the base flow conditions. Noise-source filtering zones and acoustic damping zones are then defined to delineate the relevant fluid regions, enabling the solution of the APEs.

Mesh independence validation

To assess the mesh independence of the current pantograph model, two additional mesh configurations were generated based on the baseline mesh used in the CYL analysis, maintaining identical meshing methodologies but varying mesh densities. The three mesh strategies are designated as the baseline mesh (BM), refined mesh (RM), and sparse mesh (SM). All mesh configurations preserve a consistent first-layer boundary thickness of 0.1 mm to adequately resolve near-wall gradients. The main differences between the three mesh strategies lie in the boundary layer thickness and total number of meshes. The mesh growth rate, defined as the ratio of thickness between adjacent layers within the boundary layer, is strictly controlled in this study to ensure a smooth expansion from the wall surface. This value is typically set between 1.1 and 1.2. To minimize numerical errors and avoid abrupt transitions that may cause spurious reflections or flow artifacts, the boundary layer growth rate is fixed at 1.1 in all three mesh configurations. The element distribution strategy employs finer elements in regions with high velocity gradients or complex geometries, such as pantograph arms and contact strips, while coarser elements are used in regions of relatively uniform flow. The chosen mesh parameters provide a balanced compromise between computational efficiency and accuracy, ensuring robust resolution of near-wall flow characteristics and reliable prediction of far-field acoustic sources. Numerical method validation

In flow and acoustic field studies, although a variety of computational methods are available, the reliability of the results fundamentally depends on the predictive capability of the computational model. Therefore, validation is indispensable prior to application, typically achieved by comparison with experimental measurements or high-fidelity reference simulations, thereby ensuring the accuracy and rigor of subsequent analyses. Given the geometric resemblance between pantograph components and cylindrical structures, the cylinder model is adopted for validation purposes. The far-field acoustic behavior of the cylinder in flow is examined and cross-referenced with experimental findings from Li et al.52 and numerical simulation data from Xu et al.47 Although numerical simulations cannot completely replace experimental studies due to inherent uncertainties and simplified assumptions, their application effectively reduces experimental workload and, in many cases, provides comparable predictive capability. These two methodologies mutually reinforce and validate each other, facilitating progressive refinement toward more accurate and realistic predictions.

The experiment described in the study of Li et al.52 was conducted in the D5 aeroacoustic wind tunnel laboratory at Beihang University. The tested cylinder had a diameter of 0.2 m, with a uniform inflow velocity ranging from 30 to 80 m/s at the test section inlet. Six operating conditions are set with velocity as the variable, using a gradient of 10 m/s. The air temperature was 25°C, the density was 1.185 kg/m3, and the kinematic viscosity coefficient v was 2.17 × 10−5 m2/s, corresponding to Reynolds numbers of 4.1×104–1.1×105. The computational domain of the cylinder is shown in Figure 7. It has a length of 17.5 d, a width of 2 d, and a height of 10 d, where d represents the cylinder diameter. The Q-criterion results clearly indicate that CYL exhibits a typical spanwise-synchronized vortex-shedding pattern, with coherent vortical structures consistently aligned along the span. Its vorticity field reveals a broad region of high vorticity (Figure 9A), highlighting the presence of an organized and intense vortex street. This regular and strong shedding mode tends to concentrate acoustic energy within specific frequency ranges. In CYL, the cylindrical arms generate a strong and organized separated shear layer at V-R1, leading to spanwise-synchronized shedding. As a result, a wide band of high vorticity emerges in V-W1 (Figure 9B), generating significant noise radiation. By contrast, TWI introduces disturbances in the spanwise direction, which weakens the coherence of vortex shedding and results in a more irregular spatial distribution. The corresponding vorticity field shows a marked reduction in high-vorticity regions within the wake, as spanwise energy transfer is disrupted (Figure 9C). The twisted arms of TWI induce axial and oblique secondary flows at V-R2, effectively breaking spanwise coherence and reducing the strength of individual vortices. Consequently, vortical structures near the arms are smaller and weaker than those observed at V-R1. Entering V-W2, the high-vorticity zone shrinks significantly; the lock-in mechanism is disrupted; and energy cascades earlier into smaller scales, decaying rapidly (Figure 9D). This process effectively suppresses low-frequency peaks and weakens near-far-field coupling. The COI, on the other hand, exhibits a multi-scale superposition and dissipation mode. The wake no longer features an organized vortex street but is dominated by small-scale, sparsely distributed vortices and turbulent fluctuations. The vorticity distribution in Figure 9E further confirms the reduction in high-vorticity regions, with energy progressively transferring into smaller turbulent scales. At V-R3, COI already displays multi-scale shear and local reattachment. In V-W3, the wake is governed by scattered small vortices and turbulence, with the high-vorticity band fragmented and dissipated (Figure 9F). Although this multi-scale disturbance and dissipation mechanism suppresses the formation and persistence of large-scale vortices, it introduces a certain degree of broadband enhancement. As a result, the overall noise reduction effect is generally inferior to that of TWI. In summary, weakening near-rod coherence and accelerating wake dissipation are the key pathways for noise reduction, with TWI achieving a more favorable balance between the two.

This effect arises from the three-dimensional flow separation induced by the twisted and coiled geometries, which weakens shear layer interactions and enhances wake stability. Consequently, lift fluctuations are suppressed, near-wall vortex oscillations are attenuated, and highly unstable flow structures are minimized. Considering the far- and near-field acoustic behavior, far-field noise is more sensitive to strongly coherent low- and mid-frequency sources with high radiation efficiency. The analysis of near-field sound pressure around the pantograph is crucial for a deeper understanding of the mechanisms of noise generation, as the near-field region directly reflects the characteristics of the sound sources and facilitates correlation with the flow field, forming the basis for revealing the essence of aerodynamic noise. As shown in Figure 15, the TWI outperforms the COI in suppressing near-field aerodynamic noise. Due to the influence of the base frame and insulators, the lower arms of all three pantographs exhibit generally higher sound pressure than the upper arms. The upper-arm sound pressure of TWI and COI is significantly lower than that of CYL, yet a local pressure increase appears in the midsection of the lower arms. This can be explained by the velocity distributions: the central region of the CYL’s lower arm shows markedly lower flow velocity, whereas the unique structures of the TWI and COI induce locally accelerated flow under the impact of fast-moving air, leading to enhanced sound radiation. TWI shows pronounced noise reduction at the panhead and its wake, while the COI exhibits increased sound pressure in these regions. Compared with the COI and CYL, TWI produces more relatively low-velocity zones at the head and in the wake, whereas the COI shows velocity increases near the panhead. The coiled-arm geometry alters the separation points and shear layer evolution, transforming the symmetric or quasi-periodic vortex street around cylindrical arms into more complex three-dimensional helical vortices. At certain locations, these 3D vortices generate local flow acceleration, strong shear, and upward airflow, amplifying turbulent pressure fluctuations. The near-field sound pressure increases beneath the panhead (e.g., near N17) in both TWI and COI are related to flow recirculation induced by the curved structures. In contrast, the COI achieves stronger noise reduction near N18, consistent with the velocity field showing flow deceleration in this region; a similar effect is observed at N16. By optimizing the flow field, TWI effectively suppresses near-field noise at the panhead, upper arms, knocks, and the near-field area at the rear of the pantograph, reducing wake and separation noise. While the COI’s noise reduction is mainly confined to the upper arms and lower portions of the lower arms, with limited effect at the panhead, upper regions of the lower arm, and the rear of the pantograph. This is due to the geometry of coiled arm systematically reshaping the local flow and sound source distribution. The coiled arms alter effective blockage and guidance of the flow, generating local recirculation zones and reattachment points, turning some near-field regions into zones of acoustic accumulation or “scattering-amplification.” For example, behind the knock, phase-correlated vortex-structure interactions in COI’s non-uniform wake enhance source superposition, as confirmed by vorticity analysis. Similarly, local curvature and accelerated flow in COI’s lower arms and panhead can trigger small-scale vortices or separation zones, manifesting as pronounced near-field sound pressure increases.

From the perspective of far-field noise characteristics, both TWI and COI can effectively reduce far-field noise, with TWI exhibiting a more pronounced noise reduction effect. Combined with the analysis of SPLs at near-field receivers, it is evident that TWI outperforms COI in both near- and far-field noise reduction. Although the coiled arms can suppress noise in certain frequency bands overall, their three-dimensional wake, local flow acceleration, recirculation effects, and phase superposition can lead to near-field sound pressure amplification at specific geometries and observation points. Although the absolute SPLs of sound receivers differ between the near and far fields, we found a good consistency in overall trends and noise reduction effects between them. Thus, the near-field analysis can be used to explain the evolution of far-field noise. Inspired by this, future designs could mitigate harmful phase superposition by slightly adjusting the coiling rhythm, locally thinning the structure, or introducing small-scale gaps or guide vanes. Alternatively, local damping, sound-absorbing materials, or thin streamlined attachments could be applied at these “hot spots” to reduce near-field sound pressure amplification. This analysis highlights the important role of structural optimization in controlling aerodynamic noise.

Conclusions

To meet the practical demands of engineering applications, two optimized pantograph configurations were proposed by introducing biomimetic modifications to the conventional pantograph with cylindrical arms. A comprehensive evaluation of their near-field and far-field aeroacoustic performance at 350 km/h was carried out using IDDES in conjunction with the FW-H acoustic analogy and APE. The findings not only deepen the understanding of flow-acoustic mechanisms in pantograph systems but also provide a useful reference for the engineering design of noise-reducing pantograph structures in high-speed trains. The principal conclusions are as follows.(1)Compared to the CYL, the TWI and COI show a reduction in the RMS of lift fluctuations by 68.9% and 24.7%, respectively. The geometric structures of the twisted and coiled arms induce three-dimensional fluid separation, which weakens the interaction between shear layers and suppresses aerodynamic force pulsations on the pantograph’s surface. Aerodynamically, the twisted and coiled arms contribute positively to enhancing the operational stability and flow quality of the pantograph.(2)Both the TWI and COI effectively guide the incoming flow. The twisted and coiled arms disrupt the development of wake vortices in the upper arm and lower arm regions of the pantograph. This disruption results in a more fragmented vortex structure, reduced vortex intensity and scale, and a decrease in vortex strength.(3)In the analysis of far-field noise characteristics, both the TWI and COI show a significant reduction in A-weighted overall SPLs at various sound radiation angles, compared to the CYL. The TWI exhibits superior far-field noise reduction performance compared to the COI. On the xoz, yoz, and xoy planes, the maximum noise reduction values achieved by the TWI are 4.2, 2.9, and 4.1 dBA, respectively. The twisted arms effectively suppress aerodynamic noise by introducing a longitudinal twisting structure, which induces three-dimensional disturbances, resulting in a notable noise reduction effect.(4)Regarding near-field noise characteristics, TWI and COI show significant effects in reducing the surface noise of the pantograph upper arm but lead to an increase in sound pressure on the lower arm surface to some extent. In particular, the TWI demonstrates excellent near-field noise reduction performance at the head, upper arm, and wake region of the pantograph, while COI has outstanding advantages in suppressing noise at the leading edge of the pantograph. Therefore, in practical engineering applications, the structural design of the pantograph should be reasonably optimized based on specific noise reduction needs to balance noise reduction effects with local acoustic performance.

Limitations of the study

The conclusions of this study are derived under a single operating speed of 350 km/h, and future work should extend the analysis to a wider range of speeds to systematically evaluate the flow and acoustic characteristics of optimized pantographs under different operating conditions. The geometric parameters of the pantograph arms investigated here cover only a limited design space. Future research could explore systematic variations in parameters such as amplitude, spanwise wavelength, and torsional angle to identify more optimal designs. Moreover, as this study primarily relies on numerical simulations, incorporating wind tunnel experiments or field measurements in future work would further enhance the reliability and engineering applicability of the conclusions.

Method details

Pantograph geometry modeling and biomimetic design

In this study, the pantograph geometries were constructed using SolidWorks 2021. First, a baseline pantograph model with cylindrical struts was established according to the actual geometrical dimensions and typical structural parameters, serving as a reference for subsequent comparisons. On this basis, two biomimetic modifications were introduced: (i) a pantograph model with twisted arms, in which a continuous spanwise twist was imposed on an elliptical column to generate three-dimensional flow-disturbing features analogous to seal whiskers; and (ii) a pantograph model with coiled arms, in which periodic helical ribs were superimposed on the cylindrical surface to form flow-disturbing features analogous to butterfly antennae, thereby inducing multi-scale vortex perturbations in the aerodynamic wake. During the modeling process, sequential operations including sketching, three-dimensional extrusion, rotation and arraying, surface editing, and helical sweep were performed, ensuring that all configurations maintained identical overall dimensions while exhibiting distinct geometric modifications. Finally, the three pantograph models were parameterized, scaled to 1:8 within the computational domain during modeling, and exported in STEP format for subsequent aerodynamic simulations and aeroacoustic analyses.

Numerical simulation setup and aeroacoustic methodology

In the numerical simulation, high-quality meshing and verification were first performed for the pantograph model and its computational domain. The domain was discretized using trimmed-cell grids: local refinement was applied around the pantograph body and its near-field region to ensure adequate boundary-layer resolution and accurate vortex capturing, while progressive coarsening was implemented in the inflow and wake regions to balance computational efficiency and solution stability. Additional fine refinement was imposed in critical regions where vortex shedding and dominant acoustic sources were expected, including the windward side of the arms, gaps, the panhead, and the near-wake, so that the mesh resolution met the requirements for both aerodynamic and acoustic analysis.

A two-step “steady–unsteady” simulation strategy was employed to achieve both convergence and efficiency. A steady-state solution was first obtained to provide a converged base flow as the initial condition for the unsteady simulation, thereby avoiding non-physical transients at the start of the time-dependent computation. Convergence criteria for the steady solution included sufficient residual reduction and stabilization of monitored aerodynamic quantities such as lift, drag, and pressure coefficients. The calculation then switched to unsteady simulation, in which high-order spatial discretization and strict convergence control were applied. The time step Δt was chosen based on acoustic bandwidth and CFL constraints, set to 1×10-5 s in this study, with eight inner iterations per step to ensure nonlinear convergence. The unsteady sampling period covered at least 0.5 s of physical time, corresponding to multiple characteristic vortex shedding cycles, to ensure reliable statistics and spectral analysis.

For the acoustic computations, the Acoustic Perturbation Equations method was applied in the near field, where the acoustic source region was explicitly defined by including both the near-wall surfaces of the pantograph and selected wake volumes; transient pressure and velocity histories were extracted from these regions. A damping layer was imposed at the outer boundary to minimize reflections contaminating the acoustic field. Far-field noise prediction was performed using the Ffowcs-Williams and Hawkings approach: time-domain signals were collected on impermeable or permeable control surfaces enclosing the model, and acoustic receivers were arranged in the far field for post-processing to obtain A-weighted overall sound pressure levels, narrowband spectra, and directivity patterns. Far-field receivers were evenly distributed at various polar angles to characterize noise directivity, while near-field probes were placed at representative positions near the pantograph surface to capture the temporal evolution of local acoustic sources.

Aeroacoustic data post-processing

During post-processing, a fast Fourier transform was applied to the time-domain pressure signals obtained from STAR-CCM+ to compute the narrowband spectra and the A-weighted overall sound pressure level at each sound receiver. The spectral data were then exported for subsequent plotting and quantitative analysis. Next, velocity, pressure, and vorticity field data were imported into Tecplot 360, where slicing, iso-surfaces, streamlines, and vortex-core identification were applied to visualize and analyze the three-dimensional flow structures and to reveal the underlying noise-generation mechanisms. Following this, Origin 2021 was used to produce high-quality plots such as frequency spectra, sound pressure level curves, and quantitative graphs (e.g., near-field sound pressure distribution maps), allowing the results to be presented in a more intuitive and professional manner. Finally, Microsoft PowerPoint 2021 was employed to arrange and refine all figures and field maps, including unifying fonts, adding annotations, and enhancing legends, to prepare publication-ready illustrations for the manuscript.
