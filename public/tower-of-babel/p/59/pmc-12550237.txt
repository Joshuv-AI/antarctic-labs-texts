Intracellular interaction between FcRY receptor and IgY in ovarian vascular endothelial cells during avian maternal IgY transfer
Authors: M. Okamoto, K. Furukawa, S. Mizushima, A. Murai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Maternal immunoglobulin Y (IgY) is transferred from the bloodstream to the yolk of maturing oocytes in avian species, which is crucial for the passive immunity of neonatal chicks. The receptor responsible for maternal blood IgY transfer to the yolk remains unidentified. Recently, we found that an IgY receptor, FcRY, is expressed in the vascular endothelial cells of ovarian follicles. However, there is no evidence of the direct binding of IgY to FcRY in vascular endothelial cells of avian ovarian follicles. To investigate how ovarian FcRY interacts with IgY, we performed a microscopic analysis of FcRY-IgY interactions in the primary endothelial cells isolated from quail ovarian follicles. Western blot and real-time PCR analyses showed that FcRY was expressed in the vascular endothelial cells. Microscopic analysis indicated that FcRY signals were distributed as intracellular vesicles around the center of vascular endothelial cells, suggesting that FcRY is localized to subcellular compartment of vascular endothelial cells. The percentage of QH1 (a quail endothelial marker)-positive cells was comparable to that of QH1/FcRY double-positive cells, showing that a considerable number of vascular endothelial cells express FcRY in avian ovaries. Proximity ligation assay (PLA) demonstrated that vascular endothelial FcRY binds to intracellular IgY. Importantly, the FcRY-IgY binding signal was inhibited by Pitstop 2, a selective inhibitor of clathrin-mediated endocytosis, and bafilomycin A1, a proton pump inhibitor, suggesting that FcRY-IgY interactions depend on clathrin-mediated endocytosis and acidification of intracellular vesicles. This is the first study to demonstrate direct evidence of an FcRY-IgY interaction in primary vascular endothelial cells of an avian species, which supports a key role for FcRY in maternal IgY transfer into egg yolks. These findings open potential avenues for improving avian immunity or delivering substances into eggs through IgY-Fc fusion protein strategies.

Introduction

Maternal immunity or passive immunoglobulin (Ig) transfer is essential for the prevention of infectious diseases during the developmental stages of the immune system. In birds, maternal blood IgY, which is functionally homologous to mammalian IgG, is transported to the yolk of developing oocytes to confer immunocompetence to the offspring (Patterson et al., 1962; Kowalczyk et al., 1985; Tressler and Roth, 1987). Oocyte development occurs in the ovarian follicles of mature laying hens. Avian ovarian follicles consist of concentric tissue layers that surround the oocyte and yolk, including the perivitelline membrane, granulosa cells (GC), basal membrane, and theca (Perry et al., 1978). The ovarian follicle is highly vascularized, and the branches of the arteries pass through the theca to the basal lamina to form capillaries. The deposition of yellow yolk and IgY proceeds rapidly during the approximately ten days preceding ovulation (Johnson, 1986; Etches, 1996). The yolk protein, including IgY, is transported via the blood to the ovary, and IgY is believed to be incorporated into the oocytes by a specific receptor expressed in the ovarian follicle; however, the receptor responsible for maternal blood IgY transfer into yolks has long been a mystery.

FcRY, an IgY-Fc receptor, was first discovered in the chicken yolk sac membrane and contributes to IgY transfer from egg yolks to embryonic circulation through the yolk sac membrane at the late embryonic stages in developing eggs (Ward, 2004; West et al., 2004; Zhang et al., 2017). FcRY is a member of the mannose receptor family with a molecular weight of 180 kDa as its transmembrane form. FcRY has 55% homology with the mammalian (human) phospholipase A2 receptor, but its function is similar to that of mammalian neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn). FcRY has IgY-Fc-binding capacity due to a conformational change at ∼ pH 6.0 and releases IgY above pH 7.4 (Tesar et al., 2008; He and Bjorkman, 2011). FcRY-cytoplasmic domain includes two potential endocytosis motifs (West et al., 2004), implying that FcRY may be involved in the internalization of IgY. However, FcRY cannot bind to IgY at the neutral conditions and thus FcRY is unable to interact with IgY on the surface of cells. Therefore, similar to mammalian FcRn and IgG, IgY is likely internalized into the cells via fluid-phase pinocytosis, and FcRY interacts with IgY at intracellular acidic compartments, but the details remain unclear.

Recently, we demonstrated that the first report that FcRY is expressed in vascular endothelial cells in ovarian follicles (Okamoto et al., 2024). Injection of neutralizing antibodies against FcRY reduced IgY uptake into egg yolks in hens. These results suggest that FcRY regulates maternal IgY transport from the maternal blood circulation to the yolk, as well as subsequent yolk IgY transfer into the embryonic circulation. However, there is no evidence of the direct binding of IgY and FcRY to vascular endothelial cells in avian ovarian follicles, and the precise mechanism of FcRY-dependent IgY transport in vascular endothelial cells is unknown.

In the current study, we performed microscopic analysis of the interaction between FcRY and IgY in primary vascular endothelial cells from quail ovaries using a proximity ligation assay (PLA) and inhibitors of endocytosis and intracellular trafficking, to investigate how ovarian FcRY interacts with IgY.

Materials and methods

Experimental birds and their management

Commercial female Japanese quails (Coturnix japonica) were purchased from a local hatchery (Quail Kosumosu, Aichi, Japan). All birds were maintained individually with free access to water and a commercial diet (Uzura-super®; Toyohashi Feed Mills, Toyohashi, Japan). The photoperiod was set to 16 h L:8 h D during the experiment. The room temperatures were controlled at 24 ± 2°C. Ten- to 30-week-old laying quail were used for the experiments, and egg production was recorded daily. Animal care complied with the guidelines of the Nagoya University Policy on Animal Care and Use (A230040, A240068).

Isolation and culture of vascular endothelial cells from ovarian follicle

The vascular endothelial cells were isolated from quail ovarian follicles as previously described (Chen et al., 2022). The quails were euthanized, and ovarian follicles (F1-F4) were collected. Ovarian follicles were prepared using a razor blade to release the yolk and layers, except for the theca layer. The isolated theca layers were minced and digested with 1 mg/mL type Ⅰ collagenase (031-17601, Fujifilm Wako, Osaka, Japan), 0.4 mg/mL DNase (047-26771, Fujifilm Wako) and 0.1% BSA (01-2030, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) in 5 mL of PBS in water bath at 37°C for 30 min. After that, DMEM (043-30085, Fujifilm Wako) containing 10% FBS was added, filtered by 70 μm nylon mesh and centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min. The cell pellets were resuspended in 5 mL of DMEM, centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min, and resuspended in 1 mL of DMEM. To isolate vascular endothelial cells using cell density, Percoll (590-09052, MP Biomedicals, Irvine, CA, USA) solutions layered with 10 density levels (1.025-1.065 g/mL) were prepared, as described previously (Loubser and Nikitina, 2023). The cell suspension was gently placed on top of the layered Percoll solution and centrifuged at 1,000 g for 40 min. The first 500 μL from the top of the Percoll solution after centrifugation were discarded. Then, 10 fractions of each density of the Percoll solution were collected and cultured in complete medium [DMEM containing 10% FBS, 50 μg/mL ECGs (E2759, Sigma-Aldrich), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (168-23191, Fujifilm Wako), 1% non-essential amino acids solution (06344-56, Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan), and 1% HEPES (17557-94, Nacalai Tesque)] at 37°C, 5% CO2.

Prior to the inhibitor experiment, we conducted a cytotoxicity assay using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The vascular endothelial cells were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured for several days. The cells were treated for 16 h with 10-60 μM Pitstop 2, or 0.02-0.25 μM bafilomycin A1. The cells in each well were incubated with 10 μL of CCK-8 solution and incubated for 2.5 hours at 37°C, 5% CO2. The absorbance of the incubated medium in each well was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader to determine the number of living cells.

In the inhibitor experiment, vascular endothelial cells were cultured overnight in a culture medium without FBS, ECGs, penicillin-streptomycin, or HEPES for overnight starvation. The cells were pretreated for 30 min with 15 μM Pitstop 2, or 0.1 μM bafilomycin A1. The cells were incubated with 400 μg/mL of chicken IgY in the medium, including the inhibitors, for 2 h. After washing, the interaction between FcRY and IgY was detected using the PLA described below.

Western blotting analysis

Specific antibodies against FcRY were produced as previously described (Okamoto et al., 2024). The expression pattern of FcRY was examined by western blot analysis. Each cell and tissue sample from quail (vascular endothelial cells, theca layer, and GC layer of yellow ovarian follicle) was homogenized with RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitor cocktail (11836153001, Roche, Basilea, Switzerland). Samples were mixed with 5×SDS buffer [625 mM Tris-HCl, 10% (w/v) SDS, 62.5% (w/v) glycerol, 0.0625% (w/v) bromophenol blue, 250 mM DTT] and incubated 100°C for 5 min. SDS-treated protein samples (equivalent to 5 μg of protein) were separated by SDS-PAGE. The blotting PVDF membranes were incubated with rabbit anti-FcRY antibody (1:2,500) or rabbit anti-β actin (1:1000; 20536-1-AP, Proteintech, Rosemont, IL) and then with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody (1:2,000; Cell Signaling technology, Danvers, MA). The membranes were visualized using a chemiluminescence detection method (SuperSignal® West Dura Extended Duration Substrate; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) with a charge-coupled device camera (AE-6960/C, Atto, Tokyo, Japan).

Real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from tissues using the reagent (Molecular Research Center, Inc., Cincinnati, OH, USA) and treated with DNase using a Turbo DNA-free kit (Ambion, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from the total RNA using a high-capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems). Gene expression was quantified via real-time PCR using QuantStudio 1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and THUNDERBIRD® SYBR™ qPCR Mix (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Expression data were normalized to endogenous 18S rRNA expression levels. Specific primer pairs were designed using Primer Express Software (version 3.0; Thermo Fisher Scientific) based on the analyzed sequence data or NCBI GenBank data. The primer sequences for FcRY were GCAGAGCTACCTCAGCAACTTG forward and TCAGGCCTGTCCACAGCAT reverse (Access No. XM_015869157.2). The primer sequences for 18S rRNA were CCCTCCCGTTCCTTGGATA for the forward primer and GCGCTCGTCGGCATGTA for the reverse primer (Access No. OK523374.2).

Visualization of FcRY in the isolated vascular endothelial cells

The cultured vascular endothelial cells were fixed with Mildform® (Fujifilm Wako) for 10 min after washing twice with PBS. The cells were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min. The cells were treated overnight with rabbit anti-FcRY antibody (0.92 mg/mL, 1:500) and QH1 mouse monoclonal antibody (1:125; AB_531829, DSHB), a quail vascular endothelial cell marker, at 4°C. After washing, the cells were incubated with biotin-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:250 in blocking buffer; BA-1000, Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA) and AlexaFluor® 568-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (1:1000; ab175473, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) for 1 h in a dark room. The cells were then washed and incubated with Dylight®488-conjugated streptavidin (1:100 in PBS; SA-5488, Vector Laboratories) for 30 min in a dark room. Subsequently, the cells were quenched and DAPI stained using the Vector® TrueView® Autofluorescence Quenching Kit with DAPI (SP-8500, Vector Laboratories) for 5 min in a dark room. The cells were sealed and examined using a confocal laser scanning microscope (FV1000-D, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with operational software (FV10-ASW). The acquired images were adjusted for contrast using the Fiji ImageJ ver. 1.53.

Proximity ligation assay (PLA)

The interaction between FcRY and IgY was visualized using a DuolinkTM in situ PLA kit (Sigma-Aldrich), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, isolated vascular endothelial cells were cultured overnight in a culture medium without FBS, ECGs, penicillin-streptomycin, or HEPES for starvation. The cells were incubated with 400 μg/mL of chicken IgY (Rockland, Philadelphia, PA) for 2 h. Subsequently, the cells were rinsed with PBS and fixed with Mildform® for 10 min. The cells were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100/PBS for 5 min and treated overnight with rabbit anti-FcRY antibody (0.92 mg/mL, 1:500) and mouse anti-chicken IgY at 4°C. After washing and blocking, the cells were incubated with the PLA probe solution (anti-mouse PLUS and anti-rabbit MINUS) for 1 h. The cells were incubated with the ligation-ligase solution for 30 min and then with the amplification-polymerase solution for 100 min in the dark. The cells were dropped onto Duolink Mounting Medium with DAPI and sealed. Images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope equipped with operational software. The PLA signals per cell were counted in 89.8 × 89.8 µm2 areas on the 10 images using Fiji ImageJ ver. 1.53.

Statistical analyses

Statistical differences in the PLA signals were analyzed as previously described (Ooki and Hatakeyama, 2021). In case of over three groups, data were analyzed using Kruskal-Wallis test. Mean values were compared using Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. Comparisons between two groups were performed using Mann-Whitney test. All error bars are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), and differences between means were considered significant at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.4.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA).

Dot-shaped PLA red signals were detected in the cells incubated with IgY (red signals indicated by white arrows; Fig. 2A, left), but were hardly detected in the cells without IgY (middle) and in the isotype control (right). The cytotoxicity assay to determine the optimal concentration of the inhibitor showed Pitstop 2 at 10 μM, and bafilomycin A1 at 0.25 μM or less had little effect on the cell viability (Fig. S1A and B). Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that FcRY was expressed in the subcellular compartments of isolated vascular endothelial cells in avian ovarian follicles. This is the first report showing that FcRY in vascular endothelial cells binds to intracellular IgY via clathrin-mediated endocytosis and requires an acidic intracellular compartment for effective IgY binding. These results suggest that ovarian vascular endothelial cells expressing FcRY contribute to the transfer of maternal blood IgY into egg yolks of maturing oocytes in avian species.

FcRY was first discovered in the yolk sac membrane during the late embryonic stage, and its main function is to transfer the deposited yolk IgY into the circulation of the embryo via the yolk sac membrane (West et al., 2004). In our established primary vascular endothelial cell culture system, the proportions of FcRY and QH1 double-positive cells varied depending on the density fraction (25-71%; Table 1), but their proportions were relatively higher than expected. Additionally, the percentage of QH1-positive cells was nearly equal to that of QH1/FcRY double-positive cells. These results suggest that a considerable number of vascular endothelial cells express FcRY in avian ovaries. Meanwhile, several FcRY-immunoreactive cells were not stained with QH1, suggesting that FcRY is expressed in cells other than vascular endothelial cells in the avian ovary.

When vascular endothelial cells were treated with Pitstop 2, the FcRY-IgY binding signals were not detected by PLA analysis. In rat inner medullary collecting duct (IMCD) cells overexpressing chicken FcRY, fluorescence microscopic analysis showed that clathrin-positive compartments contained endocytosed IgY, and little or no intracellular IgY was detected when clathrin-mediated endocytosis was blocked by potassium depletion in the media (Tesar et al., 2008). Pitstop 2 selectively inhibits clathrin-mediated endocytosis by blocking the C-terminal domain function (von Kleist et al., 2011), indicating that clathrin-mediated endocytosis is involved in the binding of FcRY-IgY in vascular endothelial cells.

Endocytosis plays an important role in the uptake of antibodies into cells; this is also the case for mammalian FcRn and its ligand IgG. In rat intestinal epithelial cells, electron tomography studies have shown that clathrin is also associated with the endocytic and exocytic processes of IgG involving FcRn (He et al., 2008). However, it remains unclear whether mammalian FcRn or avian FcRY directly induces clathrin-dependent endocytic signals during Ig uptake into cells. Neither mammalian FcRn nor avian FcRY can bind to each Ig under neutral pH conditions (pH 7.4) (West et al., 2004; Okamoto et al., 2024), implying that the interaction of Igs with FcRn or FcRY at the surface of cells in blood capillaries is not permissive because of the near-neutral pH of the extracellular environment (Ward et al., 2015). Thus, IgY may be internalized into endothelial cells via fluid-phase pinocytosis, accompanied by endocytic signals induced by other ligands and receptors (Pannek et al., 2023). In the present study, the FcRY signals were distributed as like intracellular vesicles in the vascular endothelial cells. In the PLA analysis, FcRY-IgY binding signals were detectable only under the condition of plasma membrane permeabilization, whereas the FcRY-IgY binding signals disappeared under the condition of non-plasma membrane permeabilization, suggesting that FcRY mainly binds to intracellular IgY, but hardly interacts with cell surface IgY. Further investigations are needed to determine how FcRY contributes to the induction of endocytosis and the involvement of clathrin in the intracellular trafficking of IgY.

FcRY-IgY binding signals disappeared after treatment with bafilomycin A1, which prevented the acidification of intracellular vesicles. These results are consistent with the binding properties of FcRY and its intracellular trafficking pathway for transporting IgY. FcRY undergoes a conformational change at pH 6, assuming a more compact form that binds IgY than the elongated form at neutral and basic pH which does not bind IgY (West et al., 2004). In FcRY-expressing cell lines, IgY is efficiently internalized at pH 6 but not at pH 8 (Tesar et al., 2008). The intracellular trafficking pathways of FcRY and its ligand were identical to those observed for mammalian FcRn and its ligand IgG. Mammalian FcRn is localized in early and recycled endosomes of placental syncytiotrophoblasts. IgG from maternal circulating blood enters syncytiotrophoblasts, and FcRn binds to IgG in the early or recycling endosomes and is transported to the opposite side and then to the fetal circulating blood (Simister et al., 1996; Leach et al., 1996; D’Hooghe et al., 2017; Na et al., 2025). The early (pH ∼6.0) and recycling endosomes (pH ∼6.5) are mildly acidic (Presley et al., 1993; Maxfield and McGraw, 2004). The trafficking pathway of FcRn and IgG in recycling endosomes requires the actin motor myosin Vb and the GTPase Rab25 (Tzaban et al., 2009; Pyzik et al., 2019) and microtubules (McCarthy et al., 2000). In addition, calmodulin, which can bind to the membrane proximal part of the cytoplasmic tail of FcRn in a calcium dependent fashion, is involved in this process (Dickinson et al., 2008). Similarly, in vascular endothelial cells, FcRY is thought to be localized in early or recycling endosomes. We speculated that FcRY binds to IgY within endosomes, transports IgY to the opposite side of the plasma membrane, and releases IgY extracellularly under neutral conditions. In FcRY-expressing cell lines, microtubules are required for FcRY-IgY transcytosis (Tesar et al., 2008), similar to FcRn-IgG transcytosis, but the necessity of the other intracellular molecules has not been determined.

Unfortunately, the primary culture of vascular endothelial cells in transwells failed to reproduce IgY transcytosis because the cells could not grow to fill the gaps between the cells (unpublished data). One reason for the formation of gaps between cells is that the isolated vascular endothelial cells contained some fibroblasts and thus it was unable to form a single layer of vascular endothelial cells. Therefore, it would be necessary to increase the purity of the isolated vascular endothelial cells by using a cell sorter and to reach confluency with high trans-endothelial electrical resistance (TEER) values (Bora et al., 2022). By reproducing IgY transcytosis in vascular endothelial cells and clarifying the intracellular localization of the FcRY-IgY complex, the role of FcRY in the process of maternal IgY transfer into the yolk can be confirmed.

In conclusion, this study clarified the cellular function of FcRY in the primary vascular endothelial cells of avian ovarian follicles. FcRY binds to intracellular IgY internalized into acidic endosomes via clathrin-mediated endocytosis. Thus, ovarian FcRY in vascular endothelial cells plays a critical role in maternal blood IgY transfer into egg yolk. The present findings provide a new strategy for increasing the amount of IgY in avian egg yolks and enhancing avian immunity by controlling FcRY-IgY interactions. This mechanism could potentially be applied to transfer specific substances into egg yolk using Fc fusion proteins, as well as IgY.
