Commentary on “Liver function effects of SGLT2 inhibitor and GLP‐1 receptor agonist combination treatment in patients with type 2 diabetes (post hoc analysis of RECAP study)”
Authors: Yu Zhang, Yiyan Han, Qu Zheng, Xingxing Lin
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

The recent article by Tsuriya et al. (2025) in the Journal of Diabetes Investigation offers a significant post hoc analysis of the RECAP study, addressing a crucial gap in the understanding of the hepatoprotective effects of combination therapy with sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) and glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RA) in patients with type 2 diabetes. This study underscores its clinical relevance by incorporating data from 643 patients across 18 centers, thereby reflecting a wide range of clinical practices 1 . The longitudinal design, with a mean follow‐up period of 61.8 months, evaluates liver function dynamics using the fibrosis‐4 (FIB‐4) index and aminotransferase levels at three distinct time points: baseline, initiation of combination therapy, and final observation. The findings indicate significant reductions in aspartate aminotranstransferase (AST) and alanine aminotranstransferase (ALT) levels following the initiation of combination therapy. The methodological strengths of this study include the use of rigorous propensity score matching to mitigate confounding variables, analysis of treatment sequences (comparing SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP‐1 receptor agonists in preceding groups), and innovative subgroup analyses stratified by FIB‐4 categories and ALT thresholds (≥30 IU/L) to identify responsive subpopulations, thereby reinforcing personalized treatment approaches. The conclusion—that combination therapy enhances liver function and reduces fibrosis progression in high‐risk patients (with a baseline FIB‐4 score of ≥1.3)—is consistent with emerging evidence regarding the pleiotropic benefits of these agents. This conclusion is further supported by statistical rigor achieved through multiple imputation for missing data and the application of Rubin's rule for pooled estimates. However, despite these strengths, several limitations necessitate cautious interpretation of the findings:

INADEQUATE ADJUSTMENT FOR MASLD DIAGNOSTIC CRITERIA

The study predominantly utilizes surrogate markers, namely fibrosis‐4 (FIB‐4), Hepatic Steatosis Index (HSI), and alanine aminotranstransferase (ALT), without integrating established diagnostic criteria for metabolic‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). Although the fibrosis‐4 (FIB‐4) index is practical, its reliability is compromised in individuals under 35 years of age or those experiencing acute inflammation, due to its significant age dependency. Notably, the study does not report critical confounding variables such as abdominal obesity, triglyceride levels, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), and insulin resistance. This omission is particularly consequential, given the pivotal role of metabolic dysregulation in the progression of MASLD 2 . In the absence of these data, the observed “hepatoprotective” effects may be misattributed, potentially conflating metabolic improvements with the direct effects of the drug under investigation. The authors' utilization of the Hepatic Steatosis Index (HSI), which includes body mass index (BMI) and diabetes status, offers a partial mitigation of the issue but does not meet current standards, such as the FibroScan‐AST (FAST) score or Magnetic Resonance Imaging Proton Density Fat Fraction (MRI‐PDFF). Future research should integrate established non‐invasive tests (NITs) with greater specificity for detecting steatosis, such as the Controlled Attenuation Parameter (CAP) via transient elastography or MRI‐PDFF, as well as tests for active fibrosis, including the Enhanced Liver Fibrosis (ELF) test or Vibration‐Controlled Transient Elastography (VCTE). Despite the challenges associated with obtaining histological data in large cohorts, it remains the gold standard for confirming resolution of metabolic‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH) or improvement in fibrosis.

SELECTION BIAS AND GENERALIZABILITY

The mean body mass index (BMI) of the cohort (29.6 kg/m2) surpasses the national average for type 2 diabetes in Japan (24.3 kg/m2), indicating a predominance of severe obesity within the sample. This discrepancy limits the applicability of the findings to lean type 2 diabetes populations, where the pathophysiology of metabolic‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) may differ. Furthermore, the exclusion of patients with cirrhosis or severe liver dysfunction restricts the study's insights into advanced disease stages, which are the most likely to benefit from hepatoprotective therapies. It is essential for future studies to report discontinuation rates and their underlying reasons. Conducting similar analyses in cohorts with lower body mass index (BMI) or diverse ethnic backgrounds would improve the generalizability of the findings. Pragmatic trials or registry studies that capture real‐world discontinuation rates could effectively address this bias.

HETEROGENEITY IN DRUG EXPOSURE AND REGIMENS

The majority of patients were administered dulaglutide or liraglutide, frequently at doses lower than those demonstrated to be effective for MASH in clinical trials, such as semaglutide at 2.4 mg. The impact of specific GLP‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RAs) or the attainment of higher doses on hepatic outcomes within combination therapies remains insufficiently investigated 3 . Frequent alterations in medication further complicate the attribution of observed effects. Specifically, medication types were altered in 16% of patients for SGLT2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) and 26% for GLP‐1RAs. This variability obscures the ability to discern the impact of individual agents on liver outcomes. The interpretation of sequence effects, such as the claim that “prior SGLT2i use appeared beneficial,” lacks support from propensity score‐matched results, which revealed no significant differences between groups in aspartate aminotranstransferase (AST), alanine aminotranstransferase (ALT), or fibrosis‐4 (FIB‐4) at the final observation. The conclusion is at risk of a type I error due to marginal P‐values observed in sensitivity analyses. Therefore, analyses stratified by GLP‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RAs) type (short‐ vs long‐acting), achieved dosage, and treatment adherence are warranted. Furthermore, head‐to‐head comparisons or dose‐ranging studies within combination therapy are necessary to elucidate optimal regimens for hepatic benefit.

UNMEASURED CONFOUNDERS IMPACTING LIVER HEALTH

Several critical confounding variables impacting liver function and the progression of metabolic‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) were not considered in the analysis 4 . These include detailed alcohol consumption, viral hepatitis status, dietary patterns such as fructose intake, levels of physical activity, and baseline liver fat quantification. Additionally, the absence of waist circumference data impedes the precise evaluation of metabolic syndrome components. Future prospective studies should systematically collect and adjust for these variables. Employing comprehensive MASLD risk scores that integrate metabolic and lifestyle factors, in conjunction with non‐invasive tests (NITs), would facilitate a more holistic assessment.

CONCLUSION

Tsuriya et al. provide robust evidence suggesting that the combination therapy of SGLT2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) and GLP‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RA) improves liver biochemistry and may slow fibrosis progression in patients with high‐risk type 2 diabetes, with a potential preference for initiating treatment with SGLT2i. This finding highlights the dual metabolic and potential hepatic benefits of this therapeutic combination. However, as the editor correctly points out, the retrospective design and large sample size (n = 530) of the RECAP study inherently limit the feasibility of histological assessment, which remains the gold standard for confirming the resolution of metabolic‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH) or improvement in fibrosis. To conclusively determine the effects of SGLT2i/GLP‐1RA combination therapy on liver histology, including steatosis, inflammation, ballooning, and fibrosis, in patients with type 2 diabetes and metabolic‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD)/metabolic‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH), future prospective studies specifically designed for this objective are imperative. These studies should prioritize the following:

Targeted prospective cohorts: Emphasize the recruitment of patients with biopsy‐confirmed MASLD/MASH, particularly those exhibiting significant fibrosis, who are suitable candidates for combination therapy. While large, population‐based studies such as RECAP provide valuable insights into real‐world effectiveness, dedicated prospective cohorts with histological endpoints are crucial for assessing the therapy's efficacy on liver histology.

Targeted prospective cohorts: Emphasize the recruitment of patients with biopsy‐confirmed MASLD/MASH, particularly those exhibiting significant fibrosis, who are suitable candidates for combination therapy. While large, population‐based studies such as RECAP provide valuable insights into real‐world effectiveness, dedicated prospective cohorts with histological endpoints are crucial for assessing the therapy's efficacy on liver histology.

Serial non‐invasive tests (NITs) with selective biopsy: Implement longitudinal monitoring through advanced NITs, such as MRI‐PDFF for steatosis and VCTE with CAP or MRE for fibrosis, at predetermined intervals. Importantly, these studies should include paired liver biopsies at both the baseline and the conclusion of the study for all participants or, more practically, for a predefined subset. This paired biopsy approach is essential for evaluating histological changes.
