Introduction

The unique physiological processes of women, such as menstrual cycles, pregnancy, lactation, and menopause, along with genetic factors, environmental exposures, and psychological influences, increase susceptibility to certain cancers [1, 2]. Gynaecological cancers (GCs) including ovarian cancer (OC), cervical cancer (CC), and uterine cancer (UC) are a significant group of malignancies affecting the female reproductive system and pose a serious global health threat, account for 38.3% of all cancers in women, presenting a significant global health issue [3]. Breast cancer (BC) has the highest incidence and mortality rates, with over 2.31 million new cases and 670,000 deaths reported globally in 2022. Currently, one in eight women diagnosed with cancer has BC, a trend that is rising, particularly in high-income countries [3]. Although BC is predominantly a female disease, it can also occur in men, although rarely, with less than 1% of all cases occurring in males [4]. In most countries, BC is not considered part of gynecologic oncology, but it is included in some regions, such as Germany, due to its relevance to female reproductive health. Consequently, while the global burden of breast cancer, as reported in this study, primarily reflects its impact on the female population, it is important to recognize that the disease’s occurrence in men could influence some reports. OC, the deadliest gynecological malignancy, has a 5-year survival rate of just 47.4%, mainly due to late detection and recurrence [5]. UC, which makes up 20–30% of all female reproductive tract cancers, has seen a dramatic increase in global incidence, rising approximately 2.5 times from 1990 to 2022 [3, 5]. Despite the effectiveness of screening and the human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination programs that have reduced CC, it remains the fourth most common cancer in women worldwide [6].

These cancers not only contribute to high morbidity and mortality but also significantly affect fertility, gender identity, and mental health. For women of reproductive age, cancer treatments, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgery, can lead to infertility and other reproductive health issues [7, 8]. Moreover, the impact of these cancers on mental health is substantial, with patients often experiencing significant psychological stress and emotional distress. Therefore, a comprehensive assessment of the impact of these cancers must consider their multifaceted effects on patients’ quality of life. Several factors, including population aging, lifestyle, healthcare access, and economic conditions, contribute to the varying epidemiological impact of GCs. While studies have focused on specific age groups, such as adolescents and women of reproductive age, comprehensive data covering all female age groups is lacking. With the global aging population, the number of postmenopausal and elderly women is steadily increasing. Long-term studies have shown that inappropriate menopausal hormone therapy can elevate the risk of OC, endometrial, and BC. Elderly women also face unique challenges, including comorbidities, which complicate treatment and lead to poorer outcomes.

There is a critical need for updated data on the global burden of GCs, including incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). These indicators are essential for shaping preventive strategies and understanding regional disparities. To date, no comprehensive report has forecasted the burden of GCs for the next 30 years, presenting a significant gap in the literature.

This study presents a novel approach to understanding the global burden of GCs by integrating age-period-cohort (APC) analysis with autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) and Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) models, leveraging data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study. To our knowledge, no prior research has combined these advanced epidemiological modeling techniques in this context. By utilizing these models, we provide a comprehensive and dynamic analysis that accounts for both short-term trends and long-term cohort effects in GCs, offering insights that have not been previously explored. This combined methodology enables a more accurate assessment of the evolving global burden of GCs, with implications for both regional and global health policies.

Method

Data sources

The study analyzes the global disease burden of GCs from 1990 to 2021using data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 database (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/), published by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) [9]. The GBD 2021 offers a comprehensive assessment of global health, covering 371 diseases, injuries, and 88 risk factors across 204 countries and territories [10]. As the data is publicly available, no additional ethical clearance was required. The cancers analyzed in this study, as defined in the GBD 2021 framework, include BC (malignant neoplasms of the breast; ICD-10 code C50), OC (ICD-10 code C56), UC (ICD-10 code C54), and CC (ICD-10 code C53). Relevant data were extracted using the GBD 2021 results tool, with the following parameters applied: "cause of death or injury" was selected under GBD Estimate; "incidence," "deaths," and "DALYs" were chosen under Measure; "number," "percent," and "rates" were specified as the metric; the four cancers listed above were designated as the cause; data were requested for global, five socio-demographic index (SDI) regions, 21 GBD regions, and 204 countries and territories; and the time frame was set from 1990 to 2021. Upon finalizing these selections, the search function enabled the retrieval of the necessary data for this analysis.

Data collection

Information on the incidence, deaths, and DALYs for OC, CC, BC, and UC was extracted from the GBD 2021 [11]. DALYs, which combine years of life lost due to premature death and years lived with disability, were also analyzed. Age-standardized DALYs rates (ASDR) were assessed to identify trends from 1990 to 2021. The SDI, a composite measure based on education, per capita income, and fertility rates, reflects socio-economic conditions that influence health outcomes [12]. SDI is scaled from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating more developed conditions. It is divided into five quintiles representing levels of socio-economic development. Locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) was used to examine the correlation between the burden of GCs and socio-economic development across regions [13].

Risk factor

GBD 2021 categorizes risk factors into environmental, behavioral, and metabolic groups [14]. This study focuses on key risk factors contributing to mortality from GCs, including alcohol consumption, high red meat diets, high body mass index (BMI), high fasting plasma glucose, low physical activity, occupational carcinogens, secondhand smoke, smoking, and unsafe sex. Occupational exposure to carcinogens is a composite risk factor that encompasses exposure to various hazardous substances in the workplace, such as asbestos, arsenic, benzene, beryllium, cadmium, chromium, diesel engine exhaust, formaldehyde, nickel, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, silica, sulfuric acid, and trichloroethylene.

APC model

The APC model analyzed the independent effects of age, period, and birth cohort on the burden of GCs [15, 16]. In this classical APC model, the counts of GCs were assumed to follow a Poisson distribution and a log-linear Poisson model was fitted:

Period and cohort effects were expressed as risk ratios (RR), calculated as age-specific ratios for each period and cohort relative to a reference cohort. These RR are adjusted for age, thus allowing for a precise examination of temporal and generational factors influencing the burden of GCs. This decomposition aids in understanding the distinct and interacting roles of age, period, and cohort effects on GC epidemiology.

Future projections

The ARIMA model was used to forecast future trends in the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) of the four GCs over the next 30 years [17, 18]. The ARIMA model is expressed as ARIMA (p, d, q), where p is the autoregressive order, d is the number of differences, and q is the moving average order. Model selection was performed using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), a statistical measure that helps balance the Goodness-of-fit and the complexity of the model. The optimal ARIMA model was chosen based on the lowest AIC value. This model was then used to generate forecasts of the ASIR of GCs over a 30-year horizon, providing a quantitative prediction of the future disease burden based on historical trends.

Additionally, a BAPC model, utilizing integrated nested Laplace approximations (INLA) were also employed to predict ASIR trends for the next 30 years [19]. Utilizing INLA, this approach operates within the Bayesian framework, where prior distributions for age, period, and cohort effects are specified. These priors are subsequently updated with observed data to derive posterior distributions. This Bayesian updating process not only Yields more accurate estimates of the effects but also provides credible intervals that quantify the uncertainty associated with these estimates. The 95% credible intervals (CI) for the BAPC model are derived from the posterior distribution percentiles obtained through Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling. Specifically, the 95% CI is extracted directly from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the posterior distribution, thereby offering a robust measure of the parameter uncertainty.

The study utilized both the ARIMA and BAPC models to project trends in the incidence of gynecological cancers. The ARIMA model, commonly used for short-term projections, is effective for capturing linear trends based on historical data. However, it does not account for cohort-specific effects. In contrast, the BAPC model incorporates age, period, and cohort effects, offering a more nuanced view of long-term trends and generational shifts in disease burden. While ARIMA is suited for short-term forecasting, the BAPC model is better for capturing the complex interactions between age and cohort dynamics, providing a comprehensive analysis of disease trends.

Statistical analysis

The APC model was implemented using the age-period cohort tool (https://analysistools.cancer.gov/apc/). Statistical analyses and data visualizations were conducted using R version 4.4.1 with packages including ggplot2, dplyr, BAPC, tidyverse, forecast, and INLA.

Result

Global and regional disease burden of GCs

The global burden of GCs exhibits significant variation across cancer types, regions, and socio-economic contexts. Among these, BC remains the most prevalent and deadliest, with continued increases in global incidence, particularly in high SDI regions. BC accounted for 2,082,737 (95% UI: 1,940,351 to 2,225,083) incident cases and 660,925 (95% UI: 609,171 to 707,182) deaths. The global ASIR has increased by 0.89% from 1990 to 2021, reflecting improvements in early detection and screening; however, the burden remains substantial in low SDI regions, where access to screening is limited (Table S2). OC had 298,876 (95% UI: 270,730 to 324,501) incident cases globally in 2021 and caused 185,609 (95% UI: 167,962 to 201,013) deaths. Despite slight declines in ASIR in high SDI regions, the global burden of OC remains high, particularly in middle-income countries where late-stage diagnoses are more common. The overall global ASIR for OC decreased by only −0.07% from 1990 to 2021, masking significant regional disparities, with notable increases in ASIR observed in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa (Table 1). CC has decreased substantially in high SDI regions and screening programs. However, it remains a significant burden in low SDI regions, where ASIR and age-standardized mortality rates (ASMR) continue to rise. In 2021, CC had 667,426 incident cases (95% UI: 613,030 to 726,422) and 296,667 deaths (95% UI: 272,059 to 321,906). The ASR for CC has decreased in most high-income regions, but low-income regions, especially sub-Saharan Africa, continue to experience rising ASIR and ASMR (Table S2). UC exhibited the most dramatic increase, with 473,614 (95% UI: 429,916 to 513,667) incident cases and 97,672 (95% UI: 86,516 to 108,062) deaths in 2021. From 1990 to 2021, the global ASR for UC increased by 0.71%, with particularly large increases observed in North America, Europe, and Australia (Table S3).

Global and regional incidence, deaths, and DALYs for OC in 2021, and the percentage change in ASR from 1990 to 2021

Burden for GCs in 204 countries and territories

From 1990 to 2021, significant regional differences were observed in OC, CC, BC, and UC. The UAE exhibited the highest values for ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR for both OC and UC (Fig. 1, Figure S1 and S2). In contrast, CC was more prevalent in Venezuela and various regions of Africa (excluding North and West Africa). BC showed higher ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR in the UAE, Qatar, Western Europe, North America, Australia, and New Zealand. Furthermore, OC and CC showed increased disease burdens in regions such as Ecuador, Venezuela, Georgia, Russia, Italy, South Africa, and Egypt. BC exhibited notable increases in North Africa, the Middle East, and South Asia, while UC showed substantial increases in ASIR in Italy, Saudi Arabia, and Japan.

ASIR of four GCs, along with the annual percentage change in ASIR from 1990 to 2021 across 204 countries and territories. ASIR: Age-standardized incidence rate per 100,000 population. APC: Average annual percentage change in ASIR from 1990 to 2021. Positive values indicate increasing incidence, while negative values indicate decreasing incidence

The annual percentage change from 1990 to 2021 indicated notable increases in OC ASIR in Ecuador, Venezuela, and Georgia (Figs. 1, S1, and S2). CC showed slight increases in ASIR in Russia, Italy, South Africa, and Egypt, while decreases were observed in other regions. BC exhibited relatively large increases in ASIR in North Africa and the Middle East, with ASMR and ASDR notably increasing in South Asia, Africa, and the Middle East. UC saw significant increases in ASIR in Italy, Saudi Arabia, and Japan, with China also experiencing a marked rise in UC ASIR.

The ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR of OC exhibited a fluctuating increase with rising SDI values up to an SDI of 0.81, beyond which these rates declined (Fig. 2A, Figure S3A, and Figure S4A). Conversely, CC demonstrated an inverse relationship with SDI (Fig. 2B, Figure S3B, and Figure S4B). For BC, ASIR correlated positively with SDI, while ASMR and ASDR followed a fluctuating trend, increasing until an SDI of approximately 0.75, after which they declined (Fig. 2C, Figure S3C, and Figure S4C). Similarly, the ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR of UC rose with SDI values below 0.81, then declined as SDI continued to increase (Fig. 2D, Figure S3D, and Figure S4D).

Global burden of GCs by age

Analysis of the burden of GCs by age group revealed the highest incidence in the 40–59 and 60–79 age groups, except for UC, which had no cases in individuals under 20. Mortality from GCs decreased with increasing age.

For OC, incidence and mortality decreased with age, with the most significant decline in the 60–79 and ≥ 80 age groups (Fig. 3A, Figure S5A). The DALYs rates for OC were highest in the 60–79 age group, showing a general downward trend in the 40–59, 60–79, and ≥ 80 age groups. The incidence of CC was highest in the 40–59 and 60–79 age groups, with a decrease in older age groups (Fig. 3B). Mortality from CC showed a consistent decline in the 40–59, 60–79, and ≥ 80 age groups (Figure S5B).

The burden of GCs of cases and incidence rates globally by age group from 1990 to 2021. (A) OC, (B) CC, (C) BC, (D) UC

DALYs rates were highest in the 40–59 age group, with a general decline across all age groups, except for those under 20 (Figure S6B). The incidence of BC decreased with age, with an initial rise in the ≥ 80 age group, followed by a decline, and an increasing trend in the 40–59 and 60–79 age groups (Fig. 3C). The mortality for BC remained stable without a significant trend (Figure S5C). DALYs rates for BC decreased with age, initially increasing in the ≥ 80 age group, followed by stabilization, then a decrease, with an upward trend in the 40–59 and 60–79 age groups (Figure S6C). The incidence for UC peaked in the 60–79 age group, followed by ≥ 80, 40–59, and 20–39 age groups, showing an increasing trend over time in the 40–59, 60–79, and ≥ 80 age groups, with no cases in the 20–39 age group (Fig. 3D). Mortality from UC in the ≥ 80 age group initially decreased, then increased, and finally decreased again, while the 40–59 and 60–79 age groups showed a downward trend (Figure S5D). The DALYs rates for UC were highest in the 60–79 age group, with a decreasing trend across all age groups, and no cases under 20 years (Figure S6D).

APC modeling to assess the independent effects of the burden of GCs

APC modeling revealed that the incidence and DALYs for OC increased with age, peaking in the 85–90 age group before declining (Fig. 4A). CC incidence peaked in the 55–60 age group (Fig. 4D). BC incidence continued to rise until the 85–90 age group (Fig. 4G), while UC incidence increased until the 85–90 age group, then fluctuated with a general decline (Fig. 4J). Mortality rates for OC and CC increased up to the 90–95 and 55–60 age groups, respectively, before decreasing (Figures S7A and D). In contrast, BC mortality increased steadily with age (Figure S7G), while UC mortality increased up to the 90–95 age group (Figure S7J). DALYs for OC, CC, BC, and UC were highest in the 65–70, 50–55, 55–60, and 65 70 age groups, respectively (Figure S8).

The period effect showed an increase in incidence until around 2000, followed by a decline (Fig. 4B). In contrast, CC incidence steadily decreased over time (Fig. 4E), while BC and UC incidence steadily increased (Figs. 4H and K). Mortality rates for OC and CC, along with DALYs, showed a decreasing trend, while BC and UC mortality and DALYs decreased until approximately 2015, and then began increasing (Figures S7 and S8). Cohort analysis revealed that OC incidence increased after 1967, followed by a decline in subsequent birth cohorts (Fig. 4C). CC incidence continued to decrease over time (Fig. 4F), while BC incidence increased in later birth cohorts (Fig. 4I). UC incidence declined after 1987 but fluctuated thereafter (Fig. 4L). Mortality and DALYs for OC and CC decreased in later cohorts (Figures S7C and F), while BC mortality and DALYs decreased initially, then increased after around 1972 (Figure S7I). UC mortality and DALYs decreased in later birth cohorts (Figure S7L).

Risk factors

Key risk factors identified for GCs included alcohol use, a diet high in red meat, high BMI, high fasting plasma glucose, low physical activity, occupational carcinogens, secondhand smoke, smoking, and unsafe sex. We assessed the ASMR attributed to these risk factors for the four major GCs in 1990 and 2021.

For OC, high BMI and occupational carcinogens were the primary risk factors (Fig. 5). The ASMR for OC due to high BMI exceeded that of occupational carcinogens, with this disparity becoming more pronounced in 2021 compared to 1990. ASMR for OC was notably higher in high SDI regions, especially in Australasia in 1990 and Central Europe in 2021.

Distribution of risk exposure factors contributing to OC mortality in 1990 and 2021

The mortality of CC was most strongly associated with smoking and unsafe sex (Figure S9). The ASMR for CC attributed to unsafe sex was significantly higher than that due to smoking. The ASMR for CC was most significant in low SDI regions, peaking in Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa in 1990 and Southern Sub-Saharan Africa in 2021. For BC, key risk factors included alcohol use, a diet high in red meat, high BMI, high fasting plasma glucose, low physical activity, secondhand smoke, and smoking (Figure S10). The ASMR for BC due to diet high in red meat was notably higher than for other risk factors. High SDI regions exhibited the highest ASMR, with Western Europe leading in 1990 and Southern Sub-Saharan Africa in 2021.

UC mortality was exclusively attributed to high BMI (Figure S11). The ASMR for UC was greatest in middle-high SDI regions in 1990 and high SDI regions in 2021. Eastern Europe displayed the highest ASMR for UC in both 1990 and 2021.

Discussion

Our study highlights the significant global burden of GCs in 2021. BC accounted for the highest DALYs at 20,254,802, followed by CC at 9,911,653, OC at 5,163,256, and UC at 2,562,943. From 1990 to 2021, both BC and UC exhibited an increasing ASIR, whereas OC and CC demonstrated declines in both ASIR and ASDR. Additionally, regional disparities were evident, with BC's ASIR showing a positive correlation with the SDI, while CC exhibited an inverse relationship with the SDI. The burden of OC and UC tended to increase with rising SDI values but showed a decline after reaching an SDI threshold of 0.81.

OC remains the deadliest gynecological malignancy, with a five-year survival rate of 31% [20]. The highest incidence was reported in moderate SDI regions, while high-SDI regions experienced the highest mortality, highlighting disparities in early diagnosis and treatment access. The disease burden peaks in those aged 60–79, with mortality rates correlating positively with age until 90–95 years, after which they decline. OC is often diagnosed at advanced stages, where prognosis is poor. Early biomarkers Like combining cancer antigen 125 (CA125) and human epididymis protein 4 (HE4) are gaining attention for their potential to improve early detection. Several meta-analyses have shown that the combination of CA125 and HE4 can significantly improve the sensitivity and specificity of OC detection compared to using CA125 alone, with pooled sensitivity and specificity values reaching up to 85% and 90%, respectively [21]. However, further research is needed to validate their use in population-based screenings, especially in low-resource settings where access to advanced diagnostic tools is limited [20]. Moreover, genetic advances, such as BRCA gene testing, provide opportunities for targeted therapies but require increased access to genetic counseling and testing, particularly in lower-resource settings [21, 22]. CC, once the leading cause of cancer-related death in women, has shown a decline in ASIR and ASDR in high SDI regions, largely due to widespread HPV vaccination and screening programs [23, 24].

However, the situation remains dire in low SDI regions, where the lack of access to vaccination and screening continues to contribute to high rates of CC [25]. Vaccine coverage disparities by region and target age groups are a significant concern. For example, in high-income regions, HPV vaccination coverage rates among adolescent girls can exceed 80%, while in low-income regions, coverage rates may be below 30% [23]. This highlights the urgent need to scale up vaccination programs in low-resource settings to achieve global elimination of CC [24]. The World Health Organization's (WHO) call for the global elimination of CC is an important step, but achieving this goal will require robust health infrastructure improvements, particularly in resource-limited settings [26, 27]. Scaling up HPV vaccination programs and increasing access to screening and treatment in these regions will be critical to reducing the global burden of CC [28].

BC, the most common malignancy among women, showed higher ASIR in high SDI regions due to better screening and early detection, resulting in lower mortality despite higher incidence. The high ASIR in high SDI regions can be partly attributed to the maturity of screening programs and the increased detection of early-stage cancers. However, it is important to note that molecular subtypes of BC, such as triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) and hormone receptor-positive (HR +)/HER2-negative breast cancer, may have different screening and treatmentents [29]. For example, TNBC is more aggressive and often requires more intensive treatment protocols, while HR +/HER2-negative breast cancer may benefit from targeted hormonal therapies [30]. The disparity in survival between high-income and low-income countries suggests that improving early diagnosis and treatment access in lower-resource regions is essential [31]. Specifically, enhancing access to mammography and ultrasound, along with targeted therapies, could reduce mortality. UC, particularly endometrial cancer, has seen a noticeable increase in incidence rates in high-income countries [32], likely driven by increasing life expectancy and improved diagnostic capabilities. However, despite rising incidence, mortality rates have not risen correspondingly, suggesting that survival rates have improved due to early detection and effective treatment. In contrast, certain countries with aging populations, such as Italy and Japan, report higher mortality rates, which may be associated with challenges in early diagnosis and limited access to newer treatment modalities. Moreover, the rising prevalence of obesity, a known risk factor for UC, may be exacerbating trends in incidence, particularly in high-SDI countries where obesity rates are highest. Racial and ethnic disparities also play a significant role in UC incidence and mortality. For example, in the United States, Black women have a higher incidence of UC compared to White women, and they also experience higher mortality rates [33]. Additionally, comorbidities such as diabetes and hypertension can further complicate treatment outcomes and contribute to higher mortality rates [34]. Addressing these disparities through targeted public health interventions and improved access to healthcare is crucial for reducing the global burden of UC. Disparities in cancer treatment and survivorship, particularly among reproductive-age women, are heavily influenced by social determinants of health, including socioeconomic status, healthcare access, and cultural factors. Women in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) often face significant barriers, including delayed diagnosis, limited access to specialized care, and high out-of-pocket costs, which result in poorer cancer outcomes [35]. These barriers are particularly pronounced for reproductive-age women, who also contend with challenges related to fertility preservation after cancer treatment. While fertility preservation techniques are more widely available in high-income settings, their high cost makes them largely inaccessible to women from lower socio-economic backgrounds.

Cultural attitudes toward cancer care also contribute to delays in treatment, particularly in LMICs. In some regions, there is stigma associated with cancer diagnosis, which can discourage women from seeking timely treatment. In contrast, even in high-income countries, disparities persist, particularly among marginalized populations. For instance, women from lower socio-economic backgrounds may face difficulties in accessing fertility preservation services, even when they are offered, due to financial constraints or lack of awareness. Additionally, social support systems play a critical role in the mental and emotional well-being of women undergoing cancer treatment. Those lacking adequate social support are more likely to experience psychological distress, which can negatively impact treatment adherence and overall quality of life during survivorship. High BMI emerged as a major risk factor for OC, BC, and UC, consistent with previous epidemiological findings [36, 37]. High BMI influences patient prognosis through various mechanisms, particularly in postmenopausal women, where obesity (BMI ≥ 30) doubles the risk of estrogen receptor-positive (ER +) BC compared to those of normal weight [32]. Additionally, estrogen-dependent EC, which accounts for 85% of UC cases, is frequently observed in obese patients [38]. A recent population-based study demonstrated that for every 5 kg/m2 increase in BMI in premenopausal women, the risk of OC rises by 9% (HR 1.09, 99% CI 1.04–1.14) [39]. Obesity increases estrogen levels by contributing to higher adipose tissue, which impacts the liver's synthesis of sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), thus increasing free circulating estrogen levels and heightening the risk of EC [40]. Changes in the tumor microenvironment also play a critical role, as obesity upregulates factors such as IL-6 and FGF-2, potentially leading to resistance to chemotherapy and targeted therapies in patients with BC [41]. Additionally, adipocyte-derived exosomes can protect OC cells from chemotherapy-induced apoptosis [42]. Furthermore, obesity is associated with postoperative complications, exacerbating healing issues and increasing the risk of infections, thereby delaying the initiation of adjuvant therapies and ultimately impacting overall survival [43].

Despite these findings, several limitations must be acknowledged. Data from low-income regions may be incomplete or inaccurate, leading to potential biases in burden estimates. Additionally, the GBD study does not account for all relevant risk factors, such as genetic predispositions and environmental exposures, which may impact the accuracy of cancer burden estimates. Regional disparities in healthcare resources further complicate accurate assessments, especially in underdeveloped regions where diagnostic and treatment services are limited. Moreover, the GBD dataset may lack the granularity needed to distinguish specific cancer subtypes, which could affect the precision of burden assessments for particular forms of gynecological cancers. Future research should focus on addressing these data gaps by improving data quality, particularly in underrepresented regions, and integrating a broader range of risk factors, including genetic markers and environmental exposures, into disease burden models. By enhancing the depth and accuracy of the data, a more comprehensive understanding of the global burden of gynecological cancers can be achieved, ultimately guiding more targeted and effective public health interventions.

To reduce the global burden of GCs, comprehensive policy changes are needed, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Expanding HPV vaccination programs, improving access to cervical screening, and increasing awareness of the importance of early detection of BC, OC, and UC are essential steps. Additionally, addressing obesity through public health interventions aimed at improving lifestyle factors, such as encouraging healthy diets and physical activity, could significantly reduce the incidence and mortality of these cancers. Finally, ensuring that women, particularly those in lower-income regions, have access to high-quality healthcare and cancer treatments is fundamental to reducing the global burden of GCs. International collaboration and increased investment in cancer research and healthcare infrastructure will be key to addressing these challenges. By focusing on both prevention and treatment, we can make significant strides toward reducing the global burden of GCs in the coming decades.

Conclusions

In summary, the burden of GCs exhibits significant regional disparities globally. High-income countries predominantly bear a high burden of BC and UC, whereas CC and OC more severely impact low-income countries. The burden of GCs has been complex, with some types, such as OC and BC, showing an increasing trend in the middle and high SDI regions. To effectively address the escalating global situation of GCs, tailored public health strategies must be implemented, considering the socio-economic development characteristics of each region. In low SDI regions, emphasis should be placed on CC screening, HPV vaccination, and early diagnosis of OC. In contrast, high SDI regions should focus on enhancing early screening and diagnosis of BC and optimizing treatment options. Ultimately, global policies for the prevention and treatment of GCs should pay more attention to regional differences, integrate resources, and promote international cooperation to reduce the overall burden of GCs in the future.
