Chlorpromazine inhibits EAG1 channels by altering the interdomain coupling
Authors: Kyle Kihn, Ze-Jun Wang, Xi Chen, Mahdi Ghorbani, Purushottam B Tiwari, Jeffery B Klauda, Tinatin I Brelidze
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEAG1 depolarization-activated potassium selective channels are important targets for treatment of cancer and neurological disorders. EAG1 channels are formed by a tetrameric subunit assembly with each subunit containing an N-terminal Per-Arnt-Sim (PAS) domain and C-terminal cyclic nucleotide-binding homology (CNBH) domain. The PAS and CNBH domains from adjacent subunits interact and form an intracellular tetrameric ring that regulates the EAG1 channel gating, including the movement of the voltage sensor domain (VSD) from closed to open states. Small-molecule ligands can inhibit EAG1 channels by binding to their PAS domains. However, the allosteric pathways of this inhibition are not known. Here, we combined molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, network analysis, surface plasmon resonance (SPR), and mutagenesis-coupled electrophysiology to investigate the allosteric pathways of EAG1 channel regulation by a small-molecule ligand. MD simulations and network analysis revealed that chlorpromazine, a PAS domain small-molecule binder, alters interactions between the PAS and CNBH domains. Consistent with computational predictions, chlorpromazine affected interactions between the isolated PAS and CNBH domains probed with SPR. Our network analysis also indicated that binding chlorpromazine to the PAS domain alters coupling between all major regulatory domains of EAG1, including the intracellular PAS/CNBH domain ring, VSD and pore of the channel. Interestingly, chlorpromazine binding to the PAS domain did not substantially alter Cole-Moore shift characteristic of EAG1 channels, suggesting that chlorpromazine has little effect on the VSD movement from the deep closed to opened states. This further suggests that, if chlorpromazine binding alters coupling between the PAS/CNBH domain ring, VSD, and pore, as predicted by the network analysis, these alterations occur at potential more depolarized than the ones eliciting the Cole-Moore shift. Taken together, our study provides an insight into the allosteric pathways of EAG1 channel regulation by small-molecule PAS domain binders.

Significance

This study investigates allosteric pathways of EAG1 channel inhibition by binding of a small-molecule ligand chlorpromazine to their intracellular N-terminal Per-Arnt-Sim (PAS) domains. Using a combination of computational simulations and surface plasmon resonance, we find that binding of chlorpromazine to the PAS domain alters interactions between the PAS domain and the C-terminal cyclic nucleotide-binding homology (CNBH) domain. Our computational simulations further suggest that chlorpromazine binding to the PAS domain alters coupling between the intracellular ring formed by the PAS/CNBH, VSD, and pore domains of the channel. These findings should facilitate the understanding of molecular mechanisms of EAG1 channel inhibition by ligands and design of EAG1 channel-targeting pharmacological agents.

Introduction

Voltage-gated potassium channels (VGPCs) are essential for cellular signaling as they are key players in controlling membrane potential (1,2). VGPCs share a common architecture, including a tetrameric assembly from subunits containing six membrane-spanning segments, with segments one through four (S1–S4) forming the voltage sensor domain (VSD) and segments five and six (S5–S6) forming the centrally located pore domain (PD) (1,2). While the general architecture is similar for VGPCs, their distinct functional features are frequently conferred by intracellular domains. The intracellular domains of VGPCs function as regulatory and ligand-binding modules, and are promising targets for pharmacological regulation and drug design (3). Mechanisms of VGPC regulation by ligand binding to their intracellular domains are not well understood.

Ether-a-go-go (EAG1) VGPCs, also known as KCNH1 and Kv10.1, are founding members of the KCNH family of ion channels and key regulators of neuronal excitability (4,5,6,7,8,9). Gain-of-function mutations in EAG1 channels are associated with Zimmerman-Laband and Temple-Baraitser syndromes characterized by severe developmental abnormalities and seizures (6,7,8). While, under normal conditions, EAG1 channels are predominantly expressed in the brain, they are overexpressed in the majority of primary human tumors and inhibition of their activity decreases tumor progression (10,11,12,13,14,15,16). Therefore, EAG1 channel inhibitors have therapeutic potential for treatment of cancer and neurodevelopmental disorders.

Similar to other potassium channels, EAG1 channels are tetramers with each subunit containing the intracellular N-terminal Per-Arnt-Sim (PAS) domain, VSD formed by transmembrane segments S1–S4, pore-forming S5–S6 transmembrane segments, the C-linker connecting the intracellular cyclic nucleotide-binding homology (CNBH) domain to the S6 transmembrane segment, and a distal C-terminus that is missing in the cryo-EM structure (Fig. 1 A) (17). The centrally located pore of the channel is formed by the S5–S6 transmembrane segments of all four subunits (Fig. 1 B). EAG1 channels open in response to membrane depolarization and are characterized by a delayed opening from deep hyperpolarized potentials (18,19,20,21). The delayed opening can be visualized when EAG1 channels are first subjected to increasingly depolarized voltage prepulses followed by a voltage pulse to a high depolarized membrane potential, such as +40 mV, where the channels are in an open state (Fig. 1 C). As illustrated in Fig. 1, D and E, EAG1 channels reach the open state slower from deep hyperpolarized prepulse potentials than from more depolarized prepulse potentials. Although this phenomenon is frequently referred as the Cole-Moore shift, it is different from the original lag in potassium current development at hyperpolarized voltages observed by Cole and Moore, where the kinetics of current activation was the same for different voltages (18,22). With this caveat in mind, to stay consistent with the previous literature, we refer to the delayed opening from deep hyperpolarized potentials in EAG1 channels as the Cole-Moore shift. The mechanism of the Cole-Moore shift in EAG1 channels is not completely understood; however, it is thought that the delayed opening from the extreme hyperpolarized steps is due to the multiple closed states that the VSD in EAG1 channels has to transition from to reach the open state (18,22,23).

In KCNH channels, PAS domains interact with CNBH domains from adjacent subunits, forming an intracellular tetrameric PAS/CNBH domain ring located at the entrance to the pore of the channel (Fig. 1 B) (17,24,25). The rotation of the tetrameric ring is thought to regulate the opening and closing of KCNH channels (26,27,28). The PAS/CNBH domain ring also binds calmodulin, which inhibits currents from EAG1 channels (17,29,30). All of the known structures of EAG1 channels are bound to calmodulin (17,28,31). However, the frequency of EAG1/calmodulin complex formation and the relative fraction of calmodulin-free versus calmodulin-bound EAG1 channels under physiological conditions is not known. It has been shown that, despite structural similarity with the cyclic nucleotide-binding domains of hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-modulated channels, CNBH domains of EAG1 and homologous EAG-related gene (ERG) channels do not regulate channel activity by direct binding of cyclic nucleotides (32,33). Instead of cyclic nucleotides, the cyclic nucleotide binding pocket of KCNH channels is occupied by an intrinsic ligand, a conserved short stretch of amino acids of the CNBH domain itself (34,35,36). The intrinsic ligand is an important structural element for KCNH channel gating (37,38). In EAG1 channels, mutations in the intrinsic ligand have been shown to slow channel activation and shift the conductance-voltage relation to more depolarized potentials (23). Similar to EAG1 channels, mutations in the intrinsic ligand of EAG-like (ELK) channels shift the voltage dependence of activation to more depolarized potentials (34). Moreover, in ELK channels the intrinsic ligand undergoes dynamic rearrangements during voltage-dependent gating and is essential for voltage-dependent potentiation, a stabilization of channel opening by prior depolarization, observed in ELK channels (39). The intrinsic ligand is also essential for the PAS and CNBH domain interactions in ERG channels and mutations in the intrinsic ligand diminish these interactions in the full-length human ERG channels (40,41), although similar mutations had no effect on the interaction affinity between the isolated PAS and CNBH domains (42).

Recent studies show that the PAS domains of EAG1 channels can function as ligand-binding domains. Small-molecule ligands, such as tricyclic drugs chlorpromazine (CPZ) and imipramine, and undecylenic acid, directly bind to the PAS domains and inhibit EAG1 currents through this binding (43,44,45). How binding of the small-molecule ligands to the intracellularly located PAS domains can affect ionic current flow through the centrally located pore of the channel is not known and investigation of this mechanism was the primary motivation for this study. PAS domains of KCNH channels share structural similarity with PAS domains in non-ion channel proteins (46,47). While PAS domains from different proteins are structurally similar, they have low amino acid sequence conservation and are frequently targeted for drug design (47,48,49,50). It is highly likely that many more EAG1 PAS domain small-molecule binders will be identified in the future. Therefore, uncovering the mechanisms of EAG1 channel regulation by PAS domain small-molecule binders is important for understanding the fundamental steps in the channel gating and for development of novel therapeutics targeting EAG1 channels.

Here, we used a combination of molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, surface plasmon resonance (SPR), and network analysis to investigate allosteric pathways involved in EAG1 channel inhibition by CPZ. Our MD simulations and network analysis showed that CPZ binding to the PAS domain alters interactions between the PAS and CNBH domains. The CPZ-induced changes in the PAS and CNBH domain interactions in the full-length EAG1 channels predicted with the computational modeling were consistent with our SPR experiments, which indicated that CPZ affects interactions between the isolated PAS and CNBH domains. Our network analysis also indicates that CPZ binding to the PAS domain alters information flow (IF) between the intracellular PAS/CNBH domain ring, VSD, and pore of the channel. CPZ had little effect on the Cole-Moore shift in EAG1 channels, suggesting that the effect on the VSD domain predicted by the network analysis occurs at potentials more depolarized than the ones eliciting the Cole-Moore shift. The uncovered allosteric pathways of EAG1 channel inhibition by CPZ provide an initial framework for understanding ligand-dependent gating in EAG1 and other KCNH channels.

Materials and methods

MD simulations of full-length EAG1 channels

For simulating the full-length EAG1 channel in the CPZ-unbound (apo) and CPZ-bound states we first prepared an apo state full-length mouse EAG1 (mEAG1) model using the rat EAG1 (rEAG1) cryo-EM structure determined in the complex with calmodulin (PDB: 5K7L) (17). The apo state was prepared in the absence of calmodulin in CHARMM-GUI with 500 POPC lipids per leaflet, CHARMM36m parameters for the protein and CHARMM parameters for the lipids (51,52). The protein-membrane system was solvated with TIP3P water and Na+ and Cl− ions were added to a final concentration of 150 mM. To simulate the CPZ-bound state, CPZ was docked into the PAS domains of the apo state model stripped of calmodulin. Docking of CPZ to the PAS domain was performed with Rosetta Ligand Docking using the ROSIE server (53,54,55,56) with docking centered on the known PAS domain ligand binding pocket (46,47). The system for the CPZ-bound (holo) EAG1 was then prepared in the same manner as for the apo state. Both apo and CPZ-bound states embedded in membrane were equilibrated according to the CHARMM-GUI six-step procedure. During the equilibration phase, the temperature was controlled with a Berendsen thermostat and the pressure was maintained at 1 bar using the Berendsen barostat. For the production run, we used a Nosé-Hoover thermostat with a coupling constant of 1 ps−1 to maintain temperature at 310 K and a Parrinello-Rahman barostat with a compressibility of 4.5 × 10−5 bar−1 for the pressure. Triplicate simulations of apo and CPZ-bound systems were ran for 1 μs for each using OpenMM 8.0 as the engine and writing out trajectory frames every 100 ps.

Contact analysis between PAS and CNBH domain

To analyze CPZ-dependent changes in the distances between the interacting residues on the PAS and CNBH domains, we generated histograms for the minimum distances between the interacting residue pairs located on the PAS and CNBH domains of the adjacent subunits in apo and holo states for the triplicate 1 μs MD simulations. For this analysis, for each apo and CPZ-bound replicate, coordinates were scanned frame-by-frame and contact occurrence was evaluated between residues in the PAS and CNBH domains. Our MD simulations output frames every 100 ps of simulated time, generating 10,000 frames per 1 μs simulation. Since each simulation is for a tetramer that gives us 40,000 measurements for distances between the PAS and CNBH domains from the adjacent subunits per 1 μs simulation and 120,000 measurements for triplicate simulations for apo and holo EAG1 tetramers. Before the distance calculations, each trajectory was stripped of nonprotein atoms, such as water molecules and ions, and the remaining protein atoms were unwrapped to account for periodic boundary conditions and the EAG1 channel tetramer was centered in the simulation box for consistent alignment. The distance calculations were carried out by measuring the distances between the closest nitrogen (N) and oxygen (O) atoms for each intersubunit residue pair. Hydrogen-bond contacts were defined as interactions between N and O atoms at ≤3.5 Å, and salt-bridge contacts were defined as any interaction between O atoms on Asp/Glu side chains and N atoms on Lys/Arg side chains at ≤4.0 Å. Multiple atom–atom contacts between the same residue pair within a single frame were counted once, and contact frequencies were computed as the percentage of analyzed frames.

To identify contacts most altered by CPZ binding, we compared contact frequencies between the apo and CPZ-bound simulations for each residue pair forming either hydrogen bonds or salt bridges between that PAS and CNBH domains. For each contact, we computed the mean change in frequency between the holo and apo states (Δ value) across the three replicates, calculated the z-score and used a 95% confidence interval (CI), z-scores > 1.96, analysis for Δ to identify interactions between the PAS and CNBH domains that are significantly affected by CPZ binding. Contacts with the frequency outside the 95% CI were classified as significantly changed between the holo and apo states. The distribution of distances for these contacts for all of the analyzed frames (120,000) is shown in Fig. 3 C. The contacts that had a positive shift in the frequency between holo and apo states were reported as stabilized (strengthened) in the CPZ-bound state, while the ones that had a negative shift were reported as destabilized (weakened) in the CPZ-bound state.

Protein expression and purification

DNA encoding PAS (residues 7–136) and CNBH (residues 552–708) of mEAG1 channels (GI no. Q60603) was synthesized by BioBasic (Markham, ON, Canada) and subcloned into pETM11 bacterial expression vector containing an N-terminal 6-His affinity tag followed by a tobacco etch virus protease cleavage site. The DNA sequences were verified with sequencing by Genewiz (South Plainfield, NJ, USA) The PAS and CNBH domains were expressed in BL21 (DE3) Escherichia coli cells as previously described (42,44). The cells were grown at 37°C to an optical density at 600 nm of 0.6–0.8, induced with IPTG at 18°C overnight and harvested by centrifugation. The cells were resuspended in 150 mM KCl, 1 mM TCEP, 1 mM ABSF, 2.5 mg/mL DNaseI, and 30 mM HEPES (pH 7.5). Cells were lysed with an Emulsiflex-C5 (Avestin, Ottawa, ON, Canada). Insoluble protein was separated by centrifugation in a 45-Ti rotor at 30,000 rpm for 1 h at 4°C. The PAS and CNBH domains were purified by Ni2+ affinity chromatography using HisTrap HP column (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) and eluted on a linear gradient to 500 mM imidazole. The protein was further purified on a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 column (Cytiva) equilibrated with 150 mM KCl, 1 mM TCEP, 10% glycerol, and 30 mM HEPES (pH 7.5). The protein concentration was determined using a Bradford Protein Assay Kit (Pierce, Waltham, MA, USA).

The purified protein was stored at −80°C in aliquots and thawed immediately before the experiments. The molecular weight of the PAS and CNBH domains used in the study was verified on Coomassie blue-stained gels and with mass spectrometry at the Proteomics and Metabolomics Core Facility at Georgetown University Medical Center.

SPR measurements

All SPR binding experiments were performed on a CM5 chip (Cytiva) at 25°C using a Biacore T200 Instrument (GE Healthcare, Marlborough, MA, USA). The purified CNBH domains were immobilized on the CM5 chip using a standard amine coupling chemistry in the presence of 10 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.5) as the immobilization buffer (buffer used to directly dissolve ligands), as we described before (42). HBS-P buffer (150 mM NaCl, 10 mM HEPES, 0.05% [v/v] surfactant P20 [pH 7.4]) was used as the immobilization running buffer (buffer that runs in the background during immobilization). Purified PAS domains in the kinetics running buffer (150 mM KCl, 1 mM TCEP, 10% glycerol, and 30 mM HEPES [pH.7.5], supplemented with 0.05% Tween 20) were injected over the immobilized CNBH domains at 0.3, 1, 3, and 10 μM concentrations in triplicate for 60 s at a flow rate of 50 μL/min (association phase), followed by buffer-only injections for 150 s (dissociation phase), in the absence and presence of 10, 30, and 50 μM CPZ dissolved in the kinetics running buffer. Injection of glycine (pH 2.0) for 15 s was used to regenerate the chip surface. The SPR data were doubly corrected to eliminate the nonspecific binding by subtracting the SPR response to the blank (buffer-only) injections and also response to the control surface with no immobilized protein. The buffer for the blank injections contained CPZ at the tested concentrations to make sure that the observed increase in the SPR response for PAS/CNBH interactions is not due to the CPZ binding to the PAS domain but rather due to the effect of CPZ on the PAS/CNBH domain complex formation. CPZ displays nonspecific binding to the CM5 chip surface at concentration ≥100 μM. Therefore, the effect of CPZ was examined at concentrations ≤50 μM.

Results

CPZ alters interactions between the PAS and CNBH domains in EAG1 channels

To gain insight into the structural mechanism of EAG1 current inhibition due to CPZ binding to the PAS domain, we performed MD simulations of full-length EAG1 channels in the absence and presence of CPZ bound to the PAS domains of all four subunits. The mEAG1 model used for the MD simulations was built using the cryo-EM structure of full-length rEAG1 channels (PDB: 5K7L) solved in the complex with calmodulin bound to the intracellular PAS/CNBH domain ring (17). EAG1 channels are open at 0 mV, the condition aligned with the one used during the cryo-EM structure determination. Therefore, the VSD is expected to be in a depolarized conformation in the cryo-EM structure (17). However, since EAG1 channels are inhibited by calmodulin, the PD is expected to be in a closed state. For the MD simulations, we stripped the structure of calmodulin and embedded it into a lipid membrane (Fig. 2 A). The frequency of EAG1/calmodulin complex formation under physiological conditions is not known and it is likely that EAG1 is free of calmodulin at least part of the time in the native environment. Moreover, the interplay between EAG1 inhibition by calmodulin and CPZ has not been examined experimentally. Therefore, for the current study, we focused on investigating the effect of CPZ on EAG1 channels in the absence of calmodulin. Removing calmodulin is expected to have some impact on the EAG1 structure. However, since the root mean-squared deviation (RMSD) for individual replicates and averaged 1 μs simulations reached a plateau (Fig. 2, B and C), this suggests that the atomistic models used in our study correspond to an equilibrated structure of mEAG1 channels in the absence of calmodulin. The CPZ-bound (holo) full-length EAG1 model used for the MD simulations was generated by docking CPZ into the PAS domains of all four subunits of the full-length EAG1 apo model stripped of calmodulin. CPZ was docked inside the PAS domain because our previous SPR and fluorescence-based studies indicated that CPZ directly binds to the PAS domain, and our mutagenesis-coupled electrophysiology studies indicated that, by binding to the PAS domains, CPZ causes inhibition of currents through EAG1 channels (44). Moreover, the binding site used for CPZ docking is known to accommodate small-molecule ligands in structurally similar PAS domains in non-ion channel proteins (46,47). The generated apo and holo models contained the N-terminal PAS, VSD, and PD domains, the C-linker connecting the C-terminal CNBH domain to the S6 segment of PD, and the distal C-terminal region (Fig. 2 A). MD simulations were run at room temperature for 1 μs for both CPZ-unbound (apo) and CPZ-bound EAG1 models in the absence of a membrane potential. MD simulation trajectories have been deposited in the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/7238w). The RMSD for the domains of one of the channel subunits and whole subunit protein backbone for each replicate simulation, and the averaged RMSD for all four subunits and triplicate simulations for the duration of the simulations in the presence and absence of CPZ, are shown in Fig. 2, B and C. For the RMSD calculations the structural models for a full-length EAG1 subunit at a given time in the simulation and beginning of the simulation were globally aligned and the RMSD for the individual domains or whole protein backbone was calculated. The root mean-square fluctuations (RMSF) for the apo and CPZ-bound states for one of the channel subunits for each replicate simulation and averaged RMSF for all four subunits and triplicate simulations are shown in Fig. 2 D. The comparison of the RMSF plots between the apo and holo states of the channel suggest that there are differences in dynamic fluctuations in the PAS, VSD, PD, and distal C-terminal regions in these two states (Fig. 2 D).

Comparison of the H-bonds and salt bridges at the PAS and CNBH domain interface computed in the apo and CPZ-bound states indicated that CPZ binding increases the probability of forming some hydrogen bonds and salt bridges, while decreasing the probability of others (Fig. 3, A and B; Table S1). Histograms of the bond distances for the interactions that are strengthened or weakened in CPZ-bound (holo) states are shown in Fig. 3 C. In total, we identified seven residue-pair interactions between the PAS and CNBH domains that are strengthened with CPZ binding and five that are weakened (Fig. 3 C; Table S1). Not all bonds are formed or broken at the same time. For instance, in the pose depicted in Fig. 3 A only an N15-L664 bond is formed among the five residue-pair interactions that have higher probability of forming in the apo state. Notably, several of the identified contact residues participate in multiple interactions, including interactions that are strengthened and also interactions that are weakened by CPZ binding. For instance, L664 participates in two residue-pair interactions that are strengthened with CPZ binding (P13-L664 and T16-L664) and one residue-pair interaction that is weakened by CPZ binding (N15-L664). Similarly, Q62 participates in one residue-pair interaction that is strengthened by CPZ binding (Q62-V635) and one that is weakened by CPZ binding (Q62-T698). Taken together, the MD simulations indicate that CPZ binding causes a rearrangement of residue interactions at the PAS and CNBH domain interface.

CPZ strengthens interactions between the isolated PAS and CNBH domains of EAG1 channels

MD simulations suggest that CPZ binding may affect PAS and CNBH domain interaction affinity as some bonds at the PAS and CNBH domain interface are weakening and some are strengthening as a result of CPZ binding. Experimentally determining the total effect of CPZ binding on the affinity of PAS and CNBH domain interactions in the intact full-length EAG1 channels is not feasible. Previously, we have shown that SPR can be used to determine the affinity of the isolated PAS and CNBH domain interactions in ERG1 channels homologous to EAG1 (42). Therefore, to experimentally test if CPZ binding can affect interactions between the isolated EAG1 PAS and CNBH domains with SPR, we immobilized the isolated CNBH domains of EAG1 channels on the CM5 sensor chip and applied the isolated PAS domains over the range of concentrations without or with CPZ (Fig. 4). The experiments were repeated on three different CM5 chips. The SPR response increased with the increase in the PAS domain concentration in the absence and also in the presence of CPZ. To determine the interaction affinity between the PAS and CNBH domains, the SPR response profiles were fitted with the two-state reaction model, using Biaevaluation software, version 1.0, as we have described before (42,59). The association and dissociation rate constants determined from the fits over the range of the tested PAS domain concentrations for each of the three experiments are listed in Table S2. The rate constants were used to calculate the affinity of the PAS and CNBH domain binding. The averaged PAS and CNBH domain interaction affinity determined in the absence of CPZ was 2.5 ± 0.3 μM. This affinity is slightly higher than the affinity for EAG1 PAS and CNBH domain interactions of 13.2 ± 2.3 μM, determined using fluorescence anisotropy (25). The difference in the affinities is not surprising, as ligand binding affinities determined using different methods frequently differ (60). Increasing the CPZ concentration increased the PAS and CNBH domain interaction affinity determined with SPR in a concentration-dependent manner, from 2.5 ± 0.3 μM in the absence of CPZ to 0.9 ± 0.3 μM in the presence of 50 μM CPZ (Fig. 4; Table S2). Thus, the SPR experiments revealed that CPZ strengthened interactions between the isolated PAS and CNBH domains in a concentration-dependent manner.

CPZ alters IF between PAS/CNBH, VSD and PD domains in EAG1 channels

To inhibit ionic current flow through EAG1 channels, the conformational changes induced by CPZ binding to the PAS domain must be communicated to the PD via a network of residue-residue interactions. To shed light on the allosteric pathways involved in the transition between the apo and CPZ-bound states, we employed MD network analysis. Network analysis was recently used to investigate the coupling between the VSD and PD in the voltage-gated potassium channel KCNQ1 (57). For the network analysis, MD trajectories were converted to a residue interaction network, where each node corresponded to an individual amino acid residue within the full-length EAG1 channel. The weights on the edges (connection between nodes) were defined by the spatial proximity and correlated motions of residues in the MD trajectories generated with the MD simulations of atomistic models of the full-length EAG1 channels, as described above. The final network encoded all residues (nodes) and interactions (edges) in the channel. The allosteric pathway was measured by calculating the flow of information through the network by defining the PAS domain (binding pocket) as the source of information and the channel pore as the sink. We assumed that, to inhibit the ionic current flow through the EAG1 pore, perturbations of interactions between residues in the PAS domain induced by CPZ binding will have to spread via diffusion in the network to the residues in the PD. To identify the key residues and pathways, IF betweenness analysis was performed to account for all pathways between the source and sink residues (57,61). The averaged IF values for three MD simulation replicas for EAG1 channel residues for apo and holo states are shown in Fig. 5 A. In both apo and holo states, the IF analysis suggests that there is an allosteric connectivity between the PAS, VSD, PD, and C-linker/CNBH domains, as indicated by the increased IF for these regions.

To investigate how CPZ binding to the PAS domain influences the allosteric network, we calculated the difference in the averaged IF profiles between the CPZ-bound and apo states of the EAG1 channel (ΔIF = IF(holo) – IF(apo)) (Fig. 5 B). The statistical significance of ΔIF was evaluated using a 95% CI. Residues with statistically significant ΔIF are indicated as black dots in Fig. 5 B and mapped onto the structure of the full-length EAG1 channel, with residues with higher IF in the holo state shown as red spheres and lower IF in the holo state shown as blue spheres in Fig. 5 C. This analysis revealed that CPZ caused statistically significant changes in the allosteric connectivity between all major domains of the channel, including PAS, VSD, PD, and CNBH domains (Fig. 5, B and C). Some of the key residues known to be important for EAG1 and other potassium channel gating had altered IF due to CPZ binding. For instance, CPZ increased IF to R357, a conserved arginine in the S4 segment of VSD that contributes to the gating charge movement during the voltage-dependent gating of EAG1 channels (31). CPZ increased IF to G487, a conserved residue in many potassium channels thought to function as a “glycine gating hinge,” contributing to the conformational changes in the pore lining S6 segment during potassium channel gating (62). CPZ decreased IF to F691 located close to the intrinsic ligand of EAG1 channels, an important structural element for KCNH channel gating (34,36,63). It is important to note that our network analysis does not provide information on whether the changes in IF are associated with an increased inhibitory or activatory IF. In other words, increased IF to VSD indicates that the movement of the voltage sensor is affected but does not provide information on whether its movement is inhibited or facilitated by CPZ binding to the PAS domain. Taken together, the network analysis indicates that CPZ binding alters the allosteric coupling between the major domains in EAG1 channels. Future studies using fluorescence-based or cryo-EM methods should provide more insight into the specifics of the allosteric changes.

CPZ binding to the PAS domain does not affect Cole-Moore shift in EAG1 channels

The network analysis indicated that coupling between the VSD and PAS domains was affected by CPZ binding to the PAS domain (Fig. 5, B and C). To experimentally test the coupling between the VSD movement and CPZ binding at hyperpolarized potentials, we used the Cole-Moore shift that reflects slow activation of the channel from deep hyperpolarized states (18,26). The Cole-Moore shift is prominent in EAG1 channels (Fig. 1 E). Although the mechanism of the Cole-Moore shift is not fully understood, it is thought to reflect that, to reach an open state from an extreme hyperpolarized states, the VSD of EAG1 channels has to transition through multiple closed states (18,22,23). To determine if binding of CPZ to the PAS domain affects the VSD movement through the closed states, we recorded the Cole-Moore shift in the absence and presence of CPZ for EAG1 currents recorded with the TEVC in Xenopus laevis oocytes. The voltage protocol used for the Cole-Moore shift and elicited currents are illustrated in Fig. 1, C–E. Previously, we have shown that, for currents recorded with the TEVC, the IC50 for EAG1 current inhibition by CPZ is 30 μM (44). Therefore, the Cole-Moore shift was recorded in the absence (Fig. 6 A, black trace) and presence of 30 μM CPZ (Fig. 6 A, red trace). The Cole-Moore shift was further analyzed by plotting the averaged normalized currents recorded at 10 ms after the prepulse against the prepulse potentials (Fig. 6 B), as in (28), and time to reach 80% of maximal current at the end of the test pulse against the prepulse potential (Fig. 6 C), as in (20). The plots in Fig. 6 B were fit with the Boltzmann function to determine V1/2 —the prepulse potential that produces half-maximal Cole-Moore shift. CPZ shifted the V1/2 from −74.5 ± 0.7 to −72.4 ± 0.7 mV. Although statistically significant (p < 0.0004 by Student's t-test), the effect is small, indicating that CPZ does not substantially affect VSD movement between the deep hyperpolarized states to the open state.

We have shown that Y71 in the PAS domain restricts access of CPZ to the PAS domain cavity (43). Mutating Y71 to a smaller glycine or valine increases EAG1 channel inhibition by CPZ through binding to the PAS domain. Therefore, we tested if Y71G mutation can unmask the effect of CPZ on the Cole-Moore shift. Consistent with our previous findings, currents from Y71G mutant EAG1 channels showed higher inhibition by CPZ than the currents from WT channels (Fig. 6 D). Plots of the averaged normalized currents recorded at 10 ms after the prepulse against the prepulse potentials and time to reach 80% of maximal current at the end of the test pulse against the prepulse potential are shown in Fig. 6, E and F, correspondingly. Noteworthy, Y71G mutation shifted the V1/2 by ∼6 mV to more depolarized potentials relative to that of WT EAG1 channels, a small but statistically significant shift (p < 0.0001 by Student's t-test), and caused a small increase in the time to reach 80% of maximal current at the end of the test pulse (Fig. S1). PAS domain has been shown to play an essential role in generating the Cole-Moore shift in EAG1 channels (19,20,21,23,28). Therefore, the effect of the Y71G mutation on the Cool-Moore shift is consistent with this role of the PAS domain. Similar to WT channels, CPZ had small but statistically significant effect on the V1/2, which shifted from −68.9 ± 0.5 mV in the absence of CPZ to −66.9 ± 0.5 mV in the presence of CPZ (p < 0.0001 by Student's t-test). Importantly, the magnitude of the CPZ-induced shift in V1/2 was less than that for the WT channels, suggesting that increased EAG1 current inhibition by CPZ due to the Y71G mutation does not lead to the increased shift in V1/2. This further supports our conclusion that CPZ binding to the PAS domain does not substantially alter VSD transition from deep hyperpolarized states to the open state.

Discussion

In this study, we show that binding of small-molecule ligand CPZ to the PAS domain of EAG1 channels alters the interface between the PAS and CNBH domains and allosteric coupling between the major regulatory domains of EAG1 channels. Our MD simulations indicate that CPZ binding alters residue interactions between the PAS and CNBH domains. Consistent with this, SPR experiments showed that CPZ alters interactions between the isolated PAS and CNBH domains. Network analysis of the MD trajectories further indicated that CPZ alters allosteric coupling between the major regulatory domains of EAG1 channels, including the intracellular PAS, CNBH, VSD, and PD domains. CPZ had statistically significant but small effect on the Cole-Moore shift in EAG1 channels, suggesting that it does not substantially alter the movement of VSD at deep hyperpolarized potentials. Therefore, the effect of CPZ on the VSD coupling to the PAS, CNBH, and PD domains most likely occurs at potentials more depolarized than the ones eliciting the Cole-Moore shift. Taken together, our findings provide the first glimpse into the allosteric pathways involved in EAG1 channel regulation by small-molecule ligands targeting their intracellular PAS domains.

Our MD simulations on the full-length EAG1 channels and SPR experiments indicate that ligands, such as CPZ, can alter the interface between the PAS and CNBH domains in the intracellular tetrameric ring formed by these domains (Figs. 3 and 4). It has been suggested that opening of EAG1 channels is facilitated by a rotation of the tetrameric ring that leads to a rearrangement of the PD and opening of the channel (28). Indeed, comparison of the closed and partially open structures of EAG1 channels suggests that the opening of the channel is accompanied by a rotation of the intracellular domains (28). Moreover, it was proposed that stabilization of the interactions between the PAS, VSD, and CNBH domains by calmodulin inhibits currents from EAG1 channels (28). Further support for the theory that opening of KCNH channels is facilitated by the rearrangements of the PAS and CNBH domains is provided by studies on the ELK channels based on the combination of patch-clamp fluorometry and incorporation of a fluorescent noncanonical amino acid (39). Studies on ERG channels using conformational-sensitive antibodies also revealed alterations in the PAS/CNBH tetrameric ring assembly during the channel gating (64) and a more recent report, based on the combination of electrophysiology and MD simulations, also indicated rearrangements between the intracellular domains and rotation of the tetrameric PAS/CNBH domain ring during the ERG channel gating (27). Consistent with the changes in the PAS/CNBH domain interface during KCNH channel gating, cross-linking the PAS domain to the interacting regions affected the gating by favoring a closed state of the ERG channels (65). While it is not possible to extrapolate our SPR results for the isolated domains in the context of full-length channels, taken together, the MD simulations and SPR results indicate that binding of CPZ to the PAS domain alters the PAS and CNBH domain interface. The CPZ-induced changes in the PAS and CNBH domain interactions could affect the rotation of the tetrameric PAS/CNBH domain ring and the coupling of this rotation to the VSD and PD domains, inhibiting the channel opening. Combining the results presented in our study with the previously proposed gating rearrangements in KCNH channels, we propose a model for EAG1 current inhibition by CPZ binding to the PAS domain (Fig. 7). In this model, EAG1 channel opening is mediated by the rotation of the PAS/CNBH domain tetrameric ring facilitated by the VSD movement in response to membrane depolarization. CPZ binding to the PAS domain alters PAS and CNBH domain interactions (potentially stabilizing them), inhibiting the rotation of the tetrameric ring and favoring the closed state of the channel. Since calmodulin binds to the PAS/CNBH domain ring and inhibits EAG1 currents (66,67,68), it would be important to investigate if CPZ and calmodulin act through a similar mechanism in future studies.

Our network analysis suggests that CPZ binding also alters coupling between the tetrameric PAS/CNBH ring, VSD, and PD domains in EAG1 channels (Fig. 5). The exact nature of the alterations is not clear, however, since opening of the channels is thought to be mediated by the rotation of the tetrameric ring, either decoupling or reduced coupling between the tetrameric ring, VSD, and PD domains could lead to reduced opening of EAG1 channels, explaining CPZ-mediated EAG1 current inhibition. Interestingly, CPZ had very little effect on the Cole-Moore shift in EAG1 channels (Fig. 6). The Cole-Moore shift is thought to reflect the transition of the VSD between multiple closed states to reach an open channel state (18). In EAG1 channels, the Cole-Moore shift is mediated by the PAS domain via interactions with the VSD and CNBH domains (19,20,21,27,28). Deletion of the PAS and CNBH domains or swapping EAG1 PAS and CNBH domains with the corresponding domains of homologous ERG1 channels removes the Cole-Moore shift (28). Consistent with this, we also did not observe a Cole-Moore shift for EAG1 channels lacking the PAS domain, both in the absence and presence of CPZ (Fig. S2, A and B). Our finding that CPZ did not substantially affect the Cole-Moore shift in the WT EAG1 channels and had even smaller effect on the Cole-Moore shift in the mutant Y71G EAG1 channels with enhanced CPZ sensitivity suggest that CPZ does not substantially alter VSD movement at potentials eliciting the Cole-Moore shift. At first, it seems that this contradicts the findings of the network analysis that suggest that CPZ alters coupling between the major domains, including VSD. However, our MD simulations and network analysis were performed at 0 mV, or in the absence of a membrane potential, where the Cole-Moore shift is no longer observed. Taken together, the network analysis and electrophysiology results suggest that CPZ binding alters coupling between the PAS/CNBH ring, VSD, and PD at potentials more depolarized than the ones eliciting the Cole-Moore shift in EAG1 channels.

It is important to note the limitations and caveats of interpretations of our study. Many of our conclusions are based on computational modeling and would be strengthened if experimentally validated. However, an experimental validation might be challenging. Our MD simulations revealed an extensive network of hydrogen bonds and salt bridges between the PAS and CNBH domains affected by CPZ binding (Fig. 3). Disruption of individual bonds most likely will be insufficient to account for the integrated effect of CPZ on the network of interactions. Moreover, many of the residues in the network of interactions affected by CPZ binding contribute to both, interactions that are strengthened and interactions that are weakened by CPZ binding. Finally, individual residue mutations may allosterically affect neighboring bonds, further complicating the interpretation of the results. Therefore, due to the intricate and intertwined nature of the interaction network altered by CPZ, we feel that it will be futile to test the predicted interactions with electrophysiology coupled to mutagenesis. This is why, to experimentally validate that CPZ binding alters PAS and CNBH domain interactions, we employed SPR. Although, these experiments indicated that CPZ strengthens and, therefore, alters interactions between the isolated PAS and CNBH domains (Fig. 4), these conclusions may not be valid in the context of the intact full-length EAG1 channels as the relative orientation and interactions between the PAS and CNBH domains might be different from the ones for the isolated domains. It is even more difficult to experimentally validate the conclusions of our network analysis that revealed allosteric coupling between the major regulatory domains of EAG1 (PAS/CNBH ring, VSD, and PD) and CPZ-dependent alterations of this coupling. Perhaps future studies using fluorescence-based and/or cryo-EM methods could provide a validation for these findings.

In this study we focused on the allosteric pathways dependent on CPZ binding to the PAS domain of EAG1 channels. However, the PAS domain is not the only binding site in EAG1 channels for CPZ. Although the deletion of the PAS domain substantially decreases EAG1 current inhibition by CPZ, there is still a residual inhibition of EAG1 channels lacking the PAS domain (44). CPZ also inhibits ERG channels and it has been proposed that this inhibition is due to the CPZ binding inside the PD of ERG channels (69). Therefore, the residual inhibition by CPZ observed in EAG1 channels lacking the PAS domain could be due to the binding of CPZ inside the PD. There also could be additional binding site(s) for CPZ located in the extracellular regions of the channel and/or VSD, as these regions where shown to harbor binding sites for small-molecule ligands in potassium channels (70,71,72,73). Since our MD simulations and the network analysis were performed on EAG1 channels with CPZ docked only into the PAS domains, to fully understand the allosteric pathways of CPZ action in EAG1 it will be important to identify the additional site(s) of CPZ binding in EAG1 channels and to uncover the allosteric pathways associated with the CPZ action through these additional binding sites.

KCNH channels are the only known ion channels that contain a PAS domain (5,37). Although distinct in their amino acid sequence, structurally the PAS domains of KCNH channels share homology with an expansive family of PAS domain-containing proteins in which the PAS domains serve as small-molecule binding modules (46,47). Recent studies firmly established that PAS domains can function as ligand-binding domains in KCNH channels, providing a unique mechanism of current regulation for this family of potassium channels (43,44). It has been shown that the PAS domains of KCNH channels harbor binding sites for structurally distinct ligands, including tricyclic drugs, such as chlorpromazine and imipramine (43,44) in EAG1 channels, heme (74) in ERG3 channels, and undecylenic acid (45) in EAG1 channels. Considering the evolutionary function of PAS domains as ligand-binding modules and their suitability for drug targeting due to remarkably diverse amino acid sequences despite the structural conservation (50), identification and design of additional KCNH PAS domain ligands is imminent. This raises the importance of mechanistic understanding of KCNH channel modulation by the PAS domain ligands. Our study provides the first account of allosteric pathways involved in EAG1 current modulation by the PAS domain small-molecule ligands. Most likely similar mechanisms apply for EAG1 channel modulation by other tricyclic drugs, including imipramine. Future studies will determine if the pathways of regulation are conserved for other KCNH channels and PAS domain small-molecule ligands.
