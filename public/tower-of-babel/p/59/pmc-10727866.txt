Two Cases of Delayed Bullous Local Reactions to the ChAdOx1 Adenovirus Vector Vaccine against SARS-CoV-2
Authors: Eun Ji Hong, Hee Jung Yoon, Tark Kim, Ahrim Moon, Sul Hee Lee, Young Lip Park
Published: 2023

Dear Editor:

In the COVID-19 pandemic, mRNA vaccines (mRNA-1273, BNT162b2), as well as adenovirus vector vaccines (ChAdOx1, Ad26.COV.2), are currently approved in Korea. Although the efficacy and safety of these vaccinations have been demonstrated through many randomized controlled trials, unexpected adverse drug reactions (ADRs) have been reported in the real world as more people become vaccinated1. Herein, we report two cases of an unusual presentation of delayed bullous local reactions after the first dose of ChAdOx1.

Two female patients, one aged 64 years and the other aged 68 years, presented with a large annular erythematous patch with multiple tense bullae after the first ChAdOx1 administration (Fig. 1). No immediate local reaction occurred, except for mild tenderness. However, skin lesions developed at the injection site 10 days and 9 days after vaccination, respectively. The patients complained of severe itching, warmth, and mild swelling without any systemic symptoms, and there were no other cutaneous lesions on their bodies. They did not use any adhesive plasters or dressing materials at the lesion locations, and had no history of allergic diseases or ADRs. Chest X-rays, echocardiograms, and laboratory tests showed no abnormalities. Histopathological examination of the first case revealed a subcorneal blister and mild perivascular infiltrates composed of lymphocytes and eosinophils in the superficial dermis (Fig. 2). IgG, IgA, IgM, C3, and C4 were not detected in the immunofluorescence study. The patients were treated with ice bag application, antihistamines, and topical corticosteroids, and their skin lesions improved after 1 to 2 weeks.

Histopathological findings of skin biopsy specimens from a 64-year-old woman showed a perivascular lymphohistiocytic infiltrate with many eosinophils in the superficial dermis (A: H&E, ×100; B: H&E, ×400).

“COVID arm,” a delayed large injection site reaction following COVID-19 vaccines, is usually characterized by localized pruritus, an erythematous rash (spreading to the hands in severe cases), swelling, and warmth. According to the literature, it mostly occurs about 7 to 10 days after the first dose of mRNA-1273 vaccination, with minor, earlier reactions after the second administration1. Similar cases have been reported after the administration of BNT162b2 vaccines, so PEG 2000 used in mRNA vaccine production has been suspected to cause a delayed hypersensitivity reaction2.

Similar reactions have also recently been reported with ChAdOx1 vaccination, suggesting that COVID arm is not vaccine-specific3. Our two unique cases represent a severe form of “AstraZeneca arm” with bullous eruptions that can be misdiagnosed as cellulitis or autoimmune bullous disease. Interestingly, some cases of new-onset bullous pemphigoid have been reported after COVID-19 vaccination; therefore, when elderly patients present with a bullous eruption with severe itching, we need to distinguish it from autoimmune bullous diseases4.

The histological findings from a recent study described COVID arm as post-vaccine erythema annulare centrifugum based on the immunological reaction pattern5. However, our cases showed a subcorneal blister and superficial perivascular lymphocytic infiltration with many eosinophils, suggesting that the exact pathophysiological mechanism remains unclear. Unlike anaphylaxis or autoimmune bullous diseases, COVID arm is generally not a contraindication to subsequent vaccination because it is self-limited. Dermatologists should be aware of potential delayed local reactions to ChAdOx1, provide whole-body physical examinations and skin biopsies if necessary, and encourage completion of the vaccination schedule.
