Long-Term Safety and Efficacy of Dupilumab Treatment in Children Aged 6 Months to 5 Years with Severe Atopic Dermatitis
Authors: Amy S. Paller, Andreas Pinter, Lara Wine Lee, Roland Aschoff, Jacek Zdybski, Christina Schnopp, Faisal A. Khokhar, Amy H. Praestgaard, Ashish Bansal, Brad Shumel, Mike Bastian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfants and young children with severe atopic dermatitis (AD) have a high burden of disease with a strong impact on quality of life. Here we assess long-term efficacy and safety of dupilumab in pediatric patients aged 6 months to 5 years with severe AD.MethodsThis is a subgroup analysis of patients aged 6 months to 5 years enrolled in the ongoing LIBERTY AD PED open-label extension (OLE) study of dupilumab who had previously participated in the parent study LIBERTY AD PRESCHOOL part B and had severe AD (Investigator’s Global Assessment [IGA] = 4) at parent study baseline. Patients received weight-tiered dupilumab every 4 weeks (200 mg for patients weighing 5 to < 15 kg; 300 mg for patients weighing 15 to < 30 kg). Key endpoints included the incidence and rate of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs), the proportion of patients with a ≥ 75% improvement in Eczema Area and Severity Index (EASI-75) from parent study baseline, proportions of patients achieving IGA = 0/1 and IGA ≤ 2, and the proportion of patients with a ≥ 6-point improvement in Children’s Dermatology Life Quality Index (CDLQI) for patients aged ≥ 4 years or Infants’ Dermatitis Quality of Life (IDQoL) questionnaire for patients aged < 4 years.ResultsThis analysis included 121 patients, of whom 50 completed the week 104 visit. TEAEs were reported in 88% of patients; most TEAEs were mild or moderate and not related to treatment. Common TEAEs included upper respiratory tract infection, nasopharyngitis, and cough. Conjunctivitis events were reported in 19% of patients and were mild or moderate, with a median duration of 8 days. No conjunctivitis event led to treatment discontinuation, and most events resolved during the study. One drug-related event (severe urticaria) led to treatment discontinuation, but it was not serious and resolved over time. Serious TEAEs were reported in 17 patients (14%), including one drug-related pinworm event. No serious TEAE led to treatment discontinuation. By week 4 of the OLE study, patients who had received placebo in the parent study exhibited efficacy improvements comparable to patients who had received dupilumab. By week 104, 96% of patients achieved EASI-75 from parent study baseline, 27% of patients achieved an IGA score of 0/1 (clear/almost clear skin), 92% of patients achieved IGA ≤ 2 (clear skin to mild AD), and the least squares mean percent change in EASI from parent study baseline was − 89%. Additionally, 89% (23/26) of patients achieved a ≥ 6-point (clinically meaningful) improvement in CDLQI, and 100% (3/3) of patients achieved a ≥ 6-point improvement in IDQoL.ConclusionLong-term treatment with dupilumab for up to 2 years showed acceptable safety and sustained efficacy in signs, symptoms, and quality of life in patients aged 6 months to 5 years with severe AD, with a rapid improvement for patients who had received placebo in the parent study. Long-term safety in the OLE study was consistent with the short-term safety profile observed in the parent study, with no new safety signals detected. Key Points

Treatment with dupilumab for up to 2 years in patients aged 6 months to 5 years with severe atopic dermatitis (AD) was associated with a low incidence of drug-related and serious adverse events, with no new safety signals identified.Sustained improvements in AD signs, symptoms, and quality of life were observed. Patients who had received placebo in the parent study showed a rapid response to treatment by week 4 of the OLE study.Our findings support continuous, long-term use of dupilumab in infants and young children with severe AD.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is one of the most common inflammatory diseases in childhood, with 80–90% cases diagnosed before the age of 6 years [1–3]. Up to 15% of pediatric patients are estimated to develop severe AD, characterized by persistent skin lesions, intense itch, and a high susceptibility to skin infections [1, 4]. Early onset of AD and high disease severity are predictors for AD persistence beyond childhood and an increased risk of developing atopic comorbidities, such as food allergies, asthma, and allergic rhinitis [2, 5]. Severe AD can strongly impact a child’s quality of life, with sleep disturbances, emotional distress, and impaired family life [6, 7].

Topical corticosteroids are the first-line treatment for AD, but they are often ineffective at controlling severe AD [8–10]. Off-label systemic immunosuppressants are associated with long-term safety concerns [11, 12]. Dupilumab, a fully human monoclonal antibody that blocks signaling of interleukin (IL)-4 and IL-13, is currently the only systemic treatment approved for children as young as 6 months of age with severe AD in Europe and moderate-to-severe AD in the USA [13, 14].

Dupilumab has demonstrated sustained efficacy and an acceptable safety profile in pediatric patients aged 6 months to 5 years with moderate-to-severe AD in the 16-week, placebo-controlled LIBERTY AD PRESCHOOL trial [15] and in the subsequent long-term LIBERTY AD PED open-label extension (OLE) study for up to 1 year [16]. A subgroup analysis of LIBERTY AD PRESCHOOL demonstrated efficacy and acceptable safety of dupilumab in pediatric patients aged 6 months to 5 years with severe AD after 16 weeks of treatment [17]. In this article, we assess the long-term safety and efficacy of dupilumab in infants and young children with severe AD who participated in the 16-week LIBERTY AD PRESCHOOL trial and continued in the long-term OLE study for up to 2 years.

Methods

Study Design

LIBERTY AD PED-OLE is an ongoing, phase III OLE study of dupilumab in pediatric patients with AD, including infants and young children with moderate-to-severe AD who previously participated in the randomized, placebo-controlled, phase III LIBERTY AD PRESCHOOL clinical trial of dupilumab (parent study) [15]. The full inclusion and exclusion criteria of both the parent study and the OLE study for patients aged 6 months to 5 years have been previously reported [15, 16] and are summarized in Appendix S1 in the electronic supplementary material (ESM). Here, we present a subgroup analysis of the OLE study with patients aged 6 months to 5 years who had severe AD at parent study baseline, defined as having an Investigator’s Global Assessment (IGA) score of 4.

In the parent study, patients were randomized 1:1 to placebo or weight-tiered dupilumab (200 mg for body weight 5 to < 15 kg; 300 mg for body weight 15 to < 30 kg), plus low-potency topical corticosteroids, every 4 weeks for 16 weeks. The full study design, safety, and efficacy results of the parent study have been previously reported [15], including for a subgroup of patients with severe AD at baseline [17]. After the end of the parent study, the OLE study had a screening period of 4 weeks, in which patients were assessed for eligibility and did not receive dupilumab. The treatment period started immediately after the screening period (OLE baseline). In the treatment period, all patients received subcutaneous dupilumab every 4 weeks according to a weight-tiered regimen (200 mg for patients weighing 5 to < 15 kg, or 300 mg for patients weighing 15 to < 30 kg). The treatment period may last up to 5 years. This subgroup analysis presents results through week 104 of the OLE study treatment period (week 124 from parent study baseline), with a data cutoff date of February 21, 2024, for patients aged 6 months to 5 years with severe AD (IGA = 4) at parent study baseline.

Patients were permitted to use topical corticosteroids, oral antihistamines, or topical calcineurin inhibitors without restriction, but the use of other systemic medications for AD was prohibited, except as a rescue treatment. Quantitative measures of concomitant medication use were not recorded. Patients who achieved IGA = 0/1 (clear or almost clear skin) and maintained it continuously for 12 weeks after week 40 of the OLE study were discontinued from dupilumab. Treatment was reinitiated if patients regained IGA ≥ 2. Analyses of these data are ongoing and will be presented in a separate publication.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint in the OLE study was the incidence (number of patients per 100 patient-years, nP/100 PY) and rate (number of events per 100 patient-years, nE/100 PY) of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs). Key secondary endpoints were the incidence and rate of serious TEAEs, TEAEs related to treatment, and TEAEs leading to permanent treatment discontinuation. Key efficacy outcomes were the proportion of patients with a ≥75% improvement in Eczema Area and Severity Index (EASI-75) from parent study baseline, proportions of patients achieving IGA = 0/1 and IGA ≤ 2, least squares (LS) mean percent change in EASI from parent study baseline, and the proportion of patients with a ≥ 6-point improvement in Children’s Dermatology Life Quality Index (CDLQI) for patients aged ≥ 4 years or Infants’ Dermatitis Quality of Life (IDQoL) questionnaire for patients aged < 4 years. Additional efficacy outcomes included the proportion of patients with ≥ 50%/≥ 90% improvement in EASI (EASI-50/EASI-90) from parent study baseline, mean change in percent of body surface area (BSA) affected by AD from parent study baseline, mean percent change in SCORing Atopic Dermatitis (SCORAD) index from parent study baseline, mean change in sleep quality Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) from parent study baseline, and mean change in skin pain NRS from parent study baseline. Laboratory safety and biomarker analyses included mean and median changes in hematologic and serum chemistry parameters from parent study baseline.

All safety and efficacy outcomes were evaluated in the safety analysis set, which included all patients who received the study drug. Efficacy outcomes were assessed from parent study baseline. All analyses were descriptive, with no formal statistical hypothesis, and based on observed data only. No missing values were imputed.

Compliance with Ethical Standards

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the International Conference on Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice guideline, and applicable regulatory requirements. An independent data and safety monitoring committee conducted blinded monitoring of patient safety data. The local institutional review board or ethics committee at each study center oversaw trial conduct and documentation. All patients, or their parents/guardians, provided written informed consent before participating in the trial. Pediatric patients provided assent according to the ethics committee (institutional review board/independent ethics committee)-approved standard practice for pediatric patients at each participating center.

Results

A total of 121 patients who had severe AD at parent study baseline were enrolled in the OLE study (Fig. 1). Of these, 23 patients withdrew from the study prematurely, 113 completed the week 52 visit, 50 completed the week 104 visit, and 98 were ongoing at the time of database lock (February 21, 2024). Enrollment of patients occurred at different timepoints; therefore, a number of the ongoing patients at the time of database lock had not yet reached week 104 of treatment, resulting in a lower number of patients who completed week 104 than the number of ongoing patients. Serious TEAEs were reported in 17 patients (14.0%; 8.0 patients/100 PY), for a total of 21 events (9.9 events/100 PY) (Table 3). All serious events resolved over time (one with sequelae) and did not lead to treatment discontinuation (Supplementary Table 1, see ESM). One serious adverse event (pinworm) was judged by the investigator as being related to treatment; it was moderate in intensity, did not lead to treatment discontinuation, and resolved after 15 days.

Two patients (1.7%; 0.9 patients/100 PY) reported TEAEs that led to permanent drug discontinuation (Table 3). The events were mild panic attacks and severe urticaria (Supplementary Table 2, ESM). Urticaria was deemed related to the study drug by the investigator; it resolved over time and was not considered serious. Panic attacks were not considered related to treatment.

The 5 most frequently reported TEAEs by Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MeDRA) Preferred Term (PT) were upper respiratory tract infection (36 patients, 29.8%), nasopharyngitis (33 patients, 27.3%), atopic dermatitis (33 patients, 27.3%), cough (28 patients, 23.1%), and COVID-19 (23 patients, 19.0%) (Table 3). Conjunctivitis was observed in 23 patients (19.0%; 11.6 patients/100 PY), for a total of 49 events, including PTs conjunctivitis, allergic conjunctivitis, bacterial conjunctivitis, viral conjunctivitis, and atopic keratoconjunctivitis (Table 3 and Supplementary Table 3, ESM). Of these, 12 patients experienced mild conjunctivitis (9.9%; 5.6 patients/100 PY) and 11 patients experienced moderate conjunctivitis (9.1%; 5.3 patients/100 PY) (Supplementary Table 3, ESM). No conjunctivitis events were serious or severe, or led to treatment discontinuation. The median (interquartile range) duration of conjunctivitis events was 8 (5–17) days. Of the 23 patients with conjunctivitis, 7 patients experienced their first conjunctivitis event by week 16 of the OLE study, 8 patients between week 17 and week 52, and 8 patients beyond week 52, with 10 patients experiencing more than one conjunctivitis event throughout the study (see ESM, Supplementary Table 4 for more details). In the majority of patients, conjunctivitis events resolved over time; at the time of database lock, two patients (1.7%; 0.9 patients/100 PY) had ongoing conjunctivitis (Supplementary Table 3, ESM).

Injection-site reactions were reported in 4 patients (3.3%; 1.8 patients/100 PY) (Table 3) and were observed at weeks 1, 25, 33, and 61 of the OLE study (Supplementary Table 5, ESM). Skin infections (manually adjudicated, excluding herpes viral infections) were observed in 18 patients (14.9%; 8.8 patients/100 PY) (Table 3). Of the skin infection events, 6 occurred by week 16 of the OLE study, 4 occurred between week 17 and week 52, and 8 occurred beyond week 52. Herpes viral infections were observed in 17 patients, including herpes simplex (2 patients), eczema herpeticum (2 patients), oral herpes (5 patients), and varicella (10 patients) (Table 3 and Supplementary Table 6, ESM). All varicella events were primary varicella. The vaccination status of the patients is unknown; however, it should be noted that vaccination against varicella is not mandatory in the European countries where the study was conducted. Patients with varicella were from Poland (9 patients) and the United Kingdom (1 patient).

Hematologic counts (eosinophils, neutrophils, lymphocytes, platelets) and serum chemistry values (creatinine, total bilirubin, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, lactate dehydrogenase) were similar between patients who had received placebo and patients who had received dupilumab in the parent study, with no meaningful changes observed during the OLE study (Supplementary Figs 1–2, see ESM).

Discussion

In children aged 6 months to 5 years with severe AD at parent study baseline, treatment with dupilumab for up to 104 weeks in the OLE study was well tolerated and provided sustained clinical benefits in AD signs, symptoms, and quality of life, with a trend towards further improvement with continued treatment. The overall safety profile was consistent with long-term results previously obtained with children aged 6–11 years [18], adolescents [19], and adults [20], and in the 16-week parent study with the same age group [15, 17]. Adverse events that led to permanent drug discontinuation were infrequent, with only one event (severe urticaria) related to the study drug. This event was not considered serious and resolved after one day. Of the serious adverse events, only one (pinworm) was related to the study drug, but it resolved over time. No serious adverse events led to permanent drug discontinuation.

Conjunctivitis events were mild or moderate, and none led to treatment discontinuation, consistent with results observed in older children, adolescents, and adults [18–20]. Most conjunctivitis events resolved over time, with a median duration of 8 days. Injection-site reactions were rare, with no reported serious events, and no meaningful changes in hematologic or serum chemistry parameters were observed during treatment.

The exposure-adjusted incidence of skin infections was lower than in the parent study [21]. Skin infections are common in pediatric patients with severe AD, due to a compromised skin barrier and increased colonization by Staphylococcus aureus [22]. However, dupilumab does not target immunity defenses against bacterial, viral, or fungal infections and has been associated with a lower risk of non-herpetic skin infections compared with placebo, which may further improve with long-term treatment [21, 23]. No skin infections led to treatment discontinuation. Varicella was observed in 10 patients with unknown vaccination status, from countries where vaccination against varicella is not mandatory. Varicella infections may have a significant clinical impact on pediatric patients with severe AD, as an impaired skin barrier function is known to increase the risk of skin infections [22], which may lead to complications.

Vaccination against varicella is recommended, and in some countries mandatory for children without contraindications, with no identified safety concerns for children with AD [24, 25]. Regulatory labeling for dupilumab currently advises against the use of live vaccines during treatment; therefore, it is recommended to complete age-appropriate vaccinations prior to initiating treatment [26].

At the beginning of the OLE study, patients who had received placebo in the parent study had substantially worse disease control compared with patients who had received dupilumab, including improvements in AD extent and severity, and quality of life. By week 4 of the OLE study, patients who had received placebo in the parent study had similar scores as patients who had received dupilumab in all measured outcomes, suggesting a rapid clinical response to dupilumab even with severe AD. Efficacy scores were maintained consistently during the OLE study, corresponding to substantial long-term improvements in AD signs, symptoms, and quality of life. Notably, over 95% of patients who reached week 104 achieved EASI-75. Although only 26.5% of patients achieved IGA ≤ 1, corresponding to clear or almost clear skin, over 90% of patients achieved IGA ≤ 2 by week 104, indicating that the severity of AD decreased from severe to mild in most patients during 2 years of treatment with dupilumab. This outcome suggests that sustained therapy with dupilumab can significantly alleviate the burden of AD for patients and their families. Treatment with dupilumab can help achieve long-term disease control and may warrant earlier treatment consideration in guidelines and routine clinical practice for severe AD in infants and young children.

This is the first long-term analysis of dupilumab safety and efficacy in infants and young children with severe AD. However, several limitations should be considered. The non-randomized, non-controlled design of the study may lead to overestimation of treatment effects due to patient selection and the absence of a comparator group. Analyses were based on observed data without imputation, thereby reflecting outcomes among participants with available measurements at each time point; therefore, study attrition over time may influence the interpretation of long-term results and introduce potential bias. In addition, the low number of patients under 2 years of age (n = 8) and the predominantly White population may limit the generalizability of the results. Future studies including a broader age range and more racially diverse populations are needed to better understand the applicability of these findings across different demographic groups.

Conclusion

Continuous treatment with dupilumab for up to 2 years demonstrated a long-term acceptable safety profile and sustained efficacy in signs, symptoms, and quality of life in infants and young children with severe AD. Long-term safety in the OLE study was consistent with the short-term safety profile observed in the parent study, with no new safety concerns. Rapid improvements in efficacy scores were observed in patients who switched from placebo in the parent study to active treatment in the OLE study.
