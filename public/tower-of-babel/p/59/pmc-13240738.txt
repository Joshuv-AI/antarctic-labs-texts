Symptom management in cancer care: An evolutionary concept analysis for a digitally enabled collaborative framework
Authors: Wenjing Yu, Shuo Qiao, Xiaoli Wang, Xiaoman Wu, Xiao Yang, Lijuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAn evolutionary concept analysis was conducted to clarify the core attributes, antecedents, consequences, and distinctions of “symptom management”. Based on this analysis, an updated conceptual framework was developed to integrate digital health and collaborative care.MethodsThis study employed Rodgers’ evolutionary concept analysis method. Through a systematic search of the literature related to cancer symptom management in databases including Web of Science, PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, CINAHL and Chinese databases, a total of 23 studies that met the inclusion criteria were included in the conceptual analysis.ResultsResearch has identified six core attributes of symptom management, namely dynamism, continuity, multidimensionality, multi-stakeholder collaboration, diversity of intervention methods, and individual variability. Key antecedents (including patient self-management capacity, health literacy, professional assessment, and systemic support) and primary outcomes (such as improved quality of life, reduced symptom burden, as well as potential risks of overtreatment and inequity) have been clearly delineated. In addition, symptom management has been conceptually distinguished from related concepts such as “symptom control”, “palliative care”, and “self-management”. Furthermore, a novel paradigm for symptom management has been proposed, characterized by digital empowerment and multi-stakeholder collaboration.ConclusionsCancer symptom management is a multidimensional, dynamic, complex process requiring multi-professional collaboration. This conceptual analysis provides a clear definition and theoretical framework for the field, informing future research, intervention development, and clinical practice. In particular, a conceptual foundation is established for advancing the integration of digital health technologies and collaborative care models. Future research should focus on cross-contextual validation, standardization of measurement instruments, and the balanced integration of technological solutions with patient-centered care.Keywords: Symptom management, Cancer, Concept analysis, Digital health

Introduction

Cancer is the second leading cause of death worldwide, and its increasing incidence is associated with substantial health and economic burdens.1 Patients with cancer frequently experience multiple, complex, and persistent symptoms, and inadequate symptom management has been associated with declines in quality of life, reduced treatment adherence, and inefficient utilization of health care resources.2 Effective symptom management and the alleviation of suffering are central goals in oncology nursing and cancer care.3,4 Traditionally, symptom management has been defined as “a dynamic, multidimensional process of assessment, intervention, and evaluation aimed at alleviating or eliminating the perception of symptoms and improving patient outcomes.”5 However, with the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence, electronic patient-reported outcomes, and telemedicine technologies, this conventional definition is increasingly regarded as inadequate for contemporary clinical practice. Consequently, reconstructing a digitally enabled concept of symptom management has become an urgent necessity for contemporary clinical practice, rather than merely an academic exercise.

However, any discussion or reconstruction of the concept of symptom management must be grounded in a clear definition of “symptom” itself. Symptom is defined as a “subjective experience that reflects changes in an individual's biopsychosocial functioning, sensations, or cognition”.6 In contrast to signs, which represent objective indicators of disease, symptoms are highly individualized and multidimensional.7 They encompass not only physical sensations, but also psychological, social, and existential responses.8, 9, 10 The onset and trajectory of symptoms are influenced not only by the tumor and its treatment, but also by pre-existing health status, coping resources, social support, and cultural beliefs.11

With the further advancement of symptom science, the concept of symptom management has also attracted the interest of researchers. Symptom management is a fundamental component of high-quality cancer care, aimed at alleviating the substantial burden caused by multiple, complex, and often persistent symptoms.12 Despite its clinical centrality, the concept of “symptom management” within oncology and nursing literature remains conceptually fluid and inconsistently operationalized. This ambiguity reflects an underlying epistemological tension between a biomedical model that views symptoms as objective targets for eradication and a holistic nursing paradigm that conceptualizes symptoms as subjective, multidimensional experiences requiring ongoing interpretation and person-centered responses.13,14

The evolution of this concept itself reflects its complexity. The symptom management model proposed by the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF) represented a shift toward a more systematic framework by emphasizing the dynamic interplay between symptom experience, management strategies, and outcomes, while explicitly integrating psychosocial factors and patients’ subjective assessments.5,15 Dodd et al. subsequently incorporated self-management theory, thereby positioning patients and their families as active participants who contribute to management effectiveness through symptom recognition, self-regulation, and shared decision-making.6 More recently, contemporary health care transformations, characterized by the integration of patient-reported outcomes (PROs),16 the proliferation of digital health technologies,17 a shift toward collaborative care and self-management,18 have further expanded the scope of symptom management practice. In parallel, the concept has evolved from a static focus on isolated interventions to a dynamic process-oriented perspective, with an emphasis on interactions within symptom clusters and their longitudinal trajectories.19,20 However, the evolution of symptom management practices within the contemporary digital health care ecosystem was not originally contemplated during the development of these models, and the resulting structural incompatibility is thus established as the foundational basis for this conceptual analysis.

The ambiguity of concepts, coupled with rapid development, collectively constitutes the core challenge at present. On the one hand, the integration of precision medicine, artificial intelligence-based predictive models, and digital follow-up tools has situated symptom management within increasingly complex clinical decision-making processes.21,22 Ambiguously defined conceptual boundaries may result in heterogeneous research outcomes and poorly specified intervention objectives, thereby undermining the feasibility of evidence synthesis and clinical translation. Conversely, theoretical development is not only constrained, but the risk of conceptual misuse or oversimplification in practice is also significantly heightened, by variations in the conceptualization of symptom management across disciplines, including oncology nursing, medicine, psychology, and public health, coupled with an absence of clearly defined core attributes, antecedents, and outcomes.

Therefore, in the context of the rapid transformation in contemporary cancer care and research, a systematic conceptual analysis of “symptom management” has become both urgent and necessary. A conceptual analysis of symptom management in oncology could provide a coherent and actionable theoretical foundation for model development, outcome measure selection, and intervention design, thereby supporting high-quality research and patient-centered clinical practice. The objectives of this study are to: (1) define the core attributes of symptom management influenced by digital health; (2) examine its antecedents and consequences; (3) differentiate it from related concepts; and (4) propose a novel conceptual framework characterized by continuous, data-driven approaches, human-machine collaborative decision-making, and spatiotemporal care delivery, providing a theoretical foundation for the advancement of genuinely digitally empowered cancer symptom management.

Methods

This study used concept analysis to systematically review and delineate the core concept of “symptom management” in oncology. The purpose was to synthesize diverse perspectives and uses of the concept in the existing literature, clarify its meaning and boundaries, and examine its applicability to the caring sciences, thereby providing a foundation for subsequent theory development and practice.23,24 Concept analysis emphasizes the development and clarification of concepts as mental constructs; through the systematic organization and appraisal of relevant information, it makes patterns and shifts in concept use explicit and thus supports the advancement of disciplinary knowledge. Given that symptom management is strongly influenced by context, professional development, and sociotechnical factors, and that its meaning may be altered by evolving health care models, information technologies, and nursing roles, Rodgers’ evolutionary concept analysis was adopted as the methodological framework for this study. This inductive approach conceptualizes concepts as dynamic and context dependent, comprising a constellation of interrelated attributes. The analytic process seeks to elucidate these attributes and their relationships, while recognizing that concepts may evolve cyclically across settings and over time.25

Rodgers and Knafl outline seven main activities in the evolutionary method: (1) Identify the concept of interest, any surrogate terms and uses of the concept in literature; (2) identify the appropriate sample for data collection; (3) collect the data; (4) analyze the data to identify characteristics of the concept; (5) identify references to the concept, its antecedents and consequences; (6) identify any related concepts; and (7) identify implications that may guide further development of the concept.26 Through this process, the study aimed to produce a clearer, more operational, and context-sensitive conceptualization of oncology-related symptom management while preserving the concept's openness and capacity for ongoing evolution.

Data sources and sample

The data sources for the conceptual analysis were restricted to peer-reviewed articles published in academic journals. Searches were conducted from database inception to April 2026 across Web of Science, PubMed, Excerpta Medica Database (Embase), Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Data, and Chinese Sci-Tech Journal Database (VIP). Supplementary searches were conducted via Google Scholar and manual retrieval. The retrieval strategy was defined as (“symptom management” OR “symptom self-management” OR “symptom control” OR “symptom manag∗”) AND (“oncology” OR “cancer” OR “neoplasm∗” OR “malignan∗”). For the Chinese database search strategy, see Supplementary Table S1.

Articles were included if they (1) were published in peer-reviewed journals; (2) explicitly addressed the concept of symptom management, symptom self-management, or symptom control; (3) were conducted within the context of oncology or cancer care; (4) at least one digital health component was explicitly incorporated into the definition, model, intervention, or discussion of symptom management; and (5) were published in either Chinese or English. No temporal restrictions were applied in order to capture the historical evolution of the concept. Articles were excluded if they (1) consisted of dissertations, workshop reports, conference proceedings, or editorials/commentaries lacking empirical data or theoretical frameworks; (2) focused solely on biological mechanisms of symptoms without addressing management strategies; (3) were pharmacological trials focused exclusively on drug efficacy rather than the holistic process of symptom management; or (4) were duplicate records.

Data analysis

All included literature underwent initial review and rereading by the first author. The first author conducted content analysis using Braun and Clarke's thematic analysis method to extract and synthesize data.27 Information from all included literature was systematically organized according to the components of Rodgers' methodology to identify alternative terminology, attributes, antecedents, consequences, and related concepts pertaining to symptom management.26 The classification of research terms was based on authors' use or formulation of definitions proposed by Rodgers and Knafl. Alternative terms expressed the conceptual essence through different vocabulary; related terms shared commonalities with the concept but differed in characteristics; antecedents referred to past events or phenomena associated with or triggering the expression of the research concept; and attributes reflected the concept's defining features. To ensure accuracy, each literature piece underwent a final rereading. During manuscript drafting, literature excerpts were frequently referenced to aid articulation. Thematic data were presented in exemplar structures encompassing all elements of symptom management. Co-authors reviewed thematic data and provided feedback, which informed the final analysis and exemplar structures. The data included can be found in the supplementary materials.

Results

Search results

A total of 1885 publications were initially included. After removing duplicates, excluding conference abstracts and book reviews, screening the titles and abstracts, and then reading the full texts, 87 studies remained. Following Rodgers' recommendation to sample approximately 20% of the available literature for concept analysis, the top 20% of the 87 eligible articles by citation count were selected. Five additional recent studies were added to capture the most current developments, resulting in a final sample of 23 studies. Consequently, the 87 articles were ranked by citation count from highest to lowest to identify the high-impact papers most frequently cited in subsequent research. Ultimately, the top 20% of articles by citation count were selected, totaling 18 papers. To broaden the research perspective and ensure the evidence remained current, five additional studies published between January 2025 and April 2026, authored by researchers not appearing in the aforementioned 18 studies, were included. Consequently, a total of 23 studies were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table S2).

Attributes

From this analysis, six unique defining attributes were identified to characterize instances of symptom management: dynamism, continuity, multidimensionality, multi-stakeholder collaboration, diversity of intervention methods, individual variability (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table S3).

The antecedents, attributes and consequences of Symptom management.

Dynamism

The “dynamism” of symptom management reflects its nature as a continuous, adaptive process rather than a one-off activity. As highlighted by Uçgun and Ebru Akgün Çitak, management strategies must remain flexible to accommodate different treatment phases.28 For example, VR applications transition from pre-treatment education and anxiety management to per-treatment pain distraction, and finally to post-treatment emotional rehabilitation. Because effective management follows a cyclical “assessment—intervention—reassessment—adjustment” model, health care professionals must adopt forward-thinking and systematic strategies. Integrating this dynamic approach throughout the disease trajectory is essential for achieving the goal of patient-centered care.2,5,6,29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36

Continuity

The second attribute of symptom management is continuity, which emphasizes the need to focus on the entire disease course through long-term follow-up and monitoring. Rha et al. employed the SMILE system to enable patients to repeatedly assess symptoms at baseline and prior to each chemotherapy cycle, thereby achieving longitudinal, cross-cycle tracking of symptom trajectories.36 Both quantitative and qualitative research consistently indicates that symptom management must prioritise long-term follow-up and sustained attention. Quantitative studies incorporate multiple follow-up visits in their design to systematically track symptom changes and intervention outcomes.37, 38, 39 Concurrently, qualitative research further corroborates that patients universally expect health care professionals to provide long-term, sustained monitoring and attention to their symptoms.2,29,30,35 This approach helps alleviate symptom-related discomfort and enhances quality of life.

Multidimensionality

The attribute of multidimensionality was emphasized in 15 articles. Research indicates that effective symptom management in cancer patients requires moving beyond a narrow focus on physiological indicators to comprehensively incorporate dimensions such as psychological and social functioning. This holistic approach has been shown to more effectively alleviate symptom burden and enhance quality of life.40 For example, studies across diverse cancer populations demonstrate that symptoms including sleep disturbances, fear, and vomiting adversely impact emotional well-being.41 Feelings of loneliness were frequently reported, alongside a commonly expressed desire for earlier access to psychological support.42,43 Consideration must also be given to the broader impact of symptoms on patients’ social roles, family relationships, daily activities, occupational functioning, and social engagement.

Multi-stakeholder collaboration

The fourth key attribute is multi-stakeholder collaboration. The multidimensional nature of symptoms necessitates a dual approach to symptom management, employing both pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments, and typically requires multidisciplinary collaboration involving doctors, pharmacists, physiotherapists, psychotherapists, and others. Importantly, this collaboration extends beyond health care professionals to include patients’ family members and friends, who often play an active role in observing symptoms, initiating assessments, and facilitating communication with health care providers.44, 45, 46 For example, family members may notice visible changes such as weight loss and seek nutritional support, or arrange routine tests like hemoglobin checks to detect treatable anemia. While such involvement is often perceived as supportive, it can also sometimes heighten patient distress if not sensitively handled. Research by Katarina Karlsson et al. further supports that effective symptom management relies on this broad, coordinated engagement across both professional and personal support systems.47

Diversity of intervention methods

Diversity of intervention methods is identified as the fifth key attribute. Rather than being conceptualized as a singular model, symptom management is structured as an integrated system wherein approaches led by health care professionals, digital technology support, patient self-management, and the integration of online and offline modalities are combined. Different disease stages, clinical scenarios, and patient requirements are accommodated by this comprehensive framework. For instance, real time data driven insights are provided by electronic monitoring systems based on patient reported outcomes, while psychosomatic symptoms are effectively alleviated by customized digital programs and virtual reality technologies through multidimensional support and immersive education.48, 49, 50 Positioned as a multifaceted clinical adjunct, digital technology serves a dual function: it is designed to facilitate the collection, analysis, and prediction of real-time symptom data, while concurrently providing tailored multidimensional support. However, the implementation of digital health technologies may inadvertently exacerbate feelings of alienation.51 Consequently, the provision of empathetic, humanistic nursing care, integrated with professional clinical oversight, must be maintained as an indispensable core driver of health care delivery. The efficacy of clinical interventions and patient autonomy are optimally enhanced when the centrality of humanistic nursing is reinforced, rather than overshadowed, by technology-driven solutions.

Individual variability

Individual variability are the sixth attribute identified. Patients' requirements for symptom management are frequently influenced by a variety of factors, including variations in disease and treatment regimens, demographic and sociocultural considerations, psychological and cognitive characteristics, as well as individual preferences and values.36,38,43,52, 53, 54 Samuel et al.'s research revealed that among breast cancer patients, women of different ethnicities exhibit distinct needs regarding symptom management.2 Moreover, patients' socio-economic resources may also influence their engagement with and access to symptom management.

Antecedents

Antecedents refer to the conditions or circumstances that set the stage for a concept to emerge. These are the factors that occur prior to the phenomenon or serve as instances from which the concept can arise.25 For patients, the ability to recognize symptoms early, implement self-management strategies, and utilize technological tools such as health applications or wearable devices are core elements of effective symptom management.30,33,39,55 Furthermore, factors such as health literacy, cultural background, and emotional state exert a decisive influence on the effectiveness of symptom management.2,47 These individual characteristics are often shaped by broader sociocultural contexts, wherein patients’ understanding of their condition and willingness to engage in self-care significantly impact treatment outcomes. Health care professionals also occupy a central role in symptom management. Their ability to effectively utilize health care systems, identify symptom clusters, and deliver personalized interventions constitutes a critical prerequisite for successful management.34,38,47,55 Their communication skills, cultural competence, and patient-centred approaches enhance collaboration, ensuring patients receive the support necessary for effective symptom management.2,35,36

Furthermore, effective symptom management is fundamentally predicated upon systemic and contextual supports, including technological resources, policy backing, and structured patient education materials.2,31,39 Health care systems integrating symptom monitoring tools, implementing patient education policies, and promoting health technology adoption furnish the necessary infrastructure for collaborative symptom management between patients and health care providers (Fig. 2).

Consequences

In the context of this concept, consequences are defined as the outcomes that emerge following its implementation (Fig. 2).

Positive consequences

Empirical research consistently demonstrates that implementing systematic and proactive symptom management for cancer patients yields significant clinical benefits. Notably, these interventions significantly enhance the quality of life related to health.2,6,29,30,34 By identifying and addressing adverse events at an early stage, such systematic approaches prevent symptom exacerbation and reduce severe distress. Furthermore, effective symptom management has been proven to optimize health care resource utilization by decreasing preventable emergency department visits and hospital readmission rates.29,30 This structured monitoring provides objective longitudinal data that establishes a clear foundation for executing dynamic and proactive symptom management. Simultaneously, effective symptom management fosters communication centered on the patient, thereby enhancing patient autonomy and advancing shared decision mechanisms in oncology care.

Negative consequences

The negative consequences and their corresponding mitigation strategies are primarily manifested across three levels. At the patient level, a lack of effective guidance and feedback for frequent reporting can easily diminish treatment adherence, exacerbate symptom burden, and trigger trust issues and feelings of isolation.56 Therefore, a closed-loop, personalized feedback mechanism must be established to ensure timely responses and emotional validation.

At the clinical level, massive volumes of unintegrated electronic data significantly increase the cognitive load of health care personnel and exacerbate burnout. To address this, intelligent systems should filter high-risk reports to reduce redundancy, while EHRs must integrate structured, visual symptom data for rapid decision-making.

At the systemic and economic level, restricted by internet connectivity, digital literacy, and socioeconomic disparities, older adults and rural populations face higher barriers to usage, thereby exacerbating health inequalities.57 To ameliorate this, equitable participation for all patients must be safeguarded by establishing public electronic terminals, retaining non-digital channels, and adopting accessibility design principles.

Surrogate and related terms

Surrogate terms

Symptom control

Symptom management and symptom control are often used interchangeably in oncology and nursing literature. In many studies, researchers substitute the term symptom control for symptom management to describe efforts aimed at alleviating or eliminating patient symptoms through pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions. Symptom control primarily emphasizes symptom severity and clinical efficacy, focusing on the effectiveness of specific interventions.58 Its objective is to reduce symptom burden and achieve quantifiable outcomes, rather than to elucidate the broader process of responding to symptoms. The present analysis adopts the term symptom management to underscore the dynamic and participatory nature of symptom-related care, extending beyond the pursuit of symptom relief alone.

Palliative care

Palliative care is often conflated with symptom management; however, the two differ fundamentally in scope, objectives, and underlying philosophy. Symptom management can be conceptualized as an effective clinical model primarily focused on addressing specific problems. Palliative care, by contrast, constitutes a more fundamental and integrative approach to holistic patient care and is formally recognized as a medical subspecialty. Its core objective extends beyond symptom control to the proactive and comprehensive enhancement of overall quality of life for patients and their families facing serious illness.59 While symptom management remains one of its core competencies, palliative care simultaneously encompasses additional dimensions, including advance care planning, alignment of goals between clinicians and patients, family conferences, the management of spiritual distress, the facilitation of social support networks, and bereavement care.60 In other words, while symptom management excels at “resolving problems caused by illness”, palliative care emphasizes “nurturing the person living with illness” across the entire disease trajectory. Consequently, palliative care should not be regarded as a synonymous extension of symptom management, but rather as a higher-level conceptual framework centered on quality of life, within which symptom management is embedded and subsumed.

Symptom management is grounded in a broader conceptual framework that recognizes symptoms as subjective and context-dependent experiences. It emphasizes integrated and ongoing strategies that involve collaborative participation among patients, families, and health care professionals. Consequently, although these alternative concepts share a common goal of alleviating patient suffering, they differ in scope and conceptual depth, with symptom management offering a more comprehensive and process-oriented understanding of symptom-related care (Supplementary Table S3).

Related terms

Symptom assessment

Effective symptom management begins with thorough assessment, which serves to identify the nature, frequency, severity, and functional impact of a patient's symptoms.38 While assessment provides a necessary snapshot of the patient's condition at specific intervals, management involves the continuous delivery of interventions throughout the course of the illness. If assessment results are not integrated into ongoing clinical management processes, as exemplified by instances where high symptom scores reported by patients are neither reviewed nor acted upon by the clinical team, feelings of neglect may be engendered among patients, whereby the intrinsic value of the assessment is significantly undermined.54 This ongoing process requires clinicians to regularly re-evaluate the patient and refine treatment strategies as the clinical picture evolves (Supplementary Table S3).

Self-management

Self-management focuses on the proactive role of patients and their capacity to self-regulate when dealing with symptoms. It involves the ability to monitor and interpret changes while applying daily strategies to lessen the symptom burden.43 Within oncology and chronic disease care, self-management is a key element of symptom management that promotes patient autonomy and maintains care continuity outside of clinical settings.53 However, because self-management is primarily based on patient-directed behaviors, it does not encompass the specialized activities performed by health care providers, such as professional assessment, clinical judgment, and structured collaborative interventions35 (Supplementary Table S3).

Symptom experience

Symptom experience is generally defined as patients' subjective perceptions, cognitive appraisals, and emotional responses to symptoms, underscoring their impact on physical and psychological well-being.34 The concept conceptualizes symptoms as subjective and context-dependent phenomena, reflecting individuals’ unique experiences within the broader contexts of illness and daily life.31 Whereas symptom experience focuses on the subjective process of living with symptoms, symptom management is concerned with sustained, goal-directed actions undertaken in response to that experience (Supplementary Table S3).

Symptom cluster management

The core characteristic of symptom clusters is that multiple coexisting symptoms often share a common biological mechanism or exhibit mutually reinforcing synergistic effects.61 Consequently, symptom cluster management is implemented at the level of these shared mechanisms or is directed toward prioritizing the treatment of core warning symptoms. Compared with traditional symptom management, symptom cluster management is regarded as an evolutionary shift, representing a transition from the isolated treatment of individual clinical manifestations to the holistic regulation of complex symptom networks. Fundamentally, this paradigm is considered to represent a natural extension and evolution of the concept of symptom management and is intended to better address the modern era of multimorbidity (Supplementary Table S3).

A conceptual framework for digital symptom management

Based on the aforementioned analysis, a collaborative framework for digital symptom management was developed in this study (Fig. 3). A closed-loop trajectory from “Capabilities” and “Enablers” to “Collaborative Outcomes” is delineated by the model, illustrating how the paradigm shift from traditional provider-led care to a participatory model is driven by digital transformation. The fundamental drivers underpinning digital symptom management are explicitly elucidated within this model. Patient-side capabilities are fundamentally anchored in digital health literacy. This literacy is defined as the capacity to process, communicate, and utilize health information within digital environments in a goal-driven manner, whereby knowledge is translated into practical action.62 Practical manifestations of this capacity are demonstrated through self-monitoring, real-time symptom reporting, and active engagement in care. Conversely, health care provider capabilities are centered on professional responsiveness, which encompasses the accurate interpretation of multidimensional data, remote assessment, and timely interventions informed by data insights. The alignment of these bidirectional capabilities is regarded as an essential prerequisite for effective digital collaboration.

Symptom management and digital health technologies.

Such collaboration is enabled by a robust digital infrastructure. For instance, vital signs data are captured via smart sensing devices, information sharing is facilitated through integrated electronic health record (EHR) systems, and data streams are processed through AI-based analytics tools and telemedicine platforms. These technological modalities serve as a conduit for clinician-patient communication, thereby mitigating temporal and spatial constraints.

Ultimately, this framework translates into tangible clinical value. Within this framework, digital tools not only enhance the immediacy of communication but, more fundamentally, support shared decision-making between clinicians and patients. Through coordinated care and follow-up, patients are enabled to transition from passive symptom bearers to active managers of their own health.

Model case

Based on the attributes of the concept of symptom management, we have constructed a case study. This case effectively illustrates the core principles of modern symptom management (Fig. 4). Dynamic and continuous components are integrated, as reflected in adaptive rather than static management strategies. Facilitated by the mHealth platform, these strategies are continuously adjusted in real time. These adjustments are strictly dictated by daily symptom data and the specific recovery phase of the patient. This recovery trajectory encompasses the transition from hospitalization to home care. Consequently, a seamless care continuum across both temporal and care settings is successfully established. Multidimensional assessment is intrinsically linked to multidisciplinary collaboration. Nursing interventions address both physiological symptoms, such as pain and fatigue, and psychosocial dimensions, including cognitive misconceptions and emotional distress. Concurrently, the patient, nurses, technical support personnel, and follow-up teams collectively form an integrated collaborative care network. Within this network, a diversity of intervention approaches is reflected in the integrated application of structured assessment, individualized education, digital technologies, and self-management toolkits. All practices are grounded in respect for individual variability, with the entire management plan tailored to surgical characteristics, technology acceptance, and personal beliefs. Discussion

The six attributes identified in this analysis, namely dynamism, continuity, multidimensionality, multi-stakeholder collaboration, diversity of intervention methods, and individual variability, collectively advance the conceptualization of symptom management beyond earlier theoretical models. The classic UCSF Symptom Management Model articulated a seminal triad comprising symptom experience, management strategies, and outcomes. The present findings both validate and further elaborate the interactive nature of this model, while extending it by explicitly foregrounding the temporal and adaptive dimensions of symptom management, reflected in dynamism and continuity, as well as the structural and contextual layers associated with multi-stakeholder collaboration and diversity of intervention methods. Furthermore, this analysis challenges purely linear or provider-centric applications of the model by positioning patients and their social networks as active co-agents within the symptom management process. This shift is consistent with the symptom management theory,6 in which self-management is more fully integrated into the core conceptual model.

This conceptual analysis demonstrates that symptom management is a highly context-dependent concept. Despite frequent use in oncology, substantial variation in interpretation is observed across research and clinical contexts. Furthermore, the concept is frequently conflated with constructs such as symptom control and palliative care. This conceptual ambiguity extends beyond semantic overlap. More fundamentally, divergent disciplinary epistemologies regarding the nature of a “symptom” are reflected. In the medical paradigm, symptoms are predominantly conceptualized as objective manifestations of disease or treatment requiring control.63,64 Conversely, within the nursing paradigm, symptoms are framed as subjective experiences situated within the individual's life context. This includes psychological and existential dimensions that require interpretation, assessment, and ongoing response.15,65 Alignment is found with the growing emphasis on psycho-oncology. Here, symptom management is extended beyond physical control to encompass the “whole-person” experience. Often “hidden” symptoms, such as anxiety, depression, and fear of recurrence, are thus addressed.66 This epistemological divergence is directly reflected in clinical practice. Consequently, implementation settings and intervention logic are influenced, and the respective roles of different disciplines are shaped. A high degree of contextual diversity is exhibited by the practice of symptom management itself. Encompassed settings include oncology acute wards, outpatient clinics, community and home-based care, palliative care services, and cancer survivor management programs.67 Nevertheless, the health care setting remains the core domain for both conceptual development and practical application.

Symptom management is recognized as a multidisciplinary collaborative endeavor involving physicians, nurses, pharmacists, and other allied health professionals. However, it is consistently indicated in the literature that nurses are typically the primary implementers.68,69 This pivotal role is not merely attributable to the frequency of nurse-patient contact but is determined by the core principles of nursing science. Nurses are positioned as the vital link between the patient's subjective experience and the objective treatment plan by their unique role in continuous assessment, care coordination, and supportive relationships.70 This alignment is supported by symptom management theory. The nurse's ongoing coordinating role in symptom assessment, intervention, and reassessment is emphasized by this theory.5 Both clinical necessity and disciplinary theory are reflected by the pivotal role of nurses. Consequently, the nursing profession's responsibility to clarify the conceptual boundaries, core attributes, and key elements of symptom management within cancer care is underscored. Finally, the practical logic of symptom management is being fundamentally reshaped by the rapid penetration of digital technologies. Entirely new challenges are thus presented to the traditional role of nurses.

The collection and preliminary classification of symptom data are increasingly automated by artificial intelligence and algorithms. Consequently, the nursing role must be redefined from traditional “data collectors” to “data interpreters” and “emotional supporters”. A fundamental prerequisite for this role transition is the acquisition of advanced digital health literacy by nursing professionals. In clinical practice, this advanced literacy dictates that nurses be equipped to critically evaluate algorithmic outputs, distinguish between false alarms and actual clinical deterioration, and identify potential biases in automated symptom triage.71 Although technology is frequently presented within the literature as a solution to alleviate work pressure, the risk of nurses being relegated to passive screen watchers who merely process alerts rather than care for patients is significantly exacerbated in the absence of deliberate role redefinition and targeted literacy training. As noted by Low,72 the transformation of raw, continuously collected sensor data into clinically meaningful “digital biomarkers” is necessitated by multidisciplinary collaboration. This requires both clinical and technical expertise. Furthermore, amidst the continuous evolution of digital health technologies, nursing value is no longer derived from the routine collection of metrics, but rather from the contextualization of these digital insights within the unique psychosocial reality of each patient. Consequently, assurance must be provided that technological efficiency does not supplant the essential human touch inherent in psychosocial oncology and holistic care practices.

The evolution of symptom management has been contributed to by advancements in digital health technologies. A gradual shift from provider-led models toward more collaborative patient-provider approaches is supported. Dynamic, real-time, and personalized symptom management plans are facilitated by digital systems. This is achieved by integrating patient-reported outcomes (PROs), remote monitoring tools, and self-management support. Continuous capture of patients’ subjective symptom experiences is enabled by PROs. Thereby, limitations associated with reliance on objective clinical metrics alone are addressed.73,74 In parallel, continuous monitoring and timely clinical responses are supported by remote technologies across temporal and spatial boundaries.75 A symptom management model based on digital health technology is particularly vital for elderly individuals living alone with advanced cancer. Isolation and loneliness are frequently experienced within this population.76 The psychosocial and spiritual needs of this population are frequently left unaddressed. This potentially culminates in a loss of self-identity and a diminished self-perception. However, enhanced communication capacities with health care teams have been observed through digital health interventions. In particular, older adults with cancer who experience social isolation or geographic remoteness may benefit from these interventions.77 Communication gaps are bridged by such digital models. They serve not only as clinical tools but also as vital conduits to help patients with advanced cancer maintain a sense of social connection and dignity throughout disease progression. It is noted, however, that obstacles accompany this technology-enabled transformation. A series of practical challenges is presented, spanning institutional, clinical, and humanistic dimensions. The overall effectiveness of symptom management may be undermined if these challenges are left unaddressed.

Patient data are fragmented into “information silos” by systemic barriers and privacy restrictions. Consequently, comprehensive clinical decision-making is hindered.38 Even with data integration, health care teams may be overwhelmed by vast information in the absence of effective filtering mechanisms. The prompt identification of intervention signals is thus rendered difficult.78 Concurrently, cognitive load among health care professionals is increased by the deployment of technology. For instance, nurse fatigue and occupational burnout are significantly driven by the widespread adoption of electronic health records (EHRs). Cognitive fatigue is more frequently observed among health care staff who perceive insufficient time for EHR alert tasks.79,80 More crucially, health inequalities may be exacerbated by the application of technology. Interpersonal interactions within therapeutic care may be diminished by an overreliance on digital tools. Simultaneously, a dual burden is imposed on elderly cancer patients by disparities in digital literacy, technological adherence, and information processing abilities. Age-related physiological decline and complex disease management must be navigated by this population. Independent use of digital health tools is often obstructed by sensory impairments, reduced mobility, or cancer-related cognitive impairment (CRCI).81

Technical hurdles are further amplified by the particular challenges faced by elderly patients. Low digital health literacy is identified as the primary barrier to the adoption of digital technologies.82 Furthermore, attention is often narrowed to personally relevant content due to cognitive decline. The vast volume of information on digital platforms is perceived more as a burden than an aid by this demographic.83 This implies that inequalities in cancer care may be exacerbated and health equity threatened if age-friendly designs are not integrated into digital tools. Consequently, technological innovation must be integrated into future digital symptom management. Simultaneously, the care relationship must be prioritized, and a dynamic equilibrium between humanized care and technological support must be sought.

Implications for future theory development and research

Based on the findings of this study, several key directions for future theoretical and empirical research are identified. The dynamic and continuous nature of these phenomena is considered to pose substantial challenges to static, cross-sectional research designs. As a result, longitudinal designs and experience sampling methods are required. Such approaches are essential for the systematic examination of co-evolutionary mechanisms linking symptom clusters and management outcomes across time and care settings. The prominence of multi-stakeholder collaboration and individual variation indicates the need for a meso-level theory that extends beyond an exclusively individual-level focus. This theory requires focused examination of role negotiation, responsibility allocation, and communication patterns among patients, family members, and multidisciplinary teams. The theory must also clarify the collaborative logic that governs symptom assessment, intervention, and feedback across stakeholders. Finally, digitally enabled symptom management models remain a key focus of future research. Pathways for integrating patient-reported outcomes with objective clinical data need to be explored. This integration may enhance the comprehensiveness and real-time capacity of symptom assessment. At the same time, greater attention must be directed toward age-friendly design and algorithmic optimization of these tools. This approach is intended to strengthen clinical decision-support functions while preserving the multidimensional, personalized, and relational nature of nursing practice. Such efforts are expected to help address challenges related to limited digital literacy and cognitive decline among older patients when adopting these technologies.

Limitations

This analysis has limitations. Although this sample is representative and influential, it primarily reflects Chinese and English literature and may not fully capture the research perspectives of resource-poor regions. The focus on highly-cited work may bias towards established paradigms, though the inclusion of recent articles aimed to counter this. Future conceptual work should explore cultural variations in the attributes.

Conclusions

This conceptual analysis clarifies longstanding conceptual ambiguity by defining six core attributes of symptom management: dynamism, continuity, multidimensionality, multi-stakeholder collaboration, diversity of intervention approaches, and individual variability. By situating symptom management within the contemporary context of interdisciplinary collaboration, patient-centered participation, and digitally enabled care, understanding of symptom management in cancer care is deepened. The findings reaffirm symptom management as a core domain within nursing research and practice. Nurses are positioned as playing an irreplaceable role in continuous symptom assessment, stakeholder coordination, and the provision of humanistic care. Through these activities, both clinical demands and disciplinary theory are integrated. Furthermore, the study clarifies that symptom management constitutes a shared responsibility among patients, families, and multidisciplinary teams, moving beyond traditional provider-centric models. The digitally enabled symptom management conceptual framework developed in this study provides a robust foundation for future theoretical advancement, methodological innovation, and assessment tool development. Its evaluative scope extends beyond symptom relief to include the effectiveness of collaborative processes and the adaptability of intervention approaches. Collectively, these findings provide precise conceptual underpinnings for systematic, patient-centered, and sustainable symptom management across the cancer care continuum.
