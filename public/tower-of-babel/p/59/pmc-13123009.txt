Comparative study on the expression levels of antithrombotic genes in five sympatrically distributed leech species
Authors: Rui Ai, Zichao Liu, Zhengguang Zhang, Lizhou Tang, Rujiao Sun, Zuhao Huang, Fang Zhao, Gonghua Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Medicinal leeches are rich in various antithrombotic components, holding significant pharmaceutical value. To investigate the differences in potential antithrombotic capacity among species, this study utilized transcriptome sequencing technology to compare the expression differences of antithrombotic-related genes in five sympatrically distributed leech species (Whitmania pigra, Whitmania laevis, Whitmania acranulata, Hirudo nipponia, and Hirudo tianjinensis). The results revealed significant differences in the overall expression levels of antithrombotic genes among different species. Most samples clustered by species in the cluster analysis, indicating that the expression patterns of these genes are highly species-specific. Analysis of antithrombotic gene functional groups (coagulation inhibitor, platelet aggregation inhibitor, fibrinolysis enhancer, anti-inflammatory agents, and tissue penetration enhancer) showed that, except for W. laevis, the other four species exhibited the highest total expression in the coagulation inhibitor group. Among them, W. pigra, W. acranulata, and H. nipponia had a significantly greater number of dominantly expressed functional groups (N ≥ 2) compared to W. laevis and H. tianjinensis (N = 1). Gene family comparisons revealed that the number of dominantly expressed gene families in W. pigra, W. acranulata, and H. nipponia was significantly higher than that in W. laevis and H. tianjinensis. Additionally, 28 highly expressed key antithrombotic genes were identified across the five species, with the vast majority of these genes accounting for over 50% of the total expression within their respective gene families. According to the above results, we speculate that W. pigra, W. acranulata, and H. nipponia exhibit higher potential antithrombotic capacity at the transcriptional level. These molecular traits align, at least in part, with the empirical wisdom accumulated through centuries of traditional Chinese medicine practice regarding the therapeutic value of specific leech species, although further rigorous pharmacological validation is required to substantiate our hypothesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12769-w.

Introduction

Thrombosis refers to the pathological process in which abnormal blood clots form within blood vessels and obstructs blood flow, potentially leading to tissue ischemia, organ infarction, and functional failure. It is a core pathological mechanism of cardiovascular diseases [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), atherosclerotic thrombotic diseases (such as myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke) and venous thromboembolism account for 27% of global annual deaths, totaling over 25 million cases each year [2]. The treatment of thrombosis varies depending on the severity and location of the thrombus, as well as the overall health condition of the patient [3, 4].

Leeches belong to the phylum Annelida, with most species inhabiting freshwater environments, while a few are adapted to terrestrial or marine life and temporarily parasitize the surfaces of reptiles, fish, and mammals [5]. Approximately 680 species of leeches have been identified worldwide, classified into 5 orders and 13 families [6]. Based on feeding habits, they can be divided into blood-sucking and non-blood-sucking categories. The most widely used medicinal leech in Europe is Hirudo medicinalis, traditionally employed to remove stagnant blood and improve local circulation [7, 8]. In China, the medicinal value of leeches was documented as early as in the Shen Nong Ben Cao Jing and Compendium of Materia Medica [9, 10], where they were categorized as a “blood-breaking and stasis-removing” herb. Currently, the Pharmacopoeia of the People’s Republic of China (PPRC) officially includes Hirudo nipponia, Whitmania pigra, and Whitmania acranulata as legal medicinal materials [11]. They are commonly used in the clinical practice as oral preparations in traditional Chinese medicine for treating thrombosis-related diseases such as stroke, coronary heart and cardiovascular diseases [12–14].

In recent years, with in-depth research, the medicinal value of leeches as traditional antithrombotic agents has been increasingly recognized, demonstrating promising clinical applications. At the same time, the types of thrombotic diseases have become more diverse, and leeches have emerged as an important therapeutic option due to their significant efficacy. However, leech species are highly diverse, and the true medicinal value of different species is often debated. The unique medicinal properties and mechanisms of action of different leech species require further investigation. For instance, H. nipponia and Hirudo tianjinensis are morphologically very similar [15], and historically, H. tianjinensis was even mistaken for H. nipponia in usage. Recent studies have shown that the two species share almost identical chromosome numbers and genome sizes [16]. Similar confusion exists among W. acranulata, Whitmania laevis, and W. pigra [17, 18]. This highlights the necessity of clarifying the unique medicinal advantages and mechanisms of action of different medicinal leech species.

Modern pharmacological studies have shown that the medicinal components of leeches primarily consist of various macromolecules with antithrombotic activity, including coagulation inhibitors, platelet aggregation inhibitors, fibrinolysis enhancers, anti-inflammatory agents, and tissue penetration enhancers, which are mostly protein or peptide-based substances [19–21]. For example, hirudin is a small protein comprising approximately 65 amino acids and is currently recognized as the most potent natural thrombin inhibitor [22, 23]. It functions by binding to thrombin and forming a highly stable non-covalent complex [22, 24]. Compared to chemical anticoagulants such as warfarin, hirudin carries a lower risk of bleeding complications [25]. Notably, the genes encoding these antithrombotic macromolecules and their expression products (proteins or peptides) exhibit significant heterogeneity among different leech species and even within the same species. For instance, the number, sequence polymorphism, and function of hirudin-encoding genes vary highly among closely related species and even within a single species [26–28]. Therefore, systematically comparing the expression differences of genes encoding antithrombotic proteins or peptides in different leech species are great significance for elucidating the differences in medicinal values among species.

In summary, elucidating the pharmacological characteristics of different medicinal leeches, further exploring their active components, and developing new drugs play a crucial role in preventing and treating thrombotic diseases and reducing their mortality rates. This study focuses on a comparative transcriptomic analysis of five sympatric leech species (W. pigra, W. laevis, W. acranulata, H. nipponia, and H. tianjinensis). Given that the active components of leech-based medicines are predominantly proteins or peptides, their pharmacological effects can be achieved by regulating the expression of specific target genes. Therefore, we consider the expression characteristics of these genes as an important basis for evaluating the efficacy of medicinal materials [29]. Based on this, we hypothesize that higher expression levels of antithrombotic genes correspond to more significant pharmacological effects. By integrating published genomic data with the transcriptomic results of this study, we systematically compared species-specific gene expression patterns, functional classifications, and gene families to reveal the molecular mechanisms underlying the medicinal differences among leech species.

Materials and methods

RNA extraction and sequencing

Individuals of five leech species (W. pigra, W. laevis, W. acranulata, H. nipponia, and H. tianjinensis) were collected alive from the same field area in Tianjin, China (GPS coordinates 39°28′04″N, 117°28′48″E). The collection of these live specimens was conducted with permission from the relevant management authorities and in compliance with animal use ethics guidelines, following the institutional animal ethics protocol (No. JGSU-EP-2024-00). After collection, the specimens were allowed to rest for 24 h. Subsequently, 12 individuals were randomly selected from each species, resulting in a total of 60 samples. The anterior segment tissues including the salivary glands and surrounding tissues from each leech were dissected and was promptly frozen in liquid nitrogen and then stored at -80 °C (to prevent RNA degradation) for subsequent experiments. We chose this tissue region rather than the salivary gland nor the whole body because of two reasons. Firstly, antithrombotic genes such as eglin and guamerin in leeches were found to be present in a variety of tissues, not only in salivary glands [30]. Secondly, the antithrombotic activity of head tissue is significantly higher than that of posterior segment tissues [31], so taking head tissue can not only obtain higher target gene content, but also avoid microbiome contamination in the digestive tracts. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and purified with the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Chatsworth, CA, USA). All samples underwent quality control to ensure that the RNA integrity number was above 7.0 and the OD260/280 ratio fell within 1.8-2.0, meeting the purity and integrity requirements for subsequent library construction. Poly(A) mRNA was isolated from the total RNA using oligo(dT)-attached magnetic beads. The mRNA was then fragmented into short fragments (300–500 bp) by heating at 94 °C for 5 min. The fragmented RNA was reverse-transcribed into first-strand cDNA using Superscript II reverse transcriptase and random primers, followed by second-strand cDNA synthesis using GEX second-strand buffer, DNA polymerase, RNase H, and dNTPs. Following end repair, the cDNA fragments were A-tailed and then ligated to double-ended adapters. The cDNA fragments of approximately 300 bp were selected using Phusion DNA polymerase and enriched through 15 cycles of PCR amplification. Finally, the constructed cDNA libraries were subjected to paired-end sequencing (150 bp per read) on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform, with an average effective sequencing depth of no less than 30 million reads per sample. The raw sequencing reads were processed using fastp v0.20.0 with default settings and parameters [32] to remove adapters and low-quality regions, resulting in clean reads for each sample, which were used for subsequent bioinformatic analyses.

Transcriptome expression quantification

To obtained the relative expression levels of genes in each sample, all CDS regions obtained from whole-genome annotation of their corresponding species were used as a reference to construct a transcriptome index using Salmon [33]. During the index construction process, the k-mer value was set to 31 as a core parameter to improve the accuracy and efficiency of alignment. Subsequently, the transcriptome sequencing data (reads) of each sample were aligned to the constructed index file to obtain the expression levels of each coding sequence in each sample. Salmon automatically performs corrections for GC bias and sequence bias during the alignment process to enhanced the accuracy of expression quantification. Finally, transcripts per million (TPM) was adopted as the standardized metric for gene expression levels. The TPM values of each CDS in each sample were calculated to represent the relative expression levels of genes across different samples. The calculation of TPM values takes into account the effects of transcript length and sequencing depth, enabling a relatively accurate reflection of the actual expression of genes. This study only focused on the antithrombotic genes identified in our previous genome-wide studies: W. pigra (21 gene families, 79 genes), W. laevis (20 gene families, 77 genes), W. acranulata (19 gene families, 102 genes), H. nipponia (22 gene families, 86 genes), and H. tianjinensis (22 gene families, 83 genes) [16, 18] (also see Supplementary Table S1). The TPM values of these genes were used in further statistical analyses.

Overall, gene expression analysis across species

To evaluated the overall expression differences of antithrombotic genes among the five species (W. pigra, W. laevis, W. acranulata, H. nipponia, and H. tianjinensis), statistical analysis was performed using SPSS v26.0 [34]. The gene expression levels of each sample were represented by TPM values. The Shapiro-Wilk test was conducted, and the results indicated that the data did not meet the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances. Therefore, non-parametric statistical methods were employed for subsequent analyses. The Kruskal-Wallis H test was performed using the “Analyze → Nonparametric Tests → Independent Samples” module. Additionally, to explore the differences in expression patterns among the species, hierarchical clustering analysis was conducted on all samples using TBtools-II (Toolbox for Biologists) v2.327 [35], and a hierarchical clustering heatmap was generated. Row standardization was performed using the Z-score normalization method, and the clustering algorithm was based on Euclidean distance and the average linkage method. In order to optimize inter-sample comparisons, minimize the influence of extreme values on the clustering results, and enhance visualization, TPM values were normalized to a range of 0 to 18.

Analysis of gene functional group expression

This study adopted the functional classification system for antithrombotic genes validated by our previous publications [16, 18], categorizing the target genes into the following five functional groups: anticoagulants, antiplatelet agents, thrombolytic agents, anti-inflammatory agents, and tissue permeability substances. The coagulation inhibitors include 13 gene families: antistasin, bdellastasin, bdellin, eglin, guamerin, hirudin, hirustasin, hirustasin_like, LDTI, lefaxin, piguamerin, progranulin, therostasin. The fibrinolysis enhancers include four gene families: destabilase, GGT, LCI, and lumbrokinase. The platelet aggregation inhibitors include three gene families: apyrase, decorsin, and saratin. The anti-inflammatory agents and tissue penetration enhancers include one gene family HMEI and hyaluronidase, respectively. The overall expression level of each functional group in a given sample was calculated as the sum of TPM values for all constituent genes.

To investigated the expression differences among different functional groups within a species, statistical analysis was performed using SPSS. Since the Shapiro-Wilk test indicated that the TPM data did not follow a normal distribution, the non-parametric Friedman test was used to assess the overall significant differences in expression among the five functional groups within each species. If the Friedman test showed significant overall differences (P < 0.05), the functional group with the highest expression level was selected as the reference group. Subsequently, the Wilcoxon test was used for pairwise comparisons to analyze the significance of expression differences between the other four functional groups and the reference group. To control the potential false positive risk arising from multiple comparisons, all P-values obtained from pairwise comparisons were adjusted for false discovery rate (FDR) using the Benjamini-Hochberg method [36]. The mean expression level of each functional group within a species, expressed as a percentage of the total expression, was visually represented using stacked bar charts in Origin v10.2.5.212 software (Origin Lab Corporation).

To compared the expression differences of antithrombotic functional groups across different species, the Kruskal-Wallis test was employed to evaluate the significance of expression differences among species. If the Kruskal-Wallis H test results showed a significant difference (P < 0.05), the species with the highest expression level was selected as the reference group. Subsequently, the Mann-Whitney U test was used for pairwise comparisons to analyze the significance of expression differences between other species and the reference group. All P-values from pairwise comparisons were corrected for the FDR using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Functional groups with no significant difference in expression level compared to the reference group were considered equivalently dominant expression functional groups. Finally, bar charts of the expression levels (represented as means) of each functional group across species were plotted using Prism software [37] to display the species-specific differences in the expression of antithrombotic functional groups.

Expression analysis of gene families

We first integrated the TPM values of all members within the same gene family to calculate the total TPM for each gene family. Subsequently, the Shapiro-Wilk test was performed, and the results indicated that the data did not meet the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance. Therefore, non-parametric statistical methods were employed in the subsequent analyses. The Kruskal-Wallis H test was used to analyze the differences in expression levels of these 22 gene families among the five species, assessing the statistical significance of the expression differences. For each gene family, the species with the highest expression level was defined as the dominant species. Using the dominant species as a reference, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied for pairwise comparisons with the other four species to evaluate the significance of expression differences between the dominant species and the other species. All P-values from pairwise comparisons were corrected for the FDR using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Additionally, Prism software [37] was used to generate expression characteristic plots of the dominant gene families in each species.

The Shapiro-Wilk test results showed that the data did not meet the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance. The Friedman test was used to analyze the differences in expression levels among members within each multi-member gene family, assessing whether the differences were statistically significant. If the Friedman test indicated significant differences, the member with the highest expression level within the family was taken as the reference, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied for pairwise comparisons among the internal members to evaluate the significance of expression differences between the highest-expressing member and the other members. Additionally, the proportion of the member with the highest mean expression level (ranked first in TPM) within the total TPM of the gene family was calculated to assess its contribution to the overall expression of the family.

Results

Comparison of expression patterns across species

Analysis of relative expression levels based on transcriptomic data (Table 1) revealed that among the five species, W. pigra exhibited the highest relative expression level, followed by W. laevis, H. nipponia, and W. acranulata, while H. tianjinensis showed the lowest relative expression level. The TPM values of H. nipponia and W. pigra exhibited considerable variation (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S2-S6), indicating substantial fluctuations in expression levels among individuals. In contrast, the TPM values of the other species showed relatively smaller fluctuations. The Kruskal-Wallis H test indicated a significant difference in the overall expression levels of antithrombotic genes among the five leech species (H = 28.073, df = 4, P < 0.001). Further pairwise comparison analysis revealed that the relative expression level of H. tianjinensis was significantly lower than that of W. pigra (P = 0.000) and W. laevis (P = 0.007), while the relative expression level of W. acranulata was significantly lower than that of W. pigra (P = 0.003). No significant differences were observed among the other species.

The expression differences among different functional groups related to antithrombotic functions within individual species revealed that W. pigra, W. acranulata, H. nipponia, and H. tianjinensis all exhibited the highest expression in the coagulation inhibitor functional group, while W. laevis showed the highest expression in the platelet aggregation inhibitor functional group (Table 2). Using the highest expression functional group as the reference, the Wilcoxon test showed that the expression levels of the coagulation inhibitor functional group in W. pigra, W. acranulata, and H. tianjinensis were significantly higher than those of the other functional groups (P < 0.05). In H. nipponia, the coagulation inhibitor functional group showed no significant difference only when compared with the platelet aggregation inhibitor functional group but was significantly higher than the remaining functional groups (Z ≥ 3.059, P < 0.003). In W. laevis, the platelet aggregation inhibitor functional group showed no significant difference only when compared with the coagulation inhibitor functional group but was significantly higher than the remaining functional groups (Z ≥ 3.059, P < 0.003). For all comparisons, the FDR-corrected P-values remained significant (P < 0.05). The analysis of statistically significant differences indicated that all five species exhibited a bias toward dominant expression in the coagulation inhibitor functional group, while H. nipponia and W. laevis also showed a bias toward dominant expression in the platelet aggregation inhibitor functional group, as illustrated by the percentage of expression levels of different functional groups within each species relative to the total expression (Fig. 2).

Comparison of gene family expression

To enhance the comparability of gene expression across species, we calculated the relative expression levels of each gene family in the five species based on transcriptomic data (Table 3). The Kruskal–Wallis H test showed that the expression differences of all 22 gene families among the species were significant (P < 0.05). We defined the species with the highest expression level of each gene family as its “dominant expressing species” and marked the family as an “advantageously expressed gene family” of that species (indicated in bold and underlined in Table 3), which served as the reference group. Furthermore, pairwise comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test (vs. the other four species). The results showed that 16 advantageously expressed gene families had significantly higher expression levels in their dominant species than in all other four species (Z ≥ 2.367, P < 0.05; FDR-corrected), demonstrating strong species-specific advantages. The remaining six advantageously expressed gene families had significantly higher expression levels in their dominant species than in three of the other species (Z ≥ 2.367, P < 0.01; FDR-corrected), but showed no significant difference compared to the species with the second-highest expression level (indicated in bold but not underlined in Table 3) (P ≥ 0.05). Moreover, the average expression levels of these two were similar, and both were regarded as advantageously expressed gene families.

Expression divergence among gene family members

To investigate the expression patterns of gene family members, we previously identified the dominantly expressed gene families in each species and conducted a statistical analysis of the expression characteristics of their members (Table 4). The Friedman test revealed that among the 22 analyzed dominantly expressed gene families with multi-gene members (excluding 6 single-gene families), there were significant differences in expression levels among the members (P < 0.05). Using the highest-expressing member within each family as a reference, further pairwise comparisons using Wilcoxon tests showed that in 15 families, the highest-expressing member had significantly higher expression levels than other members (P < 0.05). In the remaining seven families, although the difference between the highest-expressing member and other members did not reach statistical significance, the former still exhibited a relative expression advantage.

More importantly, we observed a general pattern: in the vast majority of highly expressed gene families (except for those with a larger number of members), the top-ranked member by TPM contributed to more than 50% of the family’s total TPM. Discussion

Species-level expression patterns

Transcriptomic data indicate significant differences in the overall expression of antithrombotic genes among species. The hierarchical clustering results revealed an anomaly: the three species of the genus Whitmania did not cluster together, and the two species of the genus Hirudo also failed to cluster together. Particularly noteworthy is that W. pigra did not cluster with its closer relatives, W. acranulata or W. laevis, but instead clustered with H. nipponia. This suggests that blood-feeding and non-blood-feeding habits may not be the key factor determining gene expression patterns, or at least not the sole factor. Gene expression characteristics in leeches exhibit high variability among species. For example, H. medicinalis and Hirudo verbana both belong to the genus Hirudo and are very closely related (historically, H. verbana was even misidentified as H. medicinalis) [38]. However, studies have shown that these sister species exhibit vast differences in the expression of key genes, such as hirudin [26]. This fully demonstrates that gene expression in leeches is highly species-specific, and its differentiation process is extremely sensitive and intense, capable of producing significant differences over a short period. Therefore, the anomalous clustering of W. pigra with H. nipponia observed in this study is likely due to the unique molecular regulatory strategies of each species.

Significant differences in gene expression among different species (even closely related species) inevitably have adverse effects on the medical application of leech-related drugs. The core requirements of drugs lie in the stability of their efficacy and a high degree of safety. Existing studies have shown that the antithrombotic pharmacological activity of different leech species exhibits significant interspecies variability and lacks stability [39]. Therefore, the efficacy characteristics corresponding to different species must be strictly evaluated and distinguished. The raw materials of different leech species must never be mixed or misused. Taking the PPRC [10] as an example, it includes three species of leeches as official medicinal materials. However, it is undeniable that the antithrombotic activity of H. nipponia is far higher than that of W. pigra and W. acranulata. Studies also indicate that the anticoagulant activities of W. pigra and W. acranulata differ. For instance, some reports suggest that the antithrombin activity of W. pigra is higher than that of W. acranulata [40], while others reach the opposite conclusion [41]. This study further suggests, through gene expression analysis, that there are substantial differences in the gene expression patterns between W. pigra and W. acranulata. Therefore, in practical medicinal applications, different leech species should not be treated as the same type of medicinal material. Instead, they must be strictly distinguished and precisely applied according to their species.

Expression of functional groups within and between species

Although the expression patterns of different functional groups within each species vary significantly, they all exhibit a dominant expression of anticoagulant functions. This indicates that anticoagulation consistently holds a predominant position in functional prioritization, reflecting a common tendency across species. This tendency is easily understandable in blood-feeding species (such as H. nipponia and H. tianjinensis): anticoagulation is the critical first step in the blood-feeding process, preventing blood coagulation and ensuring successful feeding, followed by other mechanisms such as thrombolysis and antiplatelet aggregation. Why, then, do the three non-blood-feeding species of the genus Whitmania also exhibit the same pattern? We speculate that the anticoagulant-related molecules retained in extant species of Whitmania are likely not recently evolved traits crucial to their current non-blood-feeding survival strategy but rather ancestral legacy features (ancestral traits). Traditional taxonomy once classified the genus Whitmania under the family Haemopidae, which exhibits significant behavioral differences from the family Hirudinidae. However, modern phylogenetic studies have revealed that species of Whitmania are closely related to those of the genus Hirudo, even more so than to genera such as Poecilobdella or Haemopis [42, 43]. This suggests that Whitmania is actually a recent branch that diverged from a common ancestor with Hirudo. For this reason, we hypothesize that the ancestors of these three Whitmania species also possessed strong blood-feeding capabilities, with functional groups dominated by anticoagulation. Although modern Whitmania species have lost their blood-feeding habits, this functional dependency has not yet fully shifted. The dominant expression of anticoagulation is retained as an ancestral trait, reflecting evolutionary conservatism.

The results of the interspecific functional group comparison also showed that W. pigra, H. nipponia, and W. acranulata exhibited relatively high expression levels in multiple (≥ 2) key functional gene groups, demonstrating a broad-spectrum expression advantage. This indicates that these three species possess a prominent molecular foundation for various potential pharmacological functions, which may be closely related to their traditional medicinal value. Notably, the three species exhibiting multi-advantage characteristics in functional gene expression completely coincide with the three medicinal leech species recorded in the PPRC. This result is consistent with the species selection experience formed through long-term practice in traditional Chinese medicine and provides certain modern molecular evidence supporting the inclusion of these species in the PPRC. Although the current pharmacopoeia does not make fine distinctions regarding the pharmacological efficacy of leech medicinal materials from different sources, this study suggests from the perspective of functional group expression profiles that W. pigra (with particularly prominent anticoagulation expression), H. nipponia (broad-spectrum high expression), and W. acranulata (prominent thrombolytic function expression) may possess distinct molecular functional tendencies. This provides a preliminary molecular-level hypothesis for future exploration of “precision medication” for leech medicinal materials, i.e., optimizing medicinal material selection based on different thrombus types (such as coagulation abnormalities or platelet aggregation abnormalities).

Species-specific gene family expression patterns

This study, through comparative transcriptomic analysis of five leech species, revealed significant interspecific differences in the expression patterns of gene families related to antithrombotic activity. The results showed that H. nipponia, W. acranulata, and W. pigra possess 8, 8, and 6 dominantly expressed gene families, respectively, whereas W. laevis and H. tianjinensis each possess only three. This difference in the number of dominantly expressed gene families suggests that the first three species may possess a broader or potentially stronger antithrombotic capability at the gene expression level. Most interestingly, the three species which showed a greater number of dominantly expressed gene families perfectly correspond to the three traditional medicinal leech species recorded in the PPCR, once again supports the plausibility that traditional empirical selection may have a molecular basis.

Specifically, H. nipponia exhibits relatively less controversy regarding its antithrombotic efficacy within its species and is traditionally considered a species with high medicinal value. Our results indicate that the dominant gene families of H. nipponia hold advantages in anticoagulation (bdellin, hirustasin, progranulin), thrombolysis (GGT), antiplatelet aggregation (decorsin, apyrase), anti-inflammatory (HMEI), and tissue penetration (hyaluronidase), demonstrating its molecular characteristics as a “versatile” antithrombotic medicinal resource at the gene expression level. Notably, the expression level of the antiplatelet aggregation gene family decorsin in H. nipponia is seven-fold higher than that of its closely related species H. tianjinensis, and this gene was not detected in the other three species. Therefore, this gene deserves to be a key candidate for future functional validation, particularly for the detection of its antiplatelet aggregation function and subsequent pharmacological research.

The W. acranulata has faced some controversy regarding its medicinal value in antithrombotic applications due to its non-blood-feeding habits and the absence of certain potent antithrombotic proteins such as hirudin and decorsin. However, existing studies have demonstrated that various antithrombotic proteins in this species remain active and exhibit significant antithrombotic effects [39, 44]. The results of this study indicate that the number of highly expressed antithrombotic gene families in W. acranulata is comparable to that of H. nipponia, supporting its potential as an important medicinal resource from the perspective of expression abundance. Notably, the average expression level of the thrombolysis functional group in W. acranulata was the highest among the five species studied, with the expression of the destabilase gene family being significantly higher than in other species. Therefore, in the exploration of antithrombotic drug development, W. acranulata could be prioritized as a potential candidate species for subsequent research on novel thrombolytic agents.

The W. pigra is notable for its large size and abundant resources, with multiple previous studies demonstrating its significant antithrombotic effects, particularly its potent anticoagulant and antithrombotic activities in vivo [14, 44]. As the only species within the Whitmania genus reported to carry the hirudin genes, recent research has confirmed that its sample extracts display antithrombin activity, and at least one hirudin has been proven to be active [28]. Our findings indicate that the expression level of the anticoagulation functional group in W. pigra was significantly higher than in the other four species. Additionally, in terms of the number of dominant gene families, W. pigra ranks second only to H. nipponia and four of its dominant gene families (antistasin, hirudin, lefaxin, and progranulin) are all highly expressed in anticoagulation functions. Therefore, we speculate that the antithrombotic effect of W. pigra may primarily stem from its robust molecular expression foundation for anticoagulation.

The W. laevis has been reported to contain components with anticoagulant and antiplatelet activator properties [45]. However, the results of this study indicate that compared to the aforementioned three pharmacopoeia-included species, its dominant functional groups are relatively limited, and the number of dominant gene families is smaller. This suggests that its potential antithrombotic capability may be lower than that of the former three species. Nonetheless, the overall expression level of its antithrombotic genes was comparable to that of H. nipponia, and its antiplatelet aggregation functional group exhibits relatively high expression levels. Notably, the average expression of its saratin gene family was significantly elevated. This finding indicates that W. laevis still possesses noteworthy expression advantages and research value in specific antithrombotic components, particularly those related to antiplatelet aggregation.

The gene expression profile of H. tianjinensis shares similarities with that of W. laevis. However, compared to the three PPRC-included species mentioned earlier, its predominantly expressed functional groups are limited, and the number of dominant gene families expressed is relatively small. Additionally, the overall expression level of antithrombotic-related genes in H. tianjinensis is significantly lower than that of its close relative, H. nipponia. In the past, H. tianjinensis was often mistaken for H. nipponia and used interchangeably due to their morphological similarities. However, our recent research [16] revealed significant differences between these two leech species in terms of sequence variation, the number of antithrombotic-related genes, and their expression levels. Based on a larger sample size, this study further suggests that H. tianjinensis indeed significantly lags behind H. nipponia in both the abundance and diversity of antithrombotic-related gene expression. Therefore, the two must be strictly distinguished for medicinal purposes to avoid misuse.

Limitations and perspectives

Integrating the findings mentioned above, this study delineates the diversity and specificity of antithrombotic molecular expression characteristics across different leech species from the perspectives of functional groups and gene families. These characteristics are correlated with traditional medicinal experience and pharmacopoeia records, providing molecular-level support for the selection of species in pharmacopoeias. It is important to acknowledge that cross-species comparisons of overall antithrombotic genes and of functional groups may be influenced by inherent differences in gene copy number and heterogeneous expression levels among genes across different species. In light of these considerations, the use of total TPM for overall antithrombotic genes and functional groups in this study was intended primarily to illustrate broad expression trends across species, rather than to support precise quantitative comparisons. To address potential biases arising from these factors, our analysis was further complemented by examining gene family counts, dominant gene families, and key transcript isoforms. The consistent expression patterns observed across these multiple analytical layers strengthen the robustness of our conclusions. It should also be noted that the selection of species for this study, and consequently, the scope of our comparative analysis, may reflect a historical and contemporary research bias toward species documented in pharmacopoeias. Future validation within a more diverse taxonomic range is therefore warranted.

The long-standing practice of traditional Chinese medicine has accumulated valuable therapeutic experience, but the modern molecular mechanisms underlying its effects are often unclear, hindering its standardization and modernization. The key to transforming traditional medicinal materials into modern drugs with well-defined components, clear mechanisms, and controllable quality lies in identifying and characterizing their active material basis. The vast number of genes encoding antithrombotic proteins in leeches poses a challenge for large-scale functional screening. Based on the hypothesis that “the activity of the pharmacodynamic material basis may be related to the potency and abundance of its components”, this study attempts to use gene expression levels as a preliminary screening criterion, aiming to prioritize highly expressed transcripts that dominate in dominant gene families (often with expression levels > 50%) as potential candidate genes for further in-depth research. This strategy helps to focus on the molecular targets most likely involved in the main physiological/pharmacological processes from a massive pool of genes.

We fully recognize that high transcription levels are a necessary but not sufficient condition for high protein abundance and strong biological activity. Post-transcriptional multi-level regulatory mechanisms may significantly influence the activity of the final products. Therefore, the highly expressed candidate genes identified in this study only represent a prioritized starting point for functional validation. They provide clues for elucidating the potential molecular basis of the antithrombotic effects in different leech species. Thus, future research must go beyond the scope of this study and follow a rigorous transformation path: (1) Verify the expression products of candidate genes at the protein level; (2) Obtain purified proteins through in vitro recombinant technology and systematically evaluate their specific biological activities (anticoagulation, thrombolysis, etc.) and potency; (3) Clarify their secretion in medicinal parts (such as salivary glands); (4) Conduct preclinical pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic evaluations. Only through this complete chain of evidence can the gene expression features revealed in this study be transformed into solid scientific evidence supporting the precise development and utilization of leech resources the research and development of modern innovative drugs, thereby truly bridging the gap between traditional experience and modern science.

Conclusions

This study systematically compared to the differences in potential antithrombotic capacities among five sympatrically inhabiting leech species (W. pigra, W. laevis, W. acranulata, H. nipponia, and H. tianjinensis). By providing more comprehensive and accurate data on antithrombotic gene expression, this study represents the most extensive analysis to date of antithrombotic macromolecules in multiple medicinal leech species. The results reveal significant species-specific differences in antithrombotic gene expression among the five leech species, with W. pigra, W. acranulata, and H. nipponia demonstrating higher medicinal potential for antithrombotic applications, aligning with the long-standing empirical selection in traditional Chinese medicine practice.
