Timing of Vasopressin Initiation in Patients with Septic Shock: An Updated Systematic Review and Meta-analysis with Trial Sequential Analysis
Authors: Anirban Bhattacharjee, Priyankar K Datta, Vivek Kumar, Rajathadri H Ravikumar, Prachee Sathe, Riddhi Kundu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsWe performed this systematic review and meta-analysis to study the timing of vasopressin initiation and its association with patient-centered outcomes in adult patients with septic shock.Data sources and study selectionWe searched Embase, PubMed, and Web of Science for eligible prospective, retrospective studies, and Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) from January 2013 to December 2024 in adult patients with septic shock that compared an early (<6 hours) vs late strategy (>6 hours) of vasopressin initiation. Our primary outcome was short-term mortality, while other secondary outcomes included hospital and ICU mortality, incidence of renal replacement therapy (RRT), occurrence of new-onset arrhythmias (NOA), and ICU and hospital length of stay (LOS).ResultsSeven studies with a total of 3,993 patients were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis. Early initiation of vasopressin was associated with a lower short-term mortality [relative risk (RR) 0.84, 95% CI: 0.71–0.99; p = 0.033, I2 = 30.2%], which was the primary outcome of our study. Early vasopressin initiation was associated with a decrease in hospital mortality (RR 0.83, 95% CI: 0.76–0.92; p = 0.0003, I2 = 17.9%), decreased requirement of RRT (RR 0.78, 95% CI: 0.61–0.99, p = 0.043, I2 = 31%), decreased ICU mortality (RR 0.86, 95% CI: 0.78–0.95; p = 0.003, I2 = 0%) and hospital LOS) [mean difference (MD) −1.83 (−3.41 to −0.25) days, p = 0.02]. There was no difference in new-onset arrhythmia or length of ICU stay between the two groups.ConclusionWe report a significant advantage of initiating vasopressin within six hours for patients experiencing septic shock. However, the studies contributing to this meta-analysis are characterized as being at high risk of bias.How to cite this articleBhattacharjee A, Datta PK, Kumar V, Ravikumar RH, Sathe P, Kundu R. Introduction

Septic shock represents the most severe manifestation of sepsis, characterized by hemodynamic instability and impaired end-organ perfusion. Despite improvements in systems of care, therapeutic advances, and increasing awareness, it continues to be associated with a mortality rate of 30–40% and imposes a significant burden on healthcare resources.1,2

Norepinephrine is recommended as the first-line vasopressor for optimizing organ perfusion in patients with septic shock. For patients who continue to remain hypotensive despite incremental doses of norepinephrine, the Surviving Sepsis guideline suggests adding a second non-catecholamine vasopressor, vasopressin, to achieve the target mean arterial pressure (MAP). The SSC 2021 guidelines recommend vasopressin as an adjunct to norepinephrine to raise MAP or decrease norepinephrine dosage, but do not provide clear guidance on the optimal timing of initiation.3,4

Arginine vasopressin, commonly referred to as vasopressin (AVP), is an endogenous peptide hormone synthesized in the hypothalamus and secreted by the posterior pituitary gland. Arginine vasopressin exerts vasoconstrictive effects by acting on V1 receptors located in the vascular smooth muscles. A relative state of AVP deficiency is well documented in patients with septic shock, with serum levels as low as 3.1 pg/mL (normal levels 1–5 pg/mL in non-sepsis) compared to cardiogenic shock (mean AVP levels 22.7 pg/mL).5,6 This has spurred research into the potential benefits of early vasopressin administration in patients with septic shock. The catecholamine-sparing effect of vasopressin may contribute to a reduced incidence of tachycardia, which could be pertinent in patients presenting with sepsis.7 However, vasopressin is not entirely a benign drug. The major concerns with vasopressin use are its powerful vasoconstrictive effect that can potentially contribute to ischemia in various vascular beds, thereby restricting its use as a second-line vasopressor in sepsis.

Approximately one in every five patients with septic shock receives vasopressin as per a large retrospective cohort of patients admitted to US hospitals.5 While there seems to be a consensus regarding the association between early vasopressor initiation and better outcomes, the timing and threshold for adding a second vasopressor remain variable. A wide dose range of 0.25–0.5 μg/kg/min of norepinephrine is recommended by SSC as the trigger to add a second vasopressor, vasopressin.4 Although the VANISH trial by Gordon et al. demonstrated potential renal protection with early vasopressin, it failed to show a mortality benefit, leaving the timing of initiation as a critical unresolved issue.8–11 A recent large-scale retrospective study reported increased mortality in patients who had vasopressin initiated late at higher norepinephrine equivalent doses.12 The VASST trial found a lower mortality in the subgroup of patients in whom vasopressin was initiated at a norepinephrine dose equivalent of less than 15 μg/minute.13,14 Therefore, early initiation of vasopressin as the second vasopressor may be beneficial in septic shock.

A previous systematic review and meta-analysis done in 2021 tried to assess the role of early vasopressin initiation in adult patients with septic shock.14 However, it included studies where vasopressin was used both as a first-line as well as a second-line vasopressor. We contend that such an approach made the included studies heterogeneous and inconsistent with our current clinical practice and guidelines, which recommend the use of AVP as a second-line agent in cases requiring escalating doses of norepinephrine. Since the publication of the previous review, additional research has attempted to address this conundrum. Therefore, we felt an updated meta-analysis was necessary to examine the evidence in light of new research and to address some of the limitations of the previous meta-analysis.

Thus, we conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis to investigate the timing of AVP initiation as a second vasopressor and its association with patient-centered outcomes in patients with septic shock. This study aimed to compare early and late AVP initiation strategies (defined as less than or more than 6 hours since shock onset, respectively) on clinically relevant outcomes, including short-term mortality, hospital mortality, ICU and hospital length of stay (LOS), requirement of renal replacement therapy (RRT), and new onset arrhythmias in adult patients with septic shock. Additionally, we also planned to incorporate a trial sequential analysis (TSA) to evaluate the sufficiency of current evidence on vasopressin timing in septic shock.

Materials and Methods

This study followed the existing standards for conducting and reporting systematic reviews and meta-analyses. The study protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251026930) before data extraction.

Eligibility Criteria

The inclusion criteria for this study comprised both interventional (randomized or non-randomized) and observational studies that adhered to specific PICO criteria. Eligible studies involved adult patients (≥18 years) diagnosed with septic shock according to the Sepsis-2 or Sepsis-3 definitions. The intervention under investigation, namely early AVP initiation, was defined as either: Early vasopressin (AVP) initiation within 6 hours of the onset of septic shock or the commencement of norepinephrine (NE), or simultaneous initiation of AVP and NE as combined first-line vasopressors. The comparator or control was defined as either non-initiation or delayed initiation of AVP after at least 6 hours of onset of septic shock or NE initiation. The 6-hour time threshold was established based on a previous literature review and consensus among the study investigators.

The primary outcome of this study was short-term mortality, defined as one of the following: (A) 28-day mortality, (B) 1-month mortality, or (C) 30-day mortality. Secondary end-points of interest included hospital and ICU mortality, requirement of RRT, occurrence of new-onset arrhythmias (NOA), and ICU and hospital LOS. The studies had to report any one of the aforementioned outcomes to be eligible for inclusion.

Exclusion criteria were as follows:

Search Strategy

We conducted an extensive literature search in Embase, PubMed, and Web of Science to identify eligible studies from January 1, 2013, to December 31, 2024, using the following keywords: “septic shock,” “Vasopressin,” “Sepsis,” “Timing,” and “ICU.” The detailed search strategy is available in the supplementary file. There were no language or geographic restrictions for study inclusion. Additionally, we performed a manual search of the bibliographies of studies, systematic reviews, and review articles to identify potentially relevant studies.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

First, the titles and abstracts were screened by two independent reviewers (AB and RK). Then, the screened relevant titles, abstracts, and full-texts of the included studies were assessed independently by the same reviewers according to the eligibility criteria. Any disagreement arising during this process was resolved after adjudication of a third investigator (PKD).

Once the studies to be included were finalized, the data required for the meta-analysis were then extracted by two independent investigators (AB and RK) in a standardized Excel sheet, cross-checked, and compared for errors. A third investigator (PKD) mediated any disagreements during data extraction.

The data included information about the primary author, year of publication, study name (if any), sample size, study design, inclusion and exclusion criteria, nature of intervention in each arm, and primary and secondary outcomes of the study. The authors were approached to provide any missing data required for the meta-analysis that were not reported in their study.

Qualitative Analysis

The risk of bias of the included RCTs and non-RCTs was estimated using the ROB-2 and ROBINS-I tools, respectively.15,16 Risk of bias assessment was performed independently by two reviewers (AB and RHR), and any disagreement was resolved by discussion.

Meta-analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio v2024.12.1.563 (Posit, PBC) with the “meta,” “metafor,” and “readxl” packages. For binary outcomes, individual study risk ratios (RRs) and their within-study variances were estimated using the Mantel–Haenszel method. Continuous outcomes were summarized as mean differences (MD), and their variances were measured via the inverse-variance method, with medians and interquartile ranges reported in some studies converted to means and standard deviations using Wan's method.17 All effect estimates were pooled under a random-effects model by inverse-variance weighting, with between-study variance (τ²) estimated using the Der Simonian–Laird method. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran's Q test (employing Mantel–Haenszel weights for binary outcomes) and quantified by the I² statistic. Pooled estimates are reported with 95% confidence intervals and two-sided p-values. Funnel plot analysis was not employed to assess publication bias due to the limited number of included studies [fewer than (<10)], as recommended by the Cochrane Handbook, which cautions against its use in small meta-analyses due to low power and increased risk of spurious findings.

Sensitivity Analyses

A post-hoc leave-one-out (LOO) meta-analysis was conducted to evaluate the robustness of the primary random-effects meta-analyses by iteratively excluding one study at a time and recalculating the pooled effect, its 95% confidence interval, two-sided p-value, and heterogeneity metrics (Q, I², τ²) under the same model specification and effect measure employed in the main analyses. Influence was assessed using qualitative criteria, including the directional stability of the pooled effect, changes in statistical significance, and significant fluctuations in heterogeneity across omissions.

Trial Sequential Analysis

Trial sequential analysis was done for the primary outcome to assess the conclusiveness of the meta-analytic evidence and the diversity-adjusted required information size (DARIS). This analysis utilized the Copenhagen Trial Unit TSA software, incorporating two-sided O'Brien–Fleming alpha-spending and O'Brien–Fleming beta-spending on a sample-size information axis with the inner wedge applied. The prespecified parameters included a type I error α = 0.05 and a type II error β = 0.20 (power 80%). The expected effect of the intervention was a relative risk (RR) reduction (RRR) of 16% in mortality, aligning with the conservative range of 10–15% and based on the pooled RR derived from the current conventional meta-analysis. As none of the included studies were classified as having a low risk of bias, the risk for the control group was independently derived from a prior systematic review and meta-analysis (≈34.7%). Heterogeneity was addressed through variance-based diversity (D²) adjustment within the random-effects model. The cumulative Z-curve was constructed in chronological order using random-effects log risk ratios and their variances from the primary meta-analysis, with conventional 95% confidence intervals reported for pooled effects. Trial sequential analysis was applied to determine boundary crossings and DARIS attainment.

The process of screening the relevant literature and selecting studies is illustrated in a flowchart presented in Figure 1. The initial search yielded 971 titles. After removing duplicates and non-relevant articles, 46 articles were evaluated for eligibility. Ultimately, seven studies were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis, covering the study period from 2013 to 2024. Meta-analysis

Primary Outcome

Five studies documented short-term mortality. One study that reported mortality without specifying the time point was excluded from the meta-analysis.25 The total sample size comprised 2,285 patients in the early vasopressin group and 1,201 patients in the late vasopressin group. Pooled analysis revealed a significant reduction in short-term mortality with early vasopressin use compared to late use (pooled RR 0.84; 95% CI: 0.71–0.99; I2 = 30.2%) (Fig. 4).

Forest plot for short-term mortality

Secondary Outcomes

Five studies (n = 3,892) reported hospital mortality. Overall, early vasopressin use significantly reduced hospital mortality compared with late use (RR 0.83; 95% CI: 0.76–0.92; p = 0.0003; I2 = 17.9%) (Fig. 5A). Three studies (n = 3,308) reported ICU mortality rates. Early vasopressin use was associated with significantly reduced ICU mortality compared to late use (RR 0.86; 95% CI: 0.78–0.95; I2 = 0%) (Fig. 5B). There was no significant difference in the incidence of new-onset arrhythmia between the “early” and “late initiation” groups in the pooled analysis of four studies (n = 685) (RR 0.94; 95% CI: 0.52–1.71); I2 = 49.7%) (Fig. 5C).

Forest plots for secondary outcomes

Forest plots for secondary outcomes

Six studies (n = 3,922) reported on the incidence of RRT requirement. Early vasopressin use was associated with a statistically significant reduction in the incidence of RRT (pooled RR 0.78; 95% CI: 0.61–0.99; I2 = 31%) (Fig. 5D).

Data pooled from six studies involving 1246 patients did not find any difference in ICU LOS between the groups (MD −0.59 days; 95% CI: −1.85 to 0.68; I2 = 51%) (Fig. 5E). However, early vasopressin use was associated with a statistically significant reduction in the length of hospital stay (MD −1.83 days; 95% CI −3.41 to −0.25; I2 = 51.8%) (Fig. 5F).

Sensitivity Analyses

Leave-one-out (LOO) sensitivity analyses demonstrated that the direction of pooled effects remained stable and generally aligned with the primary syntheses across all outcomes, although statistical significance and heterogeneity exhibited modest variation with specific omissions (Supplementary Figs S8–S14). Regarding short-term mortality, all omissions maintained a risk ratio below unity, with a loss of nominal significance when either Barzegar et al.20 or He et al.23 were excluded, and a clear non-significance when White et al.24 was removed; I² ranged from 0 to 47% across omissions, indicating that no single study fully accounted for heterogeneity. For hospital and ICU mortality, LOO re-fits preserved both direction and significance under all single-study exclusions, with low to negligible heterogeneity across omissions. In the case of RRT, the pooled effect remained directionally favorable but crossed the null with several omissions, whereas new-onset arrhythmia and both LOS outcomes exhibited no alteration in the overall non-significant conclusions, regardless of which study was omitted.

Trial Sequential Analysis

At 3,486 accrued participants, the cumulative Z-curve did not cross trial sequential monitoring boundaries for definite benefit or harm and did not enter futility; the DARIS (~4,426) was not achieved, indicating inconclusive evidence requiring additional adequately powered trials (Fig. 6).

Discussion

We investigated the possible beneficial effects of early initiation (within 6 hours of shock onset) of vasopressin in addition to norepinephrine infusion in adult patients with septic shock. We included a total of seven studies meeting the inclusion criteria. Our study results indicated that early initiation of vasopressin infusion resulted in a statistically significant reduction in the primary outcome of interest, i.e., short-term mortality. The use of an early vasopressin strategy showed additional benefits in reducing hospital and ICU mortality, hospital LOS, and the requirement for RRT. While the LOO analyses maintained the directional stability of the findings, statistical significance was not consistently observed with omissions. Additionally, TSA did not corroborate the conclusiveness of the meta-analysis for the primary outcome: the cumulative Z-curve did not cross efficacy or harm boundaries, and the diversity-adjusted required information size exceeded the accrued sample size, indicating that evidence remains underpowered and inconclusive despite the favorable trend in the conventional analysis. No difference was observed in the incidence of new-onset arrhythmia between the two groups.

The mainstay of therapy in the early stages of septic shock relies on fluid resuscitation, administration of appropriate antimicrobials, and vasopressor administration, which is titrated to optimize perfusion at the macro and microvascular levels. While early administration of vasopressors has largely shown benefits, the threshold for starting the second vasopressor agent has been left to the discretion of the physician, leading to widespread variability in practice. The important considerations for physicians at this stage would be to consider possible norepinephrine treatment failure in the face of escalating doses of norepinephrine and the purported benefit of adding a non-catecholamine vasopressor such as vasopressin. This led us to design this systematic review and meta-analysis to explore the possible beneficial role of early use of a second vasopressor, vasopressin, in patients with septic shock. We chose a cutoff of 6 hours to define early vs late vasopressin strategy based on a review of the previous literature on this topic.14 While it may be argued that choosing such a time point is arbitrary, we felt it to be the most reasonable among the available thresholds. The norepinephrine dose range to start vasopressin has been widespread and variable in the literature, and dosages have also been reported in μg/minute instead of μg/kg/minute, which negates the possibility of deriving any meaningful conclusion.10,27

We found benefits in clinically relevant outcomes of short-term mortality, hospital and ICU mortality, and hospital LOS with an early vasopressin strategy. This is a new finding, given that the previous meta-analysis did not demonstrate any advantage with early vasopressin use.14 There could be a few possible reasons for the difference in findings of our study. First, our study is a more current review, including two recent large-scale studies published since then. Second, we excluded studies comparing norepinephrine vs vasopressin as a first-line agent, as norepinephrine has been reasonably established as the first-line vasopressor in septic shock. Therefore, we omitted studies that compared vasopressin vs norepinephrine as first-line vasopressor in septic shock, unlike the previous meta-analysis, to avoid heterogeneity and the absence of guidelines supporting such an approach.14 We believe that this approach enhances the homogeneity and methodological rigor of our systematic review and meta-analysis. In essence, we feel early addition of vasopressin as a second-line vasopressor, when norepinephrine is insufficient, may confer maximum benefit to patients potentially exhibiting refractoriness to norepinephrine. This effect may not have been captured in the previous meta-analysis due to their more liberal inclusion criteria as compared to ours.

The findings of our study are consistent with a recent retrospective review showing a delay in initiation of vasopressin and rising lactate levels to be associated with a rise in in-hospital mortality in the range of 12–18%.10 Interestingly, patients with septic shock who had achieved a MAP of more than 65 mm Hg within 6 hours of vasopressin initiation were found to have better outcomes compared to the non-responders.28 A subgroup analysis of the VASST trial suggested a possible benefit with vasopressin initiation at a norepinephrine equivalent dose of less than 15 μg/minute.13 Sacha et al.12 reported a linear correlation between rising lactate levels and mortality at the time of initiation of vasopressin. It seems likely that the delay in vasopressin initiation led to worsening tissue perfusion (as reflected by rising lactate levels) and contributed to a higher mortality. The well-characterized physiological deficiency of vasopressin in the early stages of septic shock, coupled with the catecholamine-sparing effect of vasopressin and lack of immunomodulation, might be possible reasons for the benefits seen with early replacement. In our study, we report a 16% relative risk reduction with early vasopressin initiation from a baseline risk of 29.4%. This would translate to a number-needed-to-treat (NNT) of 14, suggesting we would need to treat approximately 14 patients with early vasopressin to prevent one death. Taken together, these recent findings seem to support the initiation of vasopressin at an earlier stage of septic shock when the physiology has not been deranged to the point of irreversibility.

Our study found a statistically significant reduction in the requirement for RRT between the two groups. This finding is in agreement with the previous meta-analyses on this topic.14,29 A post-hoc analysis of the VASST trial found that vasopressin was associated with a lower rate of RRT use compared to norepinephrine (17.0% vs 37.7%, p = 0.02) in patients at “risk” of kidney injury, with a possible benefit in less severe cases.30 The VANISH trial that compared vasopressin vs norepinephrine in patients with septic shock reported less use of RRT in the vasopressin group compared to the norepinephrine group, but no benefit in terms of mortality.11 This is possibly related to the ability of AVP to better maintain renal medullary blood flow and glomerular filtration rate as compared to norepinephrine in patients with septic shock.31 Our study did not find any difference in the occurrence of NOA between the two groups. The lack of incidence in new-onset arrhythmia with early use of vasopressin lends credence to the relative safety of norepinephrine from an arrhythmogenic viewpoint.32

Our meta-analysis adds to the growing evidence exploring the early use of vasopressin as the second vasopressor in septic shock, showing a nominal mortality reduction on conventional random-effects pooling with moderate heterogeneity. In contrast to the neutral benefit with vasopressin on mortality signals reported in landmark trials such as VASST and VANISH, these pooled results suggest that the early timing of vasopressin initiation and using it as an adjunct may be an important determinant of improved outcomes, although study-level risk of bias remains high/serious in the included evidence set. Importantly, the TSA indicates that the cumulative Z-curve did not cross efficacy or harm boundaries and that the diversity-adjusted required information size was not met, suggesting that the apparent benefit on conventional analysis is not yet conclusive and that additional adequately powered trials are required before firm recommendations on a fixed early-start threshold can be made.

The novelty of our analysis lies in applying a standardized timing cutoff for “early” initiation, harmonized inclusion criteria across studies, and sequential monitoring to gauge conclusiveness; together, these steps provide a more structured assessment of timing effects than prior syntheses while transparently communicating residual uncertainty when information size is insufficient by TSA criteria. Vasopressin is not entirely benign, and physicians must be mindful of its adverse effects before its indiscriminate use. Given TSA's finding of non-crossing boundaries and unmet DARIS, practice should prioritize individualized hemodynamic targets and consider vasopressin timing within broader resuscitation strategies, while the field advances with rigorously designed, adequately powered trials that test early initiation strategies against clinically relevant comparators and outcomes. This raises the research question of what the optimal strategy would be to initiate vasopressin in septic shock. Low diastolic pressure appears to be an independent marker of adverse outcomes in patients with septic shock.33,34 More recently, the vascular norepinephrine responsiveness index (VNERi), calculated as DAP divided by the product of heart rate and NE dose, has emerged as a novel physiologic marker that integrates both vascular tone and vasopressor burden. In a secondary analysis of the ANDROMEDA-SHOCK trial, patients with VNERi < 2.6 demonstrated the highest mortality (~56%), suggesting profound vasoplegia and NE hyporesponsiveness.35 It remains to be seen whether indices like diastolic shock index or VNERi can be used to better titrate our choice of vasopressor therapy. Some authors have proposed the potential role of a biomarker-based strategy and perfusion markers such as serum lactate.36 With the increasing emphasis on precision medicine and personalized strategies in sepsis resuscitation, this seems to be an exciting area of further research.

Our study has a few limitations. First, this meta-analysis was based on retrospective and prospective cohort studies and only one RCT, which leaves it prone to confounding and other biases. Although the use of TSA minimizes the effect of random errors on the results of the analysis, it does not remedy the high risk of bias in the included studies. Second, we chose a time point of 6 hours to define early vs late vasopressin initiation for reasons mentioned earlier. We cannot necessarily conclude whether such a strategy is beneficial at other time points or even earlier in the course of sepsis resuscitation. Third, the included studies did not report other safety outcomes with vasopressin, such as digital or mesenteric ischemia, which might be important considerations while using vasopressin. Finally, since the number of included studies was less than 10, we did not perform funnel plot analysis or statistical tests for publication bias, as it may draw spurious inferences.

In conclusion, the present systematic review and meta-analysis suggest that the early administration of vasopressin as an adjunct to norepinephrine in the management of septic shock may improve short-term mortality outcomes, reduce hospital and ICU mortality rates, decrease hospital LOS, and lessen the need for RRT, based on studies with high and moderate risk of bias. The conclusiveness of these findings remains uncertain, as sequential monitoring indicates that the accumulated evidence has not yet reached a definitive conclusion regarding benefit, harm, or futility. Consequently, we feel there is a need for better indicators to identify patients who are likely to be unresponsive to norepinephrine. Likewise, it would be important to identify patients who are at a higher risk of vasopressin, given its potential to cause ischemia. Further research is warranted to evaluate this strategy of early vasopressin initiation within 6 hours of septic shock onset, ideally through a well-designed randomized controlled trial (RCT). Additionally, future investigations should consider exploring individualized strategies based on norepinephrine dose equivalents, shock index, or perfusion markers as potential triggers for initiating vasopressin in septic shock.
