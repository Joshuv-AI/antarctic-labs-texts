The interplay of parental and child-related characteristics on paediatric COVID-19 vaccine non-acceptance in Hungary
Authors: Zsófia Gács, Anna Sára Ligeti, Szilvia Rudas, Júlia Koltai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs the rate of vaccine non-acceptance creates alarming public health challenges, it becomes essential to understand how the interplay between parental and child characteristics influences the uptake of voluntary vaccinations among children. The experiences gained during the COVID-19 pandemic are crucial for developing more targeted communication strategies and increasing vaccination rates in the future. This research analyses data from 13 representative Hungarian surveys focusing on parents’ attitudes towards vaccinating their children against COVID-19, giving information of 2482 respondents altogether. The reasons for vaccine non-acceptance are investigated using both quantitative and qualitative methods, with attention given to characteristics of both the parent and the child. The interplay of parental and child characteristics significantly shapes vaccination decisions on voluntary paediatric vaccination. Parental attributes—such as previous COVID-19 experience, sources of information about the virus and vaccination, socioeconomic status, and gender—and child attributes, including age, gender, and the presence of chronic illness, affected not only the likelihood of vaccine non-acceptance but also the underlying rationale. These rationales included, e.g. beliefs that the pandemic does not pose a threat, concerns about potential side effects, distrust, and arguments rooted in misinformation. The quantitative and qualitative results explain interactions of individual factors, proving that subgroup patterns may be modified by individual experience, and individualized information could further decrease the rate of non-acceptance.

Introduction

Vaccine hesitancy—particularly in high-income countries—has become an increasingly pressing issue, which the World Health Organization (WHO) had already identified as one of the top global health threats even before the COVID-19 pandemic [1, 2]. However, the COVID-19 pandemic created a unique global situation that serves as an important reference point for understanding contemporary vaccination attitudes. Moreover, our collective experiences with COVID-19 vaccination are likely to shape both public perceptions and policy responses in the event of a future pandemic. Over the past few years, a substantial body of knowledge has accumulated regarding the factors that influence acceptance and refusal of COVID-19 vaccines. Nevertheless, relatively few studies have examined how parental and child characteristics jointly determined children’s COVID-19 vaccination uptake.

The Hungarian vaccination schedule consists of several mandatory childhood vaccinations, all of which are fully funded by the National Health Insurance Fund. This leads to an exceptionally high vaccination rate for mandatory vaccines, exceeding 99% [3]. In addition, there are several recommended childhood vaccines, some of which are also funded by state insurance [4, 5]. The HPV vaccination program, for instance, was introduced in Hungarian schools in 2014, and has led to a vaccination rate exceeding 80% among school-aged children [6]. In contrast, acceptance of the COVID-19 vaccine was significantly lower: 74% of children under the age of 18 did not receive any COVID-19 vaccination, despite the fact that it was freely available to them [7].

COVID-19 vaccines became available in Hungary in the final weeks of December 2020, offering individuals free access to a variety of vaccine types in an effort to boost vaccination uptake [8, 9]. Nevertheless, the coverage of the vaccine programme reached a peak in early 2022, with 71% of the adult population receiving the primary series of the vaccination, which was approximately 11% below the European Union average [7]. For the paediatric population, vaccines were made available for the 16–17 age group on 13 May 2021, followed by the 12–15 age group on 11 June 2021, and the 5–11 age group on 15 December 2021 [10].

The majority of empirical research on parental decisions-making regarding children’s COVID-19 vaccination has focused on parental characteristics. Factors such as perceived susceptibility of children may positively influence vaccination decisions, whereas vaccine distrust, exposure to misinformation, lower socioeconomic status, and concerns about the vaccine’s unprecedentedly rapid development time have been found to negatively affect parental willingness to vaccinate [11]. Several studies have applied the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB) to explain and predict parents’ intentions to vaccinate their children against COVID-19 [12]. These studies revealed that, among European parents, the component of ‘attitude towards the behaviour’ exerted the strongest influence on vaccination intentions [12]. Similarly, an Italian study highlighted the pivotal role of cognitive attitudes in shaping perceptions of vaccine safety and effectiveness in the paediatric context [13]. Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, a Hungarian study investigated parental prejudices towards non-existing vaccines. The findings indicated a similar pattern of non-acceptance towards the imagined vaccine compared to existing ones, suggesting a general predisposition towards vaccine hesitancy [14].

Despite this growing body of research, our understanding of the dynamics underlying children’s vaccination remains limited compared to that of adult vaccination behaviour. Specifically, less is known about how parents weigh different factors when deciding whether to vaccinate their children. In this study, we seek to explore how the interplay between parental and child characteristics influenced children’s uptake of the COVID-19 vaccine during the pandemic. The findings aim to inform the design of more effective vaccine recommendations and immunization campaigns, and to support the development of targeted communication strategies tailored to different social groups.

Methods

Data comes from the MASZK study, a series of surveys conducted by the University of Szeged between April 2020 and June 2022 in Hungary [8]. Surveys were administered using CATI (Computer-Assisted Telephone Interviewing) methodology. Data collection occurred monthly, with a minimum sample size of 1000 respondents per wave. A multi-step, proportionally stratified, probabilistic sampling procedure was used for sampling, which included both landlines and mobile phone numbers. In each month, data collection lasted for one week, allowing the inclusion of both weekdays (5 days) and weekends (2 days). The sample is representative of the Hungarian population aged 18 and older in terms of gender, age, highest level of education, and type of settlement. Sampling errors were adjusted post-collection using iterative proportional weighting. The data collection strictly adhered to the European and Hungarian data privacy regulations and was approved by both the Hungarian National Authority for Data Protection and Freedom of Information, and the Research Ethics Committee of the Medical Research Council of Hungary (Resolution No. IV/3073-1/2021/EKU). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

The current analysis primarily focuses on the 13 surveys collected on a monthly basis between June 2021 and June 2022. The first survey included is the date when vaccines became available for the 12–15-year-old age group. Pandemic waves were defined by COVID-19 case numbers according to Horváth et al. [10].

For respondents who had children who were not vaccinated against COVID-19, and who did not intend to vaccinate them, the reasons for non-vaccination were collected through an open-ended survey question. The coding of the 2482 answers for the above-mentioned open-ended question involved developing 33 predefined categories with inductive categorization to identify the reasons for not vaccinating children against COVID-19. As some answers included multiple statements, each response could be assigned to multiple categories. These categories were tested and refined based on feedback from researchers who coded an initial sample of 200 responses. To measure the reliability of the coding, inter-coder reliability was calculated using Cohen’s Kappa for each category (see Fig. S2 of the Supplementary Material). Any disagreements between the two researchers were reviewed and re-coded by a third researcher.

In the analysis, logistic regression models were used for two types of dependent variables. In the first set of models, we sought to identify which parental and child-related characteristics influence the decision to vaccinate a child. We first estimated a baseline model in which the dependent variable was the child’s vaccination status and the independent variables were parental and child characteristics. To gain deeper insight, we then included interactions between parental and child characteristics. Each interaction was added separately, resulting in four additional models, alongside the baseline model, in which the interaction term had a significant effect. In Model 1, we added the interaction between child’s gender and parent’s experience with severe Covid. In Model 2, we added the interaction between the child’s age and the pandemic wave; in Model 3, between the child’s age and the parent’s vaccination status. Finally, in Model 4, we added the interaction between whether the child has a chronic disease and whether the parent gets information only from social media or personal networks.

To unfold which parent- or child-related characteristics make an argument for non-vaccination more likely, in the second set of models, we created logistic regression models, where the dependent variable was the argument-category used for the non-vaccination of the child.

In both types of models, the analyses are conducted on the level of the children. In the questionnaire, if a respondent had more than one child, the vaccination intentions and the reason for non-vaccination were asked about each child separately. Consequently, the database contains siblings with the same parent and thus, same parental characteristics. To control for the bias caused by the non-independence of observations, we applied clustered standard errors in the regression models [15].

Results

The analysed sample includes data on 6570 children of 3773 parents. Of the children, 25% were aged 0–4, 37% were 5–11 years old, and 38% were 12–18 years old. The gender distribution among the children was balanced (50% boys and 50% girls), while 6% of the children were reported by their parents to have a chronic illness. Among the surveyed parents, 54% were women. The average age of the parents was 40.9 years. Of the respondent parents, 17% lived in the capital, 18% in county seats, 37% in smaller towns, and 29% in villages. Weights varied between 0.43 and 2.69.

Parental attitudes towards COVID-19 vaccination

During the fourth wave of the COVID-19 pandemic, vaccines were not yet available for children aged 5–11. Nevertheless, approximately half (51%) of parents with children in this age group reported that they would not vaccinate their child against COVID-19 even if the vaccine were available. Parents of older children aged 12–18 years exhibited a much more favourable attitude towards the vaccine, with nearly half (48%) having already vaccinated their child, 15% intending to do so, or being unsure, and only 37% expressing reluctance (see Fig. S1 of the Supplementary Material).

At the very beginning of the fifth wave, vaccination programmes for the 5–11 age group were initiated, resulting in 13% of parents reporting that their child aged 5–11 had received at least one dose of COVD-19 vaccine by the first half of 2022. Concurrently, the proportion of parents who refused vaccination of their child increased to nearly two-third (65%). Furthermore, the proportion of parents with older children aged 12–18 years who refused vaccination also increased in comparison with the previous period (42%).

The interplay of parental and child-related characteristics

First, a baseline model was estimated in which the dependent variable was the child’s vaccination status and the independent variables were parental and child characteristics (Table 1). The result of the baseline model show that parents with a university degree (compared to those with only an elementary school education) (odds ratio (OR): 1.473), and compared to mothers, fathers were more likely to vaccinate their child. However, the significance of the latter effect is weak and disappears when we control for various interaction terms (Models 1–4). As parents’ age increased, the likelihood that they had their child vaccinated also increased (OR: 1.019). A clear distinction was evident between those residing in the capital and those residing in other municipalities: a significantly lower likelihood of vaccination was observed in all other places of residence compared to the residents of Budapest (ORs were 0.667, 0.596, and 0.610). Furthermore, financial status emerged as a significant predictor of vaccination decision, with children from families in the medium and high financial categories being more likely to be vaccinated compared to those from the poorest financial background (OR: 1.283 and 1.635, respectively). Nevertheless, one of the strongest effects of parental characteristics was whether the parent had received at least one dose of vaccine against COVID-19. Vaccinated parents were more than 10 times (OR: 11.123) more likely to have their child vaccinated than unvaccinated parents. Furthermore, parents who had personal or indirect severe experience with the virus were more likely to vaccinate their child, however, this effect was not strong and became non-significant when we controlled for various interaction terms (Models 1–4) (the odds ratio was 1.156 in the baseline model). Furthermore, parents who only followed news about the coronavirus on social media or through discussions with friends were less likely to have their child vaccinated (OR: 0.659), however, this effect was also weak and disappeared when we controlled for various interaction terms.

Among the characteristics of the children, the age of the child was found to be the most important determinant: older children were more likely to be vaccinated or planned to be vaccinated (OR: 1.094).

As a next step, we analysed the interplay between the characteristics of the parent and the child on the vaccination status of the child. Although in the binomial logistic regression model no significant differences were observed between boys and girls, and in the case of children with chronic illness, when combining characteristics some differences appeared.

When a child is a boy, there is no difference in the likelihood of his vaccination among those parents, who have, and those who have not experienced serious COVID-19 related illness by themselves, or serious illness or death among their close friends or relatives. Nevertheless, if the child is a girl, it is more likely that she will be vaccinated if the parent experienced severe symptoms on themselves or on a close person they know.

The older the child is, the more likely that they are vaccinated. However, this effect is much stronger among those, whose parent is vaccinated. Among those parents, who are not vaccinated, the age of the children just slightly affects their vaccination status: almost independently of the child’s age, we can observe very low rates of child vaccination. Nevertheless, among those parents, who are vaccinated, we can observe a higher rate of vaccination among children, and this essentially high vaccination rate increases sharply with the age of the child.

If the child does not have a chronic disease, there is no difference in the vaccination intentions of those parents, who only get COVID-19 related information from social media and acquaintances, and those who also used other sources as well. However, if the child has chronic disease, those parents, who only informed themselves about COVID-19 from social media and acquaintances, are less likely to vaccine the child (compared to those who consumed other information sources as well).

Reasons for not vaccinating children

Reasons for refusing the vaccination of a child were measured with an open-ended survey question. Of the 2482 responses, 2441 could be categorized. Inter-coder reliability was then calculated for each category using Cohen’s Kappa. According to this measure, all categories reached the minimum threshold of ‘moderate’ agreement (Cohen’s Kappa >0.4) as defined by Landis and Koch [16] (for the exact values, see Table S2 in the Appendix).

The responses were categorized into five main categories. As Fig. 2 illustrates, 33% of respondents who did not intend to vaccinate their child cited concerns regarding potential side effects or the safety of the vaccines, 13% of respondents attributed their hesitancy to distrust, while 11% asserted that the threat posed by the pandemic for their children was not significant. Furthermore, 16% of respondents mentioned arguments based on misinformation, while 37% mentioned other reasons.

Reasons for vaccine non-acceptance among parents who did not vaccinate their children against COVID-19.

A detailed examination of the categories reveals that the predominating concern expressed by 15% of respondents relates to the potential side effects of the vaccines, while 11% specifically feared that vaccines were developed too quickly, and 9% were generally distrustful. Three percent of respondents said that they did not want to take responsibility for their child being hurt by the vaccine. A number of arguments linked to the age of the child were also mentioned by respondents: 9% said their child was too young to be vaccinated, 8% said the child was not at serious risk from the virus due to their age, and 5% said the vaccine was a risk to the child’s age group. A total of 8% of respondents indicated that they would vaccinate their child ‘when it becomes necessary’, 4% of respondents simply cited fear as their response, while an additional 4% indicated that their child was the one who had made the decision regarding vaccination (and not themselves) (Fig. 2).

Non-vaccinating mothers were more likely to cite concerns regarding potential side effects (OR: 1.537), while non-vaccinating fathers were more likely to cite that the virus was not dangerous (the odds ratio for the mothers compared to fathers was 0.599).

In comparison to non-vaccinating parents who had received primary education, those non-vaccinating parents who had received secondary or tertiary education were more likely to mention reasons related to the potential side effects of the vaccines (ORs: 1.322 and 1.351, respectively). Furthermore, non-vaccinating parents with tertiary education were more likely to claim that the pandemic was not a significant threat (OR: 2.391), and they were more likely to provide reasons based on misinformation (OR: 1.644). In contrast to non-vaccinating parents with primary education, non-vaccinating parents who had received vocational training or secondary education were more likely to express distrust (ORs: 1.507 and 1.797, respectively). In addition, all other non-vaccinating educational groups were less likely to cite other reasons compared to non-vaccinating parents with primary education.

In comparison with those non-vaccinating parents residing in the capital, those living in county centres and villages were less likely to assert that COVID-19 is not a threat (ORs: 0.592 and 0.643), but more likely to cite misinformation as the reason for their hesitancy (ORs: 1.573 and 1.663). Furthermore, non-vaccinating parents residing in smaller towns and villages were more likely to provide reasons related to distrust (ORs: 2.500 and 2.281).

Self-reported financial status also played an important role: non-vaccinating parents with low financial status were more likely to report fear of side effects, but less likely to report mistrust and other reasons than non-vaccinating parents with high financial status.

Non-vaccinating parents who had direct or indirect experience of severe COVID-19 were less likely to cite arguments based on distrust and other reasons (ORs: 0.719 and 0.850, respectively), but more likely to cite misinformation (OR: 1.325). The latter was also the case for those non-vaccinating parents who had only learned about the pandemic from social media or from people they knew (OR: 1.690).

In addition to their own characteristics, the parents’ reasoning was also influenced by the characteristics of their children. Fear of side effects and lack of trust were more likely to be cited in case of younger children (ORs: 0.952 and 0.978), while ‘other reasons’ were more likely to be cited for older children (OR: 1.029). Whilst non-vaccinating parents of girls were less likely to cite not perceiving the threat of the virus or misinformation (ORs: 0.771 and 0.795), parents of girls were more likely to cite other reasons (OR: 1.218). If the child has had chronic disease, non-vaccinating parents were more likely to mention fear of side effects (OR: 1.652) and misinformation (OR: 1.484) and less likely to cite that COVID is not dangerous and other reasons (OR: 0.504)—compared to non-vaccinating parents, whose child has not had chronic disease.

Discussion

Our findings indicate that parents’ own vaccination status was the strongest predictor of their willingness to vaccinate their children against COVID-19. This aligns with earlier research, including studies from Romania conducted during the fourth wave of the pandemic [17], and a meta-analysis showing that parents’ attitudes towards their own vaccination strongly mirror their attitudes towards their children’s vaccination [18]. Child age also played a significant role: older children are more likely to be vaccinated. This likely reflects the later introduction of vaccination recommendations for younger age groups, which may have heightened parental uncertainty and safety concerns.

Between the fourth and fifth waves of the pandemic, vaccine refusal increased in both age groups—substantially more so among younger children. Among parents of adolescents, the group that was previously undecided or only planning vaccination nearly disappeared, suggesting that initial hesitancy often evolved into outright refusal. This trend contrasts with findings among adults, where hesitancy decreased over time [19]. A plausible explanation is that by the fifth wave, as the perceived severity of the virus declined in Hungary [20], parents increasingly viewed vaccination as unnecessary.

Importantly, child age had little effect among unvaccinated parents; however, among vaccinated parents, vaccination rates rose sharply with the child’s age. This highlights the importance of considering both the independent and interacting effects of parental and child characteristics.

Socio-demographic factors also shaped vaccination behaviour. Fathers, older parents, those with higher socioeconomic status, and residents of the capital were more likely to vaccinate their children. These results are consistent with prior meta-analyses and reviews showing that lower socioeconomic status reduces vaccine acceptance [11, 18, 21].

Personal or indirect experience with COVID-19 increased the likelihood of child vaccination, a finding consistent with Hungarian adult data [19], but contrary to an Italian study reporting no significant association [22]. However, this correlation is also affected by the characteristics of the child. Parents of girls are more likely to vaccinate their child if they have personal or indirect experience with the virus. Parents of boys, on the other hand, do not differ in this respect. The relationship becomes clearer when the reasons for non-acceptance are also considered. The results indicated that parents of boys perceive the threat posed by the pandemic to their children as less significant.

Children’s health status also influenced decisions. If the child has a chronic disease, the sources of information used by the parents are more important: parents who relied primarily on social media or acquaintances for information were less likely to vaccinate their child. Along with the results of Capasso [13], these results also suggest that clear medical recommendations and their widespread communication are extremely important in an epidemic, particularly for vulnerable groups such as children with chronic diseases. Based on previous studies, having a child with a chronic disease increases the vaccine hesitancy, whether the disease or therapy causes immunosuppression or not [23, 24].

Parents’ stated reasons for non-vaccination varied across subgroups. Those with direct COVID-19 experience expressed less distrust but were more affected by misinformation, suggesting that personal experience may reduce scepticism towards authorities but not necessarily fill information gaps.

The previously observed phenomenon whereby fathers are more likely than mothers to accept vaccination of their children has gained an important new interpretive dimension, as our results also demonstrate that mothers are ‘more concerned’ in all areas. They list different arguments behind their non-acceptance of vaccination, such as being more concerned about the side effects and being less likely to cite the fact that the virus does not pose a danger to their children.

Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of this study. First, the analysed data are not from a panel study, but from multiple cross-sectional surveys. Accordingly, we can only hypothesize about the reasons for the changes over time and cannot draw conclusions about causality. Second, vaccination status was measured based on the self-reporting by respondents, which can be biased for various reasons, such as social desirability bias [25]. Social pressure may have led some respondents to falsely claim that they had vaccinated their child. Third, although the severity of the disease and the availability of the vaccines changed over time, we could not include response time as an independent variable because the database does not contain information on the children’s vaccination date. To account for temporal variation in disease severity and vaccine availability, we included COVID-19 waves as an independent variable in the models.

However, we believe that analysing and understanding the characteristics of parents and children that influence vaccination decisions of the children, and especially how these characteristics interact, can improve our understanding of people’s health-related behaviour. A detailed analysis of the reasons for non-vaccination could lead to more efficient and targeted communication planning in the event of a future pandemic.

Conclusion

As memories of the COVID-19 pandemic fade, understanding the drivers of vaccine acceptance becomes crucial. Our study on parental refusal of COVID-19 vaccines for children spans two pandemic waves and offers both quantitative and qualitative insights into individual and interaction-level factors. Our findings show that vaccine decision-making is multifaceted. Although certain subgroups share common patterns, individual experiences—especially a parent’s own vaccination status—strongly influence decisions. A more nuanced, targeted approach to these factors could significantly improve acceptance of future vaccines.
