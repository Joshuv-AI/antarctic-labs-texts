Healthcare workers’ perception of quality of care rendered to people living with HIV and non-communicable diseases at an urban district hospital in KwaZulu Natal, South Africa: a mixed mode study
Authors: Althea Rajagopaul, Mergan Naidoo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe integrated chronic disease model (ICDM) was implemented to address two major health concerns in South Africa (SA), namely Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) and non-communicable diseases (NCDs). Many facilities have adopted integrated care to manage HIV and NCDs better; however, some facilities have not fully implemented the ICDM due to challenges.MethodsThis study explored healthcare workers’ (HCWs) perceptions of the quality of care (QoC) rendered to patients with multiple comorbidities amidst these challenges. Wentworth Hospital in KwaZulu Natal, SA, was the study setting. The study’s three phases of data collection involved a structured questionnaire(n = 15), semi-structured qualitative interviews (n = 7), and a focus group discussion (FGD), (n = 6) with HCWs from the district health facility. Participants for both the quantitative and qualitative studies were purposively sampled as these were the HCWs who were directly involved in HIV and NCD management. Frequency analysis was conducted for the quantitative data. Deductive thematic content analysis was conducted for the qualitative interviews and aligned to the Donabedian Framework.ResultsFifteen participants, comprising of doctors and nurses, reported that HIV and NCDs were diagnosed, investigated and managed at the hospital. Ten participants reported that patients were referred to OPD for NCDs. The following themes aligned to the conceptual framework emerged: structure: availability of equipment and screening, staff shortages versus waiting times, accessibility, stigma; Process: integrated versus fragmented care, staff training; Outcomes: self-management, adherence and prioritisation of treatment, health education and counselling, patient outcomes and patients satisfaction.ConclusionHealthcare workers’ perceptions were that care was fragmented. Human resources, staff attitudes, waiting times, training, and high patient burdens were identified as factors that need to be addressed. This study contributes to the need to strengthen existing health systems, yielding insight into the QoC at this facility. There are many benefits and facilitators of integrating care for HIV and NCDs, but this requires support from structural and process elements. Future studies should be conducted on a bigger scale for KZN to provide evidence on models of care occurring in KZN and allow for the strengthening of health systems.Keywords: Quality of care, Human immunodeficiency virus, Diabetes mellitus, Hypertension, Communicable disease

Introduction

Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infection has increased worldwide [1]. It contributes significantly to the disease burden in low and middle-income countries (LMICs). Non-communicable diseases are on the increase in the number of people living with HIV (PLHIV) in South Africa (SA) [2]. At the same time, diabetes mellitus (DM) and hypertension (HT) add a significant burden to overall NCDs in SA as well as other areas. To address the dual burden of HIV and NCDs, the Integrated Chronic Disease Model (ICDM) was introduced in South Africa in 2011 [3]. However, measuring the quality of care (QoC) is imperative to ensure adequate care is provided to patients. Ameh and colleague [3] highlight that QoC is difficult to define based on patient and choice, and relative prioritisation of these elements should be contextualised and guided by the circumstances in which quality of care is assessed [4]. QoC can be “almost anything anyone wishes it to be” and there is no single criterion to measure it. The Donabedian Framework is commonly used to determine QoC in medical care. In 2017, Ameh and colleagues reported that according to the Donebedian framework, there are seven elements of quality of medical care: Efficacy, Effectiveness, Efficiency, Equity, Optimality, Acceptability and Legitimacy [3]. Although Efficacy is hard to measure, it refers to care provided under optimal conditions and is the basis against which measurements should be made. Effectiveness describes the outcome of interventions; Efficiency refers to cost reductions without compromising effects; Equity refers to the fairness in the distribution of healthcare in populations; Optimality is about balancing the costs and benefits of healthcare; Acceptability encompasses accessibility of healthcare and interpersonal patient-provider interaction; and Legitimacy refers to the social acceptability of the healthcare institution regarding the manner in which healthcare is delivered. The choice of which of these elements, as well as their relative prioritisation, should be guided by the contexts in which quality of care is being assessed [4].

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines integrated healthcare as ‘the organisation and management of health services so that people get the care they need, when they need it, in user-friendly ways, achieve the desired results and provide value for money.’ In 1994, South Africa adopted a primary healthcare (PHC) approach to healthcare delivery. Although little is known about the QoC for the ICDM in South Africa, the Department of Health in the eThekwini district of KwaZulu-Natal (KZN) adopted it. Many low and middle-income countries (LMICs) are experiencing an increase in NCDs, coupled with the increasing prevalence of HIV, and in South Africa, this is compounded by fragmented care [5]. To effectively respond to this dual burden of chronic diseases, an integrated approach for HIV and NCDs was adopted to facilitate holistic care. This approach is required to deliver care at the PHC level, where such conditions are most frequently present. To assess the QoC, we used the Donabedian conceptual framework. Ameh and colleagues [3]) used this conceptual framework, which consists of structure, process and outcome, to draw inferences about healthcare quality. Donabedian defines structure as the professional and organisational resources associated with the provision of healthcare (e.g., availability of medicines or equipment and staff training); process as the actual management of the patient (e.g., counselling, treatment and referral); and outcome as the desired result of care provided by the health practitioner (e.g., patient satisfaction with the QoC) [3]. Donabedian distinguished between two types of outcomes: (i) technical outcomes, which are the physical and functional aspects of care, such as the absence of complications and reduction in disease, disability and death, and (ii) interpersonal outcomes, which include the patients’ satisfaction with care and the influence of care on patients’ quality of life as they perceived it. Ameh and colleagues [3] cited staff shortage and work overload as barriers to the integration of HIV and NCDs. There has been progress in several African countries towards HIV and NCD service integration. However, further evaluation of the integrated HIV and NCD programmes is needed to provide insight into the associated benefits and risks and to guide future health system development. Non-communicable diseases are increasing among people living with HIV. Thus, strengthened and sustained diagnosis of non-communicable diseases through integrated non-communicable diseases and HIV care is needed to improve patient outcomes. However, there is a paucity of evidence on the barriers and facilitators diagnosing non-communicable diseases among people living with HIV in low- and middle-income countries [6].

This study was motivated by HCWs and aimed to explore healthcare worker insight to gauge the quality of care (QoC) rendered to patients with HIV and NCDs (DM and HT) in one urban district hospital in Kwa Zulu Natal (KZN). In exploring the HCWs’ perceptions of QoC that patients received, we were able to ascertain the model of care that is currently implemented in this urban district hospital, as well as the barriers and facilitators of integrated care.

Methods

Study setting and population

This study was conducted at Wentworth Hospital, an urban 196-bed district hospital in KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa. The facility includes an antiretroviral treatment (ART) clinic where people living with HIV (PLHIV) are managed. Patients with diabetes mellitus and hypertension who are also HIV-positive receive holistic, integrated care at the ART clinic. In contrast, patients with pre-existing DM and HT (without HIV) may continue their care at the outpatient department (OPD) or at their local primary healthcare (PHC) clinics.

The hospital serves a catchment population of approximately 410,000 people and receives referrals from seven nurse-led PHC clinics. It provides care for about 1,379 PLHIV, of whom roughly 25% also have HT and/or DM. The ART clinic manages both uncomplicated and complicated cases; stable patients (i.e., those with viral suppression, improved clinical status, and controlled HbA1c and blood pressure) are referred back to PHC facilities. More complex patients are referred to regional or tertiary hospitals.

Healthcare workers (HCWs) employed at the ART clinic were purposively sampled, as they were directly involved in the management of HIV and NCDs. Eligible HCWs included medical officers, medical interns, and nurses. All HCWs working in the ART clinic were invited to participate, and those who consented were enrolled.

Study design

A mixed-methods design was adopted, consisting of three phases of data collection (1 August – 15 December 2017). Parallel studies involving patients (questionnaire, n = 301; qualitative interviews) were conducted but are reported separately. The patient study has been submitted for publication in the Journal of Multimorbidity and Comorbidity and is currently undergoing peer review. Prevalence data was published in the African Journal of Primary Health Care and Family Medicine [7]. This paper reports only on the HCW component, comprising three phases:

Phase 1: A structured HCW questionnaire (HCWQ), developed by the authors, was administered to 15 HCWs after written informed consent. Six additional HCWs declined participation. The questionnaire explored perceptions of the feasibility of integrated care at the facility. Phase 1 included both closed and open-ended questions, with qualitative responses analysed thematically and integrated into the Results.

Phase 2: Semi-structured individual interviews were conducted with seven key informants directly involved in HIV and NCD care. Interviews explored the perceived quality of care, integration versus fragmentation of services, and feasibility of integration. Interviews were conducted by the principal investigator (PI), a trained qualitative researcher.

Phase 3: A focus group discussion (FGD) was held with six HCWs (five of the seven interviewees and one additional participant). Discussions explored integrated versus fragmented care, as well as barriers and facilitators to integration.

Tools were developed by the PI and researcher supervisor. It was piloted at a clinic and then changes were made. All interviews and discussions were conducted in English, with probes and documentation of nonverbal cues. Purposive sampling ensured inclusion of HCWs directly involved in HIV and NCD management. Seventeen HCWs were approached to answer the questionnaire, of which only 15 agreed to participate. Eight key HCWs were approached to participate in semi structured qualitative interviews, of which 7 agreed to participate. Six informants formed part of the FGD. Although only 4 of the original informants participated, 2 other HCWs directly involved in HIV and NCD management agreed to the group discussion.

Data analysis

Quantitative data: Data were entered and analysed using Stata (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Descriptive statistics were generated, and mean scores or frequencies were used to assess quality of care indicators.

Qualitative data: The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist guided reporting. Open-ended responses from Phase 1 were collated and thematically analysed. Data from Phases 2 and 3 were transcribed verbatim, coded, and analysed deductively using the Donabedian framework (structure, process, outcome). Two researchers (AR and DA) independently coded transcripts; discrepancies were resolved by consensus [5].

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee at the University of KwaZulu-Natal (BREC 645/116) and the KwaZulu-Natal Department of Health. Codes were used to protect participant confidentiality. All participants provided written informed consent, including permission to audio-record interviews and FGDs.

Quality assurance

Trustworthiness of the qualitative data was ensured through multiple strategies. Triangulation was achieved by comparing findings from the HCW questionnaire, semi-structured interviews, and the focus group discussion, and by cross-checking with quantitative and patient data (not reported here).

Credibility was established by repeated engagement with the data through careful listening, re-reading transcripts, and applying scientific rigour during analysis. Transcripts were returned to participants for verification.

Dependability was strengthened by independent coding of transcripts by three researchers (AR, DA, MN), with convergence and divergence discussed until consensus was reached.

Confirmability was maintained through participant verification, independent coding, and reflexive journaling, where the PI documented thoughts and interactions to reduce researcher bias.

Transferability was supported by conceptually grounding the study in contexts applicable to other urban district hospitals in KwaZulu-Natal.

Reflexive notes were kept to ensure the investigators did not influence participant responses, where the researcher wrote down thoughts and interactions during the research.

Results

The 15 participants comprised two (13%) medical officers (more than 10 years of experience), 10 (67%) medical interns (6 months of experience) and three (20%) nurses (more than 5 years of experience) who all worked in the antiretroviral treatment (ART) clinic. Patients living with NCDs only were managed in the OPD. The doctors and nurses manage PLHIV and NCDs in the antiretroviral treatment (ART) clinic.

Efficiency and efficiency saw the potential of task shifting and upskilling all staff. Efficacy was measured by asking questions around HWS integrating care and what was their perception Effectiveness of care saw that most HCWs felt that patients were satisfied with the care that they received. Optimality as reported by not duplicating services.

Equity could not be achieved as not all patients were screened for DM.

Acceptability was described by HCWs as patients being satisfied with the care received.

Legitimacy can be a achieved by allocating resources such as human resources and training staff to ensure integrated care.

Discussion

This study explored healthcare workers’ (HCWs) perceptions of the quality of care (QoC) provided to PLHIV, with or without NCDs. Using the Donabedian framework, which is also applied in South Africa’s Integrated Chronic Disease Model (ICDM) [3], we assessed how structure, process, and outcomes shaped perceptions of care. Our findings highlight that QoC is strongly influenced by resource availability, staff capacity, and patient experiences. While HCWs supported the principle of integrated care, they identified significant barriers that need to be addressed before such a model can be implemented effectively.

Key findings

One of the main findings was that HCWs welcomed integrated HIV–NCD care, recognising its potential to reduce stigma, improve continuity, and enhance patient outcomes. However, they expressed concern that without additional staff, integrated care could lengthen waiting times and reduce consultation quality. Training and upskilling of staff were therefore seen as essential prerequisites for successful implementation.

Models of care

Both integrated and fragmented models of care were observed. Ideally, HIV and NCD care should be provided simultaneously at the same point of service; however, service delivery was often fragmented. For example, the ART clinic attempted to integrate HIV, DM, and HT management (Model A), but resource constraints led many patients to continue NCD care at the OPD. Inconsistent testing practices further compounded this fragmentation, as some patients declined HIV testing at the OPD, resulting in missed opportunities for integrated care. Similar challenges with fragmented services and varied integration models have been reported across sub-Saharan Africa [8–11].

Structural dimensions included the availability of equipment and medicines, staff shortages versus waiting times, accessibility, and stigma. Equipment shortages were a challenge, as there were BP machines that did not function and could not be purchased due to financial constraints. Ideally, all patients should be screened for DM, but this was not always done due to the cost of testing [12].

Process dimensions

Process-related barriers included fragmented record-keeping, inadequate staff training, and heavy workloads. Although HIV training was prioritised, training for NCD management was inconsistent, contributing to HCWs’ lack of confidence in delivering holistic care. Staff attitudes and time pressures also shaped patients’ experiences: HCWs noted that consultation times were often rushed due to high patient loads, limiting opportunities for counselling and relationship-building. Previous studies similarly identified stigma, weak HCW–patient relationships, and long waiting times as persistent challenges to integrated chronic care [13–15].

Outcome dimensions

At the outcome level, patient adherence and prioritisation of treatment were shaped by perceptions of disease severity and health education received. HCWs observed that patients often prioritised HIV care—where adherence was reinforced through health education—over NCD management, which received less emphasis. Patient satisfaction was mixed: while some valued continuity and familiarity with HCWs, others expressed dissatisfaction with long waits and limited consultation times. These findings align with broader literature emphasising the importance of patient engagement and self-efficacy in achieving positive outcomes.

Barriers of integrated care

Although most participants acknowledged that some integrated care occurred at the hospital, significant gaps in quality of care were evident. Interns were often responsible for managing PLHIV with comorbidities, but they lacked confidence in delivering integrated care. Despite existing guidelines, adherence was inconsistent, and weaknesses were noted in screening, consultations, and medication collection. The service relied heavily on interns, while at PHC level, nurse practitioners provided more consistent integrated care. Similar challenges in clinical knowledge and capacity have been reported internationally [16, 17].

Resource limitations

Under-resourced facilities were a major barrier. Equipment shortages, such as broken or poor-quality blood pressure machines, limited screening capacity. Financial constraints meant that routine screening for DM was not always possible, mirroring findings from other African settings [18–20].

Training gaps

Inadequate training emerged as another key barrier. While HIV training was prioritised by the Department of Health (DoH), NCD training was inconsistent and often limited to continuing medical education (CMEs) initiated by hospital staff. Donor funding strongly supported HIV training, but little was available for NCDs. This imbalance contributed to selective adherence to guidelines and left some HCWs underprepared for holistic care. Studies emphasise that training should target al.l cadres, with information cascaded to colleagues, rather than focusing only on staff working in HIV care [10, 19, 20].

Staff shortages and workload

Heavy patient loads, combined with staff shortages, further compromised the feasibility of integrated care. These challenges extended consultation times, reduced the quality of interactions, and fostered reluctance among HCWs to take on additional responsibilities. Task shifting to other cadres has been proposed and applied successfully in similar contexts to mitigate human resource constraints [21–23].

Screening challenges

NCD screening was another area of weakness. Shortages of urine dipsticks and scales for patients in wheelchairs limited comprehensive care. Blood pressure machines were prioritised for the OPD, leaving the ART clinic under-resourced for HTand DM screening. These constraints mirrored findings from Zimbabwe and other African studies [24, 25]. Without adequate funding to strengthen screening, opportunities for early detection and holistic management of comorbidities are missed.

Facilitators of integrated care

Despite multiple challenges, several facilitators were identified that support the feasibility of integrated HIV–NCD care.

Improved access and equity

Integrated care enhanced accessibility by allowing patients to receive multiple services at one site, saving time and travel costs. Studies from Malawi, South Africa, Swaziland, and Kenya also highlight how integration improves equity of access and continuity of care [26]. For example, Uganda’s integrated HIV, DM, and HT model reduced travel costs and improved retention [27]. Patients were more likely to continue treatment when facilities were close to their homes, convenient, and familiar.

Multidisciplinary care and task shifting

Integrated models encouraged staff to take on multidisciplinary roles, broadening expertise and improving patient outcomes. Evidence from Cambodia and African countries shows that task shifting to nurses and lay health workers reduces HIV-related stigma and strengthens NCD management capacity. Leveraging existing human resources is therefore a key strategy for scaling integrated care [19].

National initiatives and policy

In South Africa, the Ideal Clinic initiative supports integration of chronic care, while evidence from Ethiopia demonstrates the feasibility of integrating NCD and HIV care within the same facility [28, 29]. Integration also plays an important role in stigma reduction, as patients are no longer singled out when attending specialised HIV-only services [28, 29].

Stigma reduction

Integrated care significantly reduces stigma, which HCWs identified as a major barrier in fragmented services. Studies across South Africa, Uganda, and Cambodia confirm that patients are more willing to access services when HIV care is embedded within a broader chronic disease platform.(29, 31) Health education within communities is also crucial in sustaining stigma reduction [28].

Patient satisfaction, treatment prioritisation, and self-efficacy

Patient satisfaction

HCWs perceived that many patients were satisfied with integrated services, largely due to continuity and familiarity with staff. This perception aligns with reports from rural Kenya [30] and South Africa [31], where dissatisfaction was linked to long waiting times and missed appointments.In our study, staff shortages were noted to limit consultation time, contributing to mixed satisfaction levels.

Treatment prioritisation and adherence

Patients were more likely to prioritise HIV treatment than NCD care, partly because health education strongly emphasised HIV adherence. Stigma also influenced care-seeking behaviour, with some patients defaulting on treatment to avoid disclosure. These patterns have been reported elsewhere in sub-Saharan Africa and Cambodia, where integrated care was associated with reduced stigma and improved adherence [28, 32].

Health education and self-efficacy

Although health education was provided routinely, it focused heavily on HIV, with less emphasis on NCD management. This imbalance undermined patients’ understanding of the importance of long-term adherence to NCD treatment. Godongwana et al. [33] similarly reported limited information and support for comorbidities. Developing patients’ self-efficacy through counselling and balanced health education is critical for sustaining adherence across all conditions. Treatment fatigue—coping with multiple medications, side effects, and complex regimens—was also noted as a challenge, consistent with global findings [34].

Quality of care assessment

Overall, integrated care was perceived as beneficial but constrained by shortages of medicines, staff, and training opportunities. HCWs expressed concern that these barriers limited their ability to deliver holistic, high-quality care. Nevertheless, patient familiarity with providers fostered continuity and adherence, which HCWs interpreted as a proxy for patient satisfaction. Strengthening structural and process elements, alongside stigma reduction and robust health education, will be key to improving QoC.

Limitations and strengths

This study has several limitations. First, not all medical officers participated; only those directly involved in HIV and NCD management were included. Time constraints also limited participation by some HCWs, potentially narrowing the diversity of perspectives. Second, findings cannot be generalised to all HCWs in similar settings, as the study focused on one urban district hospital. Third, the data were collected in 2017. While this raises concerns about timeliness, the challenges and contextual factors identified remain relevant, as many of the same systemic issues persist.

Despite these limitations, the study has important strengths. It is the first to explore HCWs’ perceptions of quality of care for PLHIV with comorbidities in a district hospital within the eThekwini district. By applying the Donabedian framework, the study provides a structured analysis of quality of care across structural, process, and outcome dimensions. The mixed-methods design, triangulating quantitative and qualitative data, adds rigour and depth. Importantly, the findings offer actionable insights to strengthen integrated care, and they informed the implementation of integrated HIV–NCD services at the facility in 2025.

Conclusion

This study found a high level of acceptability for integrated HIV–NCD care among HCWs, who recognised its potential to reduce waiting times, diminish stigma, and improve treatment outcomes. However, persistent barriers—including staff shortages, limited training, negative attitudes, and resource constraints—hampered full implementation. Differences in the care of PLHIV compared to those with comorbidities highlighted gaps in training, task shifting, and adherence to guidelines.

Our findings underscore the need to implement integrated care in line with the ICDM framework, addressing structural, process, and outcome dimensions simultaneously. Future research should examine integrated care models across multiple facilities in KwaZulu-Natal to compare integrated versus fragmented services. Regular assessments of both HCW and patient perspectives should be incorporated into routine quality improvement to ensure more responsive and effective health service delivery.
