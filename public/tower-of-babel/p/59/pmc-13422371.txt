Treatment-related topological reorganization of the structural connectome in major depressive disorder with suicidal ideation
Authors: Xuexia Mao, Tong Zhu, Yahui Yang, Jingyi Cheng, Xiaoqin Li, Xiaoxue Liu, Furong Lv, Lian Du, Du Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMajor depressive disorder with suicidal ideation (MDD-SI) is associated with a high risk of mortality. The specific longitudinal alterations in brain structural networks associated with SI remain unclear. We aimed to identify treatment-related topological reorganization in patients with MDD, with and without SI.MethodsThis longitudinal study leveraged graph theory and structural MRI to analyze brain network changes in 101 MDD patients (66 MDD-SI, 35 MDD-nSI) and 41 healthy controls (HC). Network properties were evaluated before and after 2 weeks of clinical treatment, with global and nodal metrics calculated to track topological reorganization. Subsequently, group × time interaction analyses were conducted to characterize treatment-specific trajectories of brain network evolution.ResultsWe identified SI-specific structural connectome alterations, characterized by increased network regularization and topological deficits in the posterior cingulate gyrus (PCG), insula, and hippocampus. Longitudinal analysis revealed that nodal centrality in the PCG and insula was significantly associated with SI severity. Crucially, a significant longitudinal interaction effect was observed in the PCG.ConclusionOur findings underscore the important role of the PCG in the structural network recovery of MDD-SI. The topological changes of the PCG may provide insight into the neurobiological mechanisms underlying suicide-related pathophysiology and treatment response in MDD-SI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08231-6.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) imposes a substantial global disease burden [1]. The disorder is frequently complicated by suicidal ideation (SI) and behavior, which represent its most lethal features and increase mortality risk by approximately 20-fold [2]. Although current therapeutic strategies including pharmacotherapy and electroconvulsive therapy are clinically effective in reducing suicide risk, the neural mechanisms underlying this recovery are not fully understood [3, 4].

Accumulating evidence suggests that while suicide risk correlates with depressive symptom severity [5], it implies distinct neurobiological underpinnings that cannot be fully explained by symptom burden alone [6, 7]. Even after controlling for depression severity, individuals with MDD continue to exhibit a substantially elevated risk of suicide [2, 8]. Neuroimaging studies have consistently identified widespread structural brain abnormalities in MDD-SI. These alterations are predominantly distributed across the frontal [9, 10], parietal [11], temporal [12, 13], occipital [14], and limbic regions [15, 16], with particularly pronounced alterations in the cingulate gyrus and prefrontal cortex [13, 16]. Collectively, these findings indicate that MDD-SI is characterized by specific and relatively stable patterns of structural deficits [16, 17]. Yet, relying solely on regional alterations fails to represent the complex, system-level interactions of the brain, and it remains unclear how the overall topological architecture of these structural networks reconfigures during the recovery process.

Graph theory offers a quantitative framework for describing the topological organization of complex systems and has become an essential methodological approach in connectome research [18], establishing small-world architecture as a fundamental organizational principle of the healthy human brain [19]. Accumulating evidence from transdiagnostic research highlights the potential of structural connectome alterations as reliable biomarkers for monitoring treatment response. Significant network reorganization following clinical intervention has been well-documented across various psychiatric conditions, including bipolar disorder [20] and schizophrenia [21]. Studies employing graph theory methods have shown that patients with MDD-SI exhibit abnormal brain network topology, and that alterations in salience network (SN) and insula are associated with treatment response [22]. Moreover, the reduced connectivity between the SN and the default mode network (DMN) has been reported in MDD-SI patients relative to HC [23]. In our previous investigation, we demonstrated that effective treatment could reverse topological disruptions in the functional connectome, specifically restoring small-world efficiency and central executive network properties in MDD-SI [24]. Thus, the quantitative analysis of network topology emerges as a viable strategy for exploring objective neurobiological biomarkers of MDD-SI and its response to treatment.

Previous studies have primarily focused on static structural or network abnormalities at baseline in patients with MDD and suicidal ideation, thereby characterizing cross-sectional disease-related states. In contrast, longitudinal changes during treatment may not simply reflect baseline differences, but may instead indicate treatment-related alterations in the structural connectome and its topological organization [25, 26]. Recent MRI evidence suggests that brain structure and its associated white matter circuits may not be entirely static [27], and that measurable changes can occur over relatively short timescales following clinical treatment [28]. In particular, longitudinal studies of electroconvulsive therapy have reported changes in white matter microstructure and distributed brain circuits after treatment, with some alterations detectable even at early treatment stages [29, 30]. In addition, ECT is characterized by rapid onset and well-established efficacy, and a course of approximately 2 weeks (about 6 sessions) is generally considered to approach the lower bound of the recommended treatment course. Therefore, the changes observed at this stage may provide preliminary clues to possible early structural reorganization. On this basis, a 2-week follow-up interval may offer a window for exploring early treatment-related structural network changes.

Given this background, using a graph-based framework, we explored treatment-related topological reorganization of the whole-brain structural connectome in individuals with MDD-SI over a 2-week course of clinical treatment. Building on prior evidence of structural network abnormalities in MDD-SI [17], we hypothesized that MDD-SI is characterized by unique topological disruptions that are reversible with treatment. Specifically, we anticipated that the restoration of network topology would correlate with reductions in suicidal ideation, providing insight into the structural mechanisms of recovery.

Methods

Participants

The study protocol gained approval from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee at the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. We assessed 101 adult individuals diagnosed with MDD who underwent electroconvulsive therapy and/or pharmacotherapy from December 2023 to October 2025. The inclusion criteria comprised: (1) participants aged 18–60 years who were right-handed; (2) written informed consent obtained from both the participant and their legal representative; (3) a diagnosis of MDD based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria, confirmed by a score meeting the diagnostic criteria on the 24-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-24); (4) consent from the patient and their legal representative to receive either ECT in combination with pharmacotherapy or pharmacotherapy alone, with signed informed consent; (5) consent from the patient and family members for undergoing MRI scanning, with written informed consent acquired. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) presence of primary or secondary organic or degenerative intracranial disorders (e.g., Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease); (2) severe physical illnesses such as cardiopulmonary disease, endocrine disorders, malnutrition, or hepatic/renal insufficiency; (3) psychiatric disorders related to psychoactive substance use, schizophrenia spectrum disorders or other psychotic disorders, or antisocial personality disorder; (4) inability to complete clinical assessments (e.g., due to deafness, illiteracy, or inability to recognize characters); (5) presence of contraindications to MRI, including metallic implants.

The diagnosis was independently confirmed by two psychiatrists using the criteria outlined in the DSM-5. Inter-rater reliability between the two psychiatrists was assessed, and any discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion.

Additionally, 41 healthy individuals were selected based on these criteria: (1) they were aged 18–60 years and were right-handed; (2) ability to complete clinical rating scales; (3) agreement to undergo brain MRI scanning with written informed consent.

Clinical symptom assessment and treatment

The Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (M.I.N.I.) served as a tool to confirm if participants satisfied the diagnostic standards for major depressive disorder [31]. The severity of depressive symptoms was assessed using the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD). Item 3 of the HAMD was used to evaluate the severity of suicidal ideation. Participants scoring ≥ 2 on this item were classified as MDD-SI, whereas those with lower scores were categorized as MDD-nSI [32]. Suicide risk was evaluated with the Nurses’ Global Assessment of Suicide Risk (NGASR). The NGASR consists of 15 items with a total score of 25; scores of ≤ 5 indicate low risk, 6–8 moderate risk, 9–11 high risk, and ≥ 12 extremely high risk [33]. While the NGASR was used to evaluate global suicide risk, HAMD item 3 was specifically chosen for group stratification because it directly captures the acute, state-dependent nature of active suicidal ideation, which aligns optimally with our 2-week longitudinal design.

Participants underwent routine clinical management tailored to their specific symptom severity. The standard of care included pharmacotherapy using antidepressants, mood stabilizers, or antipsychotics as clinically indicated. Consistent with clinical guidelines for severe or refractory depression, a portion of the cohort received electroconvulsive therapy in addition to medication. Regardless of the specific modality, all interventions were aimed at rapid symptom stabilization. Data from these participants were pooled to examine the impact of effective clinical treatment on brain network topology. All subjects underwent MRI scanning at baseline and at the 2-week follow-up.

Imaging data acquisition

Structural MRI data were obtained using a United Imaging uMR 790 3.0-T scanner fitted with a 32-channel head coil at the Department of Radiology, The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. Before scanning, participants were instructed to remain relaxed and keep their eyes closed. To reduce head movement, foam padding was utilized. Structural images were acquired using a 3D SPGR sequence with the following parameters: TR/TE = 7 / 2.3 ms, Flip angle = 8°, FOV = 252 mm × 252 mm, Slice thickness = 1.0 mm, no gap, axial orientation parallel to the AC-PC line, Number of slices: 180 (covering the entire brain), and scan time = 238 s.

MRI data preprocessing

The analysis of structural MRI data was conducted using the Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM) software. In brief, each structural image was initially segmented into gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). This segmentation was performed using a unified segmentation model [34]. Following segmentation, the images were spatially normalized using the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) template. Finally, GM data were resampled to a voxel size of 2 mm³ and smoothed with a 6 mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel.

Brain network construction

To construct the structural brain network, the initial step involved parcellating the entire gray matter of the brain into 90 unique regions of interest (ROIs) utilizing the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) template, excluding cerebellar structures [35]. Morphological connectivity between these nodes was quantified using the Jensen-Shannon Divergence Similarity Estimation (JSSE) approach [36]. Specifically, we calculated the probability density function (PDF) of gray matter density for each ROI. Pairwise similarities were then derived via JSSE to define the network edges, producing individual 90 × 90 structural connectivity matrices.

Network properties

Brain network topology was analyzed across a sparsity range of 0.10 to 0.34 (step = 0.01) using the GRETNA toolbox [37]. This range was selected because it is commonly used in graph-theoretical studies of structural brain networks and provides a balance between overly sparse and overly dense networks [38]. The step size was set to ensure sufficiently fine-grained sampling across thresholds. This specific interval was selected to guarantee the estimability of small-world attributes, with the small-world index (σ) exceeding 1.0. To eliminate potential biases arising from arbitrary thresholding, network metrics were summarized using the area under the curve (AUC), a strategy proven to provide robust and sensitive summary scalars for detecting topological alterations. At the global level, we quantified small-world properties (clustering coefficient [Cp], characteristic path length [Lp], normalized clustering coefficient [γ], normalized characteristic path length [λ], and small-worldness [σ]) and efficiency measures (local efficiency [Eloc] and global efficiency [Eglob]) [24]. Furthermore, regional roles were assessed using three nodal centrality indices: nodal degree, nodal efficiency, and nodal betweenness centrality [39].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed utilizing R software (version 4.3.2). Given the non-normal distribution of the brain network’s topological attributes, inter-group differences were evaluated through a generalized linear model (GLM) [40], with sex, age, BMI, treatment modality and HAMD (only for comparisons between MDD-SI subgroups) accounted for as covariates. We controlled for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction; only results surviving an FDR-corrected threshold of p < 0.05 were reported.

We utilized partial Spearman correlation (correlation coefficient: rs) analyses to assess the associations between topological metrics and clinical ratings while adjusting for confounding factors. Specifically, for correlations involving general depression severity (total HAMD scores), sex, age, BMI and treatment modality were included as covariates. For correlations specifically targeting suicidal ideation and risk (i.e., HAMD item 3 and NGASR scores), the total HAMD score was additionally included as a covariate alongside sex, age, treatment modality and BMI. This strategy was employed to ensure that the identified associations with suicidality were independent of overall depressive symptom burden, preventing over-adjustment while accurately isolating suicide-specific neurobiological correlates. Group × time interaction effects were examined using a generalized linear mixed-effects modeling framework, with treatment modality included as a fixed effect to control for intervention-specific confounding (network metric ~ group * time + treatment + age + sex + BMI + (1|subject)). Effect sizes for the interaction term were expressed as standardized regression coefficients (β) with their respective 95% confidence intervals (CI). All correlation analyses were performed using an FDR-corrected significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic and clinical profiles

The study included 41 HCs and 101 patients with MDD. The MDD group was further divided into 66 patients with MDD-SI and 35 patients with MDD-nSI. Detailed demographic and clinical scale data are presented in Table 1. Between the HC group and the overall MDD group, there were no statistically significant differences in age (p = 0.351) or sex distribution (p = 0.852). The HC group showed a significantly greater BMI than the MDD group (p = 0.039). As anticipated, the MDD group exhibited higher HAMD (p < 0.001) and HAMD-3 (p < 0.001) scores than the HC group.

Within the MDD cohort, we compared the MDD-SI and MDD-nSI. The MDD-SI group was significantly younger than the MDD-nSI group (p = 0.011). Alterations of brain network properties

MDD vs. HC

The specific global topological properties did not differ significantly between MDD and HCs across the defined threshold range. Compared to HCs, the MDD group showed increased nodal degree and efficiency in the right superior frontal gyrus (dorsolateral), right inferior frontal gyrus (triangular), and right inferior occipital gyrus, and decreases in the right gyrus rectus and right superior temporal gyrus (Table 2; Fig. 2a).

MDD-SI vs. HC

As shown in Fig. 1a, the Eglob value in the MDD-SI was lower compared with HCs (p = 0.021, Cohen’s d = -1.716). Compared to HCs, the MDD-SI showed increased nodal centrality (degree and efficiency) across multiple regions, including the right superior frontal gyrus (dorsolateral), right middle frontal gyrus, right inferior frontal gyrus (triangular), and bilateral insula. Decreased nodal degree and efficiency were observed in the left and right hippocampus (Table 2; Fig. 2b).

MDD-nSI vs. HC

As shown in Fig. 1a, the γ, Eglob and σ in the MDD-nSI group were higher in comparison with HCs (p = 0.039, Cohen’s d = 1.862; p = 0.033, Cohen’s d = 1.927; p = 0.021, Cohen’s d = 2.087, respectively), and Lp was lower than HCs (p = 0.046, Cohen’s d = -1.800). In MDD-nSI, nodal centrality was increased relative to HCs in the right superior frontal gyrus (dorsolateral), right supplementary motor area, left superior parietal gyrus, and right Heschl gyrus (Table 2; Fig. 2c).

MDD-SI vs. MDD-nSI

At the global level (Fig. 1a), the MDD-SI showed higher Cp compared to MDD-nSI (p = 0.036, Cohen’s d = 0.650). In comparison to MDD-nSI, MDD-SI demonstrated significant increases in both nodal degree and efficiency in the left middle frontal gyrus. Conversely, decreased nodal centrality was detected in the right posterior cingulate gyrus, bilateral hippocampus, bilateral caudate nucleus, and right pallidum (Table 2; Fig. 2d).

MDD-SI vs. HC after treatment

After treatment, no significant differences in global network properties were observed between the MDD-SI and HCs. At the nodal level, the MDD-SI showed increased nodal centrality in the right inferior frontal gyrus (opercular) and right insula, and decreased nodal centrality in the right gyrus rectus, left cuneus, and right superior temporal gyrus, compared to the HCs (Table 3; Fig. 2e).

MDD-nSI vs. HC after treatment

After treatment, no significant differences in global network properties were observed between the MDD-nSI and HCs. At the nodal level, the MDD-nSI exhibited increased nodal degree and efficiency in the right inferior frontal gyrus (opercular), right inferior frontal gyrus (triangular), right supplementary motor area, and right precuneus versus the HCs. Decreases in nodal degree and efficiency were observed in the right fusiform gyrus (Table 3; Fig. 2f).

MDD-SI subgroup before and after treatment

Compared to baseline, the MDD-SI showed increased global γ and λ values after treatment (Fig. 1b). After treatment, the MDD-SI demonstrated higher nodal centrality in the right hippocampus, left thalamus, and right superior temporal gyrus, and lower nodal centrality in the right median cingulate gyrus and right superior parietal gyrus, when compared to baseline (Table 3; Fig. 2g). Comparing MDD-SI and MDD-nSI, nominally significant group × time interactions were observed for the λ (F = 4.530, p = 0.033; β = 0.725, 95% CI: 0.049 to 1.400), and nodal efficiency exhibited a nominally significant group × time interaction in the right posterior cingulate gyrus (F = 3.958, p = 0.046; β = -0.783, 95% CI: -1.565 to -0.003).

Furthermore, to assess the potential influence of treatment heterogeneity, we conducted supplementary stratified analyses to evaluate topological changes before and after treatment within the ECT and pharmacotherapy subgroups separately (details are provided in Table S1).

Discussion

The present study systematically elucidates the aberrant topological architecture of brain networks in MDD-SI and its therapeutic remodeling. At the global level, we identified a significant loss of network efficiency in pre-treatment patients, which was negatively correlated with the severity of both depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation. This aligns with connectomics evidence suggesting that impaired global integration serves as a neural substrate for the severity of psychopathology [32, 41]. Spatially, our results delineate a hierarchical pattern of disruption: while MDD patients generally exhibit frontal-limbic abnormalities compared to HCs, MDD-SI is distinguished by broader involvement of the frontal-basal ganglia-limbic circuit compared to MDD-nSI. This distinct spatial distribution reinforces the critical role of hippocampal-prefrontal and sensory integration pathways in SI pathology, implying that suicidal ideation is not merely severe depression but involves specific maladaptation in circuits governing impulse control and decision-making.

The human brain connectome is organized according to a small-world principle. This feature is believed to promote efficient information processing and communication across large-scale brain networks [42], which can be classified into regular and random networks [43]. We found that MDD-SI showed significantly reduced global efficiency compared to HCs, and displayed increased clustering coefficient compared to MDD-nSI. This pattern of reduced global efficiency coupled with elevated local clustering points to a transition toward network “regularization” or isolation in MDD-SI [44], reflecting a profound impairment in the brain’s capacity for long-range information transmission. Global efficiency in the MDD-SI group was negatively correlated with HAMD scores, suggesting that among individuals with MDD-SI, greater symptom severity is associated with reduced efficiency of global information transmission in the brain [45]. Previous neuroimaging studies have indicated that brain networks in MDD-SI are often characterized by topological disruptions marked by reduced efficiency [46]. These findings suggest that MDD-SI is characterized by a topological shift toward network regularization, indicating a constraint on global information transfer efficiency within the structural connectome.

At the regional level, compared with HCs, MDD-SI primarily exhibited network abnormalities within the central executive network (CEN), default mode network (DMN), and salience network (SN), with altered nodal centralities predominantly localized to the superior frontal gyrus, middle frontal gyrus, inferior frontal gyrus, insula, and hippocampus, all of which are implicated in the pathophysiology of MDD-SI [47]. Compared with the MDD-nSI group, the MDD-SI group exhibited significant topological deviations mainly within the posterior cingulate gyrus (PCG) and hippocampus. Notably, nodal centralities in the PCG, insula, and hippocampus were significantly correlated with SI severity after controlling for depressive symptom severity (HAMD). The PCG serves as a pivotal hub of the posterior DMN and is heavily involved in self-referential processing and episodic memory retrieval [48]. Structural alterations in this region may underlie the maladaptive rumination and ‘cognitive entrapment’ characteristic of suicidal individuals, preventing them from disengaging from negative self-focus [49]. As a central hub of the salience network, the insula integrates interoceptive and emotional information, and abnormalities in its nodal topology may contribute to dysregulated psychache and deficient cognitive state switching associated with suicidality [50]. Together with hippocampal deficits, which are linked to impaired stress regulation and over general autobiographical memory [51], our findings suggest that topological deficits in these hubs may represent a specific neurobiological signature associated with suicidal ideation, extending beyond the effects attributable to general depressive symptom severity.

In line with previous studies [52, 53], we found that clinical treatment has a specific impact on the structural brain networks of MDD-SI. Previous studies have reported treatment-related structural plasticity in the posterior cingulate cortex and insula [28, 54], with some evidence linking these changes to treatment response [54, 55], thereby providing preliminary support for the mechanistic relevance of the topological remodeling observed in our study. Longitudinal analysis revealed that, despite significant clinical improvement, the structural brain networks of patients with MDD-SI retained a topological profile characterized by high regularization. This dissociation suggests that structural network alterations may reflect a relatively stable vulnerability associated with suicidal risk and represent a slower-evolving process that lags behind symptomatic and functional improvement [56]. At the nodal level, we observed distinct clinical correlations within the MDD-SI group. Post-treatment centrality in the insula was associated with residual SI severity, while the longitudinal change in PCG centrality significantly correlated with the reduction in suicidal symptoms. Crucially, the PCG exhibited a significant group × time interaction in nodal efficiency, indicating that patients with MDD-SI followed a distinct trajectory of structural network reorganization compared with those without suicidal ideation [57, 58]. This divergence aligns with baseline observations of PCG topological abnormalities specific to MDD-SI, suggesting that the posterior DMN hub’s early reorganization may be uniquely tied to SI-related neural vulnerability [32]. The correlation between PCG remodeling and symptom reduction implies that restoring the topological efficiency of this DMN hub parallels the resolution of suicidal intent, potentially by alleviating the rigid self-referential processing identified at baseline [49]. In addition, the sustained association of the insula with residual symptoms highlights the salience network’s role in maintaining the “alertness” to negative internal stimuli [59]. Collectively, these results portray a nuanced recovery trajectory where PCG topological alterations track the dynamic remission of suicidal intent, whereas insular topology may serve as an index of enduring neural vulnerability.

Although this study has revealed multiple potential neural network markers, several limitations remain. First, the construction of the network relying on a single parcellation template may affect the robustness of the results. Previous studies have indicated that different parcellation methods can significantly influence graph-theoretical metrics [60, 61]. Future work should employ multiple templates and multi-scale validation to enhance the reproducibility of the conclusions. Second, the treatment modalities in this study included electroconvulsive therapy and medication. Future research should isolate the independent contributions of treatment components through stricter grouping or randomization [62]. Treatment heterogeneity may have influenced the observed network changes, as different interventions may exert partially distinct effects on structural reorganization. Although we performed supplementary stratified analyses, these findings should still be interpreted with caution due to the limited sample size. Future studies with larger samples are needed to further examine treatment-specific network alterations. Moreover, given the relatively short follow-up interval, it remains to be further established whether these early changes reflect stable treatment-related remodeling rather than transient physiological fluctuations or nonspecific effects. Third, the current study design lacks treatment blinding and randomized controls, which may introduce expectancy bias. Furthermore, our findings are entirely based on a single-center cohort. Given the inherent variability in connectomics data across different scanners and patient demographics, the absence of an external validation cohort limits the generalizability of our results. Consequently, identifying the PCG or other key circuits (e.g., the hippocampus-DMN and insula-PCG axes) as definitive clinical biomarkers is premature at this stage. Future research must employ larger, multi-center cohorts with randomized controlled designs and independent cross-validation to rigorously establish the predictive value of these structural signatures. Fourth, future studies should consider integrating structural network metrics with functional connectivity or other multimodal imaging measures to improve the predictive validity of suicide risk. Such multimodal approaches may better capture complementary aspects of brain network organization and provide a more comprehensive framework for understanding suicide-related neural vulnerability and treatment response. Fifth, this study was not pre-registered, which should be acknowledged as a limitation. As this was an observational study, prospective registration was not performed at the time. Future studies should adopt prospective registration to further improve the rigor, transparency, and reproducibility of the research. Finally, suicidal ideation was classified using a single item (HAMD-3) [32]. While this approach effectively captures acute state-dependent ideation, future studies should employ comprehensive scales to assess suicidal intent more precisely.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study identifies widespread topological disruptions in the structural connectome of MDD-SI, with prominent involvement of DMN and SN hub regions. Longitudinally, the PCG serves as a crucial hub for changes in the structural connectivity of the brain network, highlighting a divergent therapeutic recovery trajectory in MDD-SI compared to MDD-nSI. These findings provide novel insights into the distinct neurobiological substrates of suicidal ideation and suggest that structural network reorganization may be a promising avenue for understanding treatment response, which warrants further validation in larger, multicenter studies.
