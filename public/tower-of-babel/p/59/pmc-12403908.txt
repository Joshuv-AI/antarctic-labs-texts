Assessment of Infection Prevention and Control in Somali Healthcare Facilities using the WHO Infection Prevention and Control Assessment Framework
Authors: Abdikarim Abdi Adam, Hassan Dahir Mohamed, Felix Emeka Anyiam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImproving healthcare-associated infections, fighting antibiotic resistance, and controlling outbreaks are all made possible by infection prevention and control, a significant global public health concern, particularly in Africa. To pinpoint areas that needed improvement, this study aimed to assess Infection Prevention and Control in Somali Healthcare Facilities using the WHO Infection Prevention and Control Assessment Framework.Methods307 healthcare facilities in Somalia, or 30% of the 1,023 facilities listed by the Federal Ministry of Health, had their IPC practices evaluated by cross-sectional research in October and November 2022. Descriptive statistics were used in the study to examine data categorized by facility type, ownership, and location. The facilities were divided into four IPC levels—inadequate, basic, intermediate, and advanced using the World Health Organization's Infection Prevention and Control Assessment Framework (IPCAF).ResultsThe mean overall IPCAF score was 136/800, which indicates inadequate IPC practices. 85% of the institutions were public hospitals, while the majority (68.83%) were primary healthcare centers. CC1 (IPC Program) and CC 5 (Multi-modal Strategies) got the lowest values out of the eight IPC core components, suggesting major improvement. while CC 7 (Workload, Staffing, and Bed Occupancy) and CC 8 (Environments, Materials, and Equipment) had the greatest results. Inadequate IPCAFs were found in all States. The results highlight the urgent need for national and regional policies to give IPC programs and infrastructure funding the Highest priority, mainly in low-performing areas and primary healthcare settings. Regional differences highlight the need for specialized approaches to increase IPC capacity in every state.ConclusionsThe initial IPCAF assessment of Somali healthcare facilities reveals inadequate IPC levels, highlighting the need for urgent intervention to improve practices, ensure patient safety, and support the development of a national IPC policy in Somalia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13756-025-01584-9.

Introduction

Infection prevention and control (IPC) directly affects the health and safety of both service users and healthcare personnel, it is a crucial part of all health systems [1]. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and hospital-acquired infections (HAIs) can still be successfully avoided with infection prevention and control (IPC) strategies [2]. HAIs are infections acquired through admission, treatment, or procedure in healthcare facilities, especially among immuno-compromised patients. These infections, which do not exist at admission, increase susceptibility and damage, particularly during epidemics, which worry the global IPC system [3]. HAIs are often caused by multidrug-resistant organisms, pose important risks to patients, visitors, and healthcare workers, strain health systems, and increase costs. They are among the most common adverse events in healthcare [1]. More than 1.4 million people worldwide are infected with HAIs [4]. The prevalence of healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) is estimated to be 25% in low and middle-income countries (LMICs) and 7% in high-income countries. This significantly heightens the burden on the healthcare system, leading to longer hospital stays, Higher work pressure on healthcare providers, a rise in AMR, and financial burden on the health system [5]. Approximately 90,000 people die from healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) in the US each year, which amounts to over 2 million recorded cases. Mortality rates associated with HAIs range from 7 to 46%, and can cause prolonged hospital admissions of 5 to 21 days in Asia. According to reports, patients with HAIs had a higher mortality rate in Africa, approximately 22%. [6]. In Somalia, as in many LMICs, overcrowding, a lack of staff, poor cleaning, a lack of handwashing facilities, poor hand hygiene compliance, a lack of IPC training, ongoing conflicts, political instability, and inadequate infrastructure have all had an impact on infection prevention and control (IPC) practices [2]. Additionally, AMR is a worldwide health emergency that calls for a harmonized response. It is associated with increased mortality, prolonged hospital admissions, and financial loss, particularly in LMICs [7]. In healthcare settings, improving infection prevention and control (IPC) provides practical ways to reduce the risk of HAIs and AMR [8]. The Infection Prevention and Control Assessment Framework (IPCAF), a standardized tool for assessing IPC practices in healthcare facilities, was created by the WHO. A systematic, score-based questionnaire with 81 evidence-based indicators that are in line with the eight fundamental IPC components suggested by the WHO is used. To facilitate alignment with international standards, the tool analyzes both strengths and shortcomings. IPCAF can be used in a variety of healthcare facilities, both for self-assessment and joint assessments. Based on their scores, facilities are categorized into four levels: Basic (partial implementation, further improvement required), Intermediate (most components in place, continued development recommended), Advanced (full implementation in line with WHO standards), and Inadequate (major gaps, urgent action needed) [9, 10]. Important advantage of the IPCAF lies in its capacity for repeated use, allowing healthcare facilities to scientifically monitor infection prevention and control (IPC) trends and measure progress over time, mainly in response to identified shortages [11].HAIs and AMR can have a serious and diverse impact, resulting in everything from prolonged hospital admissions and chronic problems to irreversible impairment and early mortality. Furthermore, the patient, family, and community are all impacted socially and psychologically. This puts more pressure on the healthcare system and raises expenses [1]. Studies from Austria, Indonesia, India, Japan, Turkey, China, and Germany reveal advanced IPCAF Score levels [12–18]. This indicates the IPC core components are fully implemented in alignment with WHO recommendations. Additionally, studies from Malawi, Pakistan, and Rwanda reveal intermediate IPCAF Score levels [3, 19, 20], indicating that most IPC practices were implemented successfully. In contrast, Studies conducted in African countries, including Uganda, Côte d’Ivoire, Ethiopia, Bangladesh, depicted Basic IPCAF Score levels [21–24], indicating certain functional IPC elements, but they are not properly implemented, and they must be improved further. A global report on IPC shows a significant positive relationship between a country's World Bank income rank and IPC implementation at the national level. Compared with low-income countries, high-income countries are more likely to have advanced IPC implementation, with significant differences in IPC program implementation observed according to income level [1].Limited data on infection prevention and control (IPC) in Somalia hinders efforts to manage healthcare-associated infections (HAIs). The lack of a harmonized IPC policy, poor infrastructure, staff shortages, and inadequate sanitation complicate IPC implementation. Without assessments like the WHO’s IPCAF, identifying gaps and improving practices is challenging. Developing national IPC policies and data is crucial to strengthen Somalia's health system and address HAIs and antimicrobial resistance (AMR). The study aims to the assessment of Infection Prevention and Control in Somali Healthcare Facilities using the WHO Infection Prevention and Control Assessment Framework, finds important gaps, and seeks to provide information for locally led, actionable initiatives that will increase patient safety, lower healthcare-associated infections (HAIs), AMR, and improve the general standard of healthcare in Somalia.

Methods

Study design and participants

A nationwide infection prevention and control (IPC) cross-sectional survey was carried out in October and November 2022 by WHO Somalia in collaboration with the Federal Ministry of Health (FMOH) and State Ministries of Health. The WHO Infection Prevention and Control Assessment Framework (IPCAF) was used in the survey to assess the state of key IPC components at the national and healthcare facility levels. A list of 1,023 medical facilities across the five federal states and the Benadir administration was created by the Ministry of Health after discussion on the IPC situation in Somalia. A 30% sample of these facilities—totaling 307—was surveyed, with 204 located in urban areas and 103 in rural areas. The sample comprised 95 hospitals and 212 primary healthcare centers. The distribution of the assessed facilities was as follows: 39 in Benadir Administration, 55 in Southwest State, 33 in Hirshabelle State, 40 in Jubaland State, 53 in Galmudug State, and 87 in Puntland State. The study used simple random sampling, meaning that each facility had an equal chance of being selected.

Sampling Design

We selected 307 healthcare facilities through simple random selection throughout Somalia, which means that each healthcare facility has an equal chance of being selected. To improve geographic representation and facilitate regional comparisons, we also used a state-level stratified sampling technique. During this stage, healthcare facilities were chosen by disproportional simple random sampling from each state, which was considered a distinct stratum. This two-phase sampling design ensures both national-level representation and sufficient coverage of healthcare facilities in every state. Slovin's formula was utilized to determine the suitable sample size for this study, resulting in a minimum necessary sample size was 288 healthcare facilities. Assessment Process and Data Collection Procedure

In cooperation with state ministries and the Federal Ministry of Health (FMoH), an IPC consultant chose 307 medical facilities throughout Somalia to take part in the study. At the primary, secondary, and tertiary healthcare levels, these facilities included both public and private healthcare facilities. All urban and rural areas were included in the sample, which was intended to be nationally representative. The facilities were split as follows: 87 in Puntland State, 40 in Jubaland State, 53 in Galmudug State, 33 in Hirshabelle State, 55 in Southwest State, and 39 in Benadir Administration.

To collect data, the FMoH and state ministries assembled a team of 55 investigators (48 men and 7 women). The following was the distribution of investigators by region: The Benadir Administration has five, Hirshabelle has four, Southwest has fourteen, Jubaland has twelve, Galmudug has ten, and Puntland has ten. The FMoH, WHO, and State Ministries of Health jointly monitored and oversaw the investigator process, which used the IPCAF questionnaire included in the ODK toolbox. To guarantee the accuracy and completeness of the data, the data collectors got hands-on training on the WHO's IPCAF evaluation tool, data gathering strategies, and interview procedures. Twenty-seven supervisors from the FMoH, state ministries, and WHO oversaw the data collection process. We requested secondary data from the Department of Public Health, the Ministry of Health, Somalia, and received it to perform the study.

Data Quality Assurance

We initiated this process prior to any data being collected, defining the roles of those responsible for data collection, the locations and methods for data collection, and the software that would be used. Every investigator was given hands-on training, coaching, and mentoring to guarantee they were proficient with the designated software while gathering data. After the data was collected, a team of 27 supervisors monitored the process to ensure that the data was appropriately documented, reviewed, updated, coded, cleaned, and stored.

Multiple measures were taken to ensure high data quality: standardized tools—for instance, the WHO/UNICEF Healthcare Facility Questionnaire tailored to the Somali context; digital data collection using ODK to reduce manual errors; fieldwork supervision by Federal Ministry of Health and WHO personnel; daily form validation; swift resolution of inconsistencies; systematic data backups; and, finally, data cleaning and verification in Excel before analysis in STATA 16.1. Together, these steps strengthened the validity and reliability of the data.

Data Analysis

In October and November 2022, a coordinated data collection effort was conducted at healthcare facilities. Frequency distributions were used in a descriptive analysis of the categorical variables to summarize the data, such as means, medians, interquartile ranges, frequencies, and percentages were employed. The distribution of the data was evaluated using a normality test after data collection, to provide an overview of the facilities'features. Score values from IPCAF, which were used to evaluate the effectiveness of IPC programs at the Healthcare facilities visited, were the main focus of the analysis. The IPCAF score, which indicated the overall IPC performance of each healthcare facility, was the dependent variable in the analysis. The independent variables were facility type (primary vs. secondary), ownership (public vs. private), and location (rural vs. urban). The analysis was built on the score values gained, which quantified the performance of the IPC program of the healthcare facilities visited. STATA version 16.1 and Excel were used to clean and analyze the survey data.

Results

A 30% sample size of 307 healthcare facilities was chosen for the study out of the 1,023 total Somalia health facilities surveyed. These facilities were health facilities in six Somali states included in the study; Puntland had the largest (28.34%), followed by Southwest (17.92%), Galmudug (17.26%), and Jubaland (13.03%), followed by Benadir (12.7%) and Hirshabelle (10.75%). Primary healthcare facilities represented the majority of the facilities evaluated (69.06%), followed by secondary healthcare facilities (30.94%). 33.55% of the facilities were in rural locations, while the remainder (66.45%) were in urban areas. Examination of IPC Core Components in Somali Healthcare Facilities

Based on the WHO standard range for IPCAF categorization, the total IPCAF score was 136/800 (IQR: 13.1, 18.9), which indicates an inadequate IPC level (see Table 2). The following were the scores for each of the IPC core components: The scores for CC1 (IPC program) were 9.06, CC2 (IPC guidelines) were 16.34, CC3 (IPC education and training) were 14.40, CC4 (HAI surveillance) were 14.83, CC5 (multi-modal strategies) were 6.42, CC6 (monitoring and audit feedback) was 17.48, CC7 (workload and staffing) was 23.11, and CC8 (built environment) was 34.46. Utilizing the Component-Based Analysis Method

IPC Program (CC1): The IPC program had a median (IQR) score of 6.25 (0, 6.25). Only 6% of healthcare facilities had an IPC program with unclear goals, while the majority (87%) did not have. Another 6% had a program with goals and an annual activity plan. Furthermore, more than 87% lacked IPC teams, IPC professionals, or staff members who were specifically focused on IPC activities. Additionally, over 90% of institutions did not have an IPC committee.

IPC Guidelines (CC2): The median (IQR) score was 17.5 (2.5, 20). The majority of facilities had no guidelines for hand hygiene, transmission-based precautions, outbreak management, surgical site infection prevention, or health worker safety, and over 77% of the guidelines were inconsistent with national or international standards. Additionally, 84% of facilities did not implement the guidelines, 79% of healthcare workers lacked IPC training, and 83% of front-line workers were not involved in the planning or implementation of the IPC guidelines. Additionally, 80% of facilities did not routinely monitor the implementation of the guidelines.

IPC Education and Training (CC3): 20 (0, 20) was the median (IQR) score. Less than 20% of facilities had IPC leads who were trained, whereas 25% had non-IPC professionals who were qualified to train employees. 81% did not provide any IPC training, while only 4% regularly trained new hires. The majority of employees lacked IPC training, including managers and cleaners. Furthermore, 83% of facilities failed to incorporate IPC training into clinical practice, and 72% did not assess the efficacy of the training.

HAI Surveillance (CC4): The median (IQR) score was 27.5 (0, 27.5). A defined HAI surveillance program was present in less than 5% of facilities, while over 90% lacked surveillance personnel; over 78% lacked IT support, standardized data collection methods, or reliable case definitions; and about 70% did not review data, evaluate surveillance priorities, or maintain microbiology and laboratory support for surveillance.

Multi-modal Strategies (CC5): The median score was 0 (0, 0). Multi-modal measures for IPC interventions, such as monitoring, feedback, communication, and cooperation with patient safety and quality improvement teams, were not used by nearly 90% of facilities.

Monitoring, Audit, and Feedback (CC6): The median (IQR) score was 27.5 (2.5, 30). The majority of institutions did not perform surveys or feedback audits, and more than 90% lacked monitoring and audit procedures, with 80% lacking clear monitoring plans with specific objectives.

Workload, Staffing, and Bed Occupancy (CC7): The median (IQR) score was 40 (0, 40). While 70% of facilities lacked staffing assessments, only 22% of facilities had acceptable staffing levels. Over 54% of facilities failed to guarantee one patient per bed, more than 50% did not maintain sufficient distance (> 1 m) between beds, and the majority did not satisfy the WHO or national staffing guidelines.

Built Environment, Materials, and Equipment (CC8): The median (IQR) score was 40 (12.5–52.5). Approximately 28% of facilities had operational hand hygiene stations, 42% had access to water, and 38% lacked any. Reliable power sources were present in 51% of the facilities, and 23% had improved latrines. Although just 28% of the facilities had the proper cleaning supplies, 57% of them had ventilation. Just 23% of facilities maintained consistently sterile equipment, while the majority lacked adequate waste collection systems. Of the facilities, 34% had disposable goods available.

All of the assessed states, regardless of facility level, currently have insufficient IPC measures in place. This emphasizes how urgently state-level efforts to increase IPC capacity, offer training, and effectively distribute resources are needed. The relative progress made by PL and JL suggests that they could be used as early models for improvement plans. Healthcare Facilities and IPCAF Scores: Distribution of Healthcare Facilities by Total IPCAF Score Level and Percentages

According to the WHO reference range, we examined the IPCAF scores of healthcare facilities and found that most of them (229, 74.6%) were categorized as inadequate. 52 facilities (17%) were graded at the basic level, 25 facilities (8.1%) attained the intermediate level, and just one facility (Waberi MCH-Primary Healthcare in Bay, Southwest Somalia) attained the advanced level (0.3%).

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive Assessment of Infection Prevention and Control in Somali Healthcare Facilities using the WHO Infection Prevention and Control Assessment Framework(IPCAF) across the 307 assessed healthcare facilities. The overall IPCAF score for all Somali healthcare facilities is below 200, indicating Inadequate IPCAF that there is a need for substantial improvement in the quality of infection prevention and control. An analysis of the eight IPC core components revealed significant gaps across all areas. The built environment (CC8) achieved the highest score, reflecting relative improvements in infrastructure, such as access to water and hygiene facilities, in some healthcare facilities. In contrast, the IPC program (CC1) had the lowest score, emphasizing the absence of structured IPC programs, dedicated personnel, and functional IPC teams in the majority of facilities. Other core components, such as IPC guidelines (CC2), education and training (CC3), and HAI surveillance activities (CC4), also scored poorly, underscoring systemic deficiencies in policy development, staff training, and data collection mechanisms. Similarly, multi-modal strategies (CC5), monitoring and feedback mechanisms (CC6), and workload and staffing (CC7) demonstrated low scores, highlighting the lack of integrated IPC strategies, insufficient monitoring practices, and inadequate staffing levels in healthcare facilities. In almost all the healthcare facilities evaluated, essential IPC activities such as IPC programs, guidelines, and training were largely absent. This absence significantly hindered the ability of facilities to achieve higher performance levels. The total IPCAF score across all evaluated healthcare facilities in Somalia was 136 out of 800, reflecting an insufficient level of infection prevention and control implementation. These findings underscore the urgent need for significant improvements in IPC practices and infrastructure. Similar studies have been conducted in public hospitals in Islamabad, signifying a wider regional challenge in achieving acceptable IPC standards [25]. Comparable IPCAF scores between Islamabad and Somalia highlight issues that are widespread in LMICs, such as a lack of infrastructure, a lack of resources, and an inability to deploy IPC. In contrast to our study's results, which showed an inadequate application of infection prevention and control (IPC) measures in Somali healthcare facilities, numerous countries have stated significantly advanced IPCAF Level performance these including China, Turkey, Japan, Austria, India, and Indonesia [8]. These countries have successfully implemented all core IPC components in alignment with the World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations. Intermediate IPCAF levels were observed in countries such as Rwanda, Malawi, and Pakistan [8], reflecting considerable progress toward the effective implementation of IPC strategies. In contrast, many low- and middle-income countries, including several in Africa—Uganda, Côte d’Ivoire, Ethiopia, and Bangladesh [8], continue to run at a basic IPC level. These lower scores highlight substantial gaps in the implementation of WHO-recommended IPC core components [8]. Somalia is different from these nations because of its prolonged political conflict, unstable healthcare system, lack of financing, acute scarcity of skilled medical personnel, and crucial IPC infrastructure. In comparison to Advanced, Intermediate, and basic IPCAF, these systemic issues make it difficult to execute even the most fundamental IPC measures, which leads to noticeably lower IPCAF scores. In Somalia, regional disparities in IPC implementation were evident, with none of the regional states meeting optimal standards. Some regions performed relatively better (Puntland and Jubaland), but the overall findings indicate widespread inadequacy in IPC practices in the country. This suggests an urgent need for targeted interventions tailored to the country’s needs to strengthen core components and address systemic barriers, ultimately improving the ability of healthcare facilities to prevent healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) and combat antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Advanced IPCAF levels were found in middle- and high-income countries, whereas lower IPCAF levels were observed in low-income countries [8]. A global report on IPC shows a significant positive relationship between a country's World Bank income rank and IPC implementation at the national level. Compared with low-income countries, high-income countries are more likely to have advanced IPC implementation, with significant differences in IPC program implementation observed according to income level [1]. The study highlights the crucial need for a national IPC policy, staff training, better-quality WASH infrastructure, surveillance systems, context-specific approaches, and a Global partnership to reinforce IPC and address Somalia’s health system challenges.

Policy Implications

To create a robust IPC system in Somalia, policy implications highlight the necessity of a multi-sectoral coordination and resource-based approach. Resolving systemic weaknesses is essential for protecting the nation's delicate healthcare system from new health risks as well as for preventing infections linked to healthcare.

Strengths and Limitations

The study's Strengths include a national assessment using the WHO IPCAF framework that covers Public, Private, urban, rural, Primary, and Secondary Healthcare facilities across all Somali states, together with extensive data collection and investigator training. The limitations include its cross-sectional design, which prevents causal inferences, the possibility of reporting bias regardless of efforts to ensure data quality, and the absence of qualitative methods to capture full understandings of IPC practices. Multi-modal strategies have not been established as they are relatively new ideas that pose problems of understanding for stakeholders and are challenging to inform in our context of a low-income country (Table 4). Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of infection prevention and control (IPC) in Somali healthcare facilities, identifying notable deficiencies in IPC measures. The study's overall IPCAF score is 136/800. Pointing to problems with investment, training, and infrastructure. Resources, IPC experience, and strong government leadership are necessary for reforms. Somalia requires additional external assistance, a national program, and improved IPC measures. Future studies should concentrate on the effects of IPC interventions, leadership, training, geographical inequities, and new monitoring technology.
