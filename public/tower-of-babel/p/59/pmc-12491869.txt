Prevalence and risk factors of pediatric seizures in COVID-19 infection: A study from Thailand
Authors: Natnicha Praphaphanthasak, Vitchayaporn Emarach Saengow
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCOVID-19 is an emerging disease primarily presenting with respiratory tract infections in children. Recent reports have highlighted neurological manifestations, including seizures. However, there is a lack of studies regarding the prevalence and risk factors for pediatric seizures associated with COVID-19 in Thailand.MethodsThis retrospective study involved pediatric patients aged 1 month to 15 years who had clinical seizures as a primary symptom alongside COVID-19 infection. Patients diagnosed with Multisystem Inflammatory Syndrome in Children (MIS-C) were excluded. The study was conducted at Maharat Nakhon Ratchasima Hospital from December 1, 2019, to December 30, 2022. Demographic data were recorded. The prevalence of seizures and associated risk factors were evaluated.ResultsA total of 963 pediatric patients were included in the study, with ages ranging from 1 month to 15 years. The mean age was 6.8 years (± 4.6 SD), with a male predominance (53.2 % male, 46.8 % female). Among these, 40 cases presented with clinical seizures, resulting in a seizure prevalence of 0.76. In the seizure group, ages ranged from 8 months to 14 years, with a mean age of 3.67 years (± 3.45 SD). This group also exhibited male predominance. Notably, 0.76 % of the patients were under the age of 5 years, and the majority (95 %) experienced febrile seizures associated with the Omicron variant. The identified risk factors for seizures included younger age (less than 5 years) and the severity of COVID-19 infection. A 2-year follow-up review indicated favorable neurodevelopmental outcomes.ConclusionThe prevalence of seizures in pediatric patients with COVID-19 varied according to the viral variant, with the Omicron variant being associated with a surge in febrile seizures. Younger age and the severity of COVID-19 were found to increase the risk of seizure occurrence.Keywords: Pediatric seizure, COVID-19, Risk factors, Outcomes

Introduction

COVID-19 has emerged as an infectious disease, with reports indicating that over 660 million individuals have been affected globally [1]. In the pediatric population, clinical manifestations primarily involve respiratory tract infections and inflammatory processes, including Multisystem Inflammatory Syndrome in Children (MIS-C) [2]. There is increasing evidence documenting the neurological manifestations associated with COVID-19, which can present as nonspecific symptoms such as headaches and dizziness. More specific conditions include stroke, seizures, Guillain-Barré syndrome, encephalopathy, and meningitis/encephalitis [3].

Previous studies have reported the prevalence and etiology of seizures in children diagnosed with COVID-19, identifying febrile seizures as the most common presentation [4]. The reported prevalence of seizures varies across different studies. Additionally, several risk factors have been identified as potential contributors to the development of seizures in the pediatric population during COVID-19 infection. These include underlying neurological disorders, iron deficiency anemia, obesity, and older age [5].

However, there are few reports regarding the prevalence of pediatric seizures in the context of COVID-19 within Thailand. Therefore, this study aims to identify the prevalence of and risk factors for pediatric seizures among children with COVID-19 infection.

Method

The IRB was approved (038/2023). This is a retrospective study that recruited pediatric patients aged 1 month to 15 years who were diagnosed with COVID-19 and presented with seizures and respiratory tract symptoms. The study included patients admitted to Maharat Nakhon Ratchasima Hospital from December 1, 2019, to December 30, 2022. Patients diagnosed with Multisystem Inflammatory Syndrome in Children (MIS-C) were excluded from the study; the criteria for the diagnosis of MIS-C are outlined in Appendix 1.

Demographic data were collected, including age, sex, underlying diseases, developmental history, seizure semiology, duration of seizures, frequency of seizures, etiology of seizures, metabolic laboratory results, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, neuroimaging, and electroencephalography (EEG). Data collection was conducted through a review of medical records by a pediatric neurologist. The diagnosis of clinical seizures was based on seizure semiology, and the classification of seizures as provoked or unprovoked along with epilepsy classification was based on the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) guidelines. The diagnosis of definite COVID-19 infection was confirmed by a positive nasal swab PCR test for COVID-19. The severity of COVID-19 infection was based on criteria such as poor intake, dehydration, pneumonia, respiratory failure, the need for mechanical ventilation and unstable vital signs.

Treatment for seizures included the prescription of anti-seizure medications in cases of prolonged seizures, repetitive seizures with clinical suspicion of status epilepticus, or clinical and investigation suspicion of epilepsy. After clinical improvement, patients were scheduled for follow-up at the pediatric neurology clinic. Neurodevelopmental outcomes were reviewed through medical records by the pediatric neurologist, including history and physical examination, and developmental assessment using the Denver-II Developmental Screening Test. The follow-up duration was continued for at least 2 years following COVID-19 infection. The flow of this study is illustrated in Fig. 1. The prevalence of seizures in COVID-19 infection was calculated. Risk factors for seizures related to COVID-19 were identified through univariate analyses.

Results

In this study, a total of 963 pediatric patients, aged from 1 month to 15 years, were recruited who were diagnosed with COVID-19 and admitted to Maharat Nakhon Ratchasima Hospital. The mean age was 6.8 years ±4.6 SD, with a male predominance (53.2 % male, 46.8 % female). The distribution of cases with a diagnosis of seizure for each individual year and COVID-19 variant in Thailand is shown in Fig. 2.

Distribution of COVID-19 variants and pediatric patients.

We identified 40 cases that presented with clinical seizures. The prevalence of seizures in this population was found to be 0.76 % as illustrated in Fig. 3. In the seizure group ages ranged from 8 months to 14 years with a mean age of 3.67 years ±3.45 SD. There was also a male predominance in this group with 55 % being male and 45 % female. Most patients who had clinical seizures exhibited respiratory symptoms and had moderate to severe clinical manifestations such as dehydration and pneumonia which met the criteria for admission. 7.5 % of patients with seizures had known underlying epilepsy. 70 % of the patients were under the age of 5 years, and 95 % presented with fever and seizures and were classified as febrile seizures. Two patients were diagnosed with severe encephalitis, specifically acute necrotizing encephalitis (ANE), with the diagnostic criteria detailed in Appendix 2. The most common seizure semiology is generalized tonic-clonic (GTC) seizures.

Prevalence of pediatric seizure in COVID-19.

Due to resource limitations, metabolic laboratory tests, neuroimaging (CT brain), and electroencephalography (EEG) were not performed in every case. Among those evaluated, 10 % exhibited abnormal metabolic results, including hyponatremia and hypokalemia. A CT scan of the brain was conducted on 7 patients who presented clinically with acute encephalopathy and abnormalities consistent with acute necrotizing encephalitis (ANE) were found in 2 patients. EEG assessments were completed in 6 patients, with 66.67 % demonstrating focal onset epileptiform discharges. CSF analysis was conducted in 6 cases and showed a normal CSF profile.

Regarding the risk factors for seizure occurrence, we found that being younger than 5 years and exhibiting more severe COVID-19 symptoms significantly differentiated the seizure group from those without seizures. Conversely, underlying conditions such as epilepsy, developmental delays, and comorbidities (including obesity and iron deficiency anemia) did not show statistically significant differences between the two groups, as presented in Table 1.

Discussion

COVID-19 has emerged as an infectious disease since 2019. The main clinical manifestation is respiratory tract infections. Recent literature has documented a range of neurological manifestations associated with COVID-19 in children, including nonspecific symptoms such as headaches and myalgia [3,4]. More specific neurological symptoms, including seizures, have also been reported. Previous studies in adults have reported a prevalence of seizures in COVID-19 of approximately 1–2 %, influenced by disease severity and associated inflammatory processes [1,3]. In contrast, seizures in the pediatric population often manifest as primary symptoms rather than as consequences of disease severity. However, there are few studies that report the prevalence of pediatric seizures in COVID-19 patients in Thailand. This study aimed to review the prevalence of pediatric seizures, identify risk factors associated with COVID-19 infection and evaluate neurodevelopment outcomes.

The prevalence of seizures in children with COVID-19 varies across different ethnic groups and strains of the virus, with reported rates ranging from 0.7 % to 52.9 % [6]. In our study, we observed a seizure prevalence of 0.76 %, which is consistent with previous studies. A study conducted in Korea reported a prevalence of 1 % [7], while a study in Turkey found a prevalence of 1.6 % [8]. Additionally, a study in the United States reported a lower prevalence of 0.5 % [9]. A study in Israel in 2021 noted that 6.2 % of their patients experienced seizures, with most presenting with fever and having underlying neurological disorders [6].The study by Anastasopoulou S et al. demonstrated a 13.4 % prevalence of seizures, particularly among children older than five years, with underlying neurological diseases identified as a significant risk factor for seizures occurring without fever [10]. The study in Brunei documented a prevalence of 4.5 %, focusing predominantly on febrile seizures in children,particularly during the Omicron surge [11]. Study in Turkey reported a prevalence of 5.74 %, with primarily characterized as febrile seizure, identifying risk factors such as elevated procalcitonin levels, eosinophilia, and low hemoglobin levels [12]. Study of Xu P et al. in China reported a prevalence of 1.8 % of seizures in the context of the Omicron variant, a finding that correlates with our study [13]. The study in Italy reported a prevalence of 4.7 % [14].

The characteristic of the patients in this study were consistent with previous findings, notably a predominant male population and a common presentation of generalized tonic-clonic seizures, as reported by Anastasopoulou S et al [10] Most seizures were controlled with anti-seizure medications, with approximately 80 % of patients experiencing a single seizure episode during their hospitalization. Notably, our study documented two patients progressed to status epilepticus, ultimately diagnosed as acute necrotizing encephalitis (ANE).Prior studies have similarly reported the occurrence of seizure in patients with COVID-19, indicating that seizures are typically controlled with one to two anti-seizure medications, as noted by Kurd M et al. and Thongsing et al. [ (6,15)] In our research, febrile seizures were predominant among patients with COVID-19, consistent with findings from previous studies [12,13,16,17]. The age at which seizures occur has varied across different studies. In our study, we found that children under 5 years of age presented a higher risk factor for developing seizures which explained by most common seizure in our study is classified as febrile seizure which has common age under 5 year old. This finding was different with the results of Pascarella et al., who reported that individuals over the age of 6 years were at greater risk of seizure and the studies which included patients with non-Omicron variant reported the older age and had seizure without fever. [8,14]

In regions with limited resources, neuroimaging and electroencephalography (EEG) were performed in only a small number of cases. Previous literature indicates that neuroimaging findings in patients with COVID-19 are often non-specific, with reports documenting both normal results and non-specific changes on brain computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Lesions of splenial changes on MRI have been recorded in cases diagnosed with Multisystem Inflammatory Syndrome in Children (MIS-C); however, our study did not include patients with MIS-C [ (19)]. Regarding electroencephalography (EEG) findings in COVID-19 infection, most studies involving both pediatric and adult populations report a limited number of cases. Abnormal EEG results in patients with encephalopathy typically manifest as diffuse slowing accompanied by bi-frontal activity [19]. Our study detected focal spikes abnormalities on EEG, correlating with findings from Kurd M et al., where abnormal EEG patterns were identified in 17 out of 25 patients [6]. Furthermore, Pascarella A et al. reported abnormalities in 5 out of 38 EEG results [14]. Due to limitations and a limited number of patients, the correlation between seizures in COVID-19 and EEG findings may require further study for the better conclusion.

The pathogenesis of seizures in the context of COVID-19 is believed to be related to a cytokine storm and may exhibit neurotrophic effects, particularly with the Omicron variant [18]. The differences in seizure prevalence across different studies reflect variations induced by different COVID-19 variants. The Omicron variant appears to exhibit a higher association with seizure occurrences, particularly febrile seizures, highlighting the neurotrophic effects of the Omicron variant [12,13].

The review after two years follow-up post COVID-19 infection revealed that 87.5 % of patients had normal neurodevelopmental outcomes. However, 10 % of patients later experienced recurrent unprovoked seizures, which were diagnosed as epilepsy. 5 % of patients were diagnosis of ANE, had cerebral palsy with globally delayed development. This result correlated with a study in Brazil, which reported a neurodevelopmental disability rate of 28.6 % following COVID-19 infection [19].

However, due to the limitations of a small sample size and limited resources, further studies on seizures and COVID-19 infection in pediatric populations should be conducted for more information.

Conclusion

The prevalence of seizures associated with COVID-19 varies among different viral strains, with the Omicron variant linked to an increased incidence of febrile seizures, particularly in younger children. Additionally, the higher severity of COVID-19 symptoms appears to escalate the risk of seizure development.

Essential diagnostic criteria for MIS-C in children [1]

0–19 years of age with fever >3 days and two of the following:

a) Rash or bilateral non-purulent conjunctivitis or muco-cutaneous inflammation signs (oral, hands or feet).

b) Hypotension or shock.

c) Features of myocardial dysfunction, pericarditis, valvulitis, or coronary abnormalities (including ECHO findings or elevated Troponin/NT-proBNP),

d) Evidence of coagulopathy (by PT, PTT, elevated d-Dimers).

e) Acute gastrointestinal problems (diarrhea, vomiting, or abdominal pain). and Elevated markers of inflammation such as ESR, C-reactive protein, or procalcitonin and No other obvious microbial cause of inflammation, including bacterial sepsis, staphylococcal or streptococcal shock syndromes.

AND Evidence of COVID-19 (RT-PCR, antigen test or serology positive), or likely contact with patients with COVID-19.
