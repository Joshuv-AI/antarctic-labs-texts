Estimating controlled direct treatment effects on pain intensity using structural mean models
Authors: Rui Wang, Patrick J. Heagerty, Kwun Chuen Gary Chan, Pradeep Suri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.This study introduces structural mean models to deal with concurrent analgesic use in pain clinical trial, which targets alternate estimands with clinical relevance.

1. Introduction

Randomized controlled trials (RCT) are widely used in evaluating new pain treatments. Because of ethical considerations, as-needed pain medication use is typically allowed in pain RCTs.3,5 At the same time, the effect size of intercurrent analgesics may equal or exceed that of the randomized treatments being studied in pain randomized controlled trials.21 These aspects may contribute to the fact that most pain treatments have only small treatment effects as estimated using intention-to-treat analysis. Recent FDA guidance explicitly recognizes potential problems posed by intercurrent treatments such as analgesic use.10 However, intercurrent analgesic use is often ignored in pragmatic RCTs or dealt with using analytic approaches (eg, inclusion of analgesic use in a binary “responder outcome”) that can reduce power or impart bias20 or using design features (eg, strict study-mandated rescue medication use) that can encourage participant dropout and lead to biased estimates of treatment effect.17 Importantly, analgesic use may be a mediator between pain intensity at one time point and pain intensity at another time point, so adjustment for analgesic use as a covariate is generally not appropriate.20 Formal statistical methods are needed to guide the development of strategies that can account for intercurrent analgesic use.

Our team has proposed simple methods and novel measures to account for intercurrent analgesic use that might improve power and accuracy in pain RCTs.20,21 However, other approaches with longer histories of use might achieve the same purpose. Well-established biostatistical methods from the causal mediation literature should be able to address the problem of how to account for unmeasured confounding by differential postrandomization analgesic use.8,11,22 However, to our knowledge, such methods have not been used to account for intercurrent treatment in pain RCTs, a critical research gap.9 A recent consensus statement on pragmatic pain RCT methods states “Proper adjustment for such effects, when warranted, requires the use of sophisticated causal inference methods” (Ref. 9, page 7), but it provides no examples of or guidance on which causal inference methods to use or how they may achieve (or fail to attain) the goal of accounting for intercurrent analgesic use.

Given that certain appropriate causal inference methods were developed decades ago, it may be that the long delay in their uptake in pain RCTs is due to the lack of familiarity among pain researchers and questions about how best to use such methods. Therefore, this study sought to illustrate the use of causal inference methods to address intercurrent analgesic use in a pain RCT, with an emphasis on one method, structural mean models (SMMs), while allowing comparisons to other novel approaches our group has developed and benchmarking to the standard in pain RCTs, strict intention to treat analysis (strict ITT). The study aim was to illustrate the use of contemporary causal inference methods, including but not limited to structural mean models, to account for intercurrent analgesic in an RCT examining the treatment effect of epidural steroid injections (ESI) on pain intensity, among patients with lumbar spinal stenosis.6 This study is to our knowledge the first to compare a range of contemporary causal inference methods to account for intercurrent analgesic use with traditional approaches.

2. Methods

This study analyzed data from the Lumbar Epidural Steroid Injections for Spinal Stenosis (LESS) trial (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT01238536).6 To facilitate accessibility to readers, this article contains basic information about methodology but relegates technical biostatistical details to the Supplemental Materials (http://links.lww.com/PR9/A383).

2.1. Conceptual overview

In a pain RCT, the effect on pain intensity of treatment vs control can be estimated using a standard difference in mean estimator or regression-based estimator due to the randomization of treatment assignment, also referred to as13 the ITT effect. However, intercurrent analgesic use may partially nullify the effect on pain intensity (Fig. 1). Accordingly, the outcome of greater interest may be the controlled direct effect of treatment assignment on pain intensity, which represents the average effect of treatment vs control if all participants in the study sample had not taken intercurrent analgesics at the primary pain intensity endpoint.

Here R denotes the randomized treatment assignment, M denotes the analgesic use, X denoted the observed baseline confounders, U denotes the unmeasured confounder, and Y denotes outcome. θM,0 is the effect of analgesic use on outcome, θR,0 is the direct effect of randomized treatment assignment on the outcome.

This analysis focused on treatment effects at 3 weeks because only the 3-week comparison in the LESS trial was truly randomized (as treatments were allowed to be repeated after the 3-week assessment, depending on the participant's choice and not in a randomized manner). The primary ITT analysis in LESS ignored analgesic use, as do most pragmatic pain RCTs.6 This “original” analysis in LESS targeted an estimand that is the difference in mean leg pain intensity (LPI) and back pain intensity (BPI) for all randomized subjects at 3-week follow-up, irrespective of whether the difference is attributable to ESI or whether it is potentially impacted by concurrent analgesic use, which we refer to henceforth as the “strict ITT estimand” (Table 1). In this study, we used SMMs, which are a family of modern semiparametric causal model, to parameterize the controlled direct and indirect effects and analyze the treatment effect of ESI on 3-week LPI and BPI numerical rating scale (NRS) outcomes. By doing so, we targeted an alternative estimand, which we refer to here as “the difference in mean leg pain intensity for all randomized subjects at 3-week follow-up that is attributable to ESI (the initially randomized treatment) and not affected by concurrent analgesic use at the time of the primary endpoint.” For simplicity, we refer to this henceforth as “the attributable to ESI estimand” (Table 1). The attributable to ESI estimand is a specific example of what we term the “attributable to randomized treatment estimand” in a pain RCT, which does not necessarily have to be a procedural pain treatment and could just as well be a behavioral, rehabilitative, or pharmacological pain treatment.

2.2. Study sample and assessments

The LESS trial was a double-blind, randomized, controlled trial involving 400 participants aged 50 years or older recruited between April 2011 and August 2013. Participants had symptoms of neurogenic claudication and lumbar central canal spinal stenosis on imaging. Participants were randomized to receive ESI with glucocorticoids plus lidocaine (the “steroid” group) or an epidural injection with lidocaine alone. Outcomes were measured at 1, 2, 3, and 6 weeks after randomization through phone, in-person reviews, or mailed questionnaires.

2.3. Baseline measures

Baseline characteristics including sociodemographics (age, sex, ethnicity, race, current employment status, education, marital status), body mass index, smoking status, baseline analgesic use, LPI, and BPI.

2.4. Analgesic use

Concurrent analgesic use was measured using participant-completed written weekly diaries. Diaries inquired about the use of any opioid or nonopioid medications (prescribed or over-the-counter) and the doses taken on each day of each week, prompting participants to contact study coordinators if they had questions about medications or doses. Diaries were returned by mail or in-person; if returned in-person, diaries were reviewed with the coordinator. For this analysis, analgesic use was defined as a binary variable with a value of 1 if there was any analgesic use (including opioid medication and/or nonopioid medication) in week 3, and a value of 0 if no analgesic use was reported. Further details regarding the theory underlying the analgesic use assessments are provided in the Supplemental Materials (http://links.lww.com/PR9/A383).

2.5. Outcome measures

Outcome measures included LPI and BPI. Leg pain intensity reflected average leg pain intensity on an 11-point numerical rating scale in the past week. Back pain intensity reflected average back pain intensity on an 11-point NRS in the past week.

2.6. Statistical analysis

2.6.1. Descriptive statistics

Summary statistics for baseline characteristics and primary outcomes were described by arms.

2.6.2. Intention to treat analysis

A “strict ITT” analysis was used to evaluate the effectiveness of treatment assignment. Two regression-based methods were used for estimating the ITT effect: (1) regressing outcome on treatment assignment, recruitment site and baseline values of both outcome measures (method 1) as was done in the original trial6 and (2) regressing outcome on treatment assignment and all other baseline covariates (method 2) for potential improvement of efficiency.4

2.6.3. Traditional regression-based mediation analysis

Traditional regression-based mediation analysis was applied.1 Let R be the baseline randomization (R=1 refers to those who were randomized to receive glucocorticoids plus lidocaine, R=0 refers to those who were randomized to receive lidocaine alone), M be the binary concurrent analgesic use, and Y be the outcome measure (LPI or BPI), X be covariates. The regression model is specified asY=α0+βX,0TX+βR,0R+βM,0M+ϵ,where the error term ϵ is assumed to be independent of X,R, and M, which corresponds to the case where there is no unmeasured confounding between M and Y. Here βR,0 can be interpreted as the direct effect of randomization on LPI, βM,0 can be interpreted as the effect of analgesic use on LBP.

2.6.4. QPAC1.5

We applied the QPAC1.5 method.20 The QPAC1.5 is a simple method that combines information from concurrent analgesic use and pain intensity to create a composite outcome, resulting in a single value that reflects both pain intensity and concurrent analgesic use. It assumes a perceived beneficial effect on pain intensity of 1.5 NRS points among all analgesic users who have self-selected to use analgesics, based on an earlier study.21 The QPAC1.5 targets the attributable to ESI estimand. A weakness of the QPAC1.5 is that, unlike approaches such as SMMs, it assumes the same (beneficial) effect of intercurrent analgesic use on pain intensity, without variability. Strengths are that it can be easily applied by nonbiostatisticians, and it is easily understood. Additionally, the value assumed by the QPAC1.5 for the average perceived beneficial effect of analgesics can be altered to fit knowledge regarding the average perceived effect in a given target population.20

We used 2 steps to apply the QPAC1.5. The first step was calculating the pain intensity-concurrent analgesic use composite outcome Y∼ by Y∼=Y+1.5M. The second step was regressing Y∼ on randomized treatment assignment R and other covariates.

2.6.5. Structural mean models

We adopt the potential outcome framework16,18 and use Y(r,m) to denote the potential outcome we would have observed if R were set to be r and M were set to be m. The SMM is specified asE[Y(r,m)−Y(0,0)│X,U]=θR,0r+θM,0m,where θR,0 is the attributable to ESI estimand, which is also called controlled direct effect in causal inference literature,15 θM,0 is the effect of analgesic use on the outcome.

In contrast to traditional Baron-Kenny–type methods,1 unmeasured confounding between analgesic use and the outcome is allowed because the actual analgesic use M that does not appear as an independent variable in SMM. Following Ten Have et al.,22 the moment conditions in SMMs can be constructed by treating interactions between randomization and baseline covariates as instrumental variables. Three different estimation methods were applied for estimating θR,0 and θM,0: estimating equations or EE,23 g-estimation,14,24 and GMM.7 In contrast to EE and g-estimation, GMM allows us to use multiple instruments to estimate the direct effect of randomized treatment assignment. Technical details are found in the Supplementary Materials (http://links.lww.com/PR9/A383).

Different construction of instrumental variables could lead to different results. We centered and scaled the covariates when constructing instruments. For estimating equation and g-estimation approaches, we used the interaction between age and randomization as an instrument because both estimating equation and g-estimation approaches only allow for one instrument, and age had a relatively large correlation with analgesic use in the LESS data. For GMM, we used all the baseline covariates to construct instrumental variables and used an efficient weighting matrix.7 The use of additional instruments and the efficient weighting matrix in GMM aims to improve estimation efficiency compared with the estimating equation and g-estimation approaches that use a single instrument. We also used the J-test to test if the moment conditions implied by the SMM were corrected specified.

3.3. Results using traditional regression-based mediation analysis

For LPI, the treatment effect estimate for the attributable to ESI estimand was −0.756 (95%: −1.321 to −0.191), meaning that compared with the lidocaine alone group, LPI for participants who were assigned to glucocorticoids plus lidocaine was 0.756 lower if all participants had not taken analgesics (Table 4). This value for the treatment effect on LPI is generally similar in magnitude to that obtained for strict ITT using method 1 (−0.751) and method 2 (−0.782) from Table 2 (Fig. 2). Similarly, for BPI, the estimate is −0.875 (95%: −1.415 to −0.335), which means that, compared with the lidocaine-alone group, BPI for participants who were assigned to glucocorticoids plus lidocaine was 0.875 lower, if all participants had not taken analgesics. This value for the treatment effect on BPI is generally similar in magnitude to that obtained for strict ITT using method 1 (−0.833) and method 2 (−0.932) from Table 2. The direct effects of analgesic use were estimated as 0.262 (95% CI: −0.374 to 0.897) for LPI and 0.579 (95% CI: −0.036 to 1.194) for BPI. The point estimates are positive with 95% CIs including 0, suggesting a nonsignificant yet detrimental effect of analgesic use (ie, analgesics worsen pain intensity).

3.5. Results using structural mean models

For LPI, the treatment effect estimates for the attributable to ESI estimand were −0.864 (95% CI: −3.207 to 1.478]) for EE, −0.935 (95% CI: −1.779 to −0.090) for g-estimation, and −0.653 (95% CI: −1.218 to −0.089) for GMM (Table 5). These treatment effect estimates with EE and g-estimation are slightly bigger than the ITT estimates and estimates from traditional regression-based mediation analysis (Fig. 2). Interestingly, the estimate of the attributable to ESI estimand from g-estimation is very similar to the corresponding results of QPAC1.5 and the estimate of θM,0 (−1.55) is very similar to the effect of intercurrent analgesic use assumed by the QPAC1.5 (−1.5 NRS points). The estimate of the attributable to ESI estimand from GMM, however, is slightly smaller than the estimates from ITT and traditional regression-based mediation analysis. The estimate of the direct effect of analgesics from GMM is positive, with the 95% CI including zero. Compared with the estimating equation approach, g-estimation greatly shrank the standard error estimate (from 1.195 to 0.431), and GMM further shrank the standard error estimate to 0.288. Estimates for θM,0 showed similar patterns, although the 95% CIs for θM,0 were too wide to be informative. The P-value for the J-test was 0.443, not suggesting model misspecification.

For the BPI outcome, the estimates for the attributable to ESI estimands are −0.919 (95% CI: −3.145 to 1.308) for EE, −1.048 (95% CI: −1.842 to −0.253) for g-estimation, and −0.826 (95% CI: −1.374 to −0.277) for GMM (Fig. 3). The overall patterns of these estimates are very similar to those seen when analyzing LPI: estimates of the attributable to ESI estimand from EE and g-estimation are slightly bigger than the ITT estimates and estimates from traditional regression-based mediation analysis (Fig. 2). The estimate of the attributable to ESI estimand from g-estimation is very similar to the corresponding results using the QPAC1.5. The estimate of the attributable to ESI estimand from GMM is similar or slightly smaller than ITT estimates and estimates from traditional regression-based mediation analysis. The patterns for standard errors are also similar to those seen with the LPI estimates: g-estimation greatly shrinks the standard error estimate compared with EE, and GMM further shrinks the standard error estimate compared with g-estimation. The P-value for the J-test was 0.322, not suggesting model misspecification.

4. Discussion

This study applied contemporary causal inference statistical methodology to analyze pain RCT data while considering intercurrent analgesic use. Compared with strict ITT and traditional regression-based mediation analysis, 3 of 4 of the contemporary causal inference methods we studied that focus on the controlled direct effect (the QPAC1.5, SMM with EE, SMM with g-estimation) resulted in increased magnitude of the treatment effect estimates. By contrast, one method, SMM with GMM, did not increase the size of effect estimates. These findings indicate potential benefits from using these causal inference methods in pain RCTs, but it also indicate some instances where they may not perform as expected.

Recent guidance has drawn attention to how intercurrent treatment, such as analgesic use, may affect RCT treatment effect estimates.10 “Hypothetical strategies” targeting estimands other than strict ITT have been suggested as alternatives that may better account for intercurrent treatment.10 However, there have been few studies comparing methods for targeting alternative estimands in pain RCTs.20 In this study, estimates of the attributable to ESI estimand using the QPAC1.5, SMM with EE, and SMM with g-estimation were around 0.1 to 0.2 NRS points larger compared with strict ITT and traditional regression-based mediation analysis estimates. Although these differences are relatively small, they are still of an absolute magnitude that could push the estimated treatment effect in an RCT over the most common threshold used for clinically meaningful change in pain intensity, which is 1.0 NRS points. In relative terms, these increases in treatment effect sizes of 0.1 to 0.2 NRS points are roughly 12% to 26% larger than treatment effects with strict ITT for LPI (0.751 NRS points) or BPI (0.833). Compared with SMM with EE, SMM with g-estimation and SMM with GMM greatly improved estimation efficiency, as reflected by smaller treatment effect SEs. In general, EE is not recommended because it is less efficient than g-estimation and GMM in that it can yield larger variance of the EE estimator, consistent with this study's findings.22 The critical difference between g-estimation and GMM is that g-estimation can only allow for one instrumental variable, whereas GMM allows for multiple instrumental variables. The difficulty of applying g-estimation in practice is to choose an appropriate instrument because difference choices of instruments could lead to different results. In this study, we examined correlations between potential instruments and analgesic use and then selected the interaction between age and randomization as an instrument because a stronger instrument will typically yield a more stable estimate according to the instrumental variable literature.12 Generalized method of moments can incorporate multiple instrumental variables, thereby reducing the arbitrariness in selecting instrumental variables. Although theoretically including more instruments always improves efficiency when the sample size is sufficiently large, GMM might not outperform g-estimation if weak instruments were selected and sample size was not large enough. Indeed, in this study, compared with the results produced by GMM, results from g-estimation seem more reasonable and consistent with the accepted clinical use of analgesics, which is to reduce pain intensity: g-estimation resulted in nonsignificant, although negative, point estimates for θM,0, suggesting beneficial effects on pain intensity from analgesic use. By contrast, GMM gave positive estimates for θM,0, suggesting detrimental effects of analgesics on pain intensity. This may also explain why, unlike g-estimation and EE, GMM did not result in larger treatment effects of ESI as compared with strict ITT.

We have reported on the QPAC1.5 and its potential ability to mitigate shrinkage in estimated treatment effect sizes due to concurrent analgesic use.21 This study differed from our previous report in that, when using the QPAC1.5, this analysis accounted not only for opioid use, but for all analgesics (including both opioid and non-opioid medications). We have also recently reported that the QPAC1.5 increased the size of estimated treatment effects in a meta-analysis of interventional pain procedure RCTs.19 The QPAC1.5 is a less flexible method to account for concurrent analgesic use, as compared with SMMs, with potential weaknesses such as assuming the same value for the effect of intercurrent analgesic use on pain intensity, without variability. It was included as a comparator method in this study not because it is necessarily preferable to more rigorous causal inference methodssuch as SMMs, but because it may ultimately achieve the same goal. This notion was supported by this study's estimates of treatment effects on pain intensity using g-estimation, which were similar to estimates produced using the QPAC1.5. Potential advantages which might offset theory-based shortcomings of the QPAC1.5 are that it is easy to implement without complex biostatistical expertise; it can be adapted to new pain samples if studies analogous to21 indicate a different average perceived effect of analgesics in a given target population; and it can be used in the clinical setting for individual patients given that the mathematical correction used in the QPAC1.5 is applied at the person level (rather than at the group level).

Although theoretical and practical concerns may exist about the methods used to account for concurrent analgesic use in this article, as no single method is currently widely-accepted as best practice, it is generally accepted that not accounting for intercurrent treatment is a potential problem.9 Design features used in explanatory trials to mitigate concurrent analgesic use, such as strict requirements to use study-provided rescue medication, may not only limit generalizability but also create bias in treatment estimates due to differential participant dropout.17 For this reason, the lack of current knowledge about which method best accounts for concurrent analgesic use should not prevent the use of such methods in pain RCTs. This study was the first to systematically compare different methods to account for intercurrent analgesic use in a pain RCT. It is meant as a demonstration of how these methods generally could be used in any pain RCT. However, it represents only a single step in examining the potential usefulness of such methods to account for intercurrent treatment in pain RCTs. We suggest that analyses using these methods be regularly included in pain RCT reporting, so an evidence base can be built regarding which methods work best, and how they should be implemented across pain RCTs. Once researchers know the expected increases in treatment effect size on pain intensity in absolute and/or relative terms across different pain conditions and treatments, this knowledge can inform sample size calculations and study planning. Over time, this evidence base will facilitate discussion and eventual agreement about best practices for alternative estimands that can account for intercurrent analgesic use (and perhaps for other intercurrent treatments).

A limitation of this study was its use of self-reported data on analgesic use, which potentially has imprecision and/or bias as compared with direct observation of analgesic use. Therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility that the self-reported nature of this study's analgesic use data impacted the study findings.

Some methodological extensions to the current work can be considered in future studies. First, it is possible to explicitly model effect modification to avoid the no effect-modifier assumption in our SMM approach; however, such models will introduce more parameters to estimate. Second, a rigorous way to construct and select instruments might be useful for further improving the performance of g-estimation and GMM. Third, both g-estimation and GEE may be further improved using machine learning for the estimation of conditional mean functions.2

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the potential usefulness of contemporary causal inference methods to account for intercurrent analgesic use in pain RCTs.
