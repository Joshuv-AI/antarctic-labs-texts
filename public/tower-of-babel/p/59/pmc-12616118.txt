Prevalence of os acromiale in Japanese population: Comparison between patients with shoulder pain and asymptomatic subjects
Authors: Shinji Kawaguchi, Shoji Fukuta, Jun Kawamata, Koichi Sairyo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOs acromiale, a condition in which the acromial apophysis fails to fuse, and its prevalence varies across ethnic groups. Although higher rates have been reported in African and Caucasian populations, data are scarce for Asian populations, particularly for Japanese individuals. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of os acromiale in a Japanese population and its association with rotator cuff-related symptoms.MethodsWe retrospectively identified 2221 shoulders of 1411 Japanese subjects aged over 25 years who underwent computed tomography (CT) of the shoulder at our institution between September 2010 and March 2023. The shoulders were divided into an asymptomatic group, which included 1386 shoulders of 693 patients with no documented history of shoulder pain who underwent chest CT for non-orthopedic reasons, and a symptomatic group, which included 836 shoulders of 800 patients who underwent arthroscopic surgery for rotator cuff tears or tendinopathy. Os acromiale was identified using three-dimensional CT and classified by location.ResultsOs acromiale was identified in 16 shoulders (0.72 %), 10 of which were classified as pre-acromial and 6 as meso-acromial. The prevalence was significantly higher in the symptomatic group than in the asymptomatic group (1.56 % vs 0.22 %, p = 0.002). No significant difference was observed according to sex or laterality.ConclusionThe prevalence of os acromiale in Japanese was 0.72 %. The prevalence of this condition is markedly lower in this population than in African and Caucasian populations. However, its significantly higher occurrence in symptomatic patients suggests a potential role in shoulder pain associated with rotator cuff lesions. These results emphasize the importance of recognizing os acromiale as a possible anatomical contributor to shoulder symptoms in clinical practice.Keywords: Os acromiale, Shoulder, Computed tomography, Prevalence, Rotaor cuff lesions, Asymptomatic subjects

Introduction

Acromial apophysis develops from four separate centers of ossification in the acromion: the pre-acromion (the anterior center), meso-acromion, (the middle center), meta-acromion (the posterior center), and basi-acromion (located at the base of the scapular spine).1 These centersstart to ossify during adolescence and fuse between the ages of 22 and 25 years,2 usually in the posterior to anterior direction. Os acromiale occurs when the apophysis of the acromion does not fuse correctly.1,3 The etiology of this pathology is not fully understood, and two hypotheses have been proposed in the literature. One is that os acromiale results from mechanical strain on the developing acromion,4 and the other is that there is a genetic basis for the condition.5,6

The prevalence of os acromiale has been reported to range from 0.7 % to 18.2 % in anatomical and radiographic studies, and bilateral involvement has been found in 33.3 %–62 % of cases.7, 8, 9 The higher frequency of os acromiale in Africans than in Europeans supports a genetic contribution7 There are few reports on os acromiale in Asian populations. The prevalence of os acromiale has been reported to be markedly lower in Koreans compared with other ethnic groups.9 To our knowledge, however, there have been no studies on the prevalence of os acromiale in the Japanese population. The objective of this study was to determine the prevalence of os acromiale in the Japanese population and to examine its association with rotator cuff-related symptoms. We hypothesized that1 the prevalence of os acromiale in the Japanese population would be low, similar to that reported in Koreans, and2 os acromiale would be more common in patients with rotator cuff-related symptoms than in asymptomatic individuals, suggesting a potential role in the development of shoulder pathology.

Methods

The study was approved by our institutional review board. We retrospectively identified all patients who underwent imaging of the shoulder at our institution between September 2010 and March 2023. The inclusion criteria were Japanese ethnicity and age over 25 years, as fusion of the acromial apophysis is known to be complete by this age.2 The exclusion criteria were glenohumeral joint deformity, narrowing of the acromiohumeral interval, and deformity suggestive of prior trauma.

The study population was divided into an asymptomatic group and a symptomatic group. The asymptomatic group included patients who underwent computed tomography (CT) of the chest for non-orthopedic reasons in our institution between September 2010 and January 2012. In asymptomatic group, written informed consent was obtained for the use of CT data beyond the original purpose. Symptomatic group included patients who were identified in the shoulder clinic database maintained by the senior author (S.F) to have undergone arthroscopic surgery for rotator cuff tear or tendinopathy between April 2016 and March 2023. Arthroscopic rotator cuff repair was performed for rotator cuff tears, and subacromial decompression was performed for tendinopathy. All patients in the symptomatic group underwent both preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and CT of the affected shoulder. In symptomatic group, the need for informed consent from all individual participants included in the study was waived.

For the asymptomatic group, three-dimensional (3D) CT images of the scapula were reconstructed and used to screen for the presence of os acromiale, using imaging software (Aquarius NET Server; TeraRecon Inc., San Mateo, CA). For the symptomatic group, the 3D images that had been reconstructed by radiology technicians using Synapse Vincent (Fujifilm Holdings Co., Tokyo, Japan) and entered in the electronic medical records were reviewed. Os acromiale was defined as the presence of a free fragment separated from the distal acromion with regular edges. Written informed consent was obtained for the use of CT data beyond the original purpose. Care was taken to distinguish between os acromiale and a normal acromial apophysis. If os acromiale was suspected on 3D-CT, then coronal, sagittal, and axial multiplanar reconstructed images were reviewed to confirm its presence. If present, we classified os acromiale into the four types: pre-acromial, meso-acromial, meta-acromial, and basi-acromial (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). All images were evaluated by 2 orthopedic surgeons (S.K. and S.F.) working independently.

Interobserver agreement was assessed using Cohen's kappa coefficient. The frequency of os acromiale was calculated and compared between the symptomatic and asymptomatic groups, using Fisher's exact test. Relationships between the prevalence of os acromiale and demographic data were evaluated using the chi-squared test. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS ver. 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 2221 shoulders from 1411 patients in both the asymptomatic and symptomatic groups were assessed. The demographic data are shown in Table 1. Three-dimensional CT identified 16 cases of os acromiale in the overall study population. The interrater reliability was almost perfect (κ = 0.91, p < 0.001). In cases of disagreement regarding the presence of os acromiale, the two examiners re-evaluated the images and reached a consensus through discussion. Ten cases were pre-acromial and 6 were meso-acromial. The prevalence of os acromiale was 0.72 %. Os acromiale was identified in 9 men (0.67 %) and 7 women (0.80 %). There was no significant sex-related difference in prevalence. The frequency of os acromiale was similar between the right shoulder (n = 9, 0.73 %) and left shoulder (n = 7, 0.71 %) (Table 2).

Prevalence of os acromiale.

The asymptomatic group included 1386 shoulders of 693 patients (281 women, 412 men; mean age 65.6 ± 11.0 years). Three cases of os acromiale were identified in this group. One case was pre-acromial, and the other 2 cases were meso-acromial and identified in 1 shoulder each in the same patient. The frequency of os acromiale was 0.22 %. No details were available regarding past history of shoulder symptoms or the dominant side in this group.

The symptomatic group included 836 shoulders in 801 patients (505 men, 296 women); mean age 64.9 ± 11.1 years). The diagnoses were full-thickness rotator cuff tear in 715 shoulders, partial-thickness rotator cuff tear in 51, and rotator cuff tendinopathy in 70. Twelve cases of os acromiale were identified in this group.

The prevalence of os acromiale was found to be significantly higher in the symptomatic group than in the asymptomatic group (1.56 % compared with 0.22 %, p = 0.002). Of the 15 cases of os acromiale identified in the overall study population (8 men, 5 women), 9 were pre-acromial and 4 were meso-acromial, with 1 case of bilateral os acromiale of the meso-acromial type in 1 male patient.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate the prevalence of os acromiale in the general Japanese population. The results revealed that the prevalence was 0.22 %, which is significantly lower than reported in previous studies.3,5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11,16, 17, 18 The prevalence of this pathology varies according to ethnicity, suggesting involvement of genetic factors. The highest prevalence has been reported for Africans (13.2 %–18.2 %), with a lower prevalence reported for Caucasians (3.4 %–9.5 %).3,7,10 Sammarco examined 1198 skeletal specimens for evidence of os acromiale and reported a higher frequency in African-Americans (13.2 %) than in Caucasians (5.8 %).7 Case et al. compared the frequency of os acromiale between South African cadaveric specimens and medieval archaeological samples in Denmark and found a significantly higher frequency in the South African specimens (18.2 % vs 7.7 %).3 The findings of a meta-analysis of 23 studies were consistent with the earlier reports.8 A recent population-based study of MRI scans for both shoulders of 3050 subjects in northeastern Germany found that os acromiale had a prevalence of 1.9 %,11 which is lower than previously reported. Our finding of a prevalence of 0.22 % in the general Japanese population is the lowest reported to date, indicating that the prevalence of os acromiale in Asians may be much lower than in other ethnic populations.

The reported prevalence of os acriomiale varies between patient-based radiological studies and population based studies. Investigation of 1000 axial radiographs from German patients who had undergone open rotator cuff repair found that os acromiale had a prevalence of 6.2 %.12 Similarly, Burbank et al. identified os acromiale on radiographs in 6 of 88 (6.8 %) of North American patients who visited a shoulder clinic.13 However, most of these patients were asymptomatic. Kumar et al.9 identified 13 cases of os acromiale on axial radiographs among 1568 Korean patients who visited a shoulder clinic and reported the prevalence of the condition to be 0.7 %.

The prevalence of os acromiale tends to be lower when the diagnosis is made based on plain radiographs than when using skeletal specimens or cadavers.9,14 This suggests that the sensitivity of plain radiographs for detecting os acromiale is low. If the diagnosis of os acromiale is made based on plain radiographs alone, os acromiale may be missed or misdiagnosed as a fracture of the acromion or pseudoarthrosis. There have been some reports on the diagnosis of os acromiale using MRI.14,15 Review of 726 MRI scans at an Italian institution and found that the prevalence of os acromiale was 3.4 %.16 The MRI study of 848 Thai patients with shoulder problems revealed a prevalence of os acromiale of 2.13 %.17 A retrospective study from Saudi Araia analyzing MRIs in patients with shoulder pathology found a 3.32 % prevalence of os acromiale.18 Our prevalence of 1.56 % in symptomatic Japanese patients using CT scans is comparable with that in Italian and Thai populations.

Os acromiale is generally asymptomatic and is most often found incidentally on axillary radiographs of the shoulder or by other imaging modalities.7,10,13,19 However, os acromiale can cause symptoms. Pain deriving from os acromiale may occur as a result of movement at the non-union site or impingement on the rotator cuff tendons caused by inferior tilt of the acromial fragment during contraction of the deltoid muscle when elevating the affected arm.20 Repetitive subacromial impingement may contribute to tendinopathy or partial-thickness tears, and in the long term, may predispose to full-thickness rotator cuff tears. Symptomatic os acromiale has been reported in various athletes, particularly those in sports involving throwing and swimming.4,21,22

Several studies have reported improvement in symptoms after surgical intervention for os acromiale resistant to conservative treatment, suggesting that unstable os acromiale might contribute to shoulder pain. Resection has been recommended for small os acromiale,12,20 and arthroscopic excision without detachment of the deltoid insertion is becoming increasingly common.23, 24, 25, 26 For large fragments, internal fixation using K-wires, tension bands, or cannulated screws is performed to preserve deltoid function, and one grafting is sometimes employed to enhance clinical outcomes.12,20,27

The reports of excellent outcomes following surgery suggest that unstable os acromiale might be a cause of shoulder pain. However, until now, there have been no comparisons of the prevalence of os acromiale between patients with shoulder pain and that in the general population. We found that the prevalence of os acromiale was significantly higher in patients with shoulder pain than in the general population, even though both groups had a low prevalence. This finding also suggests that os acromiale might be a cause of shoulder pain.

One of the limitations of this study is that most of the patients in the symptomatic group underwent CT only on the symptomatic side. Had CT been performed for both shoulders in all patients, the prevalence of os acromiale in the symptomatic group might have been higher. Another limitation is that it was not known whether patients in the asymptomatic group had a history of shoulder pain, as with cadaveric research. Finally, we could not investigate the correlation between the prevalence of os acromiale and its clinical implications.

In conclusion, this study found that the prevalence of os acromiale was 1.56 % in Japanese patients with shoulder pain and 0.22 % in the general patient population, which suggests that os acromiale is a potential cause of shoulder symptoms. In addition, the prevalence of os acromiale in the Japanese population was lower than that reported in any reported studies. Although os acromiale is a relatively rare disease, clinicians should keep it in mind as a differential diagnosis in patients who present with shoulder pain. When planning rotator cuff repair, it is advisable to routinely evaluate the presence of an os acromiale, and, if present, to carefully assess its potential contribution to the patient's symptoms.
