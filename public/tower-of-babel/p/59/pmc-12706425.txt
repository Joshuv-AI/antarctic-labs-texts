Nipocalimab Dose Selection in Generalized Myasthenia Gravis
Authors: Belén Valenzuela, Martine Neyens, Yaowei Zhu, Sindhu Ramchandren, Anne‐Gaëlle Dosne, Jocelyn H. Leu, Ruben Faelens, Leona E. Ling, Juan‐José Pérez‐Ruixo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNipocalimab is a fully human immunoglobulin G (IgG)1 monoclonal antibody (mAb) designed to selectively block the IgG binding site of neonatal fragment crystallizable receptor (FcRn) to inhibit IgG recycling and decrease circulating IgG, including pathogenic IgG autoantibodies (such as antiacetylcholine receptor, anti‐muscle‐specific kinase, and anti‐low‐density lipoprotein‐related protein 4 antibodies in generalized myasthenia gravis [gMG]). A mechanistic model, integrating serum nipocalimab concentrations, FcRn occupancy, and total serum IgG data from five phase 1 studies in healthy adult participants and one phase 2 (Vivacity‐MG) study in adult participants with gMG, was developed. The relationship between total serum IgG reduction and placebo‐corrected MG‐Activities of Daily Living score change from baseline in participants with gMG was also characterized. Nipocalimab exhibited nonlinear target (FcRn)‐mediated disposition, causing rapid, reversible, and concentration‐dependent FcRn occupancy and IgG reduction (up to 85%) in healthy participants and participants with gMG. The PK of nipocalimab after a single intravenous (IV) administration is consistent with that after repeated IV administrations, with no accumulation following every 2 weeks (Q2W) dosing. The PK of nipocalimab and its effect on IgG reduction were similar between healthy participants and participants with gMG. Model‐based simulations indicated that the IV dose of 15 mg/kg Q2W, starting 2 weeks after a 30 mg/kg IV loading dose, was the lowest Q2W maintenance dose predicted to achieve the target of 70% median of the average change in IgG reduction in participants with gMG and was the recommended dose for the pivotal phase 3 Vivacity‐MG3 study in a gMG population.

Introduction

Generalized myasthenia gravis (gMG) is a chronic neuromuscular disease characterized by variable and fatigable muscle weakness, ranging from mild to life‐threatening (myasthenic crisis) [1]. gMG often affects multiple muscle groups whereby serum autoantibodies, including antiacetylcholine receptor (AChR), anti‐muscle‐specific tyrosine kinase (MuSK), and antilipoprotein receptor‐related protein 4 (LRP4), impair cholinergic transmission at the neuromuscular junction and weaken or prevent muscle contraction [1]. Approximately 85% of gMG patients are anti‐AChR+, 8% are anti‐MuSK+, 1%–2% are anti‐LRP4+, and the remaining 5%–6% of gMG patients are considered seronegative, without known pathogenic autoantibodies [2, 3]. The 2019 incidence rate of adult gMG ranged from 0.15 to 61.33 per million person‐years [4].

Historically, acetylcholinesterase inhibitors and/or immunomodulators and immunosuppressants were the mainstays of gMG treatment, whereas rapid‐acting plasmapheresis and intravenous immunoglobulins were used in myasthenic crisis [1, 5, 6]. However, side effects, comorbidities, and difficult‐to‐treat MG disease limited their use [1, 7], creating an unmet need for safer options with improved efficacy. One emerging treatment strategy for IgG autoantibody‐mediated diseases, including gMG, relies on blockade of neonatal fragment crystallizable (Fc) receptor (FcRn)‐ligand interactions. Because FcRn binds to the Fc portion of IgG and extends IgG half‐life by preventing its lysosomal degradation [8], inhibition of this interaction increases IgG clearance and reduces the half‐life of IgG and IgG‐based pathogenic autoantibodies.

Nipocalimab (ImaavyTM; JNJ‐80202135; M281), a fully human IgG1 monoclonal antibody (mAb), selectively binds to human FcRn with high affinity (Kd ≤ 31.7 pM, pH 6.0; Kd ≤ 57.8 pM, pH 7.4, respectively) and blocks the binding of internalized IgG to FcRn, inhibits IgG recycling, and decreases IgG levels [9, 10, 11]. Nipocalimab rapidly reduced circulating levels of IgG, including anti‐AChR antibodies [9, 10]. In the first‐in‐human study, nipocalimab exhibited FcRn‐mediated disposition and concentration‐dependent serum pharmacokinetics (PK), with more than dose‐proportional area‐under‐the‐curve (AUC) increases within the range of nipocalimab intravenous (IV) doses (0.3–60 mg/kg) in healthy participants [9]. Consistent with nipocalimab's mechanism of action, a similar rapid, concentration‐dependent, and reversible FcRn receptor occupancy and IgG reduction (up to 85%) were observed in healthy participants and those with gMG [9, 10]. In a randomized phase 2 study (Vivacity‐MG) [10] in participants with gMG, nipocalimab treatment led to the reduction of total IgG and pathogenic autoantibodies and improvement in patient‐ and physician‐reported outcomes of gMG scales, with a tolerable safety profile. Notably, the kinetics and magnitude of IgG reductions were associated with improvements in the Myasthenia Gravis Activities of Daily Living (MG‐ADL) score (described in Text S1) in participants with gMG [10].

We describe the mechanistic model integrating serum nipocalimab concentrations, FcRn receptor occupancy (RO), and total serum IgG reduction data from phase 1 studies in healthy participants and a phase 2 study (Vivacity‐MG) in participants with gMG. We also characterized the relationship between total serum IgG reduction and MG‐ADL scores in participants with gMG. Model‐based simulations were performed to guide the selection of appropriate nipocalimab dosing, which was then selected for the pivotal phase 3 study in adult participants with gMG.

Methods

Studies

Data from five phase 1 studies in healthy participants [9, 12, 13, 14, 15] and one phase 2 study (Vivacity‐MG) [10] in participants with gMG were used to develop the mechanistic population PK/RO/IgG/MG‐ADL model (Table 1). Nipocalimab IV dose regimens ranged from 0.3 mg/kg single dose to 60 mg/kg Q2W for up to 8 weeks. The dose regimens evaluated in the phase 2 study were 5 mg/kg Q4W, 30 mg/kg Q4W, 60 mg/kg single dose, and 60 mg/kg Q2W in participants with gMG. Bioanalytical methods for serum nipocalimab determination, RO, and total IgG determination and software are described in Text S2, S3, respectively.

Model‐Based Analysis

The time course of serum nipocalimab concentrations and RO data were described using an open two‐compartment distribution model, with parallel linear (CL) and target‐mediated clearance (Figure 1) [16]. After IV dosing, free nipocalimab was distributed into the volume of the central compartment (V c ). Nonspecific distribution from the central compartment into the peripheral compartment was characterized by intercompartmental clearance (Q) and peripheral volume of distribution (V p ). The CL, V c , Q, and V p were allometrically scaled. Nipocalimab binds to FcRn following a second‐order rate constant (k on ). The resulting nipocalimab‐FcRn complex was dissociated according to a first‐order rate constant (k off ) and generated free nipocalimab and FcRn, or was eliminated through a first‐order process, represented by rate constant k int . FcRn was also assumed to be produced following a zero‐order process, characterized by k syn , and degraded by a first‐order process with an elimination‐rate constant represented by k deg . The nonlinearity of nipocalimab PK was explained by the limited FcRn at a given time, relative to free nipocalimab concentration. Because serum nipocalimab concentration is generally in excess relative to FcRn on the endosomal cell membrane, the stoichiometry was assumed to be 1:1 and the quasi‐steady‐state approximation of the target‐mediated disposition model was used to describe the data [17]. The nipocalimab‐FcRn binding affinity, K D , defined as k off /k on , could not be estimated independently; thus, the steady‐state constant (K ss ), defined as K D + k int /k on , was estimated directly. Additionally, the exploratory analysis showed that the RO did not reach 100%, even at very high serum nipocalimab concentrations. Because not all FcRn is available to bind nipocalimab, the maximum fraction of FcRn available to bind nipocalimab (FRmax) was estimated directly from the data to avoid bias in model fitting. The M3 approach was used to analyze serum nipocalimab concentrations below the limit of quantification (LOQ) jointly with concentrations above the LOQ [18].

An indirect‐response model was used to describe the time course of total serum IgG concentrations. Total serum IgGs were assumed to be produced according to a zero‐order process, characterized by synthesis rate k syn_IgG , eliminated through a first‐order process, determined by degradation constant rate k deg_IgG , or recycled following a first‐order process, governed by the recycling constant rate k rec_IgG , which was also dependent on the fraction of free FcRn receptor available to bind nipocalimab relative to its baseline value. Because total serum IgG at baseline, IgG 0 , can be estimated directly from the data, and the system at baseline is assumed to be at steady state, k syn_IgG becomes explicitly defined as (k deg_IgG —k rec_IgG )·IgG 0 . As the total serum IgG elimination rate constant in the absence of nipocalimab, defined as k total , is equal to k deg_IgG —k rec_IgG , and the IgG elimination rate constant when nipocalimab saturates the FcRn is k deg_IgG , then k deg_IgG is higher than k rec_IgG and, consequently, k rec_IgG can be expressed as a fraction f of k deg_IgG . To constrain the estimation of f to be between 0 and 1, the IgK parameter, defined as f/(1‐f), was used to define k rec_IgG as kdeg_IgG·IgK1+IgK. Therefore, IgG 0 , k deg_IgG , and IgK were estimated directly from the total serum IgG data. Development of the IgG model was performed using a sequential process. Individual estimates of model parameters obtained from the PK/RO analysis were used to predict the individual time course of RO, which was used as an input function into the IgG model.

The MG‐ADL at time t, MG‐ADL(t), is the sum of three components. The first component corresponds to the MG‐ADL at baseline (MG‐ADL0). The second component represents the MG‐ADL change from baseline (CFB) due to the placebo effect, ΔMG‐ADLP(t). A placebo model was developed to describe the initial reduction in MG‐ADL following treatment initiation, followed by a slow linear reduction with time observed in the data. The effect of MG‐ADL0 on ΔMG‐ADLP(t) was also evaluated. The third component is the placebo‐corrected change from baseline (pcCFB) in MG‐ADL due to nipocalimab, ΔΔMG‐ADLDrug(t) . The relationship between nipocalimab‐induced relative total serum IgG concentration CFB and ΔΔMG‐ADLDrug(t) was quantitatively characterized by a linear relationship. To quantify the potential delay between the total serum IgG CFB and ΔΔMG‐ADLDrug(t), an effect compartment model was evaluated. In addition, the effect of MG‐ADL0 on the slope of the relationship between total serum IgG concentration CFB and ΔΔMG‐ADLDrug(t) was also evaluated.

The development of the IgG/MG‐ADL model was also performed using a sequential process. Individual estimates of model parameters obtained from the PK/RO/IgG analysis were used to predict the individual time course of total serum IgG concentration CFB, which was used as an input function into the IgG/MG‐ADL model. Equations of the PK/RO/IgG/MG‐ADL model are provided in Text S4. An exponential error model was used to characterize the interindividual random effects on model parameters, except for the IgG/MG‐ADL model, where an additive model was used. Residual unknown variability in serum nipocalimab concentrations and RO was modeled using an additive plus proportional error model; a proportional error model was used for total serum IgG, and an additive error model was employed for MG‐ADL CFB. Adequacy of the PK/RO/IgG/MG‐ADL models was assessed by the ability to capture the central tendency and variability of the data, as evaluated by the visual predictive check (VPC) and to provide population and individual predictions without significant bias, as confirmed by goodness‐of‐fit plots. Precision of the parameter estimates was also evaluated.

Model‐Based Simulations

Model‐based simulations were conducted to select the IV dose and dose regimen for the pivotal phase 3 study (Vivacity‐MG3) in participants with gMG. Four dosing scenarios were evaluated, varying in maintenance dose level (15 vs. 30 mg/kg Q2W), dose frequency (15 mg/kg Q2W vs. 30 mg/kg Q4W), and loading dose (with/without 30 mg/kg loading dose). A total of 10,000 virtual participants for each scenario were evaluated for a 24‐week treatment duration. The population simulations accounted for interindividual variability (IIV) as well as residual unknown variability and covariate effects. Individual body weights were sampled from a random normal distribution centered at a mean (SD) of 88.5 (25.7) kg, with a range of 45–188.2 kg, consistent with the body weight (BW) distribution in the phase 2 study (Vivacity‐MG) [10]. Individual MG‐ADL0 were sampled from a negative binomial distribution with a mean of 9.8 and dispersion parameter of 239.2. The distribution shape and dispersion parameter were estimated from the phase 2 study [10], and the mean and boundaries (ranging from 6 to 24 points, inclusive) were taken from the phase 3 Vivacity MG3 study inclusion criteria [19]. Proposed dosing for the phase 3 study was selected among the simulated scenarios as the lowest maintenance dose predicted to achieve the target median of the average IgG CFB over a dosing interval of –70%. This target was selected to exceed total serum IgG reductions from baseline observed for other anti‐FcRn mAb at dosing regimens approved for treating patients with gMG [20, 21]. A nipocalimab loading dose would be recommended if it led to higher total serum IgG reductions at 2 weeks after treatment initiation versus without the loading dose.

Additionally, a total of 550 absolute ΔMG‐ADL from 67 participants with gMG in the phase 2 (Vivacity‐MG) study [10] were used to conduct the IgG/MG‐ADL analyses. Overall, approximately half of the participants were female (53%), 68% were White, and 25% were Asian.

An indirect‐response model that accounts for the FcRn‐induced IgG recycling was suitable to describe the time course of total serum IgG concentration and its variability after nipocalimab IV administration in healthy participants and participants with gMG (Figure 2B,E). A slightly lower IgG baseline was estimated in participants with gMG from the study MOM‐M281‐004 (Vivacity‐MG) relative to healthy participants (p < 0.001). Overall, the IgG model parameters were similar in healthy participants and participants with gMG, suggesting that RO was also similar between these two study populations, even if confirmatory RO data were not measured in the phase 2 study in gMG (Vivacity‐MG).

The IgG/MG‐ADL model, characterizing absolute ΔMG‐ADL as the sum of the IgG‐dependent nipocalimab effect and the placebo effect, was able to describe the MG‐ADL data of study MOM‐M281‐004 (Vivacity‐MG) and their variability well (Figure 2F). A slight delay between total serum IgG reduction and ΔΔMG‐ADLDrug(t) (p < 0.001, T 1/2 = 1.67 days) supported the inclusion of an effect compartment in the model. The relative total serum IgG concentration CFB was found to be the most adequate driver of the nipocalimab effect on the ΔΔMG‐ADLDrug(t). Using total serum IgG concentration, absolute total serum IgG concentration CFB, free serum nipocalimab concentrations, or RO as drivers of the nipocalimab effect on ΔΔMG‐ADLDrug(t) worsened the model fit when compared with using relative total serum IgG concentration CFB. Higher MG‐ADL0 was associated with increasing both the placebo and IgG‐dependent nipocalimab effect on ΔMG‐ADL. According to the IgG/MG‐ADL model, each nipocalimab‐induced 10% reduction in total serum IgG was estimated to result in a 0.22‐ and 0.40‐point decrease in ΔΔMG‐ADLDrug(t), for a MG‐ADL0 score of 7 and 14, respectively.

Comparison of the IgG profile following the maintenance dose of 15 mg/kg Q2W and 30 mg/kg Q4W (Figure 3B) supports that it is better to administer lower doses more frequently because the average IgG CFB (and MG‐ADL pcCFB) is markedly deeper with Q2W than with Q4W dosing due to a higher proportion of the Q2W dosing interval having full RO compared with the Q4W dosing. This is why smaller fluctuations of IgG and MG‐ADL reductions are observed with Q2W relative to Q4W dosing. Thus, 15 mg/kg Q2W was considered more appropriate than 30 mg/kg Q2W or 30 mg/kg Q4W.

A loading IV dose of nipocalimab 30 mg/kg would lead to higher total serum IgG CFB reductions 2 weeks after the start of treatment than treatment without a loading dose (−68% vs. −54%) (Figure 3C). Under the same placebo effect as observed in the phase 2 study (Vivacity‐MG) [10], MG‐ADL pcCFB reductions at Week 2 are predicted to be higher in the presence of the loading dose (−1.97 vs. −1.57 points). The total serum IgG CFB and MG‐ADL pcCFB reductions after 1 month of treatment are predicted to be similar with or without a loading dose, suggesting that the loading dose effect would be completely washed out after 1 month.

Discussion

After IV administration, the concentration–time profile of nipocalimab followed a biexponential decline, reflected by the two‐compartment disposition model. The nipocalimab Vc was estimated at 3.23 L for a 75 kg BW, similar to the serum volume, and was associated with low IIV (15.0%). Like other mAbs, nipocalimab distributed into the serum and then into the interstitial space. Antibody distribution may occur by convective transport from the blood to tissue interstitial or receptor‐mediated endocytosis [22]. The Vss of nipocalimab (3.85 L for 75 kg BW) was in the low range of values reported for other mAbs [22]. This indicates limited extravascular distribution of nipocalimab, most likely due to FcRn binding in vascular endothelial cells, thus decreasing the amount of free drug available for distribution outside the vasculature.

Nipocalimab elimination from serum follows a dual process, consisting of both nonspecific (linear) and nonlinear (FcRn‐mediated) pathways. The linear clearance was estimated at 0.655 L/day for 75 kg BW, equivalent to 27.3 mL/h, which is higher than that for endogenous immunoglobulins (8.75–10 mL/h) [22, 23]. Nonlinear clearance due to FcRn binding reaches its maximum capacity (FcRn 0 × k int × V c /k ss = 677 L/day) at serum concentrations substantially lower than the k ss value. At concentrations of 6.05 μg/mL, the nonlinear clearance from FcRn binding reaches its half‐maximal capacity. At serum concentrations higher than 55 μg/mL (> 9 × kss), nonlinear clearance is > 90% saturated and total clearance approaches linear clearance. The apparent k ss value estimated from this analysis (42.6 nM, or 6.05 μg/mL) is higher than the in vitro k d . Much of this difference can be explained by the QSS assumption, since k ss is the sum of K d and k int /k on . Furthermore, in vitro and in vivo conditions are different (i.e., in vitro and in vivo are closed and opened systems, respectively), which may contribute to observed differences. Nipocalimab PK after single IV dose is consistent with repeated IV administration, with no accumulation following Q2W dosing, and no evidence of time dependency of nipocalimab PK parameters. Higher BW was associated with higher CL and Vc, consistent with the principle of allometry.

Since nipocalimab is generally in excess to FcRn, it was assumed the nipocalimab–FcRn binding was rapidly in equilibrium with a 1:1 stoichiometry. Under that assumption, the typical FcRn 0 value (143 nM) represented the baseline total FcRn concentration and was associated with low to moderate IIV (< 30%). The nipocalimab–FcRn complex internalization half‐life was estimated as 16 min. To enable model convergence, k deg was fixed to 1.3 days−1, consistent with the recently reported FcRn half‐life (11 h) [24]. Interestingly, the elimination rate of the nipocalimab–FcRn complex (k int = 62.4 days−1) was estimated to be much higher than the elimination rate of FcRn (k deg = 1.3 days−1) or the linear elimination rate of nipocalimab (CL/V c = 0.203 days−1). This was observed for other mAbs, such as omalizumab [25], denosumab [26], or daratumumab [27]; however, the turnover of FcRn is much faster than the turnover of the targets for those mAbs, suggesting that the reduction of free FcRn is rapidly compensated for by synthesis, especially during the period of the dosing interval when nipocalimab concentrations are below LOQ. It also implies that the steady‐state elimination rate of nipocalimab is dependent on the total amount of FcRn available for nipocalimab binding, which is mainly determined by the FcRn synthesis rate. On average, the rate of production of FcRn (k syn × V c , computed as FcRn 0 × k deg × FR max × V c ) is estimated to be 569 nmol/day (approximately 7.97 μmol in 14 days). A 15 mg/kg dose delivers a total of 7.92 μmol of nipocalimab for a participant of 75 kg. These data support the selection of a 15 mg/kg dose administered Q2W for gMG treatment; this is predicted to be sufficient to cause rapid and deep (> 90% RO) FcRn inhibition, maintained during the first 6 days of the Q2W dose interval in > 65% of participants with gMG.

The PK/RO/IgG model suggests that in the absence of nipocalimab (e.g., completely available free FcRn), the estimated IgG degradation rate (0.0360 days−1) was associated with a half‐life of 19.3 days, similar to values reported in the literature [28, 29]. At full RO (≥ 90% RO), nipocalimab was estimated to decrease IgG half‐life from 19.3 to 3.19 days, leading to an 83.5% decrease in total serum IgG concentrations. This corresponds to the physiological maximal percentage inhibition of IgG at steady state that can be achieved by fully blocking FcRn [30, 31], and is also in line with the lower IgG total serum concentrations reported in two individuals with an abnormality of endogenous protein catabolism of IgG (i.e., FcRn receptor) leading to an approximate 82% decrease in total serum IgG concentrations [32]. The decrease in FcRn recycling and the associated IgG reduction is proportional to the fraction of free (unoccupied by nipocalimab) FcRn relative to FcRn0, which determines the ultimate FcRn recycling rate. The estimated IgG degradation rate constant, when FcRn recycling is blocked (0.217 days−1), was consistent with the reported intrinsic IgG catabolic rate (0.180 days−1) [33].

The relationship between nipocalimab‐induced relative total serum IgG CFB and nipocalimab‐induced MG‐ADL pcCFB was quantitatively characterized by a linear relationship [10], where each 10% reduction in total serum IgG CFB achieves a typical value of 0.22‐point decrease in MG‐ADL pcCFB for the median baseline MG‐ADL of 7 points. A small delay between the IgG CFB and MG‐ADL CFB was observed and accounted for by empirically including a virtual effect compartment in the model, with an estimated half‐life of 1.17 days. The slope of the relationship between IgG CFB and MG‐ADL pcCFB increased with increasing MG‐ADL0. Consequently, a 70% IgG reduction CFB resulted in a 1.51 (95% CI: 0.26–2.77) and a 2.77 (95% CI: 0.62–4.74) point decrease in MG‐ADL pcCFB for a MG‐ADL0 of 7 (median baseline in the phase 2 Vivacity‐MG study [10]) and 14 (maximum baseline in the phase 2 Vivacity‐MG study [10]) points, respectively. The placebo effect included in the MG‐ADL model resulted in an initial 1.08‐point decrease in MG‐ADL after treatment initiation for a MG‐ADL0 of 7 points, further decreasing by 0.0596 points per week of treatment thereafter.

An IV dose of 15 mg/kg administered Q2W, starting 2 weeks after a 30 mg/kg IV loading dose, was predicted to provide rapid, deep, and sustained FcRn inhibition > 90% during the first 6 days of the 2‐week dose interval. This led to an average total serum IgG CFB of approximately −70% and provided a clinically meaningful reduction of 1.94 points of the absolute MG‐ADL pcCFB in participants with gMG assuming an MG‐ADL baseline distribution with a mean (range) of 9.8 (6–24) points. The loading dose was predicted to enable greater total serum IgG CFB and MG‐ADL pcCFB reductions early during treatment, assuming the same placebo effect as observed in the phase 2 Vivacity‐MG study [10].

Alternative dose regimens were considered less appropriate than the proposed regimen. Doubling the nipocalimab IV maintenance dose to 30 mg/kg Q2W was associated with minimal additional average total serum IgG and MG‐ADL reductions, compared with 15 mg/kg Q2W maintenance dosing. Doubling the nipocalimab IV maintenance dose and dosing interval (i.e., 30 mg/kg Q4W) did not achieve the target of a −70% average relative total serum IgG CFB and resulted in larger peak‐to‐trough fluctuations in both total serum IgG concentrations and MG‐ADL score reductions within a dose interval, compared with the 15 mg/kg Q2W maintenance dosing.

In summary, the integration of PK, RO, IgG, and MG‐ADL data generated during nipocalimab development demonstrated that the IV dose of 15 mg/kg Q2W, starting 2 weeks after a 30 mg/kg IV loading dose, was the lowest Q2W maintenance dose predicted to provide rapid, deep (> 90%), and sustained FcRn inhibition during the first 6 days of the 2‐week dose interval period in 65% of participants and was the recommended dose for the pivotal phase 3 study (Vivacity‐MG3). This dose is predicted to lead to a steady‐state average total serum IgG CFB of approximately −70%, fluctuating between a nadir of −80.1% and a predose of −65%, providing a clinically meaningful reduction in absolute MG‐ADL pcCFB in participants with gMG, as has recently been shown in the pivotal Vivacity‐MG3 phase 3 study, where a 1.45 point reduction (p = 0.002) in LS mean MG‐ADL pcCFB after 22–24 weeks of nipocalimab treatment was observed in patients with gMG receiving IV nipocalimab 15 mg/kg every 2 weeks starting 14 days after a 30 mg/kg loading dose [34].
