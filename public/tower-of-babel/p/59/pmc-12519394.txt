Municipal officials’ perspectives on challenges and opportunities in shaping urban built environments for active living in Thailand: a qualitative study
Authors: Thitikorn Topothai, Chompoonut Topothai, Nicholas Alexander Petrunoff, Viroj Tangcharoensathien, Rapeepong Suphanchaimat, Falk Müller-Riemenschneider
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMunicipalities play a critical role in shaping built environments that support active living, yet little is known about how municipal officials interpret these agendas, implement them or balance them with competing policy demands. This study explores how municipal officials in urban Thailand perceive and implement built environment strategies, manage constraints and identify opportunities to promote active living.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative study using semistructured interviews with 27 informants: eight elected officials, 15 senior appointed officials and four technical staff from seven municipalities across Thailand. Municipalities were purposively selected to reflect variation in geography across seven regions and administrative levels, including metropolitan, city and town contexts. Thematic analysis combined deductive coding based on policy-related topics such as roles, coordination and governance challenges with inductive coding to identify emergent themes.ResultsThree themes emerged. First, municipal officials commonly viewed the built environment through a recreational lens, prioritising parks and leisure spaces for their visibility, popularity and alignment with community expectations. Planning was influenced by leadership preferences and external models. Second, implementation was hindered by institutional fragmentation, overlapping mandates, weak cross-sector coordination and contextual factors such as complex land use, unregulated street activity, motorcycle-dominated mobility and political sensitivities. These challenges were more pronounced in smaller municipalities with limited technical capacity. Third, enabling factors included strengthened decentralisation, national incentives (eg, additional floor area near transit hubs), partnerships to repurpose underused spaces, digital tools such as mobile apps for responsive service delivery, adaptive leadership for public communication and leveraging local assets to support inclusive, context-sensitive development.ConclusionIn urban Thailand, municipal strategies for active living continue to emphasise recreation over mobility. Implementation remains constrained by institutional and contextual challenges. Advancing more inclusive and responsive environments will require stronger governance, supported by decentralisation, community partnerships, digital tools, local assets and adaptive leadership.Keywords: Environmental health, Health education and promotion, Health policy, Public Health

Introduction

Urbanisation and increasingly sedentary lifestyles have contributed to a global rise in non-communicable diseases, especially in middle-income countries. Physical inactivity is a major health risk, accounting for 7.2% of overall mortality and 7.6% of cardiovascular mortality worldwide.1 Despite global targets to reduce physical inactivity by 15% by 2030,2 global rates increased from 23.4% in 2000 to 31.3% in 2022,3 while East and Southeast Asia saw a rise from 20.8% to 24.6%.4 In Thailand, physical inactivity rose from 18.5% in 2008 to 28.1% in 2021, with over 75% of adults reporting more than 7 hours of sedentary time daily.5 These trends highlight the need for systemic and context-specific approaches that support active living.

Active living refers to incorporating physical movement into everyday routines through walking, cycling, household activities and recreation.2 6 7 It depends not only on individual motivation but also on environments that support movement in safe, accessible and convenient ways.2 7 The built environment, including land use, street design, transport systems and public spaces, plays a critical role in enabling active lifestyles.8,12 In many Thai cities, however, urban environments remain unsupportive.11 13 14 Dense neighbourhoods often lack sidewalks, safe crossings and adequate transport links. Other barriers include the widespread use of motorcycles, the presence of informal street vending and extreme weather conditions such as heat and heavy rain. While awareness of physical activity has grown, investment in infrastructure and long-term urban planning remains limited.15 A global review ranked Thailand near the bottom for urban health and sustainability, reflecting ongoing policy and planning challenges.11

Local governments are well positioned to promote active living because of their authority over land use, infrastructure, transport and public spaces.16 However, their actions are shaped by broader institutional, political and cultural factors. International experiences show that these conditions strongly influence implementation. In the UK, active travel gained support when aligned with economic objectives rather than health promotion alone.17 In Australia, partnerships between municipalities and universities helped advance walkable and inclusive planning.18 In both Vietnam and the USA, collaborative planning was facilitated by progressive local leadership and political commitment, enabling effective coordination across sectors.19,22 In contrast, Jamaica’s national initiatives struggled due to weak local authority and limited coordination.23

In Thailand, the authority and capacity of municipalities vary by level. While city municipalities often show stronger implementation, smaller ones can also contribute when supported by adequate resources and partnerships.13 24 A review of recent 5-year plans in the seven municipalities included in this study indicates that active living projects account for only a modest share of total planned spending, with large variation across localities.25,31 These differences suggest uneven prioritisation and limited integration of built environment strategies into local planning. Despite national frameworks that promote active living and position local governments as key implementers,14 little is known about how municipal officials interpret these agendas, apply them in practice or manage competing policy demands. This gap highlights the need to understand not only what municipalities plan but also how and why certain priorities are pursued or overlooked across different contexts.

This study addresses a key knowledge gap by examining how municipal officials in urban Thailand perceive and implement built environment strategies for active living. It explores how they set priorities, navigate constraints and identify opportunities. By comparing municipalities with varying responsibilities and resources, the study offers insights into how local governments shape active living efforts across diverse urban contexts.

Methods

Research design

We employed a qualitative design informed by an interpretive epistemology, acknowledging that participants construct meaning through their experiences, roles and institutional settings. The study focused on municipal officials involved in planning, coordination and implementation. Semi-structured interviews were conducted across different administrative levels. Thematic analysis was used to identify patterns in how officials navigated constraints, allocated resources and understood their roles in shaping the built environment.

Sampling and recruitment

Seven municipalities were purposively selected to reflect variation in administrative level and regional context: two metropolitan areas (Bangkok and Pattaya), three cities (Chiang Mai, Hat Yai and Khon Kaen) and two towns (Ang Thong and Kanchanaburi) (figure 1). Participants were identified through purposive and snowball sampling, with a focus on departments responsible for urban planning, public works, public health, environment and transport. After completing interviews in two municipalities, the sampling strategy was refined to concentrate on departments most directly involved in planning and implementing built environments that support active living. Participants included both elected leaders (mayors, deputy mayors and advisors) and appointed senior officials (permanent secretaries and department directors), as well as technical staff. This allowed us to capture perspectives across strategic and operational levels.

Geographic distribution of seven selected municipalities across Thailand.

Data collection

An open-ended interview guide was developed to explore how municipal officials interpret and implement built environment strategies to support active living. The guide was structured around three guiding questions: (1) How do municipal officials interpret and prioritise built environment strategies for active living? (2) What barriers and contextual challenges do they face in implementation? and (3) What opportunities, adaptive practices or enabling factors support more effective implementation? These questions reflected domains commonly used in implementation research, including technical, financial, political and legal considerations, and were adapted to the Thai municipal context. Key concepts such as physical activity, sedentary behaviour and built environments were defined to ensure a shared understanding between researchers and participants. The guide was piloted and refined during early interviews.

Each interview lasted between 40 and 70 min and was conducted in municipal offices to ensure participant comfort and confidentiality. All participants received an information sheet and provided written informed consent for audio recording, transcription and the use of their data for research purposes. They were also verbally informed that their interviews would be translated into English as part of a PhD research project conducted by researchers affiliated with the National University of Singapore. Interviews were conducted in Thai by TT and CT, audio recorded with permission, transcribed verbatim and translated into English using ChatGPT. All transcripts were anonymised before translation and manually reviewed by TT and CT, who are fluent in both Thai and English, to ensure accuracy and maintain confidentiality. Data saturation was reached when no new themes or concepts emerged from subsequent interviews.

Data analysis

We used a thematic analysis approach that combined both deductive and inductive elements, following the codebook method described by Braun and Clarke.32 TT and CT independently conducted line-by-line coding of all transcripts using NVivo (version 14) and developed preliminary codebooks. Deductive codes were guided by the research questions, while inductive coding allowed new insights to emerge from the data. Regular team discussions were held with NAP, FMR, RS and VT to refine the codes and ensure consistency in interpretation. Through this iterative process, a unified codebook was developed. Final codes were then organised into sub-themes and overarching themes.

Reflexivity

The research team comprised public health researchers from the National University of Singapore and Thailand’s Ministry of Public Health, including two PhD candidates (TT and CT), two Thai health policy researchers (VT and RS) and two academic staff (NAP and FMR). TT and CT, both in their late 30s, brought expertise in qualitative methods and urban health governance, with TT having direct experience working with local governments on active living. Their backgrounds as medical doctors, prior engagement with municipal stakeholders and familiarity with decentralisation policy helped build trust and rapport with senior officials. Before each interview, they reviewed the municipality’s 5-year plan and local geography to inform the discussion. While their established roles supported access, the team remained attentive to potential influence on participant responses and maintained neutrality throughout data collection and analysis. VT and RS are civil servants with extensive experience in Thai health policy. NAP and FMR specialise in physical activity promotion and urban health in Asia.

Of 32 participants invited, 27 (84%) from various municipal departments participated (table 1). The sample included eight elected officials (29.6%), 15 senior appointed officials (55.6%) and four technical staff (14.8%). Three main themes emerged. First, built environments were commonly viewed through a recreational lens, with parks and public spaces seen as promoting well-being and livability. Planning priorities were shaped by leadership preferences, community expectations and adaptation of ideas from domestic and international models. Second, implementation was constrained by institutional barriers, fragmented coordination and contextual challenges including land limitations, informal economies and political dynamics. Third, enabling factors included decentralisation, national policy support, cross-sector collaboration, digital tools and the strategic use of local assets and adaptive leadership to advance inclusive, context-sensitive development. Policy perspectives on built environments

Common understanding of built environment through a recreational lens

Across municipalities, participants frequently framed the built environment in terms of recreational infrastructure. Parks, playgrounds and sports facilities were widely cited as core municipal responsibilities and viewed as visible, tangible investments that aligned with public expectations. These spaces were considered politically safe and broadly supported by the public.

“Policies focused on community health and recreational spaces receive positive responses and full support from residents.” (Appointed senior officer, Metropolitan municipality)

This framing reinforced the perception that physical activity belonged primarily to the recreational domain, rather than being integrated into daily routines or the broader urban fabric. Sidewalks and pedestrian infrastructure were typically described in utilitarian terms, maintained for safety and access, but rarely seen as tools to promote active living. However, participants from larger or more urbanised municipalities described efforts to better align transport systems, land-use planning and public space design with goals related to health and active lifestyles.

“We're working on improving the accessibility of our public transport systems to ensure that they cater to the needs of all residents.” (Appointed senior officer, Transport, Metropolitan)

Built environments for well-being and inclusive development

Participants commonly described public parks and recreational areas as central to promoting physical activity, supporting mental well-being and enhancing urban livability. These spaces were also seen as inclusive environments that foster social connection and a sense of community belonging.

“We see families exercising and having picnics in the park, and grandparents watching their grandchildren play in the playground.” (Elected leader, City municipality)

Parks often served as venues for cultural events, public meetings and local celebrations, reflecting everyday social and cultural practices within the community. Participants also emphasised that investments in green and recreational spaces not only supported healthier lifestyles but also enhanced the city’s image and contributed to economic vitality.

“We’re building a walkway along the Lam Tha Daeng canal [a river just outside the city center] with a significant budget. The plan is to turn it into a floating market and host long-boat races there.” (Elected leader, Town municipality)

Drivers in shaping built environment priorities

Participants explained that built environment priorities were shaped by leadership, community input and external influences. In many municipalities, planning decisions reflected the preferences of elected leaders and senior officials. Personal interests in health, livability or aesthetics often translated into support for parks and green spaces, which were viewed as visible, popular and politically rewarding. In contrast, infrastructure such as walkways or connectivity upgrades tended to receive less attention.

“Personally, I prioritize health, exercise, and green spaces.” (Appointed senior officer, Town municipality)

Community engagement was also seen as a central influence. Participants emphasised that public expectations played a key role in shaping municipal agendas, particularly in contexts where citizen satisfaction contributed to political legitimacy.

“It comes down to people’s expectations…people see what other cities have accomplished, and they expect us to deliver the same.” (Appointed senior officer, City municipality)

Participants frequently drew on domestic and international examples to inform local planning. Learning from other Thai municipalities through study visits and peer exchange helped inspire new ideas. Global models, particularly from Singapore or Japan, offered aspirational benchmarks, though adaptation to local context remained key.

“Many of us feel that Singapore offers a good model, especially for [accessible] public transport…” (Appointed senior officer, Public Works, City municipality)

Challenges in implementing built environment policies

Institutional barriers within local government units

Participants identified several internal constraints limiting built environment implementation. A common challenge was limited financial flexibility. While most municipalities could maintain basic infrastructure, projects promoting active living often required additional national funding.

“We have a large budget, but it needs to allocate funds across many areas, like waste management, roads, education, and healthcare.” (Appointed senior official, Transport Department, Metropolitan municipality)

Administrative processes were often described as rigid and slow, constrained by outdated regulations and overlapping mandates between ministries. These issues created confusion and limited the decision-making authority of local governments.

“One law may permit action, but another from a different ministry can create obstacles.” (Appointed senior officer, City municipality)

Human resource limitations were also a concern. Smaller municipalities struggled to recruit skilled staff, while larger ones faced high turnover and weak accountability, especially among non-local or retiring officials.

“Officials who move here and plan to retire don't always feel accountable to the community, since they aren't locals.” (Appointed senior officer, City municipality)

Institutional capacity varied widely. Smaller towns often relied on central agencies due to gaps in expertise, planning tools and data. In contrast, metropolitan municipalities had specialised departments and stronger systems, enabling more effective regulation and integration of urban development goals.

“We set and enforce standards of Floor Area Ratios…to ensure the right balance and density in every neighborhood.” (Appointed senior officer, Urban Planning, Metropolitan)

Fragmented coordination and limited engagement

Participants consistently identified coordination challenges with external actors, especially for large-scale projects involving transport, utilities or land use. Administrative silos, misaligned timelines and weak interagency governance often delayed implementation. Political uncertainty and jurisdictional constraints further complicated efforts, particularly when projects crossed local boundaries or involved state-owned land.

“Even though the project [light rail system] is led by local and private sectors, we still need central approval for land use.” (Elected leader, City municipality)

Private sector engagement was described as sporadic and constrained. While participants saw its potential to support development, unclear institutional roles, limited risk-sharing mechanisms and regulatory ambiguity discouraged investment. A lack of experience with public–private partnerships also reduced confidence.

“It’s unclear whether the private sector should invest in public transport, and if so, which sectors would benefit.” (Appointed senior official, Transport Department, Metropolitan municipality)

Managing diverse expectations and securing consensus were described as ongoing challenges. Sustaining community engagement was also difficult. While consultations were commonly conducted during the initial planning stages, continued participation throughout implementation was often limited.

“Everyone has different opinions, so getting the community on board is tough.” (Elected leader, City municipality)

Contextual disparities across municipalities

Participants consistently noted that physical, cultural and political contexts shaped the feasibility of built environment initiatives. In older cities, dense urban layouts, heritage preservation requirements and concerns about community identity often constrained infrastructure development. Several participants described encountering resistance from residents who were concerned about potential disruptions to historic areas.

“Our city has a long history. So, it’s hard to push new projects especially in the existing old structures.” (Elected leader, City municipality)

Participants also pointed to fragmented land use, high-density zones and unresolved property rights as obstacles in planning. In areas with informal markets or insecure tenure, residents were often wary of development due to fears of displacement or loss of income.

“Sidewalk vending has become a cultural norm, but clearing the space often upsets people as it affects shopping convenience.” (Appointed senior officer, Public Works, Town municipality)

Transport behaviour was another influential factor. Officials noted that motorcycles were the primary mode of transport in many areas due to their convenience, which reduced reliance on public transport. Some also observed that the rise of food delivery services further discouraged walking, while cycling was generally viewed as a recreational activity rather than a practical mode of transport.

“Songthaew [two-row shared pickup trucks] are unlikely to be used here—our town is small, and most households already have motorcycles for daily travel.” (Appointed senior officer, Public Works, Town municipality)

Political dynamics also influenced implementation. Several participants noted that alignment between mayors and municipal councils often played a critical role in determining whether projects advanced.

“Political differences can hinder project approval, as beneficial initiatives may be blocked over concerns about who gets the credit.” (Appointed senior officer, City municipality)

Opportunities and enablers for advancing built environments

Empowering local action through decentralisation, policy incentives and shared learning

Participants emphasised that advancing built environments required more empowered and responsive local governance. Decentralisation was seen as a key driver, enabling municipalities to address local priorities and develop tailored solutions more effectively than top-down approaches.

“Formulating policies at the central level can lead to fragmented efforts…decentralization could speed things up and solve problems faster.” (Appointed senior official, Public Works Department, Metropolitan)

At the same time, national incentives and regulatory frameworks were viewed as important for aligning development with broader public goals. Tools like floor area bonuses, zoning flexibility and transit-oriented development policies encouraged developers to contribute to inclusive, walkable environments. Participants noted that these mechanisms supported land use and transport integration.

“Bonuses and incentives [for additional unit allowance] can provide [for developers] to encourage the creation of green spaces…” (Appointed senior official, Urban Planning Department, Metropolitan)

Peer learning was also seen as a key enabler. Participants described adapting ideas from other municipalities, both domestically and internationally, through site visits, study tours and informal exchanges. Referenced examples included well-maintained and well-designed green or recreational spaces, integrated transport systems and compact city planning, which served as inspiration for local adaptation.

“Simply put, we observe what others are doing and openly acknowledge that we’re learning from them to apply in our own context.” (Appointed senior officer, Town municipality)

Co-creating urban spaces through partnerships and dialogue

Participants emphasised that collaboration across sectors was key to improving built environments, especially in municipalities with limited resources. Public–private partnerships added flexibility and funding, such as tax incentives for property owners to temporarily repurpose vacant land into gardens, parks or markets.

“If you have unused land and want to avoid paying property taxes, you can let the municipality use it to create community kitchen gardens or temporarily turn it into a sports field.” (Elected leader, City municipality)

Local authorities also partnered with private transport operators to introduce electric buses and reduce car dependency. While municipalities did not always manage transport systems directly, they coordinated services and ensured compliance.

“We collaborate with the private sector, especially [bus company name], to operate electric smart buses that reduce private car use and serve the municipal area.” (Appointed senior officer, City municipality)

Participants noted that commercial areas were being adapted for broader community use. Malls added greenery to become spaces for rest and interaction, and school grounds were opened after hours for public recreation.

“As shopping malls transform into showrooms…adding greenery and improving the aesthetics can create more relaxing spaces.” (Appointed senior officer, City municipality)

Community engagement was supported through public meetings and local committees, which helped build trust and align projects with public needs. Participants stressed the importance of transparent communication, especially when projects required trade-offs.

“We listen to the people…it’s important to be able to answer to the public by showing them what we've done, where we've invested, and what we've achieved.” (Elected leader, Town municipality)

Participants also emphasised the importance of adaptive collaboration and communication. Resistance to projects was common, particularly when they impacted private property or daily routines. Instead of rigid enforcement, officials preferred gradual improvements accompanied by ongoing dialogue with the community.

“When some households resist pathway renovations, we use public relations and improve surrounding areas gradually…any inconvenience is temporary.” (Appointed senior officer, City municipality)

Digital tools for responsive governance and local mobility

Participants highlighted the growing role of digital technologies in improving municipal responsiveness, coordination and service delivery. Mobile applications supported issue reporting, public feedback and real-time communication, helping to reduce delays and minimise reliance on paper-based systems. Messaging platforms such as LINE were commonly used to streamline interdepartmental communication and improve planning coordination.

“Our bureau has used its own app for five years to let the public report issues, enabling quick responses and reducing paperwork. We've resolved 99% of cases with full records.” (Appointed senior officer, Public Works, City municipality)

Social media, though capable of amplifying criticism, was recognised as a useful platform for dialogue and feedback. Participants emphasised the need to engage with public concerns constructively, focusing on the substance of issues rather than reacting emotionally.

“…it’s challenging with so many differing opinions on social media…still, we must allow space for dialogue and focus on the message, not the emotion.” (Appointed senior officer, Public Works, City municipality)

In transport, participants noted that digital solutions helped address gaps in formal systems. Ride-hailing apps such as GrabBike, LINE Taxi and Bolt offered flexible first- and last-mile options in areas with limited public transit. Real-time tracking tools like ViaBus improved convenience and public confidence, expanding mobility access without the need for major infrastructure investments.

“The solution could be using technology, like mobile apps for shared rides or hiring motorcycle taxis.” (Appointed senior official, Transport Department, Metropolitan municipality)

Leadership and local assets for inclusive urban development

Participants emphasised the importance of strong leadership and strategic use of local assets in advancing inclusive urban development. Effective leadership was viewed as essential for navigating political dynamics, building support and sustaining project momentum. Moving major initiatives forward required articulating a shared vision, coordinating across levels of government and securing funding from central agencies. Participants valued leadership teams that combined institutional experience with local credibility, which helped strengthen decision-making and build public trust.

“For major projects, we begin with surveys, then coordinate with both local and national administrations. Our MPs [members of parliament] represent us nationally, and we [the mayor] must present the idea to the budget office and demonstrate its potential.” (Elected leader, Town municipality)

Cultural identity and place-based assets were seen as important drivers of development. Municipalities sought to integrate heritage, landscape and local character into planning efforts to strengthen community identity, support tourism and enhance the quality of public spaces.

“As a key destination for beach activities, our city is improving the beach infrastructure to attract tourists and generate income.” (Appointed senior official, City municipality)

Participants described flexible approaches to managing informal street activity as essential for preserving urban vibrancy and supporting economic inclusion. Time- and location-specific allowances were used to balance the needs of pedestrians with the livelihoods of street vendors.

“We enforce sidewalk rules during busy hours but allow flexibility at night to keep the city lively and convenient. Sidewalks are cleaned before dawn for daytime use.” (Appointed senior officer, City municipality)

Improving local quality of life was a central goal, particularly in smaller municipalities. Even modest upgrades to parks or community spaces were viewed as meaningful contributions to livability and well-being.

“Although we're a smaller city, our focus is on improving the quality of life for our residents through recreational spaces…” (Appointed senior officer, Town municipality)

Discussion

This study provides a systems perspective on how municipal officials in urban Thailand interpret and implement built environment strategies to support active living. The findings illustrate how individual beliefs, institutional arrangements and community dynamics interact to shape local responses to national policy agendas and urban development priorities.

A recurring theme was the tendency to frame the built environment primarily through a recreational lens. Parks and leisure spaces were often prioritised due to their visibility, popularity and political safety. This reflects earlier public health approaches in Thailand and other settings, where active living was associated more with aesthetics and recreation than with mobility or equity.7 33 34 Although global frameworks now emphasise daily mobility and transport as integral to active living, these shifts have been slower to gain traction locally.3 6 13 24 35 In contrast, high-income countries increasingly promote walkability, mixed-use development and transit connectivity as core strategies to embed movement into daily routines.9 11 36 For example, in Singapore, nearly half of adult physical activity is transport-related.37 Despite long working hours and extended sitting time, overall sedentary behaviour is lower than in Thailand, suggesting that supportive environments can help counter occupational risks. While Thailand’s recreation-focused approach aligns with cultural preferences, it may constrain the broader potential of built environments to support movement throughout daily life.

Municipal officials recognised the broader co-benefits of recreational infrastructure, including social cohesion, urban identity, environmental quality and local economic development. This framing helped facilitate cross-departmental collaboration and aligned with Thailand’s National Physical Activity Plan, which promotes multisectoral action and shared outcomes.14 Similar ‘win–win’ approaches have been observed internationally, where active living is integrated with urban renewal, social inclusion and sustainable transport.10 11 36 38 39 In the United Kingdom, positioning active living around congestion reduction or economic gains has often been more effective than health-based messaging.17 Likewise, Thai municipalities have implemented sidewalk upgrades, traffic calming and transit improvements, although these are rarely framed as health initiatives. Reframing such efforts around livability and co-benefits may help strengthen institutional commitment. In Australia, partnerships between municipalities and universities have supported evidence-based planning,18 while in Thailand, peer-to-peer learning has facilitated informal policy diffusion. These examples underscore the importance of shared learning and relational networks in advancing active living strategies.

Despite growing opportunities, institutional constraints remained a major challenge. Municipalities faced rigid procedures, outdated regulations and limited financial autonomy. Competing demands such as waste management and social services frequently diverted attention and resources away from built environment improvements. Although decentralisation policies have expanded local authority in Thailand, many municipalities continued to operate within centralised planning frameworks and ambiguous mandates, which constrained their ability to act effectively. Similar challenges have been documented in Europe, the Caribbean and Southeast Asia, where underinvestment and bureaucratic complexity weaken long-term planning and implementation.17 19 23 33 In such contexts, planning systems often limit innovation and hinder strategic coordination. In Thailand, capacity gaps were more pronounced in smaller municipalities, which commonly lacked technical expertise, digital tools and access to reliable data. In contrast, larger urban areas with dedicated planning staff and stronger institutional support were better equipped to coordinate cross-sectoral efforts. These disparities highlight the need for planning reforms that enhance institutional flexibility, strengthen decentralisation and enable data-driven decision-making across diverse municipal settings.18 40

Cross-sector coordination and multilevel governance remained limited. Fragmented mandates, unclear roles and weak engagement with civil society and the private sector hindered implementation. Similar issues are reported in Vietnam and the United Kingdom, where overlapping responsibilities reduce alignment with local needs and limit stakeholder involvement.17 19 European cities have also struggled to integrate health goals into urban planning,33 reflecting a broader pattern of intersectoral pluralism, where competing agendas and diffuse authority complicate coordination. Despite these challenges, municipalities in Thailand employed public consultations, local committees and digital tools to build support and manage trade-offs. Innovations such as mobile apps and real-time transport tracking improved responsiveness and enabled more adaptive, context-sensitive planning, as also observed in Vietnam.41 Public–private partnerships helped repurpose underused spaces, expand transport options and revitalise urban areas. These efforts were most effective when grounded in shared goals, transparency and trust. As shown in other contexts, collaboration also enabled municipalities to secure funding, reallocate resources and demonstrate public support for built environment initiatives.42

Effective leadership and contextual responsiveness were both essential to the successful implementation of built environment strategies for active living. Leaders played a central role in setting priorities, navigating political dynamics and sustaining interdepartmental collaboration. Political alignment often influenced whether projects advanced, while officials who combined technical expertise with political credibility were better able to engage stakeholders and overcome bureaucratic barriers. These qualities were especially important given that active living policies are highly sensitive to implementation contexts and often constrained by diffuse accountability, limited institutional ownership and varying local conditions.19 22 At the same time, local transport patterns, land use and informal urban practices shaped how interventions were designed, delivered and received. Strategies that reflected local culture, values and spatial dynamics were more likely to gain public support and generate meaningful outcomes. International evidence similarly suggests that initiatives aligned with local norms and conditions tend to achieve stronger uptake and more sustainable impact.19 22 42

This study drew strength from purposive sampling across municipalities with varied administrative levels, regional and socio-economic contexts, enabling comparison across metropolitan, city and town settings. Including both elected and appointed officials provided insights into decision-making at both strategic and operational levels. Thematic analysis captured locally grounded insights and informal practices often missed in top-down evaluations. However, the focus on municipal perspectives excluded national agencies, private developers and community voices. Future research should include a broader range of stakeholders to better understand intergovernmental coordination and long-term implementation dynamics. While the findings are grounded in the Thai context, they may offer transferable insights for other countries with similar governance structures, urban challenges and decentralisation processes.

Conclusion

This study shows how municipal officials in urban Thailand conceptualise and implement built environment strategies to support active living. These environments were often framed around recreation and livability, with planning shaped by leadership preferences, community expectations and adapted external ideas. Implementation was constrained by institutional barriers, fragmented coordination and local challenges such as land use, informality and political dynamics. In response, municipalities drew on decentralisation, national policy support, cross-sector collaboration, digital tools, local assets and adaptive leadership to advance inclusive, context-sensitive development. Moving forward, stronger municipal capacity and better integration of built environment strategies into broader urban policy will be essential to promoting active living.
