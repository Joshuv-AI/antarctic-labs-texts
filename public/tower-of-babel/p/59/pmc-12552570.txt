Patterns of altered in vivo brain metabolism in patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and asymptomatic C9orf72 mutation carriers: a cross-sectional 1H and 31P magnetic resonance spectroscopic 7T imaging study
Authors: Henk-Jan Westeneng, Abram D. Nitert, Kevin van Veenhuijzen, Carrie Wismans, Graziella Donatelli, Harold H.G. Tan, Wytse van Hoek, Michael A. van Es, Dennis W.J. Klomp, Alex A. Bhogal, Jan H. Veldink, Jannie P. Wijnen, Leonard H. van den Berg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundProcesses leading to the onset of neurodegeneration in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) are largely unknown. To gain insight into disease mechanisms, we measured brain metabolism in vivo in asymptomatic C9orf72 mutation carriers and patients with ALS.MethodsWe enroled 15 asymptomatic family members with (AFM C9+) and 18 without a C9orf72 mutation (AFM C9−), 4 patients with ALS with (ALS C9+) and 35 without this mutation (ALS C9−), and 25 population-based controls (CO). Two-dimensional proton (1H) and whole-brain phosphorus (31P) magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging (MRSI) data were obtained using a 7T MR scanner. 11 brain metabolites were compared between groups using weighted Bayesian linear multilevel models.FindingsCompared to AFM C9−, AFM C9+ showed evidence of neuronal dysfunction (decreased total N-acetyl aspartate/total creatine (tNAA/tCr)), widespread increased membrane breakdown product (glycerol phosphorylethanolamine/phosphocreatine (GPE/PCr)), glutamate excitotoxicity (increased glutamate + glutamine/tNAA (Glx/tNAA)) and, in frontoparietal regions, an increase in the glycogen precursor uridine diphosphoglucose/PCr (UDPG/PCr). Compared to AFM C9+, neuronal dysfunction and membrane breakdown are similar in ALS C9+, but glutamate excitotoxicity and increased glycogen precursor are more severe and widespread, also involving the primary motor region. Moreover, lower total adenosine triphosphate/PCr (tATP/PCr) emerged in ALS C9+, and signs of disturbed membrane synthesis, intracellular second messenger system and glial pathology (myo-inositol + glycine/tCr (mI + Gly/tCr)). ALS C9− is characterised by glutamate excitotoxicity, increased tATP/PCr, and lower phospholipid levels.Interpretation1H and 31P 7T MRSI can detect evolving patterns of altered brain metabolism in asymptomatic mutation carriers and patients with ALS. Abnormalities in patients with ALS C9+ appeared to be different from those in patients with ALS C9−. Metabolic markers, measured in vivo, can serve as biomarkers for inclusion or stratification as well as for drug-target engagement in clinical trials. This method can facilitate identification of new and personalised drug targets to prevent or treat this devastating disease.FundingALS Foundation Netherlands.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

To identify relevant studies, we searched PubMed using the keywords “amyotrophic lateral sclerosis” or “motor neuron disease” and “chemical shift imaging” or “magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging” or “magnetic resonance spectroscopy” in English, Dutch, French, and German up to January 11, 2024. Our focus was on in vivo human studies examining brain metabolism in patients with ALS and their asymptomatic family members. A recent review by Christidi et al. (2022)1 summarised the strengths and weaknesses of studies in this area. Since that review, two additional studies have been published. In total, 84 studies examined patients with ALS. None of these studies reported the C9orf72 mutation status, nor examined asymptomatic mutation carrier or non-carrier family members of patients with ALS. Integrated 1H and 31P magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging (MRSI) was not performed in any of these studies. Most reviewed studies have small cohort sizes (ten studies with more than 80 participants) and perform spectroscopy in one to three voxels (seven studies used whole brain analyses). These voxels were typically positioned in motor cortices and supplementary motor areas, revealing a decrease in the neuronal marker N-acetyl aspartate. The single study employing 31P MRSI in a single coronal slice in 30 participants observed altered phosphorus metabolism in the pons of patients with ALS. Studies utilising ultra-high field strength (7T) MRSI (N = 3), though characterised by smaller cohort sizes (N = 37 or fewer), demonstrated higher spectral resolution in detecting metabolites, revealing glutamate changes in ALS.

Added value of this study

In our study of 97 participants, including symptomatic and asymptomatic C9orf72 mutation carriers, groups which had not previously been studied, we performed ultra-high field 2D 1H and 3D 31P MRSI of 11 brain metabolites to investigate neuronal, glial, glutamatergic, energy and phospholipid metabolic pathways in vivo. We observed an evolving pattern of altered brain metabolism in asymptomatic individuals with a C9orf72 mutation and in patients with ALS with this mutation, a pattern distinct from patients with ALS without this mutation. Asymptomatic family members with the C9orf72 mutation exhibited neuronal and glutamatergic dysfunction (the latter not in the precentral gyrus), and altered glycogen and phospholipid metabolism. In patients with ALS with this mutation, these findings were more severe and widespread, including the motor region, and other metabolites became involved indicating deficits in energy metabolism and membrane synthesis. This was distinct from the pattern found in patients with ALS without a C9orf72 mutation, which was more restricted, involving glutamatergic and energy metabolism and lower phospholipids.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our study has found evidence of ALS-related pathological processes occurring years before the anticipated onset of symptoms, which influences our thinking about when to start disease-modifying- or gene therapy. We have identified candidate cerebral metabolic markers that could serve as biomarkers for phenoconversion in future studies. We have demonstrated that the patterns of metabolic brain involvement are heterogeneous across different types of ALS, indicating distinct underlying mechanisms and suggesting the need for varied therapeutic approaches. Metabolic markers, which assess pathological processes in vivo in a non-invasive way, could serve as personalised biomarkers for inclusion, stratification, or to assess drug-target engagement in clinical trials. This approach has the potential to advance the development of precision medicine with the goal of preventing or treating this devastating disease.

Introduction

Neurodegenerative diseases are a heterogeneous group of disorders involving complex pathophysiological mechanisms that are poorly understood. No cures for these diseases exist, and their prevalence is increasing globally, both in terms of burden and incidence.2 Non-invasive in vivo assessment of processes underlying neurodegeneration during the presymptomatic and symptomatic phases can improve understanding, and thus bring treatments a step closer.

One of the most devastating neurodegenerative diseases is amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), characterised by motor neurone loss, leading to progressive muscle weakness, respiratory insufficiency and eventually death.3 Up to 50% of patients with ALS develop cognitive or behavioural impairment, and up to 15% frontotemporal dementia (FTD).3 ALS and FTD have a common genetic basis in the C9orf72 repeat expansion.3 In Europe, this mutation accounts for 44% of the familial and 5% of the apparently sporadic patients with ALS, and 22% and 6% of FTD cases, respectively.4

Previous neuroimaging studies have demonstrated structural and functional alterations in patients with ALS compared to controls.5 These alterations are more pronounced in patients with ALS with a C9orf72 repeat expansion.6 Asymptomatic carriers have progressive structural brain involvement.7 However, pathological processes underlying neurodegeneration remain poorly understood. Since metabolic changes likely precede structural changes,8 their detection might offer an opportunity for preventing structural changes, or for treating (pre)symptomatic participants before potentially irreversible structural changes occur.

Magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging (MRSI) can non-invasively map metabolite concentrations throughout the brain. Using 2D 1H and 3D 31P MRSI enables us to assess neuronal, glial, glutamatergic, energy, and phospholipid metabolites in one scan session in asymptomatic C9orf72 mutation carriers and patients with ALS. Using ultra-high field MRSI (7 T), we can measure cerebral metabolism in vivo at higher spatial resolution, with higher signal-to-noise ratios and improved identification of metabolites. With this approach, we aim to increase our understanding of the metabolic processes underlying neurodegeneration, as well as to demonstrate which processes and brain regions are involved during the presymptomatic phase, and how this relates to the symptomatic phase. Understanding these spatio-temporal dynamics of neurodegeneration in ALS may facilitate the development and timing of preventive and personalised interventions in presymptomatic individuals and patients with ALS before (further) irreversible damage has occurred.

Drawing on previous research,3,9 primarily conducted in vitro and ex vivo, we hypothesise that neuronal, glial, glutamatergic, energy, and phospholipid metabolism is affected in patients with ALS, that the C9orf72 mutation influences these deficits, and that this mutation causes subtler metabolic changes during the asymptomatic phase, which are measurable in vivo. We expect to find neuronal dysfunction in ALS. Additionally, as neurodegeneration likely begins before symptom onset, we also anticipate identifying neuronal deficits in asymptomatic carriers of the mutation. Given that glial pathology is less prominent than neuronal pathology in ALS, we hypothesise that we will find limited evidence of glial pathology. Because glutamate excitotoxicity is a core pathological characteristic in ALS, we expect to observe signs of glutamate excitotoxicity in ALS and possibly in asymptomatic mutation carriers as well. Regarding energy metabolism, we may identify indicators of glycogen metabolism deficits in carriers of the C9orf72 mutation and, in patients with ALS, disturbances in electron transport and total adenosine triphosphate (tATP). Since ALS is a neurodegenerative disease, involvement of phospholipid metabolism could include increased breakdown and decreased synthesis of membrane phospholipids. Identifying the metabolic deficits in asymptomatic individuals at risk of developing ALS, as well as in patients with ALS, could facilitate the development of effective (preventive) treatments and enable monitoring of treatment effects in patients, thus promoting personalised medicine.

Methods

Study population

Patients with ALS and unrelated controls were recruited from an ongoing population-based, case–control study performed in the Netherlands.10 Additionally, asymptomatic family members (AFM) belonging to a family with multiple patients with ALS were recruited, as previously described.11 AFM were considered to be asymptomatic if no signs or symptoms of motor neurone degeneration or behavioural or cognitive impairment could be detected at enrolment or during a follow-up period of at least one year, as previously described.7,12 All patients and AFM were tested for a pathogenic C9orf72 repeat expansion, defined as 30 or more GGGGCC repeats, using repeat primed polymerase chain reaction (PCR) methods. Participants were classified into 5 groups: controls (CO), asymptomatic family members of patients with ALS with (AFM C9+) or without a C9orf72 mutation (AFM C9−), and patients with ALS with (ALS C9+) or without this mutation (ALS C9−). None of the patients with ALS C9− had a positive family history of ALS or FTD, and none had pathogenic mutations in known ALS genes. Patients were asked to stop taking Riluzole 7 days before the MRI and to restart it thereafter. All patients fulfilled the revised El Escorial diagnostic criteria.13 Behavioural variant FTD was diagnosed according to the international consensus criteria. Participants with a history of epilepsy, stroke or any overt structural brain abnormality were excluded. Additional exclusion criteria for COs were a history of psychiatric illness, neurodegenerative disease, or a family history of ALS or FTD.

Procedures

All participants were assessed at the outpatient clinic for motor neurone diseases (MND) of the University Medical Centre Utrecht by clinicians specialised in MND. During visits, questionnaires about symptoms of ALS and the revised version of the ALS functional rating scale (ALSFRS-R) were administered,14 a neurological examination was performed, as previously described,12 and participants were screened for cognitive or behavioural deficits, using the Edinburgh Cognitive and Behavioural ALS Screen (ECAS)15 and ALS-FTD questionnaire (ALSFTD-Q).16 Clinicians were blinded as to the participants’ genetic status. AFM were enroled per family, thus including both AFM C9− and AFM C9+ to keep the clinicians blinded. During the post-processing of the spectroscopic data, the researchers were blinded, and quality control was completely automated, resulting in a negligible risk of confounding due to researcher bias.

MRI data were acquired in two consecutive sessions using a 7 T MR system (Philips Achieva, Best, the Netherlands). During the first session, high-resolution anatomical proton density (PD)- and T1-weighted images were acquired (T1: repetition time/echo time (TR/TE) = 8.0/2.9 ms, flip angle 6°, PD: TR/TE = 8.0/1.7 ms, flip angle 5°, T1 and PD: voxel size = 0.98 × 0.98 × 1 mm3, field of view (FOV) = 224 × 224 × 200 mm) using a 1H quadrature head coil with 32 receive channels (Nova Medical, Wilmington, USA). Two-dimensional (2D) free-induction (FID) 1H MRSI with tailored water suppression was performed (TR/TE = 300/2.5, flip angle = 35°, FOV = 220 × 220 × 10 mm, voxel size = 5 × 5 x 10 mm3, number of signal averages = 2, duration = 11 min) in one or two slices (Fig. 1) using a 1H quadrature head coil with 32 receive channels (Nova Medical, Wilmington, USA), as previously described.18,19 Water unsuppressed MRSI data were acquired for zeroth order phase and eddy current correction. A T1-weighted slice matching every MRSI slice was collected to map FID signals anatomically. Second-order image-based shimming was performed for all acquisitions, and an external crusher coil was used to suppress extra-cranial lipid signals.20 Raw data from individual receive channels were combined and spectroscopic images were reconstructed using coil sensitivity profiles derived from a 2D T1-weighted fast field echo (FFE) image. On each FID signal, we performed B0 correction, automatic first-order phase correction, and residual water signal removal. During the second session, we performed a whole-brain 3D 31P MRSI using a custom-made 31P–1H double-tuned quadrature head coil to acquire an FID signal for each voxel (Fig. 1). Second-order image-based shimming was performed on the whole brain volume. We choose the combination of TR and flip angle to optimise sensitivity for ATP signals (Ernst angle for a T1 of 1.6 s). As it is currently unknown whether and to what extent the T1 and T2 of metabolites are affected by ALS, it is not possible to account for this. Hamming filtering and zeroth- and first-order phase corrections were applied to each FID signal. To map all metabolic data anatomically, T1-weighted images were acquired during both sessions to co-register each scan with separately collected high-resolution whole-brain T1-weighted images (3T T1), acquired on a 3 T MR system as described previously.11Fig. 1Study design and methods. High-resolution T1-weighted scans were corrected for bias in receive coil sensitivity, proton density and T2∗ contrast by dividing them by the PD images. N4-bias field correction was performed using advanced normalisation tools (ANTs). The corrected image, T1-weighted image(s) of the slice(s), and low-resolution T1-weighted image were linearly registered to the 3T T1 using FSL and ANTs (Fig. 1). Using FreeSurfer version 6.0, deep grey matter in the 3T, T1 was segmented into seven regions per hemisphere, and cortex was parcellated using the Desikan-Killiany atlas, comprising 34 regions per hemisphere; the corpus callosum was added as 35th region.17 The cortical regions were dilated up to 5 mm from the white-grey boundary into the white matter. White matter remaining after this parcellation and dilation was defined as centrum semiovale. Because of the higher resolution of the 1H MRSI, we used all the above-mentioned regions, if intersected by the MRSI slice(s). Because of the lower resolution of the 31P MRSI, 24 larger brain regions were investigated ((legend of) Fig. 1). The primary motor regions were analysed separately because of their importance in ALS.6,7

1H and 31P FID signals were Fourier transformed and metabolites were quantified using LCModel.21 Investigated 1H and 31P metabolites and their abbreviations are summarised in Table 1. 1H metabolites were expressed as a ratio over tCr except for Glx, which was expressed as a ratio over tNAA, because of the relationship of Glx/tNAA with glutamate excitotoxicity.27 All studied 31P metabolites were expressed as a ratio over PCr, and we conducted sensitivity analyses using the total sum of 31P metabolites as reference, and in another sensitivity analysis, grey matter fraction as an additional covariable in the 31P MRSI analyses. We performed quality control of the spectra and retained only spectra with a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) ≥ 5 and line width (full width at half maximum, FWHM) ≤ 0.155 ppm. In these spectra, only metabolites with Cramér-Rao lower bounds (as provided by LCModel) ≤ 20% were used for further analysis.21 Finally, an outlier detection algorithm was applied to each metabolite. Ethics

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and the Medical Ethical Committee of the University Medical Centre Utrecht (NL55287.041.15, METC 15/650) approved this study.

Statistics

We performed four group comparisons: (1) AFM C9+ vs. AFM C9−, (2) ALS C9+ vs. AFM C9+, (3) ALS C9− vs. CO, and (4) ALS C9+ vs. ALS C9−. We used a Bayesian multilevel model with age and, if appropriate, scanner software version as covariables, and kinship, participant ID and brain region as non-nested grouping variables (i.e., random intercepts) and participant group as group-level effect (i.e., random slope) varying over each brain region. We designed our models based on theoretical considerations: covariables were selected because of their established relationship with metabolic parameters, random effects were chosen as metabolite concentrations may vary between cerebral regions, a heterogeneity that could also depend on the participant, mutation carrier status, and kinship. We used age as a covariable but not sex, as evidence of the effect of sex on brain metabolism is limited, and its inclusion did not improve the fit of our models. We used the additive genetic relationship matrix to account for the genetic relationships (i.e., kinship) among individuals within and between families, rather than nesting individuals within families, which does not account for genetic relationships within families. Grey matter fraction and, when applicable, slices were included as additional covariables in the 1H analyses. As the voxel volume of the 31P MRSI is over 35 times larger than that of the 1H MRSI, these voxels were too large to meaningfully correct for the grey matter fraction. We applied a partial volume correction by weighting analyses for the fraction of each MRSI voxel corresponding to each brain region. To accommodate potential non-normally distributed metabolite ratios, we employed the skew-normal distribution to model the data. To define consistent priors for all metabolites, we scaled metabolite ratios to mean zero and standard deviation one. We used weakly informative priors to penalise extreme (and thus more unlikely) values. Priors were centred at zero (i.e., mode of prior is zero) to reflect our assumption that no effect is the most likely finding. In summary, we have selected the priors to penalise extreme values, thus preventing overfitting without enforcing rigid and arbitrary cutoffs, and ensuring that both positive and negative effects were equally likely a priori. To account for potentially heterogeneous effects of the variable of interest (e.g., C9orf72 status) on different brain regions, β1,i is allowed to vary over brain regions. Xn comprises the nuisance variables (i.e., age, grey matter fraction, software release), and βn are their corresponding coefficients. MVN(μ, Σ) is the multivariate normal distribution with means μ and covariance matrix Σ. R is the correlation matrix corresponding to Σ. N(μ, σ) indicates the Gaussian distribution with mean μ and standard deviation σ. t(μ, σ, ν) is the half Student-t distribution with mean μ, standard deviation σ, and degrees of freedom ν. LKJcorr(η) is the LKJ correlation distribution with shape parameter η (1 indicating that the density is uniform over correlation matrices).

To statistically evaluate this approach, we conducted Bayesian leave-one-out cross-validation (BLOOCV) of our models alongside simpler alternative models with a Gaussian response distribution to evaluate the risk of overfitting. Moreover, we used graphical posterior predictive checking to assess the fit of our models. We conducted sensitivity analyses of the priors by applying power-scaling perturbations of the priors with values of 0.5 and 2, and used the cumulative Jensen-Shannon divergence (CJSD) as a measure of prior sensitivity with a default threshold of 0.05.28 Bayesian methods are valid in small groups, such as the ALS C9+ group, as they do not rely on asymptotic assumptions. We used multilevel models to control for multiple hypotheses testing per metabolite.29 Because mutual relationships between metabolites might be modified by participant group, we performed follow-up analyses if two or more metabolites per nucleus (i.e., 1H or 31P) differed significantly between groups. Using Bayesian multilevel models, we assessed the effect of participant group on the pairwise relationship between these metabolites (i.e., group–metabolite interactions). In these interaction analyses, we used the same covariables and grouping variables as described above, but, because we averaged over all brain regions, we did not include brain regions in the model. For Bayesian analyses, we reported the maximum probability of effect (MPE), which varies between 0.5 and 1.0 and represents the probability of a strictly positive or negative effect of the whole posterior distribution (whichever is the more probable). Similar to p-values, MPE has no 95% confidence interval. Outcomes were also presented as median (95% credible interval (95%-CI)). Although we acknowledge that null-hypothesis testing might lead to erroneous interpretations of results,30 we defined a 95%-CI that did not cross zero as statistically significant, which equals an MPE >0.975 and is comparable to a p-value <0.05 in a frequentist null-hypothesis testing framework.

Using BLOOCV, we demonstrated that our models fitted the data significantly better than simpler alternative models in 82% of the analyses and never performed significantly worse (Supplementary Material, Table S2). Furthermore, our models did not exhibit overfitting. The sensitivity analyses of priors indicated that the CJSD was consistently <0.05, confirming that our results were not driven by the choice of priors. The estimates using respectively PCr and the sum of 31P metabolites as the denominator for 31P analyses were similar (Spearman rho: 0.97, p-value <0.001; Figures S9–S12). Results of 31P MRSI analyses with grey matter fraction as an additional covariable (Figures S13–S16) were similar to those without this covariable. To enable researchers to replicate our findings independently, all estimates of the posterior distributions per metabolite ratio and brain region are reported in the Supplementary Material (Tables S3 and S4). Discussion

Using ultra-high field 2D 1H and 3D 31P MRSI, we investigated 11 brain metabolites in vivo to obtain insight into neuronal, glial, glutamatergic, energy and phospholipid metabolic pathways in patients with ALS and asymptomatic C9orf72 mutation carriers. To better distinguish the effects of the C9orf72 mutation from other familial effects, we used non-carriers from the same family (AFM C9−) as controls for family members with a C9orf72 mutation (AFM C9+). We observed an evolving pattern of altered brain metabolism in AFM C9+ and ALS C9+, distinct from ALS C9−. In AFM C9+, we detected altered brain metabolism characterised by neuronal and glutamatergic dysfunction, and altered glycogen and phospholipid metabolism. However, there were also indications of activated protective mechanisms in the primary motor region. These findings indicate that pathological processes are present years before the expected onset of the first symptoms and influence our thinking about when to start disease-modifying or gene therapy. In ALS C9+ we found similar abnormalities, but glutamatergic dysfunction and altered glycogen metabolism are more severe and widespread, including the motor region. Moreover, other metabolites became involved in ALS C9+: lower tATP/PCr and mI + Gly/tCr and a probable widespread increase in NAD/PCr. These could serve as candidate biomarkers for phenoconversion in future follow-up studies. In patients with ALS without a C9orf72 mutation, we found a more restricted pattern involving glutamatergic and energy metabolism and lower phospholipids. The results of our study show that metabolic markers, measured in vivo, could serve as biomarkers for inclusion or stratification as well as for drug-target engagement in clinical trials. This method could facilitate identification of new and personalised drug targets with the aim of preventing or treating this devastating disease.

Previous 1H MRS studies in ALS typically focused on one to three voxels in a region of interest, covering a relatively small amount of the whole brain at magnetic fields <7T.1,32 These studies demonstrated decreased tNAA in the primary motor area and other frontal regions.1,32, 33, 34 Recently, one 2D 31P MRSI study was performed, showing altered phosphorus metabolism in the pons of patients with ALS.35 Due to methodological differences (e.g., 2D MRSI vs. 3D MRSI, 3T vs. 7T, brain region(s) measured), it is difficult to compare results directly. Whole-brain 31P MRSI has not been used in ALS before, and no previous MR(S)I studies have integrated 1H and 31P MRSI nor performed these analyses in AFM C9+ or ALS C9+.1,35 With our more comprehensive approach, we were able to investigate multiple pathways in the same individual with less (1H MRSI) or no (31P MRSI) spatial bias.

We have hypothesised involvement of neuronal, glial, glutamatergic, energy, and phospholipid metabolism in C9orf72 mutation carriers and patients with ALS, which we discuss per metabolic pathway and relate to the available literature.

Neuronal dysfunction was already observed in AFM C9+ years before the expected disease onset, at a similar level as in ALS C9+. Longitudinal studies are needed to discern whether observed effects are developmental, pathological, or a combination of both. Our recent longitudinal imaging study suggests a greater likelihood of the latter.7 Neuronal dysfunction was also observed in the primary motor regions of ALS C9, but without reaching statistical significance. Lower (t)NAA/(t)Cr was fairly consistently observed in previous (single voxel) MRS studies of the primary motor regions.1,32 Our study is in accordance with these findings, but because our 1H MRSI slices were perpendicular to the motor regions, resulting in a relatively small sample from the entire motor region, and because of our correction for multiple comparisons, the reduction in level of tNAA/tCr did not reach statistical significance.1,32 Furthermore, the neuronal dysfunction is more severe in C9+, which could be a source of bias in previous MR(S)I studies that did not analyse the effect of C9 status.

The glial pathway was analysed through mI + Gly/tCr. We acknowledge that the function of myo-inositol (mI) is broader (Table 1) and includes membrane synthesis, intracellular second messenger system and glial pathology. ALS C9+ demonstrated abnormal mI + Gly/tCr, which was normal in AFM C9+ and, at most, marginally abnormal in ALS C9−.

Glutamatergic dysfunction is a core characteristic of ALS. Using Glx/tNAA, proposed as a measure of glutamate excitotoxicity,27 we demonstrate that glutamate excitotoxicity is present in AFM C9+, is more severe and widespread in ALS C9+ and is present in distinct brain regions in ALS C9−. We also observed opposite effects in the primary motor region (i.e., low Glx/tNAA) of AFM C9+, which might indicate activation of a protective mechanism of motor neurones against glutamate excitotoxicity. Moreover, our interaction analyses revealed lower than normal Glx in the case of high tNAA, and higher than normal Glx in low tNAA, which could indicate increased susceptibility to glutamate excitotoxicity in AFM C9+, in line with previous in vitro studies.36 Thus, in relation to glutamate excitotoxicity, AFM C9+ demonstrate signs of both vulnerability and activated protective mechanisms. Further unravelling of mechanisms increasing resistance to glutamate excitotoxicity could lead to identification of drug targets to treat or even prevent development of C9orf72 repeat expansion-related diseases. Based on these outcomes, riluzole, which inhibits glutamate excitotoxicity and is approved for ALS, would be an obvious candidate drug to investigate its effect on delaying or preventing onset of ALS in AFM C9+.

Energy metabolism is affected in AFM C9+, ALS C9+ and ALS C9−, but different metabolites are involved. We observed altered glycogen metabolism, suggestive of glycogen shunt dysfunction,24 in AFM C9+ and more severe and widespread in ALS C9+. In a recent in vitro study, this is attributed to altered glycogen mobilisation due to decreased levels of two enzymes (i.e., glycogen phosphorylase and phosphoglucomutase) catalysing glycogen degradation.37 Decreased mobilisation of glycogen leads to impaired glutamate uptake and thus increased extracellular glutamate concentrations, which likely contributes to glutamate excitotoxicity.24 We found decreased tATP and probable increased NAD in ALS C9+, which might indicate impaired electron transport and potentially mark phenoconversion, since this was not observed in AFM C9+. Impaired electron transport is in line with findings in a recent in vitro study.38 In contrast, altered energy metabolism in ALS C9− is characterised only by increased tATP.

We observe different patterns of phospholipid metabolism dysfunction in asymptomatic C9orf72 mutation carriers and patients with ALS. In AFM C9+, we observed increased membrane turnover, where membrane degeneration (GPE/PCr) appears more severe than can be compensated for by membrane synthesis (PC/PCr and PE/PCr). In ALS C9+, PE and PC metabolism, which is normally biochemically coupled,25 is likely uncoupled: PE could not increase when PC increases (Supplementary Material, Figure S8). PE is important for the synthesis of mitochondrial inner membranes and essential for normal mitochondrial morphology. Our results suggest that, especially in high PE-demanding settings, its synthesis falls short, possibly leading to a compensatory increase in PC. In ALS C9−, decreased levels of membrane breakdown products are compatible with decreased cell membrane turnover.

A limitation of this study is the relatively small ALS C9+ group. However, previous research has shown large effect sizes in ALS C9+ cases.6 Moreover, hundreds of measurements (i.e., voxels) per participant were combined using Bayesian methods, not reliant on large samples, and with sensible priors to reduce bias. Consequently, wider credible intervals in analyses involving patients with ALS C9+ reflect greater uncertainty due to the smaller sample size. A larger sample size of ALS C9+ will lead to reduced uncertainty, narrower credible intervals, and likely more significant differences. Secondly, longitudinal studies are needed to investigate whether the observed evolving pattern of metabolic dysfunction from AFM C9+ to ALS C9+ is indeed predictive of phenoconversion and how metabolic changes evolve over time in patients with C9− and C9+ ALS. Thirdly, the relatively short TR will result in increased T1-weighting. As it is currently not known whether and to what extent the T1 and T2 of metabolites are affected by ALS pathology, it is not possible to account for this. Fourthly, we used Glx/tNAA as a proxy for glutamate excitotoxicity since it comprises both glutamate (the major constituent of Glx), which is elevated in glutamate excitotoxicity, and neuronal loss or dysfunction that results from it.27 Moreover, it is reliably quantifiable. Other ratios, such as gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)/Glx, indicate a potential imbalance between these neurotransmitters, reflecting hyperexcitability rather than glutamate excitotoxicity, and are more challenging to measure accurately due to the low concentrations of GABA. Thus, although Glx/tNAA is not exactly the same as glutamate excitotoxicity, it serves as the best proxy available for this condition in MRSI. Glx/tNAA is also employed in other studies as a proxy for glutamate excitotoxicity.39 Finally, due to currently unknown factors, nearly all patients in the studied family developed ALS and not FTD or ALS-FTD. Our results might, therefore, be best interpreted as related to ALS pathology, although we found effects in brain regions related to FTD and have no other reasons to suggest that effects in presymptomatic FTD patients would be distinct.

Our results demonstrate distinct cerebral metabolic alterations in ALS C9− and AFM/ALS C9+ and signs of ALS/FTD pathology in vivo before disease onset; they provide a rationale for randomised controlled trials in AFM C9+ and uncover new disease characteristics, such as regional heterogeneity of glutamate excitotoxicity during the presymptomatic phase, as well as altered membrane turnover and glycogen metabolism in vivo. Overall, our findings will facilitate improving the design of personalised drug trials in ALS and increase the prospect of finding more effective therapies for our patients.
