A framework for maximizing the benefit from retaining regrowth on private land
Authors: Hannah Thomas, Jeremy S. Simmonds, Michelle S. Ward, Laura J. Sonter, Martine Maron
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConservation interventions often have lower‐than‐desired positive impacts, as revealed by retrospective counterfactual‐based evaluations. To address this, a prospective counterfactual‐based approach can be used to estimate potential benefits and design conservation interventions to maximize outcomes. We developed a framework for estimating the expected benefit of retaining natural regeneration of forest and woodland ecosystems (hereafter regrowth). First, we considered the additionality of retaining regrowth, by developing a model of deforestation risk. Second, we considered the potential habitat value that the regrowth provides to 29 threatened species, based on expert‐elicited estimates for each species that account for regrowth age. Then, we combined this information to identify where retention of regrowth would yield the greatest additional benefits. Applying this framework to a case study in Queensland, Australia, which contains globally significant deforestation rates and exceptional biodiversity, we found that areas with the greatest expected benefit from retaining regrowth differ greatly when both averted loss of regrowth clearing and threatened species habitat are considered together, versus when these factors are considered individually. We also found that most (56%) of the highest expected benefit areas (i.e., above the 90th percentile) were consistently identified regardless of whether species were treated equally or weighted based on their threatened status or proportion of habitat occurring in regrowth. Our results can help guide conservation investment in the region, and we recommend a similar approach be used more broadly when designing conservation interventions to maximize benefits.

INTRODUCTION

Significant loss of natural ecosystems has driven the biodiversity and climate crises (Chase et al., 2020; Li et al., 2022). We must restore ecosystems to partially recover the economic, social, and ecological benefits they provide (Chazdon & Brancalion, 2019). In recognition of this, the Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework Target 2 is to restore 30% of degraded terrestrial, marine, and freshwater ecosystems by 2030 (Convention on Biological Diversity, 2022), and 2021–2030 is the United Nations Decade of Restoration (United Nations, 2024). Large‐scale areas of natural (i.e., unassisted) regeneration occur in some landscapes (Chazdon & Guariguata, 2016). Encouraging and retaining natural regeneration (hereafter regrowth) will be an efficient and cost‐effective strategy to work toward these restoration targets.

Regrowth can be a significant natural asset on agricultural land, providing shade and shelter for livestock and habitat for pest‐controlling bats and birds (Baker et al., 2018; Frank, 2024). In addition, regrowth can provide habitat for biodiversity to persist in human‐modified landscapes (Chazdon et al., 2009). Despite this, regrowth is often recleared at a young age. For example, in the Brazilian Amazon, 60% of regrowth forests were recleared before they reached 10 years of age (Nunes et al., 2020), and in Costa Rica, half of all regrowth forests were recleared before they reached 20 years of age (Reid et al., 2019). In part, this is because regrowth can represent a cost to landowners, including the lost opportunity cost of agricultural production (Bryan et al., 2011), the cost of managing the vegetation, and the potential decrease in property value from uncleared land (Ansell et al., 2016). In addition, clearing of regrowth is often less tightly regulated than clearing of remnant vegetation (Evans et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2020).

In recognition of the financial costs of retaining regrowth, and the lack of regulation to protect it, particularly in private lands, a diverse range of conservation interventions have emerged. These include direct financial payments to landowners to retain regrowth, provision of materials to fence areas that have naturally regenerated, grant funding opportunities, or tax deductions (Ansell et al., 2016). For example, direct financial payments to landowners to retain secondary forests have been implemented in Mexico (Charoud et al., 2023) and Costa Rica (Daniels et al., 2010), and an environmental organization in Australia provided payments and management advice for landowners to fence areas of natural regeneration (Spooner et al., 2002).

These conservation interventions are often popular with stakeholders in private lands because they do not rely on stricter regulation (Salzman et al., 2018). However, there is a lack of empirical evidence to demonstrate whether these interventions have led to conservation impacts beyond what would have been expected in the absence of the intervention (i.e., a retrospective counterfactual‐based approach) (Jones & Lewis, 2015). Of those few rigorous evaluations, evidence is mixed. Sharma et al. (2023) examined the effectiveness of conservation covenants (a form of protected areas on private land) in increasing woody vegetation cover on private properties in Victoria, Australia. The program‐level impact had a positive effect; the annual increase in woody vegetation cover was 0.3–0.8% greater than would have been expected in the absence of the scheme (Sharma et al., 2023). However, the impact of individual covenants was mixed, with both increases and decreases in woody vegetation cover (Sharma et al., 2023). A payments for ecosystem services scheme in Bolivia provided financial payments, materials, and education to communities to encourage forest retention. However, a randomized control trial showed no impact on deforestation rates between control and treatment communities (Wiik et al., 2019).

In contrast to a retrospective counterfactual approach, in a prospective counterfactual approach, the counterfactual scenario is modeled before the conservation intervention is initiated (Grace et al., 2021; Sacre et al., 2020). Thus, the intervention can be designed to maximize potential conservation impact. In terms of effort to retain regrowth, counterfactual modeling would evaluate the likelihood of vegetation clearing in the absence of the intervention. If the land was unsuitable for agricultural production (e.g., steep slopes; Joppa & Pfaff, 2009), or it was not economically viable to clear, the vegetation may not be cleared, even in the absence of the intervention. Thus, there would be no additional conservation benefit resulting from the intervention. Several studies have applied a prospective counterfactual approach to evaluate the expected benefits to biodiversity if future conservation actions are placed in areas most at risk of deforestation (Monteiro et a. et al., 2020; Negret et al., 2024). For example, Negret et al. (2024) modeled protected area expansion in Colombia and demonstrated that targeting the most high‐risk deforestation areas was more than twice as effective at retaining habitat for Colombia's forest‐dependent bird species than targeting areas at lower deforestation risk, in terms of the area of land required. Few studies have applied this approach to the design of conservation interventions on private lands.

We addressed this knowledge gap by applying a prospective counterfactual approach to identify areas of regrowth vegetation that provide the greatest expected benefit, when considering both the additionality of retaining regrowth (through avoided loss of clearing) and the potential habitat value that the regrowth provides to threatened species. First, we developed a vegetation change model to simulate counterfactual future regrowth change with the modeling platform Dinamica EGO 7. Second, we calculated the relative value that regrowth provided to a selection of threatened species, based on habitat mapping, regrowth vegetation age, and expert elicited knowledge on each species' ecology. We combined this information to identify where retention of regrowth would yield the greatest benefits across three bioregions in southern Queensland, Australia. These three bioregions contain ∼80% of Queensland's regrowth and experience among the highest contemporary clearing rates in Australia (Queensland Government Department of Resources, 2024). We sought to inform the development of conservation interventions in a deforestation hotspot with rich biodiversity and restoration potential and illustrate how a prospective counterfactual‐based approach can help guide this process.

METHODS

Study area

The study area comprised three large bioregions (Brigalow Belt, Mulga Lands, and sSoutheast Queensland) in Queensland, Australia (Figure 1). The climate is subtropical, with high rainfall along most of the coastal areas and more arid conditions further from the coast. Open woodlands dominated by various species of Eucalyptus and denser Acacia tall shrublands and forests are most common, interspersed with naturally sparse areas of minimal tree cover. The dominant land use is agriculture, mainly livestock grazing and some cropping. Mining and forestry also occur, and Southeast Queensland contains the only significant urban footprint.

The three study bioregions in Queensland: Mulga Lands, Brigalow Belt, and Southeast Queensland (inset, location of Queensland's bioregions; green, woody vegetation regulated under Queensland's vegetation management framework; purple, regrowth vegetation [i.e., unregulated woody vegetation]; white, areas with no woody vegetation that are largely cleared areas with no regrowth; blue, areas with nonwoody vegetation [e.g., grasslands] [Queensland Government Department of Environment, Science and Innovation, 2024b]).

Land clearing context

The majority of clearing over the last four decades in Australia has occurred in Queensland. Reclearing of woody vegetation that regrows post‐clearing (i.e., regrowth clearing) usually accounts for around 75–80% of total vegetation clearing each year (Queensland Government Department of Resources, 2024). Most regrowth is unregulated by Queensland's vegetation management framework and thus can be freely cleared, at least under this legal framework (Thomas et al. 2024). A small amount of regrowth, termed high‐value regrowth, is regulated and can only be cleared in certain circumstances. Our study focused exclusively on the portion of regrowth that is unregulated by Queensland's vegetation management framework, and all further mention of regrowth referred only to unregulated regrowth (refer to Thomas et al. [2025] for thorough explanation of Queensland's vegetation management framework and vegetation classifications). In our study region, most of the clearing could be attributed to pasture establishment for livestock grazing, with smaller contributions from the resource sector, forestry, infrastructure, and urban development (Queensland Government Department of Resources, 2024).

Spatial analyses

Our aim for the spatial analysis was to map the relative expected benefit of retaining regrowth for a 20‐year (2022–2042) time frame in three bioregions in the southeastern part of Queensland, Australia (Figure 2). Expected benefit was based on two factors: the additionality of retaining that vegetation (i.e., the risk of clearing in the absence of incentive payments) and the habitat value of the regrowth to 29 nationally listed threatened species. To do this, we first calibrated a vegetation change model in Dinamica EGO 7 (Soares‐Filho et al., 2002; www.csr.ufmg.br/dinamica) to understand the spatial and biophysical variables that were correlated with regrowth loss. We then mapped potential habitat (see Step 6 for definition) for 29 threatened species in regrowth, weighted by the relative value of that habitat compared to habitat provided by remnant vegetation. We based this on the age of the regrowth vegetation and each species' particular habitat requirements. We then combined both of these maps to develop the final expected benefit map. Besides Dinamica EGO, all other analyses were conducted with ArcGIS Pro (ESRI, 2025). The data and code for this analysis are openly available at UQ eSpace (https://doi.org/10.48610/0cdce05).

Summary of steps required to estimate the final expected benefit of retaining regrowth over 20 years (2022–2042) (blue, steps related to estimating averted loss; green, steps related to threatened species habitat value; teal, steps related to estimating the overall expected benefit; SNES, Species of National Environmental Significance; SLATS, Statewide Landcover and Trees Study).

Estimating averted loss of regrowth clearing

In Step 1, we created annual land‐cover maps (100 m2) for each year from 2018 to 2022 (n = 4 years) and classified these into four classes: remnant woody vegetation, regrowth woody vegetation, remnant nonwoody vegetation (e.g., grasslands), and cleared land. Woody vegetation was defined as both native and non‐native vegetation with a stand size of at least 0.25 ha and a crown cover of >10%, regardless of height and age (Queensland Government Department of Environment, Science and Innovation, 2024a).

To create the above land‐cover maps, we used the Queensland Government's annual 10‐m resolution map of woody vegetation for Queensland (Queensland Government Department of Environment, Science and Innovation, 2024c). We then overlayed the Queensland Government Regulated Vegetation Management map to classify woody vegetation as either regulated or unregulated (Queensland Government Department of Resources, 2022). We extracted all areas classified as both unregulated and woody vegetation as they represent the areas of unregulated regrowth that are the focus of this study. Areas classified as both unregulated and woody vegetation also included non‐native plantations, so we removed plantation leases to concentrate on naturally occurring forest and woodland ecosystems (Queensland Government Department of Agriculture and Fisheries, 2021). Finally, we overlayed the Queensland Herbarium's Broad Vegetation Group mapping to differentiate nonwoody areas between those that are remnant vegetation (e.g., grasslands) and cleared land (Queensland Government Department of Environment, Science and Innovation, 2024b).

In Step 2, we used a set of biophysical and anthropogenic variables that correlate with spatial patterns of deforestation in Queensland (Seabrook et al., 2007; Simmons et al., 2018; Sonter et al., 2017, 2020) to evaluate which factors affected the transition of regrowth to cleared land (Table 1). All variables were categorized and matched the coordinate system and resolution of the input land‐cover maps.

First, we included slope, based on a digital elevation model of Queensland, which had been correlated with vegetation clearing in many regions because steep terrain poses physical constraints that increase the costs and technical difficulty of clearing, cultivation, and infrastructure development (Hall et al., 2022; Joppa & Pfaff, 2009; Teixeira et al., 2009). Second, we included soil type, because soil type can influence the suitability of the land for agriculture, and therefore the decision to clear vegetation. A study in the Brigalow Belt showed that the suitability of the soil for agriculture was the most important variable explaining the proportion of native vegetation retained at the property scale (Seabrook et al., 2007). Third, we included regional ecosystem threat status (a vegetation classification system in Queensland); regional ecosystems that were already classified as endangered were likely to have been preferentially cleared in the past, and so the land may have been highly suitable for agriculture (Simmons et al., 2018).

Next, we included distance to roads, distance to waterways, and distance to nearest remnant vegetation, which were all included in a vegetation change model developed for the Brigalow Belt by Sonter et al. (2017). These three variables influenced land accessibility, the cost of transporting goods to markets, and the potential for proximate infrastructure development, and thus affected land clearing decisions (Busch & Ferretti‐Gallon, 2017; Joppa & Pfaff, 2009). Property size was included because smaller properties may have been more likely to be cleared to provide satisfactory income from agriculture production (Seabrook et al., 2007) and tenure, because this can determine relevant legislation that may have influenced clearing decisions (Evans, 2016).

Finally, we included the age of the vegetation because we hypothesized that this is an important determinant influencing a land manager's decision to clear vegetation or not for policy reasons (regrowth vegetation older than 15 years can, in some cases, become regulated again under Queensland law) and practical reasons (tree canopy cover increases with age, and at a certain threshold, this may impact livestock grazing productivity). Research in other parts of the world has shown that young forests may be preferentially cleared (Nunes et al., 2020; Reid et al., 2019). Most of our datasets came from the Queensland Government's Queensland Spatial Catalogue, which hosts freely accessible datasets, except for our soil data, which are freely available from CSIRO, Australia's national science agency.

Three variables (including distance to nearest road, distance to nearest watercourse, and property size) were categorized differently among the three bioregions. This was necessary because of the differences in size and spatial attributes of each bioregion. For example, Southeast Queensland is much smaller than the Brigalow Belt and has a far higher density of roads. Thus, there were very few, if any, pixels of regrowth that were further than 10 km from the nearest road in Southeast Queensland, unlike in the Brigalow Belt.

In Step 3, we used the weights of evidence approach in Dinamica EGO 7 (Teixeira et al., 2009) to investigate how spatial variables influenced vegetation change dynamics and quantified their influence on the transition of regrowth vegetation to cleared land from 2018 to 2022. The weights of evidence approach determine the relative contributions (i.e., effect) that each variable has had on historic vegetation change. More positive weights of evidence values indicated a stronger association with a transition from regrowth vegetation to cleared land, whereas more negative values implied a stronger association with no transition (i.e., a pixel of regrowth remains unchanged). A value of 0 meant the variable had no influence on the transition of regrowth to cleared land. The only assumption of the weights of evidence approach is that all variables must be spatially independent. Thus, we confirmed all variables had a Crammer coefficient of <0.50 (a threshold that suggests they were spatially independent) (Molin et al., 2017), before continuing with the analysis.

In Step 4, we used Dinamica EGO, a landscape dynamics cellular automata model to predict transitions from regrowth vegetation to cleared land. Our model used the weights of evidence coefficients calibrated from 2018 to 2022, the 2022 land‐cover map as our baseline map on which to model transitions through time, and the spatial distribution of the variables discussed above to produce a transition probability map. A transition probability map showed the most likely areas for a transition between land‐cover types in future, based on all spatial variables (Molin et al., 2017). This step produced a transition probability map for each of the 4‐year time steps, for each bioregion (five time steps and three bioregions; 15 transition probability maps in total). This model was not validated; for an explanation, see “Sensitivity Analyses” below.

In Step 5, we multiplied transition probability by regrowth clearing rates. Each pixel of the transition probability map had a score from 0 to 1, with 1 being the highest probability of a transition from regrowth to cleared land. For each pixel, we multiplied the 0–1 score (i.e., the probability of clearing based on the spatial distribution of variables discussed above) by the regrowth clearing rate (average clearing rate from 2018 to 2022; specific to each bioregion) to get an averted loss score. At this stage, we combined the three bioregions to obtain a map showing the averted loss scores for the whole study region, over each 4‐year time step. Finally, we converted to relative averted loss scores, by dividing all pixels by the maximum averted loss score observed throughout the whole study region. The relative averted loss scores were multiplied by the relative combined species habitat scores in Step 12 and defined in Step 11.

We decreased the regrowth clearing rate for each 4‐year time step to reflect the fact that a portion of the clearing that would be allocated to young regrowth was no longer present in the landscape. This is because we did not simulate the appearance of new regrowth in the landscape, the locations of which were highly uncertain. We did this by calculating the average clearing rate of regrowth in each age class from 2018 to 2022 and then decreased the regrowth clearing rate by the relevant amount (Appendix S1).

Estimating value of regrowth habitat for threatened species

In Step 6, we created threatened species habitat regrowth maps. Although many threatened species are likely to be affected by regrowth clearing, we have focused on 29 threatened species that were highly likely to be affected by regrowth clearing (based on the fraction of their likely‐to‐occur distribution overlapping with regrowth [see below]) and had undergone expert elicitation to determine the minimum age at which they could use regrowth and the value that regrowth vegetation provides relative to remnant vegetation (see Thomas et al. [2025] for further methods on species selection and expert elicitation).

We then mapped potential habitat for each threatened species in regrowth throughout the study region. With the Australian Government's Species of National Environmental Significance (SNES) public grids (1‐ to 10‐km2 resolution), which contains species distribution maps for all nationally listed threatened species, we selected the likely‐to‐occur distribution layer (Australian Government Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water, 2024) and overlaid each product to a 2022 map of woody regrowth vegetation in the study region (Queensland Government Department of Environment, Science and Innovation, 2024c; Queensland Government Department of Resources, 2022).

In Steps 7 and 8, we determined regrowth vegetation age. We estimated the age of every pixel of regrowth within each species' mapped potential habitat for 2022 and then for each year that we had a transition probability map available (2026, 2030, 2034, 2038, and 2042). To estimate regrowth age at 2022, we used the time since disturbance dataset published annually by the Queensland Government Department of Environment, Science and Innovation (2024d). This comprises a 10‐m raster that can be used to estimate the number of years since disturbance for each pixel (which can include anthropogenic clearing, but also natural disturbance like drought or bushfires) for all woody vegetation in Queensland. This dataset only provided a record of woody vegetation disturbance over 32 years, whereas a value of 33 suggested the regrowth was at least 32 years old or more, with no disturbance detected in the past 32 years.

For all regrowth that had a value of 33 in the SLATS dataset, we estimated the actual age from a 2010 global forest age dataset at 1‐km resolution, which used a machine learning approach to estimate forest age based on forest inventory, biomass, and climate data (Besnard et al., 2021). Although some forest may have been cleared since 2010, this did not affect our approach because we only used the 2010 dataset to refine pixels that had already been classified as at least 32 years old in the SLATS dataset. First, we added 12 years to each pixel in the global forest age dataset to estimate the vegetation age at 2022. Then, we substituted every value of 33 for the value from the global forest age dataset, unless the global forest age dataset had a null value, in which case we retained a value of 33. For any disagreement where SLATS had a value of 33 and the global dataset age was estimated at <33 years, we retained the pixel at 33 because the SLATS data are more accurate at the local scale (10‐m vs. 1‐km resolution). We also applied a maximum threshold of 70 years of age, which is approximately when extensive mechanized clearing began in the study region (McAlpine & Seabrook, 2010). Finally, because we were interested in the benefit to species over multiple time steps, we created a final SLATS‐updated age dataset for 2026, 2030, 2034, 2038, and 2042 by adding the correct number of years to each pixel (i.e., 4, then 8, then 12 years, etc.).

In Step 9, we estimated relative habitat value for threatened species. For every species, across all time steps, we then reclassified each pixel to an individual species habitat score from 0 to 1, depending on the age of the vegetation and the relative value compared to remnant habitat that regrowth of that age would provide to the species (Appendix S2). These data were provided by experts during a formal expert elicitation exercise (Thomas et al., 2025). Experts estimated the minimum age of regrowth vegetation that a species could use as habitat (therefore any vegetation younger than this would provide no value), plus the age of regrowth vegetation that would provide 50% value and 75% value relative to a patch of high‐quality remnant habitat for each species. We assumed a linear increase from 0% to 50% value and another linear increase from 50% to 75% relative value to interpolate the relative value for every age of regrowth vegetation. For most species, we then extrapolated the second linear regression to estimate the vegetation age that provided 75–100% relative value. However, for species that rely on tree hollows (greater glider [Petauroides volans], yellow‐bellied glider [Petaurus australis australis], spot‐tailed quoll [Dasyurus maculatus], Corben's long‐eared bat [Nyctophilus corbeni], Coxen's fig‐parrot [Cyclopsitta diophthalma coxeni], south‐east glossy black‐cockatoo [Calyptorhynchus lathami lathami], and swift parrot [Lathamus discolor]), we capped the maximum relative value of regrowth at 75% because it is likely that tree hollows take much longer to form than the age that regrowth would reach within our study's time horizon.

In Steps 10 and 11, we combined threatened species values into a single metric. We created a raster that combined the individual species habitat scores for all species to generate a combined species habitat score. We did this by summing all individual species habitat scores for every species that occurred in a given pixel. We repeated this three times, each with different weightings for species. First, all species were weighted equally. Second, species were weighted by national threat status, with critically endangered species weighted highest (0.75), followed by endangered species (0.5) and then vulnerable species (0.25). Third, species were weighted by the proportion of their SNES likely to occur distribution layer occurring within regrowth vegetation in Queensland, where species with the highest proportion in regrowth were weighted the highest (>15%, 0.75), followed by species that had >10% proportion in regrowth (0.5), with all remaining species (5–10%) weighted the lowest (0.25). Finally, we converted these combined species habitat scores to relative scores of 0–1 by dividing by the maximum summed score for each weighting type. These relative combined species habitat scores will be combined with the relative averted loss scores in the next step.

In Step 12, we combined averted loss potential and threatened species habitat value. We multiplied the relative combined species habitat scores by the relative averted loss scores for each of the 4‐year time steps. This provided a continuous raster of the relative expected benefit to threatened species of retaining regrowth over each of the relevant time steps, under the three weighting scenarios.

In Steps 13 and 14, we then applied a discount rate of 5% to the expected benefit that accrued with each time step, with the present value formula: PV=FV11+rn where PV is the present value, FV is the future value (unadjusted expected benefit), r is the discount rate (5%), and n represents the number of years (4 after the first time step, 8 after the second time step, etc.). We expressed the expected benefit rasters in present value terms because a given benefit that occurs far in the future is less valuable than the same benefit received today (Frederick et al., 2002). We chose a discount rate of 5%, following guidelines published by the New South Wales government for conducting cost–benefit analyses for conservation (New South Wales Government Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water, 2024). Finally, we summed all of the discounted expected benefit rasters (one for each time step, 5 in total) and converted to a relative 0–1 score by dividing by the maximum summed score. Thus, each pixel represented the relative benefit of retaining regrowth vegetation over the 20‐year time frame, discounted to present value, and it was this final score that was used in all remaining mapping products and discussed throughout the results.

Sensitivity analyses

The most suitable data for calibrating the vegetation change model were available only from 2018 to 2022. First, this was because the Queensland Government employed an improved methodology for detecting and mapping changes to woody vegetation and introduced a dataset that estimated the time since vegetation had been disturbed, both from 2018. Second, no publicly available data existed beyond 2022. We hypothesized that the time since regrowth vegetation had been disturbed (e.g., what we assumed to be the vegetation age) would be an important influence on the decision to clear and thus restricted our calibration period to this time frame. Thus, our model was not validated with new data.

We tested whether our results were likely to be strongly influenced by peculiarities of the data by creating a similar model that could be validated and repeating the analyses with the validated model (Appendix S3). This validated model had a calibration period of 2014–2018. Here, we could not include regrowth age as a predictor variable because these data were not available for that period. However, all other predictors were the same. We validated the model by comparing its simulated change in vegetation cover with observed change between 2018 and 2022 with the reciprocal comparison metric (Soares‐Filho et al., 2013) (Appendix S3). The model performed similarly to other vegetation change models developed for the region (Sonter et al., 2020), which gave us confidence in our use of the unvalidated model.

We estimated the value of regrowth habitat for each species with the SLATS‐original age dataset rather than the SLATS‐updated age dataset. The SLATS‐original age dataset was a more conservative estimate of regrowth age because we left any regrowth with a value of 33 as 33 years old, giving a maximum age of regrowth of 53 years at the end of our simulation in 2042. We then repeated the analyses to determine whether the expected benefit distribution differed between the two age datasets (Appendix S4).

RESULTS

Regrowth clearing in Queensland

Of the three bioregions in which most of Australia's regrowth vegetation occurs, the Mulga Lands bioregion had the greatest potential for averting regrowth clearing (in other words, its simulated counterfactual regrowth clearing rates were highest), closely followed by the Brigalow Belt (Figure 3).

Locations of regrowth vegetation that achieves the greatest potential for averting clearing if retained (above the 90th percentile) and the greatest threatened species habitat value when all species are weighted equally over the 20‐year deforestation simulation period (2022–2042) (black rectangle, ∼150 ha that overlapped the greatest potential for averted clearing and the greatest threatened species habitat value).

Spatial predictors of regrowth clearing were similar among the three bioregions, although the spatial extent and distribution of regrowth differed (Appendix S5). We found clearing was more likely to occur when regrowth was younger than 30 years, contained threatened ecosystem types (listed as endangered and of concern), and occurred at greater distances from remnant vegetation. We also found clearing to be less likely on steeper slopes and when closer than 100 m to waterways. We found no consistent effects related to soil type or land tenure on regrowth clearing.

Regrowth habitat value for threatened species

We calculated the relative value that regrowth provided to a selection of threatened species, based on habitat mapping, regrowth vegetation age, and elicited knowledge on each species ecology (see Steps 6–1 in Figure 2 and “Methods”), over the 20 years of the deforestation simulation. Regrowth vegetation in Southeast Queensland had the greatest estimated threatened species habitat value in 2042 (i.e., at the end of the deforestation simulation), particularly in the southern part of the bioregion, due in part to the high number of threatened species in this region (Figure 3). The southeastern part of the Brigalow Belt had the next greatest potential value, followed by much of the remainder of the Brigalow Belt. Mulga Lands and the far western and northern part of the Brigalow Belt had the lowest value when considering habitat value for threatened species.

Over the 20 years of the deforestation simulation, the value of regrowth changed as it aged and became suitable for more species. Considering individual species, the greater glider (Petauroides volans) had the greatest increase in average habitat value from 2022 to 2042: 26% to 53%. The brush‐tailed rock wallaby (Petrogale penicillata) had the smallest increase in average habitat value from 2022 to 2042: 93% to 96%. The average for all species was a 12% increase in habitat value from 2022 to 2042 (Appendix S4).

Overall expected benefit of retaining regrowth vegetation

We produced an expected benefit map for retaining regrowth, based on the potential for averting regrowth clearing and its habitat value for threatened species, weighting all species equally. The pixels with the greatest benefit (above the 90th percentile) were mainly located in the Brigalow Belt (93%), followed by Mulga Lands (7%) and Southeast Queensland (<0.1%; Figure 4a). More areas from Southeast Queensland and Mulga Lands were selected when considering the greatest benefit locations above the 80th percentile and 70th percentile. For example, above the 70th percentile, 13% of the greatest benefit areas occurred in the Mulga Lands and 3% occurred in Southeast Queensland.

Regrowth areas in a 20‐year deforestation simulation (2022–2042) that provide the greatest benefit for averted clearing and threatened species habitat value (a) above the 90th percentile (black), the 80th percentile (red), and the 70th percentile (blue) (all species weighted equally) and (b) above the 90th percentile selected with one weighting type (red) (25% of total), with two of the different weighting types (blue) (19% of total), and with all three of the weighting types (black) (56% of total).

The distribution of locations with the greatest expected benefit depended on how we weighted the threatened species (Figure 4b). The largest difference was observed when weighting species by the proportion of their habitat that occurred in regrowth forest and woodland areas. For example, 7% of the greatest benefit areas occurred in Southeast Queensland when applying this weighting type, compared with <1% for the other two weighting types.

Sensitivity

We repeated the analysis with a more conservative vegetation age dataset (i.e., the SLATS‐Original Dataset, see “Methods”) when estimating threatened species habitat value. This had very little impact on the distribution of the greatest benefit areas for retaining regrowth (Appendix S4). With the SLATS‐Updated Age Dataset, 93.2% of the greatest benefit areas occurred in the Brigalow Belt, whereas the SLATS‐Original Dataset (i.e., more conservative) decreased this proportion to 92.8%.

DISCUSSION

We demonstrated a prospective counterfactual approach that can be used to guide the design and estimate the additional benefits of conservation interventions. We illustrated this approach with a case study that maps the benefit of retaining regrowth, accounting for both averted loss of regrowth clearing and its relative habitat value for 29 threatened species. In our study region, the Mulga Lands had the greatest expected benefit if only the extent of avoided regrowth clearing was considered, whereas regrowth in Southeast Queensland had the highest value for threatened species. When considering the overall additional benefit for threatened species of retaining regrowth, most of the areas with the greatest benefit occurred in the Brigalow Belt. Retaining regrowth vegetation is likely an important strategy to conserve threatened species in heavily cleared landscapes (Bruton & McAlpine, 2014), and it is most effective when conservation interventions are carefully targeted to retain areas likely to yield the greatest benefit, beyond what would have been expected otherwise.

Our results illustrate that a prospective counterfactual approach to investigating the effects of an intervention can substantially change the location for investments. When only accounting for threatened species habitat value, the highest benefit areas occurred largely in Southeast Queensland (Figure 3). Southeast Queensland is the most urbanized of the three bioregions and has the highest population density. Consequently, many vegetation types have been heavily cleared and fragmented, which could explain the high density of both threatened plant and animal species in the region (Ives et al., 2016). Sampling bias may also have contributed because this bioregion has been more heavily surveyed and so contains species for which the value of regrowth may be better understood (Oliveira et al., 2016).

We found that the areas with the greatest expected benefit changed when we accounted for the averted risk of regrowth clearing (Figure 3). Livestock production was the dominant land use in the Mulga Lands and Brigalow Belt, and hence there was a drive to clear regrowth to maintain or increase agricultural production. In the Brazilian Cerrado, a spatial prioritization to evaluate future conservation investment showed a similar pattern (Monteiro et al., 2020). When aiming to maximize conservation impact (i.e., targeting areas at high risk of vegetation removal), areas in the north of the Cerrado were selected, where more native vegetation remains intact and the opportunity for development is the highest. When aiming to maximize species representation, areas in the east of the Cerrado were selected, areas that have been subject to higher human pressure in the past and therefore have a higher density of threatened species, but less opportunity for clearing (Monteiro et al., 2020). Overall, prospective counterfactual approaches have not been widely used in the conservation sector to guide the development of policies, strategies, and plans, although our results, and these examples, illustrate their value. We add to calls for this approach to be used more widely when designing conservation interventions (Grace et al., 2021; Newburn et al., 2005; Sacre et al., 2020).

Few studies have attempted to estimate benefit as we have here, by accounting explicitly for additionality, and accounting for species‐specific responses to habitat characteristics (in our case, regrowth vegetation age). There are many ways to estimate the benefit of a conservation intervention for species. Most studies use simplified proxies that account for the amount or type of habitat, or species distributions (Fajardo et al., 2023; Venter et al., 2014; Zeng et al., 2022). Our research demonstrates a novel way to estimate benefit by applying weighted species‐specific habitat values to explicitly account for species‐specific responses to vegetation age, which may be particularly useful when the conservation intervention in question applies to regrowth vegetation. This is only possible in studies where detailed information on the threatened species and vegetation age is available.

We applied different weighting strategies to the 29 threatened species to investigate how this influenced benefit estimates and their spatial distribution. Although 56% of the greatest benefit areas (above the 90th percentile) were consistent among the three weighting types, there were also noticeable spatial differences (Figure 4); when weighting species by their proportion of habitat occurring in regrowth, more of the greatest benefit areas occurred in Southeast Queensland compared to the other two weighting types. This is because several of the species that have high proportions of their mapped habitat distribution in regrowth are mainly restricted to Southeast Queensland, at least within our study area (e.g., regent honeyeater [Anthochaera Phrygia] and swift parrot). We also explored weighting the species by threatened status. We chose this method because it is a common and well‐documented approach (Arponen et al., 2005; Díaz et al., 2020). However, it is often more cost‐effective and feasible to conserve a species listed as vulnerable compared to a species with a higher threat status (Wilson et al., 2011). Similarly, vulnerable species are often held to a less stringent standard in environmental impact assessment (or in some cases, no statutory protection) and more likely to be uplisted to a higher threat status than downlisted (Simmonds et al., 2020). Thus, careful consideration of the intended outcomes of the conservation intervention is needed before weighting by threat status.

We applied our case study to regrowth forest and woodland ecosystems in Queensland because this system is an excellent candidate for targeted conservation investment that would likely lead to demonstrable additionality if well‐designed. We found that most age classes of regrowth vegetation, except the oldest (over 29 years), were positively associated with regrowth clearing (Appendix S5). This is a notable finding because most previous studies did not consider vegetation age as a predictor of clearing risk, either because they examined only remnant clearing or because no suitable vegetation age data existed (Navarro Rau et al., 2023; Negret et al., 2019; Teixeira et al., 2009). We therefore suggest that regrowth vegetation age is an important consideration when prioritizing regrowth retention, particularly given that younger vegetation is likely at higher risk of clearing.

In addition, our results suggest that regrowth vegetation in more threatened ecosystems was at higher risk of reclearing. This is likely because these ecosystems occur on land that is relatively productive for agriculture and have therefore been preferentially cleared in the past, leading to regrowth, and because the productivity drivers of that clearing remain (Rhodes et al., 2017; Simmonds et al., 2017). Thus, vegetation age or ecosystem threat status (or, ecosystem type, if data are not available on threat status) are sensible ways to predict additionality, particularly in a study region where high‐quality data exist for both.

Targeting investment at the most at‐risk vegetation can bring the largest conservation benefit but can be less socially equitable (Haas et al., 2019; Pascual et al., 2014). For example, Alix‐Garcia et al. (2008) simulated two payments scenarios for a planned incentive scheme designed to reduce deforestation in Mexico; they found that a payment incorporating deforestation risk provided three times the expected benefit compared to a payment that did not incorporate risk, for the same cost, but the flat payment per hectare was more equitable than the risk‐weighted payments (Alix‐Garcia et al., 2008). In our study region, equity concerns could arise from the exclusive focus of regrowth vegetation. This benefits landowners who have cleared more vegetation in the past. Careful consideration should be given to potential methods that can allow landowners to also benefit from retaining remnant vegetation in high condition.

Land‐use decisions are a combination of biophysical attributes and social factors such as values, education, motivations, and opinions (Seabrook et al., 2008; Simmons et al., 2018; Simmons et al., 2020). Simmons et al. (2018) point out that studies investigating state‐ or national‐level drivers of deforestation can produce generalized patterns that may not adequately capture or identify regional landholders' behaviors and potential motivations. Regrowth vegetation that is at a relatively higher risk of clearing based on biogeographical features (productive soil, minimal slope, etc.) may never be cleared if the property owner had strong motivation to retain high tree cover, for example. A study in the Brigalow Belt showed that landowners who valued the attractiveness of native vegetation had higher tree cover on their properties (Seabrook et al., 2008). In another example, northern Australian farmers with stronger conservation and lifestyle motivations were more likely to adopt conservation practices than those with stronger economic, financial, or social motivation (Greiner & Gregg, 2011). Thus, sufficient uptake of conservation interventions that would lead to meaningful changes in regrowth retention requires careful consideration of the social dimensions of vegetation clearing decisions. Ideally, we would have considered social dimensions in our study. However, no suitable spatially explicit database on social dimensions exists at the landscape scale that our study required (see Seabrook et al. [2008] for a finer‐scale study of 30 properties conducted in the Brigalow Belt).

Economic drivers contribute to landowner decisions to clear vegetation (Busch & Ferretti‐Gallon, 2017). High agricultural commodity prices could allow farmers to generate revenue and thus have the capacity to expand production by clearing vegetation (Heagney et al., 2021). Alternatively, when expected revenue is low, increased vegetation clearing could allow farmers to generate extra production and maintain income (Heagney et al., 2021). Economic drivers in turn are influenced by current climatic and market conditions (Chatzopoulos et al., 2020). A study conducted in New South Wales suggested that farmers cleared land when agricultural market conditions were favorable, and that economic drivers had a larger influence on clearing that regulatory policy (Heagney et al., 2021). At a global scale, a meta‐analysis from 2017 showed that forests were more likely to be cleared when the economic returns from agriculture were higher. This was often due to favorable climactic conditions, lower cost of vegetation clearing, or lower cost of transporting commodities to markets (Busch & Ferretti‐Gallon, 2017). Our analytical framework captures some of these factors by proxy (Table 1), although we acknowledge that others, including temporally fluctuating and unpredictable commodity prices, are more challenging to reflect in our analytical approach to clearing.

We encountered several limitations, with the first being the lack of long‐term high‐quality data on regrowth clearing. Hence, the training period for the vegetation change model only included a 4‐year time frame, which may have reduced our ability to reliably determine associations between regrowth clearing and spatial predictors. However, we believe it was a necessary trade‐off, due to the differences in data methodology and quality before 2018 (Queensland Government Department of Environment, Science and Innovation, 2024a). Second, our results applied to a focal group of just 29 threatened species. We chose these 29 species based on their susceptibility to regrowth habitat clearing (Thomas et al., 2025). However, it would be helpful to include other threatened species that may be affected by regrowth clearing. We originally attempted to include a selection of invertebrates in this analysis; however, there were too few experts available to provide robust estimates of these species' use of regrowth (Thomas et al., 2025). Finally, our highest‐benefit areas are not meant to be imposed on policymakers, but rather to guide thinking and decision‐making when planning conservation intervention in the region. Depending on the objectives of specific intervention, it may be necessary to choose a different focal group of species (e.g., threatened plants, threatened invertebrates, or even common species) and to thoroughly consider the drivers underlying land‐use decisions.

In conclusion, our results showed the importance of a prospective counterfactual approach to designing conservation interventions. The importance of counterfactual approaches in conservation has long been recognized (Ferraro & Pattanayak, 2006). However, this approach remains more commonly used for retrospective evaluations. Our findings thus contribute to a growing literature on prospective approaches, albeit with the novel method of incorporating a spatial analysis underpinned by species‐specific ecological information. We applied our case study to three bioregions in Queensland, Australia, that have globally significant land‐clearing rates and high potential for natural regeneration. Carefully planned and designed conservation interventions will be an important aspect of nature recovery in this and other heavily cleared and modified landscapes.
