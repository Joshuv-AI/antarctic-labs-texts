Prevalence and correlates of emotion dysregulation among children and adolescents in Lebanon: results from a National Survey
Authors: Evelyne Baroud, Loay Alrojolah, Wael Shamseddeen, Lilian A. Ghandour, Martine Elbejjani, Marc Barakat, Leyla Akoury Dirani, Fadi T. Maalouf
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundData on Emotion Dysregulation among youth in Lebanon is scarce. Given that Emotion Dysregulation is associated with psychopathology and has a negative impact on youth outcomes, there is a need to better understand the prevalence, clinical and demographic correlates of Emotion Dysregulation.MethodsThis study is a secondary analysis of the Psychopathology in Children and Adolescents in Lebanon Study which recruited 1517 youth and one parent from a nationally representative sample. Participants completed The Screen for Child Anxiety Related Emotional Disorders, Mood and Feelings Questionnaire, Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire, Peer Relations Questionnaire, Conflict Behavior Questionnaire (CBQ).Parents completed a sociodemographic questionnaire and the General Health Questionnaire (GHQ). The Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire-Dysregulation Profile was used to measure Emotion Dysregulation.ResultsThe prevalence of Emotion Dysregulation was 11.26% among children aged 5 to < 8 years, 9.40% among children aged 8 to < 12 years and 6.60% among those older than 12 years. Emotion Dysregulation was associated with psychopathology across age groups and a number of correlates were identified. In the final regression models, among children aged 5 to < 8 years; Emotion Dysregulation was associated with lower odds of father employment (OR = 0.235;CI[0.078,0.704]). For those aged 8 to < 12 years old, Emotion Dysregulation was associated with acceptable school performance (OR = 2.246,CI[1.209,4.173]) as opposed to a good school performance, having a chronic physical illness (OR = 2.962; CI [1.495,5.867)], and higher parental GHQ scores (OR = 1.034;CI [1.010,10.58)] indicating worse parental mental health. Adolescents aged 12 years and older who screened positive for Emotion Dysregulation were more likely to be younger (OR = 0.688, CI[0.527,0.899]), be a bully (OR = 2.467;CI[1.036,5.875] and to have higher parental CBQ scores (OR = 1.092;CI [1.011,1.180]), indicating worse parent-child conflict. Youth who screened positive for emotion dysregulation were more likely to seek psychiatric care than their counterparts who screen negative for emotion dysregulation.ConclusionAge, school performance, parental mental health, parental conflict, chronic physical illness and bullying emerge as important correlates of Emotion Dysregulation. Findings add to the limited data on Emotion Dysregulation in Lebanon and the region and are a first step towards developing intervention and prevention strategies.

Introduction

Emotion regulation (ER) is the capacity to recognize, appraise, adjust, and manage emotions in a socially suitable way, in order to preserve adaptive functioning [1, 2]. In children, it is a process that depends on a number of elements, including children’s social interactions with environment (e.g., peers, parents), their level of development and neuro-biological factors [3, 4]. Emotion dysregulation (ED) is conceptualized as a transdiagnostic construct, and is characterized by an inability to modulate the intensity and quality of emotions [5]. ED is estimated to affect between 3 and 20% of children/adolescents [6, 7] and is associated with psychiatric disorders and symptoms, including Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD), Oppositional Defiant Disorder, Mood Disorders, Suicidality, Personality Disorders and Self-Injurious behaviors [5, 6]. More specifically, ED affects up to a third of children/adolescents who seek clinical mental health care [8] and is associated with the subsequent development of adult psychopathology [9]. Manifestations of ED (e.g. anger outbursts, aggression, irritability, impulsivity, low tolerance to frustration, negative mood and suicidality) are common among youth with psychiatric disorders and carry high morbidity [10–12]. Indeed, severe tantrums/outbursts predict continuing mood/behavior problems into adulthood, and deserve early intervention [4].

The link between ED and psychopathology is complex, ED appears to be a mediator and a predictor of outcomes in psychiatric disorders among children/adolescents [5]. In studies of children with ADHD, ED was found to be a mediator between the disorder and social skills difficulties [13]. ED is also recognized as a mediator of the relationship between substance use and substance use disorders (SUDs) [14–16]. In fact, youth with ED begin substance misuse earlier, and evolve into SUDs more quickly than those without ED [5]. Longitudinal studies indicate that low ER skills predict early adolescent smoking, and forecast a switch to regular smoking behaviors [15], with higher levels of ED predicting greater risks of smoking [17]. In depression, ED may contribute to increased reactivity to social difficulties [18]. Importantly, studies have also implicated ED as a risk factor for suicidal ideations (SI) and attempts in children/adolescents [19–21]. For example, among adolescents who experience intense and overwhelming emotions, suicidal thoughts may emerge as a way to decrease the intensity of these negative emotions and to lessen the impact of ED [22]. In fact, neural circuits specifically involved in ER also play a role in the emergence of SI [23, 24], while the successful use of suitable coping strategies may prevent the occurrence of SI among youth [23–25]. Lastly, inclination to attempting suicide may be exacerbated by behavior dysregulation and impulsivity [26].

In contrast to most Western countries, the Eastern Mediterranean region has been an area of intermittent wars and political conflict for years [27]. Living in conflict ridden areas and exposure to war, is associated with a range of mental-health sequalae, especially for children, and impacts their exposure to some developmental opportunities [28]. Lebanon, a low-middle income country, has a history of civil war, and more recent political uprisings and economic instability [29]. Culture in the Eastern Mediterranean region is also different compared to the West; for example individualism is prevalent in Western societies, while collectivism, is prevalent Eastern ones [30]. Culture plays a role in the development of ER, and deficits in ER may present differently in disctint cultures [31]. Research has shown that children raised in distinctive cultures show different levels of emotional expressiveness [32, 33]; and cultural values shape how parents perceive and react to children’s emotional reactivity, which in turn influences the development of self-regulation strategies [7]. As an example; in interdependent cultures (where cultural principles emphasize collaboration, suppressing anger, and impulse control) children regulate emotions differently than those raised in more independent cultures, where parents generally foster regulation strategies which value individuality and autonomy [34].

Data on ED in children/adolescents in the Eastern Mediterranean region is rare. In Lebanon, a recent national study showed a high prevalence of psychiatric symptoms among children/adolescents and an alarming treatment gap [35]. Specifically, 32% of children/adolescents screened positive for at least one psychiatric disorder [35] To the best of our knowledge, no studies have explored the topic of ED among children/adolescents in Lebanon, and very few have looked at the prevalence of ED in national samples.

This paper adds to the scant literature on ED among youth in the region by reporting on the prevalence, comorbidities, clinical and sociodemographic correlates of ED in a nationally representative sample of children/adolescents. Given the association between ED and psychiatric disorders, and the negative impact of ED on outcomes in youth, this increased understanding of the risk and protective factors associated with ED would allow for more contextualized and evidence informed intervention and prevention strategies. Our findings further contribute to the global evidence with data from a different context and culture of reporting ED, and from the perspective of a conflict-affected area.

Methods

A nationally representative household sample of noninstitutionalized Arabic speaking children and adolescents were recruited between 2017 and 2018 using probability sampling technique covering the country’s 26 districts as part of the Psychopathology in Children and Adolescents in Lebanon Study. In total, 1517 participants between the ages of 5–17 years 11 months and one parent/legal guardian were recruited. The sample included 234 children aged 5–7 years 11 months old, 724 children aged 8–11 years 11 months and 559 adolescents aged 12–17 years 11 months [35]. Households were eligible if they included at least one Arabic speaking Lebanese child/adolescent aged 5–17 years 11 months and a parent/legal guardian. For children aged 5–8 years, only a parent was interviewed. For those older, the child/adolescent and a parent were interviewed separately. Data was collected via tablets by two staff; one working with the parent, one with the child. Data collectors were trained by the research team on the process of recruiting participants, obtaining informed consent, and collecting data for the present study. During the initial stages, a member of the research team shadowed and supervised the trained data collectors to ensure proper adherence to all procedures. Full methodology is described in the original manuscript [35].

The study was approved by the American University Institutional Review Board (Protocol number: SBS-2015–0120), in accordance with the Belmont report. Parents/legal guardians signed an informed consent and permission for their child/adolescent to participate, children/adolescents older than 8 years signed assent forms.

Instruments and measures

General information sheet

This sheet was completed by one parent/legal guardian and included basic demographic questions pertaining to family structure, family income and parent’s education, employments and questions regarding the child/adolescent’s academic performance, medical, developmental and psychiatric history.

The mood and feelings questionnaire (MFQ)-Parent and child version

The MFQ is a 33 item self- report measure of depressive symptoms among youth aged 8–18 years [36]. It has a parent and a child version. A validated Arabic version of the MFQ was used with established cutoff scores used to establish a positive screen [37]. A child was deemed to have screened positive for clinically significant depressive symptoms if the child or parent scores were above the cutoff scores of 26 or 22 respectively, which achieve sensitivities of 73.1% and 76.9% respectively [37]. The following items: “thought family would be better off without self,” “thought about death or dying,” “thought life was not worth living,” and “thought about killing self”; were used to generate a total score for SI. The highest scores from either the child or parent reports for the items were chosen and combined to generate a total suicide score. A cutoff score of two was used to establish SI. This cutoff score showed a sensitivity of 57%, 75% specificity, a Positive Predictive Value of 10%, and an Negative Predictive Value of 97% for current SI in a sample from Wales [38].

The strengths and difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ)-Parent and child version

The SDQ is a behavioral screening questionnaire comprised of a 25-item, Likert scale, and is used to screen for emotional, conduct, and hyperactivity difficulties in children aged 5–7 years and 11 months and for conduct and hyperactivity difficulties in children/adolescents between the ages of 8 years and 17 years and 11 months [39]. A validated Arabic version of the SDQ was used [40]. SDQ also measures the subjective impact of the problem, impairment and distress and yields scores on Conduct Problems, Inattention-Hyperactivity, Emotional Symptoms, Peer Problems, Prosocial behavior, and Total Difficulties. A child/adolescent can be considered having screened positive for an emotional disorder, hyperkinetic disorder, or conduct disorder if he/she had an abnormal score on the corresponding scales (either parent or child reported) along with an abnormal impact score as defined by the established cut-of points.

The SDQ - Dysregulation profile (SDQ-DP) is a subscale that consists of 5 items from the parent-rated SDQ. Two from the SDQ emotional symptoms subscale (13: often unhappy, down-hearted or tearful; 8: many worries, often seems worried), 2 from the conduct problems subscale (12: often fights with other children or bullies them; 22: steals from home, school or elsewhere), and 1 from the hyperactivity subscale (2: restless, overactive, cannot stay still for long). The cut-off point recommended to define the parent-rated SDQ-DP is ≥ 5 points (sensitivity = 94,6%, specificity = 80%, Cronbach’s alpha = 0.52) [41]. These five items reflect the mixed behavioral phenotype of anxious, depressive, aggressive and attention problems captured by the Child Behavior Checklist – Dysregulation Profile [41]. The SDQ-DP can be considered a helpful screening measure for severe emotional and behavioral dysregulation [41]. The validity of SDQ-DP for differentiating between individuals with and without dysregulation is established [41].

Screen for child anxiety related Emotional disorders (SCARED)-Parent and child version

The SCARED [42] is a self-report scale that screens for anxiety disorders in children/adolescents. It assesses for Panic Disorder, Generalized Anxiety Disorder, Separation Anxiety Disorder, Social Anxiety Disorder and Significant School Avoidance. Cut off scores previously established in a validated Arabic version were used [43]. A child was considered to have screened positive for an anxiety disorder if they scored 26 on the child version or the parent scored 24 on the parent version; the scores correspond to a sensitivity and specificity of 67% and 55% for SCARED-Parent version; 66% and 56% for SCARED-Child version.

Child revised impact of events scale (CRIES 8)

The Impact of Events Scale (IES) was originally developed to examine the re-experiencing of a traumatic event and of avoidance of that event and the feelings associated with it. The CRIES-8 has been used to screen for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in a general population [44]. The cutoff score for a positive screen is 17, and carries a sensitivity 0.94 and specificity of 0.59 [45, 46]. It was completed by children aged 8 and above. A translated Arabic version was used, as there are no validation studies for that tool in Arabic.

Substance use

Adolescents aged 12 years and above answered a questionnaire on substance use and its frequency. Parent habits of substance use as perceived by the child were also assessed. The questionnaire included alcohol, cannabis, psychoactive medications and other elicit substances. Given that no child reported any substance use other than alcohol, tobacco and waterpipe smoking, a positive screen on any of the aforementioned three constituted a positive screen for substance use.

Conflict behavior questionnaire (CBQ)

The Conflict Behavior Questionnaire (CBQ) [47] assesses conflict and interaction behaviors as perceived by parents (CBQ-P) and adolescents (CBQ-A) [48]. CBQ was completed by adolescents older than 12, describing their conflict with each parent and by the parent/legal guardian about his/her conflict with the adolescent. Higher scores indicate increased parent-child conflict.

The peer relations questionnaire (PRQ)

The PRQ [49] abbreviated version is a 12-item scale completed by children aged 8 and above which assesses peer relations. It has three subscales of 4 items each. The scale yields scores on three different subscales: victim subscale (PRQ-Victim), reflecting the degree to which the adolescent is bullied; bully subscale (PRQ-Bully), reflecting the degree to which the adolescent engages in bullying behavior; and prosocial behavior subscale (PRQ-Prosocial) [50]. An Arabic translation with good internal consistency (0·75 to 0·86) for the three subscales [51] was used. Participants who responded with “pretty often” or “often” on at least one item of the PRQ Victim subscale were categorized as “Victims of bullying”. A similar approach was used to identify “Bullies”. Participants were then categorized as bullies only, victims only or both victims and bullies [52].

The general health questionnaire (GHQ-28)

The General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-28) [53] is a shorter version of the original 60-item GHQ, which screens for the current mental health status of respondents (the parent/legal guardian) including symptoms and behaviors [54], higher scores indicate worse symptomatology [55]. The GHQ has good validity and is equally useful in developing countries as well as when translated to different languages [53].

Data analysis

Sociodemographic and other clinical variables were compared between children who screened positive for ED and those who did not. Mann Whitney U tests and Kruskal- Wallis tests were used to examine associations with non-normally distributed continuous variables, and Pearson’s Chi square test and Fisher’s exact were used to examine the associations with categorical variables. T-test was used for normally distributed variables. Bonferroni correction was done for pairwise comparisons. In all analyses, we reported the weighted prevalence to account for sampling effect. This was done based on estimates of the distribution of the Lebanese population across each Caza and governate as per the Central Administration of Statistics. To study the correlates of screening positive for ED across the two age groups [(8 years till < 12 years) and 12 years till < 18 years)], a multistep approach was done for each. First, for each of the two age groups, the bivariate association between the various sociodemographic variables and ED were examined, and second, those that were statistically significantly related to ED (p < 0·05) were kept and classified into themes [ “Sociodemographic characteristics (including age, gender, family structure, parental education and employment and family income)”; “Scholastic characteristics (including the child’s academic performance, repeating school grades and ever needing private tutoring or special accommodation at school)”;“Parent and Peer relationships (including bullying and victimization along with parental conflict)” and “clinical characteristics (including having a positive psychiatric family history, having a chronic physical illness and parental GHQ scores)”]. Finally, multivariate logistic regression models with ED (outcome) and the variables included in each of the thematic categories were run, and variables statistically significantly associated with ED (p < 0·05) were included in the final model, which included all factors from different thematic categories as well as adjusting for the presence of a positive screen for any psychiatric disorder. Significance was set at p < 0·005 to adjust for the multiple comparisons made.

Results

Children 5–7 years 11 months

Sociodemographic characteristics

Among the 232 children (5–7 years 11 months), almost half were males (n = 122; 52.06%) with an average age of 5.88 (SD = 0.83). 26 children (11.26%) screened positive for ED. Among those, the average age was 5.74 (SD = 0.81) and most were males (n = 16; 62.62%). Lower prevalence of unemployed fathers in the ED group was noted (77.94% vs. 94.18%; p = 0.003). No other significant differences between the two subgroups were noted.

Scholastic performance

No significant differences in scholastic performance were noted between those who screened positive for ED and those who did not.

Psychiatric and medical correlates

Higher Parent/legal guardian GHQ scores were noted in the ED group (24.38 vs. 20.77; p = 0.045). No other significant differences between the two subgroups were noted. Among those who screened positive for ED, one child screened positive for conduct disorder (3.85%). No significant differences existed in the prevalence of a positive psychiatric disorder between the two groups.

Treatment seeking attitude

Among those who screened positive for ED, 1 child (3.85%) sought mental health care.

Children 8–11 years 11 months

Sociodemographic characteristics and correlates

Among the 724 participants (8–11 years 11 months), there was an almost equal ratio of females (n = 368; 50.84%) to males with an average age of 9·30 (SD = 1·15). 68 (9.40%) participants screened positive for ED, with an average age of 9.26 (SD = 1.12), and a slightly higher prevalence of males (n = 36; 52.89%). Children who screened positive for ED were more likely to come from lower income households (63.33% vs. 46.70%; p = 0.01), to have unemployed mothers compared to those who screened negative for ED (86.43% vs. 71.87%) and less likely to come from a household with a total income of more than 2000$ (3.01% vs. 13.17%; p = 0.017).

Scholastic performance

Those who screened positive for ED were more likely to have acceptable (31.05% vs. 15.98%; p = 0.001) and sub-optimal (8.85% vs. 1.96%; p = 0.001) academic performance, to have repeated school grades (23.16% vs. 8.93%;p < 0.001), received home tutoring (21.72% vs. 10.14%;p = 0.003) and received accommodation at school (8.90% vs. 2.72%; p = 0.008). Those who screened negative for ED had a higher proportion of children/adolescents with an academic performance described as good by their parents (82.06% vs. 59.10%; p < 0.001).

Peer relations

Participants who screened positive for ED were more likely to be victims of bullying (17.55% vs. 9.75%; p = 0.043) and be both a victim and a bully (13.38% vs. 5.63%; p = 0.015).

Psychiatric and medical correlates

Most participants who screened positive for ED also had a positive screen for at least one psychiatric disorder (n = 41;60.29%). They were found to be more likely to screen positive for conduct disorder (16.23% vs. 0.62%; p < 0.001), hyperactivity disorder (14.79% vs. 0.61%; p < 0.001), major depressive disorder (22.10% vs. 2.89%;p < 0.001), anxiety disorder (54.00% vs. 20.11%;p < 0.001) compared to those who screened negative for ED. No differences in screening positive for PTSD were present between the two groups (17.29% vs. 16.21%;p = 0.752). Adolescents 12–17 years 11 months

Sociodemographic characteristics

Among the 559 adolescents, average age was 14.17 (SD = 1.68) with an almost equal ratio of females (n = 287; 51.36%) and males (n = 272; 48.64%). 37 adolescents (6.60%) screened positive for ED, they were significantly younger (13.64 years vs. 14.20 years; p = 0.031) and there was a slightly higher prevalence of ED in males (54.29% vs. 48.26%; p = 0.497). They were more likely to have a household income of less than 1000$ (83.41% vs. 38.47%; p < 0.001), less likely to have a household income of 1000–2000$ (8.23% vs. 41.25%; p < 0.001) and more likely to have fathers with an elementary education or less (43.01% vs. 23.61%; p < 0.008).

Scholastic performance

Adolescents who screened positive for ED were more likely to have sub-optimal (16.24% vs. 4.59%; p = 0.002) and acceptable school performance (51.31% vs. 21.73%; p < 0.001) and less likely to have good school performance, as reported by their parents (32.45% bs 73.68%; p < 0.001). They were more likely to have ever repeated a school grade (48.23% vs. 26.35%; p = 0.004), ever received tutoring at home (27.47% vs. 11.54%; p = 0.006) and to have ever received school accommodation (13.76% vs. 2.49%; p < 0.001).

Peer relations

Higher likelihood of being a bully (37.79% vs. 15.43%; p < 0.001) and higher parent-child conflict as reported by parents on the CBQ (8.26 vs. 4.34; p < 0.001) was observed among adolescents who screened positive for ED.

Psychiatric and medical correlates

Adolescents who screened positive for ED had higher rates of positive family history for psychiatric disorders (18.61% vs. 7.88%; p = 0.02) and higher parental GHQ scores (27.34 vs. 20.11; p < 0.001). Thirty-two (86.48%) adolescents who screened positive for ED also screened positive for at least one other psychiatric disorder. Those who had a positive screen for ED were also more likely to screen positive for conduct disorder (16.19% vs. 2.88%; p < 0.001), hyperactivity disorder (16.20% vs. 2.89%; p < 0.001), major depressive disorder (29.70% vs. 6.52%;p < 0.001), anxiety disorder (67.43% vs. 18.43%; p < 0.001), PTSD (46.09% VS 22.59%; P = 0.001).

Significantly higher rates of SI were observed in youth who screened positive for ED (35.34% vs. 12.84%;p < 0.001). After controlling for correlates of SI in a separate regression mode, ED was no longer significantly associated with SI in this group.

Treatment seeking attitude

Only 5 adolescents who screened positive for ED (13.72%) ever sought mental health care. Adolescents who screened positive for ED and at least one other psychiatric disorder were more likely to seek psychiatric care compared to those who screened negative for ED but screened positive for at least one psychiatric disorder (15.90% vs. 3.09%; p = 0.009).

Logistic regression model for correlates of screening positive for ED

In the final logistic regression model, and after controlling for comorbid psychiatric disorders, adolescents who screened positive for ED were more likely to be younger (OR = 0.688,CI[0.527,0.899]), be a bully (OR = 2.467;CI[1.036,5.875] and to have higher parental CBQ scores (OR = 1.092; CI [1.011,1.180]). Discussion

In this nationally representative sample of 1283 non-institutionalized children/adolescents, the prevalence of ED was 11.26% in children aged 5–7 years 11 months 9.40% in children aged 8–11 years 11 months and 6.60% in children/adolescents aged 12–17 years 11 months, as assessed by the SDQ-DP.

Very few studies have established prevalence rates for ED on a national level, likely due to its’ transdiagnostic nature and the variability across sample types (e.g. clinical vs. non-clinical). To our knowledge, this is the first study in Lebanon to examine prevalence of ED in a nationally representative sample and one of the very few to look at prevalence of ED in a national sample in the region and globally. In non-clinical samples, there is very limited data on numbers relating to ED. Studies reveal a wide range of estimates for prevalence of emotional and behavioral symptoms. For example, in the United States, emotional/behavioral symptoms are estimated to affect approximately 7% of children between the ages of 4–17 years old [56], while in Germany, one study estimated the prevalence of emotional and behavioral symptoms to be 16% for kindergarten aged children [57]. In clinical samples in the United states, dysregulation problems are estimated to affect between 26.0 and 30.5% of children [8, 58]. In Switzerland, in a sample of children in residential treatment facilities, the prevalence of ED symptoms among 10–14-year-old children is 11.1% for girls and 13.5% for boys [59]. In Denmark, ED was present in 62.3% of approximately 1000 referred to mental health services [60]. One study in Saudi Arabia used the SDQ and found that emotional and behavioral disorders may account for 20% of children between the ages of 4–17 years old [61]. These differences may be related to methodological differences, which include different study populations and different assessment instruments, among others.

Among teenagers, age was inversely correlated with ED. This finding is in line with the existing literature. New onset psychiatric symptoms in younger children may be expressed through nonspecific symptoms of ED rather than more diagnostic specific symptom constellations. Moreover, younger children do not have the necessary cognitive development that would allow them to become aware of and label an emotion, have not yet developed problem-solving skills, and are less able to attend to and reflect on internal emotional states [62]. On the other hand, adolescents can rely increasingly on more complex strategies for coping with emotions and are able to access more adaptive strategies as their brain matures [63]. ED is nonetheless associated with self-injury in adolescents, who may harm themselves as a way to regulate intense emotions and to cope with distressing feelings or thoughts [60]. Owing to their increased emotional lability and reactivity, and underdeveloped prefrontal control, adolescents are at higher risk of accessing unhealthy ER strategies, such as non-suicidal self-injury which usually emerges during adolescence [64].

Our results showed that among teenagers, those who screen positive for ED were more likely to experience parent child conflict, a finding that is intuitive and in line with the existing literature. Adolescence is an important period for the development of relationships, and parent–adolescent relationships specifically shape adolescents’ emotional and social well-being [65]. There is an association between a stable, warm, and smooth bond between parents and adolescents and adolescents’ subsequent positive developmental outcomes, including better self-esteem [66]. For example, adolescents who have difficulties communicating with their parents about their problems are more likely to experience emotional/behavioral difficulties [67]. High levels of parent–adolescent conflict, including disagreement and hostility are consistently associated with internalizing and externalizing disorders [68], and negative outcomes such as adjustment difficulties, and suicidality [69]. In addition, there is an association between negative mood, family conflict and school problems in adolescence whereby increased parent-youth conflict is associated with more school-related problems, and whereby daily negative mood seems to account for this relationship [70].

School performance emerged as an important correlate for ED among children (aged 8–11 years and 11 months). There are many potential explanations for this and the association between ED and academic performance is multi-faceted. First, school-age children may experience emergent academic difficulties if they carry a diagnosis of ADHD, which affects between 2 and 20% of school-aged children [71], making it the most common neurodevelopmental disorder in this age group [72]. ADHD itself is associated with ED [73], whereby an estimated 40–50% of children with ADHD experience anger outbursts, irritability, or other expressions of vulnerability to low tolerance for distress [74]. Second, ED impacts the ability of children to manage stress and anxiety in academic settings [75, 76] and to access flexible, healthy coping skills. According to the literature, studies show that self-efficacy is a mediator of the relationship between emotional regulation and academic performance [73], and this may be one mechanism to explain the association between ED and academic problems. Interestingly, we found that academic performance remained a correlate even after controlling for psychiatric disorders, which underscores the importance of paying attention to and fostering adaptive behaviors in classrooms.

Across age groups, most of the children/adolescents in our sample who experience ED also screened positive for a psychiatric disorder, a finding which is expected and which is also in line with the literature. Longitudinal studies indicate that impaired ER is a risk factor for adolescent psychopathology, including anxiety, depression [77–79] and eating disorders [80]. The relationship between ER difficulties and psychopathology is complex, reciprocal and bidirectional, as ED is both a risk factor for the development and continuation of psychopathology and a consequence of psychiatric disorders, which render patients less able to regulate their emotions [81].

Higher rates of chronic physical illness were observed for children who had a positive screen for ED compared to those who didn’t. Research on the relationship between chronic illnesses and ER emphasizes that maladaptive ER negatively affects adjustment to chronic illness and increases the risk of mental health difficulties [82, 83]. Children/adolescents with chronic illnesses are a vulnerable group, as they experience interference in their normal developmental course [84] and have impairments in physical, emotional and school functioning [85]. They and their parents face challenges related to the illness course and treatment, which increases the likelihood of experiencing emotional and behavioral difficulties, and which in turns impacts adherence to treatment [82, 86]. Adaptive psychological functioning is necessary to manage the many demands of chronic illness, and to preserve optimal cognitive functioning and emotional balance [87]. It is therefore important to recognize the role of ER in adjusting to illness in order to improve behavioral and physical health outcomes for these patients [83, 87].

Last, an alarmingly low number of children/adolescents who screened positive for ED sought mental health care. There are evidence based interventions that aim to develop ER strategies for a broad range of symptoms and/or disorders, and there is preliminary evidence that improvement in ER abilities is a mediator of symptom relief [88, 89]. There is therefore a need to identify barriers to mental health care in our population, in order to improve access to treatment for vulnerable children/adolescents.

Our study has a number of limitations. Our design is cross sectional, so we cannot establish causality. We use a broad measure of ED, rather than a diagnostic instrument. Future studies should aim at identifying and validating more specific instruments to measure ED, as translation and validation of such measures is a crucial area of research that is lacking. The study relies on self-reports and data collection took place in the home, with parents in the vicinity, which may have contributed to symptom under-reporting. We used screening tools for psychiatric morbidities rather than diagnostic interviews, but we used validated Arabic version to strengthen construct validity of most measures. Finally, those who chose to participate may have been more concerned with mental health, which may have led to selective involvement, and possibly an overestimation of prevalence numbers.

In conclusion, this study is the first to report on the prevalence, comorbidity patterns, and clinical and demographic correlates of ED among a nationally representative sample of children/adolescents in Lebanon, and one of the very few to report on ED prevalence in a national sample. Future studies are needed to evaluate community-based interventions, identify barriers to care, improve access to care and investigate the course of ED over time.
