Scoping review of patient and family engagement interventions in diagnosis: a paradox of too much, yet so little
Authors: Mary A Hill, Helen Haskell, Katie N Dainty, Kerry Kuluski, Christine Shea, Layla Heimlich, Sharifa Kazi, Mark Sochaniwskyj, Alessia Priore, Taylor Leslie Marie Mason, Tess Coppinger, Simran Isani, Nicole Scala, Sara Shearkhani, Sadaf Kazi, Traber D Giardina, Kristen E Miller, Kelly M Smith, The Patient-Partnered Diagnostic Center of Excellence
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionActively engaging patients is essential for diagnostic excellence and patient safety.ObjectivesTo (1) identify and synthesise interventions facilitating patient and family engagement (PFE) across the diagnostic process, and (2a) assess patient involvement and (2b) equity considerations in their design or implementation.DesignThis scoping review followed Arksey and O’Malley’s framework and PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Review) guidelines. An advisory panel guided the review. We searched Medline, Embase, CINAHL, PsycInfo and Northern Light for peer-reviewed literature and conducted grey literature searches using DuckDuckGo and targeted websites. Search terms focused on PFE and diagnostic error. Eligible interventions were published in English between January 1999 and July 2024 and supported PFE in at least one step of the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (NASEM) diagnostic process. Narrative reviews, case studies and editorials were excluded. Interventions were mapped to the NASEM steps; data were extracted on patient involvement and equity.ResultsOf the 11 630 studies screened, 250 were included, representing 260 interventions. Most (n=213; 85.2%) were from the grey literature, and patients were primary users (n=166; 63.8%). Interventions spanned all diagnostic process steps but were most common in treatment (n=122; 46.9%) and history taking (n=100; 38.5%), with few in referrals (n=10, 3.8%) and physical examinations (n=6, 2.3%). The evidence base was weak: grey literature interventions lacked high-quality studies, and among the 37 peer-reviewed studies, three were randomised controlled trials, each limited by small samples or high attrition. Only 63 interventions (24.2%) were designed with patients, and 48 (18.5%) incorporated equity.ConclusionPFE interventions exist across the diagnostic process, but few target referrals and physical examinations. The evidence remains weak, and current interventions cannot be considered effective. Future research should prioritise equity, patient involvement and rigorous evaluation.Keywords: Diagnostic errors, Patient Safety, Communication, Patient-centred care

Introduction

Diagnostic error, defined by the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering and Medicine (NASEM) as the failure to establish an accurate and timely explanation of the patient’s health problem or to effectively communicate that explanation, is a global patient safety issue.1 Nearly every person will experience a diagnostic error in their lifetime, and many will face significant harm or death.1 2 Diagnostic errors are complex, occur in all care settings3–7 and can result from various patient, provider and health system factors. While the true burden is challenging to quantify, diagnostic errors are estimated to occur in 2.5% to 8% of outpatient appointments8 9 and contribute to 23% of in-hospital deaths or escalations to intensive care.10 In the USA alone, diagnostic errors may cause permanent harm or death in up to 795 000 patients annually.2 Populations historically marginalised due to factors such as socioeconomic status, race or stigmatising conditions have been shown to experience disproportionately higher rates of diagnostic error.3 11–14

Achieving diagnostic excellence requires a multifaceted approach that prioritises patient and family engagement (PFE) and positions patients as central members of the diagnostic team.1 15–20 Patients and families are often the only individuals present throughout the entire diagnostic journey, providing valuable information that may improve diagnostic accuracy, timeliness and communication.15 21–29 Additionally, PFE has been called the ‘Blockbuster Drug’ of healthcare,30 essential for achieving high-quality, patient-centred care. Both patients and providers recognise the value of PFE in enhancing communication, fostering trust, promoting safe practices and supporting relationship-based care.31–34 Interventions such as educational programmes, decision aids, clear communication and test result follow-up procedures, patient-centred care models and feedback systems aim to actively involve patients in clinical decision-making.15 18 34–36 Implementing interventions designed in partnership with patients can enhance sustainability, drive system-level improvements and improve health outcomes.37–39 This approach is particularly effective for historically marginalised groups, where tailored, patient-partnered interventions outperform one-size-fits-all interventions in addressing healthcare disparities.40

Although broad PFE recommendations exist (eg, encouraging patients to review medical records, use mHealth tools or report diagnostic events), explicit, actionable guidance on which interventions should be used, by whom and at what stage of the diagnostic process remains limited.41 The NASEM Committee on Diagnostic Error in Healthcare created a conceptual model to describe the diagnostic process and provided broad recommendations for PFE in diagnosis.1 The objective of this review was to identify and map PFE interventions across the NASEM diagnostic process and to assess the extent to which they incorporated patient involvement and equity considerations. By systematically mapping available PFE interventions to this model, this review identifies interventions at each step and where gaps exist. For patients, clinicians and health system leaders, the mapping provides a high-level overview of the types of interventions currently available for use in practice. For researchers, it offers guidance on where to focus future evaluation and co-design efforts.

Methods

Design

We conducted a scoping review to identify and map interventions that facilitate PFE (hereafter referred to as PFE interventions) across the NASEM diagnostic process. This review followed the Arksey and O’Malley framework for scoping reviews,42 43 which is designed to map the breadth and depth of available evidence and identify gaps in the literature. The review also adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Review (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines (online supplemental appendix A).44 45 This approach allowed us to capture grey literature sources that patients may encounter online to guide their diagnostic decision-making. The literature was reviewed and abstracted between 1 June 2023 and 4 August 2024. A protocol for this review was registered with the Centre for Open Science.46

Identifying the research question

The research question(s) and approach were developed in partnership with the Expert Advisory Panel (Expert Panel) from the international Patient-Partnered Diagnostic Centre of Excellence. This multidisciplinary panel includes patients, providers, scientific advisors and other diagnostic error experts. Engaging the Expert Panel ensured the study was comprehensive and grounded in lived experience and scientific expertise. The guiding research questions were as follows:

What interventions exist to facilitate PFE at each stage of the diagnostic process?

We operationalised PFE using Carman’s definition in which, “patients, families, their representatives, and health professionals working in active partnership at various levels across the healthcare system” p224.47 Interventions were broadly defined as tools or approaches, including toolkits, tools, infographics, slides, training materials, videos, reporting opportunities and performance improvement approaches. The definitions were intentionally broad to capture the full range of PFE interventions available.

Search strategy

Peer-reviewed literature

A medical librarian assisted with developing a search strategy for the peer-reviewed literature with terms related to diagnostic error and PFE. We tested the strategy to determine whether it successfully captured four candidate articles23 36 48 49 identified by the primary and senior authors in consultation with the Expert Panel. The final search strategy (online supplemental appendix B) was applied to Medline, Embase, CINAHL and PsycInfo databases for peer-reviewed publications and Northern Light for peer-reviewed abstracts. Searches were conducted on 1 June 2023 and 5 July 2024.

Grey literature

Before searching the grey literature, research team members received training on systematic searching and documentation, implicit bias and the research goals for the study. The primary author and an undergraduate research student conducted a systematic search50 of DuckDuckGo; the search engine was chosen over Google to reduce search history bias.51 52 Search terms included ‘diagnosis,’ ‘diagnostic error,’ ‘patient safety,’ ‘patient engagement’ and ‘family engagement.’ The first 10 pages of results were screened for each term, limiting the results to 100 per term for feasibility.50

We also conducted targeted searches of websites identified by the Expert Panel and research team members as key sources of diagnostic error and patient engagement materials, and websites frequently visited by individuals seeking health information online.53 54 This included government agencies, medical organisations, healthcare provider websites, and patient and diagnostic safety organisations (online supplemental appendix B). We reviewed website landing pages and menus for content related to PFE interventions. Website search bars were then searched, applying the same methodology as the DuckDuckGo search. PFE interventions were downloaded and recorded in a tracking spreadsheet.

Selection criteria

All records obtained in the searches were imported into Covidence, an online literature review management software, for title and abstract screening. Records were included if they included a PFE intervention and referenced diagnosis or at least one step in the diagnostic process. We operationalised the diagnostic process using the NASEM conceptual model. Following recommendations from the Expert Panel, we conceptualised the diagnostic process as including the following steps: (1) patient experiences a health problem, (2) patient engages with the health system, (3) clinical history and interview, (4) physical examination, (5) diagnostic testing, (6) referral and consultation, (7) communication of the diagnosis, (8) treatment and (9) outcomes.1 The panel recommended excluding hypothesis generation, information gathering and synthesis, and information interpretation, as these were viewed as primarily provider-centric activities. In collaboration with the Expert Panel, we developed operational definitions for each step to guide this review (see online supplemental appendix C). These definitions ensured that only interventions prompting engagement within at least one diagnostic process step were included.

Records were also excluded if they were not available in English, lacked an intervention or were published before 1999 when the Institute of Medicine’s ‘To Err is Human’ was published, bringing national attention to patient safety. Clinical interventions and interventions designed for the dental setting were also excluded. To establish interrater reliability, four research team members independently screened 30 candidate articles and grey literature interventions, achieving Cohen’s κ=0.67. Conflicts were adjudicated during weekly meetings.

Data abstraction

Following screening, peer-reviewed studies were obtained for full-text review. Studies that were inaccessible through the study site libraries and interventions that failed to meet the inclusion criteria were excluded. To avoid duplication, when the same intervention was described across multiple sources, we retained the source that included evaluation data where available. This was particularly important in the grey literature, where widely disseminated interventions (eg, Ask Me 3) appeared frequently. We did not systematically seek additional publications describing the same intervention, as our objective was to map the breadth of available PFE interventions.

Interventions included in the final review were independently reviewed and information extracted in Covidence using a coding template developed with the Expert Panel (online supplemental appendix C). Extracted information included: literature type (ie, peer-reviewed or grey literature), primary user of the intervention (eg, the group the intervention was designed to be used by, such as patients or providers), setting (eg, ambulatory care, hospital) and intervention type (eg, toolkit, training materials). Each intervention was mapped to one or more diagnostic process steps based on the operational definitions above (see online supplemental appendix C).

For our secondary aim, we examined whether PFE interventions were developed with patients and families (ie, a patient, family member, caregiver or other patient-type representative involved in developing the intervention). Finally, we assessed whether interventions incorporated equity considerations, defined as being designed or tested to address the needs of an equity deserving population. These populations were defined based on categories commonly recommended for equity monitoring (ie, race, ethnicity, language)55 56 and expanded with guidance from an equity scientist to include indigenous status, sex and gender. We also included an ‘other marginalised circumstances’ category to capture additional equity-related factors not specified in our definition (eg, body size, income, food insecurity). Operational definitions for each extraction category are provided in online supplemental appendix C. Research team members were trained in data extraction and data were reviewed weekly by the first author to resolve discrepancies.

The quality of peer-reviewed literature was scored using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) guidelines, categorising studies as high, moderate, low or insufficient quality based on risk of bias, precision, consistency and directness of results.57 58 Research team members received training in applying GRADE criteria from the first and senior authors. Two reviewers independently applied GRADE scoring in Covidence during data extraction, and discrepancies were adjudicated by the first author. Grey literature was not quality-rated.

Collating, summarising and reporting the results

Most extraction categories were not mutually exclusive and basic descriptive statistics (frequencies) were applied to quantitative data. Directed content analysis59 was used to identify themes in PFE interventions. Themes were then thematically mapped across the NASEM diagnostic process. Unlike other categories, PFE strategies were not quantitatively coded. Instead, strategies were identified qualitatively by thematically grouping the ways interventions sought to prompt engagement (eg, encouraging patients to ask questions). These results are thus presented qualitatively.

Consultation with experts

The Expert Panel was consulted during quarterly meetings and asynchronously to provide feedback at various stages of the review. This included developing the research questions, scope, search terms and strategy, website selection, candidate articles, inclusion and exclusion criteria, data abstraction, mapping, interpretation and reporting of findings.

Most interventions were found in the grey literature (n=213, 85.2%), the majority sourced from medical organisations (n=76, 35.7%) such as the American Heart Association (AHA), government agencies (n=50, 23.5%) such as the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ), or healthcare quality and safety organisations (n=49, 23%) like the Institute for Healthcare Improvement. Interventions were also identified in 37 peer-reviewed studies, most commonly cross-sectional (n=21, 56.8%), followed by randomised controlled trials (RCTs) (n=3, 8.1%), systematic or scoping reviews (n=3, 8.1%), and cohort studies (n=3, 8.1%). Peer-reviewed studies primarily examined intervention effectiveness (n=15, 40.5%), and the usability, acceptability and feasibility of implementing the tools in practice (n=9, 23.3%). Fewer studies focused on tool development (n=2, 5.4%) or impact on patient experience or safety (n=2, 5.4%). A complete list of peer-reviewed studies and grey literature interventions is provided in online supplemental appendix D.

Characteristics of PFE interventions

Patients were the primary user for most interventions (n=166, 63.8%), followed by providers (n=60, 23.1%). More than two-thirds (n=172, 66.2%) of the interventions were designed for general use without specifying care setting, while 46 (17.7%) were meant for use in ambulatory settings, including specialty care. Among the interventions, 107 (41.2%) focused on specific diagnoses, including cancers or heart diseases (n=77, 29.6%) or on undifferentiated symptoms such as angina or back pain (n=30, 11.5%). The most common intervention types were question prompt lists (n=62, 23.8%), tip sheets (n=46, 17.7%), patient education tools (n=40, 15.4%) and record-keeping tools (n=31, 11.9%) such as symptom trackers and patient journals. Mapping PFE interventions to the steps of the diagnostic process

All interventions mapped to at least one step in the NASEM diagnostic process, visualised in figure 2. However, less than half (n=120, 46.2%) explicitly referenced diagnosis or related terms (eg, ‘misdiagnosis’). The majority (n=167, 64.2%) mapped to multiple diagnostic process steps. The most frequently represented steps were treatment (n=122, 46.9%), clinical history and interview (n=100, 38.5%), and communication of the diagnosis (n=88, 33.8%). Interventions that supported PFE in treatment included shared decision-making worksheets, medication lists and question prompt lists to help patients understand and decide on their treatment options and plans. Clinical history and interview interventions included agenda-setting tools and tip sheets designed to help patients articulate their medical histories and goals for their appointments. Interventions to engage patients in communication of the diagnosis primarily included physician-targeted checklists and question prompt lists to facilitate discussions to help patients understand their diagnoses and alternative possibilities. Interventions were least commonly mapped to the referral and consultation (n=10, 3.8%) and physical examination (n=6, 2.3%) steps, and no interventions focused on referrals and consultations exclusively. PFE strategies for diagnosis

Content analysis of the interventions yielded several PFE strategies at the patient, provider and systems levels (figure 3). At the patient level, recommended strategies included recognising and acting on symptoms, speaking up, being a good historian and understanding their care and treatment plans. Provider strategies included engaging patients and families in clinical decision-making and clearly communicating diagnostic uncertainty. Recommendations at the clinic, unit or health system level focused on making patient engagement in diagnosis an operational priority and engaging patients in the design and evaluation of diagnostic measurement and improvement initiatives.

Engagement and equity considerations in intervention design

Just under half (n=117, 45.0%) of the interventions included information on how they were developed. Of these, 63 (53.8%) interventions explicitly stated that patients, families or caregivers were engaged in design and development, while 54 (46.2%) did not. For example, one study described how a patient leaflet was developed based on a literature review and expert opinions, but did not indicate direct patient involvement:

Based on a review of the literature, available patient information leaflets, and their own clinical practice, the collaborators identified 14 core points addressing all major domains of patients’ illness representations. A patient leaflet was compiled that covered and elaborated the 14 core points over 27 pages.57

For the remaining 137 interventions (54.8%), it was unclear whether patients were involved in their development.

Among included interventions, 48 (18.5%) incorporated equity considerations. The most commonly represented domains were age, with interventions tailored to paediatric and older adult populations (n=27, 56.3%); other marginalised circumstances such as obesity, mental health conditions or multiple chronic conditions (n=11, 22.9%); and interventions designed with supports for patients facing language or health literacy barriers (n=11, 22.9%). The majority of these interventions incorporated equity considerations during the development phase (n=36, 75.0%) with interventions designed to address the needs of specific equity-deserving groups, and a smaller number incorporated equity considerations during evaluation (n=5, 10.4%) or both (n=7, 14.6%). The majority of interventions with equity considerations were from the grey literature (n=32, 66.7%).

Quality review and evaluation of evidence

The overall strength of evidence supporting PFE interventions in diagnosis was low to moderate. The majority of the 37 peer-reviewed studies (n=20, 54.5%) were cross-sectional, which carries a higher risk of bias due to the lack of randomisation. Only three studies were RCTs, and their low sample sizes48 60 and high attrition rates61 significantly increased the risk of bias. Precision was also limited, as over half of the studies (n=20, 54.1%) had sample sizes of fewer than 100 participants, reducing the confidence in estimated effects. The consistency of findings was moderate. Six studies48 61–65 reported positive associations between PFE interventions and improvements in the diagnostic process steps of access,62 63 outcomes,48 65 testing64 and treatment.61 However, there was variation in outcomes measurement across studies, making it difficult to compare results or determine effectiveness. The directness of evidence also varied. Most studies (n=26, 70.3%) explicitly referenced diagnosis and were directly related to our research question, while others (n=11, 29.7%) focused on broader patient safety outcomes but were interpreted by our team to address specific steps in the diagnostic process.

Discussion

This scoping review has yielded several important findings. The results reinforce the growing recognition of patients as central members of the diagnostic team1 and confirm that PFE interventions can be applied at each step in the NASEM diagnostic process. However, the findings also highlight the uneven distribution of available PFE interventions, with few in the referrals and physical examination steps. Although an abundance of PFE interventions exists at the patient, provider and systems levels, few have been rigorously evaluated. Only three RCTs were identified, all at high risk of bias, leaving most interventions without strong evidence of effectiveness. Equity considerations were also limited, and among those that did incorporate equity, most were developed or tested for paediatric or older adult populations. For researchers, these findings point to important priorities for future codesign and evaluation efforts, while for patients, clinicians and health system leaders, the mapping offers a high-level overview of interventions currently available for use in practice.

Despite growing research interest in diagnostic errors, most studies have focused on defining the problem or developing clinician-focused solutions, often overlooking the patient’s role in identifying and preventing errors. The NASEM Committee’s definition of diagnostic error marked a paradigm shift by recognising patients as active, essential members of the diagnostic team rather than passive recipients of diagnosis.1 66 The extensive body of PFE interventions identified in this review reflects this shift and builds on our prior work and others’ demonstrating the unique and important contributions of patients and families in promoting safety in diagnosis.21–26 29 36 67 68 Importantly, these interventions do not only focus on what patients should do (eg, share their stories, ask questions, participate in clinical decision-making, follow-up with testing and treatment plans),24 27 but also recognise the responsibility of providers and health systems to engage patients. This review reflects the idea that clinical team members and health system leaders have a responsibility to build trust, encourage PFE and address the structural barriers that may hinder patients’ willingness to engage in diagnosis, particularly among historically marginalised communities.17 19

While this review identified PFE interventions across all steps of the NASEM diagnostic process, gaps remain. Interventions were most concentrated in steps where PFE is most explicit, such as clinical history and interview, where patients report their symptoms and medical histories,69–71 and communication of the diagnosis, which requires direct patient involvement. These findings align with evidence that 79% of diagnostic errors in primary care occur during patient-clinician interactions, with 56% stemming from breakdowns in history taking.9 72 Treatment was similarly well represented, a finding supported by a substantial body of literature on PFE in treatment decision-making and medication safety.73 74 In contrast, steps where the role of PFE is less clearly defined or recognised, such as referrals and physical examinations, were under-represented in both peer-reviewed and grey literature. Yet, interventions targeting these steps are warranted. For example, no interventions focused solely on referrals and consultations, and only 10 (4.0%) addressed this step at all, despite evidence of suboptimal referral completion and follow-up rates.75–77 Given that 19.5% of diagnostic breakdowns occur during referrals,9 this represents a critical gap where targeted PFE strategies may be needed.

Another finding of this review was the limited involvement of patients in intervention design and development. Over half of the interventions did not specify whether patients and families were engaged in their design. This lack of transparency was observed across both peer-reviewed and grey literature. Clear documentation of patient involvement in intervention design, development and testing is crucial for assessing the potential effectiveness and acceptability of patient-centred interventions. While nearly a quarter (24.2%) of interventions were reportedly designed with patients or families, details about their specific roles were often missing. Interventions that were designed without patient input (20.8%) represent missed opportunities to incorporate valuable perspectives. Given the limited reporting, the true percentage of interventions designed without patient involvement is likely higher.

Equity considerations were also under-represented, with only 18.5% of interventions incorporating at least one sociodemographic or social identity factor into their design and testing. Most of these focused on a single factor (eg, age) without adopting an intersectional lens that considers race, gender, language or other domains. This narrow approach is concerning, given the well-documented disparities experienced by equity-deserving groups in diagnostic testing, referrals, diagnoses, treatments and access to services.78–83 Additionally, the tendency to design interventions to address inequities, without testing them with their intended populations, underscores the need for more evaluation of PFE interventions among marginalised groups. Our finding that age was the most frequently addressed social identity domain aligns with evidence showing the distinct healthcare needs and vulnerabilities experienced by paediatric and older adult populations.3 84–86 However, more interventions may be needed to address the needs of other equity-deserving groups. Future research should intentionally integrate equity considerations into both the design and evaluation of PFE interventions to better serve equity-deserving populations and reduce diagnostic disparities.

This scoping review identified PFE interventions across the diagnostic process and examined the extent to which they incorporated patient involvement and equity considerations. The findings emphasise the growing recognition of patients as active members of the diagnostic team and confirm that PFE interventions can be implemented throughout the diagnostic process. However, gaps exist, particularly in the less overt stages of the diagnostic process, such as physical examination and referrals and consultations. Despite the abundance of available interventions, few have undergone rigorous evaluation, limiting our ability to determine their effectiveness across diverse patient populations and healthcare settings. The prevalence of grey literature suggests that patient advocacy and professional groups have identified this gap, underscoring the need for greater collaboration with researchers to develop interventions that not only meet patients’ needs, but are also rigorously evaluated. As healthcare continues to evolve, implementing evidence-based PFE interventions will be essential to improve diagnosis, reduce disparities and enhance patient safety. Future research should prioritise these interventions, integrating equity considerations and addressing structural barriers to engagement to ensure that diagnostic processes are safer, more inclusive and more patient-centred.

Limitations

This review has four primary limitations. First, although necessary to manage the large volume of literature, our search strategy may have led to the exclusion of potentially relevant interventions. Grey literature searches were subject to cut-off limits for feasibility, meaning that additional relevant interventions may exist beyond those identified in this review. This was especially true for DuckDuckGo and the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality website, where searches yielded hundreds to thousands of results.

Second, mapping interventions to the NASEM diagnostic process steps required operational definitions developed by the research team in consultation with the Expert Panel. Although informed by expert input, this process may have introduced subjectivity in the way interventions were interpreted and categorised. Additionally, the diagnostic process is complex and not strictly linear, making it challenging to classify interventions and strategies, especially those that span multiple steps.

Third, we did not formally appraise the quality of grey literature sources. While appraisal tools for grey literature exist,87 applying them across more than 200 interventions would have been resource-intensive and inconsistent with the objectives of this scoping review. In addition, the general absence of information available on how and by whom these interventions were developed or evaluated limits conclusions about their effectiveness.

Finally, many grey literature interventions came from a small number of organisations (eg, AHRQ, American Heart Association). Although we excluded exact duplicates, our review may over-represent the priorities of these groups. For example, AHRQ’s focus on medication safety may help explain the number of treatment-related interventions, while the American Heart Association’s focus on prevention, diagnosis and management of chronic conditions was reflected in the number of patient education tools. Equity considerations were more commonly reported in peer-reviewed studies than in the grey literature, likely reflecting stronger reporting expectations in academic publishing. Future research should prioritise rigorous testing of these widely disseminated tools and expand efforts to develop and test interventions targeting steps of the diagnostic process currently underrepresented by major organisations.

Conclusion

Reducing diagnostic errors requires strong evidence linking PFE interventions to error mitigation across steps of the diagnostic process. More broadly, empirical studies with methodologically rigorous designs are needed to build confidence in PFE interventions and encourage their routine adoption. Advancing this evidence base may require reimagining healthcare delivery to ensure safety throughout the diagnostic process, integrating patient-partnered and equity-driven approaches that account for cultural competence and health literacy.
