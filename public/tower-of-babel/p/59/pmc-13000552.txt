An integrated approach to smoky food flavor: Decoding key odorants, sensory contributions, and olfactory recognition mechanisms
Authors: Jingtao Wang, Xiaoling Zhou, Yalong Guo, Qingzhao Shi, Shan Wang, Qichao Ye, Wu Fan, Guobi Chai, Qidong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmoky aroma, a culturally significant and globally appreciated flavor, is a key sensory attribute in a wide range of products, from traditional smoked foods to modern beverages and e-cigarettes. A precise understanding of its chemical composition and perceptual mechanisms is essential for quality control and flavor innovation. This study established an integrated analytical framework to address this need. By quantifying 38 key odorants in smoked meats, whiskies, and e-cigarette oils, and determining their odor activity values (OAVs) in product-specific matrices, we identified guaiacol as the indispensable aroma driver across all traditional smoked products. Molecular docking simulations further revealed that guaiacol interacts with human olfactory receptors (OR1G1, OR2T1, OR10G4) through specific hydrogen-bonding and π-π interactions, providing mechanistic insights into its potent sensory impact. This work bridges the gap between flavor chemistry and sensory biology, offering a scientific foundation for the characterization, quality control, and rational design of smoky flavors in the food and beverage industry.Keywords: Smoky odorants, OAV, Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), Sensory threshold, Molecular docking

Introduction

Since the controlled use of fire for food processing, the smoky aroma generated by biomass combustion has become an integral part of daily life, profoundly influencing the development of foods and other culturally significant products worldwide. This is exemplified by a variety of smoke-processed meats, including European smoked salmon and ham, American smoked bacon and ribs, and Chinese smoked cured meats and sausages, whose characteristic profiles arise from the incorporation of volatile compounds derived from wood or carbohydrate pyrolysis (Du et al., 2021; Li and Wu, 2024; Mokh et al., 2024; Poljanec et al., 2021; L. Yang et al., 2023). Similarly, the distinctive peaty note in single malt whisky originates from smoky odorants absorbed during the kilning of malt (Parker et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025). Tobacco products, including cigars and cigarettes, also rely on combustion-generated smoke for sensory appeal, a process that naturally yields abundant smoky compounds (Lim et al., 2022). These have further inspired the flavor design of contemporary e-cigarettes. The widespread occurrence of smoky products across diverse domains underscores the pervasive sensory appeal of smoky aroma.

Although compounds such as eugenol and furfural have been reported to exhibit smoky notes in some studies, it is generally recognized that smoky aroma is primarily generated by phenolic compounds including phenol, guaiacol, and their alkylated, alkenylated, and methoxylated derivatives (Füllemann and Steinhaus, 2020; Harrison and Priest, 2009; Q. Wang et al., 2025). Phenol and its derivatives, which more commonly regarded as harmful components, exhibit intense medicinal aroma and pungent character, whereas guaiacol and its derivatives are primarily considered as flavor substances with a more typical smoky aroma (Lipan et al., 2016; Tian et al., 2025). Early studies on smoked meats identified guaiacol as a key volatile compound contributing to the characteristic smoky note, with its concentration directly correlating with smoking intensity. Subsequent investigations in smoked fish products confirmed that guaiacol, along with its methyl and ethyl derivatives, forms the backbone of smoky aroma profiles (Lacalle-Bergeron et al., 2020). In smoked cheese and bacon, guaiacol has been consistently reported as the compound with the highest flavor dilution factor, underscoring its predominant sensory contribution (Garabal et al., 2010; Qu et al., 2024). These classic studies across diverse smoked food matrices have collectively established guaiacol as the definitive molecular marker of smoky character, providing a foundation for understanding structure-odor relationships in phenolic compounds.

With the widespread adoption and continuous advancement of analytical technologies, gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) has become an indispensable tool for the separation, identification, and quantification of volatile compounds in complex food matrices (T. Zhao et al., 2025). Nevertheless, a significant gap remains in the development of tailored analytical methods suitable for different types of smoky foods, which vary widely in composition, matrix effects, and processing conditions. Beyond the qualitative and quantitative profiling of volatile constituents, identifying which specific flavor molecules serve as the key drivers of smoky aroma is crucial for ensuring product consistency, guiding flavor optimization, and supporting quality control in the food industry (Tao et al., 2025). Thus, establishing a reliable, comprehensive analytical approach capable of both characterizing smoke-related compounds and determining their sensory contributions represents an essential step forward in smoky food research and development.

In the present study, to establish a robust analytical approach for characterizing key smoky odorants, a comprehensive literature review was first conducted to compile volatile compounds consistently reported as critical contributors to smoky aroma. A panel of candidate compounds was systematically screened based on their frequency of occurrence in the literature and commercial availability, resulting in the selection of 38 reference standards for subsequent methodological development. Using these standards, a reliable GC-MS method was established and applied to qualitatively and quantitatively profile smoky flavor compounds in four types of smoked meat, three whisky samples, and two e-cigarette formulations. Furthermore, odor thresholds of the 38 target compounds were determined using the 3-AFC (three-alternative forced choice) method in relevant food matrices, and OAVs were calculated to identify key odorants (OAV ≥1). Finally, molecular docking simulations were performed to explore the binding interactions between the identified key smoky compounds and human olfactory receptors, providing insights into the molecular basis of smoky aroma perception (Fig. 1). Collectively, this work not only establishes a comprehensive analytical framework for smoky flavor analysis but also enhances the understanding of the perceptual and mechanistic underpinnings of smoky aroma, thereby supporting the quality control and targeted flavor design of smoky products.

Overview of the flavor research methodology for smoke-flavored foods. The integrated analytical framework includes: selection of 38 target smoky odorants based on literature review; sample preparation (smoked meats, whiskies, e-cigarette oils); GC-MS quantification; sensory evaluation (threshold determination in product-specific matrices and OAV calculation); and molecular docking simulations to elucidate interactions between key smoky compounds and human olfactory receptors.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

To establish a representative panel of smoky odorants for method development and quantification, a comprehensive literature review was first conducted. A total of 61 volatile compounds previously reported with the sensory descriptor “smoky” were identified from 49 relevant publications (Table S1). From this initial pool, candidate compounds were prioritized based on their frequency of occurrence across the surveyed literature. Compounds reported in fewer than three independent studies were considered to have incidental or context-specific associations with smoky aroma and were therefore excluded from further consideration. Based on this criterion, 38 compounds were ultimately selected as target analytes, ensuring representation of diverse chemical classes (including phenols, guaiacol derivatives, and methoxyphenols) and structural variations known to influence odor characteristics (Table 1). All 38 selected compounds are commercially available from standard scientific supply platforms.

Samples

Smoked meat: Four commercial smoked meat products were included in this study: (1) Smoked pork (Tang Gods Group Co., Ltd., Hunan, China), produced from pork leg meat and smoked using fruitwood; (2) Smoked bacon (Smithfield, USA), produced from pork belly rib meat and smoked using hickory wood; (3) Parma ham (Casale, Italy), produced from pork hind legs and smoked using oak wood; (4) Smoked salmon (Johnross, Scotland), produced from locally farm-raised salmon and smoked using fruitwood. All meat samples were stored at −20 °C and thawed at room temperature for 2 h prior to analysis.

Whisky samples: Three single malt whiskies with distinct smoky aroma profiles were selected: Laphroaig Quarter Cask (Isle of Islay, Scotland), known for its pronounced peaty and smoky character; Lagavulin 16-Year-Old (Isle of Islay, Scotland), representing the robust peaty style typical of southern Islay; and Yamazaki 1923 (Japan), which presents a subtle balance of smoky and fruity notes, often considered an introductory whisky with a smoky profile. All whisky samples were stored at 4 °C before analysis.

E-cigarette oils samples: Two commercially available e-cigarette oils from the RELX HuanYing series (RELX, China), both described as tobacco-flavored, were analyzed: No. 31 QunYuShanJian (characterized as naturally fragrant tobacco) and No. 72 RanQingSangBa (characterized as rich and full-bodied tobacco). All e-cigarette oils were stored at room temperature in a cool, dark place.

Sample pretreatment

To preserve the original flavor profiles, volatile compounds were extracted from all samples using an ethanol solution containing a known concentration of the internal standard (4-chlorophenol). Pretreatment procedures were optimized for each sample matrix as follows.

Smoked meat samples

Approximately 1 g of homogenized meat (cut into ∼1 mm3 particles) was weighed into a conical flask. Then, 5 mL of the ethanol extraction solvent was added. The mixture was subjected to ultrasonication for 30 min at 80% power. The extract was filtered through an organic solvent membrane filter, and 1 mL of the filtrate was transferred to a GC vial for analysis.

Whisky samples

A 1 mL aliquot of whisky was pipetted into a 10 mL volumetric flask. The ethanol extraction solvent was added to the mark, followed by the addition of an appropriate amount of anhydrous sodium sulfate. The flask was shaken vigorously, allowed to stand for 30 min, and then filtered. After standing, the anhydrous sodium sulfate was observed to retain its original fine granular appearance, remaining free-flowing and showing no signs of caking or dissolution. This indicates that the added amount was sufficient to completely absorb the residual water from the diluted sample, achieving effective drying. Subsequently, 1 mL of the filtrate was placed in a GC vial.

E-cigarette oils samples

Exactly 1 g of e-cigarette oils was weighed into a 5 mL volumetric flask. The ethanol extraction solvent was added to the mark, followed by an appropriate amount of anhydrous sodium sulfate. After shaking and standing for 30 min, the anhydrous sodium sulfate remained granular and free-flowing, indicating complete absorption of any residual moisture from the diluted sample. The solution was then filtered, and 1 mL of the filtrate was collected in a GC vial.

GC-MS analysis

GC-MS analysis was performed on an Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph coupled with an Agilent 5977B mass selective detector, equipped with an Agilent 7693A automatic liquid sampler. Separation was achieved using a DB-WAX capillary column (60 m × 0.25 mm i.d. × 0.25 μm film thickness). High-purity helium (99.999%) was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min (inlet pressure: 16.5 psi). The injection volume was 1 μL in splitless mode. The oven temperature program was as follows: initial temperature of 50 °C (held for 0 min), increased to 160 °C at 5 °C/min (held for 0 min), then to 190 °C at 1 °C/min (held for 0 min), and finally to 240 °C at 3 °C/min (held for 5 min). The total run time was 68 min, with a solvent delay of 11 min. The mass spectrometer operated in electron ionization (EI) mode at 70 eV. The ion source and quadrupole temperatures were maintained at 230 °C and 150 °C, respectively. Mass spectra were acquired in full-scan mode over a range of m/z 40-350.

Quantification method

Quantification of the target smoky odorants was performed using 4-chlorophenol as internal standard method to correct for potential instrumental and procedural variations. A mixed stock standard solution containing all 38 target analytes was prepared in the ethanol extraction solvent. This stock solution was subsequently serially diluted (at ratios of 1:2, 1:5, 1:10, 1:20, 1:50, and 1:100) to generate a calibration curve with seven concentration levels. The internal standard concentration was kept identical in all calibration standards and during sample extraction. A linear calibration curve for each compound was established by plotting the peak area ratio (analyte to internal standard) against the corresponding concentration ratio. The concentration of each odorant in the samples was then calculated based on its respective calibration curve.

To ensure reliability of the analytical method, validation was performed following international guidelines for food analysis. Precision was evaluated by analyzing mixed standard solutions at three concentration levels in six replicates, with results expressed as relative standard deviation (RSD). Accuracy was determined through recovery experiments by spiking sample matrices with the mixed standard at three concentration levels (approximately 50%, 100%, and 150% of endogenous concentrations), followed by extraction and analysis; recovery rates were calculated as (measured - endogenous)/spiked × 100%. Linearity was assessed using seven-point calibration curves, with correlation coefficients (R2) calculated for each compound. Sensitivity was evaluated by determining limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) based on signal-to-noise ratios of 3 and 10, respectively.

To ensure consistency in quantification units across solid and liquid samples, all results are expressed in μg/kg. For whisky samples, although 1 mL aliquots were taken for analysis, each aliquot was weighed under the same experimental conditions to determine its exact mass. Quantification was then performed based on this measured mass, yielding results directly in μg/kg without the need for unit conversion. The LOD and LOQ presented in Table 3 were also established using the same mass-based approach to maintain uniformity throughout the study.

Determination of sensory detection thresholds and odor activity values

Ethical statement

The study involved a panel of eight trained and experienced experts (4 males, 4 females, and aged 22-32 years), all of whom were free from rhinitis and non-smokers, all participants had prior experience in sensory evaluation. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant, confirming their willingness to participate and authorize data usage. Panelists retained the right to withdraw from the trial at any time without providing any reason. All panel members were thoroughly informed about the details of the study. To prevent fatigue and carryover effects, the panel members were allowed a 5-min break between the evaluations of different samples.

Sensory panel and procedure

All assessors underwent a structured training program lasting 12 h, distributed over two weeks. The training protocol included: (1) familiarization with the sensory descriptors and odor characteristics of the 38 target smoky compounds using reference standards; (2) practice sessions on the 3-AFC (Three-Alternative Forced-Choice) method using blank and spiked samples; and (3) exercises to improve concentration, memory, and verbalization of olfactory perceptions. To ensure data reliability, panel reproducibility was assessed by having each assessor evaluate a reference compound (guaiacol at a known concentration) in triplicate across three separate sessions. Inter-assessor consistency was evaluated using the Panel Consensus Index (PCI), which exceeded 0.85, indicating good agreement among panel members.

To simulate the respective product matrices for sensory threshold determination, three different substrates were selected based on their physicochemical compatibility with each product type and their established use in sensory research. For smoked meat products, sunflower oil was selected as the sensory substrate due to the lipophilic nature of smoky odorants and the lipid-rich composition of meat. Vegetable oils, such as sunflower oil, are widely used as neutral carriers in fatty food systems, as they provide a stable, non-volatile medium that mimics the lipid environment of meat without contributing interfering background aromas (Pollner and Schieberle, 2016). For whisky samples, a 40% (v/v) ethanol aqueous solution was used to replicate the hydroalcoholic matrix of distilled spirits, which typically range from 40% to 50% alcohol by volume (Z. Wang et al., 2024). This ethanol concentration ensures that the solubility and partitioning behavior of odorants closely resemble those in actual whisky. For e-cigarette oils, a propylene glycol/glycerol mixture (55:45, w/w) was selected as the sensory substrate (Rao et al., 2017). This specific ratio represents a common formulation in commercial e-liquids, with propylene glycol and vegetable glycerin serving as the primary solvent carriers that influence throat hit, vapor production, and flavor delivery. The use of this solvent mixture as a sensory matrix ensures that odorant thresholds are determined in a medium that authentically reflects the chemical environment of e-cigarette aerosols.

The sensory assessment employed the 3-AFC method (Han et al., 2025). For each compound, a series of solutions with successive concentration differences by a factor of 2 was prepared in the corresponding substrate. During a session, assessors were presented with three coded samples per concentration level, only one of which contained the target odorant, while the other two were blank substrates. Starting from the highest concentration, assessors were required to identify the sample containing the odorant. The testing proceeded to progressively lower concentrations until the assessor could no longer correctly identify the spiked sample. The testing followed a descending staircase design. For each assessor, the geometric mean of the last correctly identified concentration and the first incorrectly identified concentration in a testing series was recorded as a reversal point. The individual detection threshold was then calculated as the geometric mean of all such reversal points obtained across multiple testing series.

All stock solutions and serial dilutions for threshold determination were prepared gravimetrically by accurately weighing both the odorant compounds and the corresponding substrate (sunflower oil, 40% ethanol solution, or propylene glycol/glycerol mixture) to ensure mass-based concentrations. Therefore, all odor thresholds are expressed in μg/kg, consistent with the units used for quantified sample concentrations (Füllemann and Steinhaus, 2020).

Threshold and OAV calculation

The group detection threshold for each compound was defined as the geometric mean of the individual thresholds obtained from all eight panelists (Giguère et al., 2016). To evaluate the sensory contribution of each smoky odorant in the flavor products, the OAV was calculated using the following formula: OAV = C/DT, where C is the quantified concentration of the compound in the product, and DT is its group detection threshold in the corresponding matrix. Compounds with an OAV ≥1 were considered to contribute directly to the overall smoky flavor of the sample (Dunkel et al., 2014).

Molecular docking

To explore the potential interaction patterns between key smoky odorants (ligands) and olfactory receptors (ORs), an in silico molecular docking approach was employed. The amino acid sequences of target ORs, selected based on their known relevance to smoky or phenolic odorant detection (e.g., OR1A1, OR2J2, OR2W1), were retrieved from the UniProt database. The canonical SMILES notations of the ligand molecules were obtained from PubChem. Both receptor sequences and ligand SMILES are compiled in Table S2. Evaluation of olfactory receptor protein model in Fig. S2.

The binding mode prediction was performed using the Chai-1 web server (https://lab.chaidiscovery.com/dashboard), a computational tool designed for predicting ligand interactions with G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), including olfactory receptors (Elliott et al., 2025; Thiyagarajan et al., 2025). For each receptor, the server's built-in pipeline was used to generate a three-dimensional structural model. Docking simulations were then conducted by submitting the prepared ligand structures (in SMILES format) against the active site of the corresponding receptor model. Default parameters of the Chai-1 platform were applied, and the docking poses were ranked according to the calculated scores.

To ensure reproducibility and validity, the binding site for each olfactory receptor was defined based on conserved structural features of class A GPCRs and alignment with known human olfactory receptor structures. Specifically, the orthosteric binding pocket was defined as the region between transmembrane helices TM-3, TM-5, and TM-6, where odorant molecules are typically accommodated. Final binding poses were selected based on the presence of key interactions (hydrogen bonding and π-π stacking) consistent with established odorant-receptor interaction patterns.

The resulting docked complexes, representing the most favorable binding poses for key smoky odorant-OR pairs, were retrieved. These structures were subsequently imported into PyMOL for visualization and analysis. In PyMOL, non-covalent interactions (such as hydrogen bonds) between the ligand and key amino acid residues within the receptor binding pocket were identified and graphically rendered to elucidate the molecular basis of recognition.

Unique quantitative and qualitative ions were selected for each compound to ensure specific identification and accurate quantification in complex samples, thereby minimizing potential interferences. The method demonstrated excellent precision, with RSDs for all compounds below 5% (Table 2). Accuracy, evaluated through recovery experiments at three concentration levels (low, medium, and high), was satisfactory for the vast majority of analytes, with recovery rates predominantly within the accepted range of 80-120%. A few exceptions, such as isoeugenol at the medium level (86.57%) and 2,3-dimethoxyphenol at the low level (84.14%), were observed. These slight deviations may be attributed to matrix effects or compound-specific volatility during sample preparation and are considered acceptable for a multi-analyte method of this scope (Mokh et al., 2024). The overall high precision and accuracy confirm the reproducibility and reliability of the extraction and analysis protocol.

The calibration curves for all 38 smoky odorants exhibited excellent linearity across their respective concentration ranges, with correlation coefficients (R2) exceeding 0.999 for most compounds (Table 3). This strong linear relationship is crucial for reliable quantification across the expected concentration spans found in various smoky products. The sensitivity of the method was assessed by determining the LOD and LOQ (Park et al., 2021). In Table 3, LODs and LOQs varied among compounds, reflecting differences in their ionization efficiency and chromatographic behavior. For instance, guaiacol and phenol, being central to smoky profiles, exhibited relatively higher LOQs (3.67 and 1.99 μg/kg, respectively), while others like 2-bromo-6-methoxyphenol showed remarkable sensitivity (LOQ of 0.13 μg/kg). This comprehensive sensitivity profile ensures that the method is capable of detecting trace-level odorants critical to aroma perception, as well as quantifying major components.

Smoked meats

The analysis of smoked meats (Tang Gods Pork, Smithfield Bacon, Casale Parma Ham, Johnross Salmon) confirmed the central role of phenolic compounds. Guaiacol and phenol were present at high concentrations in the pork, bacon, and salmon samples (e.g., guaiacol: 5.7-12.3 mg/kg; phenol: 2.8-9.6 mg/kg), aligning with their status as primary markers of wood smoke (Lingbeck et al., 2014). Their significantly lower levels in Casale Parma Ham suggest a milder smoking process or the use of smoking techniques that impart less of these characteristic compounds.

Beyond these, other alkyl- and methoxy-substituted phenols contributed significantly to the complex profiles. 2,6-Dimethoxyphenol was exceptionally abundant in bacon and salmon (up to 28.2 mg/kg), often associated with the smoke from hardwoods. 2-Methoxy-4-methylphenol and 4-ethyl-2-methoxyphenol were also major contributors in meat samples. Interestingly, eugenol was detected in all meats, likely originating from certain wood spices or smoking materials (Hitzel et al., 2013). The significant variation in the concentration of specific compounds, such as the high 3-methoxycatechol in bacon and pork, points to the influence of raw material (e.g., fat content) and specific smoke generation conditions (wood type, temperature).

Whiskies

The three whiskies displayed a pronounced and distinct smoky odorant profile dominated by furfural and phenol. Furfural, a product of carbohydrate pyrolysis during malt kilning, was the most abundant volatile across all whiskies (10.1-19.0 mg/kg), underscoring its role in the “heated” or “caramelized” background of smoky whisky aroma. Phenol and guaiacol were consistently present, with their concentrations highest in the heavily peated Laphroaig and Lagavulin, directly correlating with their perceived smoky intensity (Slaghenaufi et al., 2016). The Islay whiskies (Laphroaig, Lagavulin) further showed elevated levels of C6-alkylphenols (e.g., 2,4-dimethylphenol, 4-ethylphenol), which contribute pungent, medicinal notes characteristic of the region's profile. In contrast, the Japanese Yamazaki 1923 contained these compounds at much lower levels, aligning with its subtler, more integrated smoky character, and featured a relatively higher concentration of 4-vinyl-2-methoxyphenol, which can impart sweet, spicy nuances.

E-cigarette oils

The two RELX e-cigarette oils presented a highly engineered flavor profile. Strikingly, neither of the core smoke markers guaiacol nor phenol was the dominant compound. Instead, eugenol was found at the highest concentration (2.5-3.4 mg/kg), likely used to provide a warm, clove-like top note that mimics certain tobacco aromas (Cohen et al., 2024). Guaiacol and phenol were present at moderate levels (0.4-1.0 mg/kg) as supporting actors. The profile was supplemented with a variety of other phenols and derivatives, such as 2,6-dimethoxyphenol, 4-vinyl-2-methoxyphenol, and 2-methoxy-4-propylphenol, creating a blended, rounded “smoky” impression without the overwhelming medicinal punch of a single dominant phenol.

The compositional differences observed across the three product categories also reflect fundamental variations in how “smoky” flavor is defined and perceived in each context. In traditionally smoked foods, smoky aroma emerges directly from biomass combustion, representing an authentic imprint of the smoking process itself (Iwona et al., 2025; H. Wang and Chambers Iv, 2018). For peated whiskies, the smoky character is a deliberately cultivated regional signature, arising from controlled peat combustion during malt kilning and subsequently evolving through years of barrel maturation (Haug et al., 2023). In contrast, e-cigarette oils employ a constructed smoky flavor, where individual aroma chemicals are artfully blended to evoke a recognizable yet smoother smoky impression suitable for inhalation (Hayes and Baker, 2022). These distinctions are essential for interpreting the sensory significance of individual compounds across different product matrices, as the same chemical may contribute differently to the overall flavor perception depending on the product context and consumer expectations.

Identification of key aroma-active compounds

The sensory significance of the quantified smoky odorants was rigorously evaluated by calculating their OAVs, which bridge the gap between chemical concentration and human perception (Z. Wang et al., 2023). Critically, the odor thresholds used in these calculations were determined in matrices that simulate the actual consumption context: sunflower oil for smoked meats, a 40% (v/v) ethanol solution for whiskies, and a propylene glycol/glycerol mixture for e-cigarette oils. This approach ensures the ecological validity and relevance of the OAV results.

In smoked meats, guaiacol emerged as the unequivocal primary contributor due to its exceptionally high OAVs, a consequence of its very low threshold in an oily matrix (Table 5). Its OAV reached 122.2 in Tang Gods pork and 76.8 in Smithfield bacon, solidifying its role as the defining smoky odorant. Notably, 4-allyl-2,6-dimethoxyphenol showed an OAV as high as 198.1 in bacon, while 4-ethylphenol and 3-methoxycatechol also presented significant OAVs above 1 in various meat samples, collectively constructing the complex, layered smoky profile. In contrast, the key odorant profile of peated whiskies was characterized by the potent contribution of 2-methoxy-4-propylphenol, with OAVs up to 30.9. This compound, alongside guaiacol and other alkylphenols like 3-ethylphenol and 4-ethyl-2-methoxyphenol, highlights the distinct sensory imprint of peat smoke derived from a hydroalcoholic medium. For e-cigarette oils, the OAV analysis uncovered a deliberate flavor design strategy. Eugenol presented overwhelmingly high OAVs, exceeding 145 in both samples, establishing it as the engineered cornerstone of the “tobacco” flavor. Guaiacol and 2-methoxy-4-propylphenol played significant but secondary reinforcing roles in this designed profile.

This OAV-directed perspective crucially demonstrates that chemical abundance is not predictive of sensory impact (Zhu et al., 2023). This is exemplified by furfural and phenol, which were quantitatively major but sensorially marginal across most samples due to their high thresholds. Consequently, the OAV framework successfully shifts the analytical focus from mere composition to perceptual relevance. It provides a definitive, product-specific priority list of key smoky odorants that not only explains the characteristic aroma of each product type but also establishes a targeted chemical basis for understanding their molecular interaction with OR in the subsequent investigation.

Beyond the specific matrices examined in this study, the identified key smoky compounds have been widely recognized as universal contributors to smoky aroma across diverse food systems. Guaiacol, for instance, has been consistently reported as a dominant odorant in smoked salmon, smoked cheese, and smoke-tainted wines, underscoring its ubiquitous role as a smoky aroma marker across aquatic, dairy, and beverage products (Jónsdóttir et al., 2008; Rizzo et al., 2022). Similarly, 4-ethylphenol, identified here as a key contributor in peated whiskies, is also well-documented impact odorants in smoke-affected grapes and wines, where they impart characteristic “smoky” and “medicinal” notes (W. Yang et al., 2023). Eugenol, while serving as the primary smoky driver in e-cigarette oils in this study, is also a key aroma compound in spices such as clove and cinnamon, and has been identified in smoked meats where it contributes warm, spicy nuances (Lao et al., 2024). These cross-product consistencies highlight that while the relative importance of individual compounds may shift depending on matrix and processing conditions, a core set of phenolic odorants, particularly guaiacol and its alkylated derivatives, forms the universal chemical basis of smoky aroma perception (Scavarda et al., 2021).

Interaction of guaiacol with human olfactory receptors

Understanding the perceptual mechanisms of smoky aroma at the molecular level requires elucidating how key odorants interact with ORs (Xiao et al., 2024). While chemical and sensory analyses identify what compounds are important, molecular docking reveals how they might be recognized, providing a mechanistic basis for their odor potency and quality. In this study, we focused on guaiacol for in-depth computational analysis. This choice was predicated on its established role as the quintessential smoky odorant, consistently exhibiting the highest OAVs in traditionally smoked foods as identified in Section 3.3. To investigate its recognition, three human ORs were selected based on prior literature evidence for their activation by smoky phenolics: OR1G1, OR2T1, and OR10G4 (Gu et al., 2025; Mainland et al., 2013).

The molecular docking simulations revealed distinct yet complementary binding modes of guaiacol within the orthosteric binding pockets of these receptors (Fig. 2). A detailed analysis of the binding interfaces showed that hydrogen bonding plays a foundational role in anchoring the ligand (Y. Zhao et al., 2024). Specifically, the hydroxyl group of guaiacol consistently formed a hydrogen bond with a conserved aspartic acid, histidine or serine residue in the binding pocket. Furthermore, π-π stacking was prominently observed between the aromatic ring of guaiacol and hydrophobic amino acid residues (e.g., phenylalanine, tyrosine) within the receptors.

Stereoscopic views illustrate the binding modes of the key smoky odorant guaiacol (green) with human olfactory receptors (OR1G1, orange; OR2T1, light blue; OR10G4, dark green), highlighting direct hydrogen-bond interactions. Planar views depict the hydrophobic contacts and π-π interactions between guaiacol and the olfactory receptors.

These findings with guaiacol suggest analogous opportunities for structural refinement. The identified key interactions serve as direct design targets. For instance, strategic alkylation or methoxylation of the guaiacol scaffold could be pursued to strengthen hydrophobic contacts or introduce new favorable π-π interactions with the receptor's aromatic residues, potentially yielding analogs with significantly lower odor thresholds or altered, more desirable smoky character profiles. Conversely, modifications that disrupt the crucial hydrogen-bonding network would likely diminish activity, providing a strategy to modulate or reduce overly pungent phenolic notes (Niu et al., 2024). A seminal example from vanilla flavor chemistry illustrates this principle: the simple ethylation of vanillin, which effectively adds a single carbon atom to the methoxy group to produce ethyl vanillin, results in a flavor potency approximately three to four times greater than that of vanillin itself (Spence, 2022). This dramatic enhancement is attributed to improved hydrophobic interactions and a more favorable binding orientation within the olfactory receptor binding pocket (Zeng et al., 2023, 2024).

Therefore, the molecular docking establishes a foundational framework for the rational development of next-generation smoky flavorants. By leveraging these structural insights, it becomes possible to engineer molecules that offer enhanced potency, improved stability, or tailored sensory profiles, ultimately enabling more precise and innovative flavor design in food and beverage applications.

Conclusions

This study established an integrated analytical framework that bridges chemical analysis, sensory evaluation, and molecular modeling to decode the complex smoky aroma in diverse commercial products. The combination of targeted GC-MS quantification, matrix-specific odor threshold determination, and OAV calculation successfully shifted the focus from chemical abundance to sensory impact, identifying guaiacol as the indispensable aroma driver across all traditional smoked products. Molecular docking simulations provided initial mechanistic insights, revealing that guaiacol's potent sensory effect arises from specific hydrogen-bonding and π-π interactions with human olfactory receptors (OR1G1, OR2T1, OR10G4). This work offers an actionable, product-specific blueprint of key smoky odorants, establishing perceptually-relevant criteria for quality control, authenticity assessment, and targeted flavor optimization. The elucidated binding motifs of guaiacol present a template for structure-guided modification of smoky aroma molecules, enabling rational design of next-generation flavorants with enhanced potency or tailored sensory profiles.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, while the selected commercial samples represent typical products within each category, the limited number of samples per product type may not capture the full variability arising from differences in raw materials, processing conditions, or geographical origins. Future studies should expand the sample set to include a broader range of products to validate the generalizability of the findings. Second, the molecular docking predictions, while providing valuable structural insights, remain computational in nature and require experimental validation. Further work involving in vitro functional assays (e.g., calcium imaging or cAMP accumulation assays using olfactory receptor-expressing cell lines) is needed to confirm the predicted interactions between guaiacol and the identified olfactory receptors. Addressing these limitations will further strengthen the mechanistic understanding of smoky aroma perception and support the development of more robust flavor design strategies.
