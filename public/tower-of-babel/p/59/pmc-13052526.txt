Cystoid macular oedema after trabeculectomy versus tube shunt surgery in non-diabetic patients
Authors: Abdelrahman M Elhusseiny, Hashem Abu Serhan, Taher K Eleiwa, Mohammad Ayoubi, Reem H ElSheikh, Muhammad Z Chauhan, Sami H Uwaydat, Mohsen Adelpour, Richard K Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo compare the hazard of postoperative cystoid macular oedema (CME) between trabeculectomy and tube shunt implantation, focusing exclusively on non-diabetic patients to minimise confounding factors.MethodsThis is a retrospective cohort study using the TriNetX United States Collaborative Network. Patients with glaucoma who underwent trabeculectomy or tube shunt surgery were identified via International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision and Current Procedural Terminology codes. Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to balance baseline characteristics. The primary outcome was the hazard of CME at 1 and 3 months postoperatively. Adjusted HRs (aHRs) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsAfter 1:1 PSM, 2928 patients were included in each cohort. At 1 month, CME was significantly less frequent in the trabeculectomy group (1.15%) compared with the tube shunt group (1.85%) (p=0.02). This difference widened at 3 months, with CME rates of 2.19% in the trabeculectomy group vs 4.20% in the tube group (p<0.0001). Trabeculectomy was associated with a 35% reduction in CME hazard at 1 month (aHR: 0.64; 95% CI 0.44 to 0.94; p=0.023) and a 45% reduction at 3 months (aHR: 0.55; 95% CI 0.42 to 0.72; p<0.0001) compared with tube shunt surgery. Male sex (aHR: 1.32), topical alpha agonist use (aHR: 1.61) and macular puckering (aHR: 2.75) were independently associated with increased CME risk.ConclusionsTrabeculectomy is associated with a significantly lower hazard of postoperative CME compared with tube shunt surgery in non-diabetic glaucoma patients.

Introduction

Glaucoma remains a leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, necessitating surgical intervention when medical and laser therapies fail to adequately control intraocular pressure (IOP).1 Trabeculectomy and tube shunt implantation are the two most commonly performed filtering glaucoma surgeries, each with established efficacy in reducing IOP and preserving visual function.2 However, both procedures carry risks of postoperative complications that can impact the overall visual outcome. Among these, cystoid macular oedema (CME) is particularly concerning, as it may cause significant and sometimes prolonged visual impairment despite successful IOP control.3 4 Gietzelt et al4 reported that postoperative CME was significantly more frequent after tube shunt implantation (19.4%) than after trabeculectomy (4.8%). Similarly, the Tube versus Trabeculectomy study found that postoperative CME was more common after tube shunt implantation (5%) than after trabeculectomy (2%).5

Cystoid macular oedema following glaucoma surgery has been hypothesised to arise from several mechanisms, including postoperative inflammation, alteration of the blood–retinal barrier and changes in intraocular fluid dynamics.2 6 Although CME has been reported as a postoperative complication of glaucoma surgery, direct comparative analyses of CME hazard between trabeculectomy and tube shunt implantation remain limited.5 Existing literature offers limited insight due to heterogeneous patient populations, lack of adjustment for confounding factors, small sample sizes, and minimal focus on CME as a primary surgical outcome.5710 Differences in surgical technique, postoperative wound healing, inflammation levels and adjunctive therapies (such as antifibrotic agents in trabeculectomy or tube material-related inflammation in shunt surgery) could plausibly influence the relative risk of CME between these procedures.2 3 11

By leveraging the breadth and depth of multicentre electronic health record data, researchers can obtain more generalisable estimates of complication rates and identify meaningful differences between surgical procedures. However, such analyses should carefully account for potential confounding factors inherent to observational data, such as baseline disease severity, comorbidities and variations in surgical indications and techniques.12

In this study, we used a large real-world dataset to perform a comparative analysis of the hazard of CME following trabeculectomy versus tube shunt implantation in non-diabetic patients. By doing so, we hope to provide evidence that can support individualised surgical decision-making and enhance visual prognostication for glaucoma patients undergoing incisional filtering glaucoma surgery.

Materials and methods

This retrospective cohort study used deidentified patient data sourced from the TriNetX platform, a global health research network encompassing information from over 250 million individuals across more than 30 countries. The dataset, contributed by nearly 100 healthcare institutions worldwide, includes demographic, clinical, laboratory and genomic variables. For this analysis, records were obtained in March 2025 from 69 healthcare organisations (HCOs) affiliated with the United States (U.S.) Collaborative network (figure 1).

Flow chart illustrating the categorisation of a study population divided based on the type of filtering surgery.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria and study groups

Patients were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes. TriNetX uses a federated real-world data platform that harmonises diagnostic codes to common standards. As part of this process, ICD-9 codes are internally converted to their ICD-10-CM equivalents to ensure consistency and comparability of clinical data across HCOs. The study population included individuals aged 18 years or older with a documented coded diagnosis of glaucoma (ICD-10: H40–H42) who underwent their first filtering procedure—either trabeculectomy or tube shunt implantation. These procedures were identified using Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes: 66 170 for trabeculectomy and 66 179 or 66 180 for tube shunt placement. In the trabeculectomy group, patients with a history of or who underwent tube shunt surgery during the study period were excluded. Similarly, in the tube group, we excluded patients with a history of or who underwent trabeculectomy during the study period. Patients who underwent cataract surgery on the same day as trabeculectomy or tube shunt implantation were excluded to minimise confounding related to cataract surgery-associated CME.

To minimise confounding, patients with systemic or ocular conditions known to elevate the risk of CME were excluded. These conditions included diabetes mellitus (ICD-10 E08–E13), central or branch retinal vein occlusion (ICD-10 H34.81, H34.83), venous engorgement (ICD-10 H34.82), exudative age-related macular degeneration (ICD-10 H35.32), degenerative myopia with choroidal neovascularisation (ICD-10 H44.2A), and uveitis (ICD-10 H20, H30), or retinal vasculitis (H35.06). Patients with a history of ocular trauma (ICD-10 S05), pars plana vitrectomy (CPT 67036, 67042) or trans-scleral cyclophotocoagulation (CPT 66710) were also excluded, as were individuals with a previous diagnosis of CME prior to the index date. Additionally, we have matched patients on the type of glaucoma, whether open-angle glaucoma (H40.1) or primary angle-closure glaucoma (H40.2).

The index date was defined as the earliest date a patient satisfied all inclusion criteria within the study period, which spanned from 1 January 2004 to 1 January 2024. Eligible patients were required to have undergone at least one ophthalmic examination within the first postoperative month and another between 1 and 3 months postsurgery to ensure adequate follow-up.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this research. This study was based exclusively on deidentified electronic health record data obtained from the TriNetX research network. Given the retrospective nature and reliance on anonymised records, there was no direct opportunity for patient or public involvement in developing the research question, selecting outcomes, designing the study or interpreting the findings.

Propensity score matching

To minimise confounding, propensity score matching (PSM) was used to achieve comparable baseline characteristics between patients undergoing trabeculectomy and those receiving tube shunt surgery. The matching procedure incorporated a wide range of demographic and clinical factors, including age, sex, race, ethnicity, nicotine dependence, systemic comorbidities and concurrent use of both topical and systemic medications.

Systemic conditions included in the matching algorithm comprised hypertensive disorders (ICD-10 I10–I1A), chronic kidney disease (ICD-10 N18), systemic lupus erythematosus (ICD-10 M32), inflammatory polyarthropathies (ICD-10 M05–M14), and multiple sclerosis (ICD-10 G35). Prior ocular history was also considered, including macular puckering (ICD-10 H35.37), prior cataract extraction—with or without minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS) (CPT codes 66982, 66984, 66987, 66988, 66989, 66991) and previous goniotomy procedures (CPT 65820).

Medication-related variables encompassed both topical IOP-lowering therapies such as beta-blockers, alpha agonists, prostaglandin analogues, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, and netarsudil. Additionally, systemic medications that could potentially influence the risk of postoperative CME were included—these comprised systemic beta-blockers, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, calcium channel blockers (CCBs), corticosteroids, fingolimod, taxanes (eg, docetaxel), niacin, tamoxifen and interferons.

To mitigate potential detection bias and account for differences in frequency of eye visits, the analysis integrated ophthalmologic visit CPT codes (92002, 92004, 92012, 92014) into the matching model. Propensity score matching was executed using a 1:1 nearest neighbour method with a calliper of 0.1 SD. The effectiveness of the matching process was evaluated by comparing standardised differences for all covariates before and after matching.

Clinical endpoints and statistical analysis

The primary clinical endpoints of this study were the cumulative incidences of CME (ICD-10 H35.35) occurring within 1 and 3 months following glaucoma surgery. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to estimate the probability of CME development over time in each surgical cohort.

To address potential biases resulting from the exclusion of unmatched individuals during PSM, a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model analysis was also conducted. This secondary model adjusted for previously outlined covariates to test the consistency of the findings despite the reduction in sample size. Adjusted HRs (aHRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for each outcome, accounting for adjusted covariates.

Descriptive statistics were applied to characterise the study population at baseline. Continuous variables were summarised as means with SDs, while categorical variables were expressed as percentages. All statistical procedures were conducted through the TriNetX analytics platform, and a two-sided p<0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Prior to PSM, the trabeculectomy group consisted of 3419 patients, while the tube shunt group included 6397 individuals. Following matching, 2928 patients were retained in each cohort, with baseline characteristics well-balanced across most variables. The average age at the index date was 68.6 ± 13.4 years for the trabeculectomy group and 69 ± 14.7 years for the tube group. The gender distribution was comparable, with males comprising 47.5% of the trabeculectomy group and 47.0% of the tube group, while females made up 52.5% and 53.0%, respectively.

Racial and ethnic demographics were similar between groups. Among those who underwent trabeculectomy, 65.8% were White, 18.1% Black or African American and 3.5% Asian. Additionally, 83.3% identified as non-Hispanic or Latino, and 5.2% as Hispanic or Latino. In comparison, the tube shunt group included 67.5% White, 17.5% Black or African American and 3.4% Asian participants, with 84.2% identifying as non-Hispanic or Latino and 5.3% as Hispanic or Latino.

After PSM matching, comorbidity profiles were well-balanced. Hypertension was present in 43.6% of the trabeculectomy group and 44.1% of the tube group. Inflammatory polyarthropathies were found in 9.2% of both groups. Chronic kidney disease occurred in 4.8% of each group. Conditions such as multiple sclerosis and systemic lupus erythematosus were rare (less than 1%) and evenly distributed. Macular puckering was documented in 6.5% of trabeculectomy cases and 5.8% of tube cases.

Medication usage was also closely controlled. Corticosteroid use was reported in 79.9% of trabeculectomy patients and 80.9% of tube shunt patients. Topical β-blockers were used in 64.6% and 64.9%, and prostaglandin analogues in 64.0% and 64.3%, respectively. Brimonidine was prescribed in 50.9% of the trabeculectomy group and 52.3% of the tube group. Dorzolamide usage was 47.8% vs 48.1%, and acetazolamide use was 17.5% vs 20.1%. Netarsudil was prescribed in 11.6% of trabeculectomy patients and 11.4% of tube patients. Other systemic medications such as niacin, tamoxifen, docetaxel, taxanes and interferons were used infrequently (< 5%) and similarly between groups.

Procedural histories were equally matched. Standard cataract surgery had been performed in 30.9% of the trabeculectomy group and 30.7% of the tube group. Complex cataract procedures occurred in 7.9% and 8.2%, respectively. Goniotomy was performed in 3.2% of trabeculectomy cases and 3.3% of tube shunt cases.

Cystoid macular oedema after trabeculectomy versus tube

At 1 month after surgery, CME was significantly less frequent in the trabeculectomy group (1.15%) compared with the tube shunt group (1.85%) (p=0.02). This difference became more pronounced at the 3-month mark (2.19% in the trabeculectomy group vs 4.20% in the tube group, p<0.0001). Cox proportional hazards analysis showed that trabeculectomy was associated with a significantly reduced hazard of developing CME within the first month postoperatively (aHR: 0.64; 95% CI 0.44 to 0.94; p=0.023). This effect remained significant at the 3-month time point (aHR: 0.55; 95% CI 0.42 to 0.72; p<0.0001).

Additional factors independently associated with an increased hazard of postoperative CME at 3 months postoperatively included male sex (aHR: 1.32; 95% CI 1.05 to 1.65; p=0.01), the use of topical alpha agonists (aHR: 1.61; 95% CI 1.15 to 2.26; p<0.01), and the presence of macular puckering (aHR: 2.75; 95% CI 1.99 to 3.79; p<0.01). Other evaluated variables, including topical prostaglandin analogues, systemic and topical beta-blockers, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, netarsudil, systemic CCBs, hypertension, inflammatory polyarthropathies and systemic lupus erythematosus, were not significantly associated with postoperative CME hazard.

Discussion

In this large-scale, retrospective cohort study, we demonstrated that trabeculectomy carries a significantly lower hazard of postoperative CME compared with tube shunt implantation in non-diabetic glaucoma patients. This association remained consistent at both 1 and 3 months postoperatively and held up under multivariable adjustment. To our knowledge, our study provides the largest comparative analysis of CME development between these two procedures, directly addressing several limitations found in earlier research.

Our findings align with those reported in the literature, including studies by Gietzelt et al,4 Bhakta et al3 and Serhan et al,2 all of which similarly suggested a reduced risk of CME following trabeculectomy compared with tube shunt surgeries. These studies collectively support the notion that trabeculectomy may offer a more favourable postoperative outcome with respect to the development of CME. However, despite this emerging trend, previous research in this area has notable limitations. Most prior studies were retrospective and based on small, single-centre cohorts and frequently included patients undergoing combined procedures such as cataract extraction, thereby restricting statistical power and generalisability. Additionally, diabetic patients—who carry an inherently elevated baseline risk for CME—were often included without stratification, thereby complicating the interpretation of surgery-specific risks. Many analyses also lacked rigorous control for systemic comorbidities and medication exposures, which can influence inflammatory responses and CME development.5 7 9 10 13

In contrast, our study provides the most comprehensive and methodologically rigorous evaluation to date of surgery-specific CME risk in glaucoma patients. We addressed prior limitations by excluding individuals with diabetes mellitus, wet age-related macular degeneration, prior CME (including postcataract CME), and ocular trauma, all of which represent major sources of confounding variables. Additionally, we minimised inter-eye correlation bias by including only patients who underwent trabeculectomy without prior ipsilateral or contralateral tube implantation, and vice versa. We also employed robust PSM across systemic, ocular and pharmacologic variables in one of the largest real-world multicentre cohorts to date. By isolating the effect of surgical modality in a non-diabetic population and defining CME as a primary outcome, our study offers a refined, high-validity comparison that advances current understanding and provides clinically actionable insights for glaucoma surgical decision-making and patient selection for the type of glaucoma surgery.

Bhakta et al3 evaluated 185 glaucoma patients who underwent tube surgery and reported a 22% incidence of CME, whereas our study found a markedly lower rate of approximately 4%. This discrepancy likely stems from differences in study design. Specifically, we excluded high-risk patients and relied on diagnostic codes, which may underdetect milder cases of CME. While the rate of CME varies between studies, the central finding of our analysis lies in the adjusted comparison between trabeculectomy and tube surgery.

Our findings align with earlier reports suggesting higher postoperative inflammation and prolonged wound healing associated with tube shunt devices may predispose to greater CME hazard.14 Tube shunt surgery involves the implantation of a foreign body into the eye, which may lead to chronic inflammation and increased cytokine release, both of which can compromise the blood-retinal barrier and promote fluid accumulation in the macula. This inflammation is further exacerbated by the mechanical irritation of the implanted tube, which may persist for a longer period than the healing process after trabeculectomy.11 15 Conversely, trabeculectomy, while also associated with some inflammation, may induce a less persistent inflammatory milieu, leading to reduced hazard of CME.

In our study, male sex was found to be an independent risk factor associated with a higher hazard of developing CME 3 months postoperatively. This finding is consistent with some previous literature suggesting that male patients may be more predisposed to certain inflammatory processes following ocular surgery.1618 The exact mechanism underlying this association remains unclear, but it may relate to sex-based differences in immune response, hormonal influences or the presence of other unmeasured or unknown factors that affect postoperative healing.18

The use of topical alpha agonists was identified as an independent risk factor for an increased likelihood of developing CME 3 months postoperatively. This aligns with findings from recent literature, which suggests that alpha agonists may contribute to an inflammatory response that predisposes the macula to oedema.19 20 In contrast, the use of topical prostaglandin analogues did not show a similar association with postoperative CME, consistent with a study by Zhou et al19 and Niyadurupola et al,21 which compared the incidence of acute CME after initiating prostaglandin analogues versus other glaucoma medications. Their findings suggested that prostaglandin analogues were not significantly associated with an increased risk of CME, supporting the notion that their inflammatory effect was minimal.19 21

Our findings also revealed that the presence of macular puckering is an independent risk factor for the development of CME postoperatively. Macular puckering, characterised by the formation of epiretinal membranes, can disrupt normal retinal architecture and increase the vulnerability of the macula to oedema.22 This condition is often associated with vitreoretinal interface abnormalities, which may contribute to compromised retinal function and an increased likelihood of CME following glaucoma surgery.23 24

Our analysis found that systemic CCBs were not an independent risk factor for postoperative CME. However, CCBs have been associated with reduced retinal thickness. Danni et al25 reported that systemic use of CCBs was associated with a decrease in central retinal thickness after cataract surgery, suggesting that CCBs may have a protective effect on retinal tissue by reducing inflammation or vascular changes. While our study did not identify a direct association between CCB use and CME, these findings highlight the need for further investigation into the potential impact of CCBs on postoperative retinal health. It is possible that systemic CCBs may have paradoxically masked the clinical presentation of CME in our cohort.

While this study leverages a large, multi-institutional, real-world dataset and applies robust PSM to control for confounding, several limitations are acknowledged. First, as a retrospective observational analysis, the design inherently precludes definitive causal inference. The reliance on ICD-10 and CPT codes may lead to outcome misclassification, particularly for milder or subclinical cases of CME, and may vary based on documentation practices across institutions. However, this is a limitation of all claims-based data analysis.19 20 Although we applied a strict diagnostic code-based definition, excluded diabetic patients and matched for high-risk comorbidities, variability in coding precision and clinical thresholds for diagnosing CME may have affected case ascertainment. A broader limitation in the postoperative CME literature is the inconsistency in diagnostic practices, some studies implement routine optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging postoperatively,22 while others, particularly retrospective real-world studies, perform OCT only in patients with decreased vision.26 Consequently, subclinical or visually insignificant cases may be underdiagnosed or missed entirely, contributing to the variability in reported CME rates across studies.27

Second, TriNetX does not provide access to clinical imaging (eg, OCT or fluorescein angiography scans), which limits validation of CME diagnoses or stratification by severity. Nor does it include patient-reported outcomes such as visual function or postoperative symptom burden, which are increasingly relevant for surgical outcome evaluation. Additionally, it is unclear if the postoperative CME was visually and clinically significant.

Medication-related factors also represent an important limitation. While medication records in TriNetX reflect prescriptions or dispensations, they do not guarantee patient adherence, particularly relevant for topical ophthalmic medications that may influence postoperative inflammation and recovery. As with most large real-world ophthalmic and population-based databases, including eye-specific registries such as the American Academy of Ophthalmology IRIS Registry, detailed information on medication duration and cumulative exposure was not available, limiting our ability to assess the effect of prolonged prostaglandin analogue use on postoperative outcomes. Similarly, adherence to postoperative anti-inflammatory therapy could not be verified. Because CME was evaluated during the acute postoperative period (within 3 months of surgery), variability in compliance with prescribed anti-inflammatory eye-drops may have influenced postoperative inflammation and the observed risk of CME.

Third, we lacked access to detailed surgical data, including intraoperative variables (eg, use of antimetabolites, implant type, surgical duration) and surgeon experience, which could influence postoperative inflammation and CME risk. We also could not account for environmental or behavioural factors that may modify healing or inflammatory profiles, such as smoking, systemic medication compliance or local follow-up intensity. Also, the dataset lacks granular disease severity data, so differences in glaucoma severity (which could influence surgical choice and inflammation) may persist despite matching.

Although our cohort was diverse, the data were limited to the TriNetX U.S. Collaborative Network and may not be generalisable to populations in other healthcare systems or in countries with differing treatment patterns, surgical training standards or genetic backgrounds. Additionally, our analysis was restricted to non-diabetic patients to minimise confounding and better isolate the association between the surgical procedure and postoperative CME; therefore, the findings may not be generalisable to individuals with diabetes. Finally, we included a heterogeneous group of glaucoma patients with different glaucoma subtypes and severities. Despite these limitations, by leveraging a multicentre electronic health record platform and applying rigorous PSM and Cox hazards model adjusting for confounding factors, our analysis provides one of the most robust comparisons to date, minimising confounding from systemic and ocular comorbidities. The exclusion of diabetic patients and other high-risk retinal conditions further strengthens the surgical specificity of our findings.

In conclusion, this large, real-world cohort study of non-diabetic glaucoma patients revealed that trabeculectomy was associated with a significantly lower hazard of developing postoperative CME compared with tube shunt implantation. However, future prospective studies incorporating imaging-based endpoints are warranted to validate these results and to further clarify the visual significance and clinical outcomes of this complication.
