TAVR Using a Premounted Dry Valve in a Patient With Severe Aortic Stenosis and Tortuous Descending Aorta
Authors: Siyang Fan, Fei Yuan, Xinmin Liu, Yunfeng Yan, Ziwei Xi, Jing Yao, Guangyuan Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTranscatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) with wet tissue valves have several limitations that compromise their durability and lifespan, and it is technically challenging in patients with a tortuous descending aorta.Case SummaryWe report an 81-year-old woman with severe symptomatic aortic stenosis, characterized by a functionally bicuspid aortic valve with extensive calcification, horizontal heart configuration. and tortuous descending aorta. The patient underwent successful transfemoral TAVR using a premounted dry valve system with cerebral embolic protection and a minimalist approach.DiscussionThis case confirms the feasibility of long sheath-assisted TAVR using a premounted dry valve system. Given the availability of premounted dry valves in China, the use of a premounted dry valve with potentially enhanced durability was chosen to avoid the risk of reintervention in this elderly patient.Take-Home MessageSuccessful TAVR using a premounted dry valve system in patients with complex anatomy requires meticulous preprocedural planning, advanced imaging analysis, and appropriate device selection.

History of Presentation

An 81-year-old woman presented with a 6-month history of progressive exertional dyspnea that had acutely worsened over the preceding 20 days. She reported orthopnea and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea requiring her to sleep in an upright position. Despite initial diuretic therapy at a local hospital, her symptoms persisted, prompting referral to our center for further evaluation and management.

On initial evaluation, the patient appeared hemodynamically stable with evidence of heart failure. Physical examination revealed a late-peaking systolic ejection murmur radiating to the carotid arteries, bilateral pulmonary rales, and peripheral edema.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated severe aortic stenosis with a peak velocity of 4.66 m/s, mean transaortic gradient of 53 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction of 40%. Additional findings included mild to moderate aortic regurgitation and mild to moderate tricuspid regurgitation. Left ventricular hypertrophy and enlargement were also noted.

Preprocedural computed tomography angiography revealed several critical anatomical features. The patient has a functionally bicuspid aortic valve with calcific fusion of the right-noncoronary cusp commissure. Anatomical assessment revealed a mean aortic annulus diameter of 22.9 mm and a left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) diameter of 23.8 mm (Figures 1A and 1B). Calcification assessment demonstrated severe calcification (HU-850 volume: 1,239 mm3) (Figure 1C), concentrated in the noncoronary sinus and extending from the LVOT to the edges of the noncoronary leaflet, causing significant supra-annular restriction. The zone of most significant supra-annular constraint was at 2 mm above the annular plane, exhibiting a mean diameter of 21.4 mm. Supra-annular dimensions showed progressive dilation, increasing from 21.4 mm at 2 mm to 24.9 mm at 10 mm above the annulus (Figure 1D). Regarding coronary risk assessment, the left coronary ostium height was relatively low; however, because of the short leaflet length and adequate sinus dimensions, the risk of coronary obstruction appeared low (Figures 1E and 1F). The cardiac angle was 64°, consistent with a horizontal heart (Figure 1G). The membranous septum length was 2.4 mm, indicating an increased risk for permanent pacemaker implantation (Figure 1H). The ascending aorta length was 57.4 mm (Figure 1H), which was shorter than the typical delivery system capsule length, potentially posing challenges for coaxial alignment during system advancement. Vascular access assessment demonstrated a favorable aortic arch angle with minimal calcification. However, the descending aorta showed significant tortuosity (Figure 1I). Bilateral iliofemoral arteries were atherosclerotic but adequate in caliber. Management

Given the patient's advanced age, high surgical risk, and complex anatomy, the multidisciplinary heart team recommended long large-bore sheath–assisted transfemoral TAVR with cerebral embolic protection device (Figure 2A). The procedure was performed under conscious sedation using a minimalist approach. Right femoral access was obtained, and a 65-cm large-bore sheath was advanced to the descending aorta across the tortuous part of the descending aorta. Notable sheath angulation was observed in the thoracic aorta because of the patient's anatomical configuration (Figure 2B). A cerebral embolic protection device was deployed via the right radial artery before valve manipulation. Third-degree atrioventricular block occurred immediately after the stiff wire entered the left ventricle and compressed the right-noncoronary commissure, necessitating temporary pacing at 60 beats/min. An 18-mm balloon was used for predilatation, demonstrating significant calcific restriction (Figure 2C). The snare could not be advanced given kinking of the large-caliber sheath, thus the native valve was crossed using a 26-mm premounted dry valve (Prostyle A, KingstronBio) with difficulty, where resistance was encountered at the right-noncoronary commissure region because of heavy calcification (Figure 2D). Initial positioning was targeted at approximately 2 mm below the annular plane to account for anticipated downward movement during deployment (Figure 2E). During valve expansion, the prosthesis migrated approximately 2 mm inferiorly, resulting in a final position at the intended annular level (Figure 2F). Assessment in the working projection confirmed optimal positioning with mild paravalvular leak localized to the noncoronary sinus region, consistent with the area of severe calcification. After valve implantation, angiography of the aortic root and descending thoracic aorta revealed no evidence of significant vascular dissection or rupture (Figure 2G). Outcome

The patient tolerated the procedure well without immediate complications except for third-degree atrioventricular block. Postprocedural echocardiography confirmed satisfactory valve function with mild paravalvular regurgitation. Given the short membranous septum (2.4 mm) and calcification at the right-noncoronary cusp commissure, the patient ultimately required permanent pacemaker implantation because of persistent third-degree atrioventricular block before discharge. At follow-up evaluation, she reported resolution of orthopnea and significant improvement in exercise tolerance.

Discussion

This case highlights several important considerations for TAVR in patients with complex aortic valve anatomy, particularly regarding valve technology selection in patients with extensive native valve calcification and tortuous access vessels.

The presence of severe aortic valve calcification is associated with higher rates of paravalvular regurgitation and annular rupture.1 The presence of extensive calcification extending from the LVOT to the aortic annulus creates a “barrel-shaped” configuration with significant supra-annular restriction. In this case, the relatively preserved annular dimension (22.9 mm) contrasted with the restricted supra-annular area (21.4 mm at 2 mm), necessitating careful valve sizing to achieve adequate expansion while avoiding annular rupture. Given the patient's potentially >10 years of remaining life expectancy, the dry valve anticalcification technology offers theoretical advantages in delaying structural valve degeneration, supporting a “lifecycle management” strategy. The 18-F low-profile delivery system with distal lubricious coating allowed successful traversal of the tortuous descending aorta without vascular injury. And the supra-annular design enables the valve to sit above the calcified native annulus and LVOT, avoiding supra-annular restriction. The low-profile stent design provides balanced radial force for asymmetric calcification.

The use of a premounted dry valve system treated with a specialized anticalcification technology in this case offers distinct advantages relevant to both immediate procedural success and long-term durability (Table 2). Unlike traditional glutaraldehyde fixed bioprosthetic valves that remain stored in liquid preservative solutions, dry valve technology employs advanced tissue processing that eliminates the need for liquid storage. This dry storage approach preserves the native collagen structure more effectively and reduces calcification potential through several mechanisms. First, the absence of liquid storage minimizes aldehyde-induced tissue damage and reduces residual glutaraldehyde content, which has been implicated in chronic inflammatory responses and subsequent calcification. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that dry-processed pericardial tissue exhibits significantly reduced calcium deposition compared to conventional wet-stored valves, with potential implications for extended valve longevity.2 Second, the dry valve platform facilitates premounting on the delivery system, enabling more precise crimping profiles and potentially reducing leaflet trauma during deployment. In this case of severe asymmetric calcification, the ability to achieve controlled expansion and optimal apposition against the irregular calcified surface was critical. The dry valve's enhanced conformability may improve sealing in the presence of asymmetric calcium distribution, as observed in the noncoronary sinus region. Third, for an 81-year-old patient with life expectancy potentially exceeding 10 years, valve durability represents a significant consideration. While current TAVR platforms have demonstrated satisfactory performance up to 5 years, concerns regarding structural valve deterioration remain, particularly in younger patients and those with significant residual native valve calcification. The anticalcification properties inherent to dry valve technology may offer theoretical benefits in reducing the risk of structural valve degeneration, though long-term clinical data are still emerging. The reduced calcification potential could translate to slower progression of leaflet stiffening and preserved hemodynamic performance over time. A pivotal study that enrolled 120 high-surgical-risk, severe aortic stenosis patients from 11 centers in China demonstrated low mortality (10.9%) and complication rates (27.7%) and sustained improvements in hemodynamics (mean gradient: 13.2 mm Hg) and cardiac function of the Prostyle system at 2-year follow up.3 The system was officially approved for market by China's National Medical Products Administration in April 2025. Potential Advantages and Current Limitations of This Technology

Vascular injury is a significant and common complication of the TAVR procedure. Vascular complication rates as high as 30.7% were documented in the pivotal North American PARTNER 1B trial when large 22-F to 24-F sheaths were used for transfemoral access.4 Many cases of severe aortic stenosis complicated by aortic tortuosity have been reported in which TAVR was successfully performed without significant vascular complications.5, 6, 7 The use of a 65-cm-long sheath in the present case represented a critical technical decision for managing the patient's significantly tortuous descending aorta. The long sheath provided continuous coaxial support throughout the severely angulated descending thoracic aorta, effectively creating a smooth “superhighway” for valve delivery system advancement. This is particularly relevant given the observed “significant angulation” and “large bend” in the thoracic aorta after sheath placement. Without adequate sheath support, the relatively stiff delivery system would have directly contacted the aortic wall at multiple angulation points, risking intimal injury, plaque disruption, or even aortic dissection in the setting of atherosclerotic disease. By extending the working channel closer to the aortic arch, the long sheath minimized friction between the delivery system and the native aortic wall. This reduction in resistance facilitated more controlled and predictable valve advancement, which was essential given the horizontal heart configuration (64°) and need for precise coaxial alignment. The improved pushability translated to enhanced maneuverability during the critical phase of valve crossing through the heavily calcified native valve. The patient exhibited diffuse iliofemoral atherosclerosis and a “large bend” in the descending aorta—anatomy that predisposes to procedural complications including vascular injury, device entrapment, and embolization of aortic debris. The 65-cm sheath length allowed the sheath tip to be positioned in the proximal descending thoracic aorta, bypassing the most severe tortuosity and providing a stable platform from which the delivery system could navigate the remaining aortic arch and valvular anatomy. This strategy effectively converted a complex, high-friction delivery path into a more manageable procedure. It is noteworthy that despite the protective benefits, the long sheath itself exhibited significant angulation when positioned in the thoracic aorta, confirming the severity of the patient's native anatomy. This observation underscores the importance of careful sheath selection and positioning—adequate length must be balanced against the potential for sheath kinking or collapse if advanced too far into an angulated segment. In this case, the 65-cm length appeared optimal, providing sufficient support while maintaining sheath integrity. The “all-in-one” approach using a single large-bore access site for both the long sheath and subsequent valve delivery further minimized vascular trauma by avoiding multiple access sites in already diseased iliofemoral vessels.8

Conclusions

Successful TAVR in patients with severe calcification, horizontal heart, and complex root geometry and tortuous descending aorta requires meticulous preprocedural planning, advanced imaging analysis, and appropriate device selection. The integration of cerebral protection, premounted dry valve technology, and minimalist techniques can facilitate safe and effective treatment in this challenging patient population.
