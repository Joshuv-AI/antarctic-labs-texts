Cholesterol-Inflammation Fusion Hypothesis in Atherosclerosis
Authors: Rui-Xiang Zeng, Sha Li, Jian-Jun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Atherosclerosis (AS) remains the leading cause of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality worldwide despite advances in multidimensional prevention and treatment. We propose a novel cholesterol-inflammation fusion hypothesis, a unifying framework that conceptualizes AS as a self-perpetuating disease driven by the bidirectional and synergistic interplay between dysregulated cholesterol metabolism and chronic vascular inflammation. Mechanistically, cholesterol crystals, oxidized low-density lipoprotein, and aggregated low-density lipoprotein promote macrophage and vascular smooth muscle cell lipid accumulation and activate inflammatory signaling, whereas cytokines impair cholesterol efflux and amplify lipid accumulation. Clinical evidence demonstrates that controlling either lipid or inflammatory pathways alone leaves residual risk, whereas simultaneous regulation yields the greatest benefit. This paradigm provides a conceptual basis for dual-target therapeutic strategies. This review outlines mechanistic insights and translational implications of this fusion hypothesis, aiming to guide future precision risk stratification and therapy design in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.

The landscape of atherosclerosis (AS) research has undergone profound changes over the past decades. Although progress in the prevention and therapeutics of AS has been made in multiple ways, the current situation is still not satisfactory, which may be because of insufficient understanding regarding the underlying mechanisms. Two dominant paradigms, the cholesterol hypothesis and the inflammation hypothesis have long competed for primacy.1,2 The cholesterol hypothesis, deeply embedded in clinical guidelines, established low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) as a central culprit of plaque initiation and progression. Remnant cholesterol has also been recognized as an important atherogenic lipid fraction that contributes to residual cardiovascular risk.3 In parallel, the inflammation hypothesis reframed AS as a maladaptive immune response, emphasizing cytokine signaling, immune-cell recruitment, and chronic vascular injury.1 Each perspective has generated transformative therapies, statins on one side, canakinumab and colchicine on the other; however, both paradigms fall short of fully eliminating cardiovascular risk. This persistent residual risk raises a fundamental question: is AS a cholesterol disease complicated by inflammation, or an inflammatory disease fueled by cholesterol, or rather are both inseparably fused?

Despite remarkable advances in lipid-lowering strategies, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) remains the leading global cause of death.2 This residual risk underscores the limitations of single-pathway approaches and compels a search for new conceptual frameworks. Emerging evidence reveals that cholesterol and inflammation are not parallel contributors but intertwined processes. Intracellular cholesterol oversaturation leads to formation of cholesterol crystals, which pierce lysosomal membranes and activate inflammasomes, whereas inflammatory cytokines impair cholesterol efflux and entrap lipids within the arterial wall.4, 5, 6, 7 Once established, this vicious cycle of metabolic and immune disruption becomes remarkably resistant to conventional monotherapies.

Contemporary evidence now reveals that lipids and inflammation are not merely parallel contributors but rather dance in a carefully choreographed, mutually reinforcing biological interplay.1 Although prior work in vascular immunology and immunometabolism has elucidated important interactions between lipid metabolism and inflammation, these processes have largely been discussed as interlinked yet conceptually separable, and no existing framework has systematically unified them.1,7 Here, we propose the cholesterol-inflammation fusion hypothesis, which advances existing frameworks by explicitly conceptualizing AS as a self-sustaining, bidirectional fusion loop in which dysregulated cholesterol metabolism and chronic vascular inflammation become functionally inseparable. This fusion model emphasizes not only molecular cross talk but also cellular convergence across macrophages and vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), and introduces the notion of fusion risk as a distinct and clinically relevant form of residual cardiovascular risk.

This article aims to go beyond summarizing evidence. Instead, we argue that recognizing AS as a fusion disease provides a paradigm shift with direct clinical consequences. It compels us to redefine residual cardiovascular risk as fusion risk, to integrate lipid and inflammatory biomarkers for precision stratification, and to design dual-target therapies that disrupt the cycle at its vulnerable nodes. Hence, we outline the conceptual principles, mechanistic insights, clinical validation, and therapeutic implications of the cholesterol-inflammation fusion hypothesis, while also highlighting the challenges and research priorities that will determine its translational impact.

The cholesterol hypothesis can be traced back to Anitschkow’s seminal experiments in 1913, in which cholesterol-fed rabbits developed arterial lesions resembling human AS.8 This discovery positioned cholesterol as the central initiator of plaque formation and laid the foundation for subsequent lipid-centric interventions. Decades later, large-scale randomized controlled trials such as the 4S (Scandinavian Simvastatin Survival Study)9 and the HPS (Heart Protection Study)10 validated that lowering LDL-C reduces cardiovascular events, embedding cholesterol reduction into clinical guidelines worldwide.

In parallel, the inflammation hypothesis emerged as another explanatory paradigm. In the late 20th century, Ross11 famously characterized AS as an inflammatory disease, reframing vascular lesions as chronic immune responses to endothelial injury. This perspective gained decisive clinical support in the 21st century, most notably from the CANTOS (Canakinumab Anti-inflammatory Thrombosis Outcomes Study), which demonstrated that targeted inhibition of interleukin (IL)-1β reduces cardiovascular events independent of lipid lowering.12 Together, these findings established inflammation as not merely an epiphenomenon but a causal driver of disease progression.

Over time, however, it has become clear that neither cholesterol-centric nor inflammation-centric models fully capture the complexity of AS. Residual cardiovascular risk persists despite optimal lipid lowering, and anti-inflammatory strategies alone cannot replace cholesterol control.1 Recent advances in molecular biology and single-cell technologies further reveal that lipid accumulation and immune activation are deeply intertwined, converging on common cellular pathways such as inflammasome activation, impaired cholesterol efflux, and maladaptive macrophage phenotypes.13, 14, 15

Thus, the historical trajectory of AS research reflects a gradual convergence: from a unidimensional lipid model to a parallel recognition of inflammation, and ultimately toward the emerging realization that these processes are inseparably linked. This evolution sets the stage for the cholesterol-inflammation fusion hypothesis, which conceptualizes AS as a self-amplifying disease driven by the reciprocal reinforcement of metabolic and immune dysfunction.

Cholesterol-Inflammation Fusion Hypothesis: Concept and Principles

The cholesterol-inflammation fusion hypothesis conceptualizes AS as a disease in which dysregulated cholesterol metabolism and chronic vascular inflammation are inseparably intertwined. Traditional models have treated cholesterol and inflammation as parallel contributors. However, emerging evidence demonstrates that these processes are reciprocally reinforcing, forming a self-sustaining cycle that drives plaque initiation, progression, and destabilization.12,16,17

As shown in Figure 2A, in this framework, cholesterol functions as both substrate and signal. Excess low-density lipoprotein (LDL) and its modified forms, such as oxidized LDL (ox-LDL) and aggregated LDL (agLDL), accumulate within the intima and act as danger-associated molecular patterns, promoting foam cell formation and inflammatory signaling. In contrast, intracellular accumulation of free cholesterol derived from lysosomal hydrolysis of LDL cholesteryl esters can precipitate into cholesterol crystals, which also trigger innate immune activation.4,18, 19 In parallel, inflammatory mediators impair lipid handling: cytokines such as IL-1β and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α downregulate ATP-binding cassette transporters (ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 [ABCA1] and ATP-binding cassette transporter G1 [ABCG1]), disrupt high-density lipoprotein (HDL)-mediated efflux, and enhance lipid trapping within macrophages and VSMCs, which also contribute significantly to foam-cell formation in plaques.20, 21, 22, 23, 24 These reciprocal processes are not additive but synergistic, locking the vascular wall into a pathologic state resistant to monotherapy.Figure 2Frame and Pathway of the Cholesterol-Inflammation Fusion Hypothesis(A) Overview of bidirectional cholesterol-inflammation cross talk. ox-LDL and cholesterol crystals activate inflammatory pathways and cytokine release, which in turn impair cholesterol efflux and promote lipid accumulation, forming a self-reinforcing cycle that drives atherosclerosis. The principles of the fusion hypothesis can be summarized as follows: 1) bidirectionality: cholesterol drives inflammation, and inflammation impairs cholesterol handling; 2) self-perpetuation: the cycle of lipid deposition and immune activation sustains itself once established; and 3) residual risk as fusion risk: the persistence of cardiovascular events despite LDL-lowering or anti-inflammatory therapy reflects failure to disrupt the fusion loop.

This paradigm shift compels a redefinition of ASCVD pathogenesis: from a dual-factor model to a fusion disease model, with direct implications for risk assessment, biomarker integration, and dual-target therapeutic design.

Mechanistic Insights: A Tangled Web of Interactions

Mechanistic studies reveal a dense web of molecular and cellular interactions that underpin the fusion hypothesis. These interactions can be organized into 3 major fusion nodes: inflammasome activation, cytokine-driven lipid dysregulation, and macrophages that simultaneously exhibit lipid accumulation and proinflammatory activity, together with contributions from VSMCs and endothelial cells (Figure 2B).

Cholesterol as inflammatory instigator

If we consider the arterial wall as a battleground, lipids act as both combatants and provocateurs. When cholesterol saturates its soluble carriers, it crystallizes into rigid, needle-like structures. Cholesterol is not merely a structural component of plaques but a potent inflammatory trigger. When cholesterol surpasses its solubility threshold within the intima, it crystallizes into sharp, rigid structures.4 These cholesterol crystals are phagocytosed by macrophages, causing lysosomal rupture and release of cathepsins, which act as danger-associated molecular patterns that activate the nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain, leucine-rich repeat and pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, a central mediator of innate immunity.19,25

ox-LDL is equally important. Beyond being a biomarker of dyslipidemia, ox-LDL actively induces inflammasome assembly via scavenger receptors, particularly cluster of differentiation 36 (CD36), leading to nuclear factor-κB activation and upregulation of pro–IL-1β.25, 26, 27 CD36-deficient macrophages fail to secrete IL-1β in response to ox-LDL, underscoring its critical role.25,26 The consequence is caspase-1–dependent maturation of IL-1β, which promotes endothelial adhesion molecule expression, leukocyte recruitment, and secretion of downstream cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α.19 Together, cholesterol crystals and ox-LDL act as inflammatory instigators, directly linking lipid overload to sterile inflammation and setting the stage for a self-perpetuating cycle (Figure 3A).Figure 3Mechanistic Evidence for the Cholesterol-Inflammation Fusion Hypothesis(A) Cholesterol triggers inflammation: cholesterol crystals, ox-LDL, and agLDL act as danger signals, inducing lysosomal stress, NLRP3 inflammasome activation, IL-1β release, and downstream cytokine cascades. Beyond ox-LDL and cholesterol crystals, agLDL, formed through particle aggregation and binding to matrix components, is efficiently taken up by macrophages and promotes foam cell formation and intracellular cholesterol accumulation, which are central to the inflammatory milieu of atherosclerotic lesions.28 Recent work further demonstrates that agLDL processing engages innate immune pathways, including Toll-like receptor 4–associated signaling, linking agLDL catabolism to inflammatory responses during lesion development.19 Although direct cytokine induction by agLDL alone remains less well characterized, accumulating evidence supports its dual role in lipid loading and proinflammatory modulation in AS progression.29

Inflammation disrupts cholesterol metabolism

The inflammatory cascade reciprocally sabotages cholesterol homeostasis. Proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6) suppress ATP-binding cassette transporters (ABCA1 and ABCG1) in macrophages, crippling cholesterol efflux and accelerating foam cell formation.5,6,30,31 Foam cells also derive substantially from VSMCs, which contribute over one-half of the total foam cell population and exhibit reduced cholesterol efflux due to lower ABCA1 expression, further promoting lipid retention and local inflammatory signaling.23,24 In addition, inflammatory cytokines reduce endothelial nitric oxide bioavailability, impairing barrier integrity and facilitating LDL infiltration.32,33

At the systemic level, IL-6 stimulates hepatic production of acute-phase proteins such as C-reactive protein and serum amyloid A, contributing to reduced HDL and increased triglycerides.34,35 Chronic inflammation modifies HDL composition, creating dysfunctional particles unable to support reverse cholesterol transport.36,37 In parallel, oxidative stress and enzymatic modification render LDL particles more immunogenic, driving further uptake by macrophages.27,38

Inflammatory cells also destabilize plaques: proteolytic enzymes such as matrix metalloproteinases weaken the fibrous cap, increasing rupture risk and linking inflammatory feedback on cholesterol metabolism to acute clinical events.39,40 Thus, inflammation does not simply accompany lipid deposition; it enforces cholesterol retention, systemic dyslipidemia, and plaque instability (Figure 3B).

Cellular Drivers of Cholesterol-Inflammation Fusion

Single-cell RNA sequencing has uncovered distinct macrophage subpopulations that simultaneously display lipid-laden and proinflammatory phenotypes. Triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells (TREM) 2hi macrophages exemplify this fusion state: enriched in lipid-handling genes (ABCA1, CD36, and TREM2) and inflammatory mediators, while showing metabolic rewiring toward glycolysis.41,42 These macrophages embody the dual nature of the disease—impaired efflux capacity, enhanced cytokine secretion, and sustained inflammatory activity.

Functionally, these dual-activity macrophages act as amplifiers of the lipid-inflammation loop, promoting foam cell formation, necrotic core expansion, and plaque vulnerability. Beyond macrophages, VSMC-derived foam cells further contribute to plaque lipid burden and modulate inflammatory responses, highlighting that multiple cell types participate in the cholesterol-inflammation fusion process.23,24 Endothelial cells also actively participate in the lipid-inflammation fusion process by upregulating adhesion molecules and chemokines that govern leukocyte recruitment and transmigration, increasing endothelial permeability to atherogenic lipoproteins, and producing proinflammatory cytokines that amplify local immune responses within the arterial wall.32,43 The presence of these cell types in plaques, but absence in healthy arteries, underscores their role as cellular engines of the cholesterol-inflammation fusion hypothesis (Figure 3C).

Evidence from basic research: interplay between lipids and inflammation

Experimental studies provide strong validation for this tangled web. In cholesterol-fed, balloon-injured rabbits, vascular injury synergizes with hypercholesterolemia to accelerate plaque formation, marked by enhanced LDL infiltration, macrophage recruitment, and NLRP3 inflammasome activation with elevated IL-1β levels.19,44 In apolipoprotein E-deficient mice, additional inflammatory stimuli (eg, lipopolysaccharide, mechanical stress) exacerbate plaque size and complexity, yielding features of human plaque vulnerability such as thin fibrous caps and large necrotic cores.45,46

In vitro, macrophages exposed to cholesterol crystals undergo lysosomal rupture, triggering inflammasome assembly and IL-1β release. Genetic deletion of NLRP3 or caspase-1 markedly reduces AS despite persistent hypercholesterolemia.19,47 Novel regulators such as ABCA1-seRNA have been shown to control macrophage polarization and cholesterol homeostasis, with overexpression reducing lipid burden, systemic cytokine levels, and plaque size in apolipoprotein E-deficient mice22 (Figure 3D).

In addition to macrophage-centered mechanisms, VSMCs also participate in the lipid-inflammation interplay central to AS. Lipid infiltration promotes VSMC phenotypic switching toward foam-cell and macrophage-like states in early human lesions, with increased lipid burden correlating with lesion progression and inflammatory features.48 Moreover, ox-LDL can activate Toll-like receptor 4–mediated inflammatory signaling in VSMCs, upregulating key enzymes involved in cholesteryl ester formation and further promoting foam cell formation independent of macrophages.49 Some in vitro models also reveal that VSMCs exposed to cholesterol crystals modulate local immune responses by releasing inflammatory mediators that influence neutrophil recruitment and activity.50

Together, these findings, spanning animal models, cellular systems, and high-resolution omics, demonstrate that dysregulated cholesterol metabolism and chronic inflammation are inseparably fused. This mechanistic framework provides the biological substrate for the cholesterol-inflammation fusion hypothesis.

Lipid-lowering therapies: efficacy with residual inflammatory risk

Lipid-lowering therapies such as statins, ezetimibe, and proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors markedly reduce LDL-C and have become cornerstones of ASCVD prevention.9,10,51, 52, 53 Statins, in particular, demonstrated robust reductions in cardiovascular events across primary and secondary prevention trials, firmly establishing LDL-C as a causal factor in AS. PCSK9 inhibitors such as evolocumab and alirocumab extended these benefits, enabling unprecedented LDL-C lowering and further event reduction in FOURIER (Evaluation of Cardiovascular Outcomes After an Acute Coronary Syndrome During Treatment With Alirocumab) and ODYSSEY OUTCOMES (Evaluation of Cardiovascular Outcomes After an Acute Coronary Syndrome During Treatment With Alirocumab).51,52

However, despite achieving very low LDL-C levels, substantial residual cardiovascular risk persists. Post hoc analyses of PROVE IT–TIMI 22 (Pravastatin or Atorvastatin Evaluation and Infection Therapy–Thrombolysis In Myocardial Infarction 22) and IMPROVE-IT (Improved Reduction of Outcomes: Vytorin Efficacy International Trial) showed that elevated high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) predicted recurrent events independently of LDL-C.54,55 In FOURIER, patients with high baseline hs-CRP continued to experience significantly higher event rates even under intensive PCSK9 inhibition.56 These findings underscore that lipid lowering alone cannot eliminate cardiovascular risk and highlight inflammation as a critical residual driver.

Anti-inflammatory therapies: efficacy with residual lipid risk

The complementary role of inflammation was first demonstrated in CANTOS, where selective IL-1β inhibition with canakinumab reduced recurrent major adverse cardiovascular events without affecting LDL-C levels.12 However, canakinumab treatment was associated with a significantly increased risk of fatal infection and sepsis, and these side effects observed in the canakinumab trial led to the termination of the study. Hence, the trial provided definitive proof-of-principle that inflammation is a modifiable driver of ASCVD but also highlighted the need to carefully weigh risks and benefits.

Broader-acting agents such as colchicine have since confirmed benefit. In the COLCOT (Colchicine Cardiovascular Outcomes Trial), low-dose colchicine (0.5 mg/d) significantly reduced the risk of major ischemic cardiovascular events, primarily in high-risk post–myocardial infarction patients within 30 days of their event.57 Similarly, the LoDoCo2 (Low-Dose Colchicine 2 Trial) showed reduced cardiovascular events in patients with stable coronary artery disease.58

Nevertheless, these therapies did not replace lipid lowering. Absolute risk reductions with canakinumab or colchicine were modest compared with statins, and residual risk remained substantial when LDL-C was uncontrolled. Furthermore, safety and cost considerations limited widespread adoption of canakinumab. Thus, although inflammation control provides incremental benefit, it is not sufficient as a stand-alone strategy.

Dual-pathway validation: synergy of lipid and inflammation control

The strongest validation of the fusion hypothesis comes from studies that assessed lipid and inflammatory pathways simultaneously. In JUPITER (Justification for the Use of Statins in Prevention: an Intervention Trial Evaluating Rosuvastatin), patients achieving both LDL-C <70 mg/dL and hs-CRP <2 mg/L experienced a 65% reduction in cardiovascular events compared with those achieving only one target.59 Post hoc analyses of PROVE IT-TIMI 22 and IMPROVE-IT confirmed that patients who simultaneously achieved dual targets had the lowest event rates.54,55

These observations have translational implications: residual cardiovascular risk is not purely cholesterol risk or inflammatory risk but rather fusion risk. Drugs with dual activity are emerging examples of this paradigm. For instance, bempedoic acid not only lowers LDL-C but also reduces hs-CRP, providing simultaneous modulation of lipid and inflammatory pathways.60 Similarly, ongoing studies with agents targeting metabolic-immune cross talk (eg, IL-6 blockers, lipoprotein[a]-lowering therapies) may further refine dual-target interventions.

Taken together, clinical trial evidence converges on a central insight: optimal cardiovascular risk reduction requires simultaneous disruption of both lipid-driven and inflammation-driven processes. Monotherapy targeting either pathway leaves residual risk unaddressed, whereas dual-pathway control offers the greatest protection. These findings directly validate the cholesterol-inflammation fusion hypothesis and provide a strong rationale for precision strategies that integrate lipid and inflammatory biomarkers into therapeutic decision-making.

Discussion

The cholesterol-inflammation fusion hypothesis reframes AS as more than the sum of its parts. Rethinking residual risk as fusion risk

The persistence of cardiovascular events despite optimal lipid-lowering or anti-inflammatory therapy suggests that residual risk is not merely leftover cholesterol or incomplete inflammation control, but fusion risk—a risk sustained by the self-perpetuating interaction between these 2 drivers. This perspective reframes clinical decision-making: the failure to address both arms of the cycle leaves patients vulnerable, regardless of LDL-C levels or inflammatory biomarker suppression.

Translational implications

The fusion framework carries direct consequences for patient care by reframing residual cardiovascular risk as a fusion risk driven by the coexistence of lipid burden and vascular inflammation. Accordingly, risk stratification should evolve from traditional cholesterol-centric models toward integrated lipid-inflammation assessment. In the near term, this approach can be pragmatically operationalized using guideline-endorsed biomarkers, with LDL-C serving as the primary lipid target and hs-CRP as the most widely validated indicator of residual inflammatory risk. Notably, emerging evidence suggests that optimal hs-CRP thresholds for defining elevated inflammatory risk may differ across populations, with several East Asian cohorts demonstrating prognostic value at hs-CRP levels lower than the traditional Western cutoff of ≥2 mg/L.61,62 Incorporating population-specific hs-CRP thresholds may therefore refine fusion risk stratification and patient selection. Beyond these core markers, additional lipid fractions and inflammatory mediators, including lipoprotein(a), remnant cholesterol, and upstream cytokines such as IL-6 or TNF-α, may provide complementary information for risk stratification and mechanistic insight; however, they are not yet recommended as routine therapeutic targets in current guidelines.

Imaging approaches may further complement fusion risk assessment in selected high-risk or research settings. Molecular imaging techniques such as positron emission tomography can visualize arterial inflammatory activity, whereas advanced magnetic resonance imaging may delineate plaque composition and lipid burden. Together, these modalities offer a means to interrogate the spatial convergence of lipid accumulation and inflammation within plaques.63,64

From a therapeutic perspective, this framework supports coordinated dual-pathway interventions. Established strategies, such as statins or PCSK9 inhibitors combined with colchicine or IL-1β blockade, may yield synergistic benefits. Beyond existing regimens, novel agents targeting fusion nodes, including NLRP3 inflammasome inhibitors or modulators of TREM2hi macrophages, represent promising frontiers. Therapies that prevent LDL aggregation or stabilize ApoB100 may further enhance dual-pathway approaches by reducing agLDL retention, lysosomal stress, and downstream inflammatory signaling.

Collectively, these developments outline a stepwise translational roadmap in which fusion risk assessment progressively informs patient selection and personalized deployment of combined lipid-lowering and anti-inflammatory therapies.

Challenges and controversies

Important questions remain. The causal hierarchy between cholesterol deposition and inflammation activation requires further clarification. Patient heterogeneity, including genetic background, metabolic comorbidities, and microbiome influences, may modify the fusion loop and affect treatment response. Safety is another concern: long-term anti-inflammatory therapy may increase infection risk, whereas aggressive lipid lowering raises questions about metabolic adaptation. Addressing these uncertainties will require carefully designed mechanistic studies and large-scale, dual-target clinical trials.

Conclusions

This review goes beyond summarizing evidence to argue for a paradigm shift: AS is best understood as a fusion disease driven by the intertwined processes of dysregulated cholesterol metabolism and chronic vascular inflammation. Recognizing this reality compels us to redefine residual cardiovascular risk as fusion risk, to incorporate lipid and inflammatory biomarkers into precision stratification, and to design therapies that disrupt the cycle at its vulnerable nodes.

By reframing the pathophysiology of AS through the lens of the cholesterol-inflammation fusion hypothesis, we not only deepen mechanistic understanding but also create actionable opportunities for clinical translation. Future efforts should focus on identifying fusion-specific biomarkers, mapping molecular cross talk at single-cell resolution, and developing dual-target interventions capable of breaking the cycle. Such strategies may ultimately deliver the next generation of cardiovascular prevention and treatment, moving closer to the long-sought goal of reducing the global burden of ASCVD.
