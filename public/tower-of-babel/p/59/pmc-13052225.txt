Evaluation of Stress Ulcer Prophylaxis Use Among Adult Patients Admitted to the Intensive Care Unit in Northwest Ethiopia Hospitals During 2024: A Multicenter Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Samuel Agegnew Wondm, Samuel Berihun Dagnew, Fisseha Nigussie Dagnew, Getachew Yitayew Tarekegn, Tirsit Ketsela Zeleke, Abebaw Abie Gedif, Tilaye Arega Moges, Tigabu Eskeziya Zerihun, Sisay Sitotaw Anberbr, Eyayaw Ashete Belachew, Ashenafi Kibret Sendekie, Fasil Bayafers Tamene
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimStress‐related mucosal bleeding among critically ill patients in intensive care unit (ICU) contributes substantially to morbidity and mortality. Despite growing concerns globally over inappropriate stress ulcer prophylaxis (SUP) use, limited data exist from Ethiopia, particularly in ICU settings. The objective of this study was to assess the appropriateness of SUP indications and associated factors among ICU‐admitted patients in Northwest Ethiopia hospitals and to evaluate physicians' knowledge related to SUP prescribing practices.MethodsA multicenter cross‐sectional study was conducted from June to December 2024 across three comprehensive specialized hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia. The American Society of Health‐System Pharmacists (ASHP) criteria were applied to assess the appropriateness of SUP indications among ICU admitted adult patients. Additionally, a structured survey was administered to prescribers to evaluate their knowledge and attitudes regarding SUP. Binary logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with inappropriate SUP use, with a two‐sided significance level of p < 0.05% and 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsA total of 410 patients and 56 prescribers were included in the final analysis. Prescribers' knowledge regarding stress ulcer prophylaxis was suboptimal, with a mean knowledge score of 60.2%. Among patients receiving SUP, 64.4% of prescriptions were inappropriate, including 45.9% overutilization and 18.5% underutilization. Independent factors of inappropriate SUP use included older age ( ≥ 60 years) [AOR = 2.1; 95% CI: 1.2–3.3; p = 0.004] and longer hospital stays ( > 7days) [AOR = 3.1; 95% CI: 1.9–4.9; p = 0.001].ConclusionsInappropriate use of SUP was widespread among ICU patients in Northwest Ethiopia. Older age and prolonged hospitalization were significantly associated factors. Regular prescriber education, involvement of clinical pharmacists, and routine audit with feedback are recommended to reduce inappropriate SUP use, particularly among older patients and those with prolonged hospital stays.

Introduction

Stress ulcers are single or gastroduodenal mucosal abnormalities that can result in different clinical symptoms, ranging from moderate to severe ulceration or erosion of the mucosa to potentially fatal hemorrhage [1]. Mucosal damage and ulceration are significantly influenced by decreased blood circulation and mucosal ischemia, inadequate perfusion, and circulatory disturbance [2]. Critically ill patients who need to be admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) frequently develop gastrointestinal (GI) stress ulcers, and 75%–100% of ICU patients have mucosal damage within 24 hours of their arrival [3]. Furthermore, when patients do not undergo stress ulcer prevention, occult bleeding occurs in 15%–50% of critically ill patients, and overt bleeding occurs in 5%–25% of these patients [4].

Morbidity associated with stress ulcer‐associated hemorrhage, the cost of medications, and the possible complications associated with stress ulcer prophylaxis (SUP) use are important considerations when prescribing prophylaxis [3]. Investigating the causes of inappropriate SUP use is also essential because unnecessary SUP prescribing in ICU patients exposes them to avoidable adverse effects, increases healthcare costs, and contributes to polypharmacy without proven clinical benefit. In resource‐limited settings, such as Northwest Ethiopia, the absence of standardized institutional protocols, limited involvement of clinical pharmacists, and potential gaps in prescribers' knowledge may further increase the risk of inappropriate SUP use. Identifying patient, prescriber, and system‐related factors associated with inappropriate SUP prescribing is therefore critical to inform targeted interventions, guideline implementation, and rational medication use in ICU settings.

The American Society of Health‐System Pharmacists (ASHP) was used to assess the risk factors for ulcer bleeding from stress ulcers among patients admitted to the ICU [5]. For various factors related to the patient's age, health, and medications, SUP may be administered inappropriately [6, 7]. Previous research has demonstrated that older, sicker patients who are on numerous drugs are at a greater risk of receiving an inappropriate prescription for proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) [7, 8]. Because several healthcare professionals view acid suppressive therapy (AST) as innocuous, other variables could be associated with the prescriber's perspective [9, 10]. The serious side effects of inappropriate PPIs and histamine‐2 receptor antagonists (H2RAs) use in hospitalized patients may increase healthcare costs [11, 12, 13].

The inappropriate use of SUP continues to be a widespread issue globally in ICU, with prevalence ranging from 20.5% to 96.4% [3, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Previous studies in Ethiopia were conducted to determine the prevalence of inappropriate use of SUP among patients admitted to non‐intensive care units [19, 20, 21]. No study has evaluated the appropriateness of SUP use among patients admitted to the ICU in Ethiopia, particularly in Northwest Ethiopia. Therefore, the objective of this study was to assess the appropriateness of SUP indications and associated factors among ICU‐admitted patients in Northwest Ethiopia hospitals and to evaluate physicians' knowledge related to SUP prescribing practices. The findings of the study will help researchers and decision‐makers aggressively understand how clinicians use SUP and provide practical solutions for SUP management.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

Currently, Northwest Ethiopia has five comprehensive specialized hospitals. All five hospitals are public comprehensive specialized hospitals that provide comparable adult ICU services, follow similar national ICU care protocols, and serve similar patient populations. The number of ICU beds in the five hospitals ranges from 18 to 20 beds, and all have mixed medical–surgical ICUs, which minimizes structural heterogeneity between hospitals. Due to a shortage of budget, an institution‐based multicenter cross‐sectional study was conducted at the ICU wards of three comprehensive and specialized hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia. In addition, a survey of prescribers regarding SUP knowledge and attitude was conducted. The hospitals were selected using the lottery method among five hospitals in the area. The hospitals were Tibebe‐Ghion comprehensive and specialized hospital (TGCSH), Felege‐Hiwot comprehensive and specialized hospital (FHCSH), and Debre Markos comprehensive and specialized hospital (DMCSH). This study was conducted from June 15 to December 15, 2024. The hospitals have provided healthcare services to over 15 million people in their catchment area, including intensive care unit services.

Population, Inclusion, and Exclusion Criteria

The study population consisted of adult patients admitted to the ICU of selected hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia during the study period. Patients were included if they were aged 18 years or older, were admitted to the ICU for at least 24 h, and had complete medical records documenting demographic data, clinical characteristics, stress ulcer risk factors, and medication use. Patients were excluded if they had active gastrointestinal bleeding at ICU admission, developed active gastrointestinal bleeding during hospitalization, were receiving acid‐suppressive therapy for therapeutic indications, such as peptic ulcer disease, gastroesophageal reflux disease, or upper gastrointestinal bleeding, had a history of gastrectomy or major gastric surgery, had gastric or esophageal malignancy and had incomplete or missing medical records [22].

Ethics Approval

The ethical review committee of the Department of Pharmacy at Debre Markos University approved this study (Ref No:PharDP/142/01/2024). Permission was obtained from the selected hospitals to proceed with the study.

Sample Size Determination and Sampling Technique

Cochran's single population proportion formula was used to determine the required sample size for this study. To the best of the authors' knowledge, no previous study has been conducted on this topic in Ethiopia, a proportion of 50% was assumed to maximize the sample size. Then, computed for n = P(1−P) × Z2/W2), where W is the marginal error (0.05), n is the desired sample size, and Z is the usual normal distribution set at 1.96 (or 95% CI). The p‐value indicates that a positive prevalence was used to determine the sample size. Then, computed for n = 1.962 * 0.5(1‐0.5)/0.052, n = 384. The intended sample size was 422 after a 10% non‐response rate was included.

According to the past 6‐month data, 1024 patients were admitted to the selected hospitals. After considering proportional allocation, 102,141 and 179 patients were recruited from DMCSH, FHCSH, and TGCSH, respectively, using the consecutive sampling technique. After the exclusion of patients who did not fulfill the inclusion criteria, 99,138, and 173 patients were considered in the final analysis from DMCSH, FHCSH, and TGCSH, respectively, making the final sample size 410 patients (Figure 1).

Variables

The outcome variable was the inappropriate use of SUP indications. Sociodemographic, clinical, and drug‐related characteristics were the independent factors. Institutional and prescriber risk factors were also included as independent variables.

Data Collection Instruments

The data collection instrument was created by the authors following a study of relevant literature [23, 24, 25, 26]. The instrument includes data on the study's sociodemographic and clinical features, risk factors for stress ulcer development, medications recommended to avoid stress ulcers, rationale for SUP, and suitability of the regimen. Age was categorized based on World Health Organization(WHO) criteria for developing countries, with age ≥ 60 years defined as older adulthood [27]. Body mass index (BMI), expressed as kilograms per square meter (kg/m²), was categorized according to the WHO definition as underweight ( < 18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m²), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m²), and obese ( ≥ 30.0 kg/m²) [28]. Polypharmacy status was also defined based on WHO criteria as no polypharmacy(1‐4), polypharmacy (use of at least five medications), and hyper‐polypharmacy (use of ≥ 10 medications) [29]. Length of hospitalization was categorized into ≤ 7days and > 7 days [21]. Comorbidities were quantified using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score [19, 30]. Trained data collectors examined medical charts and conducted in‐person interviews with patients and/or caregivers to gather data and distribute survey questions to physicians. The ASHP was used to determine the risk of stress ulcer development. SUP was considered inappropriate when prescribed in the absence of an approved indication or when not prescribed in the presence of an approved indication [5]. Appropriateness was assessed based on ASHP indication criteria and did not include dose, route, or frequency.

The prevalence of inappropriate SUP use was calculated as the number of patients who experienced at least one inappropriate SUP practice divided by the total number of study participants included in the final analysis. Prescription reviews were conducted daily during weekdays (5 days per week) throughout the ICU stay, from admission until discharge or death. For patients with prolonged ICU stays, SUP prescriptions were reassessed to capture changes in indication over time. Each completed data collection form was initially evaluated by one investigator (SAW) for SUP appropriateness based on ASHP guideline criteria and subsequently cross‐checked for accuracy and consistency by two additional investigators (SBD and FBT), all clinical pharmacists. Any discrepancies in assessment were resolved through discussion and consensus, guided by the ASHP recommendations.

To investigate prescriber and instructional‐related factors influencing SUP use, we developed a structured, self‐administered questionnaire in English. The survey items were constructed based on factors identified in previous studies and adapted from published questionnaires in the literature [13, 31, 32, 33]. The initial draft of the questionnaire was reviewed by clinical pharmacy experts to ensure content relevance and clarity. The final questionnaire consisted of four sections with a total of 27 items: prescriber sociodemographic characteristics (4 items), prescribers' knowledge of SUP (10 items), prescribers' attitudes toward acid‐suppressive therapy (5 items), and institutional and system‐related barriers to appropriate SUP use (8 items). The knowledge section included multiple‐choice and yes/no questions covering SUP indications, risk factors, agents, dose, route, duration, adverse effects, and drug interactions [5]. Attitude and institutional‐barrier items were assessed using dichotomous (yes/no) responses. Internal consistency of the questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach's alpha, which demonstrated acceptable reliability (α = 0.78). Prior to the main data collection, the questionnaire was pretested on 10% of prescribers (n = 7), and minor modifications were made based on feedback to improve clarity and comprehension.

Data Quality Control

To ensure uniformity, clarity, and convenience of data collection, the questionnaire was pretested on 5% (22 patients) of the sample population at DMCSH before the actual data collection period. Following minor modifications to the questionnaire, the real data collection information process started. Under the supervision of three clinical pharmacy lecturers, four qualified clinical pharmacists and three nurses collected the data. A half‐day training on the study's objectives, types of data collection tools, and ethical concerns for the data‐gathering process was provided to the data collectors. Every day of the data collection period, the completed questionnaire was examined for accuracy, completeness, and data cleanliness by the principal investigator.

Statistical Analysis

Patients were eligible if they were adult patients ( ≥ 18 years) admitted to the ICU during the study period, had an ICU stay of ≥ 24 h and patients with complete medical records containing data on SUP indication, risk factors, and prescribed medications. A survey on prescribers' knowledge, attitude, and perceived barriers related to stress ulcer prophylaxis was assessed using a structured questionnaire consisting of 4 sociodemographic characteristics, 10 knowledge questions, 5 attitude questions, and 8 institutional barrier items. The knowledge section assessed prescribers' understanding of stress ulcer risk factors, SUP indication criteria, SUP agents, duration of SUP, adverse effects, and drug interactions of SUP agents. Each right response was worth one point toward the knowledge score. The mean grade of the chosen alternatives among the right possibilities was used for the drug–drug interaction, side effects, and administration route. After being translated to a percentage, the overall knowledge score was expressed as an out of 10 and then converted to percentage. Because no validated cutoff exists for assessing SUP knowledge, scores were categorized into two groups: ‘low’ for scores below the overall mean and ‘high’ for scores above the overall mean, following the approach used in previous studies [31]. Prescribers' attitudes toward SUP prescribing were evaluated using statements related to prescribing habits, perceived safety of SUP, fear of gastrointestinal bleeding, and influence of colleagues. Responses of attitude were recorded as yes/no and expressed as a proportion. Institutional and system‐related barriers to appropriate SUP use were also assessed, including workload, absence of clinical pharmacists, lack of hospital protocols, and polypharmacy were expressed by proportion. Before being imported into Epidata version 4.6 and exported to STATA 17 (StatCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) for analysis, the data were coded, cleaned, and verified for completeness. Frequencies, proportions, and averages with standard deviation (SD) were used to describe the variables. A histogram was used to determine whether the continuous variables had a normal distribution. The relationships between the dependent and independent variables were ascertained using bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analysis. To find independent predictors of inappropriate use of SUP, the multivariable logistic regression analysis included independent factors with a two‐sided p‐value of less than 0.2 in the bivariable analysis. In the multivariable logistic regression, variables were deemed statistically significant when a two‐sided P‐value was less than 0.05. The model was well‐suited to improper SUP use, according to the results of the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test for logistic regression (p = 0.67). To determine whether the categorical variables showed multicollinearity, a contingency coefficient test was used. Continuous variables were tested for multicollinearity and negligible correlation (less than 5) using the variance inflation factor (VIF) [22].

Operational Definitions

Organ dysfunction: Renal insufficiency (creatinine clearance < 40 ml/min, or serum creatinine > 2.8 mg/dl), Hepaticimpairment(bilrubin > 8.8 mg/dl, seum aspartate transaminase > 500U/L, and albumin < 4 g/L) [5].

Stress ulcer prophylaxis: Stress ulcer prevention therapy with at least one dose of acid‐suppressive drugs among ICU‐admitted patients [5].

Appropriate SUP use: SUP was considered appropriate if a patient had at least one major risk factor or two or more minor risk factors and received AST according to ASHP guideline [5], otherwise it is inappropriate SUP use. Appropriateness was assessed solely based on ASHP indication criteria and did not include dose, route, frequency and durations.

Results

Sociodemographic and Clinical Characteristics of the Patients

A total of 410 patients (97.2%) out of the 422 contacted for the research were included in the final analysis. Women comprised two‐thirds of the patients 274 (66.8%). Parenteral feeding was used for more than three‐fourths of the patients 329 (80.3%). More than half of patients 230 (56.1%) had moderate Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) scores. More than half of the patients (53.2%) spent ≤ 7days in the hospital, with a mean ( ± SD) length of hospital stay of 9.2 ( ± 1.8) days. More than half of patients 206 (50.2%)had normal BMI. Disease of the circulatory system 196 (47.8%) was the most common diagnosis of hospital admission. More than one‐third 145 (35.4%) of patients had polypharmacy (Table 1).

Association between Polypharmacy and Comorbidity With Age

Patients aged > 60 years show a markedly higher prevalence of polypharmacy (46.4%) and hyper‐polypharmacy (42.8%), while only a small proportion have no polypharmacy (10.8%).There was a significant association between age and polypharmacy‐status (χ² test, p < 0.01). Severe comorbidity was markedly more prevalent among patients aged ≥ 60 years (41.7%) than those aged < 60 years (5.6%). Age was also strongly associated with comorbidity burden as measured by the Charlson Comorbidity Index (χ² test, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Overall, prescribers' knowledge scores (mean ± SD) was 60.2% ± 18.5. More than two‐thirds of prescribers 38(67.9%) had poor knowledge regarding SUP use (Table 3).

Among the 56 prescribers who completed the response for stress ulcer risk factors, coagulation‐related problems (35.7%), history of upper gastrointestinal bleeding (33.9%), non‐per oral (NPO) for > 5 days (30.4%), and mechanical ventilation for > 48 h (28.6%) were the most frequently identified risk factors (Figure 2).

Medication‐Related Characteristics

Of the 410 patients admitted to the ICU, a total of 305 (74.4%) received stress ulcer prophylaxis, of which 81.3% received PPIs, exclusively omeprazole, and the rest of the participants received H2‐receptor antagonists (H2RAs), exclusively cimetidine (18.7%). Among patients on stress ulcer prophylaxis, nearly two‐thirds of patients 264 (64.4%) (95% CI: 59.8–69) received inappropriate stress ulcer prophylaxis. Of inappropriate SUP use, more than one‐third of patients188 (45.9%) received overutilization, and underutilization was encountered in 76 (18.5%) patients. More than one‐third of patients 124 (40.7%) continued SUP prophylaxis upon discharge from the ICU (Table 5).

Factors Associated With Inappropriate Stress Ulcer Prophylaxis

Bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with the inappropriate use of SUP. Following bivariable logistic regression analysis, multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed for variables with P‐values 0.2. In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, old age ( ≥ 60) [AOR = 2.1, 95% CI (1.2–3.3); p = 0.004] and longer hospital stays (> 7 days) [AOR = 3.1, 95% CI (1.9–4.9); p = 0.001] were significantly associated with inappropriate use of stress ulcer prophylaxis (Table 6).

Discussion

The objective of this study was to evaluate the appropriateness of SUP indications and associated factors among ICU‐admitted patients in Northwest Ethiopia hospitals using ASHP‐derived criteria for critically ill patients and to assess physicians' knowledge related to SUP prescribing practices.

Nearly three quarters (74.4%) of patients admitted to the ICU received a prescription for SUP. This result was comparable with a study conducted among ICU admitted patients in Iran (80.4%) [16] and in the USA 87.1% [15]. The high prevalence of SUP use in ICU settings is expected, given the greater burden of physiological stress, invasive interventions, and severity of illness in these patients. Among patients who received SUP, PPIs were prescribed in more than a third‐fourth of patients (81.3%) for SUP. This finding is comparable with a study done in the USA (70.9%) [34]. This study was higher than a study conducted in Malaysia (23.9%) [35], Jordan 56% [36] and Ethiopia 29.1% [19]. Our finding was lower than a study conducted in Ethiopia (92.7%) [21] and China 96.1% [23]. PPIs are preferentially used for SUP in our setting. Among the types of PPIs, omeprazole 40 mg IV daily was 29.8%. This finding was lower than a study conducted in China, which found 95.3% [23]. The study conducted in USA found that 75% of patients received omeprazole 20 mg capsule daily for SUP. In our study, omeprazole was the only prescribed PPI. The possible reason might be that omeprazole was available in both IV and PO dosage forms during the study period. Injections are administered to patients with nothing‐by‐mouth situations or who encounter severe motility difficulties [37]. Oral PPIs formulation effectiveness was comparable to that of injectables at comparable doses, but they were more affordable and had fewer difficulties than IV formulations [38]. Previous study has shown that pantoprazole was the most commonly prescribed PPI [39]. The absence of hepatic CYP2C19 inhibition may explain why pantoprazole is preferable among clopidogrel users [40]. A study conducted in Switzerland found that 94% of patients received esomeprazole [41]. A study conducted in Malaysia found that ranitidine was the most commonly prescribed SUP [36]. This discrepancy may result from national guidelines and the availability of differing from one another.

Our study found that 64.4% (95% CI: 59.8–69) of patients received inappropriate SUP. The overall inappropriate use of stress ulcer prophylaxis was lower than a study conducted in ICU settings in other countries, such as Brazil (43%) [17], Iran (20.5%) [18], Texas (53%) [3] and Spain (48.4%) [42]. The low percentage of inappropriate SUP in these countries may be due to greater availability of clinical pharmacy services and protocol‐driven prescribing. Moreover, Ethiopian hospitals ‘lack of adequate clinical pharmacy services [43]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to decrease the use of inappropriate SUP.

In our study, overuse of SUP (45.9%) was the most prevalent inappropriate SUP. This result was comparable with the findings of another study [38, 39, 44, 45]. On the other hand, only 18.5% of patients did not receive SUP, while there are indications. Previous investigations have indicated a comparable outcome [26, 46]. Underutilization of SUP also has negative impacts on patients. When patients do not get SUP, occult bleeding occurs in 15%–50% of critically ill patients, and overt bleeding occurs in 5%–25% of these patients [4]. GI bleeding is a life‐threatening illness with a 30‐day mortality ranging from 5% to 12% [47]. In addition, a 90‐day mortality rate of more than 50% in ICU admitted patients with clinically significant bleeding [48]. Initiatives to reduce inappropriate use of SUP are essential and urgently needed. For SUP, Ethiopia has no nationally prepared guidelines. Therefore, national recommendations are necessary to advise clinicians as well as clinical pharmacists on how to prescribe SUP for patients admitted to the ICU. Clinical pharmacy professionals can be assigned to track the daily prescription of SUP.

Among patients who received SUP, 40.7% were taking SUP at discharge. Our finding was comparable with previous studies, which found that 27%–39% of patients inappropriately continued SUP upon discharge [14, 34, 49, 50, 51, 52]. SUP should be started at the onset of risk factors and continued during the high‐risk period [53]. The extended SUP duration may have resulted from hard workloads and misconceptions about the benefits of longer SUP durations [54].

A lack of knowledge of prescribers regarding the proper indication, course of treatment, and potential side effects could contribute to inappropriate SUP use. It is possible that prescribers consider PPIs to be safe drugs because SUP side effects are seldom recorded. Our research prescribers' mean (± SD) knowledge score was 60.2% ( ± 18.5), which is comparable to a prior Chinese study in which the mean knowledge score was 59.47 ± 15.75 out of 100 [55]. The low knowledge score in our study may have resulted from the fact that it was conducted at university teaching hospitals, where medical residents and interns prescribed drugs. Furthermore, all patients who were released after more than 10 days declared that medical residents were more likely to prescribe SUP inappropriately [56]. When PPIs and H2RAs are taken for brief periods of time, the frequency of major clinical adverse responses in adults has been found to be minimal [5]. SUP may have been given to patients frequently, and physicians may not be aware of the most recent guidelines [7, 57].

In the multivariable variables' analysis, inappropriate use of SUP was significantly associated with older age and longer hospital stays. In our study, age ≥ 60 years was a predictor of inappropriate SUP. Prior studies have provided evidence for this finding [58, 59]. This finding agreed with our data, which found that nearly half of older adults had polypharmacy (46.4%), and 42.8% experienced hyper‐polypharmacy. There was a significant association between age and polypharmacy‐status (χ² test, p < 0.01). Severe comorbidity was also markedly more prevalent among patients aged ≥ 60 years (41.7%) than those aged < 60 years (5.6%). In addition, age‐related physiological changes and perceived vulnerability to gastrointestinal complications may lead prescribers to continue or initiate SUP without reassessment, contributing to inappropriate SUP use.

A Longer hospital stay was also significantly associated with inappropriate prescription of SUP. This finding agreed with previous studies [21, 23, 58]. Patients with prolonged ICU stays are more likely to develop major risk factors for stress‐related mucosal bleeding, thereby meeting guideline‐based criteria for SUP over time. Although prolonged ICU stay was associated with inappropriate SUP use, evolving risk factors over time may complicate real‐time appropriateness assessment, underscoring the need for daily reassessment rather than one‐time prescribing.

In this study, SUP appropriateness was assessed mainly based on indication criteria according to ASHP guidelines, without considering dose, route, frequency, or duration. This may have underestimated inappropriate SUP use, as prescriptions with correct indications but inappropriate dosing, route, frequency, or prolonged duration were classified as appropriate. Therefore, the true burden of inappropriate SUP use may be greater than reported.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

This study has notable strengths, including its multicenter design across three comprehensive specialized hospitals, a large sample size with a high response rate, and the use of ASHP guidelines to objectively assess SUP appropriateness and enable international comparison. The inclusion of a prescriber survey further allowed exploration of knowledge and institutional barriers influencing prescribing practices. However, the cross‐sectional design limits causal inference, and the assessment of appropriateness was restricted to ASHP indication criteria without evaluating dose, frequency, route, or duration, potentially underestimating inappropriate SUP use. Although conducted in Northwest Ethiopia, findings may not be generalizable to other resource‐limited ICU settings with differing patients, practices, or guideline availability. Future studies recommend incorporating comprehensive criteria to better reflect real‐world prescribing practices.

Conclusions

Inappropriate SUP use was encountered in nearly two‐thirds of the patients. More than two‐third of the physicians involved in the study had poor knowledge regarding SUP use. Inappropriate use of stress ulcer prophylaxis was significantly associated with older age and longer hospital stays. Regular prescriber education, involvement of clinical pharmacists, and routine audit with feedback are recommended to reduce inappropriate SUP use, particularly among older patients and those with prolonged hospital stays. These findings support the urgent integration of ICU‐specific SUP protocols, routine deprescribing review at ICU discharge, and structured clinical pharmacist involvement to improve patient safety and resource utilization.
