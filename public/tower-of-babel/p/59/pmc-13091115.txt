Real-world complications, utilization patterns, and patient selection for the vertiflex interspinous process device: A national cohort analysis

Introduction

Lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) is a degenerative condition characterized by the narrowing of the spinal canal, lateral recess, or neuroforamen, affecting approximately 11% of older adults in the United States [1]. The condition imposes substantial clinical and economic burdens, with annual healthcare costs exceeding $4 billion USD and representing one of the most common indications for spine surgery in older adults [2]. Initial management typically involves conservative approaches such as medication, epidural steroid injection, or physical therapy but lumbar laminectomy remains as the gold standard surgical treatment for severe cases or those unresponsive to conservative measures [3]. While surgical decompression has demonstrated superiority over conservative management in randomized controlled trials, it has also been associated with short-term complications such as epidural hematoma or durotomy, prolonged recovery times, and long-term issues like sagittal plane instability[[4], [5], [6]].

For patients with moderate LSS, interspinous process devices (IPDs) can be utilized to limit spinal extension, thereby reducing axial compression of the stenotic segment on the lower lumbar nerve roots. This extension blockade induces mild segmental flexion, which increases the cross-sectional area of the spinal canal, restores foraminal height, and offloads the facet joints [7,8]. First-generation IPDs were surgically implanted following lumbar laminectomy; however, these devices exhibited high reoperation rates (up to 85% in institutional studies), frequent device migration, and significant challenges if device removal was required, leading to limited adoption and even device discontinuation [[9], [10], [11]]. In contrast, the Vertiflex Superion® device (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA; FDA approved in 2015) introduced a minimally invasive approach with percutaneous placement that does not require laminectomy, nor an open surgical approach by utilizing fluoroscopically guided placement via 10 mm cannula. It is currently the only FDA-approved stand-alone IPD in the U.S., indicated for patients with one-to two-level moderate LSS from L1-L5 with intermittent neurogenic claudication who have failed at least six months of conservative treatment[12].

Despite promising short-term outcomes from the FDA Investigational Device Exemption (IDE) trials suggesting comparable symptom relief to surgical decompression while offering faster recovery and fewer short-term complications, significant concerns remain regarding Vertiflex IPD's real-world performance [[13], [14], [15]]. The FDA trials reported a two-year reoperation rate of 23.2%, raising concerns about long-term durability and cost-effectiveness [16]. Additionally, controversy surrounds the widespread utilization of these devices by non-surgical physicians, prompting several major surgical societies to issue position statements opposing placement by providers without formal orthopedic or neurosurgical training [17]. Furthermore, there is limited real-world data on Vertiflex outcomes beyond the original FDA trials, which are now more than a decade old. Additionally, there remains a lack of clarity regarding optimal patient selection criteria for Vertiflex placement compared to traditional surgical decompression. Poor outcomes have frequently been linked to technical errors and inappropriate patient selection, highlighting the need for better guidance in clinical decision-making[18,19].

This study aims to address these gaps through analysis of a large national insurance database. Our specific objectives were to (1) determine two-year reoperation rates following Vertiflex IPD placement and identify factors predictive of reoperation; (2) compare demographic, clinical, and regional factors associated with Vertiflex IPD utilization versus traditional surgical decompression; (3) assess 30-day complication profiles and opioid utilization patterns before and after Vertiflex placement; and (4) evaluate temporal trends in Vertiflex utilization relative to traditional lumbar decompression techniques.

Methods

Data Source and study population

This retrospective cohort study utilized the PearlDiver Mariner 170 Database (PearlDiver Technologies; Colorado Springs, CO, USA), a nationwide insurance claims database containing approximately 170 million de-identified patient records. The database encompasses demographics, diagnostic, and procedural data across all payer types from both inpatient and outpatient settings. We queried this database to identify patients who underwent interspinous spacer device placement between January 2017 and April 2023. Due to the use of de-identified data and the retrospective design, this study was exempt from institutional board review approval, and informed consent requirements were waived.

Identification of study cohorts

Patient cohorts were identified using International Classification of Diseases 10th edition (ICD-10) diagnostic codes and Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) procedural codes. The primary intervention cohort consisted of patients who underwent placement of single-level (CPT-22869) or multilevel (CPT-22870) interspinous spacer devices without open decompression. These CPT codes are Category I procedural codes and are device-neutral by definition. However, during the study period (2017–2023), the Vertiflex Superion was the only actively marketed IPD in the United States with an FDA indication for stand-alone implantation without decompression. Earlier devices such as X-STOP® had been discontinued prior to this timeframe, and devices such as Coflex® are FDA-approved for use in conjunction with open decompression (typically billed under CPT-22867). Although rare off-label use or miscoding cannot be completely excluded in an administrative claims database, CPT 22869/22870 during this period would be expected to represent Vertiflex implantation in the vast majority of cases. Exclusion criteria included age <18 years and surgical indications for trauma, infection, or malignancy.

For comparative analyses of patient selection and annual utilization trends, we identified a reference cohort of patients who underwent single or multi-level lumbar laminectomy or laminotomy without fusion using established CPT codes.

To analyze reoperation rates, we identified subsequent lumbar surgical interventions following index IPD placement and categorized them as (1) lumbar fusion with or without decompression, (2) lumbar decompression without fusion, and (3) device removal. Interspinous fracture events were also identified. Escalation of pain management was defined as subsequent lumbar epidural steroid injection, spinal cord stimulation, or intrathecal drug delivery system placement. Time-to-event analysis using Kaplan–Meier survival methods was performed to estimate two-year cumulative incidence of surgical reoperation, interspinous fracture, and escalation of treatment, with patients censored at last known database enrollment to account for variable follow-up.

Primary endpoints

Reoperation rates and predictors: We estimated two-year cumulative incidence of reoperation using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. Separately, we identified predictors of reoperation occurring at any time during the study period using univariate and multivariable logistic regression. Predictor variables included demographics (age, gender, insurance, region), surgical indications, and comorbidities (Elixhauser Comorbidity Index [ECI], obesity, osteoporosis). ECI was utilized as a measure of overall medical comorbidity burden and as a proxy for patient frailty in multivariable analyses. We also calculated the mean and median time intervals between index IPD placement and subsequent surgical interventions. Minimally invasive procedures including lumbar epidural steroid injections, spinal cord stimulation, and intrathecal drug delivery systems were analyzed separately as escalation of treatment rather than surgical reoperation.

Patient selection factors: We compared demographic characteristics, surgical indications, and comorbidity profiles between patients receiving Vertiflex IPD versus those undergoing traditional surgical lumbar decompression to identify factors associated with IPD utilization.

Short-term outcomes and opioid utilization: We assessed surgical and medical complications occurring within 30 days of IPD placement. Additionally, we analyzed opioid prescription patterns before and after IPD placement (at 1, 2, 12, and 24 months) to evaluate changes in pain management requirements.

Utilization trends: We examined annual utilization patterns of Vertiflex IPD relative to traditional lumbar decompression from 2017 through 2022 (excluding partial-year 2023 data). The proportion of total decompression cases involving Vertiflex IPD was calculated by dividing the number of IPD procedures by the total number of lumbar decompression procedures without fusion performed each year.

Statistical analysis

After assessing the data for normality, continuous and categorical variables were compared using student's t-test and Pearson chi-squared test of independence, respectively. Univariate logistic regression and multivariable logistic regression controlling for age, gender, and ECI were used to identify independent predictors of IPD selection and subsequent reoperation. Time-to-event analysis using Kaplan–Meier survival methods was performed to estimate two-year cumulative incidence of surgical reoperation, interspinous fracture, and escalation of treatment, with patients censored at last known database enrollment. For opioid utilization analysis, opioid prescription status was treated as a binary variable (present/absent) at each time point. Paired comparisons between baseline and postoperative time points (1, 2, 12, and 24 months) were performed using McNemar's test to assess within-patient changes in opioid use over time. Absolute differences with corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated for each comparison. To address multiple comparisons and minimize type I error, we applied Bonferroni correction, defining statistical significance as p < 0.0042. All statistical analyses were conducted using Rstudio (Version 4.4.2) within the PearlDiver Mariner Database program.

Results

Study population

Between January 2017 and April 2023, we identified 6321 patients who underwent Vertiflex IPD placement and 373762 patients who underwent lumbar laminectomy or laminotomy without fusion. Demographic comparisons revealed that patients who underwent IPD placement were significantly older (71.83 ± 8.85 vs 59.10 ± 15.22 years), more likely to be female (55.43% vs 45.10%), and had a higher comorbidity burden (ECI: 6.62 ± 3.88 vs 4.72 ± 3.54) compared to the surgical decompression cohort (all p < 0.001). IPD patients were less likely to undergo multilevel procedures (42.2% vs 61.0%, p < 0.001). Notably, surgical specialists (i.e. orthopaedic spine surgeons or neurosurgeons) performed only 8.62% of IPD placements. Complete demographic data are presented in Table 1.

Predictors of Vertiflex IPD utilization

Multivariable analysis identified several factors significantly associated with IPD utilization compared to traditional surgical decompression. Patient characteristics associated with a higher likelihood of IPD placement included advanced age (70-79: aOR 2.29 [2.18-2.41]; 80-99: aOR 3.54 [3.23-3.77]), female gender (aOR: 1.61 [1.53-1.70]), Medicare insurance (aOR: 1.68 [1.59-1.77]) or Medicaid insurance (aOR: 1.28 [1.09-1.48]), residence in the Western (aOR: 1.49 [1.40-1.59]) or Southern United States (aOR: 1.26 [1.20-1.32]), and a higher comorbidity burden(ECI ≥5; aOR: 1.45 [1.38-153]) (all p < 0.004).

Conversely, factors associated with a significantly lower likelihood of Vertiflex utilization included younger age (<49: aOR: 0.08 [0.06-0.09]; 50-59: aOR: 0.36 [0.33-0.44]; 60-69: aOR: 0.88 [0.83-0.93]), male gender (aOR: 0.62 [0.59-0.65]), commercial insurance (aOR: 0.55 [0.52-0.58]), Northeast (aOR: 0.65 [0.61-0.71]) or Midwest US residence (aOR: 0.68 [0.64-0.72]), and lower comorbidity burden (ECI ≤4; aOR: 0.69 [0.65-0.73]) (all p < 0.001). Surgical indications associated with a significantly lower likelihood of Vertiflex utilization included disc herniation (aOR: 0.32 [0.30-0.35]), spinal stenosis (aOR: 0.42 [0.40-0.44]), spondylolisthesis (aOR: 0.64 [0.59-0.69]), and radiculopathy (aOR: 0.84 [0.80-0.89]). 30 Day complications

Within 30 days postoperatively, 215 patient (3.4%) experienced complications, with medical complication (2.71%) occurring more frequently than surgical complications (0.81%) The most common surgical complications included surgical site infection (23 patients, 0.38%), implant-related complications (20 patients, 0.32%), wound dehiscence (16 patients, 0.25%), and the need for irrigation and debridement (14 patients, 0.22%). Fewer than 11 patients experienced nerve injury, spinal cord injury, hematoma, or seroma. Medical complications included respiratory failure (58 patients, 0.92%), pneumonia (33 patients, 0.52%), cerebrovascular accident (30 patients, 0.47%), and sepsis (15 patients, 0.24%). Fewer than 11 patients experienced deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, cardiac arrest, or myocardial infarction.

Escalation of pain management

During the study period, 1903 patients (30.11%) underwent lumbar epidural steroid injection, 419 (6.63%) received spinal cord stimulator implantation, and 58 (0.92%) underwent intrathecal drug delivery system placement following IPD implantation. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated a two-year cumulative incidence of 30.0% [95% CI 28.8–31.2%] for lumbar epidural steroid injection, 6.3% [5.6–6.9%] for spinal cord stimulator implantation, and 0.8% [0.6–1.1%] for intrathecal drug delivery system placement. These interventions were analyzed separately from surgical revision. Escalation data are summarized in Table 2.

Reoperation rates and timing

Of the 6321 patients who underwent Vertiflex IPD placement, 431 (6.82%) required reoperation during the study period. These reoperations included 186 (2.94%) lumbar fusions, 181 device removals (2.86%), and 155 (2.45%) decompressions without fusion.

Kaplan–Meier time-to-event analysis of the full cohort demonstrated a two-year cumulative incidence of any secondary surgical intervention of 6.30% [95% CI 5.7–6.9%]. At two years, the cumulative incidence of decompression without fusion was 2.7% [2.2–3.1%], lumbar fusion was 2.7% [2.3–3.1%], and instrument removal was 2.7% [2.2–3.1%]. The two-year cumulative incidence of interspinous fracture was 1.4% [1.1–1.7%]. Complete surgical outcome data are presented in Table 3.

Predictors of reoperation

After multivariable analysis and Bonferroni correction, only gender was found to be a significant predictor of surgical reoperation, with male gender associated with increased risk (aOR: 1.43 [1.12-1.83]), and female gender conferring decreased risk (aOR: 0.70 [0.55-0.89]) (p = 0.004). Other factors including age, surgical indications, and comorbidities were not significantly associated with reoperation. Opioid utilization patterns

Analysis of patients with complete two-year follow-up revealed significant changes in opioid utilization following IPD placement. At baseline, 54.28% were prescribed opioid medications. Opioid utilization decreased to 40.90% at one-month, 33.30% at two months, 29.44% at 12 months, and 27.74% at 24 months postoperatively. McNemar testing confirmed that these reductions were statistically significant at all time points (all p < 0.001), corresponding to absolute decreases of 13.38% [95% CI: −15.12% to −11.65%] at one month, 20.98% [−22.62% to −19.34%] at two months, 24.84% [−26.63% to −23.05%] at 12 months, and 26.54% [−28.42% to −24.67%] at 24 months compared to baseline. Discussion

Real-world data on complications and patient selection for Vertiflex IPD placement outside of FDA IDE trials remains limited. In this study, 3.4% of patients experienced 30-day medical or surgical complications and 6.30% [95% CI 5.7-6.9%] of patients required subsequent lumbar surgery within two years of Vertiflex placement. Predictors of IPD utilization compared to surgical lumbar decompression without fusion included advanced age (80–99 years: aOR 3.54; 70–79 years: aOR 2.29), Medicare insurance (aOR 1.68), female gender (aOR 1.61), residence in the West region (aOR 1.49), and a higher comorbidity burden (ECI ≥5: aOR 1.45). Utilization of IPD peaked in 2020 comprising 1.57% of total lumbar decompressions but declined to 0.80% of cases in 2022. These findings suggest that Vertiflex IPD is primarily selected for older, medically complex patients who may be less suitable candidates for traditional surgical approaches. The two-year reoperation rate found in this population was substantially lower than previously reported in the literature.

This study, which included 6321 patients, represents the largest analysis of Vertiflex IPD placement to date. The observed two-year reoperation rate is notably lower than those reported in previous FDA trials, which documented a two-year reoperation rate of 23.2% and a five-year rate of 25.2% [16,20]. Outside of FDA trial cohorts, data on Vertiflex IPD placement remains limited. For instance, Rosner et al. analyzed Medicare claims data for 3614 patients who underwent IPD placement and reported a two-year surgical reoperation rate of 9.8% [21]. However, their focus on Medicare patients likely selected an older population, and their inclusion of ICD-10 codes for open placement of IPD devices may have inadvertently captured other devices placed after laminectomy, introducing heterogeneity into the analysis. Similar issues with heterogeneity have been observed in other database studies that combined multiple device types or grouped IPDs with percutaneous minimally invasive lumbar decompression (MILD) procedures, thereby limiting the generalizability of their findings[[22], [23], [24]].

The lower reoperation rate observed in this study may reflect differences in patient selection, evolving procedural experience, or other temporal factors; however, cross-study comparisons should be interpreted cautiously given differences in patient demographics and comorbidity burden when compared to the FDA trial populations. Common causes of reoperation include interspinous fractures, device migration, and increased postoperative pain—issues that can often be mitigated by proper device sizing and improved technique during implantation [25]. For instance, ensuring the implant is properly sized and fully advanced fully to the spinolaminar junction can prevent migration and reduce fracture risk [25]. Interestingly, male gender was the only factor significantly associated with increased reoperation risk (aOR 1.43). While this association has been noted previously, its underlying mechanisms remain unclear; some studies conversely suggest that women may present with more advanced spine disease but there has been no differences in clinical improvement following surgery[24].

Historically, high reoperation rates have overshadowed the clinical success of Vertiflex IPD placement; however, these findings suggest that contemporary real-world reoperation rates may differ from those reported in early FDA cohorts. It is also important to note that since most procedures were performed by pain medicine physicians, referral patterns for surgical revision may differ from those in surgical trial populations, potentially influencing observed reoperation rates. Because cross-study comparisons were not risk-adjusted, greater medical complexity in this real-world cohort may have influenced candidacy for revision surgery and contributed to lower observed reoperation rates. While this study did not directly compare the Vertiflex IPD to lumbar decompression reoperation rate, the two-year reoperation rate reported here aligns closely with rates for lumbar laminectomy (6.9%–9.4%), previously reported in systematic reviews aggregating prospective studies on lumbar decompression[26,27].

The 3.4% short-term medical and surgical complication rate observed in this study aligns with prior research on Vertiflex IPD placement, which has demonstrated lower short-term complication rates compared to surgical lumbar decompression or fusion and is largely expected due to its minimally invasive nature [15,28,29]. In comparison, the 30-day complication rate for lumbar laminectomy without fusion has been reported in the literature to range from 4.8% to 5.6% [[30], [31], [32]]. Additionally, the interspinous fracture rate observed in this study (1.6%) is notably lower than previously reported rates in the literature; Patel et al. documented a fracture rate of 16.4% at two years postoperatively during FDA trials. Interspinous fractures are often attributed to factors such as osteoporosis, dorsal position of the implant, over-distraction during implantation, improper device sizing, or poor technique—all areas that may have improved since the initial FDA cohort through better patient selection and refined techniques [7,15]. However, interspinous fractures captured in this national insurance database required formal diagnostic coding and therefore likely represent symptomatic events only and underreporting their true prevalence compared to studies actively scanning for these fractures.

FDA IDE trials have shown significant improvements in patient-reported outcomes following Vertiflex IPD placement, including reductions in pain and claudication symptoms, along with improved functionality and quality of life [16,20]. When compared to lumbar laminectomy outcomes from the SPORT trial, Vertiflex demonstrated comparable improvements in Oswestry Disability Index scores and greater effect sizes for physical function and symptom severity, as measured by the Zurich Claudication Questionnaire [14]. Additionally, Nunley et al. reported a reduction in opioid usage from 50% at baseline to 25.2% at 12 months and 13.3% at 24 months after Vertiflex placement [33]. While the opioid usage findings from this study partially align with those of Nunley et al., they indicate no further improvement beyond 12 months. It is important to note the small sample size and limited generalizability of FDA trial data necessitate cautious interpretation of these results, especially since the majority of the data regarding Vertiflex success comes from the single FDA IDE patient cohort and are at a high risk of bias. It is also important to note that the Vertiflex IPD has never been directly compared to traditional lumbar decompression.

Beyond FDA trials, Tekmyster et al.’s multicenter study across 86 clinical sites reported high patient satisfaction rates, approximately 80% at one year postoperatively, with 75% of patients indicating they would undergo the procedure again [34]. Biomechanical studies further support the efficacy of Vertiflex IPD; for example, Goya et al. demonstrated that the device prevents motion at the implanted level without significantly altering spinal anatomy, Nunley et al. demonstrated preserved sagittal alignment at five years post-op, and Falowski et al.’s cadaveric study highlighted increased spinal canal and foraminal dimensions following implantation[20,35,36].

This study reveals that patients undergoing Vertiflex IPD placement are generally much older and have higher comorbidity burdens compared to those receiving surgical decompression procedures, an observation not previously reported in the literature. Interestingly, this real-world data slightly diverges from the manufacturer's and FDA trial recommendations, which advocate for using Vertiflex IPD in patients with mild LSS before their condition progresses to require surgical intervention, potentially targeting a younger population [12,16,20]. A survey of surgeons also identified the inability to tolerate general anesthesia as an indication for choosing IPD placement, despite this indication not being officially endorsed by the manufacturer or reported elsewhere [37]. This aligns with our findings that Vertiflex is frequently utilized in older patients with significant comorbidities may be poor candidates for more invasive surgeries. Overall, these findings suggest that Vertiflex IPD may play an important clinical role as a less invasive option for managing mild LSS in patients unsuitable for traditional surgical procedures.

Other factors associated with Vertiflex IPD usage included Medicare or Medicaid insurance compared to commercial insurance, as Vertiflex is covered by public insurance options but is not covered by many commercial insurances [38,39]. The limited commercial insurance coverage for interspinous process devices, compared to broader coverage under Medicare, may also contribute to the older age of patients receiving IPDs versus those undergoing traditional lumbar decompression. Residing in the South or the West region of the US were also linked with increased IPD placement, which could be linked to regional variation in insurance coverage, provider emphasis on minimally invasive options, and availability of procedure. Scoliosis, degenerative spondylolisthesis, and lumbar disc herniation were all associated with decreased Vertiflex IPD usage, as these can serve as contraindications to Vertiflex placement[12].

Annual utilization trends revealed an increase in IPD use from 0.21% of total decompressions in 2017 to a peak of 1.57% in 2020 before declining to 0.79%. Vertiflex was acquired by Boston Scientific in 2019; however, utilization declined after 2020. The decline in Vertiflex utilization after 2020 may reflect broader shifts in minimally invasive stenosis management, including increased adoption of alternative percutaneous interventions such as the MILD procedure, which avoids implantable hardware. Additionally, the position statement from several major spine societies advising against the placement of IPDs and other biomechanic-altering interventions by non-surgical physicians was published in 2021, which could've possibly contributed to the decline and additional focus on MILD[17].

It is important to contextualize these findings within recent regulatory developments. Although our study period ended in early 2023, the Superion IPD device was subject to a Class II recall in June 2025 following reports that the system was not consistently meeting expected performance during the implantation procedure, including instrument and device breakage [40]. As part of the recall, clinicians were instructed to halt new implantations and cease distribution of affected product, although routine explant of existing devices was not recommended. Consequently, the present findings may have limited immediate applicability to new implantation practices; however, they remain highly relevant for counseling, surveillance, and revision considerations in patients with existing implants. Furthermore, should corrective modifications permit reintroduction of the device in the future, these real-world safety and utilization data will remain clinically informative.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. While procedural data from the PearlDiver database is highly reliable due to its use for billing purposes, diagnostic coding is subject to potential miscoding or incomplete physician input, limiting clinical nuance. Additionally, as an administrative claims database, PearlDiver does not capture validated patient-reported outcome measures such as the Oswestry Disability Index or Zurich Claudication Questionnaire. Therefore, clinical effectiveness could not be directly assessed, and outcomes were instead inferred using proxy measures including reoperation rates, complication rates, and opioid utilization trends.

Although CPT 22869/22870 are device-neutral procedural codes, the Vertiflex Superion was the only FDA-approved stand-alone interspinous spacer actively marketed in the United States during the 2017–2023 study period. While rare off-label device use (e.g., implantation of devices intended for use with decompression) or isolated miscoding cannot be entirely excluded, such occurrences are expected to represent a small minority of cases and are unlikely to substantially alter the overall findings.

The absence of radiographic data precludes assessment of spinal stenosis severity, spondylolisthesis grade, implant positioning, or other anatomical factors that may influence outcomes. Furthermore, ICD-10 diagnostic codes do not reliably capture radiographic severity grading, limiting the ability to stratify patients by degree of stenosis within an administrative claims dataset. Additionally, interspinous fractures identified in this study required formal diagnostic coding and therefore likely represent symptomatic fractures only, potentially underestimating true fracture incidence compared to studies utilizing routine imaging surveillance. Low rates of surgical intervention may reflect patient preference or poor surgical candidacy rather than improvement in patient outcomes, potentially overestimating clinical success. Additionally, comparisons with other IPDs (e.g., XSTOP or Coflex) or decompression procedures were not included here, limiting broader contextualization.

Conclusion

This study underscores that Vertiflex IPD is predominantly being utilized in older patients with more comorbidities compared to those undergoing surgical lumbar decompression. This trend likely reflects its selection as a minimally invasive alternative for patients who are poor candidates for conventional surgical decompression. The observed two-year reoperation rate of 6.30% is lower than previously reported in FDA IDE trials; however, cross-study comparisons are not risk-adjusted, and differences in patient age and comorbidity burden may partially account for this variation. Improvements in patient selection and procedural technique over time may also contribute to the observed difference. Vertiflex utilization peaked in 2020, accounting for 1.57% of lumbar decompressions, but declined to 0.80% in 2022. This decrease may be attributed to ongoing controversy surrounding the procedure and a growing emphasis on alternative treatments, such as the MILD procedure. Further research is needed to investigate long-term outcomes and improve patient selection criteria, including independent randomized controlled trials comparing Vertiflex IPD to surgical lumbar decompression. Additionally, prospective collection of real-world patient-reported outcomes is essential to validate findings and strengthen comparative effectiveness analyses. These efforts will help define the clinical utility of Vertiflex IPD and support evidence-based decision-making in the management of moderate lumbar spinal stenosis.
