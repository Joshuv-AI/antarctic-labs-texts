Molecular characterization of macrophage-related prognostic factors in glioblastoma revealed by combined analysis on single-cell and bulk transcriptome data
Authors: Zhihao Wei, Hongchao Liu, Yajun Yang, Mengting Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlioblastoma (GBM) is an aggressive primary tumor in the brain. The use of single-cell transcriptomic analysis can help to identify distinct cell subtypes and their functional states, and offer potential for advancing personalized therapeutic strategies for GBM.MethodsSingle-cell data were preprocessed using the Seurat and Harmony packages, and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified via the FindAllMarkers function. The high-dimensional WGCNA was carried out on the myeloid cells of single-cell data to screen out the macrophage-related modules, followed by performing enrichment analysis on the module genes with clusterProfiler package. Through univariate Cox-LASSO regression with package survival, the module genes were further screened and compressed to determine the core genes and construct a prognostic model. Patients were stratified by the cutoff values of the risk scores into high- and low-risk groups. The IOBR package was used to evaluate the differences in immune infiltration. The expression differences of immune checkpoints were compared, and the drug sensitivity of GBM patients was tested by the R package oncoPredict.ResultsThe proportion of Macro_PLIN2 subpopulation was significantly more in the tumor group, showing a higher activity in the blue and red modules. Eight core genes were further identified, namely SARNP, TGM2, G0S2, ACAP1, UPP1, POR, SLC43A3, and HPCAL1. Immune infiltration analysis revealed strong positive correlations between most core genes and the stromal score, immune score, and ESTIMATE score. The expression of PDCD1, PD-L1, CTLA4 and TIGIT in the high-risk group was significantly higher than those in the low-risk group. The drugs BI.2536, Daporinad, KU.55,933, and Ribociclib showed significant associations with the expression of the majority of core genes.ConclusionThis study reveals the molecular characteristics of key prognostic factors in GBM, highlighting the importance of immune cell abundance and drug sensitivity in glioma treatment, and provides potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for future clinical research and treatment strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03816-1.Keywords: Glioblastoma, Macrophage, Immune infiltration, Immune checkpoint, Drug sensitivity

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most aggressive and lethal primary tumor in the brain, with a dismal median survival time of only 16–18 months [1–3]. GBM originates from astrocytes, and the associated risk factors that lead to the development of GBM involve genetic factors, environmental exposures (e.g., ionizing radiation and chemicals), viral infections, and abnormalities at the cellular molecular level, including the activation and inhibition of key signaling pathways, gene mutations, and epigenetic alterations [4]. The current standard of care for GBM patients consists of a combination of surgical resection, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy, which is not very effective [5]. New therapeutic approaches, including targeted therapy and combined chemo-immunotherapy, hold promise for improving clinical outcomes [6]. Immune checkpoint differences refer to differences in the activity and expression of immune checkpoint molecules in different individuals or different tumors. Investigating variations in immune checkpoint expression may help uncover new therapeutic strategies for GBM immunotherapy. In addition, it has been found that the search for biomarkers associated with GBM and the establishment of prognostic models can help predict the future health status of patients as well as be informative for targeted therapy [7, 8].

Single-cell analysis is an effective method that characterizes genome-scale molecular data at the level of single cell [9]. It can reveal intercellular heterogeneity, i.e., cell-to-cell differences, which is not possible with traditional bulk sequencing (sequencing on a multicellular basis) [10, 11]. Among single-cell analysis techniques, scRNA-seq measures the levels of RNA expression within a single cell, thereby helping researchers to identify and understand cell subtypes and different functional states, cellular plasticity, and cell differentiation or reprogramming trajectories [12, 13]. Recent advances in single-cell technology have enabled high-throughput molecular analysis of cells across modalities and locations, including large-scale cell atlas construction, refinement of cell subsets and identification of rare cell types, as well as understanding transcriptional dynamics and gene regulatory relationships [14].

Therefore, based on the above background, the identification of disease-related core genes by multi-omics techniques [15] (including single-cell analysis) and the development of prognostic models are necessary and offer higher-efficacy therapeutic choices for patients. Our central hypothesis was that a deep integrative analysis of scRNA-seq and bulk transcriptome data could reveal novel macrophage-driven molecular heterogeneity in GBM, leading to a more robust prognostic signature and the identification of potential therapeutic targets. In addition, the differences in immune cell abundance, immune checkpoints and drug sensitivity were examined by dividing the samples into high- and low-risk groups, which will provide more references for immunotherapy and personalized treatment of GBM.

Materials and methods

Acquisition of datasets

This study included three datasets. Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database was accessed to obtain the single-cell data GSE162631 of GBM patients (four disease samples and four paraneoplastic samples). Transcriptomic data of GBM patients, including 170 disease samples, was downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database as a training set. Transcriptome data mRNAseq_325 from GBM patients, including 272 disease samples, was downloaded from the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) database as a validation set.

Blood cis-expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) data were obtained from the eQTLGen consortium, comprising 16,989 genes derived from 31,684 healthy individuals of European ancestry. Statistically significant cis-eQTLs (false discovery rate, FDR < 0.05) along with allele frequency data were retrieved. GWAS summary statistics for gliomas in European populations were sourced from the FinnGen database (finngen_R10_C3_GBM_EXALLC), including 253 cases and 314,193 controls.

Pre-processing of data

The 10×count matrix of single-cell data was analyzed using the Seurat (version 4.1.2) package [16]. To exclude some low-quality cells and low-expressed genes, cells containing 500–5000 features and 1,000–15,000 counts were retained while excluding cells with mitochondrial genes exceeding 10% of total counts. After normalizing the scRNA-seq data with the NormalizeData function [16], 2000 highly variable genes were identified using the “vst” method in the FindVariableFeatures function [16]. The RunPCA function was employed to conduct principal component analysis (PCA) after data normalization with the function ScaleData [16], and the number of PCs was selected by visual plotting with the ElbowPlot function. Visualization plots were performed using the RunUMAP and RunTSNE function [16], setting n.neighbors to 30 and dims to 1:30.

Batch correction was performed using the R package harmony [17] to avoid batch effects with parameters (group.by.vars = “Batch”, theta = 2, lambda = 1, plot_convergence = TRUE). Using the FindNeighbors function [16] with the first 30 PCs, a shared nearest neighbor plot was constructed, and then the cells were clustered by the FindClusters function [16] at resolution = 0.8. Cell clusters were annotated based on classical markers and cell markers from a past study [18].

The transcriptome data was processed by first deleting the null loaded probes, deleting a probe when it corresponds to more than one gene. When more than one probe matched to the same gene, the median expression value of these probes served as the gene expression.

Identification of modular genes

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between each cell type in the single-cell dataset were identified with the FindAllMarkers function by setting min.pct = 0.1, logfc.threshold = 0.25, and retaining genes with p < 0.05.

The hdWGCNA is an advanced method based on WGCNA, which is specialized in processing high-dimensional transcriptomics data, including spatial transcriptomics data and scRNA-seq [19]. It identifies gene modules by constructing co-expression networks between genes, and performs module signature gene analysis, module enrichment analysis, etc., so as to reveal biological background information and potential biological mechanisms. Utilizing the TestSoftPowers function in hdWGCNA to identify soft thresholds. Employing the ConstructNetwork function (networkType = “signed”, tom_type = “signed”, pamStage = TRUE, mergeCutHeight = 0.15) to build co-expression networks. Applying the ModuleFeaturePlot function to depict modular gene expression. Additionally, proteins interaction network was generated with the ModuleCorrelogram function to select the top ten genes in each module and compute the correlations.

Enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis tool was used for the annotation of genes and their products, including three main categories: cellular components (CC), molecular functions (MF) and biological processes (BP). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) is a collection of multiple databases containing information about genomes, biological pathways, diseases and chemicals. GO analysis and KEGG pathway analysis were performed on the modular genes using the enrichGO, enrichKEGG functions of the package clusterProfiler 4.0 to infer their biological significance [20]. The parameters used were pvalueCutoff = 0.05, pAdjustMethod = “BH”, and qvalueCutoff = 0.5.

Acquisition of core genes and prognostic analysis

The survival package [21] coxph function was applied to preform univariate Cox analysis on the module genes to screen the prognostic factors related to GBM, and glmnet function [22] was employed to conduct the LASSO with family = “cox” and nfolds = 10 to obtain core genes of GBM and develop the prognostic model based on the core genes. Survival information was visualized using the ggsurvplot function [23], and the difference boxplot of the core genes was plotted using ggboxplot [24]. The samples were allocated by the cutoff expression value of the core genes into high- and low-risk groups, followed by survival comparison between the two groups using Kaplan-Meier (KM) curves. Expression profiles of core genes were assessed for differences between high- and low-risk patients and between tumor and adjacent normal tissues.

Immune infiltration analysis

The ESTIMATE algorithm in package IOBP [25] was performed to estimate the immune score, stromal score, and tumor purity of the samples. Differences in the three scores between samples from the tumor and normal group were compared with the Wilcoxon test. Subsequently, the infiltration scores of immune cell were quantified by the CIBERSORT program with 1000 permutations to ensure results significance. The LM22 matrix used in CIBERSORT is a well-established and validated leukocyte gene signature matrix derived from purified human leukocyte subsets, and its accuracy for deconvolving bulk tumor transcriptomes has been extensively validated in numerous studies [26].

Immune checkpoint analysis

The immune checkpoint genes include a total of 85 genes such as PDCD1, CD274 and TIGIT, etc. Based on bulk transcriptome data, we compared the immune checkpoint profiles between the high- and low-expression cohorts of the core genes.

Drug sensitivity analysis

Using R package oncoPredict [27], the sensitivity of drugs in GBM patients was predicted based on cell line expression data and drug response information in the GDSC2 (http://www.cancerrxgene.org) database, combined with the expression profiles of TCGA-GBM cohort in the training set to estimate the IC50. Additionally, the correlation between core gene expression and drug IC50 values was also calculated.

Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis

MR analysis was performed using the R package TwoSampleMR V.0.5.6.24. Instrumental variables were selected as single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) significantly associated with gene expression (FDR < 0.05) and located within ± 1 Mb of the gene. Linkage disequilibrium clumping was performed (r² < 0.01, window size = 10,000 kb) using European ancestry data from the 1000 Genomes Project. Exposure and outcome datasets were harmonized using built-in functions. For single-SNP associations, effect estimates were derived using the Wald ratio method. When multiple SNPs were available, inverse-variance weighted analysis was applied. Horizontal pleiotropy was assessed via the MR-Egger intercept test (applied when ≥ 3 SNPs were available). Heterogeneity among Wald ratios was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test.

Real-time PCR analysis

To investigate the mRNA expression levels of core genes in normal human astrocytes and U87MG cells, we performed real-time PCR analysis. The normal astrocytes and U87MG cells were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum at 37℃ in a 5% CO₂ incubator. When cells reached 60%–70% confluence, they were harvested for RNA extraction using the Qiagen RNeasy Mini Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop 2000, and RNA integrity was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis. Subsequently, 1 µg of RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the Thermo Fisher High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit. PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix on a real-time PCR system. Gene expression levels were normalized to the housekeeping gene GAPDH and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate, and the results were presented as the mean ± SD. PCR primer sequences were recorded in Supplemental Table S1.

Statistical analysis

All statistics were performed using R software (version 4.1.2). And p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Single-cell data analysis

After quality control of the single-cell dataset, 77,168 cells remained, and 24,983 genes were detected (Fig. S1). Among these, 2000 genes including IGLC2, CXCL5, and IGLC3 were defined as highly variable genes, and the top 30 PCs were retained for later analysis (Fig. S2), using Harmony to remove batch effects between samples (Fig. S3). A resolution of 0.8 was selected for subsequent analysis by comparing across multiple resolutions, and a total of 23 distinct cell clusters were detected (Fig. 1A, Fig. S4, Fig. S5). Subsequently, marker-based annotation categorized these into eleven major cell types, including endothelial_cell (2650), CD8+T (821), glia_neuronal (110), macrophage (19407), microglia (46455), microglia_macrophage (1538), mural_cell (995), neutrophils (1972), plasmablasts (478), proliferating (1820), unknown (922) (Fig. 1B). The percentage of cells in the different groups was counted, and the results showed an increase in the percentage of macrophage and a decrease in the percentage of microglia (Fig. 1C).

Single-cell data annotation of major cell types. A Distinct cell clusters identified at a resolution of 0.8. B t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) plot for major cell types. C Differences in the percentage of major cell types

Macrophage subtypes re-annotation

Re-annotation of macrophage identified one dendritic cell population, one mono population, and four macrophage subtypes, and comparison of the change in the percentage of these cells showed that the Macro_PLIN2 subtype was significantly increased in the tumor group (Fig. 2A-C, p < 0.001), and thus more attention was paid to the Macro_PLIN2 subtype in the later study.

Single-cell data re-annotated of macrophage subtypes. A Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) plot of macrophage subtypes. B Distribution of macrophage subtypes in GBM and normal samples. C Difference in the percentage of macrophage subtypes

Identification of module genes

The hdWGCNA analysis of myeloid cells in single-cell data was performed to screen for macrophage-associated modules, and the optimal soft threshold was found to be six (Fig. 3A). A total of 12 modules were identified (Fig. 3B), of which the blue module and red module were highly activated in Macro_PLIN2 cells (Fig. 3C and D). Thus, the genes of these two modules were identified as key genes. The proteins interaction network was built using the top ten genes of each module and the correlation was calculated, and it was found that there was correlation in all modules except brown-red, green-greenyellow, and yellow-green modules (Fig. 3E and F).

The hdWGCNA analysis of single-cell data. A The determination of soft threshold. B Cluster dendrogram analysis. C Top 10 hub genes expression profile per module. D Correlation between modules and macrophage subtypes. E Inter-module correlation plot. F Protein network distribution between modules

Enrichment analysis of module genes

All 515 genes in the blue and red modules were analyzed for functional enrichment (Fig. 4). KEGG analysis suggested that module genes were mainly enriched in the hypoxia inducible factor-1 pathway, nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) signaling pathway, interleukin-17 pathway, and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (Fig. 4A). The analysis of GO-BP indicated that module genes were enriched in the response to hypoxia, cytokine production and nucleotide phosphorylation (Fig. 4B), and the analysis of GO-MF showed that these module genes were enriched in the cadherin binding, histone demethylase activity and dioxygenase activity (Fig. 4C), while GO-CC analysis revealed that the module genes were enriched in focal adhesion, secretory granule membrane and ficolin-1-rich granule (Fig. 4D).

Functional enrichment of module genes. A KEGG pathway analysis. B GO-BP analysis. C GO-MF analysis. D GO-CC analysis

Prognostic analysis of core genes

Module genes were further compressed using univariate Cox-LASSO regression, and the results showed that eight core genes were significantly associated with GBM prognosis, namely SARNP, TGM2, G0S2, ACAP1, UPP1, POR, SLC43A3 and HPCAL1 (Fig. 5A). Then the prognostic model was constructed according to the expression and coefficient of each core gene, and the cutoff of risk score categorized the GBM into high- and low-risk groups. Next, KM curves were performed for the prognostic model, and it was found that the patients in the high-risk cohort had worse survival compared with GBM patients with lower score in both the training set and validation set (Fig. 5B and C, p < 0.01). Notably, in both the training set and the validation set, the high-risk GBM group showed a markedly higher risk score, and notably shorter survival time (Fig. 5D and E).

Development and external validation of prognostic model for GBM. A Cox-LASSO regression to screen the key prognostic genes. B, C KM curves analysis of the training and validation datasets. D, E The risk score distributions, survival outcomes, and prognostic gene expression profiles across training and validation cohorts

Prognostic and expression patterns of core genes

To investigate the role of core genes in GBM, we performed differential expression analysis to identify their expression, and found that TGM2, G0S2, ACAP1, UPP1, POR, SLC43A3 and HPCAL1 were significantly increased, while SARNP were significantly decreased in high-risk group (Fig. 6A, p < 0.05). Further survival analysis also suggested that the markedly elevated expression of SARNP, TGM2, G0S2, ACAP1, UPP1, POR, and SLC43A3 predicted worse prognosis (Fig. 6B, p < 0.001). Besides, we also investigated and verified the mRNA expression of the core genes via GEPIA web server and real-time PCR, and the PCR results showed that the expression of TGM2, G0S2, ACAP1, UPP1, POR, and SLC43A3 were upregulated in GBM, while SARNP were downregulated (Fig. S6). These cellular experimental findings were consistent with the results of bioinformatics analysis.

Validation of core gene expression and survival. A Expression differences of core genes between high- and low-risk groups. B KM survival curves of prognostic genes

Immune infiltration and core genes

Immune assessment of the transcriptomic data of GBM revealed that immune, stromal, and ESTIMATE scores were significantly higher in the tumor group than in the normal group (Fig. 7A, p < 0.001). Subsequent analysis of immune infiltration revealed that the infiltration levels of eosinophils, natural killer (NK) cells activated, CD8+T, activated mast cells and follicular helper cells were significantly higher in the normal group than in the tumor group, whereas macrophages M2, NK cells resting and neutrophils showed significantly higher abundance in tumor cohort compared to normal cohort (Fig. 7B, p < 0.05).

Immune infiltration analysis in GBM. A Differences in stromal score, immune score, ESTIMATE score and tumor purity between tumor and normal group. B Differences in the infiltration levels of immune cells between tumor and normal group. C Heatmap of correlation between stromal, immune, ESTIMATE scores, tumor purity and core genes. D Heatmap visualization of the correlation matrix between core gene expression and immune cell infiltration levels.*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001

Given that immune cells have been shown to influence tumor development, quantitative analysis was performed to examine associations between core gene expression patterns and immune cell infiltration profiles. In GBM, apart from POR, which did not show a significant correlation, and SARNP, which correlated inversely with the other six genes, ACAP, G0S2, HPCAL, SLC43A3, TGM2, and TGM2 all exhibited significant inverse correlations with tumor purity while showing positive associations with stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores (Fig. 7C, p < 0.01), suggesting that the core genes acted as regulators to promote or inhibit immune cell infiltration and have different effects on cancer development. Further analysis of the correlation between immune cells and core genes showed that NK cells activated was negatively correlated with most of the core genes, while T cells regulatory (Tregs) and activated CD4 memory cells were positively correlated with most of the core genes (Fig. 7D, p < 0.05).

Immune checkpoint analysis

The clinical significance of immune checkpoint differences lies in their ability to influence an individual’s responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy. Quantitative analysis of the TCGA-GBM training set identified 74 immune checkpoints, and revealed markedly higher expression of immune checkpoint molecules (PD-L1, PDCD1, CTLA4, TIGIT) in the high-risk cohort versus low-risk group (Fig. 8A, p < 0.001). Correlation analysis revealed significant associations between the majority of immune checkpoints and core genes, suggesting that our macrophage-derived signature was linked to an immunosuppressive microenvironment and the core genes within this signature may modulate response to immunotherapy (Fig. 8B, Fig. S7).

Correlation analysis of immune checkpoints and core genes. A Expression difference of CTLA4, PDCD1, PD-L1 and TIGIT between high and low-risk groups. B Heatmap of the top 20 correlation between core genes and immune checkpoints

Drug sensitivity analysis

In order to assess the impact of core genes on therapeutic sensitivity, we conducted a systematic analysis of drug sensitivity based on pharmacogenomics. Based on the training dataset, drug sensitivity analysis revealed differential responses between risk groups: high-risk GBM specimens exhibited significantly higher predicted sensitivity to KU.55,933 (ATM inhibitor) and Ribociclib (CDK4/6 inhibitor), while low-risk samples were more sensitive to BI.2536 (PLK1 inhibitor) and Daporinad (NAMPT inhibitor) (Fig. 9A, p < 0.001). Based on drug-target information, we also found that BI.2536, Daporinad, KU.55,933, and Ribociclib drugs were most associated with core genes (Fig. 9B, Fig. S8). The strong correlation between core genes and drug responsiveness suggested their potential role in modulating the efficacy of both chemotherapy and targeted therapy. These findings indicated the clinical utility of core genes as predictive biomarkers for treatment response in GBM.

Correlation analysis of drug sensitivity and core genes. A Difference in sensitivity of BI.2536, Daporinad, KU.55,933, and Ribociclib between high and low-risk groups. B Heatmap of the top 20 correlation matrix between core genes and drug IC50 values

MR analysis of the core genes and GBM

MR analysis on the core genes and GBM showed no significant relationship between the eQTL of the genes and the occurrence of GBM (Table S2), suggesting that the core genes did not influence the development of GBM through single gene expression.

Discussion

GBM is one of the most aggressive malignancies [28]. Investigating intratumoral heterogeneity and developing novel prognostic models to enable timely testing of personalized therapeutic strategies for GBM remains a critical preclinical challenge. This research used single-cell and transcriptome data analysis to select key prognostic factors in GBM and found that eight macrophage-related genes were significantly associated with patient prognosis. Unlike previous models often based on bulk transcriptome data alone, our signature was derived from macrophage-specific genes identified at the single-cell level, ensuring greater cellular specificity. In addition, our use of hdWGCNA and more comprehensive validation across multiple cohorts strengthens the reliability and clinical applicability of our findings. Furthermore, the study suggested that eight core genes exhibited strong correlations with immune cell infiltration, immune checkpoint expression, and drug sensitivity profiles. This finding provides a new scientific basis for personalized treatment of GBM and offers potential clinical insights into targeted treatment strategies.

In current study, we finally identified eight core genes closely related with GBM, namely SARNP, TGM2, G0S2, ACAP1, UPP1, POR, SLC43A3, and HPCAL1. SARNP, fully known as Sam68 interacting RNA binding protein, plays a role in mRNA splicing and exportation, and negatively modulates E-cadherin levels through direct binding with pinin [29]. Previous studies have similarly found that decreased SARNP expression was associated with glioma due to abnormal protein synthesis or cell division [30], and SARNP expression increased in GBM cells exposed to radiation [31]. TGM2, or transaminase 2, the most structurally sophisticated and ubiquitously expressed isoform in the transglutaminase family, demonstrates significant overexpression in gliomas [32]. Aberrant expression of TGM2 was associated with GBM stemness, invasion, and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition. Emerging evidence indicates that TGM2 promotes tumor survival by enhancing DNA repair, fostering cellular plasticity, and facilitating apoptosis evasion. TGM2 is overexpressed in both radiation-resistant GBM cells and tissues, and the formation of its copolymers contributes to the enhancement of radiation resistance in GBM [33]. G0S2, known as G0/G1 switch gene 2, was originally obtained in lymphocytes with pharmacologically stimulated G0 to G1 transition [34]. G0S2 expression is elevated in clinical GBM and correlates with low survival, and its overexpression similarly contributes to the radioresistance of GBM cells [35]. G0S2 was also identified as a key metabolic regulator in gliomas. G0S2 knockout unleashed anti-tumor immunity by activating the type I interferon pathway and enhancing CD8+ T cell function, which synergized with anti-PD-1 treatment. ACAP1, a GTPase-activating protein, regulates the endocytosis recirculation and cell migration processes [36], and correlates with immune infiltration in a variety of cancers [37]. ACAP1, enriched in inflammatory, immune response, and cytokine pathways regulation, was previously identified to be associated with GBM progression as well as the drug resistance pathway [38].

Uridine phosphorylase 1 (UPP1), encoded by the UPP1 gene, regulates uridine metabolism through its reversible catalysis of uridine-to-uracil conversion [39]. Emerging evidence identifies UPP1 as a critical oncogenic driver in GBM, promoting both tumorigenesis and immune evasion [40], and its expression is up-regulated with increasing grades of glioma [41] and its hypermethylation correlates with the development of gliomas [42]. c-Jun exhibits significantly stronger binding affinity to the Vimentin and UPP1 promoters in GBM cells compared to benign glioma cells, suggesting enhanced transcriptional activation in malignant progression [43], which can be used as a biomarker for GBM [44]. POR, full name as Cytochrome P450 oxidoreductase, transfers electrons from NADPH to cellular Cytochrome P450 oxidoreductase [45], and its relationship with GBM needs further study at present. SLC43A3, or solute carrier family 43 member 3, was associated with purine and fatty acid metabolism [46], which not only exhibits a high expression status in glioma grading as well as glioma cell lines [47], but also plays an important role in promoting the malignant phenotype of GBM including proliferation and migration [48]. The high SLC43A3 expression group exhibited significant activation of key tumor-progressive pathways, including PI3K-Akt and JAK-STAT. HPCAL1, known as hippocalcin-like protein 1, is a neuronal calcium sensor protein found in the brain and retina. HPCAL1 potentiates Wnt/β-catenin signaling in GBM through dual regulation of β-catenin stabilization and nuclear import, while genetic ablation of β-catenin dramatically suppresses GBM cell proliferation and tumor growth [49]. In summary, these eight genes obtained in this study have been shown to be associated with GBM, and the constructed model is also informative for the prediction of GBM.

Analysis of the single-cell data showed increased number of macrophages and reduced number of microglia in the GBM tumor microenvironment. Meanwhile, stromal, immune, ESTIMATE scores, as well as Tregs and activated CD4 memory cells, showed significant positive correlations with most of the core genes. Tumor-associated microglia and brain macrophages interact with T cells, and this interaction may result in T cell dysfunction, which in turn impairs T cell-mediated anti-tumor responses and ultimately has an impact on the development of GBM [50, 51]. Further studies suggested significant overrepresentation of modular genes in the NF-κB signaling pathway. In tumor cells, constitutive NF-κB activation promotes tumorigenesis of GBM through inflammatory signaling, and is also an important factor leading to the development of chemotherapy resistance [52]. In addition, cytokines derived from macrophages and microglia have been shown to induce the expression of the core gene TGM2 by NF-κB-mediated regulation [53]. In conclusion, it is likely that macrophages and microglia influence the core genes expression through the transcriptional regulation of NF-κB pathway, thus playing an important role in the development of GBM.

Immunotherapy targeting GBM stem cells has progressed, and differential gene expression and metabolic phenotypes influence the response of GBM stem cells to immunotherapy [54]. TIGIT expression was significantly higher in the high-expression group than in the low-expression group. TIGIT is an inhibitory receptor containing the structural domains of T-cell immunoglobulin and tyrosine inhibitory motifs of immunoreceptor proteins [55], which is an important immune checkpoint protein, and higher TIGIT expression allowed tumor cells to escape immune surveillance and killing. In addition, this study found that BI.2536 drug was associated with the most core genes. This drug has also been found as a potential drug for cancer in previous studies [56]. Treatment with BI.2536 diminished glioma stem cells self-renewal, and decreased proliferation by cell cycle arrest, leading to cell death in GBM [57]. BI.2536 represents a promising candidate for GBM therapy due to its ability to target critical pathways involved in cell cycle regulation and apoptosis. Daporinad, a NAMPT inhibitor that permeates the blood-brain barrier, exhibited potent cytotoxicity in a diverse panel of human GBM models, as well as in murine GBM neurospheres. Furthermore, Daporinad significantly attenuated cell migration in wound-healing assays, indicating its potential to suppress infiltrative tumor growth [58]. In view of these elements, personalized treatment as well as stratified treatment for patients with GBM is necessary.

This present research has some limitations. First, the current data integration analysis relied on public datasets, and different datasets may differ in terms of sample sources, processing methods, and patient heterogeneity, which may constrain the extrapolation of results to broader populations. Therefore, further exploration in combination with larger sample size and multi-omics data is needed in subsequent studies. In addition, the absence of external validation cohorts beyond CGGA or lack of wet-lab functional assays weakened clinical translation. It should be noted that the computational estimates of immune infiltration were also not corroborated by experimental techniques like immunohistochemistry or flow cytometry. The core genes and drugs obtained in this study were not experimentally validated, and further in vitro and in vivo experimental validation could help to further confirm the relationship between the relevant genes and GBM as well as the actual efficacy of BI.2536. For example, the addition of knockdown/overexpression experiments to verify the function of the core genes and colony formation to test the efficacy of BI.2536 on GBM models. In vivo validation will involve in xenograft mouse models to investigate the functions of the core genes and the specific effects of BI.2536 in glioma and optimizing its delivery to maximize therapeutic outcomes. The drug sensitivity predictions, while biologically insightful, were computational inferences derived from public pharmacogenomic databases. Experimental validation using in vitro or in vivo models is essential to confirm the differential sensitivity of high-risk and low-risk GBM to the identified compounds. While preclinical evidence exists, clinical translation for GBM would require further validation. The association between the identified signature and immune microenvironment components-such as immune cell subsets, and PD-L1 expression-warrants further investigation using multiplex immunohistochemistry or spatial transcriptomics. Our future work will also focus on the prospective validation of this prognostic model by incorporating it as a biomarker stratification tool within upcoming clinical trials for GBM, assessing its utility in predicting patient response to standard-of-care and novel immunotherapies.

Conclusion

In this study, eight core genes closely related to GBM, namely SARNP, TGM2, G0S2, ACAP1, UPP1, POR, SLC43A3, and HPCAL1, were selected through bioinformatics analysis, and an innovative prognostic model was established based on them, which provides a potential basis for clinical individualized treatment decision-making as well as new possible therapeutic targets. In addition, the new potential drug BI.2536 proposed in this study provides new ideas and references for the optimization of clinical therapeutic strategies for GBM, and brings new hope for the improvement of the prognosis of GBM patients.
