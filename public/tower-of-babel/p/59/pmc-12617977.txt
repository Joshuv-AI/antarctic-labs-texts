Impact of a Novel Oral Health Tracker cum Motivator Versus Conventional Education on Oral Health Behavior Among 10- to 15-Year-Old School Children in Lucknow: A Single-Blinded, Parallel Design—A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: S Deepak, Gaurav Mishra, Vinay Kumar Gupta, Sumit Kumar, Aman Rajput, Mohit Kumar Kanoujia, Sreeja Gummalla, N Rinsi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:Oral health plays a crucial role in overall well-being, particularly during childhood when positive habits are best established. Despite ongoing efforts, traditional oral health education methods often fall short in effectively influencing long-term behavior change. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a novel oral health tracker cum motivator (OHTM) in comparison to conventional educational methods for improving oral health behaviors among school-aged children in Lucknow.Materials and Methods:In a single-blinded, parallel design, and RCS, 64 children were randomly assigned (computer-generated sequence; opaque sealed envelopes) to either the experimental group (OHTM) or the control group (traditional education). “Single-blinded” refers to outcome assessors being unaware of group allocation. Sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.7 for 80% power, α = 0.05, and effect size d = 0.31, based on Kumar et al. (2015). Baseline and follow-up assessments at 2 months used a 15-item theory of planned behavior (TPB)-based questionnaire and the debris index (oral hygiene index-simplified). Primary outcomes were pre-specified as knowledge scores and debris scores; no secondary outcomes were registered. Statistical analyses included paired and independent t tests, repeated measures analysis of variance, effect sizes (Cohen’s d), and 95% confidence intervals (CI).Results:Post-intervention analysis revealed that the experimental group demonstrated a significantly greater improvement in both clinical and behavioral outcomes. Debris scores in the experimental group reduced from 2.06 ± 0.43 to 1.26 ± 0.36 (95% CI: −1.00 to −0.60; P = 0.017; d = 0.64), while the control group showed a reduction from 2.03 ± 0.48 to 1.48 ± 0.35 (95% CI: −0.77 to −0.33; P = 0.017). Knowledge scores improved from 9.09 ± 2.40 to 11.50 ± 1.48 in the experimental group (95% CI: 1.47–3.35; P = 0.002; d = 0.82), compared to 8.63 ± 1.77 to 10.34 ± 1.41 in the control group (95% CI: 0.98–2.44; P = 0.002).Conclusion:The findings highlight that the OHTM significantly improved oral hygiene and knowledge compared to traditional methods. Interactive, theory-based tools showed more engagement and sustained behavior change, aligned with social cognitive theory, and the TPB. These findings suggest that such tools may be scalable in school health programs. The trial is registered with the Clinical Trials Registry–India (CTRI) under registration number CTRI/2025/03/082764.

INTRODUCTION

Health behaviors such as diet, hygiene, and medication adherence play a key role in determining disease risk and overall well-being. Among these, oral health stands out as a key indicator of general health, yet it is often overlooked in health promotion efforts—especially considering its significant clinical and economic burden.[1] Poor oral health in children can result in pain, infection, impaired growth, and diminished quality of life, with long-term consequences extending into adulthood. Despite the known impact of oral diseases, oral health is frequently neglected by national policymakers, particularly in developing countries, where it remains a low public health priority.[2] The World Health Organization and United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization have underscored the need to make every school a “health-promoting school” to strengthen children’s physical, mental, and oral health from an early age.[2,3] Schools offer an ideal setting for large-scale oral health interventions due to their structured environment, wide reach, and the influential role of educators.[3]

Conventionally, school-based oral health education (OHE) has relied on didactic approaches such as lectures, posters, and pamphlets.[4,5] While these methods may convey information, they often fail to capture the attention and engagement.[1] Children have diverse learning needs and respond more effectively to dynamic, participatory tools.[5]

To address this gap, innovative strategies such as serious games, gamification, and interactive mobile technologies have emerged as promising alternatives. These approaches integrate health messages into fun, game-based learning formats—promoting motivation, active participation, and deeper understanding through real-time feedback and decision-making scenarios.[6] While digital tools have demonstrated significant benefits, low-cost, non-digital alternatives may bridge resource gaps in schools with limited technology access.

A 2024 umbrella review by Bhadauria et al.[7] confirmed that school-based oral health interventions using digital tools and interactive formats yield significantly better outcomes in plaque reduction and knowledge retention compared to conventional approaches. Another meta-analysis highlighted the growing evidence base for gamified and app-based education programs in low-resource settings.[8] However, there is limited evidence from randomized controlled trials evaluating a low-cost, non-digital oral health tracker tool compared to conventional education in school-aged children.

We hypothesized that children aged 10–15 years receiving the oral health tracker cum motivator (OHTM) would demonstrate significantly greater improvements in oral health knowledge and hygiene compared to those receiving conventional education. This study aims to assess the effectiveness of the OHTM against traditional teaching methods in improving oral health behavior in this target population [Figure 1].

MATERIALS AND METHODS

STUDY DESIGN AND SETTING

This study employed a parallel design, single-blinded, and randomized controlled study to assess the effectiveness of a novel OHTM compared to conventional OHE methods in improving oral health behaviors among children aged 10–15 years in Lucknow, India. A list of all schools in the city was prepared, and one school was selected using simple random sampling through the lottery method. All eligible children in the specified age group from the selected school were invited to participate. The study was conducted in selected schools within Lucknow city, aiming for diversity in school type while acknowledging that the sample was predominantly urban.

RANDOMIZATION, ALLOCATION CONCEALMENT, AND BLINDING

Participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups using a computer-generated randomization sequence (Random.org software version 5.0). No stratification or block randomization was applied. Allocation concealment was ensured using opaque, sealed envelopes prepared and numbered sequentially by an independent researcher not involved in the study. The envelopes were opened only after participants were enrolled and baseline assessments completed.

Blinding was maintained by ensuring that the outcome assessors were unaware of group assignments. “Single-blinded” in this study specifically refers to blinding of the outcome assessors; participants and those delivering the interventions were not blinded. Participants were informed only about the general study purpose but not the specific intervention comparison.

STUDY POPULATION AND SAMPLING

The sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.7 for a two-tailed independent t test with α = 0.05, power = 80%, and an effect size (Cohen’s d) of 0.31, derived from Kumar et al.[9] In that study, the mean reduction in debris scores was approximately 1.0 (SD ≈ 1.67) for the game-based group and 0.5 (SD ≈ 1.5) for the control group, resulting in a pooled SD of 1.58 and an estimated d = 0.31. This yielded a required sample of 64 participants (32 per group) to detect a clinically meaningful difference.

DATA COLLECTION AND ASSESSMENT

Baseline assessments were conducted for all participants before the intervention. Data were collected using a self-designed, structured 15-item close-ended questionnaire assessing oral health knowledge, attitudes, and practices. Each question was scored as 0 (wrong answer) or 1 (correct answer), with total scores ranging from 0 to 15. In addition, oral hygiene status was evaluated using the debris component of the oral hygiene index-simplified (OHI-S). The debris index-simplified was estimated on six index teeth and recorded, with scores ranging from 0 to 3.

The questionnaire was designed based on the theory of planned behavior (TPB), addressing attitudes, norms, and perceived behavioral control toward oral health. Content validity was confirmed through expert review by a panel of five specialists in dental public health and behavioral science. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach α (α = 0.78), indicating acceptable reliability.

Following pilot testing on 15 school children, minor revisions were made to improve clarity—four questions were reworded, and one was split into two separate items. The pilot size was small due to logistical constraints and to avoid contamination with the main study population; this is acknowledged as a limitation.

Primary outcomes were pre-registered as knowledge scores and debris scores. No secondary outcomes were pre-specified.

To ensure participant adherence to the intervention, weekly school visits were conducted and teacher-confirmed compliance logs were maintained. The study flow, including enrollment, randomization, intervention allocation, follow-up, and analysis, is illustrated in the CONSORT flow diagram [Figure 2]. All participants were retained at follow-up, ensuring 0% attrition. Follow-up assessment was conducted exactly 2 months after the intervention to evaluate changes in outcomes.

The study protocol adhered to ethical guidelines and was approved by the relevant institutional ethics committee (Ethical no: XXXI-PGTSA-IIA/P19). DATA ANALYSIS

All analyses were performed using an intention-to-treat approach, ensuring that all participants randomized were analyzed in their originally assigned groups.

Statistical analyses were conducted using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 24 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Microsoft Excel. Descriptive statistics summarized demographic data. Pre- and post-intervention scores for oral health knowledge and debris scores were compared within groups using paired t tests and between groups using independent t tests. Repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess time and group interaction effects. Despite minor deviations from normality in the experimental group (OHI-S pre: P = 0.020; knowledge pre: P = 0.011), visual inspection and balanced group sizes supported the use of parametric tests under the central limit theorem. No adjustments were made for multiple comparisons; this is noted as a limitation due to increase Type I error risk.

The assumption of sphericity was tested using Mauchly’s test; Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied where sphericity was violated. For ANOVA, degrees of freedom, F-values, P value, and partial eta squared (η²) are reported; future reports will also include 95% confidence intervals. The significance level was set at P < 0.05.

RESULTS

DEMOGRAPHIC CHARACTERISTICS

A total of 64 participants were included in the study, with 19 (29.7%) males and 45 (70.3%) females. The majority of participants were from urban areas (58, 90.6%), while 6 (9.4%) were from rural areas. All 64 participants (32 in each group) completed the study, resulting in 0% attrition. While complete retention is uncommon in randomized controlled trials (RCTs), this was possible due to the short study duration, school-based setting, proactive weekly monitoring, and coordination with school authorities.

Table 1 showing the demographic distribution indicates that the majority of the study participants were female (70.3%) and predominantly from urban areas (90.6%). Educational background varied, with most parents having completed intermediate or high school education. This distribution ensures diversity in the sample, although the predominance of female and urban participants may limit external validity. Group comparisons for demographics using Chi-square showed no significant baseline differences (all P > 0.05).

PRIMARY OUTCOMES

Table 2 presents the changes observed within each group before and after the intervention. In the control group, the average debris score decreased from 2.03 ± 0.48 at baseline to 1.48 ± 0.35 post-intervention—a statistically significant improvement (P = 0.017, Cohen’s d = −1.28, large effect). The experimental group, which used the oral health tracker, showed an even greater reduction from 2.06 ± 0.43 to 1.26 ± 0.36 (P < 0.001, Cohen’s d = −2.00, very large effect), indicating a more pronounced impact. For oral health knowledge, the control group improved from 8.63 ± 1.77 to 10.34 ± 1.41 (P = 0.002, Cohen’s d = 1.06, large effect), while the experimental group demonstrated a larger gain from 9.09 ± 2.40 to 11.50 ± 1.48 (P < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.15, large effect). These results indicate substantial positive changes in both groups, with consistently greater improvements in the oral health tracker group.

REPEATED MEASURES ANALYSIS OF VARIANCE

Table 4 presents the results from the repeated measures ANOVA. There was a significant main effect of time, F (1, 62) = 15.87, P < 0.001, partial η² = .20, indicating that 20% of the variance in scores was explained by improvement over the study period. The main effect of group was also significant, F (1, 62) = 10.25, P = 0.002, partial η² = .15, meaning that 15% of the variance was due to differences between the experimental and control groups, with the experimental group consistently outperforming the control group. The time × group interaction was significant, F (1, 62) = 8.34, P = 0.005, partial η² = .12, indicating that 12% of the variance was explained by the combined effect of time and group, with students using the oral health tracker showing greater improvement over time than controls. No protocol deviations occurred. No subgroup or adjusted analyses were performed. Together, these results suggest that the oral health tracker not only improved oral hygiene and knowledge but did so more effectively than traditional methods.

DISCUSSION

The findings of this study demonstrate the effectiveness of the novel OHTM in improving oral health knowledge and hygiene behaviors among 10- to 15-year-old school children. The intervention group, which used the OHTM, showed significantly greater improvements in debris index scores and oral health knowledge compared to the control group receiving conventional education. These results suggest that integrating interactive and motivational tools into OHE enhances learning and promotes sustained behavioral change.

The OHTM intervention is grounded in the TPB and social cognitive theory (SCT). TPB emphasizes the importance of attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control in influencing intentions and behaviors. OHTM addressed these domains by providing knowledge (attitude), encouraging normative behaviors in a classroom setting (subjective norms), and offering tools for daily self-monitoring (perceived control). From an SCT perspective, the tool also promotes self-efficacy, observational learning, and reinforcement—key mechanisms for sustaining behavior change. Daily tracking, feedback, and progress visualization encouraged students to believe in their ability to maintain oral hygiene behaviors, contributing to the significant improvements observed.

Our findings align with those of Ramezaninia et al.,[5] Lekaram et al.,[10] Shirahmadi et al.,[11] and Sharma et al.,[4] all of whom reported significant improvements using gamified or interactive tools over conventional methods. Notably, Patil et al.[6] in their meta-analysis reported an average effect size of d = 0.56 for gamified interventions on debris scores, which is lower than our observed effect size of d = 0.64. Similarly, for knowledge outcomes, our effect size (d = 0.82) exceeds the average pooled value reported in a 2025 umbrella review by Gugnani and Pandit,[2] suggesting the OHTM has a stronger impact. However, given the small sample size, short follow-up, and gender imbalance, this higher effect sizes should be interpreted with caution.

Approximately 70% of our participants were girls, which may have contributed to the greater observed effect. Studies from Korea and Iran have shown that female adolescents exhibit higher motivation and better oral hygiene adherence, especially in structured settings. A 2024 Iranian cluster-RCT reported 38% greater plaque reduction in female-only classrooms using gamified SCT-based interventions, mirroring our outcomes. Future trials should either stratify randomization by gender or conduct gender-stratified analyses to reduce bias.

The OHTM is a low-cost, non-digital, tabletop tool, making it suitable for large-scale deployment in resource-constrained schools. Its non-reliance on technology and need for minimal training enhance its feasibility. Teachers can integrate OHTM sessions into daily routines without disrupting curriculum time. Preliminary implementation costing indicated that per-unit production was under 100 rupees (approx. $1.14 USD) based on local manufacturing quotes, but a formal cost-effectiveness analysis is required before policy adoption.

Although the current follow-up was limited to 2 months, evidence from Hosseini et al.[12] and Marashi et al.[13] suggests that reinforcement-based, theory-driven tools yield progressively larger gains over 6–12 months. Therefore, we recommend longitudinal trials to evaluate OHTM’s sustained impact and adaptability across different educational contexts.

Despite encouraging results, the study has limitations. The small sample size (n = 64) may limit generalizability, and the 2-month follow-up may not reflect long-term outcomes. Although assessors were blinded, participant blinding was not feasible, potentially introducing performance bias. The questionnaire, while theory-based and pilot tested, has yet to be validated in larger populations. The absence of stratified randomization, combined with a predominantly female (70.3%) and urban (90.6%) sample, may limit applicability to rural or male populations. Moreover, potential confounders such as parental involvement, prior OHE exposure, and the school health environment were not measured and could have influenced outcomes. These limitations, along with the absence of adjustment for multiple comparisons, as this was an exploratory study with a limited number of planned outcomes. Findings should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Nevertheless, the study’s strengths include its randomized controlled design, blinded outcome assessment, 0% attrition, transparent reporting of effect sizes and confidence intervals, and its contribution to the growing evidence supporting the use of innovative and engaging educational tools in school-based oral health programs. No adverse events or unintended effects were reported.

These findings support the vision outlined in the WHO 2023 Global Oral Health Status Report,[14] which urges integration of oral health into school health promotion and emphasizes life-course and equity-based strategies. The OHTM represents a tangible, scalable approach aligned with these recommendations.

CONCLUSION

The OHTM appears to be an effective tool for enhancing oral health knowledge and hygiene behaviors among school children. The significant improvements observed in debris scores and questionnaire outcomes suggest that interactive and motivational approaches may foster better engagement and adherence to oral health practices. Given the small sample size, short follow-up period, and potential sample imbalances, these results should be interpreted with caution. Future studies with larger, more diverse samples, stratified randomization, and longer follow-up periods are needed to confirm its impact, assess long-term sustainability, and evaluate cost-effectiveness for large-scale implementation.
