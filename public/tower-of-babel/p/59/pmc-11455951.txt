Detecting cardiac states with wearable photoplethysmograms and implications for out-of-hospital cardiac arrest detection
Authors: Mahsa Khalili, Saud Lingawi, Jacob Hutton, Christopher B Fordyce, Jim Christenson, Babak Shadgan, Brian Grunau, Calvin Kuo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOut-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is a global health problem affecting approximately 4.4 million individuals yearly. OHCA has a poor survival rate, specifically when unwitnessed (accounting for up to 75% of cases). Rapid recognition can significantly improve OHCA survival, and consumer wearables with continuous cardiopulmonary monitoring capabilities hold potential to “witness” cardiac arrest and activate emergency services. In this study, we used an arterial occlusion model to simulate cardiac arrest and investigated the ability of infrared photoplethysmogram (PPG) sensors, often utilized in consumer wearable devices, to differentiate normal cardiac pulsation, pulseless cardiac (i.e., resembling a cardiac arrest), and non-physiologic (i.e., off-body) states. Across the classification models trained and evaluated on three anatomical locations, higher classification performances were observed on the finger (macro average F1-score of 0.964 on the fingertip and 0.954 on the finger base) compared to the wrist (macro average F1-score of 0.837). The wrist-based classification model, which was trained and evaluated using all PPG measurements, including both high- and low-quality recordings, achieved a macro average precision and recall of 0.922 and 0.800, respectively. This wrist-based model, which represents the most common form factor in consumer wearables, could only capture about 43.8% of pulseless events. However, models trained and tested exclusively on high-quality recordings achieved higher classification outcomes (macro average F1-score of 0.975 on the fingertip, 0.973 on the finger base, and 0.934 on the wrist). The fingertip model had the highest performance to differentiate arterial occlusion pulselessness from normal cardiac pulsation and off-body measurements with macro average precision and recall of 0.978 and 0.972, respectively. This model was able to identify 93.7% of pulseless states (i.e., resembling a cardiac arrest event), with a 0.4% false positive rate. All classification models relied on a combination of time-, power spectral density (PSD)-, and frequency-domain features to differentiate normal cardiac pulsation, pulseless cardiac, and off-body PPG recordings. However, our best model represented an idealized detection condition, relying on ensuring high-quality PPG data for training and evaluation of machine learning algorithms. While 90.7% of our PPG recordings from the fingertip were considered of high quality, only 53.2% of the measurements from the wrist passed the quality criteria. Our findings have implications for adapting consumer wearables to provide OHCA detection, involving advancements in hardware and software to ensure high-quality measurements in real-world settings, as well as development of wearables with form factors that enable high-quality PPG data acquisition more consistently. Introduction

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is defined as an unexpected cessation of cardiac mechanical activity and blood circulation that occurs outside of the hospital setting1. OHCA is a major public health issue with an increasing number of incidents. A 2010 systematic review estimated an incidence of approximately 4.4 million OHCA events worldwide2. It is estimated that there were about 295,000 OHCAs treated by emergency medical system (EMS) in the United States in 2022, reflecting a significant 21% increase compared to 20173,4. In Canada, OHCA incidents were estimated at 35,000 cases in 2020, with a 9% increase compared to 20155,6. In the absence of a bystander (i.e., unwitnessed events), OHCAs exhibit very poor survival rates (2.2-5.4%)4,7,8. According to the 2022 US Cardiac Arrest Registry to Enhance Survival report, survival rates in EMS-treated cases were 3 times higher for witnessed arrests compared with unwitnessed events4. Similarly, there is substantial evidence that OHCAs in public locations have significantly better patient outcomes compared to incidents that occur in residential settings (survival to hospital discharge of 18.9% for public arrests vs. 7.9% for residential arrests)9,10. This difference is attributed to the greater chance of individuals being “witnessed” in public areas compared to residential settings11. Considering that the majority of OHCAs occur in residential settings (> 75%)12, and approximately 50% of unwitnessed cases are untreated due to evidence of prolonged death4,12,13, the main barrier in improving OHCA outcomes is the timely recognition of the event.

The current American Heart Association and American College of Cardiology guidelines14 recommend the use of implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) for patients with a high risk of OHCA, such as individuals with a history of cardiac arrest15,16. However, in an overall population, the vast majority of OHCAs occurs in individuals with low incidental risk, including those without a known cardiac disease17,18. For the low-risk population, who might not be willing to undergo an invasive procedure to install an implantable sensors, less invasive technologies for continuous cardiac monitoring represent a more suitable solution. Wearable biosensor devices with continuous cardiac monitoring capabilities may serve as a “witness” in an otherwise unwitnessed event and could alert EMS on the individual’s behalf19. With a significant uptake in consumer markets20,21 and widespread use, wearable devices have the potential to substantially improve OHCA survival7.

One of the most commonly used sensors in consumer wearable devices for continuous cardiac monitoring is the photoplethysmogram (PPG)22,23. PPG is an optical sensor capable of monitoring pulsation and providing heart rate estimates with high accuracy (> 89%) compared to gold standard electrocardiogram (ECG) recordings24,25. Unlike ECG-based devices, PPGs do not require multiple points of contact or user effort for signal acquisition, hence, providing continuous cardiac monitoring capabilities with minimal user interference26. Current wearable sensors employ multiwavelength PPGs (e.g., green light, red light, infrared) in several form factors as PPG data can be collected at various anatomical locations (e.g., wrist, finger base). Given that the cessation of pulsatile blood flow is one of the immediate physiological changes following a cardiac arrest, PPGs can be utilized to detect the lack of pulsatile blood flow. Moreover, PPG-based devices can detect pulseless electric activity cardiac arrests, some of which are not distinguishable from normal cardiac function by ECG despite an absent palpable pulse27.

Although limited in number, studies have been conducted on the application of PPG-based wrist-worn devices for detecting the absence and presence of a pulse in simulated scenarios resembling OHCAs. In a multi-center study conducted in the Netherlands, the CardioWatch 287–2 was utilized to monitor cardiac activity during procedures involving induced cardiac arrest, including transcatheter aortic valve implantation, subcutaneous ICD implantation, and ventricular tachycardia ablation28. Green light PPG measurements of 5-second duration were classified as “arrest” or “non-arrest” using a two-step process, which included threshold-based amplitude evaluation and signal quality criteria assessment. The algorithm was validated among 86 patients, with 91 h of non-arrest recordings and 97 induced circulatory arrests. The results showed a cardiac arrest detection sensitivity of 98% (95% CI 92–100%) and 11 false positive alarms, predominantly associated with poor PPG signal quality. Despite the relatively high sensitivity, this cardiac arrest detection model could trigger approximately 3 false alerts per day per person. Additionally, due to experimental constraints, only short window durations of 5 s were used for circulatory arrest detection, which may have contributed to higher false positive rates compared to using PPG recordings of longer duration.

Challenges in differentiating between pulsatile and pulseless PPG-based cardiac measurements have also been reported in other applications. In a study tangentially related to OHCA detection, the Samsung smartwatch was attached on the wrist to monitor the return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) in cardiac arrest patients (no report of PPG color)29. The rate of detecting return to pulsation and rate of detecting pulselessness in ROSC detection were 0.786 and 1.000, respectively. While this signifies the device’s capability in correctly identifying the absence of a pulse on the wrist (the most common consumer wearable form factor), it also indicates a limitation that the presence of a pulse is misclassified as pulselessness. In a cardiac arrest detection scenario, this could result in a high number of false cardiac arrest predictions and high alert volumes. Thus, it is crucial to consider both the detection performance and practical aspects when using wearables for OHCA detection.

In this study, our primary objectives were to (1) to use infrared PPG measurements to develop classification models for detecting different cardiac states including normal cardiac, pulseless cardiac (i.e., resembling a cardiac arrest), and non-physiologic (i.e., off-body) measurements; (2) determine the optimal PPG-based feature combinations that maximize classification performance; and (3) evaluate the performance (F1-score, sensitivity, specificity, false positive occlusion predictions) of PPG-based classifiers on 3 anatomical sites that are the most common form factors for PPG devices, including the fingertip, finger base, and wrist. Moreover, considering that collecting high-quality PPG measurements might not be feasible at all times, we examined the potential impact of signal quality on detection performance.

Results

PPG dataset

In this study, we obtained and examined PPG recordings under three conditions, including normal cardiac, occlusion, and off-body states. Normal cardiac measurements represented physiologic data during normal blood circulation, while occlusion recordings captured cardiac characteristics in the absence of pulsatile blood flow (i.e., simulating a cardiac arrest). Additionally, off-body PPG measurements were obtained to represent conditions where the wearable device is not worn or may not be properly secured to the skin. This consideration is particularly relevant in the context of OHCA detection, as it can help mitigate the number of potential false positive cardiac arrest detections in real-world scenarios, due to the device being removed or in the absence of proper sensor placement or contact.

We performed four experiments to obtain data representing the above-mentioned cardiac and non-cardiac states. These experiments involved collecting time-synchronized reference ECG and four PPGs at 50 Hz. Thirty-one healthy volunteers with no known cardiac conditions were recruited and provided written informed consent to participate in this study. Participants aged between 19 and 58 years old (mean ± sd: 27.3 ± 7.3 years, n = 31) and included 14 females (45%). In the first experiment (Fig. 1a, Experiment 1), participants wore PPGs on common wearable sensor locations including the fingertip (the most common form factor in clinical settings), finger base (recently adopted in wearables with a ring form factor), and wrist (the most common form factor for consumer wearables)30. All participants’ baseline systolic blood pressure was recorded, which ranged between 90 and 140 mmHg (mean ± sd: 117 ± 11 mmHg, n = 31). We applied pressure on the upper arm (> 20mmHg above the participants’ systolic pressure) to induce arterial occlusion for 60 s. We used both manual pulse palpation and a stethoscope to confirm complete occlusion. Infrared (IR) PPG data at full pressure were labeled as Occlusion. Additional data without pressure applied was collected 30 s before and after. These measurements were labeled as Normal Cardiac. This procedure was repeated 3 to 5 times for each participant. In the second experiment (Fig. 1a, Experiment 2), we collected 2 min of continuous and uninterrupted IR PPG data at the same anatomical locations as those in Experiment 1 (i.e., fingertip, finger base, and wrist). All measurements from this experiment were labeled Normal Cardiac.

In the third experiment (Fig. 1a, Experiment 3), we alternated attaching and detaching PPGs at the same anatomical locations for 30 s at a time over 5 min. While attached, sensors remained on the skin (PPG data labeled as Normal Cardiac) and while detached, sensors were removed fully from the body (PPG data labeled as Off-Body). This was done to simulate normal wearable device usage as individual put on and remove devices. We further supplemented Off-Body data by collecting PPG recordings while sensors were not attached to any participants. This was done over a range of conditions, with sensors facing surfaces with various reflections/absorption characteristics as well as during dynamic movement or motionless states. All recordings were labeled Off-Body. A comprehensive list of all tests conducted by each participant, as well as detailed information about the sensor placement in each test, is available in the study’s documentation repository in Dataverse.

We collected 34 h and 44 min of Normal Cardiac, 5 h and 13 min of Occlusion measurements on fingertips, finger base, and wrist, as well as 10 h and 22 min of Off-Body data. All PPG recordings were pre-processed (Fig. 1b), following which we extracted common time-domain (time: n = 10), PSD-domain (psd: n = 11), and frequency-domain (freq: n = 5) feature sets for classification. The choice of selected features was informed by several studies focused on PPG signal analysis24,34,59,60.

Classification model training

For PPG recordings obtained from the fingertip, finger base, and wrist, a multi-class classification pipeline with grid search was developed (Fig. 1c). This included the following steps: (1) Standardization: this involved subtracting the mean and normalizing to one standard deviation for each feature in the training set; (2) Feature selection: this included training a random forest classifier with 5-fold cross-validation (cv) and a forward sequential feature selection algorithm to identify optimal feature combinations that maximize classification performance. A total of six feature sets were evaluated including independent time-, frequency-, and PSD-domain features, as well as combinations of these features, including timefreq (n = 15), timepsd (n = 21), and timefreqpsd (n = 26); (3) Model training: this used 70% of randomly selected PPG measurements to train random forest classifiers with 10-fold cv and hyperparameter tuning (number of estimators and maximum depth of the trees) with the objective of maximizing macro average F1-score; and (4) Model evaluation: this utilized the 30% hold-out testing dataset, standardized using the means and standard deviations calculated from the training datasets; and the classification performance was assessed on the standardized testing set. For each anatomical location, all models were trained on the same 70% of data and evaluated on the same 30% hold-out dataset (overview of the classification pipeline is presented in Supplementary Materials, Classification Pipeline).

Classification performance: dependence on PPG feature characteristics

For each anatomical location (i.e., fingertip, finger base, and wrist), we compared classification performances when using each of the six feature sets (Fig. 2a). Across all three anatomical sites, the classification performance of models using both time- and PSD-/frequency-domain features (i.e., timefreq, timepsd, timefreqpsd) were higher than the models trained on independent time-, frequency- and PSD- -domain features (i.e., time, freq, psd). Classifiers trained on timefreq, timepsd, timefreqpsd features had identical or very similar performance on each anatomical location. For the remainder of the results section, we will only present the output of classification models trained on the highest-performing timefreqpsd feature set.

Various time-, frequency-, and PSD-domain features were selected and contributed to achieving optimal classification performances. For instance, both the PPG Mean and 1.17 Hz component of the PPG PSD (equal to heart rate of ~ 70 beats per minute (BPM)), were common among classification models across all anatomical sites. Several other features were common among at least two classifiers. These included the PPG signal Mean Crossing on the fingertip and wrist, the signal Total Power on the fingertip and finger base, and the 2.34 Hz PSD (the second harmonic of 1.17 Hz) on the finger base and wrist. The Kruskal-Wallis test for independent samples revealed a statistically significant difference (P < 0.001) in the selected features across at least two classes (for further detail refer to Statistical Analysis in Supplementary Materials). For instance, the Total Power of PPG measurements showed significant differences between the Normal Cardiac, Occlusion, and Off-Body recordings (Fig. 2b). Another example is the PPG Mean, which showed significant differentiation between Off-Body and the other two states (Normal Cardiac and Occlusion) (Fig. 2b,c). Similarly, the 1.17 Hz PSD feature demonstrated significant differentiation between Normal Cardiac and the other two states (Occlusion and Off-Body) (Fig. 2b,c).

Classification performance: dependence on anatomical location

Across the classification models trained and evaluated on the three anatomical locations (i.e., fingertip, finger base, wrist), higher classification performances were observed on the finger (macro average F1-score of 0.964 on the fingertip and 0.954 on the finger base) compared to the wrist (macro average F1-score of 0.837) (Table 1). Across all anatomical sites, Normal Cardiac and Off-Body states had higher sensitivity and fewer false negative predictions compared to Occlusion (Table 1; Fig. 3a). Conversely, Occlusion had higher specificity and fewer false positive predictions compared to Normal Cardiac and Off-Body states (Table 1; Fig. 3a). Occlusion detection had the lowest performance on the wrist, with 56.2% false negative predictions (Fig. 3a) and an area under the curve of 0.91 (Fig. 3b). Analysis of the receiver operating curve revealed that when eliminating all false positive Occlusion predictions (i.e., no potential false cardiac arrest prediction), the classifiers trained on the fingertip, finger base, and wrist achieved Occlusion sensitivity (true positive rate) of 0.518, 0.825, and 0.155, respectively (Table 1).

We found that Normal Cardiac measurements taken at the fingertip and finger base had a distinct pulsatile component (PPG AC), differentiating them from pulseless Occlusion measurements on those sites (Fig. 3c). However, Normal Cardiac measurements of the wrist exhibited a weaker pulsatile component, potentially making it more challenging to distinguish them from pulseless Occlusion measurements (Fig. 3c). Considering the impact of PPG signal quality on the accuracy of cardiac characteristics, as highlighted by previous research31,32, and the recommendations for implementing signal quality assessment strategies to enhance accuracy and reliability of wrist-worn PPG devices33, we hypothesized that the poor classification performance of the model trained on wrist PPG recordings might be associated with the poor signal quality of the Normal Cardiac measurements on this site. Consequently, the secondary objectives of this study were to (1) determine the impact of PPG signal quality on the classification performance of different anatomical sites; and (2) determine the real-world implications of the anatomical performance.

Classification performance: dependence on PPG signal quality

To explore the secondary objectives, we wanted to evaluate the performance of classifiers that were trained and tested with only high-quality signals. Signal quality index measures or threshold-based approaches are commonly used to evaluate PPG signal quality62,63, and to prune high vs. low-quality PPG data previously59,64. We used a frequency-domain-based signal quality index, known as PPG Power Ratio34. We calculated this index by dividing the sum of PPG PSD components within the 0.83–1.7 Hz frequency range by the sum of PPG PSD components for the entire frequency range (0.5–5 Hz). The 0.83–1.7 Hz frequency range corresponds to the healthy cardiac frequency at rest (50–100 BPM), as is the case in our study. Consistent with this assumption, the average heart rate of our study participants (mean ± sd, n = 30) was 70 ± 11 BPM (ECG measurements were not available for one participant due to sensor malfunction).

To create the High-Quality datasets for each anatomical location, we retained PPG recordings with Power Ratio above the 25th percentile of all Normal Cardiac PPG measurements (Fig. 4a). Consequently, Normal Cardiac measurements that passed the quality threshold contained the highest signal energy within the resting heart rate frequency, while those categorized as low-quality contained artifacts affecting both the PPG waveform and its frequency-domain characteristics (Fig. 4b).

Specifically, the High-Quality training and testing sets retained Normal Cardiac data from the original training and testing sets that met the quality threshold. The High-Quality datasets comprised the same Occlusion and Off-Body data as the original training and testing sets. (Fig. 4c). The largest improvement in classification performance was observed for models trained and evaluated on PPG data obtained from the wrist, with increases of 3.79% in macro average precision, 14.73% in macro average recall, and 11.64% of macro average F1-score (Table 2). Furthermore, the Occlusion detection sensitivity (true positive rate) at each anatomical location was consistently higher for models trained and evaluated on High-Quality datasets compared to Mixed-Quality datasets (fingertip: 0.937 vs. 0.899; finger base: 0.934 vs. 0.876; wrist: 0.779 vs. 0.438) (Fig. 4d). While training and evaluating classifiers on High-Quality PPG datasets led to higher classification performances, this involved discarding a portion of the PPG recordings. The percentage of PPG measurements of high quality varied across different anatomical locations (Fig. 4f). Approximately 90.7% of PPG measurements taken at the fingertip and 86.5% of the recordings on the finger base met the quality threshold and were considered of high quality. However, PPG recordings on the wrist exhibited the lowest quality, with only 53.2% of the measurements meeting the quality threshold. Notably, the substantial classification improvements observed on the wrist were achieved by discarding nearly half of the PPG recordings obtained from this site (additional information regarding the breakdown of high- vs. low-quality measurements are available in Supplementary Materials).

Classification performance: practical implications

Training classifiers on PPG data of high quality provides valuable insights into theoretically optimal classification performances, however, consistently obtaining PPG recordings with high quality in real-world settings may not always be practical. Therefore, it is crucial to validate classification robustness in more practical sensor deployment scenarios. In our study, in addition to the previously described classification evaluation conditions, we investigated a third condition where the classifier was trained on High-Quality PPG data (training set) and evaluated on Mixed-Quality PPG recordings (testing set). This scenario represents a case where the model is trained on High-Quality PPG data, such as those obtained during controlled lab experiments and those that pass the quality threshold, but its performance is evaluated on a mix of high- and low-quality PPG recordings (Fig. 5a). This is aimed specifically at evaluating the effectiveness of a theoretically optimal model in more practical settings.

Consistent with previous models, higher classification performances were observed on the finger, with macro average F1-score of 0.914 on the fingertip and 0.867 on the finger base, compared to the wrist which had a macro average F1-score of 0.658 (Table 3). Across all three anatomical locations, models trained on High-Quality data and evaluated on Mixed-Quality data (HQ-MQ condition) had the lowest overall classification performances compared to classifiers trained and evaluated on either High-Quality (HQ-HQ condition) or Mixed-Quality (MQ-MQ condition) datasets. While the HQ-MQ condition had similar Occlusion sensitivity (i.e., correctly identifying occlusion when they occur) to HQ-HQ, it had lower specificity (i.e., incorrectly identifying occlusion when they are not occurring) at all anatomical locations. Notably, the HQ-MQ condition had remarkably higher false positive Occlusion predictions (fingertip: 0.022, finger base: 0.048, wrist: 0.226) compared to the HQ-HQ condition (fingertip: 0.004, finger base: 0.004; wrist: 0.005) (Fig. 5b). Moreover, compared to the HQ-HQ condition, the HQ-MQ condition had substantially lower true positive Occlusion predictions when eliminating all false positives (Fig. 5c; Table 3). While HQ-HQ and MQ-MQ conditions showed high sensitivity (> 0.970) for Normal Cardiac detection across all three anatomical locations, HQ-MQ condition had remarkably lower Normal Cardiac sensitivity, specifically on the wrist (fingertip: 0.904, finger base: 0.849; wrist: 0.524).

Potential impact on survival outcomes of unwitnessed OHCAs

The primary beneficiaries of a cardiac arrest detection system are the subgroup of OHCA cases that are unwitnessed at the time of onset. These cases experience delays in recognition and reporting, leading to a reduced likelihood of receiving timely treatment. To estimate the potential impact of improved detection, Hutton et al.,7 conducted a simulation analysis based on over 11,000 EMS-treated and untreated OHCAs and estimated the potential survival improvements in previously unwitnessed cases if the event were promptly recognized (e.g., either through bystander witness or via a biosensor technology). We used similar simulation models to estimate potential survival improvements (relative to baseline survival) based on the Occlusion sensitivity of our classification models (Table 4). The estimated survival rate varied depending on the evaluation condition and anatomical location. Overall, training and evaluating models on High-Quality datasets (HQ-HQ condition) could theoretically offer the highest survival improvement by at least doubling OHCA survival of unwitnessed cases. This remains consistent whether considering optimal classification performance or when eliminating all false positive cardiac arrest alarms (i.e., false Occlusion predictions). It is crucial to acknowledge that the actual efficacy of these models is strictly determined by the availability of PPG recordings with high quality, a factor that is yet to be determined in a practical real-world setting. For classifiers trained and evaluated on Mixed-Quality datasets (MQ-MQ condition), while we observed comparable survival enhancements to the HQ-HQ conditions on the finger, the potential survival improvements were remarkably lower for models trained on the wrist (63.9% for the optimal classifier and 22.2% when eliminating all false positive cardiac arrest predictions). Classifiers trained on High-Quality datasets and evaluated on Mixed-Quality measurements (HQ-MQ condition) demonstrated potential for more than doubling the OHCA survival rate (> 100%), however, the rate dropped substantially for classifiers with zero false positive Occlusion predictions. For these models, the additional survival improvements were 73%0.1 on the fingertip, 3.4% on the finger base, and 2.0% on the wrist.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the feasibility of using infrared PPG wearables for OHCA detection. We developed hardware and software for a multi-PPG wearable device capable of continuous cardiac monitoring. PPG data were collected during three distinct states: Normal Cardiac, Occlusion (simulating pulseless cardiac conditions through arterial occlusion), and Off-Body (when the device was not in contact with the skin). The Normal Cardiac and Occlusion data were collected from multiple anatomical locations, including the fingertip, finger base, and wrist. Classification models were trained using optimal feature selection techniques to identify the most relevant time-, frequency-, and PSD-domain features, aiming to maximize the F1-score. For each anatomical location, the classification performance was assessed against a strictly separated hold-out (unseen) testing set. Our findings indicate classification performances were influenced by the PPG signal quality and anatomical location. Overall, PPG measurements taken from the finger (both the tip and base) had higher signal quality compared to PPG recordings from the wrist. Higher classification performances were observed when PPG recordings of high quality were used to train and evaluate classifiers. These results highlight the feasibility of PPG-based measurements in detecting different cardiac and off-body states and underscore the potential utility of PPG-based detection of OHCA using consumer wearables.

However, our results also highlight the challenges in translating current wearable PPG technologies for the detection of OHCA in a practical setting. While we identified that Occlusion states can be effectively detected with PPG sensors placed in common wearable form factors, the best results were achieved in a theoretical condition where only data with high quality were analyzed. However, using all PPG recordings (high- and low-quality), these classifiers demonstrated lower performance, particularly at the most common wrist location where nearly 50% of the data was not of high quality. Thus, future development of algorithms for OHCA must consider the real-world conditions of data collection in order to create a practically meaningful system to detect OHCA early, while also minimizing false positive alerts and avoiding EMS burden.

The features selected for optimal classification performances were associated with both the baseline and pulsatile components of PPG waveforms (e.g., PPG Mean, PSD 1.17 Hz). For instance, a common time-domain feature among several classifiers was the PPG Window Change, indicating the total PPG signal change within the recorded time window (calculated by subtracting the last PPG recording in each segment from the first). Occlusion measurements had significantly lower Window Change compared to Normal Cardiac recordings, indicating a substantial increase in PPG baseline measurements during occlusion. This is consistent with previous reports of stable infrared PPG baseline during normal cardiac measurements and a minimum 10% increase during occlusion36. In addition to the primary heart rate frequency at 1.17 Hz, the PSD amplitude of the second heart rate harmonic at 2.34 Hz was selected as a relevant feature for optimal classification performance. This aligns with prior research, highlighting the relevance of using higher harmonics to improve green light PPG classification performance for heart rate estimation, specifically when obtaining PPG data with high quality is challenging37.

Our findings corroborate previous studies indicating substantial variations in signal quality across different body sites38. Our infrared PPG measurements on the fingertip had the highest signal quality, followed by those taken from the finger base, and the lowest quality was observed on the wrist. Consistently, previous research has shown that red/infrared PPG measurements are more accurate when taken from the finger compared to the wrist39. Our findings highlight the difficulty in obtaining PPG signals with high quality, particularly on the wrist, even under controlled laboratory settings. This is further supported by limitations identified in wrist-worn green light PPG devices for atrial fibrillation detection, such as a high dropout rate (50%) due to insufficient signal quality40. Additionally, various artifacts, such as sensor attachment issues, contribute to suboptimal measurements and potentially inaccurate PPG measurements on the wrist41. These challenges demonstrate some of the practical difficulties in using wrist-worn PPGs, despite their popularity as the most preferred form-factor for cardiac monitoring, as indicated in a recent survey (SL, JH, MK, BG, BS, JC, and CK. Wearable Devices for Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest: A Population Survey on The Willingness to Adhere. Accepted to the Journal of the American College of Emergency Physicians Open [JACEP Open]).

Achieving theoretically optimal classification performance relies on training and evaluating models on comparable datasets. Inconsistencies in the quality of training and evaluation data can introduce biases and impede classification performance42. Our findings suggest that deploying classifiers trained on PPG measurements of high quality, where ensuring high-quality signal acquisition is feasible, results in optimal classification performance. However, deploying models trained solely on data with high quality in settings where mixed-quality PPG data are prevalent leads to considerably lower performance, particularly increasing the risk of false positive cardiac arrest predictions. Therefore, when ensuring PPG data quality is not practical, it is recommended to deploy classification models trained on datasets that are more representative of PPG measurements in real-world settings, including a mix of high- and low-quality measurements.

While there were some variations in classification performances based on PPG signal quality, the models trained on measurements taken at the finger consistently outperformed those trained on PPG recordings from the wrist. In clinical settings, finger clip-type PPG devices are commonly used, allowing for PPG measurements of high quality on the fingertip. However, obtaining PPG data from the fingertip may not be feasible or acceptable for long-term monitoring during daily life. While the fingertip is not a practical location for a wearable device for continuous cardiac monitoring in non-clinical settings, the classification performance of a model trained on fingertip recordings serves as a benchmark for the best possible outcome using a wearable PPG device. Our findings suggest that the finger base serves as the second-best location to achieve high classification performance. Recently, wearable devices adopting a ring form factor with embedded PPG sensors have become available in the market. While these existing wearables currently do not have OHCA detection capabilities, our study suggests that they may offer the most suitable form factor for optimal OHCA detection applications. It is worth noting that additional factors, such as maintaining continuous connectivity between the device and a phone application, may be necessary to ensure timely notification to EMS. Therefore, potential challenges, such as battery life duration, need to be addressed in the design and development of such ring-type devices to enable continuous monitoring lasting for several hours. Classifiers trained on infrared wrist PPG recordings, representing the most common consumer wearable form factor30, had the lowest classification performance. Notably, the classification performance was considerably lower when using all PPG recordings, which included approximately 50% low-quality measurements. Therefore, considering the current limitations, an infrared wrist-worn wearable device does not appear to be a viable option for OHCA detection. Considering the widespread popularity of wristband multiwavelength PPG wearables in nonclinical settings22,43, it is imperative to employ strategies to improve classification accuracy in this location. Possible approaches may include training classification models using recordings from multiple PPGs (e.g., green light, red light, and infrared) and ensuring optimal sensor-to-skin contact with other sensor modalities (e.g., force sensors).

The performance of a wearable device for cardiac arrest detection can be assessed from different perspectives, such as patient- or system-level outcomes. At the patient level, the primary objective is to identify all cardiac arrest events. This can be achieved by maximizing true positive predictions while minimizing false positives (i.e., achieving high sensitivity). Aligning with this, a population health analysis examining the variability in classification performance of a hypothetical biosensor technology for cardiac arrest detection revealed that lower sensitivity correlated with reduced estimated survival rates7, highlighting the critical importance of accurate cardiac arrest detection to prevent potential fatalities. However, achieving high sensitivity often involves a trade-off with specificity, potentially increasing false positive cardiac arrest predictions. A wearable device with a high false positive prediction rate could impose an unnecessary burden on the healthcare system, leading to increased alarm fatigue and hindering adoption of such device44. While both hardware and software improvements can contribute to reducing false positive rates45, complete elimination may not be feasible46. This is exemplified by reported instances of false alerts in patients with implantable cardiac monitors, reaching as high as 20%47. Therefore, aiming for a balanced approach between minimizing false positive and false negative predictions is a critical step in the development of a wearable system for OHCA detection7.

While ambitious targets for cardiac arrest detection, such as sensitivity greater than 0.99 and specificity over 0.999, may be desirable, more practical objectives have been proposed as viable benchmarks (e.g., 0.90 sensitivity and 0.95 specificity)48. To achieve a balanced cardiac arrest detection performance, we optimized classification performance using F1-score, which maximized the number of true positive predictions while minimizing the number of false negative and false positive predictions. With PPG measurements of high quality, our models achieved Occlusion detection sensitivity exceeding 0.930 on the finger and 0.779 on the wrist. Occlusion specificity remained consistently high across all anatomical locations, ranging from 0.995 to 0.996. These findings suggest that the recommended OHCA sensitivity and specificity benchmarks can be achieved when using high-quality PPG measurements of the finger. However, even with such high specificity, these models could generate approximately 43 false positive alerts daily. Therefore, further adjustments are necessary to improve specificity for real-world applications. In the most conservative scenario where all false positive Occlusion predictions were eliminated, the Occlusion detection sensitivity of our models was reduced on all anatomical locations, ranging between 0.730 and 0.788. Although lower than optimal, even within this reduced sensitivity range, hundreds of OHCA cases could still be identified. This highlights the capacity to potentially double the number of lives that could be saved from otherwise fatal events. However, it is important to note that the observed sensitivity on different anatomical locations is achieved only when discarding measurements of low quality, which accounts for almost half of the recordings on the wrist. This means that for recordings that do not pass the quality threshold, no conclusive decision about the presence or absence of a pulse could be made, which have significant implications on the true performance.

Compared to models trained and evaluated on High-Quality datasets, classifiers trained and evaluated on Mixed-Quality PPG recordings had similar Occlusion specificity (ranging from 0.994 to 0.966) but lower sensitivity (0.899 for fingertip, 0.876 for finger base, and 0.438 for wrist). Notably, when aiming to achieve zero false positive Occlusion predictions, the Occlusion sensitivity was substantially reduced on the wrist to 0.155 (i.e., an estimated 2.0% potential improvement in unwitnessed survival rate). These findings suggest that utilizing mixed-quality PPG recordings would result in suboptimal sensitivity and specificity. Particularly, models trained on the wrist data might become impractical with such low sensitivity when eliminating all false positive Occlusion predictions. We speculate that this issue may be associated with the poor signal quality on the wrist (an anatomical location with low perfusion). Specifically, normal cardiac recordings with a weak pulsatile PPG component (i.e., low quality signal) may appear similar to occlusion recordings with no pulsatile component. This similarity could contribute to the misclassification of occlusion measurements as normal cardiac recordings of low quality. Nevertheless, the deployment and evaluation of wearables for cardiac arrest detection in real-world settings could better inform future considerations for achieving optimal outcomes and developing models with practical prediction thresholds.

In addition to the absence of a detectable pulse, a cardiac arrest event can be characterized by other measurable parameters, including lack of movement, apnea or agonal breathing, and drop in body temperature. For instance, an audio-based OHCA detection framework has demonstrated promising outcomes, with cardiac arrest detection sensitivity and specificity of 0.972 and 0.995, respectively49. Utilizing additional independent sensor observations of an OHCA may improve detection confidence and will be tested in future studies involving measurements of real cardiac arrest scenarios.

A carefully calibrated threshold for sensitivity and specificity should be considered for the real-world implementation of a wearable system for cardiac arrest detection, ensuring that alerts are triggered with high confidence in identifying a cardiac arrest. Once achieving an acceptable and practical performance of an OHCA detection wearable, the next step would be the integration of such a device into EMS. In these integrated systems, a staged approach could be implemented to optimize effectiveness and user experience. Initially, upon detection of a potential cardiac arrest, the wearable system could trigger an audible alarm on a connected smartphone for a predetermined duration (e.g., 30–60 s)50. This immediate audible alert, serving as an initial notification to the user, could include instructions on how to confirm their status and deactivate the alarm if they are not experiencing a cardiac arrest. If the alarm remains unaddressed by the user within the predetermined timeframe, the system could progress to a second stage by sending an automated alert to EMS, along with the patient’s location. This early detection and immediate activation of EMS could significantly reduce response time to OHCA incidents and improve the overall probability of survival. The findings of our proof-of-principle study provide motivation for more comprehensive trials to evaluate the impact of such wearables for OHCA detection.

Limitations

The current study is limited by its small size and experimental model. Future studies with larger cohorts are required to obtain PPG measurements from participants representing various demographics and cardiac conditions. While our sample size was limited to 31 participants, our study design facilitated the collection of a considerably large pulseless dataset totaling 313 min of PPG recordings, resembling those observed during a cardiac arrest event. Our dataset was notably larger than a comparable study, which consisted of approximately 77 min of pulseless PPG recordings. Additionally, we acknowledge potential disparities between PPG recordings during an actual cardiac arrest and those during an occlusion test. However, given recent evidence demonstrating similarities in PPG signals during blood flow interruption by pressure cuff inflation and a spontaneous cardiac arrest28, we speculate that the occlusion test used in our study serves as the closest means to simulate a sudden cardiac arrest in otherwise healthy individuals. While the necessary number of participants required to determine the effect of an OHCA detection device on survival in a clinical trial would likely be in the hundreds, the incidence of OHCA would necessitate a prospective recruitment of hundreds of thousands, potentially millions of participants. Therefore, alternative experimental procedures, such as obtaining PPG measurements during scheduled medical procedures (e.g., medically assisted dying procedures), offer more practical validation approaches. Considering the previously reported limitations of IR PPG compared to green light PPG for cardiac monitoring51, we speculate that the observed low signal quality, particularly on the wrist, may be attributed to the PPG wavelength (IR). Future studies could investigate and compare the classification performance of both IR and green light PPG recordings. However, we also note that the IR PPG, in conjunction with a red light PPG, can also provide information on blood oxygenation which could also help in cardiac arrest detection. Considering some of the previously reported limitations of PPG sensors (e.g., higher error rates for darker skin tones52), we investigated prediction errors across diverse demographics, including age, blood pressure, skin tone, and sex. Our findings did not reveal any association between classification error and skin tone variations, participants’ sex, age, or baseline blood pressure (refer to Classification Error Analysis in Supplementary Materials).

Methods

Recruitment

The study protocol was approved by the University of British Columbia Research Ethics Board (H22-01587) and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. We used convenience sampling to recruit participants from the University of British Columbia and the Centre for Aging SMART community in Vancouver, Canada. The study was conducted between June and October 2023. Individuals were recruited to participate in this study upon meeting the following inclusion criteria: (1) able to give a written informed consent; (2) older than 19 years of age; and (3) no history of significant cardiac disease or current use of cardiac medication. After obtaining informed consent from each participant, we collected demographics information (age, sex, skin tone) as well as baseline systolic blood pressure. We used the Fitzpatrick phototype scale to characterize participants skin tone53. Detailed information regarding the sample size analysis is provided in Supplementary Materials.

Experimental setup

Our wearable sensor devices consisted of a single-lead ECG (AD8232, SparkFun Electronics, Niwot, CO, USA) and 4 identical reflective PPGs (MAXREFDES117 [MAX30102], Maxim Integrated™, San Jose, CA, USA). The performance of this PPG sensor for cardiac monitoring has been validated in previous studies54–56. The data acquisition system included a microcontroller board (Adalogger M0, Adafruit Industries, New York, NY, USA) and an I2C multiplexer (TCA9548A, Texas Instruments™, Dallas, TX, USA) for multi-PPG data collection (Fig. 6). Synchronized ECG and PPG data were sampled at 50 Hz and logged on the Adalogger’s SD card.

Multi-PPG wearable device for data acquisition. In this setup, one PPG sensor is attached on the outer side of the wrist and one PPG sensor is placed on the inner side of the finger base.

Experimental procedure

All experiments were performed in a laboratory setting with consistent lighting condition during all tests. In experiments involving participants, an ECG sensor was used to obtain ground truth measurements of normal cardiac activity and to estimate participant heart rate. Before attaching the ECG electrodes, we applied Nuprep skin gel to prepare the skin area. Next, electrode gel was added to ECG electrodes and 3 electrodes were attached to the following areas on the chest: below the collarbone on both the left and right side, and below the rib cage on the right side. To obtain data representative of a wearable device, PPG sensors were applied to multiple sites on the extremities including tip of the finger (index and/or middle finger), base of the index finger, and wrist (volar and/or dorsal area). We cleaned the skin area with alcohol swabs for each PPG sensor placement, and sensors were attached to the skin using custom straps. Additional adhesives were used to secure the sensors on the skin when needed. Participants were instructed to remain seated and avoid voluntary movements throughout the data collection procedures.

We conducted four experiments to gather PPG measurements representing various cardiac states, including normal cardiac function, non-cardiac conditions, and events resembling cardiac arrest. In investigational research, it is common to simulate a cardiac arrest condition by disrupting the normal pulsatile blood circulation, often achieved through arterial occlusion36,57. This assumption is supported by the fact that any cardiac arrest, regardless of the underlying cause or characteristics of the individual (e.g., healthy vs. patients with a history of cardiac disease), is characterized by the absence of pulsatile blood flow28,48. In this study, we collected PPG data during occlusion tests to mimic measurements during a cardiac arrest event. Sessions involving the occlusion procedures were video-recorded and time-synchronized with the PPG data acquisition system to determine the start and end of the occlusion period and the inflation and deflation times. PPG recordings collected during the inflation and deflation of the pressure cuff were removed from the dataset.

ECG and PPG signal analysis

ECG measurements were band-passed with a 2nd -order zero-lag Butterworth filter (cut-off frequency: 0.5–24 Hz). Next, PSD analysis was used to obtain the distribution of ECG signal power across different frequency components. The heart rate was approximated with the frequency corresponding to the highest PSD peak58. For each participant, the heart rate was then computed as the average across all trials conducted in Experiments 1 and 2. This method ensured a comprehensive heart rate estimation, accounting for potential cardiac variations across different tests.

PPG recordings from all 4 experiments were preprocessed, which included segmentation, filtering, and time-/frequency-domain feature extraction of the PPG signals. The segmentation step involved splitting all PPG measurements into short non-overlapping windows containing 512 data points, equivalent of 10.24 s duration. A normal cardiac PPG waveform has two primary components, a pulsatile arterial blood-flow component (PPG AC) and a non-pulsatile baseline component (PPG DC)23. We applied a low-pass filter to the segmented raw PPG signals to remove high-frequency noise while preserving PPG DC characteristics (2nd -order zero-lag Butterworth filter; 5 Hz cut-off frequency). This created the low-passed PPG dataset. The AC component of a normal cardiac PPG signal contains key physiological characteristics, such as pulsation. To isolate this component, we applied a band-pass filter to the windowed raw PPG measurements, removing both the low-frequency baseline component (PPG DC) and high-frequency noise (2nd -order zero lag Butterworth filter; 0.5–5 Hz cut-off frequency). This created the band-passed PPG dataset. The final preprocessing step involved extracting time- and power spectral density (PSD)-/frequency-domain features from the low-passed and band-passed PPG segments, respectively. A total of 19 time-domain features were extracted from segmented low-passed PPG data. Highly correlated features (Pearson correlation coefficient |r| ≥ 0.70) were removed, leaving only one feature retained. The remaining time-domain features, formed the time-domain feature set (time: n = 10). The Welch PSD analysis61 was performed on segmented band-passed PPG data to calculate the power distribution of PPG signal across different frequencies. The output of this analysis formed the PSD-domain feature set (psd: n = 11). Additionally, a total of 10 frequency-domain features were extracted from PSD components After removing those highly correlated features, the remaining featuresformed the frequency-domain feature set (freq: n = 5). Finally, to create a rich high-dimensional feature representation for classification, three additional feature set combinations were created: time- and PSD-domain features (timepsd: n = 21); time- and frequency-domain features (timefreq: n = 15); and time-, frequency-, and PSD-domain features (timefreqpsd: n = 26). In addition to extracting the abovementioned features, we performed the short time Fourier transform analysis on band-passed PPG recordings. This analysis created a 2-dimensional dataset containing both time- and frequency-domain characteristics of PPG recordings. These datasets were used for deep learning model training and evaluation. For a comprehensive list of all features and computation methods refer to PPG Feature Extraction in Supplementary Materials.

Classification

The PPG measurements representing all Normal Cardiac and Occlusion states, collected on the fingertip, finger base, and wrist were randomly divided into a training set comprising 70% of the data and a hold-out (unseen) testing set comprising the remaining 30%. Additionally, Off-Body PPG measurements were randomly allocated into 70% for training and 30% for testing and were merged with both the Normal Cardiac and Occlusion sets accordingly. The Off-Body PPG measurements were utilized consistently across models trained and evaluated on the fingertip, finger base, and wrist. Further description of the training and testing datasets is available in Training-Testing Datasets in Supplementary Materials. First, we standardized the training dataset by removing the mean and normalizing it by one standard deviation. Next, we standardized the testing dataset using the mean and standard deviation derived from the training dataset.

We trained random forest, support vector machine (SVM), and 2D convolutional neural network (CNN) models to detect Normal Cardiac, Occlusion, and Off-Body classes. Among these models, random forest classifiers had the highest performance, and in this paper, we presented the performance of these models only (refer to SVM and CNN Classification Models in Supplementary Materials for further detail). Random forest models were trained using all combinations of the following datasets: 3 anatomical locations (fingertip, finger base, wrist); 6 feature sets (time, freq, psd, timefreq, timepsd, timefreqpsd); and 2 quality sets (High-Quality, Mixed-Quality).

Macro average precision (the number of true positive predictions divided by the sum of true positives and false positives), recall (the number of true positive predictions divided by the sum of true positives and false negatives), and F1-score (the harmonic mean of precision and recall) were used to report the overall classification performances. Specificity (the number of true negative predictions divided by the sum of true negatives and false positives), and sensitivity (similar to recall) were used to report class-based prediction performances. The false positive rate for occlusion detection was computed using Eq. (1), where FP represents the number of false positive occlusion predictions (i.e., instances incorrectly classified as occlusion, when they were actually normal cardiac or off-body states), and TN represents the count of true negative occlusion predictions (i.e., correctly classified as normal cardiac or off-body states).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation, and frequencies, were used to report participant characteristics. Extracted PPG features were presented with boxplots, displaying the median, interquartile range, and whiskers extending to 1.5 times the interquartile range. The Shapiro–Wilk test (when n < 5000) and Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (when n > 5000) were used to assess the normality of the data distribution. The one-way Kruskal-Wallis H test for independent samples was used for comparing the distributions of PPG characteristics across different cardiac and non-cardiac states. Dunn’s pairwise tests with Bonferroni correction were carried out as post hoc analyses when the Kruskal–Wallis results indicated significant differences. For all statistical tests, the significance level was set at P < 0.05, and adjustments were made using the Bonferroni method for multiple comparisons whenever necessary. Statistical evaluation was performed with SPSS 28.0.1.1 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). We used Python and Scikit-learn 1.3.0 for data preprocessing, training and evaluation of classifiers, and output visualization.
