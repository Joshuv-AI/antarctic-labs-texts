Targeted metabolomics study on peripheral blood neurotransmitters in early-onset schizophrenia
Authors: Meiqi Liu, Xinzhe Du, Kaiyao Xue, Yao Gao, Xiaohua Cao, Junxia Li, Sha Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSchizophrenia (SCZ) is a severe mental disorder, and early diagnosis and intervention are crucial for improving prognosis. An increasing number of studies have demonstrated the association between neurotransmitters and the SCZ. This study aims to explore potential diagnostic biomarkers for early-onset SCZ from the perspective of neurotransmitters.MethodsWe recruited 116 patients with early-onset SCZ and 70 healthy controls. Targeted metabolomics was used to detect the expression of neurotransmitters in peripheral blood. Further analysis was conducted using pathway analysis, correlation analysis, and machine learning methods.ResultsThere were 25 neurotransmitters that were differentially expressed and significantly upregulated in the SCZ group. These substances were enriched in four metabolic pathways, including cysteine and methionine metabolism; glycine, serine and threonine metabolism; phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis, as well as alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism. Further analysis revealed that neurotransmitters such as methionine, sarcosine, tryptophan, tyrosine, phenylalanine and glutamine are positively correlated with positive symptoms. Sarcosine is also positively correlated with negative symptoms and general psychiatric symptoms. Methionine, sarcosine, tyrosine and glutamine are positively correlated with the total score of the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS). Finally, we constructed a diagnostic model using machine learning techniques.ConclusionIn this study, we found 25 differentially expressed neurotransmitters. Among them, the neurotransmitters with key roles were correlated with the patients’ PANSS scores. Furthermore, we constructed a diagnostic model based on six neurotransmitters. Upon validation, the model demonstrated high diagnostic efficacy, providing new insights into the pathogenesis and diagnosis of early-onset SCZ.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07289-2.

Introduction

SCZ is a severe mental disorder characterized by disturbances in thinking, emotions, behavior, and cognition, with an unknown etiology. As one of the top ten causes of disability worldwide, SCZ has the heaviest disease burden, affecting 1% of the global population [1] and reducing life expectancy by about 10–20 years [2]. Early diagnosis and early intervention are important for the good outcome of SCZ, and it is of great significance to search for objective biomarkers for diagnosis.

The neurochemical hypothesis has long played a major role in the pathogenesis of SCZ. Multiple neurotransmitter systems are involved in it, especially dopamine, glutamate, γ-amino­butyric acid (GABA), and serotonin. Dopamine synthesis capacity increased in both people with SCZ and at the risk of developing SCZ, particularly in striatal systems [3, 4]. Elevated glutamate levels have been observed in first episode SCZ [5]. GABA levels change in the medial frontal cortex (mFC) of SCZ, although there are no consistent alterations trends in vivo GABA levels [6]. However, the changes of peripheral blood neurotransmitters in schizophrenia are rarely reported.

Targeted metabolomics based on liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) can be used for qualitative and quantitative analysis of neurotransmitters. This technology can quickly and sensitively detect the types and contents of neurotransmitters in samples, which is conducive to the understanding of disease mechanisms and the identification of biomarkers.

In this study, we detected the neurotransmitter content in peripheral blood of patients with early onset SCZ and healthy controls (HCs) using targeted metabolomics. Differential neurotransmitters were identified, and enrichment pathways and correlation with clinical symptoms of differential neurotransmitters were analyzed. A diagnostic model was finally constructed using machine learning methods.

Methods

Clinical data collection

In our study, 116 patients with early onset SCZ were recruited from the First Hospital of Shanxi Medical University, Henan Mental Hospital, Harbin First Special Hospital, the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University, Xijing Hospital, Taihe Hospital, and Shanxi Mental Hospital. Early onset SCZ patients were recruited according to the following inclusion criteria: (1) The biological parents were all Han nationality. (2) The age ranged from 7 to 16 years. (3) The DSM-5 clinical diagnostic criteria for SCZ were met. (4) Obtained written informed consent from the patient and their legal guardian. Patients with other mental and physical diseases such as schizoaffective disorder, mood disorder, diabetes mellitus, thyroid disease, hypertension, and heart disease were excluded. Matched with early onset SCZ in gender, age, and years of education,70 HCs were recruited from the community.

General information including gender, age, education level and blood samples were collected from the patient group and the healthy group, and PANSS was used to assess their psychiatric symptoms. The study was approved by the hospital Ethics Committee (Ethics Committee approval number: [2019] Lun Shen Zi (Y01)), and all subjects provided written informed consent before participating in the study.

Targeted metabolomics

Blood sample preparation and extraction

After the blood sample was thawed, the sample was vortexed for 10 s. 50 µL of the sample was transferred to a centrifuge tube, and mixed with 250 µL of 20% acetonitrile/methanol, vortexed for 3 min, centrifuged at 12,000 r/min for 10 min at 4 °C. Take 250 µL of supernatant into a new centrifuge tube and place the supernatant in -20 °C refrigerator for 30 min. Then the supernatant was centrifuged at 12,000 r/min for 10 min at 4 °C. After centrifugation, transfer 180 µL of supernatant for further analysis.

LC-MS/MS conditions

The sample extracts were analyzed using an LC-ESI-MS/MS system (UPLC, ExionLC AD; MS, QTRAP® 6500 + System). The UPLC conditions were as follows: column, Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm); solvent system, water with 0.1% formic acid (A), acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (B); gradient, 5% B (0 min), increased to 95% B (0–8 min), held at 95% B (8–9.5 min), and finally ramped back to 5% B (9.6–12 min); flow rate, 0.35 mL/min; temperature, 40 °C; injection volume, 2 µL. The MS conditions were as follows: source temperature, 550 °C; ion spray voltage (IS), 5500 V (Positive), -4500 V (Negative); curtain gas (CUR), 35.0 psi.

Qualitative and quantitative analysis

Based on standard substances, a MWDB (Metware Database) was constructed to perform qualitative analysis of neurotransmitter data, and quantitative analysis of the detected neurotransmitters was conducted using the Multiple Reaction Monitoring (MRM) mode of triple quadrupole mass spectrometry. Using Analyst 1.6.3 software to preprocess mass spectrometry data and MultiQuant 3.0.3 software to process mass spectrometry data, the retention time and peak shape information of the reference standards were used to perform integration correction on the chromatographic peaks of the analytes detected in different samples, ensuring the accuracy of qualitative and quantitative analysis. The peak area represents the relative content of the substance in the sample. Standard solutions of different concentrations were prepared, including 0.1 ng/mL, 0.2 ng/mL, 0.5 ng/mL, 1 ng/mL, 2 ng/mL, 5 ng/mL, 10 ng/mL, 20 ng/mL, 50 ng/mL, 100 ng/mL, 200 ng/mL, 500 ng/mL, 1000 ng/mL, 2000 ng/mL, and 5000 ng/mL, to obtain the corresponding mass spectrometry peak intensity data for each concentration standard. A standard curve for different substances was plotted with the external standard to internal standard concentration ratio on the x-axis and the external standard to internal standard peak area ratio on the y-axis. The integrated peak area ratios of all detected samples were substituted into the linear equation of the standard curve for calculation, and further calculations were performed using the formula to ultimately obtain the content data of the substance in the actual sample. Differential fold change analysis was used to screen for differential neurotransmitters.

PCA and OPLS-DA analysis

Unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the statistical function prcomp in R software. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was conducted using the OPLSR. Anal function from the MetaboAnalystR package in R software.

KEGG enrichment analysis

Identified metabolites were annotated using KEGG compound database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/compound/), annotated metabolites were then mapped to KEGG Pathway database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html).

Network pharmacology construction

To visualize the neurotransmitter -protein-pathway network and to reveal the key neurotransmitters and related proteins, differential neurotransmitters were imported into the MetScape plugin in Cytoscape (Cytoscape Consortium, CA, USA).

Machine learning strategy

We further selected candidate biomarkers from the significantly different neurotransmitters by using the R packages “glmnet” and “varSelRF” (version 3.6.1 of R). We completed the logistic regression models for several biomarker combinations using the R package “pROC” and generated the receiver operating characteristic curves (ROC) with AUC calculations.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using International Business Machines Corporation (IBM) Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 20. The p-value of < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

General clinical data

The general demographic information of the study subjects is shown in supplement Table 1. A total of 186 subjects were enrolled, 116 in the early onset SCZ group and 70 in the HC group. The results showed that there was no significant difference in age (Z = 1.951, P = 0.051), gender (χ²=3.361, P = 0.067) and education level (Z = 1.62, P = 0.105) between the EOS group and the HC group, which were balanced and comparable.

Neurotransmitter analysis

A total of 55 neurotransmitters were detected by the LC-MS/MS method. A total of 44 neurotransmitters were detected in the peripheral blood of early onset SCZ and HC, with concentrations ranging from 0 to 4070.7ng/mL (Supplemental Table 2).

The results of PCA analysis show that the variance contribution rates of the first two principal components are 72.87% and 5.23%, respectively (Fig. 1A), and the two add up to 78.1%. Further OPLS-DA analysis showed significant differences between the SCZ and HC groups (Fig. 1B), with model parameters of R2X = 0.759, R2Y = 0.279, and Q2 = 0.222. And the supervised model was confirmed by 200 permutation tests (Fig. 1C).

Further analysis revealed that a total of 25 neurotransmitters were abnormally expressed in the early onset SCZ group compared with the HC group (FC > 1.5 or FC < 0.666666666666) (Fig. 2A). Pathway analysis

Subsequently, we imported these differentially expressed neurotransmitters into MetaboAnalyst for pathway analysis. The results are shown in Fig. 3A, revealing that differentially expressed neurotransmitters were enriched in biosynthesis of cofactors, 2-oxocarboxylic acid metabolism, biosynthesis of amino acids and other pathways. Then, we constructed an interaction network based on metabolomics and network pharmacology compound-reaction network using the MetScape plugin in Cytoscape with these differentially expressed neurotransmitters. By comparing with KEGG pathways, we identified four overlapping pathways: cysteine and methionine metabolism; glycine, serine and threonine metabolism; phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis; alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, including the compounds methionine, glutathione, serine, sarcosine, threonine, choline, tryptophan, tyrosine, phenylalanine, glutamic acid, glutamine and succinic acid (Fig. 3B).

Subsequently, the correlation analysis between the above twelve neurotransmitters and PANSS score in early onset SCZ patients was carried out (Fig. 3C). We found that methionine was positively correlated with positive symptoms (r = 0.286, p = 0.002) and PANSS total score (r = 0.194, p = 0.040). Sarcosine was positively correlated with positive symptoms (r = 0.224, p = 0.017), negative symptoms (r = 0.231, p = 0.014), general psychiatric symptoms (r = 0.207, p = 0.028) and PANSS total score (r = 0.246, p = 0.009). Tryptophan was positively correlated with positive symptoms (r = 0.221, p = 0.019). Tyrosine was positively correlated with positive symptoms (r = 0.037317, p = 0.317001) and PANSS total score (r = 0.249, p = 0.008). Phenylalanine was positively correlated with positive symptoms (r = 0.238, p = 0.011). Glutamine was positively correlated with positive symptoms (r = 0.295, p = 0.002) and PANSS total score (r = 0.233, p = 0.013). No significant correlation was found in the rest.

Machine learning

Based on the above analysis, we expect to find neurotransmitters that can predict the occurrence and diagnosis of early SCZ. After the initial screening, several neurotransmitters (P < 0.05, FC > 1.5) remain. To further narrow down the scope of biomarker screening, we randomly divided all samples into training set and validation set at a ratio of 2:1. In the training set, LASSO (Fig. 4A) and random forest (Fig. 4B) were applied to identify neurotransmitters, and their intersection was retained as the candidate set (Fig. 4C). A logistic-regression model was then built, with hyper-parameters tuned via grid search coupled with 10-fold cross-validation. Model performance was assessed with the AUC of the ROC curve in both the training and validation sets. Through the above strategy, we determined a diagnostic model containing six neurotransmitters, namely ornithine, glutamine, 2-picolinic-acid, tyrosine, leucine, and choline. The diagnostic model separates early-onset SCZ from healthy individuals. The AUC value in the training set is 0.87, and the AUC value in the validation set is 0.69 (Fig. 4D).

Discussion

In this study, the concentration of peripheral plasma neurotransmitters in patients with early onset SCZ and healthy people was measured. A total of 44 neurotransmitters such as glutamate and 5-Hydroxyindoleacetic-Acid were detected. A total of 25 neurotransmitters were differentially expressed between the two groups. Through pathway enrichment analysis and interaction network and compound reaction network analysis of metabolomics and pharmacology, four important metabolic pathways were identified: cysteine and methionine and metabolism (methionine glutathione); glycine, serine and threonine metabolism (serine, sarcosine, threonine, choline); phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis (tryptophan, tyrosine, phenylalanine); alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism (glutamic acid, glutamine, succinic acid).

SCZ patients often show significant abnormalities in methionine metabolism. Study has shown that methionine excess during pregnancy alters the metabolic and transcriptomic profiles of the fetal brain, leading to long-term behavioral deficits that are associated with SCZ-like symptoms [7]. Preclinical studies indicate that methionine overload, especially when combined with microplastics or ketamine, elicits neurotoxicity and may aggravate schizophrenia-like behaviors [8]. Consistent with this study, we observed that peripheral methionine concentrations were significantly elevated in patients with early-onset SCZ and correlated positively with both positive symptom severity and the total PANSS score. In the future, prospective intervention studies will be needed to determine whether reducing the level of methionine can improve the psychiatric symptoms of early-onset schizophrenia. In our cohort, plasma glutathione was significantly elevated, a finding that appears to contradict the reduced brain glutathione reported in the perinatal phencyclidine rat model of schizophrenia [9]. This might be because we conducted tests on the peripheral blood of patients with early-onset SCZ, and the expression patterns in the blood were different from those in the brain tissue of rats. BBB transport and distinct redox kinetics can isolate central from systemic glutathione pools. Future studies should employ rodent models exposed to perinatal phencyclidine (PCP) and concurrently quantify central and peripheral glutathione levels alongside behavioral phenotypes.

In recent years, an increasing number of studies have focused on the potential role of glycine, serine and threonine metabolism in SCZ. Glycine and serine are co-agonists of glutamatergic N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR), which play an important role in neurotransmission, synaptic plasticity, and cognitive function [10]. Rampino et al. detected the serum amino acid levels in patients with SCZ and healthy individuals, finding that an upregulation of the D-serine/total serine ratio serves as a biochemical marker for the early stages of psychosis, indicating that D-serine may play a role in the early detection of SCZ [11]. We found that the level of serine in the peripheral blood of early onset SCZ patients was higher than that of HC, which further validates the important role of this substance. A recent meta-analysis has suggested that sarcosine (N-methyl glycine) holds promise as an adjunctive intervention for stabilizing patients with SCZ. However, the quality of evidence supporting this conclusion is rated as low to very low [12]. In our study, we observed elevated levels of sarcosine in the peripheral blood plasma of SCZ patients compared to healthy controls. This finding aligns with the potential role of sarcosine as a biomarker and therapeutic target in schizophrenia, although further research is needed to validate its clinical utility and overcome the limitations of current evidence quality. By using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) metabolomics analysis of serum samples from Serbian patients with SCZ and HC, combined with chemometric methods, researchers in Serbia found threonine and other potential biomarkers, which were also verified by patient samples in Brazil and China [13]. Our research results have once again confirmed the important role of threonine as a biomarker for the diagnosis of SCZ. Patients with SCZ exhibit elevated choline levels in certain brain areas, suggesting increased membrane turnover or glial activation, which points to a complex relationship between choline and SCZ [14]. Consistent with the above results, choline level was found to be elevated in plasma of SCZ patients in the present study, and it may act through glycine, serine and threonine metabolism.

Phenylalanine and tyrosine are upstream amino acids in the tryptophan metabolic pathway. They are converted to tryptophan in the body through a series of enzymatic reactions. The metabolic pathways mainly include 5-hydroxytryptamine pathway and kynurenine pathway. Disturbances in tryptophan metabolism have been identified in early-onset SCZ patients, with significant differences in metabolite levels compared to healthy controls, indicating a potential link to the pathogenesis of SCZ [15]. Phenylalanine is an essential amino acid for the synthesis of dopamine. Altered levels of phenylalanine and its metabolites in cerebrospinal fluid and blood have been reported in patients with schizophrenia [16]. The level of phenylalanine in the cerebrospinal fluid of schizophrenic patients with or without antipsychotic drugs was significantly higher than that of the controls [17]. In the study conducted by Olaoluwa Okusaga et al., non-fasting plasma levels of phenylalanine and tyrosine were measured in 950 patients with schizophrenia and 1,000 healthy controls. The results indicated that both the phenylalanine levels and the phenylalanine-to-tyrosine ratio were significantly higher in schizophrenia patients compared to healthy controls [18]. Teraishi et al. used the 13C phenylalanine breath test (13C-PBT) to detect phenylalanine kinetics in patients with schizophrenia and altered phenylalanine kinetics was detected [19]. The present study examined the expression of phenylalanine in peripheral blood of patients with early-onset schizophrenia and found that both were upregulated compared with healthy controls, consistent with the results of the above studies. It’s reported that lower levels of tyrosine in patients with SCZ have been positively associated with executive dysfunction, suggesting a potential link between tyrosine dysregulation and cognitive impairments related to the disease [20]. In contrast, we found that tyrosine levels were increased in the patient group. This might be due to the differences in the research subjects recruited. The subjects we recruited were all individuals under the age of 18 with early-onset SCZ, while the subjects in the previous study were older, above the age of 20. Furthermore, we used patients with SCZ who had not received any medication to confirm that the difference in plasma tyrosine levels between the HC and SCZ groups was entirely due to the disease itself. The results of the previous study might have been influenced by the drugs. We speculate that these differences have a very significant impact on the expression level of tyrosine. In the future, neurotransmitter profiling should be expanded to include individuals across a broader age spectrum, to gain a deeper understanding of the relationship between neurotransmitter expression levels and disease.

Glutamate and glutamic acid are different forms of the same substance. Glutamate is one of the most important neurotransmitter in central nervous system (CNS) and also has a signaling role in peripheral organs, such as activating T cell functions among them adhesion, proliferation and outward K + currents [21]. A study has shown that the level of glutamate in the cerebral cortex of first-episode SCZ patients is elevated before treatment [22]. In this study, we found that the concentration of glutamic acid in plasma of EOS was 1054.03 ng/ml. The glutamate content of early onset SCZ was about 1.6times that of healthy controls. The relationship between succinic acid and SCZ has primarily been explored through its role in gut microbiota metabolism. Studies have shown that certain gut bacteria, such as those belonging to the genus Paraprevotella, can produce succinic acid, which in turn stimulates the production of interleukin-1β (IL-1β). This cytokine can enhance the synthesis of indoleamine-2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1), promoting the conversion of tryptophan to kynurenine. This metabolic pathway has been shown to be dysregulated in patients with schizophrenia [23]. Our study found that the expression of succinate in the peripheral plasma of patients with early onset SCZ was increased, but the specific mechanism needs to be further studied. Studies have shown that the carbon and nitrogen elements in the metabolism of alanine, aspartic acid and glutamic acid may originate from lysine [24], as lysine is one of the decomposition products of the sugar-fructose metabolic pathway. During this process, glutamic acid and α-ketoglutaric acid are produced. Although lysine is not present in the above enrichment pathways, it is also related to schizophrenia. Lysine can inhibit the nitric oxide (NO) signaling pathway, which is involved in the pathophysiological process of schizophrenia and functions by regulating synaptic plasticity, neurotransmitter release, oxidative stress response and neural development [25]. Our research has found that the content of lysine in the peripheral blood of schizophrenia patients is elevated. The relationship between lysine and the metabolism of alanine, aspartic acid, glutamic acid, and its relationship with the pathogenesis of schizophrenia require further in-depth study.

In recent years, machine learning has shown significant potential in the construction of disease diagnostic models, which realizes high-precision and automated disease recognition and classification by integrating multi-dimensional data. For example, in the field of neurodegenerative diseases, the diagnostic model of Parkinson’s Disease (PD) based on wearable sensors and ML algorithms, by analyzing the patient’s gait and movement parameters (such as tremor, stiffness, etc.), using algorithms such as support vector machine (SVM), RF and K-Nearest Neighbours, patients can be distinguished from healthy people with an accuracy close to clinical standards [26]. In this study, we also used machine learning methods to construct a diagnostic model of early onset SCZ, which contained ornithine, glutamine, 2-picolinic-acid, tyrosine, leucine, and choline. Noticeably, they have all been reported to be associated with schizophrenia. Metabolomics studies have found significantly higher levels of ornithine in the serum of chronic SCZ patients compared to healthy controls, indicating that ornithine could serve as a potential metabolic biomarker for the disease [27]. Furthermore, patients with SCZ and comorbid metabolic syndrome exhibit higher levels of leucine, indicating a potential role in the pathogenesis of metabolic disorders associated with SCZ [28]. Lastly, leucine has been identified as one of thirteen metabolites in serum samples from SCZ patients, suggesting its potential as a biomarker for the disorder, with a high prediction accuracy [13]. 2-Pyridinecarboxylic acid is a derivative of pyridine and is renowned for its chelating properties, especially with metal ions such as zinc. Zinc is considered a potential diagnostic biomarker related to SCZ, as alterations in its internal balance can lead to irregular glutamatergic neurotransmission, inflammation, neurodegeneration, and autoimmune deficiencies [29]. Therefore, any potential role of 2-pyridinecarboxylic acid in SCZ may be related to its ability to affect zinc homeostasis.However, dopamine and Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), which are closely related to the pathogenesis of SCZ, were not differently expressed. It has been reported that the concentration of dopamine in peripheral blood is 0.01–0.055 ng/ml, which is the lowest concentration (dopamine concentration) in all tissues and is not within the detection range of this method (5-5000 ng/ml). GABA is a major inhibitory neurotransmitter (GABA gut) that is widely distributed in the central nervous system and gastrointestinal tract but has not been reported in peripheral blood. We speculate that levels in peripheral blood are too low to be detectable because dopamine and GABA cannot penetrate the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and are low in peripheral tissue blood [3].

Conclusions and future perspectives

In this study, we explored the expression of neurotransmitters in the peripheral blood of patients with early onset SCZ and healthy individuals. A total of 44 neurotransmitters were detected, with 25 showing significant differences, all of which were upregulated. These differentially expressed neurotransmitters were enriched in pathways such as cysteine and methionine and metabolism; glycine, serine and threonine metabolism; phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis; alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism. And we found that methionine, sarcosine, tryptophan, tyrosine, phenylalanine, and glutamine are positively correlated with positive symptoms. Sarcosine is positively correlated with negative symptoms and general psychiatric symptoms. Methionine, sarcosine, tyrosine, and glutamine are positively correlated with PANSS total score. Additionally, we constructed a diagnostic model based on these differentially expressed neurotransmitters using machine learning methods, which demonstrated promising diagnostic performance. In summary, our findings indicate that there are differential expressions of neurotransmitters in the peripheral blood of early onset SCZ patients, which hold potential as diagnostic biomarkers. Future research should focus on expanding the sample size for validation and further investigating whether these differentially expressed neurotransmitters in peripheral blood originate from brain tissue.

Although in this study we made great efforts to recruit participants from multiple hospitals, all the research centers were located within China, and all the participants were of the Han ethnicity. This might limit the generalizability of the research results to other racial groups or those with different genetic backgrounds and environmental exposures. When validating our model, we randomly divided all the samples into a training set and a validation set. In future research, an independent cohort should be recruited under the same inclusion criteria, and the same sample collection and testing methods described in this paper should be used to externally validate the diagnostic performance of the model. Secondly, this study adopted a cross-sectional design to measure the levels of neurotransmitters at a single point in time. This approach cannot determine the causal relationship between neurotransmitter imbalance and the onset or progression of early-onset SCZ. In future research, in-depth longitudinal studies are needed to determine causal relationships. Finally, this study examined the levels of neurotransmitters in the peripheral blood of patients with early-onset SCZ. However, due to the presence of the BBB, these levels may not accurately reflect the neurochemical environment within the brain. The lack of detection of dopamine and gamma-aminobutyric acid at the peripheral level further highlights this limitation. Future studies should consider using cerebrospinal fluid or neuroimaging-based metabolomics methods to obtain more direct insights.
