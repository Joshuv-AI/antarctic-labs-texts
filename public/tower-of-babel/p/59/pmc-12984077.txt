Characterizing Family 1 Glycosyltransferases (GT1, UGT) by Reverse Glycosylation: Fast Determination of Acceptor Specificity, Donor Specificity, Hydrolysis, and Enzyme Stability
Authors: Mads R. Langhorn, David Teze
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Manufacturing glycoside conjugates through conventional chemistry is a labor-intensive process that involves multiple reaction steps, generates substantial toxic waste, and often results in low yields with poor control over regioselectivity. In contrast, glycosyltransferase family 1 enzymes offer an efficient alternative, enabling the transfer of glycosyl groups in a single step with excellent regioselective control and high yields. However, the factors governing acceptor specificity and the scope of individual enzymes remain unclear, limiting their broader application. To date, there is no widely implemented, low-cost approach to the high-throughput screening of these enzymes. Here, we use reverse glycosylation with 2-chloro-4-nitrophenyl-β-d-glycosides as a generic high-throughput screening method for UGT characterization. We investigate reverse glycosylation kinetics of 34 UGTs and their donor specificity toward the glucose and galactose moieties. Then, the method is utilized in an acceptor screening with 44 acceptors against 32 enzymes, and we determined the K M parameters for 11 enzyme/substrate pairs. We showcase how this method can be employed for assaying other UGT phenomena, specifically hydrolysis of the glycosyl donor and inactivation upon enzyme dilution. The reverse glycosylation reaction with 2-chloro-4-nitrophenyl-β-d-glycosides can be catalyzed by all 33 assayed UGTs, and therefore the methodology qualifies as a cheap generic tool for high-throughput assays of these enzymes. Moreover, we provide a script for automatic determination of rates and kinetics and present an acceptor data set consisting of 2280 UGT/acceptor pairs.

Introduction

Glycosyltransferases catalyze the transfer of saccharides onto various molecules, playing crucial roles in biology and having significant industrial applications. , These enzymes are of great interest for the manufacturing of glycoside products as they offer high regio- and perfect stereospecificity for glycosylation in a single reaction step under mild conditions. In contrast, chemical glycosylation requires several protection and deprotection steps, extensive use of chemicals, and results in poor atom economy, low yields, and high costs. −

Glycosyltransferases constitute a superfamily organized into 137 families (as of March 11, 2025) based on phylogeny in the carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZy) database, with glycosyltransferase family 1 (GT1) being one of the most studied glycosyltransferase families. GT1s yield β-glycosides by inverting the stereochemistry at the anomeric carbon of the glycoside donor, most commonly of UDP-α-glycosides, hence this enzyme family is commonly referred to as UDP-dependent glycosyltransferases or simply UGTs (Figure a). The UGT enzymes follow a specific nomenclature organized by superfamily, family, subfamily, and individual gene number. The superfamily for this enzyme class is designated as “UGT”. The family is indicated by a number, determined within a given kingdom, and includes enzymes sharing ≥40% amino acid sequence identity. The subfamily is represented by a letter and groups enzymes with ≥60% sequence identity within a family. The individual gene is assigned a unique number within its subfamily. − Hence, all UGT72s present ≥40% identity among each other, and UGT72B29 belongs to the family 72, subfamily B, and is the 29th reported enzyme of this subfamily. Structurally, UGTs present a GTB-fold, which consists of two Rossmann-like domains, where the acceptor and donor recognition are governed by the N- and C-terminal domains, respectively. ,, The glycosylation reaction occurs in the interdomain cleft via an S N 2-like mechanism, where a catalytic base abstracts a proton from the acceptor, facilitating a nucleophilic attack on the anomeric carbon of the UDP-α-glycoside and the formation of a β-glycoside. ,, Individual UGTs can utilize UDP-α-glycosides with various glycosyl moieties as donors or may exhibit a preference for a specific glycosyl group. , While these enzymes possess a relatively conserved motif in the donor-binding C-terminal domain, the factors governing donor specificity remain unclear. Moreover, the key features underlying acceptor recognition remain even more elusive. The plastic nature of the N-terminal domain allows UGTs to accommodate a range of aromatic compounds within a hydrophobic pocket composed of a highly variable amino acid lineup, making it challenging to establish a clear structure–function relationship. ,,,

(a) Reaction mechanism of reverse glycosylation catalyzed by UGTs in the presence of UDP and CNP-glycosides. The reverse reaction is thermodynamically favored due to the highly activated CNP-β-glycosides, which are converted from β-glycosides to UDP-α-glycosides in the presence of UDP, producing the chromophoric CNP compound. (b) Combined mechanism of glycosylation and reverse glycosylation reactions. In the glycosylation reaction, the glycosyl donor is consumed as it reacts with an acceptor, yielding a glycoside product and UDP. The UDP then drives the reverse glycosylation reaction with a CNP-β-glycoside, producing CNP and regenerating the glycosyl donor to enable further rounds of glycosylation.

The promiscuity toward many substrates enables UGTs to synthesize a wide range of glycoside products; however, the limited understanding of their substrate scope and the underlying basis hamper the broader application of these enzymes despite their high potential. , Thus, to accelerate the discovery of substrate specificity in UGTs, high-throughput methods are highly desirable. UGT activity assays often involve the use HPLC or LC–MS to separate and measure the substrates and products after a glycosylation reaction. ,− However, this equipment is relatively expensive and requires extensive chemical use, increasing the cost of these methods, generally reducing the data collection to a handful of data points for a given enzyme/acceptor pair. Additionally, chromatographic methods must be modified depending on the analyte, making these technologies both time-consuming and labor-intensive and ultimately unsuitable for rapid screening. To that end, different high-throughput techniques have been developed that can be employed for UGTs, each differing in methodology, applicability, drawbacks, and the extent to which they have been validated on various UGTs and acceptors. − Nevertheless, despite the different detection methods developed, there is still no consensus regarding a universal, cost-effective approach for high-throughput UGT characterization. ,,

Among the various reported screening methods, Gantt et al. described a particularly simple and elegant colorimetric procedure utilizing 2-chloro-4-nitrophenyl-β-d-glycosides (CNP-β-glycosides), which can drive the standard glycosylation reaction in the reverse direction (i.e., from a β-glycoside to an α-glycoside) when in complex with a UGT and UDP (Figure a). , This, normally disfavored pathway, is rendered thermodynamically feasible due to the lower pK a of CNP compared to UDP (5.43 and 6.50, respectively) and the five resonance structures of the 2-chloro-4-nitrophenolate ion, ensuring its stability in its free state and ability to absorb light at 410 nm. ,, Hence these β-glycosides are regarded as highly activated. The setup enables detection of a “forward” glycosylation reaction between a UDP-α-glycoside and an acceptor as the dissociation of UDP subsequently triggers the reverse glycosylation reaction of CNP-β-glycoside, recycling UDP-α-glucose (Figure b), and creating a 1:1 correspondence between glycosylation turnovers and CNP release. Thus, this approach offers an inexpensive and readily applicable high-throughput method that can be monitored in real-time, requiring only the addition of CNP-β-glycoside to a standard UGT glycosylation reaction and eliminating the need for a reaction termination step or additional chemicals and enzymes, as often required with commercial assay kits. As reverse glycosylation of CNP-β-glycosides offers a straightforward process for production of various valuable nucleotide diphosphate sugars, reported usages of this method has primarily revolved around synthesis of sugar donors with the OleD variant, TDP-16, alone , or in a reaction coupled with another UGT for the glycosylation of an acceptor. − This coupled-enzyme setup allows for assessment of a given UGTs acceptor specificity, assuming that TDP-16 acts only in the reverse mode and that the assessed UGT acts only in the forward mode. However, this setup is slightly cumbersome as it requires the TDP-16 variant, and that both enzymes act in only one mode. Meanwhile, the reported use cases of this method in a single-enzyme uncoupled reaction are scarce and have been demonstrated on only five different UGTs and their mutants, − including the TDP-16 variant and the OleD Loki variant. − True high-throughput screening attempts with this method are covered to an even lesser extent. Worth mentioning in this category is the uncoupled acceptor screening of Sv0189 and the TDP-16-coupled screening of a UGT74AC1 mutant library, both of which serve as examples of successful screenings. To that end, it raises the question of why chromogenic assays with CNP-β-glycosides have not been widely adopted by the GT1 community, especially for rapid screenings, given their simplicity and low cost.

Thus, in this work, we further the potential and applicability of utilizing CNP-β-glycosides, uncoupled from the sugar donor producing TDP-16, in GT1 high-throughput assays targeting various UGTs to investigate reverse glycosylation kinetics as well as donor and acceptor specificity (Figure ). Additionally, we employ this method to assess the acceptor kinetics of UGT88E3 with 11 different acceptors and compare the results to predetermined parameters obtained with HPLC. Finally, we demonstrate the use of reverse glycosylation with CNP-β-glycosides to provide new insights into two less described UGT features, namely, UGT-catalyzed hydrolysis of the donor UDP-α-glycosyl and dilution-induced lability of UGTs. While the former has been documented before, − we show how UGTs hydrolase behavior can affect conventional overnight screenings and provide insight into the hydrolysis kinetics of various UGTs. For the dilution-induced lability, we show the loss of linearity between activity and enzyme amount upon enzyme dilution. For these pursuits, we developed a program in Python for automatic rate determination and calculation of enzyme kinetics enabling fast result responds, which is provided with this work (https://github.com/marola94/activity_to_kinetics.git).

Principle of the chromogenic high-throughput screening of UGT enzymes using CNP-β-glycosides. The UGT assay components, including an excess of a specific CNP-β-glycoside, are added to a microwell plate to facilitate reverse glycosylation, producing CNP while recycling the glycosyl donor. This process, demonstrated here with an acceptor glycosylation reaction, generates CNP as the reaction progresses. Real-time monitoring is achieved through the spectrophotometric measurement of absorbance at 410 nm, and the reaction rates and kinetics can subsequently be analyzed automatically using an accompanied Python program.

Results and Discussion

Reverse Glycosylation Kinetics

The reverse glycosylation reaction depends on the formation of a ternary complex with UDP and CNP-β-glycoside, which is reflected by the K M value for the respective substrates. Thus, in an initial screening, we assessed the kinetics of UDP, CNP-β-glucose, and CNP-β-galactose in the reverse glycosylation reaction for 12 UGTs. For UDP concentrations in the low range (5 μM and 10 μM), high rates were underestimated. Reactions with large variations in the measured absorbances were discarded when fitting the initial rates to the kinetic models, as well as reactions where the initial rate was undetectable due to a very fast reaction. Furthermore, both the Michaelis–Menten and the substrate-inhibition model were used to estimate the kinetic parameters. Saturation with regard to the CNP-β-glucose concentration was reached in 10 out of the 144 reactions (7%) at the 12 studied UDP concentrations, while 67 reactions (47%) reached saturation with CNP-β-galactose (Figures S1–S12). Note that this is generally due to a frequently faster reaction with CNP-Glc rather than to a higher affinity for the CNP-Gal substrate; and that solubility of the CNP-glycosides precludes assaying higher concentration to reach saturation. Yet, the apparent k cat obtained while varying UDP concentrations in the presence of either CNP-β-glycosides at 1 mM will be used here as an apparent k cat for CNP-β-glucose and CNP-β-galactose and demonstrate their ability to accommodate the respective glycosyl moieties. To that end, we proceeded to screen the UDP kinetics with 1 mM CNP-β-glucose and CNP-β-galactose for an additional 21 UGTs to establish an enzyme panel of 34 enzymes and their reverse glycosylation activity with the respective CNP-β-glycosides and their specificity toward the two glycosyl moieties (Table ). 32 of the enzymes were plantae UGTs, while the remaining two were derived from bacteria (UGT102A1) and animalia (UGT205B2), with sequence identities ranging from 15 to 88% (Figure S13). All plots of rates and kinetic models are available on Zenodo, under the DOIs 10.5281/zenodo.15479759 and 10.5281/zenodo.15479801.

Donor Specificity

Based on the measured activities, three of the enzymes were deemed inactive with CNP-β-glucose (RhGT1 and UGT79B206), while the remaining showed dual specificity to some extent; however, as evident from the median k cat of 12.1 min–1 for CNP-β-glucose and 1.12 min–1 for CNP-β-galactose, the majority of the enzymes were far more active with the glucose moiety, in agreement that the most common sugar donor for plant UGTs is UDP-α-glucose. , Indeed, only 8 out of 34 enzymes (RhGT1, UGT71D1, UGT74F2, UGT88J1, UGT74F1, UGT706H1, UGT102A1, and UGT79B206) showed a higher preference for the galactose moiety. UGT71C1 appears as a particularly compelling UGT, being by far the most active enzyme with the glucose moiety, with an apparent k cat more than twice as high as the second most glucose active enzyme (UGT88A10) and in conjunction is just as active as the enzyme with the highest specificity toward galactose (UGT102A1), thus proving as the most dual specific enzyme. However, Gantt et al. (2011) demonstrated the reverse glycosylation reaction with various NDP-sugars for OleD and three mutated variants, this screening exposes that all the UGTs from the panel exhibit the ability to perform this reaction and in addition display that half of the enzymes present k cat values with CNP-β-glucose comparable to most glycosylation reactions with an acceptor.

Acceptor Screening

Considering the low reverse glycosylation activity with CNP-β-galactose, we carried out a large-scale acceptor glycosylation screening with 44 acceptors (Figure S14 and Table S2) and CNP-β-glucose, discarding the two UGTs that were inactive on this substrate (RhGT1 and UGT79B206). The reactions were conducted in a 96-wells plate with 10 or 50 μM of each acceptor, 1 mM CNP-β-glucose, and 2.5 μM UDP-α-glucose. The reactions require the “forward” glycosylation reaction with an acceptor to take place first before the released UDP will induce the reverse glycosylation reaction, which then is detected by the produced CNP (Figure b). The reactions at 10 and 50 μM acceptor concentrations were carried out to assess whether the K M is likely higher or lower than 50 μM. According to Michaelis–Menten kinetics, a K M of 50 μM will give the subsequent expressions for the rates at 50 μM and 10 μM acceptor 1 v50=VMax2 2 v10=VMax6 the ratio between the rates yields the following expression for the rate at 50 μM acceptor, when the K M is 50 μM 3 v50=3·v10 thus, in cases where v 50 < 3·v 10, it gives the indication that the K M of an acceptor with a given enzyme is above 50 μM and vice versa when v 50 > 3·v 10. While this is only a very rough estimation of K M, this is a parameter that is rarely accessible from high-throughput screening assays. The 1581 reactions were visually inspected and classified into positive (rates >1 μM·h–1), negative (rates <1 μM·h–1), and inconclusive reactions (when the rate could not be determined). The full data set along with classification, rates, yield, and K M indications are given in the Supporting Information Data Set, acceptor_screening_data set.xlsx, with plots of reactions given in the supplemental zip folder “acceptor_screening_activities”. Five acceptors are absorbing in the measured wavelength of 410 nm, which leads to high apparent yields, such is the case of esculetin, daphnetin, 3′,4′-dihydroxyflavone, 4-methyldaphnetin, and tropoflavin (Figure S15). For those, rates cannot be precisely measured as we do not know which product or mixture of products is formed and to what extent they absorb light at 410 nm. Nevertheless, we can still easily assess whether an enzyme/acceptor pair is active or not by reading rates and confirming with visual inspection. Thus, it is possible to screen these compounds regardless of their absorbance at any wavelengths. Indeed, many coumarins, flavonoids, and other polyphenols, which are relevant GT1 acceptors, do absorb visible light. Few reactions (3%) were deemed inconclusive, and the activities in such instances were not considered as they were unreliable due to irregularities in the absorbance measurements, presumably caused by the meniscus effect or other inhomogeneities in the reaction solution.

Acceptor Features and Reactivity

There is an apparent preference for monophenolic acceptors as eight of these are represented in the top ten of acceptors with the most positive reactions, namely, octyl gallate, methyl protocatechuate, methyl gallate, olivetol, ethyl gallate, protocatechualdehyde, orcinol, and ethyl orsellinate (Table ). These compounds are derivatives of hydroxylated phenols containing an ester, aldehyde, or alkyl group and possess a high structural similarity. Interestingly, negative charges strongly reduce reactivity as the six carboxylate containing compounds, despite being all monophenols (sodium danshensu, gentisic acid, protocatechuic acid, hydrocaffeic acid, hydroumbellic acid, and 3,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid, see Figure S14 and Table S2) are among the acceptors with the least hits. Particularly, protocatechuic acid, which deviates from protocatechualdehyde and methyl protocatechuate by solely having a carboxyl group instead of an aldehyde or ester, produced only a single hit compared to 15 and 19, respectively. Altogether, it points to the carboxyl group strongly preventing glycosylation, with only five enzymes able to glycosylate these acceptors, and UGT703D1 being the most promiscuous with three hits against acceptors featuring a carboxylate group. To that end, a weak negative correlation of −0.31 was observed between the molecular weight and the number of positive reactions (Figure S15a). However, removing the acidic acceptors strengthened the negative correlation to −0.56 (Figure S15b), highlighting the impact of these acidic compounds and suggesting that molecular size influences acceptor accommodation but is not the only determining factor. Certainly, while the largest molecule, pyrithioxin, resulted in no hits, other large molecules, and in particular pinocembrin and phloretin, produced positive hits in 41% and 34% of the reactions, respectively. In addition, resveratrol and magnolol are particularly interesting acceptors as their positive hits were consistently accompanied by K M values below 50 μM. Specifically, resveratrol showed K M values below 50 μM in 12 out of 13 reactions (92%), while magnolol did so in all 9 reactions (100%).

UGTs Acceptor Promiscuity

Three enzymes did not show activity toward any of the 44 acceptors, namely, UGT74F2, UGT74N7, and UGT102A1. Out of the six enzymes with a preference for galactose included in the screening (UGT88J1, UGT71D1, UGT74F2, UGT74F1, UGT706H1, and UGT102A1), four rank among the 10 enzymes with the fewest positive reactions. While these enzymes were also the least active in reverse glycosylation with CNP-β-glucose, UGT74F1 displayed activity with UDP-α-glucose and five acceptor substrates. Additionally, UGT71D1 stands out as one of the 10 enzymes with the highest number of positive reactions. Although enzymes with a higher preference for galactose in reverse glycosylation might be expected to yield fewer positive reactions in the acceptor screening with UDP-α-glucose as the glycosyl donor, it has been previously reported that donor promiscuity can be influenced by the presence of an acceptor. , In addition, enzymes that exhibited a high K M for UDP with CNP-β-glucose in the reverse glycosylation reaction did not show particular limitations in the acceptor screening. Indeed, UGT88A10, UGT703D1, and UGT71A89, which are among the five enzymes with the highest UDP K M, appear to be the most promiscuous enzymes, together with UGT71F5 (Table ). Hence, despite presenting K M values in the 100 μM range for UDP, those enzymes were perfectly suitable to be assayed for acceptor screening in the presence of only 2.5 μM UDP-α-glucose. Notably, UGT71F5 is active with 21 of 44 acceptors (48%) but is not among the enzymes with the highest UDP K M. UGT703D1 also appears as a quite compelling enzyme with a K M below 50 μM in over half of the reactions (68%), while UGT708A6 prove to be very specific toward the five acceptor substrates it reacts with as all five display a K M below 50 μM. Some enzymes appear more specific to the different molecular classes within the data set. With activity toward 13 of the 18 monophenolic acceptors, UGT88C1 appears the most specific toward this class of compounds, while also being the most active against coumarins together with UGT88A10 and UGT71A89. Interestingly, UGT205B2, being the only enzyme from animalia, is the most flavonoid specific enzyme, with 8 out of 12 hits.

Acceptor Kinetics

In the view of the previous experiments with UGT88E3, which exhibited a low UDP K M (3.8 ± 0.7 μM) and a reasonable acceptor activity with 16 hits, we accessed the kinetics of the enzyme with 11 acceptors (6,7,4′-trihydroxyisoflavone, irigenin, calycosin, oroxylin A, methyl caffeate, 5,7-dihydroxy-4-methylcoumarin, 2,4′-dihydroxybenzophenone, 4′-methoyres-veratrol, kaempferol, daidzein, and genistein) by a chromogenic CNP assay and compared them to those determined by reverse phase HPLC (Table ). The reactions contained 1 mM CNP-β-glucose, 2.5 μM UDP-α-glucose, and an acceptor range 2.9–250 μM. Daidzein, genistein, 6,7,4′-trihydroxyisoflavone, and kaempferol, which are similar in structure and have low water solubility, appear to influence the UV–vis measurements by absorbing light or introducing irregularities (Figures S19, S21, S22, and S24). The k cat values of the different acceptors determined with UV–vis are restricted by the k cat of reverse glycosylation; thus, the estimated k cat is all below those determined with HPLC. Nevertheless, the K M for the acceptors, determined by UV–vis utilizing reverse glycosylation, are all within 1 order of magnitude from those achieved by HPLC.

Hydrolysis in UGTs

We conducted an overnight glycosylation screening with the conventional UGT acceptor 3,4,5-trichlorophenol (TCP) and 25 UGTs, analyzed with reverse phase HPLC and measured after 10 min and 24 h (Figure a). TCP is an environmental pollutant and human health toxin that has become a relevant target for UGT-catalyzed metabolism due to the demonstrated interaction of different chlorophenols with UGTs. , All reactions, except UGT72B82, showed conversion of TCP, whereas 14 reactions (UGT72G3, UGT88C10, UGT71E1, UGT72B68, RhGT1, UGT88C1, UGT88A10, UGT72B19, UGT71D1, UGT71C1, UGT71A89, UGT205B2, UGT72B29, and UGT72E2) displayed a decrease in the glycoside product after 24 h relative to the 10 min measurements, while four of these (RhGT1, UGT88C1, UGT88A10, and UGT71A89) were fully depleted of the TCP-glycoside product after 24 h. The fluctuating product yields clearly illustrate that reported UGT specificities are strongly dependent on the methodology used. The product reduction is a consequence of a reverse glycosylation reaction, where the UGT enzyme catalyzes the formation of UDP-Glc and TCP from TCP-glycoside and UDP. Some of the UDP is formed during the TCP glycosylation reaction; however, if it was the only source of UDP, an equilibrium would be reached, and we will not observe a decrease in TCP-glycoside concentration. The additional UDP is formed via UGT-catalyzed UDP-α-glucose hydrolysis yielding glucose and UDP. This later reaction is practically irreversible, driving the reaction toward product disappearance (Figure b). As UDP-α-glycoside hydrolysis is a thermodynamically favored reaction, we could hypothesize that the end point of any reversible O-glycosylation reaction is full product depletion due to the detachment of UDP catalyzing reverse glycosylation. In any case, this shows that the single detection of products after overnight reactions leads to false negatives for UGTs presenting high hydrolytic rates. Hence, we studied hydrolytic rates via our reverse glycosylation methodology.

Hydrolytic Rate

To assess the hydrolytic activity of UGTs, we screened 17 enzymes at three concentrations (125–500 μg·mL–1) with a chromogenic CNP assay, with 2 mM CNP-β-glucose and 5 μM UDP-α-glucose. Thus, in the event of UDP-α-glucose hydrolysis, the detached UDP will catalyze the reverse glycosylation reaction of CNP-β-glucose and release CNP to be measured (Figure c). Furthermore, we validated the hydrolysis rate detected with CNP in the chromogenic assay, with the rate of hydrolysis reflected by the produced glucose and detected with a d-Glucose HK test kit from Megazyme, proving consistency between the rates determined with each method at three different enzyme concentrations (Figure S27). Among the 17 UGTs, 13 displayed hydrolytic activity increasing linearly with the amount of enzyme (Figures d, S28 and S29), demonstrating that the reaction is catalyzed by the enzymes. Note that the hydrolysis k cat values do not necessarily coincide with the reduction of the TCP glycoside product from the screening as this reduction also depends on the reverse glycosylation kinetics of the TCP glycoside. The estimated k cat of UDP-α-glucose hydrolysis for the respective enzymes was all below the corresponding reverse glycosylation k cat with CNP-β-glucose; thus, the rate of hydrolysis is rate limiting in the assay. Strikingly, k cat values spanning over 2 orders of magnitude were observed, from <0.1 to 10 h–1, highlighting a very enzyme-dependent parameter. As evident from the apparent k cat values, the hydrolysis reaction is a slow side reaction and subordinate to a standard glycosylation reaction, with minimal effect at low enzyme concentrations and short time durations. Consequently, the hydrolytic tendency of the most hydrolytic enzymes, UGT75L5, UGT72G3, and UGT88C1, is not reflected in the chromogenic acceptor screening, which would be indicated by a high median rate, as hydrolysis of the donor glycoside is the only viable reaction when glycosylation with an acceptor is omitted. Hence, the chromogenic acceptor screening can be used even for the most hydrolytic enzymes. Similarly, hydrolysis should not affect other chromogenic methodologies used for initial rates determination and kinetics. On the other hand, with rates up to 90 μM per hour at 500 mg/L enzyme concentration, depletion of over 1 mM UDP-α-glucose would be observed within a 24 h experiment, resulting in false negative as evidenced in Figure a. Thus, since overnight reactions in the presence of a high enzyme concentration are common for acceptor substrate screenings, hydrolysis of the glycoside donor may potentially interfere with the outcome of such experiments.

Enzyme Stability

Glycosylation reactions catalyzed by UGTs have previously been shown to deviate from a linear relationship between rate and enzyme concentration, stagnating at high enzyme concentrations, while inactivating at diluted enzyme conditions. , This dilution-induced deactivation phenomenon can presumably be described by the fact that these enzymes are secluded from their biological environment when utilized for assays in vitro or in an industrial context. Thus, enzymes are lacking the stabilizing effect of surrounding biomolecules, which can be thought of as the quinary protein structure. On the other hand, crowding agents such as PEG or proteins such as bovine serum albumin did not restore activity of diluted UGTs. To assess the dilution-induced deactivation with the chromogenic assay using CNP-β-glycosides, we constructed 12 reactions for six enzymes (UGT706F8, UGT88C1, UGT36, UGT88E3, UGT72B19, and UGT71C1) with enzyme concentrations ranging from 40 μg·mL–1 to 20 ng·mL–1 along with 250 μM UDP and 1 mM CNP-β-glucose to measure their absorbance at 410 nm after 2 h. The data are presented in Figure , along with a log10 transformation for better visualization. Indeed, 5 out of 6 UGTs show inactivity due to the dilution of enzyme concentration, with UGT71C1 being resistant to this effect. UGT706F8, UGT88C1, UGT88E3, and UGT72B19 are inactivated at concentrations below 2.5 μg·mL–1, while this happens for UGT72B10 at 313 ng·mL–1. In addition, UGT71C1 is the only enzyme that displays a linear relationship between activity and enzyme concentration before an equilibrium is reached and a sublinear relationship is established. This sublinear relationship at the high enzyme concentrations is also observed for the remainder of the enzymes, which on the other hand exhibit a superlinear trend at lower enzyme concentrations where the activity is more than double upon doubling the enzyme concentration after 2.5 μg·mL–1. These observations are consistent with previous findings, which reported stability issues upon dilution for these UGTs. To gain insight into the effect of a stabilizing quinary structure by other UGTs, we created an experiment for UGT706F8 and UGT88E3 with the same enzyme dilution range (i.e., 40 μg·mL–1 to 20 ng·mL–1) and setup as before, however with 2.5 μg·mL–1 UGT88E3 added to the reactions with UGT706F8 and vice versa for UGT 38 (Figure g–h). Since both enzymes are active at 2.5 μg·mL–1 enzyme, all the reactions with the additional enzyme experience a higher absorbance relative to the corresponding reaction without the enzyme addition. Nevertheless, the superlinear trend is less pronounced as the breakoff point has shifted down to 600 ng·mL–1 and 800 ng·mL–1 for UGT706F8 and UGT 38, respectively. Thus, there is a synergistic effect of the presence of another UGT, proving the presence of a stabilizing quinary structure between different UGTs.

Conclusions

Here, we show the broad applicability of the use of CNP-glycosides for assaying UGTs. Indeed, all 34 assayed UGTs displayed reverse glycosylation activity, and we showed that the methodology could be applied to investigate donor specificity, acceptor specificity, enzyme stability, hydrolysis, and kinetic determinations. Importantly, this makes assaying UGTs fast, cheap, and scalable.

An initial chromogenic screening of reverse glycosylation kinetics for 34 UGTs with CNP-β-glucose and CNP-β-galactose proved all enzymes capable of catalyzing the reverse glycosylation reaction to a varying degree. While all enzymes exhibited activity with the galactose unit, and three were deemed inactive on CNP-β-glucose, the activity was significantly higher for reactions with this substrate, indicating a preference for the glucose moiety for the majority of the enzymes. The following acceptor screening of 44 acceptors with 32 enzymes worked for all of the assayed enzymes without a dual enzyme system to detect a reaction. The assay identified 315 reactive pairs, showing that the UGTs to be more permissive toward monophenolic compounds and rarely active against carboxylic acceptors. Acceptor K M for UGT88E3 was consistent for 11 acceptors reported in literature. To demonstrate how the chromogenic method can be established for other phenomena involving UGTs, assays considering UGT-catalyzed hydrolysis of UDP-α-glucose and stability complications due to dilution-induced deactivation were constructed. The estimated apparent k cat values of hydrolysis for the 17 enzymes screened gave a clear indication of a slow side reaction with a nominal effect at low enzyme concentrations. Nevertheless, this phenomenon can influence results of specificity screenings with extended time duration, resulting in lower yields or complete depletion of glycoside products, due to the simultaneous reverse glycosylation of the glycoside product and hydrolysis of the sugar donor taking place. The stability of UGT enzymes is greatly affected by a quinary structure as the presence of a secondary enzyme decreases the enzyme concentration at which dilution-induced deactivation occurs as well as the superlinear relationship between enzyme amount and activity at low enzyme amounts.

The chromogenic assay method using CNP-β-glycosides is broadly applicable among UGTs, owing to their inherent ability to catalyze the reverse glycosylation reaction with these activated substrates. While the challenges posed by reverse glycosylation kinetics and absorption interference highlight certain drawbacks of the method, the resulting glycosylation rates remain reliable for determining the UGT acceptor specificity. Considering the inherent reverse glycosylation ability of UGTs and the simplicity of this chromogenic method with CNP-β-glycosides, we propose it as a robust tool for general substrate specificity screenings. Moreover, the ability to integrate programmable scripts for data handling and analysis significantly reduces the time and labor, making this method truly high-throughput.

Experimental Section

Materials

All microplates used in the experiments were clear flat-bottom 96-well microplate from Grenier Bio-One, while the UV–vis measurements were made in a Biotek Epoch 2 Microplate Spectrophotometer from Agilent. 2-Chloro-4-nitrophenyl β-d-glucopyranoside (CNP-β-glucose), 2-chloro-4-nitrophenyl β-d-galactopyranoside (CNP-β-galactose), and UDP-α-glucose were purchased from Biosynth.

Protein Expression and Purification

Glycerol stocks of E. coli cells transformed with plasmids encoding UGT genes from an in-house library were used to initiate cell cultivation. The sequences of all 34 UGT proteins can be found in Table S3. Cells were cultivated until they reached an optical density of 0.6–0.8 at 600 nm, before inducing protein expression with 200 μM isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside and incubation at 20 °C for 16 h at 120 rpm. 10 μg/mL DNase I from bovine pancreas and 30 μL Triton X- were added to the cell cultures, prior to lysing cells with an ultrasonic processor from SONICS model VCX 130 for 6 min with intervals of 30 s and 80% amplitude. The lysed cell cultures were then centrifuged, and the supernatant was purified by nickel affinity chromatography with a HisTrap FF prepacked column on an ÄKTA pure chromatography system from Cytiva. The fractions with purified UGT enzyme were then pooled, concentrated, and buffer exchanged to 25 mM phosphate at pH 7.4 and 50 mM NaCl before flash freezing aliquots of 50 μL and storing them at −70 °C.

Reverse Glycosylation Kinetics

In total, 34 UGTs were screened for the reverse glycosylation kinetics with respect to the UDP, with concentrations ranging from 5 μM to 10 mM UDP distributed in 12 reactions and 1 mM CNP-β-glucose or CNP-β-galactose. Among the 34 screened UGTs, 13 were in addition screened for the reverse glycosylation kinetics with respect to CNP-β-glucose and CNP-β-galactose, with CNP-β-glucose concentrations ranging from 625 μM to 5 mM and CNP-β-galactose concentrations ranging from either 437.5 μM to 3.5 mM or from 312.5 μM to 2.5 mM. All reactions were conducted in a 96-well microplate with reaction volumes of 100 μL, 5 μg/mL enzyme (2.5 μg/mL for UGT2), and 6.25 mM phosphate buffer pH 7.5 with measurements every 10 s at 410 nm at 30 °C for 2 h.

Acceptor Screening

32 UGTs were screened for their specificity toward 47 acceptors in a 96-well microplate with each acceptor distributed at 50 and 10 μM in the microplate, thus occupying 94 of the wells. The reactions contained 1 mM CNP-β-glucose, 25 mM phosphate buffer at pH 7.5, 2.5 μM UDP-α-d-glucose, and 35 μg/mL enzyme. The microplate was incubated at 30 °C with measurements every 10 s at 410 nm for 2 h. Two negative controls were created in the remaining two wells without an acceptor.

Acceptor Kinetics

The kinetics of UGT88E3 with 11 acceptors (6,7,4′-trihydroxyisoflavone, irigenin, calycosin, oroxylin A, methyl caffeate, 5,7-dihydroxy-4-methylcoumarin, 2,4′-dihydroxybenzophenone, 4′-methoxyresveratrol, kaempferol, daidzein, and genistein) were achieved with acceptor concentrations ranging from 2.9–250 μM, diluted 2/3 in a 96-well microplate. The reactions contained 1 mM CNP-β-glucose, 2.5 μM UDP-α-d-glucose, as well as 35 μg/mL UGT88E3 and was measured in the UV–vis at 410 nm.

TCP Screening

Glycosylation of 500 μM 3,4,5 trichlorophenol (TCP) by the 26 UGTs (concentration ranging 0.2–2 mg/mL) in the presence of 500 μM UDP-α-d-glucose was assessed by measuring the TCP-glycoside yields by reverse phase HPLC, as previously described. Reactions were carried out at room temperature without stirring and quenched by sampling 10 μL of reaction mixture into 240 μL of acetic acid after 10 min or 24 h.

Coupled Hydrolysis Assay

Three 100 μL reactions containing UGT72B68 at the concentrations 500, 250, and 125 μg/mL were constructed in a 96-well microplate. The reactions contained 2 mM CNP-β-glucose and 5 μM UDP-α-glucose along with the prescribed 10 μL of NADP+/ATP, 2 μL of HK/G-6-PDH, and 10 μL of buffer solutions from the d-Glucose HK test kit by Megazyme. Thus, the detection of the reaction is coupled to both 2-chloro-4-nitrophenol (CNP) detachment measured at 410 nm and NADPH formation mediated from the d-Glucose HK test kit content, which is measured at 340 nm. The reactions lasted for 6 h at 30 °C with 1 min interval between measurements.

Chromogenic Hydrolysis Screening

For each UGT, three 100 μL reactions were generated in a 96-well microplate. Each reaction contained 2 mM CNP-β-glucose, 5 μM UDP-α-glucose, 50 mM phosphate buffer pH 7.5, and either 500, 250, or 125 μg/mL enzyme. The reactions were incubated at 30 °C for 6 h in the spectrophotometer measuring absorbance at 410 nm each 33rd s to determine the concentration of released CNP upon reaction during the experiment.

Stability Assay

UGT706F8, UGT88C1, UGT36, UGT88E3, UGT42, and UGT71C1 were diluted with a 1/2 scheme from 40 μg/mL to 20 ng/mL in a 96-well microplate. In addition, a dilution series of UGT706F8 from 40 to 20 ng/mL containing 2.5 μg/mL UGT88E3 was constructed as well as a dilution series of UGT88E3 from 40 to 20 ng/mL containing 2.5 μg/mL UGT 10. All reactions contained 50 mM phosphate buffer at pH 7.5, 250 μM UDP, and 1 mM CNP-β-glucose and were measured for 2 h at 30 °C in the UV–vis at 410 nm with 10 s interval.
