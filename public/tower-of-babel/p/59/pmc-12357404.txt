Butyrate enhances gut dysbiosis by activating the cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling pathway to inhibit the progression of endometrial carcinoma
Authors: Muheng Tao, Tingting Wu, Xin Zhou, Fengjie Li, Yingfan Chen, Kaijian Ling, Zhiqing Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGut microbiota may contribute to the progression and prognosis of extra-intestinal tumors. The relationship and mechanism between endometrial carcinoma (EC), the gut microbiota, and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) remain ambiguous. This study aimed to ascertain whether alterations in gut microbiota and SCFAs are associated with EC.MethodsWe assessed the gut microbiota composition and SCFAs concentrations in healthy volunteers and patients with EC using 16S ribosomal RNA and liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry, respectively. Subsequently, we investigated the impacts and associated mechanisms of the gut microbiota and SCFAs on cell phenotypes through cell experiments and comprehensive RNA sequencing.ResultsThe gut microbiota of patients with EC exhibited significant variation compared to that of healthy volunteers. At the phylum level, the abundance of Proteobacteria elevated in patients with EC relative to healthy individuals, whereas the abundance of Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes were diminished. Furthermore, the concentrations of acetic acid and butyric acid in the feces of patients were remarkably lower than those in healthy individuals. The fecal supernatant of patients with EC promoted the proliferation, invasion, and migration of EC cells and inhibited cell apoptosis. However, these effects were reversed following the addition of sodium butyrate (NaB) and sodium acetate. On further investigating the mechanisms underlying SCFAs involvement in regulating EC, RNA-seq analysis revealed enrichment of the cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling pathway in the EC supernatant plus NaB compared with EC. The tumor growth rate was accelerated in mice treated with the feces of patients with EC compared to untreated mice. Nevertheless, these effects were reversed by NaB gavage. NaB supplementation to EC feces increased Occludin and Claudin 1 protein expression in the colon of mice with EC, reshaped the structure of the mouse colon, decreased the interleukin (IL)-1β and tumor necrosis factor(TNF)-α levels, increased the IL-10 level, and exerted a protective role in EC progression.ConclusionsFeces of patients with EC revealed gut dysbiosis. Furthermore, fecal microbiota transplantation could promote tumor growth and disrupt the normal structure of the mouse colon; however, these effects were alleviated by butyrate, highlighting it as a viable therapeutic option.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04190-2.Keywords: Endometrial carcinoma, Gut microbiota, Fecal microbiota transplantation, Short-chain fatty acids

Background

Endometrial carcinoma (EC) is frequently observed in developed countries, and its global incidence is increasing [1]. According to United States Cancer Statistics [2], 66 200 estimated new cases and 13 030 deaths attributed to EC were reported in 2023. EC is the most prevalent malignant tumor of the female reproductive system in China, demonstrating incidence and mortality rates of 7.74 and 1.6 per 100 000 individuals, respectively [3]. EC often occurs in women aged > 50 years; however, its occurrence in younger women is increasing, with approximately 4% of cases occurring in women aged < 40 years [4]. Obesity, diabetes mellitus (DM), early menarche, late menopause, and metabolic disorders are some risk factors associated with EC [5]. Notably, the long-term survival and prognosis of patients with EC, particularly those with advanced and recurrent disease, remain unsatisfactory despite certain improvements.

EC is a multifactorial and complex disease closely related to genetic, endocrine, and other internal factors [6]. In recent years, increasing evidence has shown that the gut microbiome exerts systemic effects through metabolite production and immune modulation [7], mechanisms that may also play a significant role in the development and progression of EC. Notably, Proteobacteria, present in both the gut and female reproductive tract, showing dynamic shifts in abundance associated with gynecological malignancies, Acinetobacter (genus of Proteobacteria) is enriched in tumor micro-environments in ovarian cancer (OC) and promotes chemo-resistance via Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-mediated signaling [8]. Interestingly, similar microbial involvement has been observed in EC. Proteobacteria were observed to be enriched in feces of patients with EC while compared with control one, suggesting a potential role in disease progression [9]. Specifically, Proteobacteria may contribute to endometrial carcinogenesis through dual pathways: (1) Estrogen metabolic reprogramming—bacterial β-glucuronidase (e.g., from Escherichia) hydrolyzes conjugated estrogens into bioactive forms, elevating local estrogen levels and stimulating endometrial proliferation [10, 11]; (2) Microbes may facilitate tumor cell proliferation and survival by regulating the expression of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-8 [12], which promote EC cells invasion and migration [13, 14]. While direct causal evidence linking Proteobacteria to EC remains limited, their established roles in inflammation and hormonal modulation provide a plausible framework for further investigation. Future studies should validate these mechanisms using longitudinal cohorts and preclinical models to delineate microbiome-EC interactions.

Emerging evidence underscores the pivotal role of gut microbiota-derived metabolites, particularly short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) like butyrate and acetate, in modulating the pathogenesis of EC [15]. Butyrate exerts potent anticancer effects in EC primarily through its function as a histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor, with antitumoral effects in EC cells, butyrate treatment induces apoptosis via increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and activation of DNA damage response pathways [16], as evidenced by elevated γH2AX foci formation [17]. Additionally, butyrate also inhibits cell proliferation by regulating the expression of cell cycle-related proteins (such as p21, p27), blocking the cell cycle at G0/G1 and/or G2/M phases [18, 19]. While acetate has been less extensively studied in EC, existing evidence from other cancer models suggests it may influence tumor progression through distinct mechanisms. Acetate acts as an acetyl-CoA precursor, promoting HDAC and activating lysosomal/autophagy-related genes [20]. In vitro studies further indicate that acetate exhibits context-dependent effects, potentially suppressing proliferation via mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation while inducing cytotoxic oxidative stress at high levels [21]. These findings highlight the various roles of SCFAs in EC pathogenesis while underscoring the need for further investigation to fully elucidate their therapeutic potential in this malignancy.

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) is used to treat various intestinal diseases. Transplanting functional microbiota from healthy individuals to patients aids in reconstructing a new gut microbiome, thereby restoring the diversity of intestinal microbiota and facilitating the treatment of both intestinal and extraintestinal disorders. For example, transplantation of gut microbiota from patients with OC into tumor-bearing mice accelerated OC progression, whereas supplementation with Akkermansia muciniphila during FMT significantly suppressed tumor growth by enhancing CD8 + T cell activity and interferon-γ (IFNγ) production [22]. Although this study focused on OC, mounting evidence suggests that gut microbiota influences EC progression through three principal mechanisms: estrogen metabolism regulation via GUS-producing bacteria [23], immune microenvironment modulation through altered Treg/Th17 balance [24], and delivery of anticancer metabolites like butyrate, which inhibits HDAC [16] and regulates PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [25, 26]. Despite these advances, no studies have yet investigated FMT in EC models, creating a critical knowledge gap considering the established involvement of the microbiome in estrogen-driven malignancies..

In this study, we hypothesized that gut dysbiosis would play a key role in tumor progression in patients with EC and supplementation with SCFAs could inhibit proliferation and promote tumor apoptosis, thereby inhibiting EC progression. Accordingly, this study aimed to determine the composition and differences in the gut microbiota and SCFAs of patients with EC and healthy volunteers using 16 s rRNA and liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) for SCFAs. Additionally, we also identified the signaling pathways involved in the EC gut microbiota and butyrate via cell phenotype experiments and RNA-seq.

Methods

Human subject and sample

A total of 30 newly diagnosed patients with EC at Obstetrics and Gynecology department of the Army Medical University Southwest Hospital were included, and 10 healthy volunteers matched for age and BMI were referred to as healthy control group. The exclusion criteria for this study were: 1. Patients with primary gastrointestinal disorders (irritable bowel syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease, peptic ulcers, etc.); 2. Patients who have received pelvic or intraperitoneal radiation or chemotherapy before; 3. Patients with other malignant tumors; 4. Antibiotic usage within the past 14 days; 5. Patients who have diarrhea or constipation within the past 14 days. Feces were collected from patients with EC and healthy volunteers and stored at −80℃. This project was approved by the Ethics Committee of Southwest Hospital of the Third Military Medical University (Project No.: (A) KY2022104). Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their enrollment in the study.

Stool 16 s rRNA gene sequencing and analysis

Fecal DNA (150 mg/sample) was extracted using the Magnetic Soil and Stool DNA Kit (TianGen, DP328). The V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified with primers 515F-806R, using Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (NEB, M0532S) under the following conditions: 98℃ (1 min), 30 cycles of 98℃ (10 s), 50℃ (30 s), and 72℃ (30 s), followed by a final extension at 72℃ (5 min). PCR products were verified on a 2% agarose gel, pooled in equimolar ratios, and purified using the Qiagen Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, 28,704).

Sequencing libraries were prepared with the TruSeq® DNA PCR-Free Kit (Illumina, FC-121–3001) and assessed for quality using a Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. Paired-end sequencing (250 bp) was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq platform. Raw reads were quality-filtered using QIIME (v1.9.1). Chimeric sequences were removed via comparison with the SILVA database (UCHIME algorithm), yielding high-quality effective tags for downstream analysis.

Alpha diversity (within-sample diversity) was calculated and compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (two-tailed). Beta diversity (between-sample diversity) was visualized via Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) using the ade4 and ggplot2 packages in R (v4.0.3). Statistical significance of β-diversity difference was evaluated using PERMANOVA (Adonis) and ANOSIM. Additionally, the Simper analysis identified taxa contributing most to group differentiation (top 10 species shown). All analyses were conducted with the Vegan and ggplot2 packages in R, and statistical significance was set at p less than 0.05.

Stool SCFAs measurements by LC–MS

100 mg of feces was added to the pre-cooled 0.75 mL of methanol and vortexed with shaking. 2.5 mL of pre-cooled methyltert-butylether (MTBE) was added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature on a shaker. 0.625 mL of water was mixed by stratification the organic phase, after incubating at room temperature for 10 min, it was centrifuged at 1000 xg for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. After LC–MS analysis, the chromatographic separation was performed under the following conditions: column: Thermo Accucore C30, column temperature: 40℃, flow rate: 350 μL/min, sample size: 5μL, mobile phase A: (acetonitrile: water = 60:40) + 0.1% formic acid + 10 mM ammonium acetate, mobile phase B: (isopropanol: acetonitrile = 90:10) + 0.1% formic acid + 10 mM ammonium acetate. The raw data were imported into LipidSearch software. Peak extraction was performed by matching parent ions (mass deviation ≤ 5 ppm) and fragment ions (subions) against the LipidSearch database. Peaks were then aligned across samples using a retention time deviation threshold of 0.05 min and a signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio ≥ 3. Background ions were removed by blank subtraction, and lipid species were qualitatively and quantitatively analyzed.

SCFAs concentrations were normalized to fecal wet weight. Briefly, 100 mg of feces was homogenized in 3.875 mL of methanol/MTBE/water (total extraction volume), and supernatant concentrations (μg/mL) were converted to μg/g wet weight by multiplying by 38.75 (3.875 mL ÷ 0.1 g). Group differences in SCFAs levels were tested using Mann–Whitney U tests in Graphpad Prism V9.0 (San Diego, CA). The difference was generally considered to be statistically significant if the p value was less than 0.05.

RNA-seq

Total RNA was extracted from three experimental groups: (1) endometrial carcinoma control cells, (2) cells treated with EC feces supernatant, and (3) cells treated with EC feces supernatant plus sodium butyrate, using TRIzol reagent (Takara, 9109). RNA integrity was verified using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit on a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent, 5067–1511). For library preparation, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse transcribed using SuperScript II reverse transcriptase (Thermo Fisher, 18,064–014). cDNA fragments (370–420 bp) were size-selected using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter). Libraries were amplified with Phusion High-Fidelity DNA polymerase (Thermo Fisher) and Illumina universal/index primers, followed by AMPure XP purification. Quality control was performed on the Bioanalyzer 2100 system.Sequencing was conducted on an Illumina Novaseq platform (150 bp paired-end) after cluster generation with the TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Illumina) on a cBot system.

We used clusterProfiler R package to test the statistical enrichment of differential expression genes in KEGG pathways.The Reactome database brings together the various reactions and biological pathways of human model species. Reactome pathways with corrected p value less than 0.05 were considered significantly enriched by differential expressed genes.

Chemicals

Sodium butyrate (98.0%, CH3CH2CH2COONa, NaB, 303,410) and Sodium acetate (99.0%, CH3COONa, NaAC, S8750) were purchased from Merk (Germany).

Cell culture

Human endometrial carcinoma cell lines (Ishikawa, AN3CA) were purchased from Procell (CL-0283, CL-0505). Two cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin and streptomycin. All cell were cultured at 37℃, 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere of cell incubator.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cells were plated into a 96-well cell culture plate to determine the effects of fecal supernatant from EC patients plus different concentrations of NaB or NaAC on cancer cells. Cell viability was measured by using a CCK-8 assay (Bioground, BG0025), and placed in a CO2 incubator for growth. After co-cultured for different time gradients, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent and 90 μL cell medium were added to each well, and the OD450 nm was then measured in each well using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA) after cell were cultured at 37℃, 5% CO2 for 45 min.

5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) staining assays

We used EdU assay kits (Beyotime, C0071S) to perform cell proliferation experiment. Cells were plated into a 24-well cell culture plate for 24 h, every well was added same volume of sterile phosphate buffer saline (PBS), patients’ fecal supernatant, patients’ fecal supernatant plus 1 mM NaB or 1 mM NaAC. After 24 h being co-cultured, EC cells were added with EdU for 2 h at 37℃, 5% CO2 cell incubator. Then all cells were were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100. Next, the cells were added ready-to-use click reaction mixture for about 30 min, avoided light. Finally, DAPI (Beyotime, C1006) was used for 10 min to stain nuclei.

Wound healing assay

Cells were plated into a 6-well cell culture plate in order to reach 90% confluence. A 200 μL pipette tip was used to make a scratch on cells, then wells were placed into same volume of PBS, patients’ fecal supernatant, patients’ fecal supernatant plus 1 mM NaB or 1 mM NaAC with serum-free medium. 0 h and 48 h photographs were recorded to detect the migration ability of two cell lines using an Olympus BX53 microscope (Tokyo, Japan).

Transwell assay

Transwell chambers were placed into a 24-well cell culture plate. Put the upper membrane of the chambers with a layer of Matrigel (Corning, 354,234, diluted 1:8 in serum-free medium), and incubated at 37℃ for 6 h to allow it to solidify. Then, inoculated the upper chamber of the Transwell chamber with a cell suspension, while added cell culture medium containing 10% serum to the lower chamber. Next, placed the entire setup in 37℃, 5% CO2 cell incubator. After co-cultured for 48 h, removed the non-invaded cells from the upper chamber, and stained them with crystal violet. Finally, observed and counting the cells that invaded to the lower chamber under a microscope. We have conducted each in vitro experiment, including viability, proliferation, migration and invasion assays, with a total of 3 independent biological replicates. For each biological replicate, we included 3 technical replicates to ensure the robustness and reliability of our data.

Mice

24 five-week-old female Balb/c nude mice (specific pathogen free grade) (Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd., China), with 6 mice in each cage, were housed at a constant temperature of 26℃, with a cycle of 12 h of light and 12 h of darkness, at the Animal Center of Southwest Hospital. Animal experiments were approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of the Army Medical University (Project No: AMUWEC20242005), and performed in accordance with the relevant institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

FMT

First, feces from patients with EC were collected. 150 mg fresh feces were immediately suspended in 1 ml pre-cooled sterile PBS, mixed for 2 min on ice, then large particles were filtered. All fecal fluids were divided into 200 μL per centrifuge tube and stored at −80℃. The mice were randomly divided into 4 groups with 6 mice each group: (1) NC group, provided with food and water normally. (2) PBS group, 100 μL ABX was administered orally daily for 10 days, then 200 μL sterile PBS gavaged per day for 16 days. (3) EC group, 100μL ABX was administered orally daily for 10 days, then 200 μL patients’ feces gavaged per day for 16 days. (4) EC + NaB group, 100 μL ABX was administered orally daily for 10 days, then 200 μL patients’ feces plus 20 mg/kg body weight of NaB gavaged per day [27] for 16 days. At the end of the treatments, all mice were euthanized using CO2 inhalation, then tumor and colon tissues were separated.

Measurement of fecal endotoxin

Fecal fluids endotoxins were detected by Improved Chromogenic LAL Endotoxin Assay Kit following the manufacturer's instructions (Beyotime, C0278S).

Hematoxylin&Eosin(H&E) staining

After observation and FMT, all mice were euthanized, and their colons and tumors were dissected and fixed with tissue fixative (Servicebio, G1101). Then, tissues were embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4-μm-thick slices, then attached to the slides. H&E staining was carried out using hematoxylin and eosin staining solution (Beyotime, C0105M).

Colonic histological damage was assessed using a comprehensive scoring system that evaluate loss of crypt architecture, inflammatory cell infiltration, goblet cell depletion, and Mucosal edema [28, 29].

Immunohistochemistry(IHC)

Colon and tumor tissues were fixed in 10% formalin, performed in 4-μm-thick paraffin sections and heated at 100℃ in a microwave-oven using citric acid antigen repair buffer (Beyotime, P0083). After natural cooling, endogenous peroxidase blocked with 5% BSA, incubated with primary antibody Ki-67 (Abclonal, A20018, 1:1000), Occludin (CST, #91131S, 1:100), Claudin1 (Abclonal, A11530, 1:100) and secondary antibody (Boster, SV0004), used appropriate color agents (3,3'-Diaminobenzidine, DAB) to produce a visible color response to the bound antibody (ABclonal, RK50015). Hematoxylin (Beyotime, C0107) was used to stain the nuclei and increase contrast. Finally, sections were permanently fixed on slides using neutral gum.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA)

Mouse colons were rinsed with PBS and homogenized in lysis buffer containing phosphatase inhibitor cocktail and protease inhibitor (Beyotime, P1045) using a tissue homogenizer. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm and 4℃ for 15 min to separate the supernatant. Tissue proteins levels of interleukin (IL)−1β, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α、IL-10 were measured by ELISA kit (ABclonal, RK00006, RK00027, RK00016).

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from AN3CA cells using TRIzol reagent (Takara, 9109), and cDNA was synthesized from the isolated RNA using a reverse transcription kit (Takara, RR047A). The real-time quantitative PCR was performed with TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, RR820A) on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA).

The RT-qPCR protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95℃ for 3 min, followed by 39 cycles of denaturation at 95℃ for 10 s and annealing/extension at 57℃ for 30 s (with fluorescence acquisition). Melting curve analysis was performed from 65℃ to 95℃ with 0.5℃ increments every 5 s.

The 2 ΔΔCt method was employed to measure CERB3L2, PRKACA and FOS expression after normalization to GAPDH expression. The following primers were used: CERB3L2-F GCACCTGGACCACTTTACGG, CERB3L2-R AGCACAGGGTCATCAAAGAAG, PRKACA-F AGCCCACTTGGATCAGTTTG, PRKACA-R GTTCCCGGTCTCCTTGTGT, FOS-F CAAGCGGAGACAGACCAACT, FOS-R GTGAGCTGCCAGGATGAACT, GAPDH-F GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT, GAPDH-RGGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG.

Western Blotting(WB)

Cells and tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) and fixed with phosphatase inhibitor cocktail and protease inhibitors Cocktail (Beyotime, P1045). Then, the samples with 5 × SDS loading buffer (Beyotime, P0015L) were boiled at 100 ℃ for 10 min. Next, protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE (Beyotime, P0012A) to form different bands according to the molecular weight size, and transferred onto 0.2 μm/0.45 μm polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, ISEQ00010, IPVH00010), incubated with primary antibodies target protein.

Bcl2 (CST, #15,071, 1:1000), BAX (Abclonal, A20227, 1:1000), α-tubulin (Abclonal, A6830, 1:10,000), β-actin (Abclonal, AC026, 1:30,000), p-CREB (CST, #9198,1:1000), CREB (CST, #9197,1:1000), p-PKA (Affinity, #AF7246,1:1000), PKA (Affinity, ##AF7746,1:1000), Occludin (CST, #91131S, 1:1000), Claudin1 (Abclonal, A11530, 1:1000) at 4℃ overnight. Secondary antibodies were incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, we used the corresponding substrate ECL (ABclonal, RM00021) to observe the bands of the target protein in the ChemiDoc Gel Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA).

Terminal dexynucleotidyl transferase (TdT)-mediated dUTP nick end labeling(TUNEL)

Tumor tissues were performed in 4-μm-thick paraffin sections, utilizing Beyotime TUNEL kit (Beyotime, C1088). Samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, treated with proteinase K (Beyotime, ST533) to increase membrane permeability, then added with H2O2 to block endogenous peroxidase activity, incubated with a TUNEL reaction mixture (containing TdT enzyme and labeled dUTP). Samples were washed with PBS buffer to remove unbound markers, and DAPI was added to stain the nuclei. Finally, the number of apoptotic cells was visualized under BX53 Olympus fluorescence microscope (Beijing, China).

Fluorescence in situ hybridization(FISH)

FISH assay was performed in 4-μm-thick paraffin sections utilizing Servicebio FISH kit (GF003) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. The FISH probe targeting Proteobacteria was purchased from Servicebio: BET42a: 5-GCC TTC CCA CTT CGT TT-3. The slides were finally stained with DAPI. After that, images were captured using BX53 Olympus fluorescence microscope (Beijing, China).

Quantification and statistical analysis

For comparisons between two groups, the't-test'analysis option was chosen and the"two-tailed Student's t-test"was ensured to compare continuous variables. For three or more comparisons, select the"ANOVA"analysis option. We selected directional ANOVA or bidirectional ANOVA based on the data design, and viewed the analysis results, noted the p value to determine statistical significance. The difference was generally considered to be statistically significant if the p value was less than 0.05. We highlighted the number of biological replicates of the experiment, with at least 3 independent biological replicates for cell experiments. By following the above steps, the data analysis using GraphPad Prism V9.0 is both accurate and reliable, thus providing strong support for the experimental conclusions.

Results

Gut microbiota alterations in EC

Based on the established inclusion and exclusion criteria, we included 30 patients with newly diagnosed EC (EC group) and 10 healthy volunteers as the control group (NC group). A schematic representation of the experimental design is presented in Fig. 1. No statistically significant differences were observed in age or body mass index between patients with EC and healthy individuals (Table. S1).

Fecal samples were examined using 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Compared with the NC group, the EC group displayed a markedly reduced Shannon index (Fig. 2A), indicating decreased α-diversity in EC patients. While PCoA revealed no significant separation in β-diversity between groups (Fig. 2B), the observed reduction in α-diversity suggests potential functional alterations within specific microbial taxa rather than global community restructuring. These findings imply that particular microbiota compositional changes, rather than broad community differences, may be associated with EC development. Bar graphs were used to further reveal differences in the gut microbiota between the groups. The gut microbiota of the EC and NC groups comprised Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, and Proteobacteria. At the phylum level, the abundances of Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes were lower in the EC group than in the NC group, whereas the abundance of Proteobacteria was significantly higher in the EC group than in the NC group (Fig. 2C). Similarly, at the species level, the abundance of Escherichia coli, which belongs to Proteobacteria, was higher in the intestine of the EC group than in that of the NC group (Fig. 2D). The microbial composition at the class, order, family, and genus levels is presented in Fig. S1.

We performed linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) analysis with the LDA threshold set at 2 to determine the taxonomic abundances (Fig. 2E, F) most indicative of group differences. Notably, a marked difference was observed between the groups, and 32 enriched microbiota were identified. Among them, p_Proteobacteria, c_Gammaproteobacteria, o_Enterobacterales, f_Enterobacteriacea, and s_Escherichia coli were the dominant microbiota in the EC group, whereas o_Oscillospirales, g_Holdemanella, g_Fusicatenibacter, and g_Papillibacter were the main microbiota in the NC group.

Gut microbiota of EC influences SCFAs concentration in feces

SCFAs are produced via the fermentation of glycolytic non-digestible sugars by Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes [30]. Moreover, acetic acid (AA), butyric acid (BA), and propionate acid (PA) were the main SCFAs in the colon, with a concentration ratio of approximately 3:1:1 [31]. We further investigated whether SCFAs affected the dominant bacteria in EC. Seven SCFAs—AA, PA, BA, isobutyrate acid (iBA), isovaleric acid (iVA), valeric acid (VA) and caproic acid (CA)—were detected in the feces of the EC and NC groups. LC–MS analysis revealed significantly altered fecal concentrations of BA and AA between groups. The BA concentration was significantly higher in the NC group than in the EC group (median [IQR]: 8392.86 [5562.18–12,259.73] μg/g wet weight vs. 2852.78 [2351.39–5950.84] μg/g; U = 72, p = 0.0155). Similarly, AA levels were higher in the NC group than in the EC group (12,710.39 [11443.26–15,516.28] μg/g vs. 8392.86 [5562.18–12,259.73] μg/g; U = 61, p = 0.0057) (Fig. 3A).

Content of SCFAs and the correlation between SCFAs and EC dominant bacteria. (A) Concentration of seven SCFAs in feces in patients with EC (n = 30) and healthy volunteers (n = 10). Correlation analysis of (B) Proteobacteria, (C) Gammaproteobacteria, and (D) Escherichia_coli with AA and BA, respectively. Each dot represents a different sample. * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01

At the phylum, class, and species levels, the EC-dominant bacterial groups were Proteobacteria, Gammaproteobacteria, and Escherichia coli, we observed significantly negative correlations in Spearman correlation analyses between specific microbial taxa and metabolite concentrations: Escherichia coli abundance showed inverse association with BA (r = −0.344, p = 0.026), while both Proteobacteria (r = −0.384, p = 0.014) and Gammaproteobacteria (r = −0.384, p = 0.014) abundance correlated negatively with AA concentration. Proteobacteria abundance was also inversely related to BA levels (r = −0.321, p = 0.043), indicating taxon-specific microbial-metabolite relationships in our analysis (Fig. 3B, C, D).

Adding NaB and NaAC to EC fecal supernatant promoted cell apoptosis and inhibited cell proliferation, migration, and invasion

To explore the effects of EC feces and SCFAs on EC, we treated two EC cell lines–Ishikawa and AN3CA–with the fecal supernatant or supernatant plus BA or AA. NaB and NaAC were purchased as BA and AA, respectively, and their 1 mM concentration was used as the optimal concentration for the CCK-8 assay in subsequent experiments (Fig. 4A). The CCK-8 and EdU experiments indicated that the EC fecal supernatant maintained the proliferation of EC cells, which was significantly inhibited following NaB and NaAC addition (Fig. 4A, B), with a more pronounced effect of NaB than NaAC. Furthermore, western blotting results showed decreased expression of Bcl2 and enhanced expression of BAX in the EC + NaB group compared with the NC and EC groups, indicating cell apoptosis was activated following NaB addition (Fig. 4C). Meanwhile, NaAC addition showed a similar tendency as NaB for the two proteins, its effects were notably weaker. Moreover, the wound-healing assay revealed a decrease in the migration rate of AN3CA cells following NaB and NaAC addition, as well as a declining trend in the migration of Ishikawa cells (Fig. 4D). In addition, a Transwell assay (Fig. 4E) was conducted to detect the invasive capacity of EC supernatant supplemented with the two SCFAs. Compared to EC supernatant alone, both NaB and NaAC treatments significantly reduced invasive capacity, with NaB demonstrating a lower invasive capacity effect than NaAC. These results indicated that EC fecal supernatant maintained malignancy of Ishikawa and AN3CA cells, and may participate in EC tumor development. However, the addition of NaB or NaAC to the EC supernatant exhibited the opposite results.

NaB inhibited the malignant process of AN3CA cells by activating the cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling pathway

Our cell phenotype experiments indicated that NaB was more effective than NaAC in inhibiting EC tumor progression. Therefore, we performed gene expression analysis of AN3CA cells under three conditions: (1) EC group (treated with EC fecal supernatant only), (2) EC_NaB group (treated with EC fecal supernatant + 1 mM NaB), and (3) NC group (standard medium control). In the Venn diagram (Fig. 5A), the unique and shared gene expression profiles among AN3CA cells treated with NC, EC, and EC_NaB are depicted, highlighting 293, 254, and 1224 unique genes, respectively, and 11 892 genes expressed across all groups. In total, 6338 upregulated and 5221 downregulated genes were detected in the EC_NaB group compared to the EC group, whereas 703 upregulated and 508 downregulated genes were detected in the EC group compared to the NC group (Fig. 5B, C). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis revealed the enrichment of these genes in pathways related to EC, thyroid carcinoma, carcinogenesis, and tumor initiation (Fig. 5D). Additionally, KEGG enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes showed the enrichment of certain signaling pathways, such as cAMP and Ras (Fig. 5E).

KEGG enrichment analysis demonstrated the upregulation of some genes in the cAMP signaling pathway (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, PRKACA, CREB3L2, and FOS, which represent PKA, CREB, and FOS genes, respectively, were highly expressed in the EC_NaB group. These genes, which are known to be key signaling nodes in the cAMP/PKA/CREB pathway, showed pronounced differential expression. For example, FOS exhibited a log2FC of 2.97, indicating a substantial increase in expression levels in response to NaB treatment. Moreover, the cAMP/PKA/CREB pathway was identified as the top enriched pathway in our analysis (KEGG p = 5.95 × 10–9), further emphasizing the importance of these genes in mediating the biological effects of NaB. These genes were hypothesized to play pivotal roles in the cellular signaling cascades that are activated by NaB treatment. Their differential expression patterns provide insights into the specific molecular mechanisms through which NaB exerts its effects. Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction was performed to confirm these results (Fig. 5G). Additionally, western blotting revealed decreased phosphorylation of PKA and CREB following the addition of EC fecal supernatant, whereas their levels increased following NaB addition (Fig. 5H, I). Therefore, NaB may inhibit progression of EC via the cAMP/PKA/CREB pathway.

Effects of FMT on EC tumor progression in the mouse model

Due to the lack of orthotopic xenografts, we established the patient-derived xenografts mouse model for in vivo experiments. Five-week-old nude mice were randomly divided into four groups of six each. After 10 days of exhausting the original gut microbes, the mice were subcutaneously injected with AN3CA cells to investigate the effects of EC feces and EC feces plus NaB on tumor occurrence and development (Fig. 1). All fecal fluids of patients with EC were confirmed to contain endotoxin levels below 0.5 EU/mL [32] (Fig. S2). The tumor volume was measured every 2 days following the establishment of the animal models. The tumor volume and weight decreased following FMT of EC feces plus NaB (Fig. 6A, B, C) compared with the case of EC feces gavaged alone. Specifically, the mean volume of tumors in the EC group was 1348 ± 351 mm3, whereas that in the EC + NaB group was 589 ± 285 mm3. The H&E and IHC staining of the paraffin-embedded tumor sections revealed abnormal proliferation of tumor cells (Fig. 6D). The Ki-67 positive rate in the tumor cell nuclei of the first three groups was significantly higher (70–90%) than the EC + NaB group (nearly 60%), indicating weakening of the proliferation capacity of tumor cells following NaB addition (Fig. 6E). Moreover, TUNEL assay and western blotting were used to evaluate the impact of EC feces and EC feces plus NaB on mouse tumor cell apoptosis. The TUNEL assay revealed a higher total apoptosis rate in the EC + NaB group than in the EC group (Fig. 6F). Similarly, the levels of apoptosis-related proteins Bcl2 and BAX confirmed the hypothesis that NaB enhances apoptosis in tumor cells (Fig. 6G, H). To confirm the signaling pathway identified using RNA-seq, western blotting analysis revealed that EC fecal transplantation reduced the phosphorylation and expressions of PKA and CREB in the mouse tumor tissues. However, this effect was reversed following NaB addition to the EC feces (Fig. 6I, J). Collectively, these results illustrate that NaB effectively inhibits tumor cell proliferation and promotes apoptosis in EC.

Therapeutic effect of NaB on EC feces induced inflammation in the colon

Colon tissues were isolated from four groups of mice. H&E staining revealed an increase in inflammatory cells in the interstitial space of the mucosal lamina propria, partial thinning of the mucosal lamina propria, a reduction in crypts, disordered arrangement of crypts, irregular crypt morphology, and partial atrophy of crypts in the EC group (Fig. 7A). Furthermore, as tight junction (TJ) proteins play a crucial role in protecting the intestinal mucosal barrier, their damage can lead to increased permeability between cells, causing bacterial or toxin translocation, leading to various diseases. Therefore, we measured the levels of the TJ proteins—Occludin and Claudin1—using IHC. Compared with the NC and PBS groups, the levels of TJ proteins in the colon were lower in mice that received feces from the patients with EC (Fig. 7B, C). Conversely, following NaB addition to the feces, Occludin and Claudin1 levels were elevated. Immunohistochemical assessment revealed significant differences in TJ proteins expression between experimental groups. For Occludin, the EC + NaB group demonstrated markedly higher expression scores compared to the EC group (8.25 ± 1.39 vs. 5.50 ± 0.76; FC = 1.50, p = 0.002). Similarly, Claudin1 expression was significantly higher in the EC + NaB group (4.22 ± 1.56) than in the EC group (2.78 ± 0.67) (FC = 1.52, p = 0.018). These findings collectively indicated that NaB treatment enhances intestinal barrier function through upregulation of key TJ proteins, which was in line with the previous study that SCFAs and other metabolites can protect the intestinal barrier [33]. Based on these observations, mouse colons were ground into tissue homogenates, and the quantification of the expression of the two TJ proteins revealed similar results as those of IHC (Fig. 7D). Furthermore, pro-inflammatory cytokines in colon tissues were associated with the severity of inflammation, therefore, we measured inflammation-related factors in colon tissues using ELISA assay. Compared with EC + NaB group, the levels of pro-inflammatory IL-1β (533.7 ± 75.43 pg/mL vs. 378.5 ± 35.03 pg/mL, FC = 1.41, p = 0.0868) and TNF-α ((459.7 ± 54.99 pg/mL vs. 218.3 ± 40.95 pg/mL, FC = 2.11, p = 0.0078) were higher in the EC group than in the EC + NaB group, whereas the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 were significantly increased in the EC + NaB group compared to the EC group (608.8 ± 77.08 pg/mL vs. 236.4 ± 26.41 pg/mL, FC = 2.57, p = 0.0012) (Fig. 7E). These results suggested that NaB may alleviate the EC fecal gavage-induced intestinal inflammatory environment, potentially through modulation of inflammatory cytokine levels. Then, FISH was performed to detect Proteobacteria distribution, which exhibited high abundance in the EC group in the LEfSe analysis. Notably, Proteobacteria severely invaded the colon tissue of mice after being gavaged with the feces of patients with EC. However, their abundance decreased following NaB addition, with only a small fraction of the bacteria remaining in the colon tissue (Fig. 7F).

Discussion

This study demonstrated a high abundance of Proteobacteria in the gut of patients with EC, which disrupts normal colonic mucosal structures. It is reported that different bacterial phyla have been identified in the human gut microbiota, and compositional changes in major groups such as Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes are closely related to human health and disease. In our study, 16S rRNA analysis revealed a significantly higher abundance of Proteobacteria at the phylum level in patients with EC than in controls. LEfSe analysis further identified increased abundance of Gammaproteobacteria, Enterobacterales, and Enterobacteriacea in the feces of patients with EC. Targeting SCFAs revealed decreased abundance of Firmicutes and Bacteroides in the feces of patients with EC, accompanied by decreased concentrations of their metabolites, butyrate and acetate, which were negatively correlated with the abundance of Proteobacteria. SCFAs can enhance the release of antimicrobial peptides and IL-22 by binding to the GPR43 receptor, thereby strengthening the intestinal physical barrier [34–36]. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota leads to increased intestinal permeability and displacement of the microbiota, ultimately damaging overall intestinal health [37]. Our experiments showed that after being gavaged with EC feces altered intestinal permeability, reduced the expression of TJ proteins Occludin and Claudin1, and disrupted the intestinal barrier. Interestingly, histological and molecular experiments revealed that adding NaB reduced the expression of inflammatory cytokines in the colon tissue of mice, restored the colon mucosal structure, and significantly reduced Proteobacteria invasion. These effects were due to the addition of butyrate, while also reduced pro-inflammatory factors and increased anti-inflammatory factors in the colon, which is consistent with the previous studies [38–40].

Further, RNA-seq, western blotting, and in vitro cellular validation experiments demonstrated that signaling pathways such as cAMP/PKA were altered in tumor tissues by being gavaged with EC feces + NaB in mice. In the KEGG analysis, the cAMP/PKA/CREB pathway was selected for validation primarily due to its significant enrichment (p = 5.95 × 10–9), with key genes (FOS, PRKACA, CREB3L2) consistently upregulated upon NaB treatment. This statistical prominence suggests its functional importance in EC biology. Similar to our findings with butyrate, other activators of the cAMP/PKA pathway have also demonstrated anti-tumor effects in EC. For instance, GLP1R overexpression was reported to inhibit EC progression via cAMP/PKA signaling, with PKA knockdown abolishing these protective effects [41]. This consistency across different upstream stimuli (GPCR activation by GLP1R vs. metabolite sensing by butyrate) underscores the central role of the cAMP/PKA/CREB axis in EC suppression. Overall, these findings identified a novel mechanism through which FMT in EC treats host Proteobacteria infections, facilitates intestinal mucosal repair, and promotes tumor cell apoptosis by adding the bacterial metabolite butyrate via the cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling pathway, as we showed in the Fig. 8.

Moreover, NaB treatment significantly elevated p-PKA and p-CREB levels, while increasing the pro-apoptotic marker BAX and decreasing the anti-apoptotic marker Bcl2, directly linking pathway activation to EC cell apoptosis. This finding was consistent with observations in other cancers. For example, a study in 2017 [42] confirmed the antitumor effect of roflumilast in ovarian cancer through the activation of cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling, while EIF5A expression was shown to promote lung cancer cell growth by inhibiting this pathway [43]. These studies collectively highlighted the critical role of the cAMP/PKA/CREB pathway in tumor suppression across different cancer types, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target in EC.

Several studies have demonstrated a strong relationship between gut microbiota and certain gynecological diseases [44–46]. Most patients in our study had type I EC, which is often associated with obesity, DM, and estrogen-dependent tumors linked to metabolic syndrome. Although gut microbiota is influenced by estrogen, the gut microbiota itself can also significantly affect estrogen levels [47]. Estrogen receptors are expressed in many tissues, including colon tissue, where they maintain the integrity of the colon mucosal TJ proteins and barrier function [48]. Metabolic disorders such as obesity may occur when gut microbiome homeostasis changes. Some metabolites of the gut microbiota, such as SCFAs, are involved in biological pathways associated with obesity. SCFAs can stimulate L-cells to produce glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) by binding to GPR41 and GPR43 [49, 50]. Therefore, the gut microbiota and their metabolites regulate the risk factors for EC.

We selected standard specific pathogen-free mice instead of germ-free mice to exhaust their gut microbiome. Through FMT, we simulated the intestinal micro-environment of mice to resemble that of patients with EC. Although our research was not conducted under strictly sterile conditions, this model more accurately reflects the natural state.

Recent studies highlight that specific tumor-associated bacteria can serve as diagnostic tools and predictors of disease progression. For example, Proteobacteria can distinguish metastatic from non-metastatic prostate cancers [51]. Moreover, some bacteria affect the prognosis of tumors, and the Kaplan–Meier curve of the overall survival of patients demonstrated an association of Bacteroides, Lactobacillus, and Peptoniphilus with poor prognosis in pancreatic cancer [52].

The limitations of our study include the small sample size and single-center analysis. Future studies should focus on blood and tumor bacteria as new means of detection to predict the occurrence and development of cancer. Furthermore, large sample sizes from multiple centers are required to determine the correlation of the microbes in the feces, blood, and tumors, both in vivo and in vitro experiments are necessary to verify our findings.

Conclusions

FMT from patients with EC to mice promoted tumor growth, increased intestinal permeability, and damaged the normal structure of the colon. Notably, these effects were alleviated following butyrate addition, which was found to induce apoptosis in EC cells through the cAMP/PKA/CREB pathway and significantly decrease tumor growth. These preclinical findings suggest that butyrate supplementation may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for EC, though further clinical validation is required.
