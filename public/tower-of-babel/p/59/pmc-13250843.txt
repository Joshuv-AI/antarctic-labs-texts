A lifespan staging model of basal forebrain cholinergic vulnerability
Authors: Hayley R. C. Shanks, Sherry Chen, Kate M. Onuska, Taylor W. Schmitz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe cholinergic neurons of the basal forebrain are among the most vulnerable cell types to age‐ and Alzheimer's disease‐related dysfunction and neurodegeneration. However, the biological bases of basal forebrain cholinergic vulnerability (the why) and the temporal sequence (the how) have yet to be fully resolved. In this review, we summarize multi‐disciplinary evidence to present a lifespan staging model of basal forebrain cholinergic vulnerability, which outlines how converging biological stressors position these neurons to fail. We trace the origins of basal forebrain cholinergic vulnerability from evolutionary and neurodevelopmental pressures, which ultimately interact with midlife stressors to culminate in two divergent trajectories of cholinergic aging: resilient and vulnerable. Lastly, we highlight that the branch point in cholinergic aging trajectories may serve as a critical window for intervention if sensitive, specific, and clinically scalable in vivo cholinergic biomarkers can be developed.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common cause of dementia worldwide. 1 Despite substantial debate regarding its etiological basis, the prevailing AD hypothesis 2 , 3 states that aggregation of toxic β‐amyloid (Aβ) proteins within and around neurons leads to tau hyperphosphorylation, increased neuroinflammation, synaptic dysfunction and loss, and eventually, clinically detectable cognitive decline. 4 , 5 Although Aβ and tau are hallmarks of AD, these pathologies do not appear in the brain all at once. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Rather, Aβ and tau initially affect specific neurons in distinct brain regions, before spreading to other neurons throughout the brain. A major challenge in AD research has been to explain the cellular basis of selective neuronal vulnerability to these neuropathologies. 10 , 11 , 12

Among the most vulnerable neuronal subtypes are the cholinergic neurons of the basal forebrain (BFCNs; see Box 1). Increasing evidence suggests that BFCNs are impacted by Aβ and tau pathologies, 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 cellular dysfunctions, 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 and neurodegeneration, 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 decades before the onset of cognitive symptoms associated with AD. Although this principle of selective cholinergic vulnerability has been recognized for nearly half a century, 28 its biological basis and temporal sequence have yet to be fully resolved.

BOX 1: Human Basal Forebrain Anatomy

The basal forebrain (BF) is a heterogeneous collection of subcortical nuclei located deep in the ventral forebrain, arranged along an anteromedial–posterolateral axis (Figure 2A). These nuclei include the medial septal nucleus (MS), the diagonal band of Broca (DBB), and the nucleus basalis of Meynert (nbM). 29 , 30 , 31 Within these loci reside the magnocellular cholinergic neurons that provide the principal source of acetylcholine (ACh) to the neocortex, hippocampus and amygdala. Classical tract‐tracing and human imaging work indicates that BFCN projections collectively innervate the entire cortical mantle and medial temporal lobe, with partially segregated but overlapping corticotopic maps. 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 The anatomy of the BF has been extensively reviewed elsewhere. 38 , 39 , 40 , 41

Here, we review evidence suggesting that BFCN vulnerability cannot be solely understood as a downstream consequence of Aβ or tau pathology. Rather, converging findings across disciplines indicate that BCFN vulnerability reflects intrinsic features of the evolutionary and developmental design of these neurons, as well as their lifelong functional demands. We integrate evidence from various fields of study to develop a lifespan staging model of BFCN vulnerability (Figure 1), beginning with structural and functional constraints, progressing through midlife stressors and compensatory adaptations, and ultimately culminating in trajectories of resilience or vulnerability.

Conceptual lifespan staging model of BFCN vulnerability. Staging of BFCN vulnerability across the lifespan (A), and key mechanisms contributing to BFCN vulnerability occurring at each stage (B). BFCN vulnerability is primed from unique evolutionary pressures (Stage 0) and developmental requirements (Stage 1). In post‐developmental periods, intrinsic morphofunctional characteristics, including large somal size and expansive axonal branches, interact with pressures encountered in midlife (Stage 2), including dysfunctional lipid metabolism and transport, age‐related reductions in neurotrophin metabolism, as well as early intracellular accumulation of Aβ. To cope with these stressors, mechanisms of resilience (Stage 2R) may become activated, prolonging BFCN survival and function. Individuals may either remain resilient throughout their lifespan (i.e., superaging or normal aging, depending on the degree of resilience), or progress to vulnerable aging (Stage 2V). During Stage 2V, disease processes such as tau aggregation and spreading, choline metabolic dysfunction, and impaired trophic signaling ultimately lead to BFCN degeneration. BFCN degeneration further potentiates the progression of Aβ and tau pathology as well as inflammatory cascades throughout the brain, eventually leading to the onset of cognitive impairment during Stage 3. A LIFESPAN STAGING MODEL OF BASAL FOREBRAIN CHOLINERGIC VULNERABILITY

Stage 0: Evolutionary pressures create intrinsic BFCN susceptibilities

The unique organization of the BFCN projection system (Box 1) allows it to modulate the activity of diverse neuronal populations throughout the mammalian cortex. Recent cross‐species studies have demonstrated that BFCNs can emit precisely timed ACh signals locked to the detection of salient stimuli, rewards, and errors on millisecond timescales, while also supporting slower, tonic modulations of cortical state and gain. 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 Optogenetic recordings in the mouse BF further reveal at least two BFCN types: bursting neurons that synchronize strongly with cortical theta rhythms and emit rapid bursts following behaviorally significant events, and regular‐firing neurons that respond with precise single spikes whose coupling to cortical activity predicts detection accuracy. 47 These data indicate that BFCNs are intrinsically equipped to operate across multiple temporal regimes.

In humans, the need for such multi‐scale temporal control may be amplified by the expansion of transmodal association cortex and large‐scale networks supporting flexible attention and behavioral control. Recent work from our group has demonstrated that an anteromedial–posterolateral gradient across BF nuclei maps onto a cortical gradient in which some regions are innervated by more modular, short‐range cholinergic ensembles (more neurons, fewer branches per neuron), whereas others—most prominently midcingulo‐insular and adjacent transmodal areas—are innervated by more diffuse, highly branched projections (fewer neurons, more branches per neuron). 48 , 49 The midcingulo‐insular hub is a canonical site for coordinating switches between segregated and integrated network configurations during attentionally demanding behavior. 50 , 51 , 52 From a network neuroscience perspective, such regions are under continuous selective pressure to rapidly reconfigure the brain's functional topology to support the frequent, brief episodes of focused attention that characterize daily human life. 53

In this sense, the selective vulnerability of BFCNs in humans may stem in part from evolutionary pressures (i.e., Stage 0) required to support a progressively more complex cognitive repertoire. The BFCN system is broadly conserved across vertebrates, including bony fishes, amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals. 54 Within this system, however, the nucleus basalis of Meynert (nbM) exhibits a striking pattern of evolutionary expansion. In rodents, the nbM is approximately the same size as the medial septal nucleus (MS)/diagonal band of Broca (DBB), and only ∼20% of its neurons are cholinergic. 55 In contrast, in primates, the nbM is substantially larger than the MS/DBB, and up to 90% of its neurons are cholinergic 29 , 56 (Figure 2B). The variation in nbM size and cholinergic neuronal representation across species parallels the evolutionary expansion of the neocortex it projects to, likely reflecting an increased demand for acetylcholine (ACh) signaling to regulate the cortical microcircuits that support higher‐order cognitive functions. 54 Morphological and electrophysiological analyses have further demonstrated that cholinergic neurons occupying homologous positions in the nbM exhibit species‐specific properties. For example, compared to mice, primate nbM cholinergic neurons projecting to the basolateral amygdala (BLA) display greater intrinsic excitability and a less compact morphological structure, 42 reflected by a larger convex hull area and volume enclosing all primary arborizations (Figure 2B). Collectively, these findings suggest that evolutionary pressures have shaped both the cholinergic composition and structural organization of the nbM, as well as the functional characteristics of its constituent neurons.

Stage 1: Neurodevelopment primes BFCNs for later life vulnerability

Although the expansion and physiological retuning of human BFCNs are recent in evolutionary terms, they arise early during forebrain development relative to other brain structures. 54 At present, our understanding of BFCN development in the human brain (Stage 1) is underexplored. However, we can draw clues from other mammalian species about how a mismatch between phylogenetic history and ontogenetic timing translates to later life BFCN vulnerability. In rodents, nbM cholinergic neurons are the first BFCN population to appear. 60 Retrograde labeling studies have demonstrated that early in development, these neurons primarily innervate evolutionarily older, deep cortical layers within primary sensory regions, 57 which is thought to contribute to the maturation of cortical networks. 58 , 59 From the outset, nbM cholinergic neurons are thus positioned as long‐range regulators of developing cortical circuits, even before the full complement of association cortex has emerged. The early specification of nbM cholinergic neurons has also been shown to rely on a coordinated combination of transcription factors (i.e., NKX2.1, LHX8, ISL1), morphogens, and trophic factors to establish their large corticopetal identity 60 (Figure 2C). Recent work has further demonstrated that distinct nbM cholinergic subpopulations differentially express key trophic receptors and regulons for survival and maintenance. This dependency has also been linked to their selective vulnerability in aging and AD. 61

Non‐neuronal cells contribute an additional layer of constraint on BFCN development. In vitro and in vivo work has demonstrated that maternal embryonic microglia release specific inflammatory factors that promote cholinergic neuron differentiation in the developing BF. 62 A parallel study found that oligodendrocytes within the BF supply many of the key trophic factors necessary for proper maturation of BFCNs. 63 Although oligodendrocytes are typically associated with performing axonal myelination, 64 for developing BFCNs they may prioritize providing trophic support to facilitate axonal growth. 65 , 66 Together, these findings highlight the role of non‐neuronal cells in shaping the developmental trajectory of BFCNs. We speculate that disruptions to these support functions, whether due to genetics, developmental insults, or systemic inflammation, add further pressures on the long‐term resilience of BFCNs (Figure 2C).

Taken together, Stage 1 emphasizes that BFCN identity is established early in the brain by a narrow developmental program, which depends on specific trophic and glial interactions, and long‐range axonal commitments to distributed cortical targets. These features give rise to the basic architecture of the adult BFCN system but leave little redundancy later in life. Consequently, we hypothesize that later‐life metabolic, proteostatic, and inflammatory stressors can “unmask” built‐in liabilities shaped by Stage 0, Stage 1, and their interaction.

Stage 2: Midlife stressors unmask intrinsic vulnerabilities

In Stage 2 (Figure 1), we explore how stressors in the aging brain unmask the unique liabilities of BFCNs as well as trigger mechanisms of compensation. In the case that compensation is outweighed by stressors and pathological pressures, we discuss the ultimate dysfunction of BFCNs, which sets up the vulnerable trajectory. Although BFCNs are the focus of this review, they lie within a broader gradient of vulnerability throughout the brain (Figure 3A), with other cell types, such layer II entorhinal cortical neurons and the noradrenergic neurons of the locus coeruleus, also exhibiting early vulnerability to AD. 67 , 68

By midlife, the structural and functional liabilities of BFCNs begin to be tested, which we define as the onset of Stage 2; a universal and prolonged, yet understudied, phase of BFCN vulnerability. Beginning around ∼44 years old, 71 we hypothesize that Stage 2 marks a branch point at which distinct trajectories of BFCN aging emerge based on how the brain and body respond to midlife stressors (Figure 1). In this way, our proposed model follows the general logic of cellular aging. 72 For example, initiating pressures unfold across Stages 0–2. Adaptive antagonistic responses to these pressures appear in Stage 2R, counteracting rising strain on BFCNs. Individuals who maintain Stage 2R are characterized as resilient, while others who only transiently pass through Stage 2R, exhausting compensatory mechanisms, progress to Stage 2 V, as well as those who bypass the resilient phase entirely. Finally, progression of Stage 2 V leads to the emergence of neurodegenerative disease phenotypes, or Stage 3 (Figure 1).

Below, we emphasize five rate‐limiting intrinsic constraints which shape BFCN vulnerability in the aging brain: (1) calcium regulation and bioenergetic needs, (2) long‐range trophic signaling through retrograde transport, (3) membrane supply/turnover required for axonal arbor maintenance, (4) the accumulation of intra‐neuronal Aβ, and (5) emergence of tau pre‐tangles. Although intrinsic pressures within the brain are the focus of this review, such intrinsic constraints likely interact with the culminating effects of external pressures individuals may encounter in early‐ and midlife to predict individual trajectories of BFCN vulnerability. Recent work in preclinical models has begun to explore the mechanistic basis of these pressures. For example, cortical brain injury has been shown to induce retrograde axonal degeneration of BFCNs, eventually leading to BFCN loss. 73 Similarly, recent work has demonstrated that sleep disordered breathing conditions can promote BFCN loss in mouse models. 74 These extrinsic pressures, as well as others including hypoxia, 75 obesity, 76 and chronic inflammation, 77 likely interact with internal pressures on BFCNs, promoting differential vulnerability across individuals depending on their relative risk profile. Nevertheless, how these intrinsic and extrinsic pressures interact to potentiate cell‐type specific vulnerability in midlife is relatively understudied.

Calcium regulation and energy demands

BFCNs exhibit age‐related reductions in the expression of calcium‐binding proteins (CBP), such as calbindin‐D28K. CBPs bind calcium with high affinity to maintain intracellular calcium levels, thereby protecting neurons from calcium‐induced excitotoxicity and degeneration. 78 Previous research has shown that specific subpopulations of BFCNs, including those in the nbM, exhibit marked reductions in CBP levels in normal human aging, 20 with an even greater loss observed in AD. 79 , 80 Notably, these studies demonstrated that the BFCN subpopulation lacking CBPs is the most devastated in AD patients. In contrast, BFCNs that sustain normal CBP expression levels remain protected from late‐stage pathogenic tau species. 81 Therefore, expression of CBPs may serve as a marker of cellular resistance that, if lost, render BFCNs more susceptible to cellular damage and death (Figure 3C).

Studies in mice, rats, non‐human primates, and humans have revealed that BFCNs have exceptionally large cell bodies, approximately 30 µm in diameter. 32 , 82 , 83 , 84 Parallel research indicates that large neuronal somas have extraordinarily high energy demands, 85 , 86 requiring sustained activation of energy producing processes, such as mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. This heightened metabolic activity has been shown to increase the production of reactive oxygen species, which can drive cellular damage. 87 Consequently, BFCNs, particularly within the nbM, may be susceptible to aging‐related dysfunctions in midlife due to their extreme energy requirements and thus increased exposure to oxidative stress 88 (Figure 3B).

In addition to their large cell bodies, BFCNs also possess long axons with complex arborizations that remain highly plastic in the adult brain. 49 , 65 , 89 Estimates using human post mortem choline acetyltransferase (ChAT) immunostaining data 83 suggest that a single BF cholinergic axon can extend up to 100 meters and have close to 1,000 branch points. 32 Similarly, single‐neuron tracing studies indicate that cholinergic axons from neurons in the mouse MS/DBB can reach up to 60 cm in length. 32 , 33 Unlike shorter, heavily myelinated axons that conserve energy and exhibit resistance to AD‐related pathologies, 90 , 91 the lengthy, sparsely myelinated projections of BFCNs likely require substantial resources for continuous anterograde and retrograde transport, as well as repair and remodeling. Like other large projection neurons, 92 a persistent demand for metabolic support or upregulation of specific molecular pathways may render BFCN axons more vulnerable to cellular stress and pathological insults in midlife, 75 , 93 potentially giving rise to the cholinergic axonal abnormalities observed in aging and early AD 19 , 94 , 95 , 96 (Figure 3B).

Trophic support and signaling

The large axonal fields of BFCNs emerge as a major liability in the aging brain. To maintain their survival and cholinergic phenotype, BFCNs rely on neurotrophins, 97 , 98 , 99 , 100 , 101 which are produced in their cortical targets and undergo retrograde transport from distal axon terminals towards the soma. 101 , 102 , 103 Thus, age‐related changes in neurotrophin metabolism, axonal transport, or both, would pose a major challenge for BFCN survival in the aging brain.

Neurotrophins, such as nerve growth factor (NGF) and brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), are proteins that regulate growth, survival, apoptosis, synaptic function, and plasticity. 100 , 101 In BFCNs, neurotrophin signaling facilitates ACh synthesis and release, and promotes the expression of cholinergic enzymes and transporters. 97 , 98 , 99 Neurotrophins are synthesized in an immature (pro‐) form, and are proteolytically cleaved to maturation. 104 Through binding to their cell surface receptors, both immature and mature neurotrophins activate a range of intracellular signal transduction pathways. 101 , 104 Generally, receptors in the tropomyosin receptor kinase (Trk) family stimulate downstream signaling pathways promoting cell survival, development, and differentiation while inhibiting apoptosis. 105 , 106 , 107 The trophic functions of Trk may be further facilitated by interactions with the p75 neurotrophin receptor (p75NTR), which increases the binding affinity of TrkA for NGF. 108 , 109 Possibly reflecting the critical importance of efficient neurotrophin signaling for BFCN survival and function, p75NTR is expressed highly and relatively selectively on BFCNs in the healthy adult brain (Figure 3D).

The high expression of p75NTR on BFCNs appears to be one of the core mechanisms promoting their selective vulnerability to aging and AD. 75 , 93 In the absence of TrkA, p75NTR binds with higher affinity to proneurotrophins and has been widely implicated in axonal degeneration, dendritic spine collapse, and apoptosis, 110 , 111 , 112 , 113 , 114 particularly through interactions with co‐receptors of the sortilin family. 115 , 116 , 117 Of note, expression of receptors such as TrkA decreases with age, 18 , 118 while expression of sortilin and p75NTR increases 118 or remains unchanged. 18 This age‐related change in receptor expression may trigger a shift from trophic to degenerative signaling pathways mediated by p75NTR in BFCNs (Figure 3C). Such age‐related increases in degenerative p75NTR signaling have been linked to long‐term potentiation and plasticity deficits, mediated through underlying changes in the axonal/synaptic proteins RhoA and cofilin. 112 These plasticity deficits are not seen in p75NTR genetic knockout mice, 119 which also exhibit increases in BFCN cell counts and cortical cholinergic innervation. 120 Thus, the high basal expression of p75NTR on BFCNs, coupled with age‐related shifts in p75NTR function, are likely a crucial component of BFCN vulnerability.

In addition to changes in TrkA and p75NTR expression in the aged brain, the axonal transport mechanisms that allow neurotrophins to exert their effects are also altered, ultimately exaggerating this loss of trophic support. Retrograde transport of proNGF via TrkA, which would normally activate trophic responses, declines in aged BFCNs. 121 Concurrently, retrograde transport of proNGF via p75NTR increases, inducing BFCN axonal fragmentation, further suppressing trophic signaling, and triggering apoptotic signaling through JNK activation. 121 These age‐related deficits in BFCN axonal transport are a selective phenomenon, as the ability of cortical neurons to perform retrograde transport of neurotrophins is unchanged, 18 highlighting a unique pressure on BFCNs.

Lipid supply, membrane turnover, and apolipoprotein E ε4

BFCNs, like other large projection neurons with expansive axonal arborizations (Section 2.3.1), require large amounts of lipids to maintain membrane integrity and their complex axonal architecture. 122 , 123 , 124 Phospholipids, such as phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine, along with cholesterol, are the major constituents of cell membranes, 125 , 126 , 127 jointly supporting the structural integrity and function of neurons (Figure 3C). Lipids regulate synaptic maturation and plasticity, 128 , 129 modulating both long‐term potentiation and long‐term depression. 130 , 131 Moreover, neuronal phosphatidylcholine synthesis is essential for axonal outgrowth and branching 132 and is particularly critical in maintaining axonal integrity with age. 133 Age‐related changes in lipid metabolism 134 , 135 , 136 may therefore place particular strain on BFCNs, which require substantial lipid resources to maintain their large cell membranes and to sustain the plasticity necessary for cognitive function (discussed in Sections 2.1 and 2.3.1).

In midlife, changes in lipid synthesis may interact with dysfunctional lipid transport, particularly in individuals who carry the ε4 allele of the apolipoprotein E (APOE) gene. The APOE gene exists as three isoforms: ε2, ε3, and ε4, where the APOE ε4 isoform is the largest genetic risk factor for sporadic late‐onset AD. 137 , 138 In the brain, APOE serves as the major lipid transporter, primarily facilitating the movement of cholesterol between cells. 139 Under physiological conditions, APOE is expressed most abundantly in astrocytes, but has also been found to be upregulated in oligodendrocytes, microglia, vascular cells, and neurons under conditions of cellular stress. 140 APOE‐mediated cholesterol transport between astrocytes and neurons is essential for maintaining key neuronal processes, including membrane synthesis and repair, as well as synaptic formation, growth, and function. 141 , 142 APOE also aids in clearing cellular debris, thereby preventing unnecessary neuroinflammatory responses and supporting neuronal health. 141 Relative to APOE ε3, APOE ε4 induces neurotoxic lipid droplet formation within cells, 143 , 144 accelerates Aβ and tau pathogenesis, 145 , 146 and increases tau‐related neuronal loss and neuroinflammation. 146 Moreover, there is an interactive effect of APOE ε4 and polymorphisms in BDNF which produces increased atrophy, cognitive impairment, and AD risk, 147 , 148 indicating that APOE ε4 may also exacerbate deficits in neurotrophin signaling.

Prior to the onset of AD neuropathology and clinically detectable cognitive decline, APOE ε4 induces molecular alterations within BFCNs. In post mortem human brain tissue, APOE ε4 carriers show signs of decreased metabolism in the nbM even at Braak stage 0, 149 as well as decreased temporal cortical ChAT activity prior to the onset of dementia. 150 Neuroimaging studies of cognitively unimpaired older adults similarly reveal microstructural changes occurring within the nbM resulting from the APOE ε4 genotype. 151 Furthermore, in cholinergic neuron‐like cell cultures, APOE ε4 was found to reduce cell viability and elevate levels of phosphorylated tau. 152 APOE ε4 has also been shown to alter cholinergic synaptic machinery, producing increased mRNA levels of the vesicular acetylcholine transporter (VAChT) and reduced levels of acetylcholinesterase (AChE), as well as increased extracellular ACh. Importantly, BFCNs isolated from mice endogenously express APOE, 152 indicating that the observed in vitro dysfunction may reflect biological processes occurring within BFCNs in vivo. Together, this work highlights early lipid‐related perturbations in cholinergic metabolism that may arise in human APOE ε4 carriers before the onset of disease.

Aβ: From intra‐BFCN accumulation to extracellular diffusion

Although extracellular Aβ oligomers and plaques have been a major focus of research, intracellular Aβ accumulation may be one of the earliest pathological events in AD (Figure 4). Intracellular Aβ appears within presynaptic terminals and dendrites of vulnerable neurons in both normal aging and the AD trajectory. 13 , 153 , 154 , 155 , 156 As it accumulates, intracellular Aβ initiates and exacerbates tau pathology, 157 , 158 damages synapses, 155 , 157 , 159 and perturbs growth factor signaling cascades 160 which normally activate transcription factors to support cell survival and differentiation. 161 In cholinergic fibers, intraneuronal Aβ can impair endosomal trafficking and axonal transport, 93 , 154 which may further exacerbate age‐related declines in trophic signaling and retrograde transport, 18 , 121 placing continual strain on BFCNs. Although the exact mechanism underlying intraneuronal Aβ accumulation remains unclear, deficient autophagy stemming from cellular senescence may contribute within vulnerable cell types. 162

Studies in non‐human primates 164 and humans 13 have demonstrated the intraneuronal presence of early Aβ species within BFCNs at relatively young ages and in non‐diseased states. This appears to be a cell‐type specific phenomenon, as BFCNs show significantly higher intracellular Aβ load than morphologically similar neurons and other vulnerable neuronal populations. 13 The early and selective accumulation of Aβ in BFCNs may thus have several major consequences which could contribute to their vulnerability. First, if intraneuronal Aβ is indeed toxic, then BFCNs may experience prolonged exposure to this toxicity compared to other cell types. Thus, once the cognitive sequelae of AD appears, BFCNs will have already progressed further in the disease trajectory relative to other cells. This may explain why the BF is one of the first brain regions where degeneration is detectable in pre‐symptomatic and prodromal AD. 25 , 26 Second, if neurons exhibiting intraneuronal Aβ initiate the deposition of extracellular Aβ (for further review, see Refs. 165, 166, 167), brain regions targeted by BFCNs are likely to exhibit Aβ pathology as well. Indeed, studies have noted correlations between loss of BFCNs and Aβ plaques in regions innervated by BF nuclei. 168 , 169 Further, Aβ released from BFCNs could cause dysfunction in cholinoreceptive targets, which may exacerbate their degeneration given their reliance on growth factors from these regions (discussed in Section 2.3.2; Figure 3C).

Interactions between Aβ and p75NTR also influence BFCN vulnerability. Increasing evidence indicates that p75NTR may heighten the sensitivity of BFCNs to Aβ‐induced dysfunction and degeneration. 170 , 171 , 172 , 173 , 174 For example, previous work has shown that Aβ can bind to the extracellular domain of p75NTR, ultimately promoting loss of neurite integrity and death of BFCNs. 170 , 173 , 175 Importantly, this does not occur in the absence of p75NTR. 170 , 173 Moreover, Aβ pathology has also been suggested to increase the cell surface expression of p75NTR on BFCNs, 176 thereby creating a vicious cycle of degenerative signaling.

If p75NTR promotes degeneration, why might BFCNs express this receptor throughout their lifespan, when it becomes downregulated in most other neuronal populations after development? 177 Beyond its role in increasing TrkA's affinity for NGF, 108 p75NTR expression on BFCNs may also be crucial in facilitating the clearance of Aβ. Because binding of Aβ to the extracellular domain of p75NTR leads to its internalization by BFCNs, 178 BFCNs have been suggested to serve as a ‘drain’ for cortical Aβ, internalizing Aβ via p75NTR for degradation within the lysosomes. 178 , 179 , 180 Under physiological conditions, BFCNs may thus serve to keep cortical Aβ levels in check. However, endocytosis of Aβ can lead to Aβ oligomer formation within the endolysosomal system, inducing tau mis‐sorting and release of Aβ oligomers from the cell. 181 Aβ can also induce endosomal enlargement, disrupting trafficking of NGF signaling endosomes. 93 Thus, as cortical Aβ levels rise, lysosomal degradation processes within BFCNs could become overwhelmed, possibly leading to further BFCN dysfunction, increased release of Aβ into BFCN target regions, and the initiation of early stages of tau pathology within BFCNs.

Tau: Pre‐tangles appear in BFCNs

In addition to the detrimental effects of age on axonal transport, 121 , 182 , 183 protein transport in BFCNs is likely further compromised as tau pathology begins to arise. Under physiological conditions, tau functions as a stabilizer of microtubule (MT) assembly. 184 MTs are protein polymers and a core component of the axonal cytoskeleton. 185 The constant assembly (and disassembly) of MTs is required for maintaining neuronal structure and for facilitating key functions, including cell motility, intracellular axonal transport, and synaptic plasticity. Post‐translational phosphorylation of tau by specific kinases, as well as de‐phosphorylation by certain phosphatases, has been shown to modulate tau's ability to bind, stabilize, and promote MT assembly. 186 , 187 , 188 , 189 In AD, tau is phosphorylated up to four times more than in the non‐diseased brain. 190 The functional consequence of this hyperphosphorylated tau is a reduced capacity for binding and stabilizing MT assembly as well as an increased propensity to misfold and self‐aggregate into pre‐tangles, before forming neurofibrillary tangles. 191

Previous work has shown that BFCNs are affected by tau pre‐tangles as early as the third decade of life. 19 During the pre‐tangle stages of tau pathology, decreased expression of genes for Trk receptors and proteins involved in their downstream signaling has been detected, and these decreases become more severe as tau pathology progresses. 192 The combined effect of early tau pathology, which appears prior to the onset of BFCN degeneration, 15 and impaired growth factor metabolism, could place further stress on BFCNs in midlife. This could be further impacted by p75NTR signaling, which may exacerbate tau pathology within BFCNs by increasing tau hyperphosphorylation and promoting tau seeding. 193 , 194

Stage 2R: Mechanisms of resilience

The intrinsic vulnerabilities of BFCNs, when compounded with major AD susceptibility factors, including age, biological sex, and APOE genotype, 137 as well as other external pressures, 73 , 74 , 75 , 76 may interact to trigger a vulnerable trajectory of brain aging in some individuals. In others, however, we speculate that endogenous resilience mechanisms within BFCNs may compensate for the accumulating damage caused by these midlife stressors, resisting neurodegenerative processes and prolonging “normal” cognitive function (Figure 1). In the general context of AD, several potential genetic mechanisms of resilience have been proposed. Notable examples come from case studies of individuals carrying rare mutations in genes such as reelin (RELN) and APOE, 195 , 196 which confer resilience despite co‐occurring mutations encoding autosomal dominant genetic determinants of AD. In the broader population, similar but more subtle mechanisms are likely to contribute to resilience. 197

Converging evidence across human and animal studies points to a central role of the BFCN system in supporting resilience to pathophysiological processes, including to tau pathology. 198 , 199 , 200 This is especially evident in resilient older adults who display cognitive performance at levels consistent with what would be expected of individuals decades younger, that is, “super‐agers”. 198 , 200 Recent cross‐sectional neuroimaging work has shown that functional connectivity between the anterior BF nuclei (i.e., MS/DBB) and cortical and striatal regions is stronger in superagers than in “age‐typical” elderly individuals. 201 Moreover, anterior BF connectivity was found to be positively correlated with memory performance, 201 suggesting that this compensatory mechanism is functionally predictive. Although some reports indicate no significant differences in BF gray matter volume between superagers and age‐typical older adults, 201 others describe larger BF gray matter volumes and lower rates of longitudinal BF atrophy as well as less atrophy in memory‐related regions such as the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex. 202

Neurochemical evidence further supports the linkage between BFCN function and resilience in aging. The density of cortical AChE and its reaction products have been shown to decline in normal aging, with even more pronounced reductions reported in superagers relative to typical older adults. 198 Of note, greater decreases in AChE reaction‐product density were associated with better memory performance in elderly individuals, 198 suggesting that reduced AChE activity may serve as a compensatory adaptation to offset age‐related declines in ACh release. 203 , 204 Complementing this evidence, our group has recently shown that presynaptic components of the BFCN system may also show adaptive responses to AD pathology. Cross‐sectional positron emission tomography (PET) measures of VAChT protein levels, a marker of presynaptic cholinergic function, 205 , 206 were found to be locally upregulated in response to tau pathology and linked to longitudinal cognitive resilience in healthy older adults at increased risk for AD. 199 Together, these findings suggest that preserved or even enhanced BFCN structure and function are associated with cognitive resilience.

Developing a more nuanced understanding of the factors that determine the presence and efficacy of BFCN resilience mechanisms may be key to explaining individual variability in disease vulnerability and progression. 197 , 207 It is possible that all individuals activate similar BFCN resilience mechanisms in response to neurodegenerative processes, but that these mechanisms vary in their effectiveness across individuals. In some, such mechanisms may sufficiently counteract pathology and preserve function. In others, however, existing evidence suggests that these same processes may only transiently delay, or even accelerate, 208 , 209 disease progression. Alternatively, only a subset of individuals may possess or effectively recruit endogenous BFCN resilience functions. Although it remains difficult to define what biomarker signatures would constitute entry into Stage 2R, in vivo assays of BFCN synaptic function may serve as one promising option. Further longitudinal research into BFCN function in cognitively normal, middle‐aged adults will be critical to adjudicate these possibilities.

Stage 2V: A vulnerable aging trajectory emerges

In the following subsections, we outline the beginnings of a vulnerable trajectory of BFCN aging, Stage 2V. Under the staging framework (Figure 1), one of the primary features that distinguishes Stage 2V from Stage 2 (midlife stressors) is a loss of tau proteostatic containment. As discussed in Sections 2.3.4, Aβ is present both intraneuronally within BFCNs and begins to accumulate extracellularly throughout the cortex during Stage 2. Pre‐tangle stages of tau pathology may be present in vulnerable neuronal populations of the BF and locus coeruleus at this stage (discussed in Section 2.3.5), but minimal fibrillary species and minimal spread of tau is present. In Stage 2V, we hypothesize that the systems that have been suppressing the amplification of pathology saturate. Intracellular “seeds” persist long enough to amplify, redistribute within axons and terminals, and become prone to release. At this point, vulnerability is no longer confined to BFCNs. Instead, tau becomes propagation‐competent, enabling spread along connected targets (discussed in Section 2.5.1). During Stage 2V, biomarkers of tau‐PET imaging become abnormal and subtle sub‐clinical changes in specific domains of cognition likely occur. However, development of clinically detectable cognitive impairment does not occur until Stage 3 (Figure 4).

Cortical spreading of tau pathology

Several studies indicate that tau undergoes trans‐synaptic and trans‐neuronal spreading. 210 , 211 , 212 Given that BFCNs have widespread connections across the neocortex, hippocampus, and amygdala, 29 , 33 this may exacerbate tau propagation throughout the brain, initiating pathology at BFCN target sites and eventually promoting neurodegeneration. In support of this idea, nbM gray matter volumes are linked to increased tau PET uptake in the mesial temporal lobe. 213 Moreover, human longitudinal MRI studies indicate that antecedent BF gray matter volumes predict longitudinal gray matter degeneration 24 , 214 and dysfunction 215 of the entorhinal cortex, suggesting that BF damage precedes structural and functional decline of its target regions. Notably, opposite relationships were not significant (i.e., entorhinal cortical volumes did not predict longitudinal BF gray matter degeneration), supporting a model where subcortical degeneration later spreads to cortical regions. 24 , 214 The subcortical to cortical cascade proposed by these in vivo human neuroimaging models recapitulates the post mortem neuropathological staging models of AD. 6 , 216 , 217 Furthermore, lesion work in transgenic models of AD pathology have further shown that a selective loss of BFCN function exacerbates pathologic burden and neurodegeneration beyond that observed in AD models alone. 218 , 219 When taken together, these findings suggest that BFCN vulnerability functions as both an indicator of underlying pathology and as a contributor to its amplification, ultimately accelerating neurodegenerative processes throughout the brain.

Impaired choline metabolism

The heightened susceptibility of BFCNs to AD‐associated pathologies may further impose bottlenecks on the cellular processes necessary for maintaining their core functions. Choline is an essential nutrient which is involved in DNA methylation, ACh synthesis, and production of membrane lipids such as phosphatidylcholine. 220 In BFCNs, choline is primarily transported into the cell via the high affinity choline transporter (CHT), which supplies choline for ACh synthesis. 221 , 222 Additional choline transport is performed by the low affinity organic cation transporter, OCT2, which recycles choline to the presynaptic terminals following ACh hydrolysis in the synaptic cleft. 223 The third major choline transporter, choline transporter‐like protein 1 (CTL1), facilitates choline transport for phosphatidylcholine synthesis. 224 , 225 , 226 CTL1 is highly expressed in oligodendrocytes and various neuronal populations, including brainstem and spinal cord cholinergic neurons, but is notably absent in BFCNs. 227 , 228 Aside from these active transport proteins, choline can also be obtained from the catabolism of membrane‐bound phosphatidylcholine. 229

The selective vulnerability of BFCNs has been theorized to stem from a unique dependence on choline for both ACh and phosphatidylcholine synthesis. 209 , 230 Neuronal phosphatidylcholine biosynthesis facilitates neurite growth, branching, and plasticity, 132 , 231 , 232 which may be particularly important for developing and maintaining the extensive axonal projections of BFCNs. 32 , 33 , 60 An acute reduction in intracellular choline levels, either due to limited extracellular choline availability or impaired choline transport, could shift the metabolic prioritization of choline usage within cholinergic neurons. In this scenario, BFCNs may prioritize ACh synthesis over phosphatidylcholine production to sustain cholinergic neurotransmission. 123 , 209 Evidence from cultured striatal cholinergic neurons supports this idea. In fact, prolonged stimulation of cultured cholinergic neurons in a choline‐restricted environment reduces membrane phosphatidylcholine levels, 233 likely reflecting increased phosphatidylcholine catabolism to support prolonged ACh synthesis and release. If the exogenous supply of choline becomes chronically insufficient, increased phosphatidylcholine hydrolysis to compensate for low choline availability may ultimately deplete membrane‐bound phosphatidylcholine stores and compromise structural integrity. 233 This may contribute to progressive membrane loss, cell shrinkage, and ultimately, death of BFCNs in AD. 123

Of note, Aβ has been shown to impair cellular choline transport via CHT and CTL1, 234 , 235 and several studies have reported abnormal levels of choline and phosphatidylcholine in individuals diagnosed with a mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and AD. 236 , 237 , 238 , 239 , 240 Moreover, we recently demonstrated that individuals on the AD continuum (i.e., with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarker‐confirmed preclinical, prodromal, or clinical AD) exhibit altered serum levels of phosphatidylcholine relative to healthy controls. Notably, phosphatidylcholine levels in these individuals were found to be selectively predictive of longitudinal atrophy of the BF. 241 Although these findings are limited by the peripheral nature of the lipid measurements, a large‐scale study of human AD brain tissue also found significant associations between increased levels of phosphatidylcholine catabolites, measures of AD neuropathology, and ante mortem measures of cognition. 242 These findings allude to perturbed phosphatidylcholine metabolism as a key feature of AD. 243

Stage 3: Disease phenotypes

Stage 3 marks the emergence of neurodegenerative disease phenotypes through cellular crosstalk. As the effects of Aβ‐ and tau‐induced neurotoxicity, withdrawal of trophic support, and metabolic decline accumulate, these pressures likely combine with toxic neuron‐glia coupling to drive BFCN neurodegeneration.

Disease‐associated microglia can convert local proteinopathy into a multicellular degenerative program. Previous work in rodents and cell cultures has shown that disease‐associated microglia release a cytokine triad (interleukin‐1 α [IL‐1α], tumor necrosis factor [TNF], and C1q) that is sufficient to induce a neurotoxic astrocyte state. 244 Rather than sustaining core homeostatic functions that support synapses and neurons, neurotoxic astrocytic populations actively facilitate neuronal and oligodendrocyte death. 244 Astrocyte‐derived toxic factors have also been linked to saturated lipids packaged in APOE/APOJ‐containing lipoparticles. 245 When released by reactive astrocytes, these toxic lipid droplets induce synaptotoxicity and neuronal cell death. 245

We hypothesize that toxic neuron‐glia crosstalk uniquely affects BFCNs early in the progression of AD. BFCNs are defined by an extreme dependence on intact glial support for membrane supply and lipid trafficking across vast axonal fields (discussed in Sections 2.1–2.3). A shift toward reactive astrocyte programs, therefore, strikes a core constraint of BFCNs. This added stress continues to exacerbate midlife and early disease‐related pressures on BFCNs (Sections 2.3 and 2.5), compromising BFCN axonal integrity 96 and further decreasing retrograde transport efficiency and trophic support, as well as increasing proteostatic burden.

In addition to its well‐established role in cognition (reviewed in 44 , 60 , 246 ), there is an increasing appreciation for the role of BFCN signaling in supporting cellular homeostasis in the adult brain. Under physiological conditions, BFCNs have been demonstrated to regulate specific brain functions involved in mitigating protein clearance and inflammatory response mechanisms. In addition to receptor‐mediated clearance of Aβ (Section 2.3.4), 178 BFCNs actively regulate vascular tone and glymphatic dynamics across the cortex. 247 Cross‐species in vivo neuroimaging work has revealed that loss or dysfunction of BFCN signaling impairs glymphatic efflux and CSF‐BOLD coupling, 247 suggesting that BFCN degeneration may undermine the cholinergic control of blood flow and CSF clearance that would normally buffer proteostatic load. Furthermore, BFCNs may exert an anti‐inflammatory influence in the central nervous system, 248 , 249 similar to the well‐established cholinergic anti‐inflammatory pathway in the peripheral nervous system. 250 , 251 Thus, BFCN dysfunction and degeneration could lead to the loss of a key check on inflammatory processes in the brain. In line with this idea, previous work from our group revealed that BFCN degeneration in presymptomatic AD is linked to longitudinal increases in CSF levels of soluble TREM2, and increased plasma levels of the pro‐inflammatory complement factor C3. 25 Thus, by Stage 3, loss of BFCN signaling is no longer a mere consequence of proteopathies, membrane collapse, and trophic decline. Rather, it is an amplifier of pathology via glial crosstalk, facilitating the emergence of the clinical AD phenotype (Figure 4).

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

In this review, we have traced the origins of BFCN vulnerability from evolutionary and developmental pressures and discussed how their intrinsic features interact with lifelong functional demands to produce BFCN selective vulnerability (Figure 1). This culminates in divergent trajectories of cholinergic aging. Importantly, the proposed branch point between aging trajectories implies a prevention window. Moving forward, a practical proxy for measuring BFCN synaptic function that is scalable to midlife populations and has the ability to detect early divergence between resilient (Stage 2R) and vulnerable (Stage 2V) aging is required. Without such tools, we will continue diagnosing downstream syndromes while missing the upstream branch point.

The urgent need for scalable, in vivo biomarkers of BFCN function

Unlike Aβ and tau, which can be readily measured with several plasma, CSF, and PET biomarkers, 252 , 253 , 254 , 255 , 256 evaluating BFCN function is considerably more challenging. This is in part due to the fact that most available biomarkers cannot selectively measure changes in this subpopulation of neurons in the brain. As a result, clinically validated biomarkers that are sensitive and specific to central cholinergic vulnerability are currently limited.

In the last decade, PET imaging with the [18F]‐FEOBV radiotracer has helped to overcome the lack of in vivo, cell‐type specific biomarkers for BFCNs. [18F]‐FEOBV binds to VAChT, which is expressed in BFCNs and largely localized to the presynaptic terminals. 257 [18F]‐FEOBV PET imaging has been performed in rats, 258 , 259 , 260 , 261 mice, 49 , 199 healthy younger adults, 262 , 263 and AD populations. 264 However, this radiotracer is still relatively underutilized. Inclusion of this tracer in large‐scale consortia studies would greatly improve our understanding of why BFCN dysfunction arises and how it contributes to the clinical and pathological progression of AD.

In contrast to the relatively slow development of in vivo biomarkers for BFCNs, there has been a surge of interest in fluid biomarkers for Aβ and tau, with substantial progress being made in developing biomarker abnormality thresholds, evaluating sensitivity and specificity to AD, as well as subtyping patients based on their individual biomarker profiles. 252 , 255 , 265 , 266 , 267 , 268 To bring cholinergic biomarkers to the same standard as those for AD neuropathologies, candidate markers should be validated against [18F]‐FEOBV PET, enabling derivation of proxy measures of BF cholinergic presynaptic function that are suitable for clinical screening and therapeutic monitoring. Fluid biomarkers of the cholinergic system that could be benchmarked against [18F]‐FEOBV PET include CSF AChE 269 and ChAT, 270 which have both been previously assessed in the context of AD but have not gained widespread research use. However, given the complexity of approximating cell‐type specific changes in the brain via fluid biomarkers, it may be the case that a composite of multiple biomarkers is required to capture changes in [18F]‐FEOBV PET signal.

An interesting add‐on biomarker in this context could be p75NTR. The extracellular domain (ECD) of p75NTR has been validated as a urinary biomarker for amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, 271 , 272 and has also shown some promise in AD via measurement in the CSF, serum, and urine. 273 , 274 , 275 Although p75NTR becomes upregulated on neuronal and glial populations in injury and AD, 109 , 276 thus not representing a selective biomarker of BFCNs, p75NTR ECD may be useful in combination with cholinergic‐specific biomarkers to index aging‐ and disease‐associated changes in cholinergic neuronal systems.

As the field of cholinergic biomarkers continues to progress, their integration into established pathological frameworks of AD is essential. 163 , 277 A hypothesized version of BFCN vulnerability, integrated into the influential Aβ, tau, neurodegeneration (ATN) framework, 163 is shown in Figure 4.

CONCLUSION

Overall, we synthesize evidence across several fields of neuroscience to present a lifespan staging model of BFCN vulnerability, mapping mechanisms promoting BFCN vulnerability to each stage of their lifespan (Figure 1). We propose that innate morphofunctional BFCN vulnerabilities, established in both evolution and development, interact with midlife stressors to trigger a cascade of events that contribute to BFCN dysfunction and degeneration. The heightened vulnerability of BFCNs further aggravates the progression of Aβ, tau, and neuroinflammatory species, contributing to the spread of AD pathophysiological signatures to functionally and anatomically connected brain regions. Finally, we articulate an urgent and unmet need for in vivo cholinergic biomarkers to deepen our knowledge of the timing and progression of BFCN dysfunction. Understanding such events will be critical for identifying new avenues for AD therapeutic development.
