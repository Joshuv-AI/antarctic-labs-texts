Electroencephalographic source imaging of spikes with concurrent high‐frequency oscillations is concordant with the clinical ground truth
Authors: Colton B Gonsisko, Zhengxiang Cai, Xiyuan Jiang, Andrea M Duque Lopez, Gregory A Worrell, Bin He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveEpilepsy raises critical challenges to accurately localize the epileptogenic zone (EZ) to guide presurgical planning. Previous research has suggested that interictal spikes overlapping with high‐frequency oscillations, referred to here as pSpikes, serve as a reliable biomarker for EZ estimation, but there remains a question as to whether and to how pSpikes perform as compared to other types of epileptic spikes. This study aims to address this question by investigating the source imaging capabilities of pSpikes alongside other spike types.MethodsA total of 2819 interictal spikes from 76‐channel scalp electroencephalography (EEG) were analyzed in a cohort of 24 drug‐resistant focal epilepsy patients. All patients received surgical resection, and 16 were declared seizure‐free based on at least 1 year of postoperative follow‐up. A recently developed electrophysiological source imaging algorithm—fast spatiotemporal iteratively reweighted edge sparsity (FAST‐IRES)—was used for source imaging of the detected interictal spikes. The performance of 217 pSpikes was compared with 772 nSpikes (spikes with irregular high‐frequency activations), 1830 rSpikes (spikes with no high‐frequency activity), and all 2819 aSpikes (all interictal spikes).ResultsThe localization and extent estimation using pSpikes are concordant with the clinical ground truth; using pSpikes yields the best performance compared with nSpikes, rSpikes, and conventional spike imaging (aSpikes). For multiple spike type seizure‐free patients, the mean localization error for pSpike imaging was 6.8 mm, compared with 15.0 mm for aSpikes. The sensitivity, precision, and specificity were .41, .67, and .93 for pSpikes compared with .32, .48, and .93 for aSpikes.SignificanceThese results demonstrate the merits of noninvasive EEG source localization, and that (1) pSpike is a superior biomarker, outperforming conventional spike imaging for the localization of epileptic sources, and especially those with multiple irritative zones; and (2) FAST‐IRES provides accurate source estimation that is highly concordant with clinical ground truth, even in situations of single spike analysis with low signal‐to‐noise ratio.Keywords: electroencephalography, electrophysiological source imaging, high‐frequency oscillations, interictal spike, source localization

INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy is a significant neurological disorder affecting approximately 1% of the global population.1 Although it can present in different ways and to different degrees across individuals, it is commonly characterized by excessive neural discharges within the brain leading to auras or seizures. These seizures are debilitating and, due to their unpredictable nature, come at a cost to the patient's independence, freedom, and quality of life. 2 , 3 , 4

Pharmacological intervention is the most common first line of defense against epilepsy, but approximately one third of epilepsy patients cannot be managed by medication alone. 5 There are options for these patients to predict or reduce the frequency of seizures, such as responsive neurostimulation 6 , 7 and vagal nerve stimulation, 8 or to undergo resective surgery or laser ablation in an attempt to remove or deactivate the epileptic network. 9 A successful surgical resection is predicated on the accurate localization of the epileptogenic zone (EZ), defined as the minimum amount of cortex needed to be resected or disconnected for seizure freedom. 10 Using current clinical practice, the success rate for surgical resective treatment is approximately 80% for temporal lobe epilepsy,11 whereas that of extratemporal lobe epilepsies is only approximately 50% or even lower.‐ 12

During presurgical planning, intracranial electroencephalographic (iEEG) studies, which include electrocorticography (ECoG) and stereoelectroencephalography (SEEG), are considered to be the gold standard to determine the seizure onset zone, and this information has been widely used to estimate the EZ. 10 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 During iEEG implantation and monitoring, electrodes are placed at presumed epileptogenic brain regions to record epileptic activities. Nevertheless, iEEG remains an invasive method with restricted spatial coverage, and prolonged monitoring often entails discomfort and heightened risks related to the chronic placement of electrodes. 18 , 19

Alternatively, noninvasive electromagnetic recording techniques such as magnetoencephalography (MEG) and high‐density electroencephalography (EEG) measure the whole brain activity and are thus suitable tools to identify the EZ. 13 , 16 , 20 , 21 , 22 EEG/MEG records the electrical or magnetic field produced by brain activity, and one of the main advantages of EEG/MEG is their ability to record brain activities with high temporal resolution, 20 , 23 on the order of milliseconds. However, due to the volume conduction effect, the spatial resolution for EEG/MEG is limited, preventing them from identifying corresponding brain regions accurately. In an effort to overcome this limitation, electrophysiological source imaging (ESI) has been pursued to improve the spatial resolution of EEG/MEG. 16 , 20 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 Concretely, ESI seeks to localize and image brain sources responsible for scalp‐recorded electromagnetic signals such as electroencephalography. Through forward brain source–head volume conductor modeling and inverse source imaging, ESI quantitatively estimates brain tissues exhibiting neuronal activation. ESI has been utilized to localize the EZ from interictal spikes, 13 , 16 , 32 , 33 , 34 seizure recordings, 13 , 16 , 35 , 36 or high‐frequency oscillations (HFOs), 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 aiding presurgical planning by indicating in which regions to implant intracortical electrodes. 42

Efforts have been made to identify biomarkers that are highly associated with pathological epileptic tissue, suitable for EZ localization. Interictal spikes are a well‐used biomarker for epilepsy, but are known for a lack of specificity. 43 Although interictal spikes may originate from the EZ, some patients exhibit spurious spikes that are contralateral to the true EZ, limiting the specificity of interictal spikes. 44 Research has focused on finding features that discriminate between theorized “red spikes,” which are hypothesized to originate from the EZ and are potentially more relevant to ictogenesis mechanisms, and “green spikes,” which originate from the broader epileptic network but are not associated with the EZ. 45 This concept is loosely defined, with some preferring to consider “red spikes” to originate from zones that generate seizures whereas “green spikes” belong to zones that only generate interictal spikes, 46 thus placing red spikes in the symptomatogenic zone and green spikes in the irritative zone. Recent investigations have focused on identifying spike features that may better align with the EZ, such as HFOs. There has been mixed evidence regarding their performance, 47 with some recent papers highlighting an improvement by analyzing concordant spikes and HFOs 17 , 48 and others indicating that using only HFOs yields superior performance. 49

In our previous study, 41 we identified a group of interictal spikes consistently co‐occurring with HFOs, which we termed as pSpikes, representing an interictal biomarker that refers to HFO‐riding spikes. Although HFOs are reported to be a promising interictal biomarker for epileptogenesis, it is challenging to perform source imaging of HFOs due to the relatively low signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR) of the high‐frequency activity leading to difficulty in extracting and analyzing HFOs in noisy scalp recordings. In addition, separating pathological HFOs from physiological and artifactual ones is also a barrier limiting HFOs in clinical applications, 50 motivating a focus on imaging the spikes themselves. Although these pSpikes align with the “red spike” concept, a more systematic and comprehensive investigation is needed to determine whether and how pSpikes perform as compared to other types of spikes in source imaging. Successfully identifying subgroups of spikes that better align with the EZ can benefit both our understanding of epilepsy and the clinical identification of the EZ.

Here, we report the performance of source imaging from pSpikes as compared with other types of interictal spikes using a recently developed source imaging technique, the fast spatiotemporal iteratively reweighted edge sparsity (FAST‐IRES) algorithm, 16 which is capable of imaging the location and extent of sources objectively. Judging by the clinical ground truth of the EZ based on successful surgical resection, we test the hypothesis that pSpikes represent a suitable and refined epileptogenic biomarker that can improve the EZ localization from general interictal spikes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient information

We studied a cohort of 24 drug‐resistant epilepsy patients from Mayo Clinic, including 16 patients with seizure‐free outcome postsurgery (International League Against Epilepsy [ILAE] I–II) and eight patients with non‐seizure‐free outcome (ILAE III–IV). All surgical outcomes were evaluated using the ILAE classification 51 with at least 1‐year follow‐up duration. Each patient received high‐density (76‐channel) EEG monitoring (Xltek, Natus Medical Incorporated) as part of their presurgical evaluation. Each electrode was affixed to the scalp using a 10–10 montage, with a reference electrode placed at CPz. EEG signals were recorded at a sampling rate of 500 Hz and high‐pass filtered at >1 Hz to eliminate spurious slow activities and direct current shifts. Intracranial EEG information was available in eight patients for presurgical investigations and planning. Preoperative and postoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans were collected for all patients and coregistered for reconstruction of the resected volume, which served as the clinical evidence of the EZ localization. Thus, patient inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) refractory epilepsy patients who underwent complete presurgical workup, including high‐density video‐EEG and pre‐ and post‐operative MRI; (2) patients eligible for resective surgery; and (3) patients who underwent surgical resection and had at least 1‐year follow‐up with postsurgical evaluation available. Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects, and this study was approved by the institutional review boards of Mayo Clinic and Carnegie Mellon University.

Data preprocessing

For each patient, an individualized cortex boundary element method model was constructed based on the preoperative MRI with Curry software (Compumedics), and the sensors were coregistered using anatomical landmarks: nasion, left preauricular point, and right preauricular point. The leadfield matrix was then calculated in Curry. All head models and leadfield matrices were then exported to MATLAB (MathWorks, version 2022a) for further analysis such as source imaging and performance calculation.

Time regions containing interictal spike samples were initially identified and annotated by epileptologists at Mayo Clinic. We used commercially available automated spike detection software, Persyst 13, 52 to perform an inclusive and comprehensive detection throughout the recordings. Each detected spike was then manually reviewed and categorized based on descriptive samples provided by clinicians to maximize the number of identified spikes. 41 The extracted spikes were classified by their duration, morphology, and scalp topology into clusters, namely spike types. Accordingly, the patients with more than one type of spike were categorized as multiple spike type (MST) patients, and other patients as single spike type (SST). To identify candidate pSpikes, we first pooled all available spikes in each patient regardless of its spike type, and epoched EEG data into 1‐s segments centered around a spike, with −500 to +500 ms surrounding the predominant peak. The high‐frequency (80–240 Hz) portion of the data was filtered out and screened for candidate HFOs. Spike epochs were analyzed for segments where the signal exceeded twice the median standard deviation computed over 100‐ms sliding windows. These candidate HFOs were then analyzed for other features, such as repeated oscillations, median frequency, and spectrogram patterns. More details regarding the spike sorting can be found in Cai et al. 41 Spikes marked positive for HFOs near the rising phase of the interictal spike are categorized as pSpikes. Interictal spikes that were candidates for HFOs but did not consistently contain all required features are categorized as nSpikes. All other spikes that had no high‐frequency activity are categorized as rSpikes. Finally, individual spikes were input into FAST‐IRES to generate an estimate of the cortical source activity. Source imaging

In this study, we used the FAST‐IRES algorithm 16 to perform source imaging. This algorithm can estimate the location and extent of brain sources. Combining these terms with the data‐fitting constraint promotes solutions that are focally extended. Employing a data‐driven iterative approach penalizes regions with lower amplitudes and facilitates rapid convergence toward an extended source in an objective manner, eliminating the need for subjective thresholding. It has been demonstrated that this algorithm is one of the few ESI methods that can noninvasively estimate the extent of cortical generators in both EEG and MEG, suggesting its potential use for epilepsy presurgical planning. 16 , 33

Here, we used independent component analysis as a blind source separation method to select suitable time‐basis functions to input into the solver. The objective function of FAST‐IRES is set to minimize the source and edge sparsity while best explaining the recorded EEG data. Unlike some other ESI methods, it can objectively estimate the source extent without the need for thresholding.

To estimate the noise covariance matrix, we used the standard deviation of the first ~200 ms of the spike signal. This time range typically contains background noise, as the spike itself will occur near the 500‐ms mark. As each individual spike had a slightly different temporal waveform, we used a dynamic approach to decide which time samples to input into the FAST‐IRES solver. As it is difficult to automatically compute the start and stop times of an EEG spike, we instead computed the full width at half maximum of the spike. To guarantee the entire spike was covered, we doubled this width and used this as the input time series for the solver.

Statistics

In total, there were 217 pSpikes, 772 nSpikes, and 1830 rSpikes available across all 24 patients, and all were analyzed for this study. Here, we imaged individual interictal spikes to (1) determine whether individual pSpikes can outperform other spike types and (2) investigate the performance of FAST‐IRES with limited data. Interictal spikes are often averaged together to improve the SNR, but doing so across all spikes would unfairly benefit rSpikes and nSpikes due to their greater number. Some patients may only have five or six pSpikes but dozens of rSpikes, causing the difference in SNR. Because previous source imaging results were based on averaging spike numbers with controlled SNR, 41 analyzing individual spikes may provide unique information about the benefits of pSpike imaging.

As the video‐EEG monitoring was collected over a multiple‐day period, the spikes contained a mixture of sleep state and awake state recordings. There is evidence 55 , 56 , 57 that HFO rates change across sleep states. The dataset used in this study does not have labels for patient sleep status, so we separately labeled spikes based on whether they occurred in daytime (approximately 6 a.m.–10 p.m.) or nighttime (10 p.m.–6 a.m.). We observed that only 14.6% of pSpikes occurred during daytime, with the rest during night. In contrast, 29.1% of nSpikes and 33.5% of rSpikes occurred during daytime. Figure S1 contains a more detailed comparison of the metrics for these groups of spikes.

Randomness is introduced for a given cohort of patients because different patients have resections that vary in size, and source imaging may perform better in some patients due to the location of the EZ, cortex model, or even slight differences in EEG setup (e.g., channel impedances). Also, the approach of utilizing individual interictal spikes introduces additional randomness as patients will have different numbers of spikes. To utilize all available data while attempting to control for this randomness, we utilized a mixed linear model with the lme4 package 58 implemented in R,59 where we fit the patient ID as a random factor, the spike type (p/n/r/a) as a fixed factor, and the evaluation metrics as the responses. In this way, we can assess whether pSpikes have significantly different performance when we control for interpatient variability.

RESULTS

pSpikes achieved better performance than other spike types

We performed source imaging on the identified interictal spikes individually, for each patient who achieved seizure freedom after the neurosurgery (n = 16). The spike imaging results were divided according to the spike categories, p/n/r/aSpikes, and a significant effect was identified across the spike groups on the evaluation metrics with the mixed linear model (Figure 2; p < .001). Post hoc analysis showed that pSpikes have significantly lower localization error (8.8 ± 10.9 mm, mean ± standard deviation) compared with nSpikes (15.8 ± 19.8 mm, p < .001), rSpikes (15.8 ± 18.1 mm, p < .001), and aSpikes (15.3 ± 18.2 mm, p < .001, two‐sided t‐test, false discovery rate [FDR] corrected). Similarly, pSpikes had significantly lower SD (9.9 ± 14.2 mm) than nSpikes (16.1 ± 21.4 mm, p < .001), rSpikes (15.6 ± 18.4 mm, p < .001), and aSpikes (15.3 ± 19.0 mm, p < .001). For sensitivity, pSpikes (.46 ± .31) significantly outperformed nSpikes (.34 ± .33, p < .001), rSpikes (.32 ± .31, p < .001), and aSpikes (.34 ± .31, p < .001). pSpikes also had significantly higher precision (.56 ± .39) than nSpikes (.44 ± .42, p < .001), rSpikes (.40 ± .41, p < .001), and aSpikes (.42 ± .41, p < .001). There were no significant differences for specificity, as all spike types performed similarly well (pSpikes: .90 ± .12; nSpikes: .92 ± .11; rSpikes: .93 ± .09; aSpikes: .92 ± .10).

Merit of pSpike imaging mainly exists in patients with multiple types of spikes

We sought to further investigate the spike imaging performance in a subclass of patients with various spike types. Some patients exhibit multiple types of spikes from a given class based on the temporal waveforms or topographic features. For example, among all rSpikes for a particular patient, there is a spike type on the left temporal lobe, whereas another spike deviates toward the frontal side, potentially demonstrating multiple irritative zones. Of the 16 seizure‐free patients, nine exhibited these multiple spike types, whereas seven patients exhibited a single spike type for all spike classes. When we separately fit the model using data for these groups of patients, we found that overall, most significant improvements by pSpikes are tied to the MST patient group, as shown in Figure 3. For SST patients, pSpikes only had a significant improvement for sensitivity as compared with rSpikes and aSpikes (p < .01), but not nSpikes. For MST patients, the localization error was lowest for pSpikes compared with nSpikes (p < .01) as well as rSpikes and aSpikes (p < .001). The SD and precision (SD not shown) also showed pSpikes performing best (p < .001, all pairwise comparisons with FDR correction). pSpikes also had the highest sensitivity as compared with nSpikes (p < .01), rSpikes (p < .001), and aSpikes (p < .01). There were no significant differences for specificity as with the overall group results.

Localization error and SD are significantly higher for non‐seizure‐free patients

We analyzed eight patients who did not achieve a seizure‐free outcome postresection (ILAE III–V). We wanted to investigate whether the pSpike localization performance was significantly different on the basis of seizure freedom. We hypothesized that if pSpikes are a better biomarker, then imaging pSpikes should find some region outside of the resected zone for patients whose resection did not cover the full EZ. We compared 16 seizure‐free patients with eight non‐seizure‐free patients, and found imaging pSpikes revealed a significantly higher localization error for the non‐seizure‐free patients (8.8 ± 10.9 mm for seizure‐free patients vs. 23.7 ± 22.8 mm for non‐seizure‐free patients, p < .01), and imaging aSpikes also yielded a significant difference in localization error (15.3 ± 18.2 mm for seizure‐free patients vs. 34.3 ± 25.3 mm for non‐seizure‐free patients, p < .05). We similarly observed that pSpike imaging revealed significantly higher SD for non‐seizure‐free patients (9.9 ± 14.2 mm for seizure‐free vs. 24.3 ± 23.0 mm for non‐seizure‐free patients, p < .05). However, imaging aSpikes did not reveal a significant difference in SD across seizure freedom status (15.3 ± 19.0 mm for seizure‐free vs. 32.9 ± 25.4 mm for non‐seizure‐free, p = .10). DISCUSSION

In this work, we have demonstrated the superior performance of source imaging of pSpikes as compared with n/r/aSpikes in terms of both localization and extent estimation and have shown that the benefit of pSpike imaging mostly exists in a subpopulation of patients with multiple spike types. These results reinforce the relevance of interictal spikes and co‐occurring HFOs as a potential epileptic biomarker, superior to other spike variations or regular irritative zones. As the average localization error of pSpikes was approximately 8.8 mm for seizure‐free patients, this study demonstrates the merit of source imaging during the presurgical planning of resections. In addition, as these results were achieved using interictal noninvasive EEG recordings, it is reasonable that ESI could also be helpful in the early stages of presurgical planning, such as guiding where to implant SEEG or ECoG electrodes.

pSpikes outperform the general spike population

The significant improvement in source localization of pSpikes over aSpikes is noteworthy, as this directly demonstrates the merit of discriminating pSpikes compared to analyzing general spike populations. The mean localization error and SD improve from 15.3 to 8.8 mm and 15.3 to 9.9 mm comparing pSpikes to aSpikes (p < .001), whereas the mean sensitivity and precision increase from 34% to 46% (p < .05) and 42% to 56% (p < .001), respectively.

pSpikes as a more specific biomarker

Separating out the MST and SST patients reveals that the main improvement comes from the MST patient population. These patients are generally more difficult for conventional spike imaging, as it is more difficult to average spikes from different types together. In addition, some patients exhibited contralateral localization of some spike types even though the true EZ is localized to one hemisphere. Studying aSpikes in a prospective study would be very difficult for these patients, as the conflicting evidence makes it highly challenging to determine which hemisphere is the true origin of the EZ based on ESI alone. n/rSpikes are more likely to have these erroneous spike types that spoil their imaging performance. In other words, our results suggest that pSpikes are a more specific biomarker for epilepsy lateralization and localization, which is consistent with prior findings. Of all the spike types studied, pSpikes most resemble the “red spike” concept, whereas nSpikes and rSpikes both have features of “green spikes.” More research is needed to uncover whether these pSpikes are truly related to more pathological mechanisms of epilepsy.

Imaging individual spikes versus averaged spikes

It is also worthwhile to reiterate that this study only sought to image individual interictal spikes rather than averaging multiple spikes together. Although results in our prior work were mostly based on data after concatenation and averaging, 16 , 33 the blind source separation step in the FAST‐IRES preprocessing pipeline can in theory handle both concatenated and individual signals. The results we obtained with individual spike data in this work demonstrate the capability of FAST‐IRES source imaging with limited data and in situations where SNR is low. On average, the SNR of these individual spikes was only approximately 3–5 dB, whereas averaging spikes together can yield an SNR > 10 dB. These findings highlight the merit of FAST‐IRES imaging in a clinical setting, as collecting and imaging one individual pSpike can localize the resected region well. Thus, this is a promising approach toward shortening the overall in‐patient monitoring duration and lowering medical costs.

It is important to note that pSpike imaging may not provide a benefit for all patients over the conventional clinical workup. pSpikes seem better suited to provide useful input for cases with more uncertainty for the location of the EZ. For example, the aSpike source localization for MST patients may give contralateral results. The pSpike source localization is typically more precise in identifying the true EZ, so recording and analyzing this biomarker may better refine the iEEG electrode implantation and EZ location for resection. If all interictal spikes for a given patient happen to point toward the same region, then pSpike imaging may not provide any additional benefit.

Limitations

There are several limitations to the study design worth discussing. One main limitation is the inability to detect HFOs in the fast ripple band of >250 Hz due to the EEG sampling rate. Consequently, such HFOs might be missed, or certain spikes may be inaccurately labeled as nSpikes or rSpikes even if they contain undetectable HFOs. Generally, we observe that the HFO dominant frequency band tends to fall between 80 and 150 Hz, with few HFOs of >200 Hz. This suggests that the likelihood of missing HFOs is low, but the possibility still exists.

In addition, the relatively low abundance of pSpikes across the cohort of patients may also limit their clinical utility, because it may require longer recordings to find one or several pSpikes as compared with conventional spike analysis. For example, we observed that the average pSpike rate is approximately 5.9 ± 13.2 spikes/h across all patients, compared with 14.2 ± 11.0 spikes/h for nSpikes and 33.8 ± 34.0 spikes/h for rSpikes. This suggests an expected yield of approximately 141 pSpikes per day of clinical recording, compared with 811 rSpikes per day. Even with these limitations, the high performance of the extent metrics indicates that individual pSpike imaging with FAST‐IRES can be a supplemental tool to aid in presurgical planning for focal epilepsy patients.

CONCLUSIONS

In this study, we show that pSpike imaging with FAST‐IRES is concordant with the resected EZ in patients who became seizure‐free. Imaging individual pSpikes outperformed nSpikes and rSpikes, demonstrating the merit of selectively imaging spikes overlapping with HFOs. The improvement of pSpikes was highly tied to patients who exhibited MSTs, which are typically more difficult to image. For these MST patients, the mean localization error for pSpike imaging was 6.8 mm, with an SD of 5.1 mm, sensitivity of .41, precision of .67, and specificity of .93. In contrast, the mean localization error for conventional aSpike imaging was 15.0 mm, with an SD of 14.0 mm, sensitivity of .32, precision of .48, and specificity of .93. These results reinforce pSpikes as a valid biomarker for epilepsy treatment and research.
