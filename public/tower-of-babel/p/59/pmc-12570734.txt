Risk factor analysis and development of nomogram prediction model for central venous catheter-related thrombosis in burn patients: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Qimeng Li, Yangping Wang, Xiaorong Zhang, Jingyuan Lang, Xue Heng, Ning Li, Siwei Miao, Chuan Hu, Gaoxing Luo, Haisheng Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCentral venous catheter (CVC)-related thrombosis (CRT) has become a common iatrogenic complication in burn patients. Early and precise prediction is the foundations of effective prevention of CRT. However, there is not specific tool to predict CRT in burn populations. This study aimed to investigate the risk factors of CRT and develop a prediction model for CRT in burn populations.MethodsThis retrospective observational study was conducted at a large burn center in Southwest China from January 2018 to December 2022. All adult patients with burn injuries undergoing central venous catheterization were included in the cohort. The clinical data, thrombosis profile, and catheter management were collected and analyzed.ResultsA total of 271 burn patients (mean burn area: 53.29 ± 23.65%) with 797 CVCs were finally included. The incidence of CRT was 13.28%, with a mean time from burn injuries to CRT onset of 25.96 ± 19.00 days. Half of the thrombotic events occurred between 15 and 30 days following burn trauma. Notably, 91.07% and 87.5% of CRT cases had diameters less than 5 mm and lengths shorter than 7 mm, respectively. Compared with the non-CRT cohort, the CRT cohort exhibited significantly larger burn area, more insertion times, higher proportion of blood purification procedures, and longer in-bed durations. Logistic regression and LASSO regression analyses identified burn index and continuous blood purification as independent risk factors for CRT. Consequently, a predictive nomogram model for CRT was successfully developed, achieving an AUROC of 0.75 (95% CI: 0.675–0.834) and a mean absolute error of the calibration curve of 0.029.ConclusionThe incidence of CRT was relatively high in burn populations. The established nomogram could provide a straightforward, quantitative and effective strategy for identifying patients at high-risk for CRT. Clinicians can utilize this assessment tool periodically to facilitate the early identification of individuals at high risk for CRT.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12959-025-00787-9.Keywords: Burns, Catheter-related thrombosis, Nomogram, Prediction model

Introduction

Burn injuries are among the most prevalent and devastating traumatic conditions worldwide, accounting for approximately 180,000 fatalities annually [1, 2]. Burn injuries impose substantial burdens not only on patients’ families, but also on the national health care systems globally [3]. Various central venous catheters (CVCs) have become an important routine in the critical burn patients and emergent settings. Catheter-related thrombosis (CRT) and catheter-related infections represent the primary complications associated with central venous catheters (CVCs) [4]. We previously established a nomogram for catheter-related bloodstream infection [5].However, a definitive predictive model for CRT in burn populations remains undeveloped.

In fact, burn patients are at a high risk of developing venous thrombosis [6]. Venous thrombosis is the result of hemodynamic changes, damage to the vascular endothelium, or altered blood structure. Burn victims are often hypercoagulable due to endothelial damage, systemic inflammation and activation of the coagulation system. In addition, prolonged in-bed day, ventilator support, multiple surgeries, and sepsis in burn patients increase the risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) [7]. A prospective study showed that the incidence of VTE in burn patients could be as high as 23.3% [8]. A study by Barrett et al. indicates that burn patients experiencing thrombotic events have a mortality rate of up to 20%, which is significantly higher than the overall mortality rate observed in burn victims [9]. Due to the risk factors presenting at the time of catheterization, CRT may occur even early and frequently. In fact, the reported incidence of CRT in cancer, critical ill patients and children ranges from 5.0% to 30.3% [10–12] and may result to potentially life-threatening vascular embolic disease [13]. However, current researches on clinical profile and risk factors of CRT among the burn population is lacking.

Venous thrombosis is usually clinically silent and difficult to detect, requiring the use of ultrasound to clarify [14]. Therefore, diagnosis of CRT usually lagged behind the actual thrombus formation and delayed the timely treatment and prevention of CRT [10]. Due to the significant disease burden, morbidity, and mortality associated with CRT, prevention is becoming an increasingly important priority [15]. In this regard, it is crucial to predict in advance for the timely, appropriate prevention and treatment of CRT. Currently, there is no validated CRT risk assessment tool specifically designed for burn patients. In this study, we retrospectively analyzed the clinical features, thrombosis profiles and related risk factors of CRT in burn patients. Then, a new nomogram model for predicting CRT in burn patients. Therefore, this study aimed to provide an a straightforward, quantitative and effective support system for the timely prevention and diagnosis of CRT in burn patients. Clinicians can employ this assessment instrument on a regular basis to enable the prompt detection of individuals who are at an elevated risk for developing CRT, thereby facilitating early intervention strategies and optimizing patient management outcomes.

Methods

Study design and ethic approval

This study is a retrospective analysis conducted at the Burn Research Institute of Southwest Hospital, Third Military Medical University (Army Medical University), from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2022. Southwest Hospital is located in Chongqing, China. This study was approved by the ethical committee of the Southwest Hospital (No. KY202245). Written informed consent from participants’ legal guardians or next of kin was not mandated for enrollment in this study, in compliance with applicable national legislation and institutional protocols.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Through an exhaustive query of the Southwest Hospital Burn Registry Database, patients with central venous catheters (CVCs) treated at our facility were initially identified. Subsequently, the cohort was refined to include only burn patients, confirmed through diagnostic coding. But Patients younger than 14 years of age or CVCs that were inserted before admission or incomplete data were excluded. Meanwhile, patients admitted later than one month after injury were also excluded.

Data collection

The dataset comprises extracted clinical parameters including demographic variables (gender, age, admission date, injury date, BMI), burn injury characteristics (etiology, total body surface area affected, anatomical location, inhalation injury presence, infection status, transfusion history, duration of hospitalization, number of surgical interventions). Central venous catheter (CVC) details (insertion date, number of puncture attempts, specific venous access sites and laterality, catheter tip position, extubation date), catheter-related thrombosis (CRT) specifics such as thrombus location, diameter, and length, as well as patient outcomes including CRT incidence rate, mortality, and length of hospital stay.

Insertion and management of CVCs

The utilization of central venous catheters (CVCs) within our institution was determined by the clinical team, primarily based on indications such as fluid resuscitation, long-term parenteral nutrition, infusion of antimicrobial agents, vasoactive medications, and other pharmacologic stimuli, as well as repeated blood sampling and continuous renal replacement therapy. All CVC insertions were performed under aseptic conditions by an experienced multidisciplinary team in accordance with established CVC insertion protocols [16].The selection of catheter size, length, and insertion site was determined by the attending clinician. Ultrasound guidance was employed during catheter placement in cases of difficult venous access. Routine monitoring of hematological and coagulation parameters, including platelet count, D-dimer, and fibrinogen levels, was conducted. In cases of abnormal laboratory findings, compression ultrasonography was utilized for thrombus detection. Additionally, clinical signs such as limb swelling, localized pain, increased skin temperature, or positive Homans’ and Moses’ signs prompted immediate ultrasonographic assessment. Upon identification of catheter-associated thrombus formation, the decision to retain or remove the device was made collaboratively by the treating physicians, with vascular surgical consultation to determine the necessity of initiating therapeutic anticoagulation.

Ultrasound diagnosis of catheter-related thrombosis

Ultrasound imaging was performed utilizing a linear transducer operating at a frequency range of 5–7 MHz. Sonographic screening was conducted on patients undergoing deep venous catheterization with elevated D-dimer levels. Burn patients were examined by experienced sonographers in the supine position. Diagnostic criteria for catheter-related thrombosis on ultrasound necessitate the presence of at least two of the following features: venous echogenic filling defect, venous non-compressibility, or abnormal flow patterns on color Doppler imaging. In this study, a thrombus located at the site of deep vein catheter placement was classified as a catheter-associated thrombus. Thrombus size assessment involved selecting the transverse plane with the maximal thrombus cross-sectional area for measurement of the largest and smallest diameters, with the maximum diameter representing the thrombus’s transverse dimension. The longitudinal length of the thrombus was measured along the vessel axis.

Nomogram construction and evaluation

A Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression model was employed to identify significant predictors associated with catheter-related thrombus formation. All candidate variables were incorporated into the LASSO model, with the proportional hazards assumption verified. The optimal predictor subset was determined based on the LASSO penalization criteria. A nomogram was subsequently developed utilizing these selected features. The model’s discriminative performance was assessed through the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC). Additionally, sensitivity, specificity, and overall accuracy were calculated along with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted utilizing GraphPad Prism 6 (GraphPad Software Inc., USA) and SPSS 26.0 (IBM Analytics, USA). The Student’s t-test was employed for the comparison of continuous variables with parametric distribution. The Mann-Whitney U test was applied for the comparison of two independent categorical variables. The Chi-squared test assessed the association between categorical variables, with frequency and percentage data. Multicollinearity diagnostics were performed to evaluate inter-variable correlations prior to multivariate modeling. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses (forward likelihood ratio method; entry threshold P = 0.05; removal threshold P = 0.10) identified factors influencing CRT (Table S2). Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan-Meier estimator, and Cox proportional hazards regression was utilized to identify independent mortality risk factors. LASSO regression analysis, nomogram construction and evaluation were performed in R Studio 2022.02.0 software (Prairie Trillium Release) using ‘glmnet’, ‘rms’, ‘pROC’ and ‘ggDCA’ packages. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Between January 2018 and December 2022, a total of 306 patients underwent placement of 875 central venous catheters (CVCs), which were initially included in the study cohort. Finally, 271 patients with 797 CVCs were included in this study following a step-by-step selection process (Fig. 1).

The incidence of CRT

Among 271 patients, a total of 36 patients developed CRT with an incidence of 13.28%. The annual incidence of CRT showed a fluctuating trend from 2018 to 2022, which was 10.53%, 16.33%, 11.90%, 15.79%, and 12.12% respectively. As shown in Table 1, the mean time from burn injuries to CRT was 25.96 ± 19.00 days. Half of thrombosis occurred during 15–30 days post burns and 75.0% of thromboses within 30 days post burn injuries. The mean diameter and length of thrombosis were 3.06 ± 1.12 mm and 20.61 ± 15.17 mm, respectively, with 91.07% < 5 mm in diameter and 87.5%< 30 mm in length. Among the 36 patients who developed CRT, a total of 56 catheters developed thrombosis, accounting for 7.03% of the total catheterizations included in this study. Seventeen patients (47.22%) suffered CRT twice, and two patients (5.56%) suffered three times.

Risk factors for CRT

As shown in Table 3, patients with CRT had significantly more insertion times (4.86 ± 3.99 vs. 2.65 ± 2.22, p < 0.001) and longer in-bed days (53.64 ± 32.90 vs. 34.92 ± 25.21, p < 0.001) than non-CRT group. Patients with CRT suffered a higher proportion of bloodstream infections (72.22% vs. 45.11%, p = 0.002) and received a higher proportion of blood purification than patients without CRT (16.67% vs. 2.55%, p < 0.001). However, there was no statistical difference in the levels of D-dimer (5.29 ± 6.16% vs. 5.27 ± 7.74, p = 0.99) and platelets (252.75 ± 155.80% vs. 243.34 ± 139.18, p = 0.71) at day 0 between the patients with CRT and those without CRT.

Predictive nomogram model for CRT

To further screen out the optimal clinical features in nomogram, the LASSO regression analysis was performed and found that bloodstream infection, catheterization times, in-bed days and continuous blood purification treatment were positively related with CRT, which could also achieve the best performance (Figure S1). Then, a nomogram of predicting CRT was successfully constructed (Fig. 2A). The AUROC of nomogram reached an AUROC of 0.75 (95%CI 0.675–0.834) (Fig. 2B) and the mean absolute error of calibration curve was 0.029(Fig. 2C), indicating a good fit. The decision curve analysis showed that use of the nomogram for prediction provided a good clinical benefit (Fig. 2D).

Association between CRT and outcome

Overall, 18 patients died during hospital with the in-hospital mortality of 6.64%, and patients with CRT had higher mortality than those without CRT (13.89% vs. 5.53%, p = 0.073). Further Cox regression analysis showed that sepsis had the greatest influence for death (OR = 7.84, P = 0.001), followed by CRT (OR = 4.48, P = 0.045), and shock (OR = 3.910, P = 0.036)(Table 5). However, survival analysis by Kaplan-Meier methods found no significant difference of survival time between patients with and without CRT (p = 0.064) (Figure S2).

Discussion

During the past few decades, CVCs have become a routine procedure in emergency and intensive care settings. CRT is one of the common and potentially fatal iatrogenic complications arising in burn patients. However, the timely diagnosis of CRT is time-consuming and resource-costing, relying primarily on ultrasound screening. Although certain studies have identified risk factors associated with CRT, a targeted risk stratification model tailored to burn populations and a straightforward, quantitative predictive framework for CRT remain underdeveloped. In this study, we first constructed a practical nomogram for the prediction of CRT in burn patients on the basis of the screened risk factors. This practical nomogram, comprising of bloodstream infection, catheterization times, in-bed days and continuous blood purification treatment, was shown to be highly predictive and clinically applicable. Therefore, this study provides a straightforward, practical and quantitative strategy for the prediction of CRT in burn populations.

This model is capable of effectively identifying subpopulations with a high risk of catheter-related thrombosis (CRT), conducting routine ultrasound screening of high-risk groups, enabling early detection, early intervention, and improving patient prognosis. In our constructed model, when a patient’s nomogram score exceeds 200, indicating a greater than 50% risk of CRT, we classify the patient as high-risk and strongly recommend implementing prophylactic strategies. Burn specialists can utilize this tool for systematic assessments to promptly identify high-risk patients with CRT, ultimately mitigating the adverse impact of venous thrombotic complications associated with catheter use on overall patient outcomes and recovery.

It has been documented that the occurrence rate of CRT ranges from 5.0% to 30.3% [10–12, 17–19]. The prevalence of VTE in burn patients was 23.3% in a prospective study of routine venous color Doppler ultrasound screening [8]. However, the incidence of CRT in burn patients is rarely reported. In our study, the rate of CRT is 13.28%, which is similar with previous study in critically ill patients [4]. While the incidence of symptomatic CRT is only about 5% or lower [17, 20], the majority are asymptomatic thrombi that are not detected early due to lack of clinical symptoms, which significantly increases the risk of complications such as pulmonary embolism and may lead to serious long-term consequences [21]. The incidence of pulmonary embolism varied from 1.8% to 9.0% [22, 23], and the rate of symptomatic pulmonary embolism may be lower [24].

The atypical clinical presentation of CRT is the main reason for the wide variation in the incidence of CRT in burn patients. Due to the effects of skin damage and dressing, it is difficult for medical staff to observe burn patients’ limbs in a timely manner, resulting in delayed assessment and diagnosis. Furthermore, the symptoms of burns, including swelling and pain, can be easily confused with those of limb thrombosis, which also can result in delayed diagnosis and treatment. Currently, the most commonly used tool for screening for blood clots is bedside Doppler ultrasound. Studies have shown that the sensitivity and specificity of Doppler ultrasound for thrombus detection are 84% −97% and 93% − 96% [25]. Angiography can be performed when bedside ultrasound results are negative but the presence of thrombus cannot be clinically excluded [26]. However, it is often unfeasible in critically ill patients due to hemodynamic instability, and the viscosity of the contrast agent itself, along with the stimulation of the vessel wall, can cause local pain and potentially induce new thrombus formation.

Plasma D-dimer is a specific degradation product of cross-linked fibrin, and its increased content is often indicative of hypercoagulability and microthrombosis within the body. It has been used for the screening and diagnosis of acute DVT, assessment of thrombolytic efficacy, and assessment of the risk of DVT recurrence [27]. D-dimer has a significant diagnostic value in the identification of acute pulmonary thromboembolism. It demonstrates high sensitivity but low specificity, which renders it a valuable tool in the exclusion of PTE [28]. Nevertheless, various factors, including surgical intervention, trauma, infection, and anticoagulant therapy, can influence plasma D-dimer levels. Elevated D-dimer levels, often resulting from hypercoagulable states associated with trauma, are relatively common among burn patients, particularly those with concomitant infections, bacteremia, and pneumonia. Consequently, D-dimer may not serve as a sensitive biomarker for venous thromboembolism in this patient population. Therefore, the hypercoagulable state of burn patients renders the diagnostic utility of D-dimer in the context of venous thrombosis to be limited. Consequently, some studies have indicated that D-dimer is not an effective screening tool for deep vein thrombosis (DVT) in burn injuries [29]. Our study also revealed that elevated D-dimer levels lack sufficient specificity to differentiate between patients with and without CRT. Therefore, prospective studies are required to establish the definitive D-dimer cutoff value in burn patients and to enhance the assay’s specificity for thromboembolic screening within this populations.

Many observational studies have reported on potential risk factors for CRT. However, the risk factors for CRT may also vary across different patient populations, and there is currently a lack of targeted research for burn patients. Burn populations are at a high risk of developing venous thrombosis. Burn victims, especially those with severe burns, are prone to clotting abnormalities. Burns that produce large amounts of denatured necrotic tissue, induce intense stress, and trigger severe systemic inflammatory responses are considered the fundamental causes of coagulation abnormalities in the early stages of burn injury [6, 30].Our study showed that bloodstream infection, catheterization times, in-bed days and Continuous blood purification treatment were risk factors of CRT. Venipuncture causes damage to the vascular endothelium. When CVC is used, the infusion rate is faster and the drug concentration is higher than that of the normal indwelling needle. Pressure is generated when the fluid infused obstructs the blood return and causes blood stasis at the distal end of the blood vessel. Patients with burns that require prolonged bed rest and immobilization, bandages and scabs that interfere with blood flow are also at increased risk for thrombosis. Previous studies have shown that patients with femoral vein indwelling CVCs have a higher risk of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) complications than those with CVCs in the subclavian or internal jugular veins [31, 32]. Our study also revealed a higher proportion (69.64%) of patients with CRT had catheters placed in Femoral vein. This may be related to the need for long-term immobilization in patients with femoral vein catheterization.

In this study, the mortality rate of patients with CRT was higher than that of patients without CRT (13.89% vs. 5.53%). Cox regression analysis also showed that CRT was Positive correlation with death. However, survival analysis by Kaplan-Meier methods found no significant difference of survival time between patients with and without CRT. Therefore, this study found that CRT could worsen the prognosis of burn patients. One of the most common causes of death from cardiovascular disease is pulmonary embolism (PE), which is primarily caused by deep vein thrombosis (DVT) [33]. Studies showed that cancer patients with thrombosis have a worse prognosis than cancer patients without thrombosis [34], and thrombosis is the leading cause of death among cancer patients [35]. However, no cases of pulmonary venous embolism were observed in our cohort. Thus, CRT contributed to the deterioration of patients’ conditions and prolonged hospitalization but did not directly result in mortality.

This investigation also presents certain methodological limitations. Firstly, the reported sensitivity and specificity of duplex ultrasound for DVT detection were 84%−97% and 93%−96%, respectively [25]. Furthermore, CRT is diagnosed by ultrasound at the bedside of ICU patients in our study, the diagnostic accuracy for CRT diagnosis was closely related to the sonographers [36]. Given the operator-dependent nature of ultrasonography, inter-operator variability may influence CRT detection outcomes. Additionally, some central venous catheters (CVCs) were inserted through burn wounds, precluding comprehensive ultrasonographic assessment along the entire catheter length. These factors could lead to occasional false-negative results during ultrasonographic evaluation, thereby introducing detection bias. Secondly, this study was a single-center retrospective study. Due to the constrained sample size incorporated in this investigation, our area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) is limited to 0.75, thereby restricting the model’s robustness and translational applicability. Future research should prioritize the deployment of multicenter, large-scale, population-based screening cohorts to enhance the development of more robust predictive models. At least, our study could provide a possible strategy for predicting CRT.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study was the first to construct a practical nomogram for predicting CRT in burn patients with excellent predictability and clinical relevance. Factors of this nomogram included bloodstream infection, catheterization times, in-bed days and Continuous blood purification treatment. This study provided a straightforward, quantitative and effective strategy to predict CRT in burn populations. This visualization model will aid the clinicians to predict the CRT among burn patients and better clinical management. Clinicians can utilize this assessment tool periodically to facilitate the early identification of individuals at high risk for CRT.
