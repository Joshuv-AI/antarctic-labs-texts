An autopsy case of bullous retinal detachment and hypopyon in a patient with Philadelphia chromosome-positive acute lymphoblastic leukemia
Authors: Miho Takenaka, Yasuko Ikegami, Seiya Kamino, Tomio Arai, Shigesaburo Miyakoshi, Hiroko Takao, Hiroki Yamamoto, Miwako Yoshimoto, Jiro Numaga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo report the clinical and histopathological features of a case of bullous retinal detachment, hypopyon, and optic disc swelling in a patient with Philadelphia chromosome-positive acute lymphoblastic leukemia (Ph + ALL).ObservationsA 65-year-old Japanese woman presented with bilateral blurry vision. Four years earlier, she had been diagnosed with Ph + ALL and received chemotherapy. At initial examination, optical coherence tomography revealed subretinal detachment resembling central serous chorioretinopathy. A similar finding had been detected 1 month earlier during the remission phase, when the patient visited a clinic. Subretinal detachment resolved after systemic chemotherapy. Five months later, while in complete remission, she developed unilateral iritis and elevated intraocular pressure in the right eye, which improved with topical steroid treatment. Ten months after the initial presentation, she returned with decreased vision in both eyes. A slit-lamp examination revealed hypopyon uveitis in the right eye. Fundus examination revealed bilateral bullous retinal detachment and optic disc swelling, which was more pronounced in the right eye. At the same time, a bone marrow biopsy confirmed relapse of ALL. Therefore, systemic and intrathecal chemotherapy was initiated; however, her condition deteriorated, and she died 2 weeks later. Autopsy revealed widespread leukemic infiltration throughout the ocular tissues, resulting in multiple ocular manifestations. The findings suggested that ocular infiltration occurred either hematogenously or through direct extension from the meninges.ConclusionLeukemic infiltration of the eye was pathologically confirmed in a patient with Ph + ALL who developed ocular manifestations during the remission phase. Ocular evaluation is essential for confirming ocular relapse of ALL, and patients should be closely monitored to enable early detection and timely intervention.

Introduction

Ocular manifestations of leukemia are common and variable.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Leukemic cells may infiltrate the orbit, eyelid, retina, uveal tract, and optic nerve.1, 2, 3 Retinopathy is the most common manifestation; however, clinical findings involving the anterior chamber, retinal detachment, and optic nerve are uncommon.4,5 In addition, several autopsy series of ocular leukemia have demonstrated histopathological findings in the affected ocular tissues6, 7, 8, 9; however, the exact pathogenesis of ocular leukemic infiltration remains unclear.

Leukemia can be categorized as either acute or chronic. Acute leukemias are further classified into acute myeloid leukemia (AML), which predominantly affects older adults, and acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), which primarily affects children. Previous studies have demonstrated that ophthalmic findings are more common in AML than in ALL.5 Philadelphia chromosome-positive acute lymphoblastic leukemia (Ph + ALL) reportedly accounts for approximately 20–25 % of all ALL cases.10 Clinically, Ph + ALL in adults is characterized by a higher rate of central nervous system (CNS) involvement at presentation compared to other ALL types.11,12 Although Ph + ALL has traditionally been associated with poor long-term prognosis, the addition of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) to conventional chemotherapy has improved outcomes.10 However, reports on intraocular infiltration in Ph + ALL remain limited.13, 14, 15

In this report, we describe an unusual case of bilateral bullous retinal detachment and leukemic infiltration of the anterior chamber and optic disc during the remission phase of Ph + B-cell ALL. Additionally, we correlate the clinical findings with the pathological findings observed at autopsy.

Case report

A 65-year-old Japanese woman presented with bilateral blurry vision. Four years earlier, she had been diagnosed with Ph + B-cell ALL and received systemic chemotherapy—including induction, consolidation, and maintenance therapy—along with molecularly targeted drugs (imatinib, ponatinib, dasatinib, blinatumomab, and asciminib) and two stem cell transplants during the bone marrow remission phase. However, she experienced repeated relapses and remissions, including two CNS relapses. CNS prophylaxis with intrathecal methotrexate therapy was administered 21 times. Three months earlier, she completed a second chemotherapy regimen and achieved complete remission. She continued therapy with the TKI asciminib. One month earlier, while in complete remission, she was diagnosed with bilateral subretinal fluid by her family doctor. Five days before her initial presentation to the department of ophthalmology at our hospital, a relapse of ALL was confirmed, and systemic chemotherapy was initiated.

Upon initial ophthalmologic examination, her best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 20/22 in the right eye and 20/20 in the left eye, and her intraocular pressure (IOP) was 18 mmHg in both eyes. Slit-lamp examination was unremarkable in both eyes. Fundus examination and optical coherence tomography (OCT) revealed flat serous retinal detachment (SRD) and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) undulations in both eyes (Fig. 1). Five months after the initial ophthalmologic presentation, while she was in complete remission, the patient developed blurry vision and ocular pain in the right eye. Although her BCVA was 20/25 in the right eye and 20/20 in the left eye, the IOP increased to 43 mmHg in the right eye, despite a normal anterior chamber depth. Slit-lamp examination of the right eye demonstrated fine-pigmented keratic precipitates (KPs) and 1+ cells in the anterior chamber. Fundus examination of the right eye revealed optic disc swelling with blurred margins. As the patient was receiving systemic steroid therapy, an opportunistic infection was suspected; however, cytomegalovirus antibody test results were negative. Treatment with a topical steroid and intraocular pressure-lowering medications was initiated, resulting in normalization of the IOP. Subsequently, the patient discontinued visits to the department of ophthalmology in preparation for allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, which was performed 1 month later.

Ten months after the initial presentation (3 months after the stem cell transplantation), the patient presented with bilateral decreased vision. Her BCVA was 20/60 in the right eye and 20/40 in the left eye, while the IOP had decreased to 6 mmHg in the right eye and 14 mmHg in the left eye. Slit-lamp examination of the right eye revealed 3+ cells in the deep anterior chamber, fine KPs, and hypopyon (Fig. 2). Fundus examination revealed bilateral inferior bullous retinal detachment with shifting subretinal fluid, resembling choroidal effusion. Scattered retinal hemorrhages were also observed. Optic disc swelling with peripapillary hemorrhage was observed in both eyes, especally in the right eye. OCT showed SRD and RPE undulations in both eyes (Fig. 2). B-scan ultrasonography revealed elevated retinal detachment and choroidal thickening (Fig. 2). Computed tomography of the brain showed no evidence of increased intracranial pressure, and a bone marrow biopsy performed the same day revealed predominantly blasts, confirming a diagnosis of ALL relapse with intraocular infiltration. Therefore, intrathecal methotrexate therapy and systemic chemotherapy with L-asparaginase were initiated. Her vision continued to deteriorate, reaching 20/125 in the right eye and 20/40 in the left eye. Discussion

We described a patient with bilateral bullous retinal detachment, hypopyon in the anterior chamber, and optic disc swelling during the remission phase of Ph + B-cell ALL. Leukemic infiltration of the anterior segment of the eye, choroid, and optic nerve was pathologically confirmed at autopsy. The ocular findings of leukemia are well documented1,2; however, patients rarely present with anterior chamber infiltration,13,16,17 retinal detachment,18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23 and optic disc swelling.4,24,25 Previous autopsy series showed that the prevalence of ocular leukemic infiltration was up to 90 %,7, 8, 9 with the choroid being the most frequently infiltrated site (65 %), followed by the retina, sclera, optic nerve, orbit, conjunctiva, ciliary body, and iris.6, 7, 8, 9

The initial ocular manifestation in this patient was SRD, which preceded all other findings. Among the differential diagnoses of SRD, central serous chorioretinopathy (CSC) must be ruled out in patients receiving high-dose corticosteroids.20 In the present case, CSC was suspected at the initial presentation; however, bilateral SRD had been identified at a clinic before the initiation of systemic steroid treatment, making CSC unlikely. Careful observation of OCT revealed RPE undulations, which can help distinguish CSC from choroidal infiltration with secondary subretinal fluid.

Notably, ocular infiltration in this patient was initially detected during the leukemia remission phase. Ocular relapse may occur during hematological remission because the eye serves as a pharmacological sanctuary for systemic chemotherapy and is marginally affected by systemic treatment.1,16,24

The pathological findings at autopsy suggested that leukemic infiltration of the choroid was the cause of SRD. Choroidal infiltration was most pronounced in the posterior pole of the eye, particularly in the macular region. Leukemic cells were observed within the stroma and around the choroidal vessels, consistent with a previous report.6 The choroid has the highest blood flow per unit area in the human body. Adaniya et al. postulated that it serves as a sanctuary for leukemic cells due to its high oxygen availability, which promotes blast survival.20 The proposed mechanism underlying SRD in leukemia is as follows. Leukemic infiltration of the choroid causes decreased blood flow in the choriocapillaris, resulting in ischemia and hypoxia of the overlying RPE. This, in turn, leads to dysfunction of the outer blood–retinal barrier, resulting in SRD.14,18,20,23 Regarding the morphology of RPE, previous studies have reported secondary alterations in the overlying RPE, including destruction, atrophy, and hypertrophy.8,9,26 However, in our case, no significant RPE alterations were observed despite the severe and widespread detachment, consistent with the mechanism proposed by Stewart et al.,18 which suggests that rapid leukemic infiltration may cause only reversible RPE dysfunction without any morphological alterations.

In the present case, pathological examination revealed diffuse leukemic infiltration of the iris, especially at the sphincter. The ciliary body also exhibited diffuse leukemic infiltration. Infiltration of the ciliary body can result in dysfunction and reduced aqueous humor production, leading to decreased IOP, as observed during the final visit in this case. Leukemic cell infiltration of the trabecular meshwork was also observed, which may have contributed to increased IOP.

In addition, leukemic cell infiltration was evident in the anterior choroidal region, extending from the ciliary body, and the posterior pole. In prior studies, iris involvement was most often observed alongside choroid and ciliary body infiltration.3,6,8 Perivascular infiltration of leukemic cells was also observed along the anterior ciliary and short posterior ciliary arteries penetrating the sclera. These findings suggest that leukemic infiltration may have occurred through anterior or posterior routes by perivascular infiltration along the anterior ciliary and long posterior ciliary arteries that supply the choroid.

In the present case, leukemic cell infiltration of the optic nerve was primarily located anterior to the lamina cribrosa. It has been proposed that a barrier may exist between the optic nerve and the remainder of the CNS24 and that the intraocular portion of the optic nerve lies beyond the reach of intrathecal chemotherapy. This may lead to marginal leukemic infiltration of the retrolaminar portion of the optic nerve. Furthermore, in this case, optic nerve head infiltration consisted primarily of a perivascular distribution of leukemic cells, consistent with the findings of Ellis and Little.27 Leukemia-related optic nerve lesions, such as disc swelling, can result from direct infiltration, obstruction of the venous outflow by perivascular leukemic cells, or ischemia. In the present case, despite the severity of the optic nerve swelling, there was comparatively little leukemic infiltration. Optic nerve swelling may be associated with ischemia due to hematological abnormalities, compressive phenomena, or direct infiltration of the vessel wall.

Direct leukemic infiltration of the optic nerve is relatively uncommon. Nevertheless, the optic nerve is considered an important site of CNS leukemia.8,24,25,27 CNS infiltration in ALL rarely involves the brain parenchyma and is typically restricted to the meninges, which are an infrequent site of carcinomatous invasion.28 One study showed that ALL cells migrate along the external surfaces of vessels that pass directly between the vertebral or calvarial bone marrow and subarachnoid space.29 In the present case, leukemic infiltration of the cerebral and thoracic meninges was observed at autopsy. In this case, leukemic infiltration of the optic nerve was more prominent in the arachnoid than in the septa, suggesting that the infiltration originated from the meninges. These findings are consistent with the report by Robb, who demonstrated that optic nerve involvement is always associated with meningeal leukemia.7 Our findings, along with those of previous studies,15,17 suggest that the optic nerve links the CNS to the anterior segment of the eye. Leukemic cells may migrate along the posterior ciliary vessels within the subarachnoid space surrounding the optic nerve to reach both the anterior and posterior portions of the eye.

In conclusion, we report a case of bullous retinal detachment, hypopyon, and optic disc swelling in a patient with Ph + ALL during the remission phase. Leukemic infiltration was pathologically confirmed in the iris, ciliary body, choroid, and optic nerve. Consistent with previous studies, we believe that the ocular infiltration was hematogenous and occurred directly through the arachnoid mater (meninges). Ocular findings, especially SRD, can be an initial sign of leukemic relapse, even during hematological remission. This report highlights the importance of careful monitoring of ocular symptoms and imaging in patients with a history of Ph + ALL to ensure timely diagnosis and early intervention.
