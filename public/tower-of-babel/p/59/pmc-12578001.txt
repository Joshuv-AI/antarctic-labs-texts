Perioperative anesthesia management in elderly patients with chest trauma following a fall: a case report
Authors: Eniyew Assimie Alemu, Solomon Yeshamble Eniyew, Bethelhem Abu Teffera, Molla Amsalu Tadesse
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction and significance:Anesthetic management of an elderly patient with a retained wooden chest foreign body for emergency thoracic surgery in a low-resource setting, complicated by recent asthma exacerbation, posed significant perioperative challenges.Clinical presentation:A 65-year-old Ethiopian male falling from 5-meters height tree on wooden ground and was admitted to our tertiary hospital after four hours of injury on the left chest with a wooden foreign body in situ. On presentation, the patient underwent comprehensive evaluation and received initial resuscitative care. Following informed written consent, a thoracotomy under general anesthesia was subsequently planned and performed as definitive management. After multimodal medication premedication and applying standard monitoring for general anesthesia, balanced anesthesia was administered without adverse events. The impaled wood was removed cautiously under direct visualization of the surrounding organ injury, and a wedge resection was performed. Postoperatively, he was discharged from the hospital after 20 days of postoperative care with a good health condition and was appointed for medical follow-up after 2 weeks.Clinical discussion:Falling from a certain height can cause serious harm, including comorbidities and even immediate death. Although such incidents are rare, a fall from a height can lead to musculoskeletal injuries and internal disruptions in elderly individuals, potentially resulting in concealed bleeding and organ damage. In addition to the critical nature of these emergencies, a patient’s medical comorbidities present significant barriers to achieving optimal outcomes. Therefore, a recent exacerbation in an asthmatic patient undergoing thoracotomy for retained wood removal necessitates multidisciplinary team care to ensure the best possible outcomes.Conclusion:Essential health care for such patients needs a multidisciplinary team approach to health care service. Furthermore, meticulous airway and optimal pain management is the mainstay in perioperative anesthesia management.

Introduction

Penetrating chest injury causes morbidity and mortality due to an internal organ dysfunction that needs immediate intervention, and its incidence of penetrating chest injuries varies with respect to the trauma scene presentation, which accounts for 1–13% of hospital admissions[1]. Falling is one of the common causes of trauma in the elderly population[2,3], with a high rate of mortality in this demographic[4]. Patients after penetrating injury have different clinical manifestations based on their site of injury, characteristics of the affected population, and mechanism of injury. An elderly individual falling from a height, especially greater than 10.5 m, causes subsequent death[5], severe chest injuries, and internal organ damage. Furthermore, penetrating impaled wood to the chest following a fall would have the following injuries: rib fractures, pneumothorax, hemothorax, hemopneumothorax, cardiac injury, great vessel injuries, and lung contusions, which may necessitate thoracic surgical intervention to prevent or address life-threatening complications. In addition to primary resuscitation of these patients, the definitive management for penetrated chest injury ranges from simple thoracotomy and chest tube to complex thoracic surgery[6]. Open thoracotomy in elderly patients with comorbidities leads to challenges in perioperative care[7]. Thoracic surgery, particularly in elderly patients with chest impalement injuries, demands specialized perioperative anesthesia and surgical care, which poses significant challenges in low-resource, low-volume settings[8].

This case report and literature review illustrate an elderly patient with severe chest impalement who had a thoracotomy following a fall. Thoracotomies of these groups of populations have a unique challenge for anesthesia management[9,10]. This case report and review of the literature provide insights into effective perioperative anesthesia management of an elderly patient with a wooden impaled chest injury undergoing emergency thoracotomy in a low-resource setting, aiming to improve postoperative outcomes and enhance the quality of recovery. Detailed clinical perioperative anesthesia management of the patient was conducted according to the comprehensive and updated consensus Surgical Case Report (SCARE) guidelines[11].

Case presentation

A 65-year-old Ethiopian male presented to the emergency department with thoracic impalement caused by a wooden object falling from a height of 5 meters. Upon arrival at our tertiary hospital, he was critically ill with a wooden implement lodged in his left lower chest. The patient’s clinical symptoms included shortness of breath, fast and shallow breathing, and decreased air entry on the left side of the chest. The foreign wood had penetrated his thoracic cavity at the seventh intercostal space, measuring 4 × 5 cm with minimal bleeding from the sucking wound with an increase in diameter toward the interior chest of 35 cm left in the environment (Fig. 1). He received primary health care aid in our hospital, including oxygen therapy, analgesia, and resuscitation fluids, before being transferred to the operating room for definitive medical management. Preoperative anesthesia evaluation found that the patient had a history of asthma, which had been a recent exacerbation, but it was managed with prednisolone medication. His vital signs were unstable, despite fluid resuscitation and nasal oxygen therapy. Subsequently, surgical removal of the impaled wood via thoracotomy under general anesthesia was a definitive management. Informed consent was obtained from the patient, including permission for the use of case details in educational publications, with strict measures taken to maintain confidentiality of personal identity. The patient received dexamethasone, cimetidine, and salbutamol inhalers as multimodal premedication before anesthesia to enhance patient safety during the perioperative period. Standard monitoring for general anesthesia according to the Association of Anesthetists of Great Britain and Ireland guideline recommendation was attached to the patient, and his vital signs were recorded throughout the operation (Fig. 4). The patient was preoxygenated via facemask and high-flow nasal oxygen, which was continued throughout laryngoscopy until the airway was secured. The patient was induced with the anesthetic medications ketamine, propofol, and suxamethonium, followed by intubation of the airway using a single-lumen endotracheal tube (Fig. 1A). The tube was advanced to the right bronchus to prevent surgical site soiling to the non-operated lung and for good access to the surgical field, as the impelled wood had destructed major parts of the left lung. During the thoracotomy, the left chest was opened, and it was found that the wooden object had penetrated deeply, reaching the subclavian vasculature. The impaled wood was removed carefully to minimize blood loss and prevent air embolism. The intraoperative hypoxia was managed with the maintenance of the hemodynamic status and the application of physiologic positive end-expiratory pressure on the dependent lung. The wedge-resected lung was recruited by retracting the endotracheal tube above the carina with frequent suctioning. The necrotic lung tissue was removed as a wedge resection, and a chest tube was placed (Fig. 2A–D). The patient was extubated while fully awake, with extubation-related adverse events minimized through intravenous administration of lidocaine and fentanyl, re-expansion of atelectatic lung segments, and bronchodilation via salbutamol administered through the endotracheal tube to optimize asthma control. He was transferred to the Surgical Intensive Care Unit (SICU) with stable vital signs for further close monitoring and pain management. Postoperative care included the administration of analgesics, antibiotics, and respiratory physiotherapy.

Despite the patient being initially transferred from SICU to recovery, he developed acute respiratory failure on the fifth day of postoperative time due to poor pain management. He was readmitted to SICU, and then systemic analgesia with thoracic paravertebral nerve block was performed for better pain control (Fig. 3B). After receiving adequate pain management and respiratory care, he was discharged from SICU to the medical ward and eventually discharged from the hospital 20 days later after full recovery by subsequent health care, as shown in Table 1. Discussion

Anesthesia management of an elderly critically ill patient with chest trauma following a fall and a wooden implement in the chest requires careful planning and execution. We report a case of an elderly patient who fell from a 5-meter tree, sustaining a left-sided thoracic impalement by a wooden object, resulting in hypoxia and hemodynamic instability. Falling is a global problem affecting both adults and children, with a high rate of death[12]. It most commonly occurs in rural agricultural communities, where pre-hospital services are limited, and patients may die at the scene of a trauma event[13] or reach the hospital in a critical stage; similarly, our surviving patient arrived at the hospital after 4 hours of trauma. The study found that older age and falls from over five meters independently increase the risk of death after a fall[14]. A patient experiencing a fall from a certain height could develop pneumopericardium, especially when associated with hemothorax and pneumothorax[15]. Patients who fall from a certain height have cardiorespiratory collapse with respect to a height of falling >3 m and need pre-clinic vasopressor and airway intubation first aid health care[16]; besides this, our survived patient has the probability of getting concealed impelled wood pressure-supported vascular bleeding. At each level of care for traumatic thoracic injury, pre-clinic, emergency hospital admission, and preoperative resuscitation and management following the protocol of advanced trauma life support bring the patient better postoperative outcomes[17].

We managed perioperatively an asthmatic patient whose exacerbation was 2 weeks back with the necessary preoperative evaluation, optimization, and execution of anesthesia through emergency setup in a low-resource area. Administering anesthesia for patients with comorbidity of asthma would have an adverse effect on airway hyperactivity, anesthetic medication-induced exacerbation, and perioperative laryngeal/bronchial wall muscle spasm[18]. Despite there being no complete control of adverse events in the airway during anesthesia, the incidence could be able to decrease with valuing high attention on preoperative assessment, reducing the triggering factors, and using anti-inflammatory and bronchodilator medication[19].

The patient was monitored with a minimum of standard monitoring under general anesthesia according to the Association of Anesthetists’ recommendation of the Great Britain and Ireland guideline[20]. Despite this recommendation, we did not apply the respiratory monitoring and capnography due to inaccessibility in our setup. Additionally, our patient needs invasive monitoring as arterial line for beat-to-beat heart rate monitoring and a central venous catheter for intraoperative fluid challenge and monitoring[21]; however, this monitoring was difficult to access in low-resource setting and in emergency surgery in our setup.

The patient's airway was secured through a classic polyvinyl chloride endotracheal tube with modified rapid sequence induction with high-flow nasal oxygen to decrease intubation time and adverse events and increase the apnea time. According to the randomized controlled trial study done in France, preoxygenation through noninvasive ventilation decreases hypoxemia and hypoxic organ insults[22]. The Society of Critical Care Medicine clinical practice guidelines highly recommend that recommendations for critically ill patients needing to be intubated with rapid sequence induction, along with high flow of nasal oxygenation to decrease hypoxia and adverse events during the procedure[23].

According to the review study on pain management for thoracic surgery, after thoracotomy, patients suffered from severe pain that would result in post-thoracotomy pain syndrome. As a result, systemic analgesia with a combination of regional analgesia had better postoperative pain management for thoracotomy[24]. However, our patient was operated on with an emergency health condition, and there was no availability of equipment for the regional analgesia pain control option, and the patient was managed with multimodal systemic analgesia. After being readmitted to SICU, a catheter-based paravertebral block commenced for further pain management and subsequent physiotherapy. Due to its hemodynamic control preference and equivalent analgesic potency with thoracic epidural, catheter-based paravertebral block is an effective but less side effect for high-risk patients of pain management for thoracotomy[25]. Multimodal systemic analgesia combined with regional blocks of pain management and postoperative pulmonary rehabilitation in breathing exercises accelerates a patient’s postoperative recovery for thoracic surgery of an asthmatic comorbidity and elderly patients.

The anesthetists must take into account the patient’s hemodynamic status, respiratory function, and the extent of injury when selecting appropriate anesthetic agents and techniques. A multidisciplinary approach is essential for optimizing care and ensuring the best possible outcome for the patient.

Conclusion and recommendation

Perioperative anesthesia care of elderly patients with impelled chest trauma with asthmatic comorbidity requires thorough preoperative evaluation, meticulous perioperative airway management and hemodynamic control, and optimal pain management. Multidisciplinary approaches of anesthetists, surgeons, intensivists, and nursing staff have the most valuable contribution for improving outcomes and facilitating recovery, including postoperative pulmonary rehabilitation.
