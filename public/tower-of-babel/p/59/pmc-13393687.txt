Transnasal endoscopy and the use of virtual reality: a case report
Authors: Vrinda Bhardwaj, Chelsea C. Okoro, Saloni Dangoria, Jeffrey I. Gold
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildren diagnosed with eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) often require repeated esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) procedures to monitor disease progression and treatment response. These procedures typically involve general anesthesia, which is associated with short-term side effects and potential long-term risks. Transnasal endoscopy (TNE) has emerged as a less invasive alternative to EGD that can be performed without anesthesia. The use of virtual reality (VR) during TNE eliminates anesthesia exposure in patients requiring repeated EGD, while enhancing patient comfort and procedural success.Case presentationsThe objective of this case study is to detail the experience of a 12-year-old, Caucasian, male patient diagnosed with EoE. The patient previously underwent multiple EGD procedures to monitor disease progression, resulting in repeated anesthesia exposures. In hopes of reducing anesthesia exposure, the patient and their family opted to explore sedation-sparing alternatives to EGD, marking the patients first TNE. Despite high baseline anxiety, the patient tolerated their initial TNE procedure without vomiting or gagging, required less than one hour in the clinic per visit, experienced no post-procedural recovery time, and requested VR for follow-up TNE procedures. Utilizing VR enabled a successful, sedation-free TNE in a highly anxious pediatric patient, supporting VR as an effective sedation-sparing substitute for painful procedures.ConclusionsThis case highlights the potential of VR as a non-pharmacologic tool to enhance the tolerability of TNE in pediatric patients, thereby minimizing sedation-associated health risks in children requiring frequent endoscopic monitoring.

Background

Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) is a common exam of the upper gastrointestinal (UGI) tract to monitor eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE), requiring frequent endoscopic evaluations [1]. Pediatric EGD typically necessitates sedation or general anesthesia, and although anesthesia is not definitively linked to long-term harm, it’s associated with a range of short-term side effects such as nausea, vomiting, prolonged recovery, and post-procedural discomfort. Emerging concerns also suggest potential long-term impacts of repeated anesthesia exposure in children, including effects on neurodevelopment, potential neurotoxicity, neurocognitive development issues, and negatively affect academic performance [2–4].

To mitigate the short- and long-term effects of anesthesia during EGDs, transnasal endoscopy (TNE) has emerged as a sedative-free procedure for monitoring UGI tract [5]. However, performing TNEs can present challenges in pediatric patients: managing anxiety, procedural discomfort, and distress [6]. One framework categorizes patients as “attenders” or “distractors” [7]: attenders watch the procedure while distractors avert their attention. During EGDs, anesthesia enables “distractors” to undergo procedures without awareness of the medical environment, prompting TNEs to require an alternative strategy for distractors. During routine and painful medical procedures, virtual reality (VR) capitalizes on attentional diversion. By immersing patients in highly engaging virtual experiences, VR effectively occupies their working memory and reduces the cognitive processing of pain and anxiety [8]. VR allows pediatric patients to avoid seeing the medical environment and medical staff performing the procedure and instead allows the patient to be immersed in a virtual experience (TV show or movie) which offers comfort, stress reduction, and distraction. Removing the stressful environment of the procedure for pediatric and adolescent patients improves procedural adherence, and improves overall satisfaction. In pediatric populations, existing literature supports the safety and efficacy of TNE’s [9], and VR has emerged as a promising alternative to sedation for enhancing tolerance and comfort during these procedures [10].

As part of a larger, ongoing clinical research study at Children’s Hospital Los Angeles (CHLA), the efficacy of VR for pain and anxiety management during painful procedures was examined, and has since become an integral part of the clinic's practice. This case report describes the use of a Meta Quest (MQ) 2 VR headset on achieving procedural success during a TNE in a 12-year-old male patient with EoE. The diagnosis was initially made following a diagnostic sedated esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD), which was followed by a second sedated endoscopy for disease monitoring. Both procedures required the patient to follow an overnight NPO (nil per os) fasting period prior to the procedure, arrive at the ambulatory surgery center hours in advance, undergo intravenous (IV) placement, and spend several hours in post-procedural recovery. These procedures also resulted in significant time away from school and daily activities. Understanding that repeat endoscopies were necessary to monitor disease recurrence, the family expressed interest in a sedation-free option. In response, the patient’s clinical team offered TNE with VR. This report highlights the successful implementation of VR during TNE, emphasizing the growing importance of patient-centered care while demonstrating how VR can support both clinical efficacy and improved patient experiences in pediatric procedural care.

Case presentation

A 12-year-old male, Caucasian, patient was diagnosed with eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) after presenting with a 3-month history of abdominal pain, early satiety, and unintentional weight loss of 3.1 kg (7 lbs.). Diagnostic EGD under general anesthesia revealed edematous mucosa and linear furrowing in the mid to distal esophagus. Biopsies showed ≥ 50 eosinophils per high-power field (eos/hpf) in the distal and mid-esophagus, consistent with EoE symptomology. The patient’s initial symptom management utilized a high-dose proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy which resulted in minimal symptom improvement. Repeat sedated EGD at 8 weeks demonstrated persistent endoscopic findings and eosinophilia (≥ 40 eos/hpf) and the patient’s treatment was escalated to include swallowed topical corticosteroids and a six-food elimination diet. While clinical symptoms improved modestly, both prior sedated EGDs were associated with extended procedural preparation and recovery. Due to the complexities and additional burden that came from the sedated procedure, the patient and their parents expressed a strong interest in avoiding sedation for future surveillance endoscopies. Given the need for ongoing monitoring, the care team offered transnasal endoscopy (TNE) assisted by virtual reality (VR) as a sedation-free alternative, marking the patient’s first experience with a VR-assisted TNE procedure.

On the day of the procedure, the patient appeared visibly apprehensive. During the VR consenting process, he expressed concerns about the TNE’s pain, duration, and sensation of the numbing spray for his nostrils. On the mother’s self-report survey, she wrote, “He was VERY nervous and scared. He was looking forward to the VR to distract him.” After the TNE physician explained the procedure, the patient’s anxiety intensified, and the team was concerned that the procedure would be aborted. The patient’s hesitation increased, and he repeatedly said he was “not ready.” Curled up next to his mother, reluctant to sit in the procedure chair, the patient asked if he could do “the other procedure instead,” (i.e., the sedated EGDs). With gentle reassurance from his mother—encouraging prayer, breathing, affirmations—and support from the medical team, the patient agreed to begin the TNE. Two sprays of 1% lidocaine were administered in two phases with a break between, while the patient used a lollipop to mask the flavor of the lidocaine.

Afterward, the patient was situated in the TNE procedure room as the research team placed the VR headset on him. The patient began watching a self-chosen show, “Sonic”, on the VR headset, but soon after requested the VR headset to be removed so he could observe his environment (i.e., an attender). Less than a minute later, overwhelmed by medical room stimuli, the patient changed his mind: “Never mind, I want the VR now” (i.e., a distractor). After repositioning the VR headset on the patient, the patient could not see the medical room and was instead put into a virtual reality environment, a cartoon aquarium, with a large screen in the middle which displayed the patient’s chosen show. Volume levels on the headset were adjusted so the patient was able to hear the show while also listening to directions from physician during the procedure.

Though initially anxious, the patient successfully completed the procedure in under 4 min. Using a BF-XP190 Olympus® bronchoscope with an outer diameter of 2.8 mm, esophageal biopsies were obtained from both distal and proximal locations. The patient tolerated the sedation-sparing procedure well, without gagging or vomiting, and kept the VR headset on throughout. Using the Gold-Rizzo Immersion and Presence (GRIP) Inventory [11], the patient self-reported enjoying the VR experience and rated VR’s helpfulness as “10-Extremely”. The TNE physician and nursing team believed that the TNE would not have been possible without the use of VR. Post-procedure, the patient seemed happy and relieved without the nausea or adverse effects and was checked out from the clinic in under an hour.

Approximately three months later, the patient returned for a repeat TNE procedure, and both the parent and patient requested VR. The patient seemed noticeably less anxious than his first visit and completed the TNE with the use of VR without gagging or vomiting. The patient's there shared that, given the patient’s high anxiety, these appointments were only possible because of the VR. In the parent’s post-survey, she reported, “I’m glad to have the options to make him as comfortable as possible.”

Conclusions

This case report supports VR’s role in procedural pain and anxiety management, while highlighting sedation-free procedural success in pediatric TNEs. In pediatric TNEs, VR improved procedural satisfaction and created a more efficient, less stressful environment for patient, caregiver, and healthcare providers [6, 12]. Moving away from EGD procedures to TNE with VR facilitates a more efficient outpatient appointment by significantly reducing the procedural preparation time and patient recovery time. Given that up to 60% of adverse events related to pediatric sedated endoscopy were from anesthesia/sedation complications, sedation‐free TNE provides a safe option for patients who require repeated endoscopic monitoring [13]. Understanding the chronic nature of EoE and the need for repeated procedures for disease monitoring and surveillance, the use of VR in lieu of anesthesia reduces patients’ exposure to known negative short- and long-term side-effects of anesthesia [2, 3]. Additionally, clinicians adopting TNE with VR assistance over traditional EGD as a new standard of care procedure for pediatric patients will optimize clinic efficacy while reducing clinic costs due to removing anesthesia-associated costs, prolonged appointment times and time spent in the hospital, and cost for additional staff. Although there is a high initial cost of purchasing the VR headset, transitioning from sedated EGD to VR-assisted TNE will reduce overall clinic costs. Additionally, VR headsets are commercially available, making them user friendly and do not require specialized training or staff to implement their use.

While this case study highlights the effectiveness of using virtual reality during non-sedated endoscopy procedures for pediatric patients, there are some limitations. One limitation in this case study is that not all patients are eligible to receive virtual reality, limiting the application of the case report. For example, patients who have a history of seizures, diagnosed with visual or hearing impairments, or experience dizziness or motion sickness would be ineligible for receiving VR. The patient described in this case study is part of a larger clinical trial at the Children’s Hospital of Los Angeles; however, a smaller study group leads to a lack of generalizability for the use of VR across different pediatric populations. Additionally, the study lacks a control group, resulting in an unexplored comparison in comfortability, tolerance, and pain and anxiety levels of patients who use VR during their TNE versus patients who use no interventions during their TNE.

VR has been shown to be a valuable tool in mitigating anxiety, pain, and distress, while increasing comfort and satisfaction in common medical procedures like venipuncture [11]. Awake, endoscopic procedures can cause significant anxiety and discomfort for pediatric patients, and VR offers an immersive, engaging solution to mitigate these challenges. As one of the first studies investigating the efficacy of VR-assisted TNE in pediatric populations, this case study demonstrates how VR serves as an effective anxiety and pain management tool, improving patient cooperation, satisfaction, and tolerance of TNE, while truncating procedure time. While prior studies have explored the safety and efficacy of TNE, our case is one of the first to highlight the integration of VR as a tool to manage procedural anxiety and improve patient comfort, offering a novel, non-pharmacologic alternative to sedation for frequent EoE monitoring. This approach has significant implications for clinical practice, as it offers a safer, more efficient method of repeated monitoring for pediatric patients who require frequent endoscopic evaluations. Furthermore, this case report supports future research into expanding VR use across broader pediatric populations and other gastrointestinal conditions. Future clinical trials should investigate the broader applicability of VR-assisted TNE in diverse pediatric populations, exploring its impact on procedural outcomes, patient comfort, and healthcare costs. This case report also opens avenues for research on the long-term effects of reducing sedation exposure and overall quality of life for children with chronic conditions like EoE.
