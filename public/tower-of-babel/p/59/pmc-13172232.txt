Narrative Review “Dual Frontiers in Neurodegeneration: Pharmacological Synergies and Divergences in Parkinson’s Disease and Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus”
Authors: Omnya A Sharallah, Vishnu Malakar, Ahmed E Salama, Salma M Elmaaz, Salma A Daba, Nitesh Kumar Poddar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetes mellitus (DM) and Parkinson’s disease (PD) are both chronic diseases that have a bidirectional relationship, wherein DM increases the risk of developing PD. Both diseases are related through overlapping pathways, insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation, which can potentially lead to neurodegeneration. Insulin resistance and impaired insulin signalling present in DM may exacerbate neurodegeneration in PD. Current treatments for PD and DM can interact, such that dopaminergic drugs used to manage PD may increase the risk of altering glucose uptake, leading to exacerbated insulin resistance. Conversely, anti-diabetic drugs like GLP-1 receptor agonists and metformin may even be neuroprotective. However, not all anti-diabetic drugs provide utility in managing PD symptoms; some merely provide general metabolic change. The interplay between PD and DM remains largely uncharacterized, and due to the overlapping nature of these conditions, expanding the scope of research into novel and/or personalized causes of their combined effects may improve treatment efficacy.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Parkinson, Diabetes mellitus, Antidiabetic, Anti-Parkinson’s drugs

Introduction

The Rising Burden of Parkinson’s Disease

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is an autosomal neuronal degeneration disease accompanied by accumulation of misfolded protein α-synuclein or Lewy bodies in the substantia nigra dopaminergic neuron that involve in the occurring of several PD symptoms including bradykinesia, tremors, stiffness, and abnormal gait contribute in motor-based system and gastrointestinal disturbance, urogenital complication, olfactory function impairment (hyposmia), and cognitive decline are non-motor-based symptoms (He et al. 2020). Hyposmia is prevalent in PD patients and mostly precedes the diagnosis for years, which reflects early accumulation of α-synuclein protein in the olfactory lobe, regarded as a common biomarker for PD pathogenesis (Fullard et al. 2017). The Parkinson’s Foundation (https://www.parkinson.org/understanding-parkinsons/statistics) explains that PD is the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disease, afterwards alzheimer’s disease, and approx. one million patients in the United States of America are living with PD, and this number is expected to be 1.3 million in 20,230 (Luo et al. 2025). As per the global data, more than 10 million people are living with PD. The major risk factors of PD are linked with age, with a higher risk factor in male patients. PTEN-induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1) and parkin modulate free radicals and mitophagy, which activate MDV (mitochondrial-derived vesicle) cross to late endosomes. Growing evidence in rodent PD models demonstrates that PINK1 defects lead to an aggregation of mitochondrial impairments, causing dopaminergic neuronal death in ageing people (Deas et al. 2014). Intriguingly, biochemical and cellular studies report that PINK1 plays a significant role in the mitochondrial antigen presentation suppression (MitAP) against MPTP-induced.

ced rodent models (Matheoud et al. 2016). Cang X et al. demonstrated that PINK1 regulates insulin resistance (IR) in response to free radical generation-induced stress. PINK1 enhances hepatic IR by regulation of JNK/ERK molecular signalling pathways in palmitic acid-treated HepG2 cell lines in response to diabetes therapy (Cang et al. 2016).

Diabetes is a heterogeneous disease distinguished by hyperglycemia that is divided into type-1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) and type-2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and gestational diabetes mellitus. T2DM syndrome is accompanied by a non-autoimmune disease characterised by progressive impairment of insulin secretion from pancreatic β cells in the presence of hormonal changes (insulin resistance) or metabolic-related disease (Lu et al. 2024). T2DM covers approximately. 96% diabetes and are one of the leading noncommunicable chronic syndromes that threaten human well-being. T2DM accounts for approximately 462 million diabetic people or 6.28% of the global population. Epidemiological graphs have demonstrated that T2DM might be a major risk factor for several neurodegenerative diseases, such as diabetic neuropathy, PD, AD, and stroke (Bantounou et al. 2024).

The connection between PD and T2DM was initially identified in 1993, and it was found that PD patients who suffer from concomitant T2DM had severe motor-based PD symptoms and altered drug action (Sandyk 1993). Hence, several observational studies conducted from distinct countries such as the UK (De Pablo-Fernandez et al. 2018), Finland (Hu et al. 2007), the USA (Xu et al. 2011), Taiwan (Yang et al. 2017), and Denmark (Schernhammer et al. 2011) have identified a higher risk (23–85%) of PD patients with concomitant T2DM. Case-control survey claimed that T2DM patients scored excessively on severity scales of PD, and exhibited motor symptoms in approximately. 12 months earlier than PD patients without concomitant T2DM (Cereda et al. 2012a). Furthermore, MRI imaging results have evaluated that PD patients with concomitant T2DM endure from higher cortical atrophy compared to patients with PD alone, especially in the frontal lobes region, which is linked with cognitive decline (Petrou et al. 2016). Subsequently, recent studies have concluded that T2DM diminished the higher risk of PD pathogenesis by different percentage ratios, including 25% (Lu et al. 2014), 48% (Powers et al. 2006), and 62% (Miyake et al. 2010). Ekstrand MI et al., found that MitoPark knockout mice have decreased mtDNA genetic material expression and respiratory chain deficiency in the dopamine (DA) neurons of midbrain regions, causing Parkinsonism phenotypic effects with late and early onset of progressive cognitive function impairment, followed by the intraneuronal inclusions formation dopaminergic neuronal death which is a major pathological hallmark in PD (Ekstrand et al. 2007).

Insulin resistance has been identified in the PD middle brain regions (Morris et al. 2014), such as the substantia nigra, basal ganglia, striatum (putamen and globus pallidus), hippocampus, cerebral cortex, amygdala, and ventral tegmental area, which express enhanced insulin receptors (Athauda and Foltynie 2016a). This may lead to PD pathogenesis by α-synuclein deposition, accumulation, and its clearance (Arnold et al. 2018). Furthermore, insulin plays a significant role in the protection of neurons by insulin receptors, which stimulate downstream signalling pathways accountable for cellular growth, maintenance, synaptic plasticity, post-transcriptional regulation, translational protein modification, and cognitive function impairment (Cheong et al. 2020). Animal model studies have reported that insulin resistance might decrease the dopaminergic neuronal expression (Jones et al. 2017), turnover (Baladi et al. 2015), and reduce insulin-based dopamine production in the putamen and Globus Pallidus (Stouffer et al. 2015).

Evidence of Insulin Resistance in Parkinson’s Disease

Clinical and Experimental Evidence

An initial step to explore the relationship between DM and PD is to evaluate the assessment of epidemiological findings. Two similar meta-analyses have been published in recent decades, which have found interesting results in the same area. In 2011, the initial meta-analysis concluded that DM encompassed a higher risk factor for PD patients, with a risk ratio (RR) value = 1.37 (95% confidence interval (CI) 1.21 to 1.55, P < 0.0001 (Cereda et al. 2011). In 2016, a second prospective study incorporated seven different types of cohort studies with more than 1.7 million people and concluded that the more chances of PD in DM patients was elevated by 38% with total RR value was 1.38 (95%) CI: 1.17 to 1.62; and P < 0.0001, and this values was increased when DM duration was below 10 years and in case of women females RR values was 1.50; 95% CI: 1.07 to 2.11 compared with males RR = 1.40 (95%) CI: 1.17 to 1.67 (Yue et al. 2016). A higher incidence in female patients was demonstrated in different studies despite male preponderance in PD patients (Dorsey et al. 2018), especially in aged subjects 40 to 79 years, odds ratio (OR) = 1.71 (95%), CI: 1.60 to 1.82, P < 0.001, RR = 1.17 (95%), CI: 1.11 to 1.30 and among females more than 80 years old OR = 1.39 (95%), CI: 1.33 to 1.46, P < 0.001; RR = 1.09 (95%), CI: 1.01 to 1.18 (Deischinger et al. 2021). We estimated that this result might be linked with the protective role of sex steroidal hormone, including 17β-estradiol acts on PI3K/Akt (phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase/protein kinase B) and MAPK/ERK (mitogen-activated protein kinase/extracellular-signal regulated kinase) molecular signalling pathways. Nevertheless, in the neurodegenerative disease in central Spain people study (De Pablo-Fernandez et al. 2017), the interlink connection found between PD and DM was not statistically remarkable OR = 1.89 (95%), CI: 0.90 to 3.98, P = 0.09 (Connolly et al. 2015), moreover, patients with chronic DM of > 10 years showed significant positive connection OR = 3.27 (95%), CI: 1.2 to 8.85, P = 0.02. Recent research included T2DM patients, and the available research outcome data of T1DM patients are restricted. Klimek et al. reported that Austria claims the clinical study dataset with the cohort study on 1.8 million patients, linked with PD and DM, was identified for T1DM RR = 2.3 (95%), CI: 1.9 to 2.7, and T2DM RR = 1.5 (95%), CI: 1.4 to 1.6 (Klimek et al. 2015). Nevertheless, the article integrates only people with inpatient stays, possible individual selection criteria bias might be present. Witoelar et al., revealed that GWAS (genome-wide association studies) data weakly correlated between T1DM and PD, a higher risk of T1DM might be because of chronic disease and not due to several key factors, including autoimmune and genetic disease agents (Witoelar et al. 2017). To illustrate the feasible effects in case of DM disease duration, a large number of Korean individual cohort study including more than 15 million people, identified the PD risk factors in four different groups, such as nondiabetic, altered fasting glucose levels, < 5 years diabetes duration, and > 5 years diabetes duration (Rhee et al. 2020). PD risk factor significantly higher with the diabetes duration and higher glucose levels (DM < 5 years HR (hazard ratio) = 1.185 (95%), CI: 1.143 to 1.229, and DM > 5 years HR = 1.618 (95%), CI: 1.566 to 1.672 and also found higher for altered fasting glucose levels in patients with HR = 1.038 (95%), CI: 1.009 to 1.067 (Labandeira et al. 2022).

Pagano et al. have reported a case-control study for 78 individuals, which described that 3 years of T2DM was linked with higher progression and worsening motor scores, which reduced the DAT (dopamine transporter) in dopaminergic neurons, enhancement of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) α-synuclein protein levels and cognitive impairment in PD individuals (Pagano et al. 2018). However, non-PD diabetic subjects evaluated lower DAT levels in dopaminergic neurons and higher α-synuclein protein in CSF relative to normal subjects. Moreover, the DM and PD individuals had aged people, and prolonged disease duration, potential diabetic severity effects or prolonged duration were not estimated. Another case-control study over 178 individuals > over 3 years, identified that 97.8% T2DM patients before the onset of PD denoted worse disease severity in motor scores and daily routine activities of the MDS-UPDRS (Movement Disorder Society-Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale) with highly advanced Hoehn-Yahr stage and required higher L-dopa dosage (Cereda et al. 2012b). A current study observed that these results outcome in only T2DM controlled subjects, although this individual group had aged, onset and chronic cognitive function associated with aged people (Ou et al. 2021).

Insights from Animal Models

The impairment of insulin signalling to PD is that it appears to be linked with lower clinical pathological outcome. IR in T2DM patients with PD is closely linked with a more critical phenotype, driven disease pathogenesis and enhances the higher risk of cognitive impairment. In an animal model, it is closely associated with elevation in degeneration of dopaminergic neurons, motor, and cognitive decline (Morris et al. 2010). Regardless the paucity of animal models that combine both disease T2DM and PD pathogenesis in a single animal model, such as IR and T2DM rodent models that possess not only metabolic alterations, including peripheral IR, but also IR in neuronal cells, which permits them to be employed to analyze the connection between both T2DM and PD (Reagan 2012). Recent studies have reported that high fat diet in transgenic or normal mice induced alteration of insulin signalling pathways and IR have an enhancement of vulnerability to 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA) and 1-Methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP) toxins cause markedly enhance nigrostriatal neuronal death and inhibition of dopaminergic neuronal signalling (Morris et al. 2011a), leads to elevation of motor and cognitive deficits compared to control groups (Choi et al. 2005). The relevance to PD progression can be worsened by IR. DM mice animal models MPTP-treated displayed affirmation of hastened stimulation of glial cells and reduction of dopaminergic neurons, followed by excessive pro-inflammatory cytokines production and α-synuclein within pancreas and midbrain regions (Morris et al. 2011b; Wang et al. 2014a, b). Furthermore, IR might also be associated with the progression of non-motor symptoms. Recent studies identified that brain or NIRKO (neuronal specific insulin receptor knockout) transgenic mice evolve age-based anxiety and depressive symptoms, due to alteration in mitochondrial function and enzymes, including monoamine oxidase A and B (MAO-A and B) function, resulting in enhancement of dopamine levels, causing impairment of insulin pathways (Kleinridders et al. 2015). In addition, insulin signalling restoration might revive these neuropsychiatric behaviours. Regulating high-fat diet supplements in animal models and utilizing insulin-sensitizing compounds have been shown to prevent depression-like symptoms (Yamada et al. 2011; Sharma et al. 2012). Studies on knockout mice models upregulating Phosphoprotein Enriched in Diabetes/Astrocytes-15 (PED/PEA-15) cytosolic protein were induced in designing animal models of motor and cerebral cortex deficits associated with T2DM pathogenesis (Perruolo et al. 2016). These transgenic mouse models developed IR followed by the death of dopaminergic neurons in the cerebral striatum, leading to hypokinetic symptoms similar to PD.

Physiological Role of Insulin in the Brain

In general, insulin was primarily thought of as a peripherally produced hormone that regulates glucose homeostasis through glucose uptake stimulation in adipose tissue and muscle, and glucose secretion inhibition by the liver and fatty acid secretion by adipose cells (James Martins 2015; Martins 2017a, b). Moreover, it is clearly stated that insulin also serves as a homeostatic key factor in the brain, required for regulating its physiological activities (Athauda and Foltynie 2016b). The insulin source in the brain is still unknown. It is mostly thought to be obtained from β-cells of the pancreas and transferred to the brain regions, including CSF, directly distributed across the brainstem (area postrema) (Strazielle and Ghersi-Egea 2013). Moreover, insulin and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) receptors are also secreted by pyramidal neurons, which are present in the cerebral cortex, olfactory lobe, and hippocampus, where IGF-1 receptor densities are excessive (Kuwabara et al. 2011). Similarly, peripheral tissue of the CNS, insulin has not directly affected neuronal glucose uptake into CNS peripheral tissue, but plays a significant role in the modulation of several functions in the brain by downstream pathways and enhances cell survival. Insulin is attached to the Insulin receptor (IR) α-subunit, which stimulates the β-subunit of tyrosine kinase function, leading to autophosphorylation of the β-subunit its own amino acid residues and tyrosine residues phosphorylation of a docked protein known as insulin receptor substrate (IRS) is divided into six different proteins: IRS-1 to IRS-6. Hence, IRS-1 to 4 are mainly present on C-terminal regions, while IRS-5 and 6 are less phosphorylation in the context of insulin (Ghasemi et al. 2013). This suggests that regulating IRS protein stability acts as a critical connection between insulin signalling and the determination of the extent of insulin function. Stimulation of downstream molecular signalling can be divided into 2 main pathways: mitogen associated protein kinase (Raf-1/MEK-MAP-K) extracellular signal regulated kinase (ERK), and PI3K/Akt (protein kinase B) molecular pathways that can regulate several downstream pathways, such as mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), glycogen synthase kinase-3B (GSK-3B), caspase-9, and transcription factor FoxO (FOXO1) (Hirsch et al. 2013). These distinct signalling pathways modulate multiple functions that are altered in PD, such as neuroinflammation, autophagy, protein aggregation, apoptosis, nerve death, and synaptic plasticity (Schapira 2008a). However, it might be predictable that insulin pathways have been identified to eventually increase neuronal survival. Furthermore, by glucose-sensing neurons in the hypothalamus, insulin modulates weight gain, energy homeostasis, and peripheral degradation of lipid and glucose, while impairment of brain insulin pathways can potentially cause brain alterations in energy metabolism (Marino et al. 2011).

Insulin Signalling in PD Pathogenesis- the Akt Significance

Whether IR occurs as a main root cause of neuronal death is a fundamental hallmark. Akt plays a significant role in the downregulation of insulin pathways and acts as a major factor in physiological responses to control ageing. It phosphorylates more than 50 downstream targets in which Akt acts as a major factor of cellular activity, and in spite of several protein substrates that act to enhance cellular survival time duration (Greene et al. 2011). Fundamental studies reveal that regulation of Akt signalling pathways is closely associated with multiple age-related neurodegenerative diseases and metabolic diseases such as alzheimer’s disease (AD), PD, and T2DM (Griffin et al. 2005). Recent studies also display that impaired Akt signalling pathways might be a major factor for PD pathogenesis, exhibiting a potential correlation between IR in T2DM and neuronal death in PD (Greene et al. 2011). Post-mortem studies of PD brain reported that inhibition of Akt phosphorylation and total Akt gene expression compared with normal, and unattached polymorphism in Akt that integrates Akt (Xiromerisiou et al. 2008), may enhance the risk of PD and T2DM progression (Malagelada et al. 2008; Timmons et al. 2009). Furthermore, mutation of PINK1 (an autosomal recessive allele of PD) is linked with a reduction of Akt function. Rodent models result in the representation that Akt signalling pathway inhibition causes dopaminergic neuronal death (Xu et al. 2014; Canal et al. 2014), and impairment of Akt molecular signalling pathways might alter α-synuclein protein expression in PD (Kim et al. 2011). However, cellular death in PD might not be common as the reduction of insulin receptor activates Akt phosphorylation, resulting in IR. Subsequently, Akt negative feedback control is required to regulate optimum cellular function. Likewise, Akt is generally ineffective against the FOXO gene, and its physiological function is a crucial counterbalance to permit required stress response genes transcription and system repairing (Kloet and Burgering 2011; O’ Neill 2013). In addition, the study emphasises that reduction of PI3K/Akt activation may lead to lifespan enhancement (O’ Neill 2013); hence, sustained stimulation of Akt signalling pathways has been closely associated with cancer pathogenesis and exacerbation of AD-based symptoms (Griffin et al. 2005). This can elaborate the paradoxical result outcome from recent studies that displayed inhibited insulin pathway increases yeast, drosophila, mice, and worms’ longevity (Blüher et al. 2003; Van Heemst et al. 2005; Taguchi et al. 2007; Pawlikowska et al. 2009), and reduced the ageing effects in animal models (Selman et al. 2008).

α-Synuclein Aggregation and Autophagy

Impairment of lysosomal function, alteration of normal mechanisms by which cells disrupt abnormal protein or autophagy (cellular constituents), and α-synuclein protein aggregation into toxic proteins are targeted as significant steps within the process that cause dopaminergic neuronal death in PD. Insulin molecular pathways have been shown to regulate lysosomal function, which in turn enhances PD progression (Matsuzaki et al. 2010; Willette et al. 2015). Recent studies have reported that insulin pathways can regulate α-synuclein protein degradation by stimulation of IDE (insulin-degrading enzyme), which is a zinc-metallo endopeptidase that may disrupt insulin and similar low molecular weight peptides, which create β-pleated motifs by PI3K stimulation accompanied by IR stimulation. IDE may reduce the α-synuclein protein aggregation via attachment of the α-synuclein protein, inhibiting its formation of fibrils (Sharma et al. 2015). Subsequently, insulin signalling activation by IGF-1 or reversion of IR reduces α-synuclein aggregation and formation of toxic fibrils. Stimulation of the Akt signalling pathway, accompanied by insulin molecular signalling leading to GSK-3β phosphorylation at Ser9 residues, results in inhibition. GSK-3β acts as a multifunctional targets that increase signalling pathways that enhance apoptosis, neuroinflammation, mitochondrial function impairment, and α-synuclein protein expression (Lewy body aggregation) (Golpich et al. 2015). GSK-3β inhibition enhances autophagy and has been shown to prevent α-synuclein aggregation and expression, followed by neurotoxic effects reported by in-vitro and in-vivo studies(Duka et al. 2009; Yuan et al. 2015). Higher GSK-3β expression has been identified in PD individuals and PD rodent models linked with higher α-synuclein aggregation (Wills et al. 2010; Hernandez-Baltazar et al. 2013). In a negative feedback, higher expression of α-synuclein may also reduce IRS-1, leading to increased stimulation of GSK-3β function, causing deleterious effects (Duka et al. 2009). Autophagy impairment is also involved in neuronal death in PD. A major key target that downregulates IR/PI3K/Akt signalling pathways is mTOR kinase, which stimulates Akt and inactivation of increased autophagy, enhanced cellular survival and growth. Furthermore, impairment of these signalling pathways has been shown in patients and PD rodent models with linked autophagy dysfunction and anomalous α-synuclein clearance (Heras-Sandoval et al. 2014). Two mTOR complexes, mTORC1, play a significant role in the modulation of autophagy (inhibition and degradation of IRS-1 protein), and mTORC2 may stimulate Akt pathways. However, insulin stimulation by PI3K/Akt/mTOR molecular signalling pathways may enhance cellular growth and inactivate increased autophagy caused cellular death, hence inactivating mTORC1 may increase α-synuclein protein autophagy and enhance cell survival by mTORC2 expression of Akt(Heras-Sandoval et al. 2014) Fig. 1. Subsequently, similar research reported that drugs targeting mTORC1 inhibition, such as rapamycin may reduce α-synuclein aggregation in PD rodent models, and are being identified Aβ and tau protein accumulation in AD rodent models, identified as potential neuroprotective effects (Tain et al. 2009; Caccamo et al. 2010; Stoica et al. 2011). Reduction of insulin sensitivity has been revealed to enhance AD-related pathological symptoms in AD rodent models. Recent in-vivo experimental studies have revealed that IR can contribute to Aβ accumulation in specific brain regions, such as the frontal and temporal lobes in asymptomatic PD patients (Willette et al. 2015). Nagao H et al. demonstrated that IR preferentially activates phosphorylation linked with mTORC1/Akt molecular signalling pathways, while IGF-1 preferentially activates protein phosphorylation linked with Rho GTPase (Ras homolog family of guanosine triphosphatase hydrolase) and cellular progression (Nagao et al. 2021). Remarkably, insulin or IGF-1 can enhance Aβ clearance and trafficking from the central nervous system (CNS) via upregulation of Aβ expression in the CSF, through PI3K/MAPK molecular signalling pathways activation (Carro et al. 2002; Ashpole et al. 2015). Correspondingly, mention the therapeutic process, these effects can be inhibited with insulin signalling pathways restoration by exogenous insulin (Jolivalt et al. 2008).

Mitochondrial Dysfunction and Cerebral Glucose Metabolism

Nigro-striatal neurons require excessive energy as compared with other cells, and are susceptible to events that impair mitochondrial function. Similarly, recent studies reported that this process may alter mitochondrial activity, such as disrupt mitophagy, excessive aggregation of mtDNA mutations, and interfere with complex-1 respiratory chain, impairment of mitochondrial calcium homeostasis, and excessive oxidative stress, which are linked with PD progression (Schapira 2008b). Correspondingly, stimulation of IR/PI3K/Akt molecular signalling pathways, insulin signalling may regulate mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) function via reducing FoxO1/HMOX1 expression and modulating the NAD+/NADPH ratio, that interfere the Sirtuin 1 or Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (SIRT1/PGC-1α) signalling pathway (Martins 2016, 2017a, b), and is a key master regulator of mitochondrial network expansion (mitochondrial biogenesis) (Cheng et al. 2010). Subsequently, IR rodent models revealed that alterations in mitochondrial proteins within substantia nigra pars compacta (Khang et al. 2015), and evaluated suppression of mitochondrial complex 1 levels and impaired calcium homeostasis (Duarte et al. 2013). The result negatively regulates mitochondrial network expansion, including membrane depolarization, causing production of excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), oxidative stress, and excessive neuronal death (Kleinridders et al. 2015). Furthermore, suppressing PGC-1α expression is the major key factor for IR (Mootha et al. 2003), and significant evidence demonstrated that PGC-1α function impairment may play a significant role in PD progression. GWAS studies suggest that PGC-1α downstream responsive genes are identified in the early stage of PD patients (Zheng et al. 2010). PGC-1α polymorphism is linked with a higher risk in early onset of PD patients (Clark et al. 2011), and cell line studies indicated that reduction of PGC-1α expression is linked with excessive α-synuclein protein aggregation. Subsequently, excessive expression or stimulation of PGC-1α prevents dopaminergic neurons from using MPTP-induced neuronal death in rodent PD models, which enhances mitochondrial biogenesis and halts α-synuclein-induced neuronal death (Zheng et al. 2010). Recently growing evidence reported that chronic IR in transgenic mice abrogates the Parkin-PARIS-PGC-1α molecular signalling pathways, leads to reduction of parkin expression, PARIS aggregation, PGC-1α downregulation, causes dopaminergic neuronal death, and enhances their vulnerability to 1-Methyl-4-phenylpyridinium (MPP+), displaying a putative signalling pathway associated with IR, mitochondrial dysfunction, and dopaminergic neuronal death (Khang et al. 2015). There may close relationship between neuronal death, insulin pathways, and glucose utilisation within the brain. Recent studies demonstrated using FDG-PET (2-[¹⁸F]fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose Positron Emission Tomography) imaging analysis in the PD patients, who exhibit more extensive cortical hypometabolism than normal, which is apparent in the early stage of PD (Borghammer et al. 2010). Consequently, cognitive deficits in PD patients or rodent models can also be associated with excessive cerebral glucose metabolism. Remarkably, glucose hypometabolism in a specific region of the brain, including the frontal and parietal lobes evaluated in Parkinson’s Disease-Mild Cognitive Impairment (PD-MCI) and Parkinson’s Disease Dementia (PDD) patients compared to age-matched normal patients(Hosokai et al. 2009; Liepelt et al. 2009) and may analyze cognitive decline (Dunn et al. 2014). The majority of studies have demonstrated that in healthy people, peripheral IR is linked with cerebral glucose hypometabolism in the brain areas, including frontal, parietal, and cingulate lobes (Baker et al. 2011), and can identify critical memory function (Willette et al. 2015b). Moreover, heterogeneous causes of PD pathophysiology under cognitive deficits, cerebral glucose hypometabolism is also found in the early stage of AD. Studies have suggested that IR plays a key role as a potential biomarker in cognitive deficits and PD patients (Willette et al. 2015b). Reduction of cerebral glucose metabolism leads to enhancement in intracellular adenosine di and triphosphate (ADP/ATP), inhibiting potassium channels that regulate dopamine hormone secretion in dopaminergic neurons, and increasing the risk of cognitive decline (Willette et al. 2015b). However, the cerebral glucose hypometabolism might not directly alter neuronal glucose utilization; this suppression of cerebral glucose may initiate as a secondary cause of suppression of postsynaptic glutamate neurotransmission (causes inhibit insulin molecular signalling) and other neurotransmitters activate glucose utilization in the brain (Talbot and Wang 2014a). SIRT1 activators may be designed and developed with an excellent pharmacokinetic and tolerability drug profile for potential therapy paradigms specifically for neuroinflammatory disease. Although selective SIRT1 inhibitors selisistat, known as Ex-527/SEN0014196, entered clinical trials on healthy volunteers and Huntington’s disease patients (Dai et al. 2018).

Oxidative Stress and Neuroinflammation

Abnormal insulin molecular signalling pathways may also be associated with extracellular relevance factors to neurodegenerative disease. Inflammation is a major hallmark and plays a significant role in PD progression. Epidemiological research identifies that nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory disease (NSAIDS) drugs use contributes to the reduction of PD progression risk factors. Positron emission tomography (PET) imaging analysis in PD patients displays excessive microglial cell activation and enhanced release of pro-inflammatory mediators within the substantia nigra pars compacta at post-mortem PD patient examination. In the initial stage, microglial activation plays a protective role, while prolonged stimulation disrupts disease pathogenesis, and may be further reproduced by α-synuclein, pro-inflammatory mediators, and neuronal degeneration, a key factor in dopaminergic neuron death in PD. Several pathways are involved in insulin signalling-mediated microglial stimulation. Growing evidence suggests that PI3K/MAPK by NADPH oxidase stimulation may prevent the microglial function and following dopaminergic neuronal death in PD (Wang et al. 2011). A major downregulation of the IR/PI3K/Akt molecular pathway is NF-κB (nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells). As a transcription mediator that integrates the microglial cytokine response, as a key regulator for pro-inflammatory gene expression. NF-κB upregulation has been mediated in the dopaminergic neuroinflammation in PD progression. NF-κB functional enhancement has been demonstrated in dopaminergic TH+ neurons, microglial cells, and astrocytes within the substantia nigra of PD patients and rodent models (Hunot et al. 1997), and NF-κB inhibition acts as a neuroprotective effect in the PD models (Zhang et al. 2010). However, extensive research articles explore the correlation between NF-κB and other pro-inflammatory mediators, which can directly stimulate NF-κB (Oeckinghaus et al. 2011). Current studies reported that Akt leads to upregulation of IkBα, which is an endogenous NF-κB inhibitor, leading to inhibition of neuroinflammation (Khasnavis et al. 2012), representing a correlated pathway between neuroinflammation and insulin signalling. IR due to chronic glucose dysmetabolism may cause the generation of AGEs (advanced glycation endproducts). RAGE (receptor for advanced glycation endproducts) interaction regulates distinct intracellular molecular signalling pathways, such as stimulation of cytokines mediated NF-κB transcription factors, causing enhancement of oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and neurodegeneration. RAGE and AGE concentrations are higher within the cortex of PD, and also enhance α-synuclein accumulation and Lewy bodies formation, thus increasing neurodegeneration (Dalfó et al. 2005; Li et al. 2012). Sodium glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors reduce the acute cardiovascular death and heart failure in diabetic patients on the AGEs. Methylglyoxal-based hydroimidazolone-1 (MG-H1) is a SGLT2 inhibitors that reduce HbA1c blood levels and AGEs are linked with heart failure and cardiovascular disease.

Synaptic Dysfunction and Neurogenesis

The clinical symptoms of PD pathogenesis due to neuronal death followed by abnormal firing of associated neuronal signalling pathways cause synaptic dysfunction and are linked with the onset of PD (Picconi et al. 2012; Schroll et al. 2014). The mTORC1 function, regulated by insulin, has been implicated to modulate synaptic function via regulating synapse density and modulating PSD95 biomarker, which is required as the scaffold for biomarker for the generation of synaptic junction and dopamine-based synaptic plasticity and behavioural activity (Chiu et al. 2008). Aberrant synaptic plasticity has also been linked with motor dysfunction of PD, including levodopa-based dyskinesia (Calabresi et al. 2013), and is partially involved in the progression of cognitive dysfunction (Picconi et al. 2012). The hippocampal brain regions play a significant role in memory formation integrators, several evidence reports that hippocampal brain region structure and function are contributed in cognitive deficits in PD patients (Bouchard et al. 2008; Costa et al. 2012), positively interlink with cognitive decline and abnormal behavioural activities (Bouchard et al. 2008), may identify in the PDD progression (Pan et al. 2013). Additionally, a higher energy requirement for hippocampal neurons makes them highly vulnerable to IR alterations (Fehm et al. 2006). Studies have reported that IR and T2DM are linked with a higher risk of cognitive deficits and AD. The insulin function in regulating cognitive deficits is reported via higher density of IR within brain regions, including cerebral cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala, which is identified as upregulation in the case of spatial cognition training. Additionally, acute insulin administration enhances memory tasks in rodent models, and improves verbal cognition and behavioural in normal humans due to stimulation of hippocampal insulin signalling (BlÃ¡zquez et al. 2014). Moreover, conflicting results of studies using transgenic NIRKO mice reports without changes in cognitive function demonstrate insulin plays a major role in memory and while associated molecular signalling pathways is still elusive (Schubert et al. 2004), evidences report hippocampal IR is a major risk factor for memory alteration and loss of memory in AD (Biessels and Reagan 2015). Insulin effects on dementia appear to be based on PI3K/MAPK molecular signalling pathway stimulation (McNay et al. 2010), and may be linked via its capability to regulate synaptic function, neuronal transmission, and its density, causing neurogenesis (Steculorum et al. 2014). Rodent supplements high-fat diets to activate neuronal IR display reduction of PSD95 expression, a scaffolding biomarker, an increase in post-synaptic dysfunction and synaptopodin, which is an actin-based biomarker higher in cortical and hippocampal neurons, leading to the impairment of working cognition (Arnold et al. 2014). Stimulation of PI3K/MAPK/ERK molecular signalling pathways also integrates glutamate and GABA (γ-aminobutyric acid), increases protein formation and regulates dendrite stabilization, which is needed for hippocampus LTP (long-term potentiation) and cognitive decline (Zhao and Townsend 2009). Post-mortem evidence reports that aging reduces neurogenesis in adult people (O’Keeffe et al. 2009), mainly caused by dopaminergic neuronal depletion, having a negative impact on cellular proliferation (Regensburger et al. 2014; Lamm et al. 2014), and hence the correlation between neurogenesis within the hippocampus is still elusive. The neurogenesis alteration within the hippocampus of PD patients may be associated with cognitive alteration in cognitive processing, olfaction and depression (Regensburger et al. 2014). The insulin receptor stimulation by PI3K/Akt molecular pathway has a positive impact on the formation of neurogenesis in IR and T2DM rodent models, contributing to hippocampal and neurogenesis alteration (Ramos-Rodriguez et al. 2014). Insulin receptor can arbitrate multiple cellular functions involved in PD progression and the outcome of rodent models demonstrates that IR may lead to PD pathogenesis.

Molecular Causes of Insulin Resistance and BBB Permeability in PD

Comprehensive evidence has suggested that IR is linked with neurodegenerative disease; we must find the answer to whether IR plays a significant role in neurodegeneration. Hence, the major cause of brain insulin signalling pathways alteration in PD progression is still unknown; current GWAS have identified a strong connection between neuroinflammation, PD, and diabetes (Moran and Graeber 2008). However, two major causes identified in recent studies demonstrating IR in AD, which represent IRS-1 impairment is a significant causes of IR. This pathology is supported by several studies representing IRS-1 serine phosphorylation (IRS-1 pS) level enhancement within the cortex and hippocampus of AD people (Talbot and Wang 2014b). Current studies have found that IRS-1 pS level elevation in the substantia nigra in PD patients and rodent PD models. Recent evidence reported that peripheral and neuronal IR in AD, both cause enhanced metabolic stress and Aβ activate the release of pro-inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, that phosphorylate and stimulate IRS-1 serine enzyme, such as IKK, JNK, and Erk2. This results in IRS-1 serine active residues phosphorylation, leading to the reduction and inactivation of the insulin receptor, causing both peripheral and neuronal IR development (De Felice et al. 2014).

The insulin pathway plays a significant role in midbrain dopaminergic neuronal activity through distinct neuroprotective pathways. The growing evidence strongly suggests that local brain insulin formation exists in the 1/10th part, with most insulin crossing the BBB via specialized BBB transport systems (Bassil et al. 2022). The transport occurs through saturable receptor-mediated strategies that are dynamically regulated by metabolic processes. Its brain insulin expression atlases display limited local insulin secretion between both mRNA and protein levels (Rhea and Banks 2019). Instead, substantial evidence confirms that insulin primarily enters the brain by the BBB, a specialized, saturable transport system with remarkably higher permeability coefficients 105–390-fold higher than albumin in different brain regions (Nguyen et al. 2023). Upon reaching the brain, insulin exerts neuroprotective effects on dopaminergic neurons by stimulating PI3K/Akt/GSK-3β molecular signalling pathways, which inhibit MPP⁺-induced toxicity by reducing free radical release, reactive oxygen species formation, and calcium ion influx (Duarte et al. 2008). Insulin also enhances mitochondrial function by improving dehydrogenase enzyme function, membrane potential, and reducing mitochondrial swelling, while elevating tyrosine hydroxylase levels, which are required for dopamine synthesis. The relationship between insulin signalling and PD is highly recognized, with growing evidence showing higher IRS-1pS312 (a marker of insulin resistance) in nigral dopaminergic neurons of PD patients and co-localization within Lewy bodies, while animal models expressing human α-synuclein display similar insulin resistance markers (Bassil et al. 2022).

Similar molecular signalling pathways may support the mechanism of IR in PD. α-synuclein has been shown to enhance IRS-1 pS result in inhibition of insulin receptor signalling, representing α-synuclein may unfavourably modulate insulin receptor (Gao et al. 2015). The molecular signalling pathways that negatively support this mechanism are still unclear, while evidence reports that α-synuclein leads to sustained stimulation of mTORC1, increasing the insulin receptor negative feedback mechanism and enhancing IRS-1 degradation, PP2A (protein phosphatase 2 A) inhibition activity, generally associated with mTORC1/S6K1 inhibition via a negative feedback loop of IRS-1 disrupting pathways (Gao et al. 2015). Consequently, the vicious pathway can be exacerbated by α-synuclein-based pro-inflammatory mediator generation. Post mortem PD result outcomes have also been displayed that overexpression of pro-inflammatory mediators, such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, within CSF and substantia nigra of PD patients (Dobbs et al. 1999; Reale et al. 2009). The connection between both low-grade neuroinflammation and insulin receptor is involved in generation of neuronal and peripheral IR, and similarly peripherally identified cytokines have also been reported to alter the PD progression (Perry et al. 2007). A current meta-analysis result demonstrated that peripheral pro-inflammatory mediators in PD patients possess that excessive peripheral concentration of cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, IL-10, and C-reactive protein) in PD patients compared with normal individuals (Qin et al. 2016). Furthermore, current evidence of newly diagnosed PD patients displayed that higher concentration of pro-inflammatory mediators, including IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-10, and IL-6, were linked with lesser cognitive decline assessment values and higher motor decline, while PD patients with excessive anti-inflammatory markers IL-4 and IL-13 were involved with improvement of cognitive decline and motor function stability (Williams-Gray et al. 2016). Taken together with the modulation in normal insulin signalling or IR, cause reduction of Akt homeostasis and neuroprotective effect via FOXO1 stimulation and GSK-3β inhibition, resulting in α-synuclein exacerbation in PD patients and rodent PD models pathogenesis and neurodegeneration. The gut microbiota (GM) is widely recognized as a mechanistic link between neurodegeneration and metabolic syndrome. Microbial dysbiosis, alteration of microbial metabolite drug profiles, and impaired intestinal barrier function have been implicated in both T2DM and PD progression (Byndloss et al. 2024). A molecular target investigation and microbiota activity may provide deeper insight into disease-specific molecular mechanisms, which offer potential treatment strategies, including neuroinflammation, metabolic stress, and brain communication. Diabetic individuals’ GM shows a higher pathogenic and opportunistic gram-negative strains than the commensal ones (Pedersen et al. 2016). Although an enhancement of bacterial pathogens, including Clostridiales, Lactobacilli, Escherichia coli, Bacteroides caccae, Prevotella copri, and Bacteroides vulgates has also been identified in diabetic people. Bacteroides (gram-negative bacteria) their presence linked with lipopolysaccharides (LPS) enhancement; hence, their reduction is linked with minimal metabolic endotoxemia, lower inflammatory markers, and Proteobacteria are mainly pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α, involved in the formation of IR and T2DM (Crudele et al. 2023). GM regulates immune cells’ differentiation and function in the intestine, periphery tissues, and brain (Erny et al. 2015; Matcovitch-Natan et al. 2016). Subsequently, anti-glycemic drugs recently used for diabetes therapy might provide indirect beneficial effects in PD patients by regulating metabolic load, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, neuronal, and peripheral IR, which seems to be closely linked with PD progression and drugs that may dramatically cross the BBB and regulate the insulin molecular pathway. A recent study from rodent PD models has reported this strategy: results identify that excessive IGF-1 expression prevents dopaminergic neuronal death from MPTP and 6-ODHA-induced neuronal death, and via α-synuclein-induced dopaminergic neuronal toxicity (Krishnamurthi et al. 2004; Zhang et al. 2015; Ayadi et al. 2016). Neuroprotective effect was achieved by Ser473 (phospho-Akt) enhancement and GSK-3β inhibition (Ayadi et al. 2016). Neuroprotective effects were identified by elevated and development in motor and cognitive behavioural activity deficits. Metformin

Metformin has long been utilized in the treatment of T2DM. Metformin is the most widely prescribed medication for anti-diabetes and has been reported to exert beneficial actions on PD models. It is particularly found to inhibit substantia nigra dopaminergic neuron degeneration, promote striatal dopaminergic function, and reverse motor impairments in a mouse PD model caused by combined MPTP and probenecid (Ruderman et al. 2013). There is also some indication that metformin may be neuroprotective by correcting parkin function, an E3 ubiquitin ligase. The suggestion arises from evidence demonstrating that parkin expression is severely reduced in the substantia nigra of rodent diabetes models, leading to downregulation of PGC-1α and overaccumulation of substrates bound to parkin (Kim et al. 2014). Further, Caenorhabditis elegans PD models have shown that mitochondrial hyperactivity is an early event in PD pathogenesis. Metformin treatment normalized mitochondrial respiration, with associated improvements in motor function and neuronal survival (Sanz et al. 2021). In addition, co-treatment with metformin in a 6-OHDA-induced PD mouse model potentiated L-DOPA-induced dyskinesia by activating AMPK and BDNF signaling pathways in the striatum of the lesioned hemisphere (Ruderman et al. 2013). Metformin persuades AMPK stimulation has been reported to support growing evidence that proposed metformin is capable of lowering α-synuclein levels in-vitro as well as in-vivo assay by downstream mTOR inhibition and elevated PP2A activity, resulting in elevation of α-synuclein Ser129 dephosphorylation activity (Pérez-Revuelta et al. 2014). Ryu YK et al. reported that metformin co-treatment regulates L-DOPA-induced dyskinesia in the 6-OHDA-induced PD rodent model by activation of GSK-3B/Akt, AMPK, and BDNF molecular signalling pathways in the striatum of PD patients (Ryu et al. 2020).

The anti-glycemic drugs’ effects on insulin resistance and progression of PD

Glucagon-Like Peptide-1 (GLP-1) Receptor Analogs

Although structurally different from insulin, GLP-1 analogues stimulate molecular pathways that connect with the insulin receptor pathway and assist insulin receptors. GLP-1 analogues are linked to a wide range of cellular actions, including neuronal development, cellular proliferation, anti-apoptosis, and reduced oxidative stress in the PD brain (Lovshin and Drucker 2009). GLP-1 exerts its action through the GLP-1 receptor, and its mRNA is expressed throughout the brain (Kuang et al. 2024). Activation of GLP-1R upon binding inhibits GSK-3β, thereby decreasing α-synuclein aggregation. It also inhibits FOXO1, which promotes cellular survival and protects against apoptosis. Activation of GLP-1 receptor also inhibits NF-κB activation (Holst et al. 2011), leading to microglial suppression and a reduced production of pro-inflammatory mediators. Non-acylated incretin receptor agonists, such as exendin-4 and lixisenatide, have received particular attention in PD because they can cross the BBB and exert neuroprotective effects (Drucker et al. 2010). Exendin-4 was shown in preclinical models to reverse dopaminergic neuronal death in rodent PD models induced by 6-OHDA and LPS. Comparative studies of liraglutide and lixisenatide have demonstrated that these molecules are more potent in PD models, even superior to exendin-4. Dual GLP-1 or Glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptor agonists like DA3-CH have recently been identified to be superior in diabetes with minimal side effects (Aguirre-Vidal et al. 2024). Other dual receptor agonists, such as DA-JC1, DA-JC4, and DACH5, in addition to reversing MPTP-induced motor impairments and dopaminergic neuronal loss in PD models, reduce proinflammatory cytokines, thus further bestowing them with neuroprotective properties (Hölscher 2024). The GLP-1 agonist drugs such as liraglutide, exenatide, and exendin-4 display neuroprotective effects by several molecular signalling pathways, including regulation of insulin sensitivity, oxidative stress reduction, and improvement of mitochondrial activity (Chai et al. 2024; Lv et al. 2024). The agonist drugs stimulate the PI3K/Akt/AMPK signalling pathways, which increase mitochondrial biogenesis and enhance neuronal growth. The clinical trial study of exenatide with 45 patients, 48 days of administration of exenatide and 12 weeks of washout time duration exhibited major improvements in cognitive motor function, which persisted even without administration of the drug (Aviles-Olmos et al. 2013). GLP-1 receptor agonists have not been linked with the enhancement of cardiovascular risk in T2DM patients. Moreover, an ultimate long-term safety agonist drug profile in case of cardiovascular effects, pancreatitis, pancreatic cancer, caused by incretin-mimetic drugs, cannot be suggested due to dispersed evidence of this type of side effects (Filippatos et al. 2014). LIXIPARK (lixisenetide) showed common adverse effects were gastrointestinal effects reported by Pratley et al. 2014; and slow gastric emptying effects via stimulation of the vagus nerve and suppression of central appetite. Growing meta-analysis results reported that there may be a higher risk of myocardial ischemic events with rosiglitazone treatment, which has ignited debate and instilled uncertainty in the case of TZDs in diabetes therapy (Erdmann et al. 2009). As a result, newer therapeutic agents may also be worthy to be studied for their potential therapeutic effects in PD therapy.

Thiazolidinediones (TZDs)

The TZD class drugs enhance insulin sensitivity by acting as peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) receptor inhibitors. PPARγ is highly expressed in insulin-sensitive cells, including both liver and pancreas, while also present in specific brain regions such as nigral and putaminal nuclei (Swanson and Emborg 2014). The TZD drugs have been reported to inhibit inflammation, exert neuroprotective effects, and possess antioxidant effects in mouse models (Corona and Duchen 2015). Possible drug mechanisms for this drug’s failure to translate despite a pre-clinical result outcome can be due to pioglitazone’s act as poor BBB penetration (Maeshiba et al. 1997). Pioglitazone is the P-glycoprotein (P-gp) substrate, a primary drug efflux transport across the BBB, and current studies have demonstrated it may play a significant role as a stereoselective barrier to markedly limit the pioglitazone cross in the brain (Chang et al. 2015). Cumulative evidence demonstrated that TZD drugs, including pioglitazone and rosiglitazone, exhibited neuroprotective effects in MPTP-induced rodent PD models, leading to enhancement of motor function and behavioural response (Laloux et al. 2012). The clinical trial results in PD, human trial result outcome using TZDs in AD patients have so far been dissatisfactory (Watson et al. 2005). Meta-analysis of five retrospective trials revealed a reduction in the incidence of PD (Connolly et al. 2015). TZDs, with predominantly PPAR-γ activity, sensitised cells to insulin and activated mitochondrial membrane proteins, leading to increased respiratory chain complex I activity in neuronal cells. This action has been reported to decrease mitochondrial dysfunction, which is a PD hallmark. In addition to their action at the mitochondrial level, TZDs have shown neuroprotective actions in rat and mouse models of PD induced by MPTP, both with LPS exposure and with L-DOPA-induced dyskinesias. Likewise, in human patients, evidence exists for these protective phenomena (Ghosh et al. 2007). The activation of PPAR-γ is said to be involved in these events by modulating microglial activation, reducing NOS activity, oxidative stress, and free radical release. TZDs were found in previous research to have the additional advantage of inhibiting monoamine oxidase B (MAO-B), thereby reducing dopamine breakdown and increasing dopamine levels (Jiang et al. 2018). Pioglitazone, for example, has been shown to enhance the survival of substantia nigra dopaminergic neurons, reduce proinflammatory cytokines, and reduce iNOS expression in microglia and macrophages, thereby reducing oxidative stress and enhancing mitochondrial function in various PD models. Pioglitazone has also been shown to reduce glial activation, reduce the activation of NF-κB, and conserve dopaminergic neurons in mice treated with MPTP (Camargo Maluf et al. 2019). Lobeglitazone, a newer glitazone, has demonstrated potent effects in reversing rotenone-induced behavioural deficits, particularly at doses lower than those of rosiglitazone and pioglitazone. Experiments with new glitazone derivatives, such as G1 and G2, also showed anti-inflammatory activity, with significant inhibition of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and TNF-α, and of nitric oxide production in LPS-induced SH-SY5Y cells. Such manoeuvres are expected to be mediated through activation of PPAR-γ, leading to signalling through PGC-1α, offering a pathway to the potential neuroprotection of PD and other neurodegenerative diseases (Aguirre-Vidal et al. 2024). PPAR-sparing is the positive biomarker in early onset of pathogenesis in rodent PD models, which may display equal neuroprotective effects with lower side effects (Colca et al. 2013). Pancreatic beta cells secrete dopamine and act on dopamine receptor (D2R), and its antagonist antipsychotic drugs (APDs) alter glucose-stimulated insulin production, indicating that dopamine plays a significant role in APD-activated metabolic dysfunction (Wei et al. 2020). T2DM patients displayed HbA1C reduction levels and regulation of lipid profile. The anti-Parkinsonian drugs, including bromocriptine, lower the sympathetic function and impairment of the hypothalamic pituitary adrenal axis would particularly reduce the secretion of stress hormone (cortisol) and catecholamines, causing a reduction of glycemic levels (Camargo Maluf et al. 2019).

Dipeptidyl Peptidase-4 Inhibitors (DPP-IV) Inhibitors

DPP-IV class of drugs are newer oral anti-glycemic drugs recently approved as adjunct T2DM therapy, and the drugs are saxagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin, and teneligliptin. DPP-IV inhibitors are medications used to regulate T2DM by prolonging the activity of incretin hormones, thereby enhancing insulin secretion and suppressing glucagon release. Beyond their glycemic control properties, emerging evidence suggests that DPP-IV inhibitors may confer neuroprotective benefits, potentially influencing the risk and progression of neurodegenerative diseases such as PD (Deacon 2020). DPP-IV inhibitors have been observed to modulate inflammatory pathways. By increasing the bioavailability of substrates like GLP-1, these inhibitors can attenuate neuroinflammation, a contributing factor in PD pathogenesis. Oxidative stress plays a pivotal role in dopaminergic neuronal death in PD. DPP-IV inhibitors may mitigate oxidative damage, thereby preserving neuronal integrity. Some studies suggest that DPP-IV inhibitors might interfere with Aβ accumulation, a hallmark of Alzheimer’s disease, which shares pathological features with PD (Tang et al. 2023).

This system depends on the usage of fluids in microchannels. Major advantages of this system are its ability to utilize lower doses of samples and reagents are required for results analysis. The selection of the LoC system for the evaluation of the sample nature. Based on α-synuclein protein evaluation, the most commonly used approach for the evaluation of α-synuclein is immunofluorescence methods based on antibodies and antigen interactions (Deleersnijder et al. 2013). Microfluidic systems play a significant effective method for the identification of oligomers due to their ability to bind with lower fluid volumes and their binding with several detection strategies, such as dynamic studies. Future Perspective

Although growing preclinical and early clinical result data, multiple critical questions remain elusive. It is unclear whether the neuroprotective effects of anti-glycemic drugs, specifically GLP-1 analogs, are mediated neuron, peripherally, or through combined molecular mechanisms. Additionally, computational and experimental models suggest that time intervention is crucial, with early insulin signalling restoration likely to yield maximal benefit. Recent studies in PD exhibit that neurons become highly insulin resistant in the disease progression, and that PPARγ expression is lower with ageing. Additionally, in-vitro studies report that early disease intervention with GLP-1 analogues may halt age-dependent α-synuclein aggregation, whereas insulin administration alone may not, suggesting the existence of disease-stage- and drug-specific therapeutic windows. The first clinical trials using GLP-1 agonists in PD are currently underway, and their outcome data are eagerly anticipated to determine whether this promising preclinical result can be translated into a meaningful disease-modifying treatment. Growing evidence reported that bromocriptine and cabergoline reduce the hypoglycaemia and are safer for cardiovascular risk of disease, and may be used for standalone therapy, or in combination with other antidiabetic medications (Dara et al. 2025).

Conclusion

A growing body of evidence suggests a connection between insulin resistance and Parkinson’s disease (PD), and although the underlying molecular mechanisms remain elusive, growing evidence suggests that α-synuclein can interfere with the insulin receptor by its actions on neuroinflammation and the Akt signalling pathway. The downstream pathway regulation may further exacerbate α-synuclein oligomer production and protein accumulation. Intriguingly, recent studies show that the reduction of insulin’s pleiotropic effects in rodent models of insulin resistance and leads to PD pathology exacerbation can be somewhat counteracted via regaining or activating this signalling pathway using anti-glycemic drugs. Although GLP-1 analogues have also been shown to cross the BBB and stimulate GLP-1 pathways in rodent models of PD, it remains elusive whether the resultant positive effects are mediated centrally, peripherally, or via a combination of both. The question is also still unclear whether the neuroprotective effects identified in preclinical animal models will translate to detectable disease-site modification, although initial data from human trials are encouraging. The current management of cognitive deficits primarily targets neurotransmitter decline, and only rivastigmine is licensed for treatment in PD dementia (PDD) and dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB). Hence, it displays underlying effects on cognition and behavioural changes, it may worsen motor impairments and does not change the progressive nature of neurodegeneration. Accumulating evidence also links both insulin resistance and cognitive decline, such as neuronal and peripheral insulin resistance in DLB. Similar evidence associated with insulin resistance and AD additionally underlie the promising therapeutic effect of anti-glycemic drugs, with early human clinical trial data identifying significant clinical improvements.
