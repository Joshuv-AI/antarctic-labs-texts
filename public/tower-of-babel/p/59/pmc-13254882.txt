Quantifying Extensor Tendon and Articular Surface Involvement During Phalangeal Intramedullary Fixation
Authors: Julie Mekhail, Rhiana Rivas, Nikalus Skipp, Isabella Trancoso, Deana Mercer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePhalangeal fractures of the hand are the second most common upper-extremity fracture in the United States with a fracture incidence of 130/100,000 persons. Numerous studies have reported on the biomechanical efficacy of intramedullary (IM) hand nail fixation of the phalanges, and the large volume of literature indicates the recent increased utilization of IM hand nails. When using IM fixation, the implant must penetrate the articular cartilage during insertion. This study seeks to quantify the percentage of cartilage involvement created by anterograde and retrograde intramedullary fixation of proximal and middle phalangeal fractures with 3.0-mm and 3.5-mm IM threaded hand nails.MethodsFifty fresh-frozen fingers were studied from 10 fresh-frozen cadaveric hands. Two different techniques for implant placement were used: (1) anterograde through a flexed proximal interphalangeal joint into the middle phalanx of the index, middle, ring, and small fingers and (2) retrograde through the interphalangeal joint into the proximal phalanx of the thumb. Clinical photographs and fluoroscopy images were taken for each specimen and then uploaded into ImageJ to quantify the amount of extensor tendon and articular surface involvement.ResultsThe surface areas of the extensor mechanism and articular cartilage affected by implant insertion of all fingers were quantified. The mean extensor tendon involvement using intramedullary hand nails for phalangeal fixation was <9% (range 7.8% to 10.1%), whereas the average cartilage defect was <5% (range 4.3% to 5.9%). In all instances, the assessed articular surface area was greater with the 3.5-mm than the 3.0-mm implants.ConclusionsOur results indicate that nails inserted anterograde into the middle phalanx through the proximal interphalangeal joint of the index, middle, ring, and small fingers, as well as retrograde into the proximal phalanx of the thumb impact the extensor tendons and articular surface by approximately 9% and 5%, respectively. Intramedullary fixation may be a viable technique for treating middle phalanx fractures of the hand.Type of study/level of evidenceV, Bench research/laboratory studyKey words: Articular, Fixation, Fracture, Intramedullary, Phalanges

Phalangeal fractures make up approximately 20% of emergency department visits for upper limb fractures.1 Fractures of the middle phalanx are particularly prevalent because of the bone’s vulnerability during high-energy trauma and occupational or recreational injuries. Despite their frequency, the management of phalangeal fractures remains complex, with a wide array of treatment options tailored to fracture type, location, and severity and with variable and inconsistent clinical outcomes.

Current treatment modalities for phalangeal fractures include open reduction and internal fixation, closed reduction and percutaneous pinning with K-wires, and intramedullary (IM) nail fixation. Each technique has unique advantages and limitations. Open reduction and internal fixation with plate and screws provides rigid fixation but can lead to complications such as adhesions, soft tissue irritation, and joint stiffness.2 Closed reduction and percutaneous pinning, typically with K-wires, is less invasive and preserves soft tissues but may lack sufficient stability for comminuted or oblique fractures, potentially leading to loss of reduction or nonunion and binds the soft tissues limiting motion.3

Intramedullary hand nails are increasingly favored because of their surgical approach, which reduces soft tissue disruption and preserves vascularity, but concerns about extensor tendon injury, articular cartilage damage, and rotational instability persist.4,5

In recent years, there has been a notable shift toward the use of hand nails for fixation of phalangeal fractures, driven by their ability to provide stability while preserving soft tissue structures.6 However, this technique necessitates nail insertion through the articular surface of the metacarpophalangeal, proximal interphalangeal (PIP), or distal interphalangeal (IP) joints, raising concerns among surgeons about the potential for articular cartilage damage and its long-term implications for arthritic changes and joint function.7 Despite growing adoption of this technique, the extent of articular surface involvement and its clinical significance remain poorly quantified.3,6

To date, no study has systematically assessed the degree of articular surface involvement associated with the anterograde placement of hand nails through the PIP joint into the middle phalanx. Furthermore, few studies have evaluated the impact of hand or intramedullary nails on extensor tendon integrity, leaving considerable gaps in our understanding of the potential complications of these methods.4,5 This lack of robust data underscores the need for further investigation to provide surgeons with the evidence needed to make informed decisions about the use of hand nails for phalangeal fracture fixation.

The purpose of this study is to address these gaps by quantifying the articular surface involvement and extensor tendon involvement associated with hand nail fixation using an anterograde technique. Specifically, this study evaluates these parameters for nails placed into the middle phalanx through the PIP joint of the index, middle, ring, and small fingers, as well as the proximal phalanx of the thumb. By addressing these critical questions, we aim to provide data that enhances the utility, safety, and efficacy of this emerging technique for managing phalangeal fractures.

Materials and Methods

Specimens and radiographic screening

Fifty fresh-frozen fingers were obtained from 10 cadaveric subjects (4 women and 6 men) with a mean age of 77 years (range 52–92 years). Radiographs of each hand were taken prior to dissection to ensure the absence of previous traumatic injuries or advanced arthritis. All specimens exhibited no visible deformities and demonstrated full joint range of motion. Fractures were not simulated in the phalanges before implant placement. As this was a cadaveric study, ethics approval was not required.

Surgical techniques

Two different techniques were used for IM implant placement, both performed under fluoroscopic guidance by an orthopedically trained hand surgeon.

Anterograde through the PIP joint

This technique was applied to the index, middle, ring, and small fingers. A 0.7 mm (0.028 in) Kirschner wire (K-wire) was introduced anterograde through the proximal interphalangeal joint into the middle phalanx. The K-wire was positioned in the sulcus between the 2 condyles of the distal aspect of the middle phalanx while the PIP joint was maximally flexed. Positioning was confirmed fluoroscopically (Fig. 1). The K-wire was used as the guide for reaming, and a 3.0-mm threaded hand nail was inserted for the index, middle, ring, and small fingers. Final IM implant placement was confirmed fluoroscopically (Fig. 2). The skin was then opened to examine the extensor mechanism, and photographs were taken to document tendon involvement (Fig. 3). The joint was then exposed, and images of the articular surface with a ruler as a scale were captured.

Retrograde through IP joint

This approach was used for the thumb. A K-wire was introduced retrograde through the IP joint into the proximal phalanx while the IP joint was maintained at maximum flexion. Steps including reaming, hand nail placement, fluoroscopy, joint dissection, and photography were repeated as previously described. We reamed over the K-wire with the appropriate reamer for the 3.5-mm implant, and a 3.5-mm hand nail was inserted. Additional photographs were taken to document the changes.

Quantification of defects in the extensor tendon and articular surface

All photographs of the extensor tendons and articular surfaces were analyzed using ImageJ software (ImageJ). For the extensor tendon, the width of the tendon and the width of the defect were measured to calculate the percentage of the extensor mechanism affected by IM implant insertion. Similarly, the surface area of the articular cartilage and defect area were measured to determine the percentage of articular surface involvement caused by implant placement.

In the anterograde approach through the PIP joint, measurements focused on the articular involvement at the base of the middle phalanx. For the retrograde approach through the IP joint, measurements targeted the articular involvement of the head of the proximal phalanx. In cases involving the base of a phalanx, the total articular surface and defect areas were measured to calculate the percentage of involvement (Figure 4). For defects involving the head of a phalanx, measurements included the tip area, dorsal, and volar articular surfaces. These surfaces were stitched together using ImageJ software to quantify the entire curved articulation.

Articular surface involvement of the base of the middle phalanx using the anterograde placement of the hand nail through the PIP joint. PIP, proximal interphalangeal.

Statistical analysis

Given the descriptive cadaveric design and clustering of multiple specimens within individual cadavers, formal inferential comparative testing was not performed. Results are presented using descriptive statistics including means, ranges, and measurement reliability assessments. Inter- and intraobserver reliability were evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC).

Results

The average extensor tendon involvement of the PIP joint using the anterograde technique into the middle phalanx was 8.9% (range 8.2–9.7) in the index finger, 8.4% (range 7.4–10.3) in the middle finger, 7.8% (range 6.8–8.7) in the ring finger, and 9.1% (range 8.0–10.0) in the small finger using the 3.0-mm intramedullary hand nail.

The average extensor tendon defect of the IP joint using the retrograde technique into the proximal phalanx of the thumb was 10.1% (range 9.0–11.2) using the 3.5-mm hand nail (Table 1).

Overall, the mean extensor tendon involvement using intramedullary hand nails for phalangeal fixation was 8.9% (range 7.8% to 10.1%), whereas the average cartilage defect was 5.0% (range 4.3% to 5.9%). In all instances, the assessed articular surface area was greater with the 3.5-mm than the 3.0-mm IM implants.

Interobserver correlation was calculated to assess reliability. The analysis of inter- and intrarater reliability for 3 observers revealed excellent consistency in their assessments of extensor tendon defect and cartilage defect percentages.

Intrarater reliability was high for all observers, with values ranging from 0.90 to 0.95 for extensor tendon defect and 0.90 to 0.92 for cartilage defect, indicating strong self-consistency across repeated ratings. Interrater reliability demonstrated high agreement, with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.86 to 0.90 for both extensor tendon defect and cartilage defect (Table 3). Values between 0.75 and 0.9 indicate good reliability, and values >0.90 indicate excellent reliability.8 The ICC confirmed excellent agreement among the raters, with ICC values of 0.91 for extensor tendon defect and 0.89 for cartilage defect.

Discussion

This study provides quantitative data on the extent of articular cartilage and extensor tendon involvement caused by IM hand nail fixation for phalangeal fractures using an anterograde and retrograde approach. Our findings demonstrate that the use of 3.0-mm IM hand nails results in a mean extensor tendon involvement of approximately 8.9% and a mean cartilage involvement of 5.0%.

These results support the notion that intramedullary fixation may offer an alternative to open techniques such as plate and K-wire fixation, which have been associated with extensor tendon adhesions, hardware irritation, and joint stiffness.2 The relatively minimal articular involvement observed in this study suggests that IM hand nail fixation may be biomechanically and biologically favorable, preserving joint congruency while maintaining stability sufficient for early mobilization. Prior cadaveric and clinical studies have underscored these advantages, highlighting reduced operative time, minimal periosteal stripping, and lower infection rates.4,6

Our findings further align with studies that emphasized the mechanical viability of IM nails in phalangeal fractures. Giesen et al4 demonstrated that IM fixation provides sufficient axial and rotational stability for most fracture patterns, particularly transverse and short oblique fractures, with less soft tissue trauma. However, concerns regarding tendon violation and iatrogenic joint injury have persisted because of the required transarticular entry point. This study helps contextualize these concerns; although the tendon and cartilage are indeed impacted, the extent is modest and may fall below clinically significant thresholds, especially when weighed against the functional gains associated with early motion.

We found that measurable tendon and cartilage defects are created during implant insertion, but the magnitude of these defects was relatively limited in the specimens studied. Importantly, these data should be interpreted as descriptive anatomical measurements only. The present study does not evaluate whether the observed degree of tissue violation is clinically acceptable, whether it affects postoperative motion or strength, or it contributes to long-term degenerative change. Accordingly, conclusions regarding functional significance cannot be drawn from these cadaveric findings alone.

These results may be considered alongside a recent study by Chung et al9 who evaluated retrograde intramedullary metacarpal nail insertion through the extensor mechanism. They reported that several insertion methods did not significantly decrease extensor mechanism load to failure or stiffness under physiologic conditions, although implant insertion created a potential tensile failure site at supraphysiologic loads. Their findings suggest that limited tendon violation may not necessarily translate into early functional compromise. Although that study examined metacarpals rather than phalanges and assessed biomechanical properties rather than defect size, it provides a useful complementary context for interpreting the present findings.

The excellent inter- and intraobserver reliability achieved in our measurements further strengthens the validity of our data, supporting the reproducibility of tendon and cartilage assessments using ImageJ software. Previous studies have rarely quantified tendon involvement, and fewer have used standardized digital measurement tools. This method may be useful in future biomechanical and clinical assessments of surgical approaches in hand surgery.

Limitations of this study include its cadaveric nature, which does not account for biological responses such as inflammation, healing, or clinical outcomes like range of motion and pain. Furthermore, although tendon and cartilage involvement were measured, their direct clinical consequences—such as extensor lag or posttraumatic arthritis—remain speculative without long-term in vivo data. This study is also descriptive in nature, and our findings represent controlled measurements of defect size under standardized conditions, rather than a comprehensive assessment of all operative variables. It should be stated that there was no fracture model created, as the purpose of this investigation was to isolate and quantify the tissue disruption caused by implant insertion itself. We acknowledge that inclusion of multiple implant types, additional implant diameters, and comparative analyses across systems would have further strengthened the study design. However, this investigation represents a completed cadaveric study, and the original facilities and specimens were no longer available to permit expansion of the experimental protocol.

In summary, intramedullary hand nail fixation of the phalanges resulted in measurable but limited extensor tendon and articular cartilage defects in this cadaveric model. Hand nail IM fixation of phalangeal fractures resulted in extensor tendon and cartilage involvement, with average values below 10% and 6%, respectively. These findings provide descriptive anatomical information regarding tissue violation during implant insertion, and future clinical studies should aim to correlate these anatomical findings with long-term functional outcomes, including postoperative motion, pain, tendon function, and joint health.
