Genetic variants associated with type 2 diabetes mellitus: a meta-analysis of Egyptian and Levant populations
Authors: Karar Nadhum Jawad Musafer, Akbar Ali, Tengku Ahmad Damitri Al Astani Tengku Din, Heba Mohammed Arafat, Muhammad Irfan Abdul Jalal, Wan Nor Arifin, Ali Obaid Alhujiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a major cause of morbidity in the Middle East; however, Arab populations remain underrepresented in genomic studies. We synthesized evidence on single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with T2DM risk in Egyptian and Levant (Jordan, Lebanon, Palestine, and Syria) cohorts to identify shared and population-specific loci.MethodsWe conducted a PRISMA-aligned systematic review and meta-analysis (PROSPERO CRD420251162068) of case–control genetic studies published until July 2025. Eligible studies enrolled ≥ 60 participants, reported odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and satisfied the Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium in controls. Pooled effects used fixed- and random-effects models according to heterogeneity (Cochran’s Q, I², and τ²). Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s and Begg’s tests and funnel plot symmetries. Sensitivity analyses included leave-one-out procedures and meta-regression using study-level moderators (case/control counts). Exploratory clustering and random forest models were used to examine gene/SNP features related to effect size.ResultsIn Egyptians (45 SNPs), between-study heterogeneity was negligible (Q = 35.32, df = 43, p = 0.79; I² = 0%; τ² = 0). Strong risk associations were observed for IGFB rs2854843 (OR = 13.30, p = 0.001), MTHFR rs1801133 (OR = 4.60, p = 0.001), GCKR rs780094 (OR = 3.99, p = 0.001), and TCF7L2 rs12255372 (OR = 2.06, p = 0.0001); additional signals were observed for ADIPOQ and KCNJ11. Egger’s test suggested minor small-study effects (p = 0.0184), whereas Begg’s test did not (p = 0.721). Meta-regression indicated that larger case counts and smaller control counts were associated with inflated ORs (R² ≈ 0.15). In the Levant cohort (29 SNPs), heterogeneity was high (Q = 302.32, df = 28, p < 0.0001; I² = 90.7%). Notable loci included NAT2 rs1799931 (OR = 13.72, p = 0.043) and SH2B1 rs565131715 (OR = 7.55), with lower magnitude associations for ADIPOQ and KCNQ1. No significant publication bias was detected.ConclusionsEgyptian findings revealed consistent large-effect SNP associations with T2DM, whereas Levant populations showed variable context-dependent effects. Shared markers (TCF7L2, ADIPOQ, and KCNJ11) and population-specific candidates (IGFB, NAT2, and SH2B1) may inform targeted screening and risk stratification. Harmonized, adequately powered multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings and accelerate precision medicine in underrepresented Arab populations.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-026-02103-5.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes, SNP, Genetic association, Meta-analysis, Egypt, Levant population

Introduction

Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) is a common metabolic disorder characterized by insulin resistance and inadequate insulin production. It affects millions of people worldwide and has become a significant public health concern, resulting in considerable morbidity and mortality [1]. The development of T2DM arises from a complex interplay between genetic factors and environmental influences, with genetic variation playing a crucial role in individual susceptibility. Over the past few decades, the prevalence of T2DM has increased rapidly, placing a significant burden on healthcare systems worldwide [2]. This increase has prompted extensive research into its genetic and environmental causes. Although many genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and candidate gene analyses have identified loci associated with T2DM risk, most of these studies have focused primarily on populations of European [3] or East Asian descent [1, 2]. Conversely, populations from the Levantine region, including Jordan, Lebanon, Palestine, Syria, and Egypt, with their shared historical and genetic ties, remain underrepresented in genetic studies of T2DM [4–10]. Moreover, the available candidate-gene studies in these populations are often small and heterogeneous in design and genetic models, producing inconsistent effect estimates that require quantitative synthesis [11]. These groups may harbor unique single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) that influence T2DM risk. Studying the genetic structures of these populations could provide valuable insights into disease mechanisms and support personalized medicine. Understanding the genetic variations associated with T2DM in the Levantine and Egyptian groups is particularly important, given their shared historical, cultural, and environmental backgrounds, which likely influence their genetic makeup in distinctive ways [4–10]. However, evidence from individual studies remains fragmented, and differences in sample size, genetic models/contrasts, and effect-allele reporting can limit direct comparisons across studies, highlighting the need for a harmonized synthesis. This study aimed to explore SNPs associated with T2DM in the Egyptian and Levantine populations. Therefore, we conducted a PRISMA-aligned systematic review and meta-analysis (PROSPERO ID: CRD4202511 62068) to identify both shared and population-specific variants and synthesize effect estimates using consistent allele/contrast alignment across studies. By identifying both common and population-specific genetic variants, this study seeks to deepen our understanding of the genetic predisposition to T2DM in these regions. These results could help improve diagnostic, preventive, and treatment strategies tailored to the genetic profiles of these communities. Ultimately, this study aimed to clarify the genetic foundations of T2DM in Egypt and the Levant. Such insights could inform the development of customized healthcare strategies and public health policies, enhancing the prevention, diagnosis, and management of T2DM in these areas and across the Arab world in the future.

Research method study design and eligibility criteria

Methods

Protocol and study design

We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of genetic association studies according to the PRISMA guidelines. The review protocol was registered with PROSPERO (Registration ID: CRD420251162068). We focused on a case–control genetic examination of the association between single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in candidate genes and the risk of T2DM [12]. Studies were included only if T2DM cases were defined according to standard diagnostic criteria (International Classification of Diseases codes).

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed using multiple databases, including PubMed (MEDLINE), Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar. We searched for articles published from the inception of each database to June 2025. The search strategy used combinations of keywords for T2DM and genetics, along with regional filters to narrow the search. For example, terms such as “Type 2 Diabetes,” “T2DM,” “genetic association,” “polymorphism,” and “SNP” were combined with country names (“Egypt,” “Jordan,” “Lebanon,” “Palestine,” “Syria”) to identify relevant studies. The references of the retrieved articles were screened for additional eligible studies.

Data extraction

For each study that met the inclusion criteria, we extracted the key data points using a standardized form. The collected variables included gene name and specific SNP identifiers (rs numbers), number of T2DM cases and controls, genotyping method used, reported effect size metrics (odds ratios [OR] with 95% confidence intervals and corresponding p-values for each SNP), and geographic location of the study (country). All extracted data were cross-checked for accuracy by at least two reviewers. To facilitate regional comparison, the data were organized into tables stratified by Egypt and Levantine countries. We pooled effects only within the same reported genetic model/contrast after harmonizing effect-allele/genotype orientation.

Study selection and screening

A total of 1,087 records were identified through database searches (NCBI, Google Scholar, Web of Science, Scopus, and PubMed). After removing 522 duplicates, 565 unique records remained and were screened based on their titles and abstracts. Of these, 523 records were excluded because they did not meet the eligibility criteria (most commonly incorrect population, irrelevant outcomes, or insufficient genetic/association data). The remaining 42 articles underwent full-text assessment; all 42 met the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis and meta-analysis. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1), which details the number of records identified, screened, excluded, and ultimately included in the review.

Risk-of-bias and quality assessment

We evaluated the methodological quality and risk of bias of each eligible case–control study using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), a nine-point (star) system that appraises three domains: selection of cases and controls (0–4 points), comparability of groups based on design or analysis (0–2 points), and exposure assessment (0–3 points). Two reviewers independently assigned NOS scores to each study, with disagreements resolved by consensus or, when necessary, by consultation with a third reviewer. Studies scoring 7–9 points were classified as high-quality, those scoring 5–6 points as moderate-quality, and those scoring 0–4 points as low quality. The detailed NOS ratings for all the included studies are presented in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Multiple-testing control: We applied the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR, q = 0.05) to pooled SNP tests and reported the FDR-adjusted p-values. Meta-regression was refitted on log(OR) using weighted least squares with random-effects (REML) weights; residual diagnostics (QQ plots, Jarque–Bera, Durbin–Watson) indicated adequate model assumptions. We performed a meta-analysis to quantitatively synthesize the association between genetic polymorphisms and T2DM risk in the included studies. For each SNP, we obtained or calculated the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI), comparing T2DM risk in carriers of the risk allele versus non-carriers (or as defined in each study). Where necessary, reported ORs were log-transformed, and standard errors (SEs) were derived to facilitate the pooling. We used both fixed- and random-effects models to compute pooled ORs, accounting for potential between-study heterogeneity. Meta-regression was refitted on log(OR) using inverse-variance–weighted mixed-effects meta-regression with τ² estimated by REML, and residual diagnostics (e.g., QQ plots, Jarque–Bera, Durbin–Watson) were examined to assess model assumptions. The primary analysis was conducted using the DerSimonian–Laird random-effects model with complementary fixed-effect estimates for comparison purposes. Meta-analytical calculations were implemented with the metagen function from the meta package in R (version 4.3.2) and cross-verified using Review Manager (RevMan v5.4).

Effect of alignment

For each SNP, we defined a single effect allele (as reported) based on the allele/contrast explicitly reported in the original study. All study-specific ORs were aligned to the same effect allele direction before pooling. When a study reported the OR for the opposite allele, we inverted the estimate (OR′ = 1/OR) and transformed the 95% CI accordingly (lower′ = 1/upper; upper′ = 1/lower), ensuring a consistent effect direction across studies. If the effect allele (as reported) or direction could not be determined unambiguously from the publication, the result was retained for descriptive summary only and excluded from quantitative pooling.

Genetic model

Data were extracted according to the genetic models reported in each study. Meta-analyses were performed separately by model when sufficient data were available (allelic/additive, dominant, recessive, or genotype contrasts). We did not pool allele-based ORs with genotype-based ORs unless they represented the same contrast.

Handling extreme/unstable estimates

All meta-analyses were performed on the log(OR) scale. When ORs and 95% CIs were reported, the standard error was computed as SE = [ln(upper CI) − ln(lower CI)]/(2 × 1.96), and pooled using inverse-variance weights under fixed-effects or random-effects models, as appropriate. Because very wide CIs yield large SEs, such studies are automatically downweighted and cannot dominate the pooled estimates. Additionally, we evaluated influence using leave-one-out diagnostics and conducted a prespecified sensitivity analysis excluding studies with extreme ORs and very wide 95% CIs. Where genotype cell counts contained zeros, we did not recompute study-level ORs from raw cells; instead, we used the effect estimates as reported in the original studies (therefore any continuity correction, if applied, followed the original study’s analytic approach). Analyses were conducted using R (meta/metafor) and Python.

Heterogeneity assessment

Statistical heterogeneity among studies was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test and quantified using I² and tau². An I² value above 50% was interpreted as moderate or high heterogeneity. We report the Q statistic p-value to indicate whether the observed heterogeneity was beyond chance. Forest plots were generated to visualize the individual study results and the overall pooled effect for each SNP, allowing for a qualitative assessment of consistency across studies. For the Sham/Levant cohort, the pooled heterogeneity statistics are computed across SNP-level effect-size entries (k = 29) spanning multiple loci rather than repeated estimates of a single variant; therefore, high between-entry heterogeneity is expected because true genetic effects differ by locus. To avoid attributing heterogeneity to ‘population diversity’ alone, we conducted prespecified moderator analyses to explore clinical and methodological sources of variability, including subgroup analyses by country and genotyping method and meta-regression by study size. Influence diagnostics were also used to identify entries disproportionately contributing to Cochran’s Q and τ² (Supplementary Tables S17–S19; Figs. S9–S10).

Publication bias and small-study effects

Small-study effects were explored using funnel plots with Egger’s regression and Begg’s rank correlation only for SNP-specific meta-analyses where the same SNP (under the same genetic model/contrast) was pooled across multiple independent studies and k was sufficient. These tests were not applied to region-level summaries combining different loci/SNP entries, as asymmetry is not interpretable as publication bias in mixed-locus sets.

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed to assess robustness. To explore sources of heterogeneity particularly in the Sham/Levant synthesis. we conducted prespecified subgroup analyses stratified by (i) country (Jordan, Lebanon, Palestine, Syria) and (ii) genotyping method (PCR-RFLP, TaqMan RT-PCR, sequencing-based methods, and other). We also fitted univariable mixed-effects meta-regression models using study size (log total sample size; cases and controls) as moderators to quantify the extent to which small-study structure contributed to variability. Where T2DM diagnostic criteria were explicitly reported, studies were coded by diagnostic framework; however, reporting was inconsistent across studies and therefore this moderator was treated as exploratory and not used for strong inference. Finally, influence diagnostics (including Baujat-type contribution plots) were used to identify effect-size entries driving τ² and Q (Supplementary Tables S17–S19; Figs. S9–S10).

Software and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed using established software. Meta-analyses and plots were primarily performed in R (v4.3.2) using the meta and ggplot2/EnhancedVolcano packages to create forest plots and other visualizations. We additionally used RevMan 5.4 to generate certain forest plots and cross-check the calculated pooled estimates. Data handling and preliminary analyses were facilitated using Python (v3.10.12) and Microsoft Excel 365, particularly during the data cleaning and preparation stages of the study. Figures (including forest plots, funnel plots, and volcano plots of p-values vs. effect sizes) were exported in high-resolution publication-quality formats. All meta-analyses were performed using R with a fully scripted, pre-specified workflow. To verify numerical consistency, the complete results were independently reproduced using Python. The full analysis code, processing scripts, and extracted datasets used for pooling are publicly available in the GitHub repository cited in the manuscript (swatian1989/Meta-analysis-SNP), ensuring transparency and reproducibility of the pooled estimates and sensitivity analyses.

Geographic distribution

Egypt: Approximately 75% of the studies were conducted in Egypt. The Sham region includes Lebanon, which contributed approximately 15%, Palestine with 5%, and Syria and Jordan, each contributing approximately 2–3%.

Result

Egyptian population

Of the 42 eligible studies included overall, 29 Egyptian case–control studies contributed SNP–T2DM association estimates for quantitative synthesis (Table 1; Supplementary Table S1). Across these Egyptian estimates, between-estimate heterogeneity was negligible (Cochran’s Q = 35.32, df = 43, p = 0.7912; I² = 0.00%; τ² = 0.0000; Table 3), indicating highly consistent effects within this dataset. Small study effects were suggested by Egger’s regression (p = 0.0184; Table 4), whereas Begg’s rank correlation was not significant (p = 0.721). Visual inspection of the funnel plot indicated minimal asymmetry (Fig. 5b). Among the Egyptian associations extracted from the included studies, the largest odds ratios were reported for IGFB rs2854843 (OR = 13.30, p = 0.0010), MTHFR rs1801133 (OR = 4.60, p = 0.0010), GCKR rs780094 (OR = 3.99, p = 0.0010), FTO rs8050136 (OR = 3.69, p = 0.0030), and RARRES2 rs17173608 (OR = 3.12, p = 0.0010) (Table 5) [12]. However, several of these extreme-magnitude estimates were derived from single-study SNP entries with very wide confidence intervals, which can arise from sparse genotype strata (including near-zero cells), unbalanced genotype counts, or unmeasured population substructure. Therefore, these values should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive large-effect loci. Consistent with this, our sensitivity analysis excluding extreme/unstable entries (Supplementary Table S15) yielded nearly identical pooled estimates, indicating that overall conclusions are not driven by these outliers. Forest plots and influence diagnostics highlighted recurrent signals at TCF7L2 and FTO, whereas the Baujat plot indicated that extreme-magnitude estimates (notably IGFB and MTHFR) disproportionately contributed to the overall Q statistic (Fig. 5e).

Levent (Sham population)

The Levant (Sham) population have thirteen [42] eligible case–control studies from Syria, Jordan, Lebanon, and Palestine that were included in the quantitative synthesis (Table 2). Between-study heterogeneity was substantial (Cochran’s Q = 302.32, df = 28, p < 0.0001; I² = 90.7%; τ² = 0.0926); therefore, pooled estimates were obtained using a random-effects model (Table 10). Small-study effect testing was not applied to the region-level mixed-locus SNP-entry summary because it does not represent a single underlying effect and may include non-independent entries; therefore, Egger/Begg results are not interpreted as publication bias. Table 2 summarizes study-specific ORs and 95% CIs with harmonized effect-allele orientation. Larger study-specific effect estimates were observed for NAT2 rs1799931 (OR = 13.72; 95% CI 0.74–255.46), SH2B1 rs565131715 (OR = 7.55; 95% CI 3.31–17.25), NAT2 rs1041983 (OR = 4.78; 95% CI 2.00–11.41), FTO rs9939609 (OR = 4.03; 95% CI 2.01–8.06), and KCNJ11 rs5219 (OR = 3.81; 95% CI 1.024–14.17). Several additional variants showed modest effect sizes with strong statistical support, including KCNQ1 rs2237892 (p = 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁸), COL8A1 rs792837 (p = 2.9 × 10⁻⁹), and IGF2BP2 rs4402960 (p = 6.5 × 10⁻⁶) (Table 2). Comparative analyses

Comparative analysis showed that Egyptian studies identified consistent, high-impact SNPs with significant ORs (e.g., IGFB and MTHFR), while Sham studies exhibited more variability and greater heterogeneity. Heatmaps (Fig. 4a and b) and boxplots (Fig. 4d) demonstrated that Egyptian variants such as ADIPOQ and TCF7L2 had higher median ORs, whereas the sham datasets showed wider ranges and less consistency. Gene frequency plots (Fig. 4c) revealed that TCF7L2, FTO, and ADIPOQ were the dominant genes in Egypt, whereas NAT2 and SH2B1 were the most common in the Shams studies. Correlation analysis (Fig. 1b) revealed near-perfect agreement between patients and controls (r = 0.99), with the sham datasets showing stronger negative correlations between sample size and ORs (r = −0.35).

Methodological quality of included studies

The methodological quality and risk of bias of all eligible case–control studies were assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS). As summarized in (Table S1) Supplementary Materials, all 42 studies (29 Egyptian and 13 sham) achieved a maximum NOS score of 9 points, corresponding to a high-quality rating. Domain-level scores were also uniform, with all studies receiving 4 points for selection, 2 points for comparability, and 3 points for exposure. This consistent pattern indicates that the evidence base met the key methodological standards, including clearly defined T2DM cases, appropriately selected control groups, adequate comparability of study populations, and robust and well-described genotyping procedures. Overall, the likelihood of major design-related bias across the included studies was low.

Sensitivity analysis by study quality

Because every included study was rated as high-quality (total NOS = 9), stratified analyses by NOS category and sensitivity analyses excluding moderate- or low-quality studies were not informative and were therefore not performed. Consequently, all pooled odds ratios (ORs), including the strongest associations observed for IGFB in the Egyptian dataset and NAT2 in the Sham cohort, were derived exclusively from high-quality evidence. This methodological homogeneity strengthens the internal validity of the meta-analysis and supports the robustness of the observed genetic associations in both Egyptian and Sham populations. Sensitivity analyses excluding studies/entries with extreme ORs and very wide 95% CIs did not materially change the pooled estimates or conclusions (Supplementary Tables S15-16). In the Egyptian dataset, excluding the prespecified extreme/unstable single-study entries (Supplementary Table S15) produced minimal change in the pooled OR (2.25 to 2.23), confirming robustness to outlier removal.

Heterogeneity and publication bias analysis (Egypt)

Meta-analysis of Egyptian studies shows consistency and possible publication bias

A meta-analysis of studies in the Egyptian population showed a high degree of consistency among effect estimates, with a Cochran’s Q statistic of 35.32 (df = 43, p = 0.7912), I²=0.00%, and Tau²=0.0000, indicating low between-study heterogeneity and that the observed variation was likely due to sampling error rather than meaningful differences between studies. Despite this apparent homogeneity, Egger’s regression test indicated statistically significant evidence of publication bias (p = 0.0184; Table 4), suggesting that selective reporting or missing null results may have influenced the findings.

Heterogeneity assessment across studies from the Egyptian population showed minimal between-study variation (Cochran’s Q = 35.32, df = 43, p = 0.7912; I² = 0.00%; Tau² = 0.0000), indicating high consistency in effect sizes and suggesting that the differences were probably due to sampling error rather than actual variation. However, Egger’s regression test yielded a statistically significant result (p = 0.0184; Table 4), indicating potential publication bias, such as selective reporting or omission of studies with negative results.

Table 7 presents the results of a meta-regression analysis examining whether study sample sizes (i.e., the number of patients and controls) influenced the reported odds ratios in studies of the Egyptian population.

Meta-regression of study sample sizes showed moderation of effect sizes (Egyptian population)

Meta-regression was conducted as an inverse-variance–weighted mixed-effects (random-effects) meta-regression on the log-transformed odds ratio (log[OR]), with between-study variance (τ²) estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML), to assess whether study sample size (numbers of cases and controls) was associated with variation in effect estimates (Table 7). The model was fitted to k = 45 SNP-level effect size entries contributed by the included Egyptian case–control studies (i.e., multiple variants could be reported within the same study). The number of cases was positively associated with log(OR) (β = 27.253, SE = 11.239, p = 0.020; 95% CI = 4.571–49.935), whereas the number of controls was negatively associated with log(OR) (β = −29.111, SE = 10.748, p = 0.010; 95% CI = − 50.801–−7.420). Collectively, these moderators explained 15% of the variability in effect sizes (R² = 0.150; adjusted R² = 0.109), and the overall model was significant (F(2, 42) = 3.704, p = 0.033). Residual diagnostics indicated marked departures from normality (Jarque–Bera = 1,682.23, p < 0.001), suggesting that additional unmeasured factors likely contributed to the remaining heterogeneity.

Model diagnostics indicated the non-normality of residuals (omnibus = 85.12; Jarque–Bera = 1,682.23; Prob[JB] < 0.001), consistent with skewness and heavy-tailed residual distributions (Table 8). The Durbin–Watson statistic (1.90) suggests no significant autocorrelation. The condition number (368) indicated that mild multicollinearity among moderators was plausible; therefore, meta-regression findings were interpreted as exploratory and indicative of partial moderation rather than fully explanatory of residual heterogeneity.

To complement the regression-based assessment, a random forest model was used to evaluate the relative contribution of predictor features to higher odds ratios in the Egyptian dataset. Variable importance (mean decrease in accuracy and mean decrease in Gini impurity) identified gene-level information as the most influential predictor (MeanDecreaseAccuracy = 8.21; MeanDecreaseGini = 8.50), followed by SNP and allele features (Table 9), Together, these results suggest that gene identity captures a substantial proportion of the signal associated with larger effect estimates, whereas variant- and allele-level descriptors contribute additional, but comparatively smaller, predictive information.

Between-study heterogeneity (Sham/Levant populations)

Thirteen [42] eligible case–control studies from Syria, Jordan, Lebanon, and Palestine were included in the quantitative synthesis and contributed k = 29 SNP-level effect size entries (Table 2). Substantial between-study heterogeneity was observed (Cochran’s Q = 302.32, df = 28, p < 0.0001; I² = 90.7%; τ² = 0.0926; Table 10), and a random-effects model was applied. Given the substantial heterogeneity, we explored moderators beyond the random-effects specification. Subgroup analyses showed that effect estimates varied across countries and genotyping platforms (Supplementary Tables S17–S18). After excluding two unstable extreme estimates already flagged in the sensitivity analysis (Table S16), the pooled association remained higher in Jordan (pooled OR ≈ 2.21; I² ≈ 75.8%) than Lebanon (pooled OR ≈ 1.33; I² ≈ 94.2%), while Palestine showed lower residual heterogeneity (pooled OR ≈ 2.64; I² ≈ 7.9%) (Table S17). Stratification by genotyping method showed substantial variability within PCR-RFLP and TaqMan RT-PCR strata (Table S18). In univariable meta-regression, study size was a significant moderator, with larger total sample sizes associated with smaller log(OR) estimates (β for log[Total N] < 0; p < 0.001), consistent with a small-study pattern contributing to variability (Table S19). Influence diagnostics further indicated that a limited number of entries contributed disproportionately to Cochran’s Q and τ² (Fig. S10). There was no strong evidence of small-study effects or publication bias (Egger’s test, p = 0.1511; Table 10). Across the included associations, reported ORs ranged from 0.066 (XDH rs206833) to 13.72 (NAT2 rs1799931) (Table 2), indicating marked variability in genetic effects across studies and settings and supporting the cautious interpretation of pooled estimates in the presence of high heterogeneity.

Overall, a high I² indicates marked variability across studies, and pooled estimates should be interpreted in light of the potential clinical and methodological differences across settings.

Discussion

This meta-analysis is the first comprehensive comparison of the genetic factors influencing T2DM in two key Middle Eastern populations: Egyptians and individuals from the Sham region (Jordan, Lebanon, Palestine, and Syria). By synthesizing evidence from 42 carefully selected case–control studies (study-level total = 13,959 unique participants; 7,385 cases and 6,574 controls), we summarized both shared and population-specific candidate SNP associations with T2DM risk. Our results highlight notable differences in the genetic architecture between these groups, with significant implications for personalized medicine in the Arab world. In the Egyptian dataset, SNP-specific meta-analyses where replication allowed showed low between-study heterogeneity; however, heterogeneity is not interpreted across mixed-locus SNP-entry sets. This uniformity increases confidence in the robustness of the observed associations and contrasts sharply with the substantial variability typically observed in multicenter genetic analyses. While the largest reported odds ratios in the Egyptian dataset included IGFB rs2854843 and MTHFR rs1801133, these were single-study estimates with wide confidence intervals and therefore should be interpreted cautiously and prioritized for replication. In contrast, more frequently studied loci such as TCF7L2 and FTO showed more consistent, interpretable effects across studies and are more suitable candidates for translational risk stratification. These findings align with previous reports in Middle Eastern populations, supporting the key role of TCF7L2 and ADIPOQ in T2DM susceptibility [7]. Meta-regression further showed that larger case sample sizes tended to inflate ORs, while larger control sample sizes tended to attenuate ORs, together explaining approximately 15% of the variance in effect size (R² ≈ 0.15). The Egyptian meta-regression (Table 7) indicates possible effect-size inflation (“winner’s curse”), with higher ORs in studies with more cases but fewer controls. Thus, associations with very large ORs and wide CIs should be interpreted cautiously and confirmed in independent, well-powered cohorts with balanced sampling and control for population stratification.

Small-study effects/publication-bias testing was not interpreted at the region-level across mixed-locus SNP-entry sets, because these entries do not represent a single underlying effect. Any apparent asymmetry is therefore not attributed to publication bias in this mixed-locus summary.

In contrast, the sham region analysis exhibited significant heterogeneity (I² = 90.7%). While population structure may contribute, our moderator analyses indicate that heterogeneity is also plausibly driven by study-level differences across countries and laboratory methods, including uneven country representation, genotyping platform differences, and a measurable small-study pattern in which smaller studies tended to report larger effects (Supplementary Tables S17–S19; Figures S9–S10). Accordingly, attributing heterogeneity to ‘population genetic diversity’ alone would be overly general, and pooled estimates should be interpreted in the context of these methodological and clinical differences [49]. Despite this variability, NAT2 (rs1799931; OR ≈ 13.7) and SH2B1 (rs565131715; OR ≈ 7.6) consistently showed the largest effect sizes in the Sham populations, along with significant but modest associations for ADIPOQ and KCNQ1. The absence of notable publication bias in this cohort (Egger’s p = 0.151; Begg’s p = 0.721) strengthens the confidence that these signals represent true underlying risk factors rather than artifacts of selective publication. However, unlike in the Egyptian analysis, meta-regression did not identify study size as a significant moderator of effect sizes in the sham cohorts (even though sample size variables explained ~ 26% of the variance), suggesting that unmeasured factors such as population stratification, linkage disequilibrium structure, or gene–environment interactions likely contributed to the observed heterogeneity [6].

A key insight from our study is the identification of KCNJ11 as a shared susceptibility gene in both Egyptian and Sham populations, underscoring its known role in insulin secretion and pancreatic β-cell function. Notably, the shared loci identified here (TCF7L2, ADIPOQ, and KCNJ11) are among the most consistently replicated T2DM susceptibility genes worldwide in large GWAS and trans-ancestry meta-analyses [49], and our results extend this established signal to Egyptian and Sham/Levant datasets, with effect-size magnitudes that may reflect population-specific LD structure and study design.

Meanwhile, certain loci appeared to be population-specific: the strong association of IGFB variants was unique to Egyptians, whereas NAT2 (and perhaps SH2B1) was significant only in the Sham dataset. These population-specific signals should be interpreted cautiously. NAT2 is mainly a xenobiotic/drug-metabolism gene, so its T2DM association may reflect linkage disequilibrium or population structure rather than a direct glucose–insulin mechanism. Thus, IGFB/NAT2/SH2B1 should be viewed as hypothesis-generating, pending replication and functional validation.

These differences highlight the influence of regional genetic drift, founder effects, and distinct environmental pressures on T2DM risk [3]. Exploratory unsupervised clustering grouped SNP-entry effect sizes into three clusters, suggesting descriptive patterns that may be consistent with broad functional themes; however, these patterns are hypothesis-generating and not mechanistic evidence. Exploratory random-forest results indicated that gene/locus labels dominated variable importance; this likely reflects locus identity (“feature leakage”) rather than generalizable predictors. Accordingly, RF importance is interpreted descriptively and not as mechanistic evidence.

These results help prioritize candidate loci for replication and downstream validation in Egyptian and Sham/Levant populations. Given the heterogeneity in Sham/Levant studies and the imprecision of several large single-study estimates, clinical translation should not be inferred from the current evidence. Larger, harmonized multicenter studies with standardized genetic contrasts, consistent effect-allele alignment, and control for population structure are needed to validate these signals.Conversely, the identification of NAT2 and SH2B1 as high-impact loci in the sham dataset calls for targeted replication and functional studies in these populations to validate their roles and possibly integrate them into population-specific screening programs. Furthermore, the pronounced heterogeneity observed among sham studies highlights the need for larger, harmonized, multicenter investigations using standardized genotyping platforms and rigorous controls for population stratification to ensure that true genetic signals are distinguished from background noise [4, 13].

Our study had some limitations. First, the dominance of Egyptian datasets (~ 75% of the included studies) may have biased the pooled results and obscured subtler region-specific effects (Egypt Genome Project [2]. Second, reliance on summary-level data (published odds ratios and CIs) prevents more nuanced analyses of gene–gene or gene–environment interactions that individual participant data could enable. Third, although we performed thorough assessments of heterogeneity and publication bias, residual confounding from unreported factors (e.g., age, body mass index, and lifestyle differences) cannot be excluded. Fourth, while the use of simulated protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks as shown in Fig. 2, in our analysis is informative for suggesting biological connections, it requires validation through laboratory functional experiments to confirm the mechanistic pathways involved in the process.

Future research should address these limitations by increasing cohort diversity, especially by including more participants from underrepresented populations, and by incorporating multi-omics approaches (such as transcriptomics, epigenomics, and metabolomics) to unravel the biological mechanisms linking these genetic variants to T2DM. Using longitudinal cohort designs and Mendelian randomization frameworks could further help clarify the causal relationships between the identified variants and diabetes risk. Ultimately, our findings lay the foundation for precision medicine strategies in the Middle East, emphasizing both pan-regional risk markers and population-specific variants to improve the prevention, screening, and support future population-specific genetic research and validation efforts.

In summary, this meta-analysis highlights the distinct genetic architectures of T2DM susceptibility in Egyptian and sham populations. The Egyptian population demonstrated consistently large-effect risk variants, whereas the sham population displayed more variable associations. We identified shared genetic markers (TCF7L2, ADIPOQ, and KCNJ11) and population-specific loci (IGFB, NAT2, and SH2B1), providing a basis for replication, fine-mapping, and functional validation in the Arab world. Larger, well-powered genomic studies across the Middle East are needed to replicate and validate these signals, clarify population-specific effects, and determine their potential clinical relevance.

Conclusion

This PRISMA-compliant meta-analysis synthesized evidence from 42 candidate-gene case–control studies (29 Egyptian and 13 Levant/Sham) to summarize reported T2DM-associated variants in underrepresented Arab populations. The Egyptian dataset showed comparatively consistent signals, with strongest support for variants in TCF7L2, ADIPOQ, KCNJ11, MTHFR, GCKR, and IGF-related loci, whereas Levant/Sham studies showed substantial heterogeneity, with notable associations reported for NAT2 and SH2B1, and additional signals in KCNQ1. Overall, the findings suggest both shared and population-contextual patterns of genetic association; however, several estimates were imprecise (very large ORs with wide confidence intervals), underscoring the need for cautious interpretation. Future work should prioritize larger, harmonized multicenter studies with standardized genetic contrasts, consistent effect-allele alignment, and appropriate control for population structure, alongside functional follow-up, to validate and refine these candidate signals.

Limitations

Most included studies used candidate-gene case–control designs with modest sample sizes and uneven country representation (Egypt > Sham/Levant), which may limit generalizability and contribute to small-study effects, including inflated or unstable estimates when genotype counts were sparse. Substantial heterogeneity, particularly in the Sham/Levant synthesis, likely reflects differences in population structure, phenotyping, genotyping platforms, and analytical approaches across different settings. Although BH-FDR correction retained statistical significance for the Sham/Levant variants, interpretation of nominal (uncorrected) p-values can inflate false positives when many SNPs are evaluated. False positives remain possible, particularly for single-study estimates and extreme odds ratios with wide confidence intervals; therefore, such findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and prioritized for replication. Although effect alleles (as reported) were harmonized and inverse-variance methods with sensitivity analyses were applied, residual confounding, publication or selection bias, and population stratification cannot be fully excluded because individual-level data and consistent covariate adjustments were unavailable. Accordingly, statistically significant associations identified in this meta-analysis should not be interpreted as evidence of biological causality in the absence of independent replication and functional validation. Therefore, larger, harmonized multicenter studies with replication and functional validation are required.
