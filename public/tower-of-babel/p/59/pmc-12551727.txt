Superior Epigastric Artery Perforator Flap for Chest and Abdominal Reconstruction: Anatomical Study and Clinical Application
Authors: Matthew H. Loper, Mohamed Elkahly, Suhair Maqusi, Guilherme C. Barreiro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Tissue flaps based on the superior epigastric artery (SEA) and its perforators have been described previously. We present a study of the perforators of the SEA with anatomical cadaveric studies, clinical applications, and vessel imaging. The SEA perforator emerges just below the margin of the rib cage and supplies the skin transversely, making it a strong choice for locoregional chest and abdominal wall reconstruction. This flap offers reduced donor site morbidity by sparing the fascia, rectus abdominis, and lateral oblique muscles. This study presented an SEA perforator flap using anatomical and clinical studies.Methods:Aqueous contrast was injected into the internal thoracic arteries of 35 fresh cadavers. A total of 114 SEA perforators were dissected (62 right, 52 left), with vessel caliber and location noted on x–y axes. After achieving a reproducible technique, 5 patients underwent chest wall reconstruction and 1 underwent abdominal wall reconstruction using SEA perforator–based flaps.Results:The mean arterial caliber of SEA perforators was 0.68 mm (SD = 0.31 mm), with an average location of 2.66 cm in the x direction and 4.9 cm in the y direction (xiphoid process = (0,0)). No significant differences in vessel caliber or location were observed between sides. In clinical cases, donor site dimensions averaged 19.3 × 7.2 cm, and primary closure of donor sites resulted in no major complications.Conclusions:Flaps based on SEA perforators are versatile, reliable, and reproducible; thus, they represent a strong choice for chest and abdominal wall reconstructions.

Takeaways

Question: What are the anatomical characteristics and clinical applications of the superior epigastric artery (SEA) perforator flap, as investigated augmenting the available knowledge regarding its use in reconstructive surgery?

Findings: The study involved anatomical dissection of 114 SEA perforators in cadavers, revealing a mean arterial caliber of 0.68 mm. Five clinical cases demonstrated successful chest and abdominal wall reconstructions with no major complications and effective donor site closures.

Meaning: SEA perforator flaps are reliable and versatile options for locoregional reconstruction, offering reduced donor site morbidity and strong outcomes in chest and abdominal wall surgery.

INTRODUCTION

The abdominal wall provides a large area with an abundant vascular supply and tissue that can be used in locoregional or microvascular free flaps. It has been a popular source of flaps since the late 1980s, when Koshima and Soeda introduced the deep inferior epigastric artery perforator flap. The flap has been considered a reliable source of tissue for breast reconstruction, as well as for head, neck, and extremity reconstructions.1 Their deep inferior epigastric artery perforator flap technique excluded the underlying rectus muscle and was composed exclusively of the underlying skin and subcutaneous fat.2 Preserving the muscle has been suggested to reduce donor site morbidity and retain the underlying muscle’s blood supply.3

The superior epigastric artery (SEA) is considered the counterpart of the deep inferior epigastric artery, supplying the anterior abdominal wall and communicating with it through a series of anastomosing vessels.4 Similarly to the deep inferior epigastric artery, the SEA also gives off a series of perforators to the skin of the superior abdominal wall, which can be used to design perforator flaps. This perforator emerges immediately below the margin of the rib cage and supplies the skin in a transverse orientation to the midline.5 As such, the SEA could be useful for designing flaps from this area of the abdominal wall. It has also been noted that the SEA perforator (SEAP) flap is a strong reconstructive option due to decreased donor site morbidity, owing to the simplicity with which it can be harvested.6 SEAP flaps have been previously described by Uemura7 and others, who described vertical and transverse flaps using these perforators.8–10 We will revisit the SEAP flap through direct anatomical study, clinical cases, and clear vessel imaging.

This study described the SEA and its perforators. First, the anatomical location and caliber of the SEAP were explored in a cadaveric study. Second, the application of the SEAP flap in a clinical setting was described.

METHODS

Anatomical Study

The superior epigastric vessels emerge immediately below the lower costal rib cage margin. After entering the anterior abdominal wall, they give off numerous branches, such as the cutaneous perforating branches originating from the deep SEA. These perforators traverse the rectus abdominis muscle to supply the skin in an ipsilateral transverse orientation. Because the SEAP runs along the inferior margin of the rib cage, it may be thought of as analogous to the internal thoracic artery’s 6 intercostal perforators that run transversely in the intercostal spaces from the first to the seventh rib. Therefore, some articles will refer to it as the seventh internal mammary artery perforator of the intercostal space.7 In 35 cadaver dissections, aqueous contrast was injected into the internal thoracic artery through a muscle window in the second intercostal space, and cautious dissection was performed to expose the cutaneous perforators emerging through the aponeurosis over the rectus abdominis muscle inferior to the xiphoid process.

Initially, as noted earlier, we accessed the internal thoracic artery in the second intercostal space. Intra-arterial aqueous dye infusion facilitated the identification of SEAPs, which were then bilaterally isolated from the xiphoid process. In all dissections, it was possible to isolate at least 1 perforator on each side with a caliber greater than 0.3 mm, located at a mean distance of 4.9 cm from the xiphoid process on the longitudinal axis and 2.66 cm on the transverse axis (Fig. 1).

Clinical Cases

Following approval by the ethics committee for the analysis of research projects of the HCFMUSP (Hospital das Clínicas, Faculty of Medicine, University of São Paulo) and signed written patient consent forms, the surgical technique based on the SEAP devised in the anatomical study was applied to 6 reconstructive cases. Patients were selected based on the need for chest or abdominal wall reconstruction, particularly with nonviable skin surrounding the wound, where a flap based on the SEAP could offer a locoregional approach to reconstruction. Five chest wall reconstructions and 1 abdominal wall reconstruction were performed. Indications for the chest reconstructions included (1) wide local excision of a squamous cell carcinoma, (2) third-degree burns to the breast and sternum (Fig. 2), (3) irradiation of presternal cancer, and (4 and 5) 2 recurrent keloid scars (Fig. 3), and 1 abdominal wall reconstruction following wide local excision of a large melanoma, as described in Table 1. Patients were counseled regarding surgical risks. Informed consent was obtained for the surgical procedures. Patients underwent a selective medical work-up for surgical risk stratification.

Alternative reconstructive flaps were considered and discussed with the patients. Muscle-only flaps were excluded, as they would likely entail the need for skin grafting, limiting the elasticity and pliability that would be provided by a skin flap. Pliable, healthy skin was a requirement for the reconstructions, due to the indications listed earlier. The flaps were designed based on the SEAP and sized to optimally reconstruct the target defects, with 2 flaps proceeding past the anterior axillary line. The horizontal orientation of the flap was preferred because it included the primary angiosome, in contrast to the secondary perforator angiosome associated with a vertical flap orientation. Computed tomography angiography of the internal thoracic vessels and their branches (including the SEA and SEAP) was performed for 1 patient to aid in preoperative planning of the flap (Fig. 4). The remaining flaps were planned according to anatomical landmarks described by the cadaveric dissections. The mean dimensions of the donor flaps were 19.3 × 7.2 cm with a mean estimated area of 141.3 cm2. RESULTS

Anatomical Study

Following dye injection, 114 perforators were isolated, of which 38 (33.3%) were from female cadavers and 76 (66.7%) were from male cadavers. On the right side, 62 perforators were dissected, with a mean arterial caliber of 0.68 mm and a SD of 0.31 mm. The calibers were homogeneous in relation to the 52 perforators dissected on the left side. The minimum size considered was 0.3 mm, and the maximum size found was 1.7 mm for both sides. There were an average of 1.8 perforators on the right and 1.5 on the left per patient.

All perforators were placed on a graph according to their location relative to the xiphoid process, with the x axis oriented in the left–right direction and the y axis in the craniocaudal direction. On the right side, the perforators were at a mean distance of 4.9 cm (SD = 3.4 cm) from the xiphoid on the y axis, and 2.8 cm (SD = 1.2 cm) on the x axis. On the left side, the perforators were at a mean distance of 4.9 cm (SD = 2.7 cm) from the xiphoid on the y axis, and 2.5 cm (SD = 1.3 cm) on the x axis. Clinical Cases

All donor sites were closed primarily with drains placed, and no major donor site complications (including dehiscence, infection, or injury to the underlying structures) were noted (Fig. 8). Patients were admitted for an average of 2 days before discharge. It was noted that none of the patients experienced any major postoperative complications during the follow-up period, which was an average of 6 months per patient. Two (40.0%) patients experienced distal flap necrosis, which was managed conservatively.

DISCUSSION

The SEA diverges from the internal thoracic artery and travels through the anterior rectus muscle to supply the superoanterior abdominal wall. As it does so, we found that it gives off an average of 1.8 perforators on the right side and 1.5 perforators on the left side, a finding that was similar to that of Offman et al11 who demonstrated a total of 5 ± 1. The perforators of interest were located just inferior to the lower margin of the rib cage.4,10 Specifically, they were found on average located at a point 2.66 cm lateral and 4.9 cm caudal to the xiphoid process. This finding has been confirmed by Schmidt et al,12 as well as by Hamdi et al13 who found the perforators primarily distributed within an area between 1.5 and 6.5 cm in the x direction, and between 3 and 16 cm in the y direction. Our findings, as well as many other similar findings in the literature, suggest that the location of these perforators can be considered reliable for flap planning.7,12,14 The mean arterial caliber of the SEAPs in our study was 0.68 mm (SD = 0.31 mm). This value is supported by Offman et al,11 who demonstrated a caliber of 0.6 ± 0.2. As mentioned earlier, the SEAP can also be considered to be the seventh internal mammary artery perforator of the intercostal space. Kalender et al14 described the use of such a flap in breast reconstruction for burn sequelae using propeller rotation of an SEAP fasciocutaneous flap. Flap sizes varied from 6 × 11 cm to 7 × 12.5 cm.15 As in our study, this demonstrates that the SEAPs have sufficient caliber to perfuse islands of skin in adjacent areas, warranting consideration of the flap for locoregional reconstructions in clinical practice.

In our analyses, no correlation was found between the location of the SEAP and side (left or right). Additionally, no correlation was found between the vessel caliber and side. There was no correlation between the location of the perforators and sex of the patient, with the notable exception of a significantly smaller x coordinate for perforators on the left side of female subjects compared with their male counterparts. We suspect that this was likely due in part to the fact that fewer female cadavers were dissected (33.3%), and not due to a true anatomical difference. Patient age, weight, and height also showed no correlation with vessel caliber or location. Finally, the vessel caliber was not correlated with the location of a given vessel.

The SEAP flap design is highly versatile, allowing reconstruction of defects through advancement, rotation, transposition, or use as a propeller flap. Applications of the SEAP flap include, but are not limited to, chest wall, abdominal wall, and breast locoregional reconstructions, or use as a free flap. This flap can be considered for a wide array of indications, including sternal wounds, keloid scars, burns, and defect closure after cancer removal. Additionally, using this flap design as a direct cutaneous perforator or adipocutaneous flap allows for quick and reliable elevation.10 Finally, this flap has the additional benefit of reduced donor site morbidity, as it spares the fascia and underlying rectus abdominis and lateral oblique muscles. Our study demonstrated the clinical success of the SEAP flap, through its application to our 5 chest wall reconstructions and 1 abdominal wall reconstruction, with minimal associated morbidity. Future studies may be aimed at further applications of the SEAP flap, including its clinical use in aesthetic surgery, as proposed by Kundu et al.15 Limitations of the SEAP flap include a relatively short pedicle length, leading to restriction of the propeller flap reconstruction to surrounding areas, as well as narrow perforator caliber. Additionally, we had 2 cases of distal flap tip necrosis, likely related to a longer flap design extending past the anterior axillary line in both patients, both of which were managed conservatively. Similar complications have also been reported by Hamdi et al,13 who observed a small amount of distal flap necrosis in 2 out of 7 clinical cases using the flap, as well as in other studies.7,10 As such, the SEAP flap can be considered a useful and reliable flap for chest and abdominal wall reconstruction.
