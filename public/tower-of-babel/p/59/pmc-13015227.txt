Double inferior vena cava with azygous continuation: A rare anatomical variant demonstrated on contrast-enhanced CT
Authors: Jawdat M Alali, Mahmoud Tabouni, Ahmad L Yasin, Abdulmalik Mazooz Jaber, Mohammad Odeh, Nissar M Shaikh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuplicated inferior vena cava (DIVC) is a rare congenital vascular anomaly first described in 1916 during autopsy, with a reported incidence ranging from 0.2% to 3% and intraoperative detection rates between 0.2% and 0.6%. Although usually asymptomatic and discovered incidentally, DIVC has significant implications during surgery and interventional procedures. We report the case of a 49-year-old female with type 2 diabetes mellitus, end-stage renal disease on regular hemodialysis, and a history of failed renal transplants, who presented with fever, cough, and acute respiratory distress. She was diagnosed with Streptococcus pneumoniae pneumonia and required intensive care admission with mechanical ventilation, vasopressor support, and dialysis. During recovery, she developed abdominal pain and diarrhea. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the abdomen ruled out gastrointestinal pathology but incidentally revealed a DIVC. Imaging demonstrated a normal right-sided inferior vena cava (IVC) and an additional left-sided IVC draining the left common iliac vein. The left renal vein drained into the left IVC, which ascended cranially with azygous continuation, ultimately draining into the superior vena cava, while a rudimentary venous connection was also noted between the right and left IVCs. The intrahepatic segment of the right IVC drained normally into the right atrium. This case highlights the incidental detection of a duplicated IVC with azygous continuation, a rare but clinically significant venous anomaly, and emphasizes that awareness of such variations is essential for surgeons, radiologists, and intensivists, particularly in the context of retroperitoneal surgery, transplantation, or IVC filter insertion, where unrecognized anomalies may increase procedural risk.Keywords: Duplicated inferior vena cava, Inferior vena cava anomalies, Azygous vein, Congenital venous anomalies, Multidetector CT, Vascular variations

Introduction

The first case of inferior vena cava (IVC) duplication was described in 1916 during an autopsy by Lucas in London [1]. Since then, this rare congenital anomaly has been reported with an incidence ranging from 0.2% to 3%, with an average of approximately 1.5%, although intraoperative detection rates remain considerably lower at 0.2%-0.6% [2,3]. Most congenital IVC anomalies are clinically silent and are frequently discovered incidentally during imaging studies performed for unrelated reasons. Despite their asymptomatic nature, these anomalies have significant clinical implications, particularly in the context of retroperitoneal surgery, vascular interventions, and interventional radiology procedures [4].

Duplication of the inferior vena cava (DIVC) is characterized by the presence of a normal right-sided IVC accompanied by an additional left-sided venous channel. The left-sided IVC typically ascends to the level of the renal veins, where it communicates with the right-sided IVC either anterior or posterior to the aorta. Above the renal veins, a single right-sided IVC is usually present, whereas below the renal level, parallel venous structures can be identified on both sides of the aorta. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) is the imaging modality of choice for identifying DIVC, with classic features including a single right-sided IVC above the renal veins, a transverse venous connection crossing the aorta at the renal level, and duplicated venous channels on either side of the aorta below the renal veins [3]. Anatomical variants may also include continuation into the azygous or hemiazygous veins, further modifying normal venous return pathways and introducing additional considerations during surgical or interventional procedures.

Although often asymptomatic, recognition of DIVC is crucial because unawareness of the anomaly can lead to misinterpretation of imaging studies, inadvertent vascular injury, or complications during procedures such as central venous catheterization, retroperitoneal surgery, kidney transplantation, or placement of IVC filters. Accurate preoperative or preprocedural identification allows for appropriate planning and avoidance of potentially serious complications.

Here, we report a case of incidentally detected DIVC with azygous continuation in a critically ill patient, highlighting the importance of awareness and precise radiological evaluation of this vascular anomaly. This case underscores the need for clinicians and radiologists to consider congenital variations of the IVC when interpreting imaging studies and planning interventions in the retroperitoneal or thoracic venous system.

Case presentation

A 49-year-old female with a complex medical background presented to the emergency department with respiratory symptoms. Her history included type 2 diabetes mellitus managed with oral hypoglycemic agents, end-stage renal failure on regular hemodialysis via a right internal jugular central venous catheter, and 2 prior renal transplants that had failed more than 2 decades earlier.

She reported a 3-day history of fever and cough that progressed to shortness of breath and tachypnea. On arrival, her blood pressure was initially stable, but within 2 hours she developed dizziness and hypotension (70/42 mmHg). Chest radiography revealed right basal and middle zone infiltrates, supported by elevated inflammatory markers. Empirical intravenous piperacillin–tazobactam was initiated, along with fluid resuscitation and noradrenaline infusion for hemodynamic support.

Subsequent chest CT confirmed consolidation in the right lower and middle lobes consistent with community-acquired pneumonia. The patient deteriorated and was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU), requiring endotracheal intubation, mechanical ventilation, and ongoing dialysis. Within 24 hours, sputum cultures grew Streptococcus pneumoniae, which was sensitive to the chosen antibiotic regimen. By day 4, her condition improved, and she was successfully weaned off mechanical ventilation, followed by extubation on day 6.

During recovery, she developed new abdominal symptoms, including cramping pain, watery diarrhea, and fresh rectal bleeding. Stool studies, including Clostridium difficile toxin assay, were negative. Nevertheless, given the severity of symptoms, an abdominal CT with contrast was performed to evaluate for possible gastrointestinal pathology. This study incidentally revealed a duplicated IVC (DIVC), a rare congenital venous anomaly.

On axial contrast-enhanced CT (Fig. 1), 2 distinct IVCs were visualized: a normal right-sided IVC (blue arrow) and an additional left-sided IVC (orange arrow). The left renal vein drained directly into the left IVC (white arrow), which continued cranially in parallel with the right IVC. At the suprarenal level, both right and left IVCs were identifiable. Notably, the left IVC demonstrated a venous connection to the azygous vein, which, in turn, drained into the superior vena cava (Figs. 1D–F).

Coronal reconstructions with maximal intensity projection (Fig. 2) further delineated this venous configuration. The right IVC followed its expected course, draining the right common iliac vein (black arrow), while the left IVC drained the left common iliac vein (yellow arrow). Apart from this vascular anomaly, no additional abdominal pathology was identified. The patient’s gastrointestinal symptoms improved significantly with supportive care, after which she was transferred from intensive care unit (ICU) to the ward on day 9 and later discharged home in stable condition.

Discussion

Congenital anomalies of the IVC, including duplication, are rare but clinically significant, particularly in patients undergoing surgery or vascular interventions. The earliest reported case of IVC duplication appeared in 1912 in the Anatomical Record [4]. Anatomically, the adult IVC is divided into 4 segments—hepatic, suprarenal, renal, and infrarenal—each with distinct embryologic origins. Its formation involves a complex process of anastomosis and regression among 3 paired embryonic veins: the posterior cardinal, subcardinal, and supracardinal veins [5,6]. Persistence or regression of these veins can result in over 14 known anatomic variants, including double IVC, left IVC, retroaortic left renal vein, circum-aortic renal vein, and azygous continuation [7]. Duplication of the IVC arises from the persistence of both right and left supracardinal veins [8].

Recognition of DIVC is clinically important during retroperitoneal surgery, transplantation, central venous catheterization, and IVC filter placement to prevent complications such as inadvertent injury, hemorrhage, or recurrent pulmonary embolism [8]. Azygous continuation, resulting from failure of the right subcardinal-hepatic anastomosis, alters venous return pathways and may complicate cardiopulmonary bypass, cardiac catheterization, or interpretation of thoracic imaging [5,7]. Multiple venous anomalies can coexist in the same patient, such as a double IVC with retroaortic left renal vein and azygous continuation, creating complex hemodynamic pathways [9].

Multidetector CT has become the imaging modality of choice for evaluating these anomalies, as it provides high-resolution 2D and 3D reconstructions that accurately delineate thoracoabdominal venous anatomy [5]. Awareness of such anomalies is also essential for radiologists to avoid misinterpretation of aberrant vessels as lymphadenopathy or mediastinal masses. While studies suggest that IVC duplication alone does not necessarily increase thromboembolism risk, knowledge of these variations remains crucial for procedural planning and prevention of complications, particularly in transplant candidates [8,10].

In our patient, the incidental discovery of a duplicated IVC with azygous continuation highlights the importance of meticulous imaging review in critically ill patients. Although asymptomatic, such anomalies have significant implications for surgical and interventional planning, as well as for accurate interpretation of imaging studies.

Conclusion

Duplicated IVC with azygous continuation is a rare congenital anomaly that may remain asymptomatic but carries significant clinical relevance. Recognition of this variation is crucial for surgeons, radiologists, and intensivists, particularly in the context of retroperitoneal surgery, organ transplantation, and placement of IVC filters. Unawareness of such anomalies can lead to serious complications, including vascular injury, hemorrhage, or misplacement of interventional devices. Our case emphasizes the need for careful preoperative and preprocedural imaging review to identify venous anomalies, enabling appropriate planning and risk mitigation. Furthermore, incidental detection of DIVC underscores the broader importance of considering vascular variations during routine imaging, which can prevent misdiagnosis and optimize patient safety in complex clinical scenarios.
