Exploring public perceptions of owl species in KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa: Implications for conservation management
Authors: Lindiswa Buthelezi, Mfundo S. T. Maseko, Siboniso Magoso, Colleen T. Downs
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn a changing world, effective wildlife conservation requires translating ecology into management. This emphasises the need to understand the relationships between humans and wildlife, as the survival of often misunderstood species, such as bats, reptiles, and owls, is influenced by environmental factors and cultural and social values associated with them. Ethno-scientific approaches, such as structured questionnaires, have proven invaluable in bridging this gap. We investigated people’s perceptions, attitudes, and emotions towards owls in KwaZulu-Natal Province, South Africa.MethodsWe used a mixed-methods approach (face-to-face interviews and online questionnaires). A total of 904 questionnaires were conducted via face-to-face interviews (simple random sampling, n = 802) and online interviews (simple random sampling, n = 102); only 681 responses from the participants met the inclusion criteria. We analysed our data using cumulative link mixed models (CLMMs).ResultsRespondents mostly perceived owl species negatively, associated them with witchcraft, and were mostly unaware of the ecological importance of owl species. People’s perceptions resulted from an interplay of multifaceted variables, not just socio-demographic variables. We found that positive perceptions of owl species were influenced by an interplay of multifaceted factors, including a higher tolerance for owl species, a general understanding of the ecological importance of owl species to both agriculture and the environment, and positive feelings. We also found that socio-demographic variables alone were not significant predictors of people’s perceptions of owl species.ConclusionsFuture conservation initiatives should lean more towards implementing culturally sensitive education programmes that aim to communicate the ecological importance of owls, while also respectfully engaging with beliefs about witchcraft and religious beliefs linked to owl species. These have the potential to improve people’s tolerance of owl species and also foster positive perceptions and interactions with owl species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13002-025-00826-9.

Background

The Endangered Species Act emphasises the need to understand species’ recreational and cultural values to implement effective conservation strategies, highlighting that conventional conservation strategies, though effective, have limitations [1]. These gaps can be addressed by bridging the divide between ecology and the social sciences, through an understanding of human perceptions of species and by actively engaging humans in conservation initiatives [2, 3]. Human perceptions of species have significant implications for wildlife conservation, influencing how people want species that are linked to their cultural/religious beliefs handled; as a result, species viewed positively or admired are protected, while those viewed negatively are persecuted, repudiated, and intentionally killed [4–7]. Therefore, species’ survival depends on environmental factors and the social and cultural values associated with them [8–10].

The primary driver of these negative perceptions is the existence of cultural beliefs that have been passed down through generations via folklore [6]. Folklore is a significant body of expressive culture that encompasses myths, oral history, and superstitious beliefs [11]. In many cultures, folklore is believed to be true because they are told through/using tradition, which makes people believe in their authenticity [4, 10, 12], such as the existence of a common belief that birds are important in connecting humans and the world of spirits [9, 10, 13–15]. Folkloric beliefs have a role in shaping the way animals are socially perceived and condition human attitudes towards them [5, 9, 10, 16].

Birds of prey have, unfortunately, been heavily influenced by existing myths and traditions, resulting in complex and contradictory interpretations within and among distinct cultures [3, 4, 10]. Owls are one of the few bird species that have gathered contradictory beliefs in many parts of the world, leading to their persecution and, in some cultures, admiration [5, 8, 9, 12, 15, 17–22]. For instance, in many cultures, there exists a belief that owl species are birds of ill omen, messengers of bad luck, servants of death, devils’ birds, indicators of sickness and misfortune, associated with evil spirits and witches [5, 9, 15, 22–27]. This has resulted in owl species being intentionally killed for or against witchcraft, thereby reducing their population size [15]. However, not all owl species are perceived negatively; in some regions of India, pale-coloured owl species are considered to bring wealth and prosperity [9]. Although this belief is seemingly positive, it has resulted in the persecution of owl species, as some believe that killing owls keeps wealth within their families [9]. In some regions, owls are associated with religious beliefs, such as the connection to the Hindu Goddess Laxmi in India [9] and the Greek Goddess Athena in the city of Athens, Greece [28].

The vital ecosystem services provided by owl species are clouded by fear and the existence of cultural beliefs associated with witchcraft and bad omens [5]. For instance, owl species are effective biological control agents against rodents, insects, and other vertebrate species [5, 6, 22]. Rodents are agricultural pests threatening food security in many parts of the world, as they damage crops, such as maize (Zea mays) crops [5, 29–32], resulting in increased production costs and reduced productivity [33]. In many countries, including Israel, Malaysia, Indonesia, Switzerland, and the USA, farmers have sought to use Barn Owls (Tyto alba) to control rodent populations, and this strategy has been a considerable success [5, 28, 34–36]. However, in African countries, particularly South Africa, this method has not been widely used as owls are believed to be a bad omen and widely associated with witchcraft [5, 15], and only a few tolerate owls around their homes, but would rather chase them away or kill them [23, 24].

Addressing human–wildlife conflicts requires understanding public perceptions, cultural beliefs, and experiences with wildlife, in order to develop targeted educational strategies that primarily aim to address knowledge gaps about the importance of each species in maintaining the balance of various ecosystems [3, 10, 37, 38]. Since cultural beliefs and perceptions held by communities have the potential to provide valuable insights into species behaviour and distribution, they therefore influence people’s attitudes towards the species [10]. Recent research has suggested that integrating citizen science with local ecological knowledge is crucial, as this approach has the potential to lead to more inclusive and effective conservation strategies, thereby allowing for a deeper connection between communities and their natural environments [39]. Ethno-scientific methods, such as questionnaires, are invaluable in translating ecology into management [3, 37, 40]. Over the past decade, the use of questionnaires in ecology has increased significantly [40, 41]. Questionnaires are particularly effective for addressing various ecological topics, including evaluating public perceptions of ecological management, conducting large-scale studies, analysing the effects of human activities on wildlife, and facilitating interdisciplinary research that combines both ecological and non-ecological components [37, 40, 42], while also providing the best means of obtaining quantitative data from many sites [40].

Research on human–owl conflicts in South Africa, especially in urban–rural mosaic landscapes, is scarce despite the prevalence of cultural beliefs and potential implications for owl populations. This study addresses this gap by assessing people’s perceptions and attitudes towards owls and determining if a human–owl conflict exists. The objectives of this study were to (1) assess people’s perceptions, general knowledge, attitudes, and behaviours towards owls in KwaZulu-Natal; (2) identify the cultural and social factors that contribute to positive, neutral, and negative perceptions and feelings. We predicted that (1) feelings and perceptions of owls would be influenced by a combination of cultural beliefs, personal experiences, and demographic factors, such as age, level of education, gender, and residential areas (i.e. rural or urban); and (2) people’s perceptions of owl species will vary significantly across age groups, and educational levels, with negative perceptions more commonly observed among older individuals and those with lower levels of formal education.

Methods

Study area

KwaZulu-Natal is the second largest province in South Africa, covering 7.7% of the total surface area [42] (Fig. 1) and the second most populated province in South Africa, with a population of approximately 12.4 million people [43]. The climate in KwaZulu-Natal ranges from sub-tropical to moderate [44]. KwaZulu-Natal has a mean annual rainfall that ranges from 650 (in the eastern Grasslands) to 1400 mm (in the eastern Coastal Bushveld), with KwaZulu-Natal’s Central Bushveld receiving around 900 mm [45]. However, most of its rainfall is between October and March in the austral summer period [45].Fig. 1Map of KwaZulu-Natal showing locations where questionnaires on people’s perceptions and attitudes towards owl species were collected online and in person

KwaZulu-Natal is a province rich in cultural heritage, with the majority (80%) of its population being Zulu people [46, 47], who are well known for maintaining their traditional beliefs and practices, many of which include cultural taboos and spiritual symbolism. Though some Zulu people are Christians [48], and fall under different religious groups such as Shembe, African Zionist, Apostles, Gospel, and Lutheran churches, they still strongly believe in the teachings relayed to them by their elders and for some, such as the African Zionist and Shembe, believe in their ancestors (which is also largely influenced by location) [49]. The geographical variations, cultural complexity, and ecological richness make KwaZulu-Natal an appealing study area for diverse perceptions of people living in various parts of the province.

For this study, we used a mixed-methods approach, face-to-face interviews, and an online questionnaire to assess public perceptions of owls in KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa (Fig. 1). Face-to-face interviews were conducted between June and July 2024 in selected rural communities in the Northeast coastal region, including Mtubatuba (28.4141° S, 32.1840° E) (KwaMsane, Madwaleni, Shikishela, Ogengele, Ophondweni, Nkombose, Somkhele, Mapheleni, and Nkodibe), Mthunzini (28.9533° S, 31.7416° E) (Ongoye, Oyemeni reserve, Gwabalanda, Enqoleni, and Phongola), and Mpangeni (28.7590° S, 31.9018° E) (KwaDlangezwa) (Fig. 1). These areas were chosen for their rural characteristics and cultural diversity under traditional leadership systems. The online questionnaire was distributed from June 2024 to March 2025 and was targeted at participants across the province. Responses were received from rural, peri-urban, and urban areas, increasing the study’s geographical and demographic scope. Southern Africa is home to 12 owl species belonging to the Order Strigiformes, and Families Tytonidae and Strigidae [50]. In KwaZulu-Natal, owls are collectively referred to as Isikhova among Zulu people [51].

Data collection and survey sampling

For this study, we employed structured questionnaires as our data collection tool, as they have been used in several countries to assess people’s perceptions of raptors [e.g. 3, 6, 12, 24, 33, 42, 52–54]. From January to June 2024, we developed a questionnaire (Supplementary Information, Table S1). Once developed, the questionnaire was reviewed to address repetition of questions, spelling errors, and ambiguity [40, 55]. During the questionnaire development phase, we consulted with traditional leaders of the target communities to obtain formal consent for conducting research within their areas. The final questionnaire included 41 questions written in both English and IsiZulu, a commonly spoken language in KwaZulu-Natal, covering various aspects including demographic and social information, general knowledge about owl species such as key features people use to identify owls and their habitats, and lastly perceptions of owl species which included people awareness of the ecosystem services provided by owl species and relative importance of owl species in the environment and agriculture etc. Respondents were also shown pictures of Barn Owls (Tyto alba) and Spotted Eagle Owls (Bubo africanus) to identify which species they may have encountered in their residential areas.

Data were collected using face-to-face interviews and an online questionnaire via Google Forms (http://surl.li/scuurf). The questionnaires targeted people 18 years and above because of ethical considerations. Therefore, the study excluded all children and young individuals [4]. Online interviews were administered using the simple random sampling approach and completed by 102 participants, and it was designed to take 15–20 min. to complete. The online questionnaire was distributed through various platforms, including Email, Facebook®, WhatsApp®, and Instagram®. Facebook® was selected as the primary platform because it is the most widely used social media platform across all age groups in South Africa [56]. This platform’s broad reach made it ideal for gathering data from diverse respondents across KwaZulu-Natal. Online data collection provided an opportunity to conduct surveys targeted at otherwise difficult-to-reach populations, thereby reducing socially desirable responses [53, 57–59]. However, a problem arises when participants browse the questionnaire out of curiosity [59]. We ensured that the questions in the online questionnaire were structured so that they did not suggest a response. We included an equal number of negative and positive options, neutral midpoints, and inclusive language to avoid confusion for the responder. We also encouraged responders to complete the online survey in a controlled environment and minimise external influences.

We administered face-to-face interviews using the simple random sampling approach [42, 60], which allowed potential participants to have an equal chance of being selected, and every household in the closest proximity was selected. This resulted in 802 interviews being completed by study participants. Face-to-face interviews were conducted by approaching community members walking on the streets and approaching their homes. People were first informed of the purpose of the study before participating in interviews, and after they gave their informed (verbal) consent, the interview process began. To minimise responder bias, one interviewer interviewed each responder in the absence of other study participants, which allowed for a more honest reply; therefore, other participants did not influence responses. The main advantage of face-to-face interviews is that they allow us to rephrase questions for clarity and correct misunderstandings, encouraging respondents to participate fully without needing to write, which might encourage the interviewee to complete the interview [41].

Perceptions analyses

A total of 904 interviews were conducted and screened, and 681 met the inclusion criteria and were selected for the final analyses. Responses were excluded if they were (1) incomplete interviews, where respondents were either unable or chose not to complete the interview process; (2) people who responded that owls are not birds and could not identify owls from the images given, (3) respondents who completed the online survey but did not reside in KwaZulu-Natal, and/or had not lived in KwaZulu-Natal for more than five years.

The questionnaire had three main areas of interest: (1) demographic variables, (2) general knowledge of owls, and (3) perceptions and attitudes towards owls. These sections included both open-ended and fixed-response questions. From the questionnaires, we obtained two types of responses that reflected peoples’ perceptions of owl species and their general knowledge: (1) fixed or close-ended, such as multiple-choice answers, and Yes/No/Maybe/Not sure, which was a more effective way of gathering quantitative data; (2) open-ended questions, which allowed respondents to provide a detailed response based on their understanding; and are an effective way of gathering qualitative data. We coded open-ended questions by identifying patterns, divergences, and similarities in responses provided by the interviewees. This also allowed for a comprehensive expression of thoughts and insights necessary for analysing ecological knowledge. Therefore, qualitative responses were organised into categories, allowing for quantitative interpretations of the unknown variable within the dataset. Our questionnaire included respondents’ demographic information, such as their age, level of education, gender, and type of settlement, which reflected aspects of respondents that we hypothesised would influence their perceptions of owl species.

Statistical analyses

We analysed our data using ordinal regression models, which are widely used in ethnobiology for examining the relationship between predictor variables and ordinal response variables [61]. This approach enabled us to examine demographic and socio-economic variables, as well as other predictor variables, that influence people’s perceptions and attitudes towards owl species. We analysed our data using RStudio (version 4.5.0) [62]. To ensure that we capture the full range of responses in our analysis, we used cumulative link mixed models (CLMMs) that were fitted with Laplace approximation through the R package Ordinal [63] and the function clmm to identify the best combination of predictor variables for predicting people’s perceptions and attitudes of owl species. We performed four ordinal models, described by the equation: Logit [P (Y ≤ j|x)] = αj + β1 × 1 + β2 × 2 + …… + βnx n. This equation represents the cumulative probability that the ordinal response variable (e.g. tolerance or perceptions) is less than or equal to category j, given the values of the predictor variables x (i.e. P (Tolerance ≤ j|x)), where αj is the intercept term specific to the jth category of the ordinal response variable, X1, X2, …, Xp are the predictor variables p and β1 + β2 …… + βn are the corresponding coefficients for the predictor variables [64, 65]. These models are best used when analysing ordinal data, as our response variables had more than two levels (e.g. perceptions were 'negative', 'positive', and 'neutral', while tolerance had five levels ranging from extremely low to extremely high) [64].

Cumulative link mixed models were deemed the most appropriate for our analysis. We considered the ‘Locations’ of people as nested random factors within CLMMs to account for geographical clustering and site-level variance. The random effects were assumed to be normally distributed with a mean of zero, while predictor variables were assumed to have a linear effect on the log-odds scale. Therefore, ‘Location’ also accounted for participant variability, thereby improving the accuracy of fixed effect estimation. For this study, ‘Location’ was chosen as our random factor because studies have shown that people’s perceptions can differ within the same geographical region [9, 25]. For this study, the response variables were (1) tolerance of owl species, (2) perceptions of owl species, (3) importance of owls as ecosystem service providers (pest control), and (4) association with witchcraft. Our predictor variables were (1) age, (2) gender, (3) level of education, (4) employment status, (5) perceptions of owl species, (6) tolerance of owl species, (7) importance of owls as ecosystem service providers, (8) frequency of encounter, (9) association with witchcraft, and (10) feelings towards owl species. These predictor variables were chosen based on their perceived influence on conservation-related attitudes and beliefs; more especially because how people perceive and experience ecosystem services of species can differ by socio-demographic factors such as age, gender, employment status, and level of education [66, 67]. For instance, gender can influence people’s responses [68], and some studies have suggested that women are more likely to have negative perceptions of species, while men have more interest in animals that are considered less charismatic [69]. Recent research has also provided valuable insights into the influence of myths, beliefs, and folklore on the way people interact with and perceive species; therefore, we also considered cultural beliefs as our response variable [7, 70]. These cultural beliefs, therefore, shape people’s level of tolerance and perceptions of species [6, 70]. Highlighting that perceptions are not only influenced by socio-demographic factors but by an interplay of multifaceted factors. Furthermore, the frequency of encounters with owl species may also influence or reduce fear, as people perceive rare species as less capable of providing ecosystem services; therefore, these species tend to be viewed negatively compared to those with which people are familiar [33, 71].

Before developing our models, we assessed for multicollinearity among the predictor variables using the generalised variance inflation factors (GVIF) using the car package [72], and the function GVIF in R [62]. The GVIF was chosen because our predictor variables have more than one degree-of-freedom ordinal variables; therefore, we used the adjusted GVIF to standardise the values [73]. Variables with adjusted GVIF values closer to one did not exhibit collinearity (Supplementary information Table S2). We used the corrected Akaike information criterion (AIC) scores and the relative model weight to select and determine the best approximating model. Models were ranked by the lowest ΔAIC (< 2) and relative weight of 95%. Models within two delta AICc units of the lowest AIC and whose cumulative relative weights summed to 95% were considered the best approximating model; this was conducted using the R package AICcmodavg [74]. We also performed a Chi-square using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29) to compare the distribution of responses within each predictor variable.

Results

Sample characteristics

The total non-response rate for our perceptions study was 25% (n = 233), leading to a 75% response rate (n = 681). There was a significant difference (χ2 = 5.83, df = 1, P = 0.02) in the proportion of male and female study participants, with 55% of the study participants being female (n = 372) and 45% being male (n = 309) (Table 1). A significant number of our study participants had formal education and were in/completed secondary (matric) but did not further their studies (n = 467; 69%), and the majority were unemployed (n = 545; 80%) (χ2 = 245.64, df = 1, P < 0.001) (Table 1). General knowledge of owl species

Most respondents (n = 600; 88.1%) identified owl species as birds when asked, 'Are owl species birds?' with fixed-response options of ‘Yes/No/Not sure’. Some indicated uncertainty and, thus, responded that they were unsure whether owl species were birds or not (n = 61; 9%). While (n = 20; 2.9%) were certain that owls were not birds (χ2 = 923.0, df = 2, P < 0.001), we kept these responses because although they did not consider owls as birds, respondents could accurately identify owl species, and these respondents viewed owls as evil spirits that resemble humans rather than ‘birds’. Among those who recognised owl species as birds, the majority described owls as 'birds that are active at night', which aligns with the common perception that all owl species are nocturnal. A significant number of participants (χ2 = 679.6, df = 3, P < 0.001) could accurately identify the type of habitat that owls inhabit, with (n = 169; 24.8%) indicating that owls inhabit various types of ecosystems (e.g. mountains, forests, woodlands, rocky outcrops, farmlands, grasslands, wetlands, etc.) that provide them with food and shelter. In contrast, (n = 448; 65.8%) stated that owls only inhabit forests, (n = 45; 6.6%) mountains, and lastly, a few (n = 19; 2.8%) believed that owls live in households. When asked how they distinguish owls from other avian species, respondents stated that they have 'big eyes', 'weird/scary sounds', 'unique feathers', and 'nocturnal behaviour—active at night'. We asked our study participants how they felt about owl species; a significant majority stated that they 'hated' owl species (n = 263; 38.6%), and 22.5% said that they 'dislike' owls (n = 153), another 22.3% stated that they did not like nor love owl species therefore exhibited 'neutral' feelings (n = 152) to the species. In comparison, (n = 41; 6%) said they 'like' owls, and (n = 68; 10%) 'love' owl species (χ2 = 218.32, df = 4, P < 0.001). Our respondents differed significantly with regard to the frequency in which they encountered owl species: daily (n = 73; 11%), weekly (n = 154; 23%), monthly (n = 350; 51%), and lastly, some respondents have never encountered owl species (n = 104; 15%) (χ2 = 272.7, df = 3, P < 0.001).

Influence of socio-demographic factors on people’s perceptions and attitudes

Models that solely included socio-demographic factors had the lowest averaged AIC across all replicate models (Supplementary Information Table S3). Therefore, we ran separate models solely focussed on determining the influence of demographic variables on the four response variables. People’s perceptions of owl species showed no statistically significant predictors. However, gender was marginally associated, indicating that females had more negative perceptions of owl species than males (Coefficient = -0.29, SE = 0.17, P = 0.09). When we evaluated people’s association with witchcraft, we found that primary education was a significant predictor (Coefficient = 2.20, SE = 0.93, P = 0.02), indicating that individuals with primary school education were more likely to associate owl species with witchcraft. Age, gender, and employment, however, were not significant predictors of owl species association with witchcraft. However, people between 42 and 49 (Coefficient = 0.56, SE = 0.29, P = 0.07) did not associate owl species with witchcraft. When we evaluated people’s understanding of the ecological importance of owl species, we found that age, education, and employment status significantly influenced people’s responses; people who are between 34 and 41 (Coefficient = 1.03, SE = 0.35, P < 0.001) and those who are 50 years or older (Coefficient = 2.13, SE = 0.44, P < 0.001), were found to be more likely to view owl species as important. People with secondary (Coefficient = 1.67, SE = 0.73, P = 0.02) and tertiary (Coefficient = 1.97, SE = 0.73, P = 0.01) levels of education were more aware of the ecological importance of owl species. When we evaluated tolerance, we found that age and education were significant predictors of higher tolerance levels of owl species. We found that older individuals, particularly those who were 50 and above (Coefficient = 1.33, SE = 0.35, P < 0.001), were significantly more tolerant of owl species, and those aged between 42 and 49 (Coefficient = 0.59, SE = 0.31, P = 0.06) showed higher tolerance. Education showed a strong positive effect, whereby individuals with primary (Coefficient = 2.46, SE = 0.96, P = 0.01), secondary (Coefficient = 2.33, SE = 0.86, P = 0.01), and tertiary (Coefficient = 2.72, SE = 0.87, P = 0.002), education was significantly tolerant of owl species. However, gender (Coefficient = 0.08, SE = 0.15, P = 0.58) and employment (Coefficient = 0.07, SE = 0.22, P = 0.77) were not significant predictors (Supplementary Information Fig. S1).

Perceptions and associations of owl species with cultural taboos

Perceptions of owl species in KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa

When the study participants were asked to rate owl species as bad/good/"not sure," a significant number (χ2 = 353.8, df = 2, P < 0.001) of respondents held negative perceptions of owl species and therefore perceived them as 'bad' (n = 450; 66%). In contrast, some had neutral perceptions (n = 169; 25%) and responded, 'not sure'. Relatively few study participants perceived owl species as positive 'good' (n = 62; 9%). Our analysis showed that people’s perceptions of owl species were best explained by three key factors: (i) association with witchcraft, (ii) feelings towards owl species, and (iii) importance to agriculture and the environment (Table 3). We found that respondents who did not associate owls with witchcraft were marginally more likely to have positive perceptions of owl species (Table 2) (Coefficient = 0.47, SE = 0.26, P = 0.08), and those who were unsure (Coefficient = 1.78, SE = 0.24, P < 0.001) tended to have more positive perceptions of owl species. Additionally, we found that people who considered owl species as ‘not important’ to the environment and agriculture had strong negative perceptions of owl species (Coefficient = − 1.01, SE = 0.25, P < 0.001), while those who were unsure about owl associations (Coefficient = − 0.07, SE = 0.25, P = 0.78) tend to have positive perceptions towards owl species (Coefficient = 1.17, SE = 0.31, P < 0.001) (Table 4). When we evaluated people’s feelings in relation to their perceptions, we found that the linear term for feelings was positive and significant (Coefficient = 1.17, SE = 0.31, P < 0.001), suggesting that as feelings become more positive, so do the perceptions. However, the quartic (Coefficient = 0.67, SE = 0.23, P = 0.003) was significant and nonlinear, indicating a nonlinear relationship between perceptions and people's feelings (Table 4, Supplementary Information Fig. S1). Association of owl species with witchcraft

When asked if they associated owl species with witchcraft, we found that a significant majority (χ2 = 305.82, df = 2, P < 0.001) of the study participants did associate owl species with witchcraft (n = 442; 64.9%) and (n = 126; 18.5%) were not sure if owl species were associated with witchcraft or not, while a relatively low number (n = 113; 16.6%) of participants responded that owl species were not associated with witchcraft (Fig. 2). Respondents who associated owl species with witchcraft mostly thought of owl species as a 'messenger of death and sicknesses' and 'bad omen/bad luck' (Fig. 3). Owl associations with witchcraft were best explained by (i) people’s perceptions of owl species, (ii) feelings towards owl species, (iii) tolerance of owl species, (iv) the importance of owl species to agriculture and the environment, and (v) education (Table 3). Our results showed that respondents who were unaware of the ecological importance of owl species were significantly less likely to associate owl species with witchcraft than those who were uncertain about their importance. Similarly, participants’ feelings towards owl species influence a positive linear effect (Coefficient = − 0.81, SE = 0.32, P = 0.01), and a cubic term (Coefficient = − 0.66, SE = 0.25, P = 0.01) showed a complex nonlinear influence, indicating that positive feelings led to positive associations of owl species. Tolerance and ecological importance of owl species in KwaZulu-Natal

Tolerance of owl species

When respondents were asked about their tolerance of owl species, a significant proportion (χ2 = 190.5, df = 4, P < 0.001) expressed that they had extremely low tolerance of owl species (n = 232; 34%), low (n = 183; 26.9%), neutral (n = 155; 22.8%). Relatively few respondents could tolerate owl species, with those with extremely high tolerance (n = 80; 11.7%) and high (n = 31; 4.6%) (Fig. 4). People’s tolerance of owl species was best explained by (i) feelings towards owl species, (ii) importance to agriculture and the environment, and (iii) perceptions of owl species (Table 3). Our results showed that people who believe owl species are important to agriculture and the environment have a higher tolerance for owl species than those who do not consider owls important (Coefficient = 0.719, SE = 0.202, P < 0.001). Similarly, we also found that positive perceptions towards owl species increased tolerance to owl species, which is best explained by the linear trend for perceptions (Coefficient = 0.41, SE = 0.31, P < 0.001). We also found that positive feelings in both the linear (Coefficient = 0.29, SE = 0.33, P < 0.001) and the quadratic (Coefficient = 0.96, SE = 0.24, P < 0.001) trend indicated that individuals with positive feelings are more likely to have a higher tolerance for owl species (Table 4).Fig. 4Responses obtained in KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa, when respondents were asked, 'What is your level of tolerance of owl species?'

Ecological importance of owl species to agriculture and the environment

When we assessed people’s knowledge of the importance of owl species in agriculture and the environment, we found that (n = 217; 32%) were uncertain about the ecological importance of owl species to the environment. In contrast, (n = 264; 39%) were particularly certain that owl species have no ecological importance/significance. Few respondents knew that owl species provide ecosystem services beneficial to agriculture and the environment (n = 200; 29%) (χ2 = 9.68, df = 2, P = 0.01). The importance of owl species to the environment was best explained by (i) people’s employment status, (ii) education, (iii) tolerance to owl species, (iv) age, and (v) feelings towards owl species, (vi) peoples’ perceptions of owl species, (vii) gender, and (viii) association with witchcraft (Table 3). We found that people with positive feelings towards owl species were significantly linearly (Coefficient = 1.581, SE = 0.37, P < 0.001) and quadratically (Coefficient = 1.139, SE = 0.30, P < 0.001) more likely to perceive owl species as important. Similarly, higher tolerance levels towards owls were associated with a greater understanding of the ecological importance of owl species in agricultural landscapes and the environment, as indicated by the linear effect (Coefficient = 0.61, SE = 0.30, P = 0.042). We also found that age was a significant predictor of people’s understanding of the ecological knowledge of owl species, such that people aged between 34 and 41 (Coefficient = 0.83, SE = 0.36, P = 0.022), especially those 50 years or older (Coefficient = 1.80, SE = 0.45, P < 0.001) are more likely to view owl’s species as important. Similarly, employment status also had a positive effect (Coefficient = 0.58, SE = 0.24, P = 0.02), indicating that employed individuals are more aware of the ecological importance of owl species. In contrast, neither gender nor education was a significant predictor of the perceived importance of owl species. Additionally, we found that even if people do not associate (Coefficient = − 0.29, SE = 0.26, P = 0.26) or were not sure of the association of owl species with witchcraft (Coefficient = − 0.14, SE = 0.16, P = 0.37). We also found that perceptions of owl species were not significant predictors of the awareness of the ecological importance of owl species. However, the linear perception trend showed a marginal trend (Coefficient = − 0.26, SE = 0.15, P = 0.09), indicating that individuals who do not perceive owls negatively do not necessarily mean that they view owls as ecologically important species (Table 4). Similarly, gender was not a significant predictor (Coefficient = − 0.142, SE = 0.16, P = 0.37) of people’s awareness of the ecological importance of owl species; therefore, males and females were equally likely or unlikely to be aware of the ecological importance of owl species.

When asked if they thought the owl population was declining, we found a statistically non-significant difference in their responses (χ2 = 5.51, df = 2, P = 0.06); 37% of the respondents thought owl species were declining, while 33% thought they were not. In comparison, 30% are unsure whether owl species are declining. When asked if owls should be conserved, a significant majority (χ2 = 29.5, df = 2, P < 0.001) of the participants responded yes (n = 269; 40%) owls should be conserved, while (n = 251; 37%) stated that owls should not be conserved, and (n = 161; 24%) were not sure if owl species should be conserved or not.

Discussion

Human–wildlife coexistence often results in severe consequences for wildlife species because of human-induced activities, such as land transformation, which alters their natural habitats and increases competition for food and shelter [54]. While physical threats to wildlife species are well documented, we have often overlooked the role of human perceptions and understanding of the ecological importance of species in shared environments.

Although conventional conservation methods, such as captive breeding and protected areas, have had some success, they also have limitations, particularly in addressing human-related factors, including those related to persecution, repudiation, tolerance, coexistence, and admiration [28, 75]. Understanding people’s perceptions and attitudes towards wildlife species has been identified as an effective way of translating ecology into management [41, 54, 76, 77]. Ethnobiological tools, such as questionnaires, offer valuable insights into how people perceive and interact with wildlife, providing critical data to inform more inclusive and effective conservation strategies [2, 3, 37, 40]. Therefore, they provide indispensable evidence that includes both scientific justification and public interest [78].

Positive and negative evaluations of some aspects of conservation are often referred to as 'perceptions'. These perceptions have the power to drive the conservation of species, especially owl species worldwide. Several factors often influence people’s perceptions of owl species, including concepts such as attitudes, cultural beliefs, values, and norms, which are all key determinants of behaviours, responses, and levels of support. Molares and Gurovich [12] stated that a complex threat to owl species worldwide is cultural beliefs and superstitions, which drive people’s perceptions, as both positive and negative perceptions may lead to admiration and persecution. We found that 66% of respondents in KwaZulu-Natal held negative perceptions of owl species, primarily driven by associations with witchcraft. In KwaZulu-Natal, many individuals believe owl species are messengers of death and illness-causing health conditions such as stroke, headache, and paralysis. These beliefs, however, are expected, given the persistence of witchcraft-related beliefs in many African communities, which are deeply rooted in spiritual and materialistic insecurities [79]; particularly in South Africa, where witchcraft has been stated to be a result of jealousy and malicious people (witches) in the communities [10, 27, 80]; and some believe that witches use animals as their familiars (a spirit that is accompanying the spirit of witches—associated with their presence) and their body parts being used in traditional medicine or that when animal dies it becomes a vessel for the witches spirit and used to cause further destruction [10, 15, 27]. We also heard stories that support this claim, such that some study participants stated that owls are sent by evil neighbours (or community members) to ruin one’s bright future, while others shared stories of how they lost their wealth after seeing an owl. Interestingly, although community members are suspects, in many African cultures, family is the main suspect when it comes to such, as some believe that the strongest attacks come from within [81]. Similarly, in other parts of the world, like Kenya, Malawi, and Argentina, it has been found that people associate owl species with misfortune, bad luck, ill omen, sickness, and messengers of death [3, 4, 6, 10, 12, 15, 17, 23–25, 82, 83]. A rather common belief in many African communities is that owl species cause death/ or if a person hears or sees an owl, they will die. Leistner [84] also argued that in many African belief systems, people believe that witchcraft is a real force that is used to bring death, sickness, misfortune, and harm to others. However, some people believe that witches can use animals or that their spirits can enter the bodies of animals, particularly avian species and therefore employ destruction [85], resulting in owl species being persecuted for/or against witchcraft [10, 15].

Some studies have concluded that socio-demographic factors, such as education, age, and gender, strongly influence people’s perceptions of owl species [22, 86–88]. For instance, individuals with higher education levels hold fewer misconceptions, particularly those related to folklore, since education can reduce mythological beliefs [12, 89]. Similarly, younger people have been shown in several studies to express more positive attitudes/perceptions towards owl species, while older people tend to have stronger negative perceptions [6, 9]. However, our study revealed that perceptions of owl species were shaped by a more complex interaction of factors, including associations with witchcraft, levels of tolerance, and emotional responses, thus highlighting the multifaceted nature of people’s perceptions and emotions towards wildlife species. Socio-demographic variables alone were not significant predictors of negative perceptions and/or associations with witchcraft involving owl species; however, individuals with primary education, females, and younger individuals held more negative perceptions of owl species. This further supports the claim that younger people have more fear and disgust towards species viewed as dangerous and frightening [88]. Similarly, in Pakistan, it was found that younger people were more likely to believe that owl species are a sign of bad omen [22]. In contrast, in Hwange District, it was found that age did not significantly influence people’s perceptions of owl species [5]. In South Africa and Kenya, it was found that younger individuals had more positive perceptions of owl species and, therefore, associated owl species with rodent control rather than witchcraft [10, 24]. This is best explained by the exposure of younger people to technology and educational tools. We also found that females were marginally more likely to have negative perceptions of owl species than males. A plausible explanation for this trend is the existence of societal structures that cause division in social roles between males and females. In contrast, in Mexico, it was found that men are more superstitious about owl species than females, and believe that owl species are a bad omen, harbinger of sickness and messengers of death [90].

Our results showed that people generally held negative feelings towards the owl species, which significantly influenced their tolerance levels towards the owl species. We found that a higher tolerance of owl species was influenced by positive feelings towards these species and an understanding of their ecological importance. We also found that people who had a positive perception of owl species had a higher tolerance for them. Furthermore, we also found that socio-demographic variables such as age, gender, education, and employment were not significant predictors of people’s tolerance of owl species. However, when we evaluated people’s understanding of the ecological importance of owl species, we found that older, more educated individuals and those with positive feelings and perceptions of owl species had a greater understanding of the species’ ecological importance. These findings have practical implications for conservation management and community engagement, particularly for policymakers, conservation practitioners, non-profit organisations, and environmental educators, who can use this information to design culturally sensitive outreach programmes that have the potential to lead to more positive perceptions and increased tolerance of owl species. These beliefs lead to damage to ecosystems and missed opportunities for communities through ecologically based rodent management (EBRM) programmes. Therefore, by integrating local ecological knowledge and citizen science initiatives, communities can actively contribute to monitoring programmes and protect the owl population, ensuring that conservation strategies are effective and inclusive.

Conclusions

Our questionnaire respondents in KwaZulu-Natal mostly perceived owl species negatively, associating them with witchcraft, and were generally unaware of the ecological importance of owl species. Our results showed that people’s perceptions result from an interplay of multifaceted variables, not just socio-demographic variables. Positive perceptions were influenced by factors such as a high tolerance of owl species, a good understanding of the ecological importance of owl species to the environment, and a positive association, which does not involve witchcraft but rather recognition of owl species in rodent control. Witchcraft beliefs have resulted in the persecution of owl species for/or against witchcraft. These results highlighted the need for conservation strategies that extend beyond ecological education by directly addressing locally held beliefs, cultural frameworks, and emotional responses. Rather than framing witchcraft as ‘mythical’, it is rather more productive to acknowledge it as a significant belief system that influences human–wildlife interactions in many parts of the world, particularly in African countries/communities. Therefore, conservation initiatives could benefit from culturally sensitive education programmes that communicate the ecological importance of owls while also respectfully engaging with beliefs about witchcraft. Such approaches may improve people’s tolerance of owl species and facilitate positive perceptions and interactions, while remaining mindful of the socio-cultural context in which people’s beliefs are embedded. A limitation of our study was that it primarily consisted of Zulu participants; therefore, a cross-cultural analysis could not be conducted. We recommend that future research be done for all tribal groups in South Africa to understand common perceptions and cultural beliefs, and develop ecological educational programmes targeted at the different tribal and cultural groups.
