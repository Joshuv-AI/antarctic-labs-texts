Inflammatory indices predict poor early outcomes after tricuspid valve surgery: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Sanan Huseynov, Ali Bulut, Murat Kurtoğlu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the prognostic value of neutrophil count, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) in predicting poor early outcomes, including in-hospital mortality and complications, after tricuspid valve replacement (TVR). We retrospectively analyzed 76 patients who underwent TVR between 2014 and 2024 at a tertiary cardiovascular surgery center. Preoperative and postoperative neutrophil counts, NLR, and SII were calculated from blood samples. Patients were divided into survivor and non-survivor groups. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and Cox regression models were used to evaluate the predictors of in-hospital mortality and complications. The in-hospital mortality was 15.8%. The preoperative neutrophil count, NLR, and SII values were significantly higher in the non-survivors (P < .05). ROC analysis showed that preoperative NLR (>3.2) and SII (>580), as well as postoperative NLR (>3.55) and SII (>805), predicted mortality with high sensitivity and specificity. Cox regression analysis revealed that preoperative neutrophil count (hazard ratio [HR] = 26.06, P = .005), NLR (HR = 23.03, P = .006), and SII (HR = 1.009, P = .018) were independent predictors of mortality. Prolonged intensive care unit and hospital stays and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease were also significantly associated with poor outcomes. Neutrophil count, NLR, and SII were strong independent markers for predicting early postoperative mortality and complications following TVR. These easily accessible inflammatory indices may enhance the risk stratification and postoperative monitoring during cardiac surgery.

1. Introduction

Neutrophils and platelets, through their roles in chronic inflammation, and lymphocytes, via their involvement in immune responses, are all central mediators of systemic inflammation in cardiovascular diseases.[1,2] Novel inflammatory indices calculated by various combinations of neutrophil, monocyte, platelet, and lymphocyte counts have been defined.[3,4] While the predictive power of inflammation indices for mortality and morbidity has been demonstrated in both cardiac surgery and noncardiovascular diseases,[5–17] only a few studies have examined systemic inflammatory indices in tricuspid valve surgery.[18] However, these patients are often exposed to chronic inflammation due to severe heart failure, previous non-tricuspid valve surgeries, comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes, and surgical delays.[19] Despite advances in surgical techniques and patient care, significant improvements have not been achieved in high mortality rates and poor outcomes, making patient selection and prognostic evaluation critical.[18,20,21]

Due to the ease and low cost of calculating these so-called “novel” systemic inflammatory indices and their potential benefits, we conducted this study. This study aimed to examine the predictive value of the relatively older neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and novel systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) for early in-hospital outcomes in patients undergoing tricuspid valve replacement (TVR).

2. Materials and methods

This study includes the analysis of data from all patients who underwent TVR between 2014 and 2024 in the Cardiovascular Surgery Department of Ankara Güven Hospital. The study included patients who underwent emergency and elective surgeries, first-time and redo surgeries, isolated TVR, and combined TVR with other procedures. Patients with inaccessible medical records, those who were pregnant or breastfeeding, had autoimmune diseases, were under 18 years of age, or underwent thoracotomy were excluded.

Data were obtained from the hospital software system, medical records, and physician observation notes. For preoperative ejection fraction (EF), data were missing for 5 out of 76 patients (6.6%), and these cases were excluded from EF analyses using complete case analysis. Cardiopulmonary bypass time data were unavailable for the majority of patients, and thus this parameter was not analyzed. Patients gave informed consent for the surgical procedure. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Kirşehir Ahi Evran University for this retrospective study under protocol number 2021-04/37. The study assessed early in-hospital outcomes.

Patients were grouped according to survival status, valve type used (mechanical/bioprosthetic), diagnosis, and type of procedure. Systemic inflammatory indices were calculated based on preoperative and postoperative blood parameters. Hospital and intensive care unit length of stay, and complications were recorded. The follow-up period was defined as the duration of in-hospital stay, with a median of 11 days (range 9–48) for survivors and 23.5 days (range 10–40) for non-survivors. ICU stay was 5 days (range 2–22) and 23.5 days (range 10–40), respectively.

2.1. Statistical analysis

SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk) was used for data analysis. The distribution characteristics of the variables were first assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed variables and median (min–max) for non-normally distributed variables. Differences between survivor and non-survivor groups were determined using Student t test or Mann–Whitney U test, depending on the distribution. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to determine the cutoff points of the predictors. Patients were compared according to mortality outcomes. Predictors of mortality were analyzed using the Cox regression model and survival curves were assessed using the log-rank test. A univariate analysis was performed for each variable. Continuous parameters were dichotomized using cutoff values from the ROC analysis. Results were interpreted as hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI).

3. Results

The study cohort consisted of 76 patients who were divided into 2 groups based on mortality outcomes: survivors (n = 64, 84.2%) and non-survivors (n = 12, 15.8%). The statistical analysis results of the dataset are presented in detail for each variable. Statistical significance was set at P < .05. The significant results were interpreted as predictors of mortality.

In terms of sex distribution, the male proportion was 84.2% (n = 64) among survivors and 15.8% (n = 12) among non-survivors (P = .217). 3.2. Other clinical parameters

Among echocardiographic parameters, the EF was measured as 51.26 ± 0.86 in survivors and 49.42 ± 2.26 in non-survivors (P = .457). There were no significant differences between EF values. Hepatomegaly was recorded with a median of 0 cm (range, 0–10 cm) in survivors and 1.5 cm (range, 0–8 cm) in non-survivors (P = .237), indicating no statistically significant difference. The Euroscore was found to be a significant predictor for predicting mortality. The median EuroScore was 4 (min–max: 1–9) in survivors and 6.5 (min–max: 3–13) in non-survivors (P < .05), indicating that higher EuroScore values were associated with mortality risk. Pulmonary artery pressure was 51.81 ± 1.52 mm Hg in survivors and 53.33 ± 3.24 mm Hg in non-survivors (P = .676), showing no significant difference. Emergency surgery was required in 7.8% (n = 5) of survivors and not required in non-survivors (P = .31). The median number of redo surgeries was found 2 be a median of 2 (range: 1–3) in both groups, with no significant difference (P = .324).

ICU and hospital stay durations were significantly associated with mortality. The median ICU stay was 5 days (range, 2–22 days) for survivors and 23.5 days (range, 10–40 days) for non-survivors (P < .05). Similarly, the median hospital stay was 11 days (range, 9–48 days) for survivors and 23.5 days (range, 10–40 days) for non-survivors (P < .05). These results indicated a relationship between prolonged ICU/hospital stay and mortality (Table 1).

3.3. Laboratory findings

Preoperative and postoperative laboratory parameters and inflammatory indices were analyzed to determine their relationship with mortality.

Preoperative creatinine levels were 0.92 ± 0.03 mg/dL in survivors and 1.25 ± 0.1 mg/dL in non-survivors (P < .05), indicating that higher preoperative creatinine levels were associated with mortality.

The preoperative neutrophil count was 4.61 × 103/µL (range: 4.5–4.91) in survivors and 5.82 × 103/µL (range: 5.59–5.91) in non-survivors (P < .05). Similarly, preoperative NLR and SII values were significantly higher in the non-survivor group (P < .05). Median NLR was 2.53 (range: 2.39–2.85) in survivors and 3.82 (range: 3.6–3.94) in non-survivors. The median SII was 327 (range: 298–365) in survivors and 851 (range: 803–871) in non-survivors. These findings indicate that the preoperative inflammatory indices are associated with mortality.

Postoperative laboratory findings showed a similar trend. Postoperative creatinine was 0.92 ± 0.03 mg/dL in survivors and 2.05 ± 0.43 mg/dL in non-survivors (P < .05). Postoperative neutrophil count, NLR, and SII values were significantly higher in the non-survivor group (P < .05). The median postoperative neutrophil count was 4.52 × 103/µL (range: 4.39–4.85) in survivors and 6.82 × 103/µL (range: 6.62–6.97) in non-survivors. Median NLR was 2.3 (range: 2.07–2.55) in survivors and 4.79 (range: 4.61–4.92) in non-survivors. The median SII was 367 (range: 335–411) in survivors and 1302 (range: 1207–1349) in non-survivors.

Liver function tests, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) values, did not show a significant difference between the groups. The median ALT levels were 17 (range, 6–151) in survivors and 14 (range, 6–45) in non-survivors (P = .516). AST was 27 (range: 12–94) in survivors and 29.50 (range: 12–96) in non-survivors (P = .429) (Table 1).

3.4. Comorbidities

In the comorbidity analysis, hypertension (HT) and diabetes mellitus (DM) were not found to be associated with mortality. HT was present in 48.4% (n = 31) of survivors and 58.3% (n = 7) of non-survivors (P = .532). DM was observed in 21.9% (n = 14) of survivors and 25.0% (n = 3) of non-survivors (P = .813).

However, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) was significantly more common in non-survivors (58.3%, n = 7) than in survivors (14.1%, n = 9) (P < .05), indicating that COPD is an important comorbidity that increases the mortality risk (Table 1).

3.5. Etiology

When the etiology of the tricuspid valve pathology was assessed, rheumatic disease was found to be the most common cause: 57.8% (n = 37) of survivors and 50.0% (n = 6) of non-survivors.

Other etiologies included degenerative disease (7.8% survivors, 8.3% non-survivors), Ebstein anomaly (0.0% survivors, 8.3% non-survivors), endocarditis (1.6% survivors, 0.0% non-survivors), failed valve repair (12.5% survivors, 0.0% non-survivors), functional causes (18.8% survivors, 33.3% non-survivors), and isolated tricuspid regurgitation (1.6% survivors, 0.0% non-survivors). No statistically significant relationship was found between etiological factors and mortality (Table 2).

3.11. Cox regression analysis

Univariate Cox regression analysis was used to assess the predictors of mortality (Table 8). Among the preoperative parameters, NLR emerged as a strong factor that increased mortality risk, with a HR of 23.031 (95% CI = 2.47–214.85, P = .006). The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) also had a significant effect on mortality, with an HR of 1.009 (95% CI = 1.001–1.016, P = .018). Additionally, the preoperative neutrophil count showed a strong relationship with mortality (HR = 26.062; 95% CI = 2.614–259.836; P = .005). Among the postoperative parameters, the post-NLR was significantly associated with a 6.742-fold increased mortality risk (HR = 6.742; 95% CI = 1.284–35.39; P = .024). Postoperative SII also increased mortality with a smaller effect size (HR = 1.005, 95% CI = 1.001–1.008; P = .014). Postoperative neutrophil count increased the mortality risk by 7.594 times (HR = 7.594; 95% CI = 1.357–42.488; P = .021).

4. Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the relationship between systemic inflammatory indices and their predictive value for postoperative mortality and morbidity in 76 patients who underwent TVR. Our findings demonstrated that the NLR and SII have high sensitivity and specificity for predicting mortality.

In TVR, early postoperative mortality and morbidity are frequently observed, particularly due to delayed surgery.[22–27] It has even been emphasized that surgery does not contribute to survival in severe tricuspid regurgitation, highlighting the importance of surgical timing.[28] Previous studies have associated poor outcomes after TVR with poor general condition of patients and with prognostic factors such as hepatic/renal dysfunction, anemia, and severe postoperative tricuspid regurgitation.[20,29–31] In addition to these prognostic factors, this study showed that NLR and SII have predictive value in postoperative mortality.

Immune responses and systemic inflammation have been shown to be closely related to mortality and morbidity in cardiac surgery.[15,32,33] While neutrophils trigger the inflammatory response, monocytes interact with platelets and endothelial cells to initiate the inflammatory and thrombotic pathways. Lymphocytes play a role in mitigating inflammation.[34]

SII, calculated using neutrophil, lymphocyte, and platelet ratios and broadly reflecting systemic inflammation,[15] has been found to be associated with mortality not only in cardiac surgery,[12,15] but also in noncardiac surgery,[35,36] neoplasms,[37] and intensive care patients.[38] Hayiroğlu et al[39] emphasized the association between a high SII and mortality in heart failure patients with intracardiac defibrillators. Dey et al[5] demonstrated that a high SII is associated with poor outcomes in coronary artery bypass surgery. Yoon et al[18] reported a strong association between a high SII and 30-day mortality in patients undergoing tricuspid valve surgery. Tosu et al[40] found that a high SII is related to mortality and morbidity in patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve implantation. In our study, the SII was significantly higher in the non-survivor group both preoperatively and postoperatively. ROC analysis revealed that SII (>580 preoperatively and > 805 postoperatively) predicted mortality with 100% sensitivity and specificity. These findings are consistent with the study by Xiang et al,[15] who showed that SII is an independent predictor of mortality and morbidity in patients undergoing heart valve surgery. The high sensitivity and specificity of the optimized cutoff in our study may be due to the small sample size and the fact that patients were exposed to chronic inflammation due to delayed referral for surgery.

In the regression analysis, both preoperative and postoperative SII had a significant effect on mortality, calculated as HR = 1.009 (95% CI = 1.001–1.016, P = .018) and HR = 1.005 (95% CI = 1.001–1.008, P = .014), respectively.

Lymphopenia, neutrophilia, and thrombocytosis typically lead to a high SII, representing a nonspecific process between immune dysfunction and inflammation.[41] In patients undergoing noncardiac surgery, chronic inflammation reduces the capacity to withstand surgical stress and alters protein metabolism, thereby reducing postoperative recovery capacity.[42] The longer ICU and hospital stays observed in the non-survivor group in our study align with the reduced capacity to withstand surgical stress and impaired recovery.

The neutrophil count and NLR, Based on similar pathophysiological mechanisms, the neutrophil count and NLR reflect different aspects of the systemic inflammatory response. The lifespan of neutrophils activated by enzymes secreted by monocytes and macrophages is modulated by cytokines and growth factors, and their activation is induced by bacterial infection or immune reactions.[43,44] Neutrophils cause tissue damage by releasing proteolytic enzymes such as myeloperoxidase and elastase. The NLR measures the relative intensity of inflammation by comparing the proinflammatory role of neutrophils to the immunomodulatory role of lymphocytes. In all cardiac surgery scenarios, especially with cardiopulmonary bypass and surgical stress, neutrophils are expected to be triggered, lymphocytes are suppressed, and the balance is tipped toward inflammation, thus increasing the NLR. A meta-analysis by Vakhshoori et al[45] emphasized that neutrophils secrete destructive enzymes in the cardiac tissue and influence prognosis. Xu et al[46] also emphasized that a high NLR plays a critical role in prognosis and reported significantly higher neutrophil and NLR values in the non-survivor group.

The NLR has been associated with mortality not only in cardiac[13,47] and noncardiac surgery,[48] neoplasms[49] and ICU patients.[50] Urbanowicz et al[12] emphasized that, among these indices, neutrophil count and NLR have the highest predictive value for mortality and should be considered. According to them, it is not only the absolute count of neutrophils and lymphocytes, but also the balance between proinflammatory cytokine release by neutrophils and the immunosuppressive function of lymphocytes. A biological explanation for the relationship between the inflammatory parameters and mortality after bypass surgery is noteworthy.

Galeone et al[51] demonstrated a strong association between a high NLR and both short- and long-term mortality in patients with surgically treated valve endocarditis. Wang et al[52] highlighted that NLR is a powerful predictor of mortality, independent of traditional risk scoring systems, such as the EuroScore. Perry et al,[53] in a meta-analysis involving 13,262 patients, found that high perioperative NLR independently predicted both short- and long-term mortality. The consistency of NLR’s prognostic value of the NLR in different surgical scenarios reinforces its importance in cardiac surgery.

In our study, the high HRs for neutrophil count and NLR (preoperative HRs: 26.062 and 23.031, respectively) may reflect the inflammatory nature of TVR patients who often undergo delayed surgery. Although the HR for neutrophil count was higher than that for NLR, suggesting that it may more sensitively indicate the inflammatory burden, the normalization/balancing provided by lymphocyte count in NLR offers an additional advantage in gauging the relative severity of inflammation. The finding that the preoperative neutrophil count and NLR values were significantly higher in the non-survivor group is consistent with the study by Urbanowicz et al.[12]

Postoperative neutrophil and NLR values were also significantly higher in the non-survivor group, and their HRs were higher than other parameters, indicating that TVR patients already under inflammatory burden preoperatively may face additional inflammatory load due to surgical stress, prolonged ICU/hospital stay, low output, and pneumonia.

Stef et al[54] reported an association between high neutrophil count and NLR values in cardiopulmonary bypass with postoperative infection and low cardiac output syndrome, supporting our findings. These findings suggest that both these parameters may serve as indicators of complications.

The optimized cutoff values for NLR (preoperative: 3.2; postoperative: 3.55), showing 100% sensitivity and specificity, strongly suggest that NLR is an extremely effective index for predicting mortality risk in patients undergoing TVR. However, owing to the small sample size and delayed surgeries, exposure to high levels of inflammation may have contributed to the higher sensitivity and specificity compared to the literature.

These results are consistent with those of previous studies that showed the increasing predictive power of systemic inflammatory indices in cardiac surgery.

The significance of the Euroscore score in predicting mortality in our study was expected result (HR = 1.259, 95% CI = 1.011–1.568, P = .04). Roques et al[55] also emphasized the predictive power of the Euroscore in mortality. In our study, the higher EuroScore values in non-survivors (median: 6.5 vs 4) support this finding. However, the stronger association between the NLR and mortality suggests that inflammatory markers may provide additional prognostic value beyond traditional risk scores.

Prolonged ICU and hospital stays were also associated with mortality (P < .05). These results are consistent with the findings of Wang et al’s[52] that a high NLR is associated with prolonged ICU/hospital stay and mortality. These findings may reflect the combined effect of postoperative complications, such as low cardiac output (25%) and pneumonia (26.09%), which were frequently observed in our cohort.

The lack of a significant association between procedural details (isolated vs combined TVR) and mortality (HR = 1.976; 95% CI = 0.511–7.647; P = .324) suggests that inflammatory response may be a more critical determinant than procedural complexity.

The significant association between COPD and mortality (HR = 6.22; 95% CI = 1.801–21.493; P = .004) is consistent with the findings of Roques et al,[55] who identified chronic lung disease as a risk factor for mortality in the EuroScore study.

In contrast, no significant relationship was found between mortality and hypertension or diabetes (P = .433 and P = .862, respectively), consistent with the findings of Çakir et al,[56] who showed no independent association between these comorbidities and mortality in patients with severe heart failure.

The high prevalence of AF in both survivors (75%) and non-survivors (83.3%) indicates its common occurrence in TVR patients; however, the lack of association with mortality (HR = 0.602, 95% CI = 0.128–2.821, P = .519) is consistent with the conclusion of Gillinov et al[57] that AF was not associated with perioperative mortality in mitral valve surgery.

5. Limitations and future directions

The retrospective design and small sample size (n = 76) of our study may have introduced selection bias. Missing data regarding preoperative left ventricular EF (LVEF, n = 5 missing, 6.6%; handled via complete case analysis) and cardiopulmonary bypass parameters (majority missing, not analyzed) could affect clinical outcomes. Additionally, the biochemical mechanisms underlying the prognostic role of these indices are not fully understood and future studies should investigate the pathophysiology of neutrophil activation. A much larger cohort is needed to determine the ideal cutoff values for the indices.

6. Conclusion

This study emphasizes the critical importance of 2 systemic inflammatory indices, NLR, which is relatively well known, and SII, which is considered novel, in predicting mortality in TVR patients. Neutrophil counts may also be significant for evaluating prognosis. The low cost and ease of application of these indices, along with their high sensitivity and specificity, make them promising tools not only for risk stratification in TVR patients, but also for postoperative monitoring in cardiac surgery in general.

The significant associations of the EuroScore, COPD, prolonged ICU/hospital stay, and inflammatory indices with mortality highlight the multifactorial nature of mortality risk. This study supports the use of inflammatory indices for patient management in clinical practice.

In conclusion, the neutrophil count, NLR, and SII emerged as strong and independent parameters for predicting mortality in patients with TVR. The data suggest that these indices are not merely numerical values but also reflect the severity of perioperative systemic inflammation and provide meaningful contributions to mortality prediction. In this context, the low cost and easy applicability of these indices makes them valuable tools for evaluating perioperative mortality.
