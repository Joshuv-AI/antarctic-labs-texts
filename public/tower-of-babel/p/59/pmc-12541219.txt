Fever in Myocardial Infarction-Related Cardiogenic Shock
Authors: Matthew Pierce, Abduljabar Adi, Rohit Jain, Ahmed Calvo, Satvinder Guru, Shuojohn Li, Ramsis Ramsis, Moein Bayat Mokhtari, Dechen Samdrup, Sapana Yonghang, Joshua Roubin, Atul D. Bali, Joao D. Fontes, Matthew Griffin, Miguel Alvarez Villela
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe role of fever in acute myocardial infarction-related cardiogenic shock (AMI-CS) in the contemporary era is not well described.ObjectivesThe aim of the study was to describe the epidemiology of fever in AMI-CS and its impact on clinical outcomes.MethodsAmong all AMI-CS patients in the Northwell-Shock registry (n = 1,372), fever was identified via ≥2 temperatures ≥38.0 °C or one ≥38.3 °C. Characteristics, management, and outcomes were compared between patients with and without fever.ResultsFever occurred in 40% of patients at a median of 2 days [0-6] after admission. Patients with fever were younger, more often male, and had higher shock severity, receiving more invasive treatment. Febrile patients received more antibiotics (84% vs 42%; P < 0.001), yet only 21% had positive microbial cultures. Early fever (<72 hours) was associated with a lower rate of positive cultures (13% vs 24%; P = 0.002). In-hospital mortality was similar between the groups (30% vs 31%; P = 0.78), but patients with fever had longer hospital stays (19 days vs 9 days; P < 0.001) and more discharges to skilled nursing facilities (49% vs 36%; P < 0.001). On multivariable logistic regression, female sex (OR: 1.9; P = 0.001), non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction etiology (OR = 1.5; P = 0.047), mechanical ventilation (OR: 2.1; P < 0.001), and acute dialysis (OR: 1.6; P = 0.040) were associated with positive cultures in febrile patients.ConclusionsFever in AMI-CS is common and is associated with greater illness severity but seems to be noninfectious in most cases. Further research should focus on improving antibiotic use in this population.

Fever is a nonspecific physiologic response to different infectious and noninfectious stressors. Before the era of early myocardial reperfusion, fever was a well-known and highly prevalent occurrence in patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI).1 In the era of primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), fever has remained common and has been associated with larger infarct size,2 greater adverse left ventricular remodeling, and worsened clinical outcomes at 1 year, including higher mortality.3,4

In patients with cardiogenic shock complicating AMI (AMI-CS), fever has been described as part of a systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), which can occur with or without proven infection and is associated with higher disease severity and hospital mortality.5,6 In the SHOCK trial, where SIRS was defined by fever and leukocytosis, 20% of patients met the criteria, with fever present in 92% of cases.6 A more recent observational study encompassing all cardiogenic shock (CS) types applied the established definition of SIRS requiring at least 2 of 4 criteria (tachypnea, tachycardia, leukocytosis, and abnormal body temperature [BT]) and found that 34% of patients had SIRS. However, abnormal BT (≤36 °C or ≥38 °C) was the least frequently met criterion, present in only 30% of SIRS cases.6 Consequently, the true prevalence of fever in contemporary AMI-CS cohorts remains unclear.

Addressing this gap is particularly relevant given the growing prevalence of CS and the increased use of indwelling temporary mechanical circulatory support (MCS) devices, which could heighten the risk of infection.7,8 Fever in such critically ill patients can lead to potentially harmful interventions, such as inappropriate antibiotic use and unnecessary testing that increases resource utilization and overall cost.9,10 Understanding the patterns of fever in AMI-CS and its association with documented infections can help refine management strategies, decrease unnecessary testing, and improve antibiotic stewardship. To this end, we describe the epidemiology of fever in this population using a large multicenter urban cohort.

Methods

The Northwell-Shock Registry is a multicenter retrospective study designed to evaluate patients admitted with CS across the New York metropolitan area. Approved by the Northwell Health Institutional Review Board with a waiver for informed consent, all patient data were deidentified and securely stored in a protected database.

Study population

Adult patients (>18 years of age) treated at 11 hospitals within the Northwell Health system sharing a unified electronic medical record system. The study enrolled adult patients (≥18 years) discharged between January 2016 and August 2022 with a primary or secondary diagnosis of CS secondary to AMI, classified as having an etiology of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) or non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI). Diagnoses were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes (Supplemental Table 1).

Definitions

Fever was defined in relation to the Infectious Disease Society of America criteria as 2 or more BTs ≥38.0 °C or a single BT >38.3 °C. BTs included in this study were measured via oral, rectal, bladder, or pulmonary artery catheter probes. BTs above 43 °C were deemed nonphysiological and were excluded. For nonfebrile patients, the highest BT recorded was reported. Patients were stratified into 2 groups: fever and no fever.11

Antibiotic administration was defined as continuous treatment with any antimicrobial for at least 24 hours. Leukocytosis was defined as a white blood cell count of at least 11,500 K/μL.

A positive blood culture was identified by the isolation of a pathogen in one or more samples except when the organism was a coagulase-negative Staphylococcus, in which case 2 samples were required to be positive for the same organism. A positive urine culture was identified by the presence of >10,000 colony-forming units of any organism in a sample.

Comorbidities and procedural data were identified using ICD-10 codes, including interventions such as pulmonary artery catheterization (PAC), MCS devices, mechanical ventilation, transfusions, hemodialysis, PCI, and others.

Vital signs, laboratory values, and hemodynamic data were collected at the time of admission (initial) and at the worst recorded value during hospitalization. The vasoactive-inotropic score (VIS) was calculated using the type and dosage of inotropes and vasopressors at admission (VIS-initial) and at their maximum dosing levels (VIS-max) (Supplemental Table 2).12 Shock severity was evaluated both at admission and at the point of peak severity using the Cardiogenic Shock Working Group adaptation of the Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions (SCAI) shock stage classification.13 The Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) score version IV was also calculated for all patients upon intensive care unit (ICU) admission.14

Data analysis

Statistical analysis of the data and graph visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism and Microsoft Office tools. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± SD or as median (IQR), while categorical variables are reported as counts (percentages). Comparisons of continuous variables were conducted using unpaired t-tests with Welch’s correction, and chi-square tests were used for categorical variables. Multivariable logistic regression and unadjusted models were both used to assess variables associated with a positive microbial culture and mortality. The statistical significance for this study was determined with a threshold of P < 0.05.

Microbial culture and antimicrobial use

Patients in the fever group were significantly more likely to have a blood culture (84% vs 36%; P < 0.001) or urine culture (40% vs 18%; P < 0.001) obtained during their hospitalization. The positivity rate of blood cultures was higher in the fever group (14% vs 7%; P < 0.001), but the rates of positive urine culture were similar in both groups (18% vs 24%; P = 0.156) (Figure 3).Figure 3Prevalence of Positive Microbial CulturesStacked bar chart showing the culture results in patients with fever and no fever.

The organisms isolated from the blood cultures in both groups are depicted in Supplemental Figure 1. The prevalence of Gram+ and Gram− organisms was similar in febrile and afebrile patients. Streptococcus spp were more common among patients without fever. Otherwise, all other bacteria, including coagulase-negative Staphylococcus, had similar prevalence in both groups. Fungus was present in the fever group in 13% of patients with Candida spp. isolated in all cases. There were no cases of fungemia in patients without fever.

Patients in the fever group were twice as likely to receive antimicrobials (84% vs 42%; P < 0.001) and received them for a longer duration of time (9 days [4-19] vs 4 days [2-8]; P = 0.023). Notably, the fever group had a longer delay between admission and antimicrobial initiation (1.3 days [0.8-2.7] vs 1.1 days [0.7-2.9]; P = 0.044).

Timing of fever

Patients with fever that occurred early in the hospitalization (<72 hours) had higher prevalence of SCAI stages C-E, more leukocytosis on admission, and received more antibiotics than patients with late fever. A full comparison between early and late fever is depicted in Supplemental Table 6.

Among patients who underwent revascularization, 124 (34%) had fever before revascularization. For those who developed fever after revascularization, the median time to fever onset was 2 days (0.5-4) in coronary artery bypass grafting patients and 1 day (0.5-2.8) in PCI patients.

Patients with early fever exhibited a lower rate of positive microbial cultures compared to patients with late fever (13% vs 24%; P = 0.002) (Figure 1).

Discussion

In this large, multicenter, registry-based study, we provide a detailed overview of the relevance of fever in patients with AMI-CS. The major findings of this study are as follows. 1) Fever was highly prevalent in this population (40%) and was associated with greater disease severity. 2) The majority of patients with AMI-CS and fever did not have a positive microbial culture (89%). 3) Early fever (<72 hours) may be a distinct entity from late fever as it has a lower association with documented infectious etiologies. 4) Fever did not impact hospital mortality but it was associated with longer hospital stays and higher rates of discharges to SNFs. 5) The use of antibiotics was high in AMI-CS regardless of the presence of fever, and it was associated with lower adjusted mortality in the overall cohort. Our study has several strengths, including a stringent definition of fever, a large sample size with granular information regarding shock severity, management strategies, strict culture speciation, and insight into discharge types among survivors.

Fever in the AMI-CS population presents a major clinical challenge. On one hand, it can be a sign of sepsis, which can be a devastating complication in these already critically ill patients.5,15 On the other hand, it can be a manifestation of physiologic inflammation as part of the myocardial repair process in AMI or of dysregulated inflammation in the context of SIRS.5,16 Although the prevalence of sepsis in AMI-CS appears to be low (around 6%), its presence markedly increased mortality in both observational studies and in the SHOCK trial, where positive cultures in the setting of SIRS doubled the risk of death.6,15

In our study, cultures were obtained in most patients with fever, but proven infection was rare despite the higher rates of invasive management strategies like mechanical ventilation, PAC, and MCS in febrile patients.6 Nevertheless, 84% of febrile patients received antibiotics early during their hospitalization, before cultures could be resulted, and for prolonged periods despite negative microbial cultures in many cases. This practice aligns with the Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines and current evidence.17,18

Overall, the organisms isolated in blood cultures of patients with and without fever were similar except for higher prevalence of Streptococcus spp. in patients without fever (42% vs 13%) and Candida spp. in patients with fever (13% vs 0%) (Supplemental Figure 1). Notably, urine cultures did not seem to be associated with the presence of fever.

A high usage of antibiotics (42%) was also seen among nonfebrile patients, possibly driven by suspicion of mixed shock—a state of inappropriate vasodilation in CS linked to SIRS or sepsis and associated with worse outcomes. Signs such as vasodilation or rising leukocytosis may have prompted empirical treatment, despite the even lower culture positivity rates in this group compared to febrile patients.5,15,19,20

These findings highlight the low threshold for antibiotic usage in CS, which has been reported in previous studies.5,21 Although the advantages of treating microbial infections early seem clear, and antibiotic treatment was associated with lower adjusted mortality in this study, the adverse effects of widespread antibiotic use at an individual and population level are less often considered in critically ill patients.22

Several findings in our study point toward noninfectious etiologies of fever in AMI-CS. Fever onset was concentrated early during the hospitalization, and when occurring in the first 72 hours, it was associated with lower culture positivity rates (13%) compared to fever occurring later in the hospitalization (24%). This suggests that in this early period fever may be more frequently caused by aseptic inflammation.

Evidence shows that in the first 3 to 4 days after a myocardial infarction, there is an acute inflammatory reaction triggered by necrotic cardiomyocytes that serves as the initial phase in the myocardial repair and scarring process.16 Human studies have shown that fever in AMI after primary PCI usually occurs within this timeframe and is associated with higher levels of inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein, indicating its association with inflammation and perhaps a myocardial repair process.1,4,23 In fact, there is evidence that fever prolonged beyond this initial period may signal a dysregulation of physiological inflammation leading to more adverse clinical events.23

Multivariable regression identified female sex, mechanical ventilation, acute hemodialysis, and non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) etiology as predictors of infectious fever. These factors may guide clinicians in deciding whether to initiate antibiotics in febrile AMI-CS patients. Notably, early fever, MCS use, and PAC use were not associated with positive cultures in adjusted analysis (Figure 4). Leukocytosis was also not associated with positive cultures, a novel finding that challenges their utility as infection markers in this context.

In sum, the approach to management in this cohort remains complex, especially in those patients that may also have a vasodilatory component to their shock and be in mixed shock. Based on our results, and especially in the first 72 hours, such patients are likely suffering a primarily cardiogenic etiology of shock, although invasive hemodynamics may be confusing.24 This implies that antibiotic treatment could be delayed, and treatment with MCS and other invasive measures to support cardiac output should not be avoided for fear of bloodstream infections. A similar approach is taken in patients that have undergone cardiac surgery, where fevers that occur <48 to 72 hours postsurgery are assumed to be noninfectious and are generally neither treated with antibiotics nor even cultured.25,26 However, we acknowledge that delaying antibiotics may be challenging in this acutely ill population, where all precautions are taken to prevent patient deterioration and decrease potential legal liability from undertreatment. This underscores the need for more studies in this area to improve antibiotic stewardship in AMI-CS.27

Study Limitations

Besides the limitations intrinsic to the retrospective observational design, our study is based on ICD-10 code diagnoses of CS, STEMI, and NSTEMI, which may lead to inaccuracies in patient classification.21 We are also limited by the lack of respiratory cultures or chest imaging to approximate the diagnosis of lower respiratory infections. Moreover, microbial cultures are known to have low positivity rates in community-acquired sepsis, which suggests we may have a significant, yet undefined, number of culture-negative infections among febrile patients.28 Another important limitation is the lack of information regarding the source of measurement of BT (eg, axillary, oral, rectal, or other) for each value, which may lead to significant variations. We tried to address this by using a stringent definition of fever as noted in our methods section. We also lack information on the proportion of cardiac arrest patients who received targeted temperature management, although this is considered standard of care in our system in accordance with current guidelines. Finally, we do not have data on systemic vascular resistance to estimate the role of vasodilation in patients with and without fever.

Conclusions

The role of fever in patients with AMI-CS is complex. Fever is highly prevalent and rarely associated with positive cultures, particularly when occurring within the first 72 hours of hospitalization. Nonetheless, it is frequently treated with antibiotics. Although it is associated with higher shock severity and more invasive therapies, only female sex, mechanical ventilation, NSTEMI etiology, and acute dialysis were associated with positive cultures in adjusted analysis. While not associated with higher hospital mortality, fever was associated with longer length of hospital stay and higher rates of discharge to SNFs.
