Investigation of the Effects of Noncoding LDLR Variants on Hyperlipidaemia risk
Authors: Daniela Rojano, David Curtis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCoding and noncoding variants of the LDLR gene have been reported to cause familial hyperlipidaemia (FH) and rare coding variants have been shown to increase risk of hyperlipidaemia in the general population.MethodsHyperlipidaemia status had previously been assigned for 470,000 UK Biobank participants in a study of rare coding variants. Effects of variants obtained from whole-genome sequencing in the five-prime untranslated region (5′UTR) and promoter region of LDLR were analysed using this phenotype. Variants previously reported to be implicated in FH and associated with altered LDLR expression were entered into logistic regression analysis. Other variants in these regions with minor allele frequency ≤0.01 were entered into a weighted burden test. For this analysis, insertion-deletion variants were weighted according to rarity while single-nucleotide variants were additionally weighted using 2 pathogenicity predictors, CADD and GPN-MSA.ResultsThe previously reported common variant, rs17248720C>T, was confirmed to slightly reduce hyperlipidaemia risk. Two previously reported rare variants which were associated with markedly reduced LDLR expression, c.-101T>C and c.-121T>C, had at most only moderate effects on hyperlipidaemia risk. In the weighted burden analysis, promoter variants annotated with CADD showed evidence for association with increased risk (p = 0.00097). However, the effect size was small, with variants having CADD scores exceeding 0.5 having OR = 1.07 (0.99–1.15).ConclusionAlthough rare noncoding variants do have detectable effects on hyperlipidaemia risk, these effects are much smaller than those detected for coding variants in the same sample. This research has been conducted using the UK Biobank Resource.

Introduction

Loss of function (LOF) and other variants in LDLR have long been recognised as a cause of familial hyperlipidaemia (FH) but a study of 470,000 exome-sequenced UK Biobank participants showed that nonsynonymous variants in LDLR, especially those predicted to be damaging or deleterious by PolyPhen or SIFT, also increased risk of hyperlipidaemia in the general population [1–3]. Although statistically significant at p < 10−9, the effect size of such variants was much smaller than for LOF variants, with an estimated OR of 1.61 (1.39–1.86) for those annotated as probably damaging by PolyPhen, compared with an OR of 23.48 (13.63–40.46) for LOF variants. In addition to coding variants in the exons of LDLR, there have also been reports of noncoding variants identified in individuals with FH, and these were deposited in the UCL low-density lipoprotein receptor gene variant database which is now hosted at the Leiden Open Variant Database (https://databases.lovd.nl/shared/genes/LDLR) [4]. This lists 226 variants in the five-prime untranslated region (5′UTR) and promoter region of LDLR. For some of these, functional studies have shown that the reported variant acts to reduce gene expression [5–7]. Conversely, a common variant 2 kb upstream of LDLR which is associated with reduced risk of non-FH, rs17248720C>T, was shown to result in an increase in transcriptional activity [8]. Most recently, a 5′UTR variant, c.-35C>G, which introduces an upstream translation initiation codon, was identified in a 100,000 Genomes patient with FH and was shown to result in reduced protein synthesis [9]. Whole-genome sequence data have now been made available for UK Biobank participants, allowing for a more general investigation of the role of noncoding LDLR variants in susceptibility to hyperlipidaemia in the general population [10].

In the previous study of rare coding variants in UK Biobank participants, LDLR nonsynonymous variants in general had little effect on hyperlipidaemia risk but the effect was confined largely to those variants annotated as probably damaging by PolyPhen [1, 3]. However, PolyPhen uses predicted amino acid changes to assess the likely pathogenicity of variants and hence cannot be used to annotate noncoding variants. A recent study described a deleteriousness prediction score based on a DNA language model termed GPN-MSA (genomic pretrained network with multiple-sequence alignment) [11]. GPN-MSA is a masked language model trained on whole-genome multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) across 100 vertebrate species. By modelling the probability distribution of nucleotides in their evolutionary context, the model captures patterns of conservation and constraint without relying on external annotations or labelled training data. It predicts the likelihood of reference vs. alternate alleles and outputs a log-likelihood ratio score that reflects how unexpected a substitution is within the learned sequence model. Because it is based only on DNA sequence, it can be applied to both coding and noncoding variants, and the authors reported that it performed well compared to the CADD predictor when applied to nonsynonymous variants [12]. We compared the performance of GPN-MSA and a number of other pathogenicity predictors using rare coding variants for 18 gene-phenotype pairs and showed that it had superior performance to CADD and was able to detect association between hyperlipidaemia and nonsynonymous LDLR variants, albeit with less power than PolyPhen [13].

A study of rare variants with high CADD scores in a subsample of 166,740 UK Biobank participants reported a number of associations with 42 blood cell count and biomarker measurements [14]. This revealed gene-wise association of rare coding variants in LDLR with apolipoprotein A, apolipoprotein B, and cholesterol but no significant associations with noncoding variants in LDLR were reported.

The present study set out to investigate noncoding variants in LDLR promoter and 5′UTR regions in the same 470,000 UK Biobank participants who were previously used in the study of coding variants and hyperlipidaemia, with the aim of gaining insights into the relative contributions of coding and noncoding variants to disease risk.

Methods

The UK Biobank Research Analysis Platform was used to access the whole-genome sequence data which had been obtained using the methods previously described [10]. UK Biobank had obtained ethics approval from the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee which covers the UK (Approval No: 11/NW/0382) and had obtained written informed consent from all participants. The UK Biobank approved an application for use of the data (ID 51119), and ethics approval for the analyses was obtained from the UCL Research Ethics Committee (11527/001).

The LDLR MANE Select transcript NM_000527.5/ENST00000558518.6 was used with reference GRCh38. Two regions of interest were studied. The first region included the promoter and extended from the previously implicated upstream variant rs17248720 at chr19: 11087511 to the transcription start site at chr19:11089462, while the second region consisted of the 5′UTR extending from the transcription start site to the coding start site at chr19:11089548. With the exception of rs17248720 itself, attention was restricted to variants with minor allele frequency (MAF) ≤ 0.01.

As described previously, a composite hyperlipidaemia phenotype had been obtained for 470,000 participants based on information from self-report, diagnoses recorded in medical records and prescribed medication [1]. Cases were defined as participants diagnosed with hyperlipidaemia according to any of these criteria, while all other participants were taken to be controls. Using this dichotomous clinical phenotype meant that the results of this study would be directly comparable with the previous study of coding variants, which had been carried out using the same phenotype. It also has an advantage over using direct measures of blood lipids in that there is no need to take into account any effects of lipid-lowering medications.

To obtain population principal components reflecting ancestry, version 2.0 of PLINK (https://www.cog-genomics.org/plink/2.0/) had previously been run on the exome sequence data with the options --maf 0.1 --pca 20 approx [15, 16]. These principal components would not be expected to differ markedly from those which might be derived from genome data, and their use enhanced comparability with the previous analysis of coding data.

Those variants from the Leiden Open Variation Database which had previously been reported to have a functional effect on LDLR expression and which were observed in the UK Biobank sample were entered into a multiple logistic regression analysis with hyperlipidaemia as the outcome and with sex and principal components as covariates.

To investigate the association of other variants, a weighted burden analysis was performed similar to that which had been used to test for the effect of coding variants [1]. Attention was restricted to variants with MAF ≤ 0.01. As described in detail elsewhere, a weighting scheme using a parabolic function based on allele frequency was used such that variants with MAF close to 0.01 received a weight of 1, while extremely rare variants with MAF close to 0 received a weight of 10 [17]. Small insertions and deletions (INDELs) were assigned this frequency-based weight. However, single-nucleotide substitution variants (SNVs) were additionally assigned functional weights consisting of the raw CADD v1.7 score and the GPN-MSA score [11, 12]. The GPN-MSA score was multiplied by −1, meaning that variants in which the alternate allele had a lower likelihood, suggesting a possible biological effect, would be assigned a higher weight. The overall weights for each SNV consisted of the product of these functional weights and the frequency-based weights. This is the same approach as has been used for testing association of rare coding variants with multiple phenotypes, and a previous systematic investigation revealed that utilising frequency-based weights in this way provided increased power [18]. For each region, each participant would receive an INDEL score, a CADD score, and a GPN-MSA score, consisting of the sums of the weights of all the relevant variants carried by that participant. The analysis specification files incorporated standard QC approaches which had been applied to previous datasets. Variants were excluded if there were more than 10% of genotypes missing or if the heterozygote count was smaller than both homozygote counts. If a participant was not genotyped for a variant, then they were assigned the participant-wise average score for that variant. However, in practice, these measures had little relevance to the current dataset because the variants were almost completely genotyped and the maximum number of genotypes missing for any variant was 8 out of 467,591 participants.

To test for association, multiple logistic regression was performed using as covariates sex, 20 principal components, and the genotypes of the previously reported variants with effects on expression. For the null model, hyperlipidaemia status was predicted solely from these covariates. For the alternative model, hyperlipidaemia status was predicted from these covariates along with the INDEL, CADD, and GPN-MSA scores. The likelihoods of these models were compared in a likelihood ratio test and a p value obtained assuming that twice the log likelihood difference followed a chi-squared distribution with 3 degrees of freedom.

Because these analyses demonstrated an association between hyperlipidaemia and CADD scores for promoter variants, a follow-up analysis was carried out to characterise the effect of variants with high CADD scores and hyperlipidaemia risk. Raw counts for variants with CADD scores above 0.5, accounting for approximately 10% of all variants, were entered into a logistic regression analysis with the same covariates as used previously but without any weighting of scores by frequency. Data manipulation and statistical analyses were carried out using GENEVARASSOC, SCOREASSOC, and R [17, 19, 20].

Results

There were 105,654 hyperlipidaemia cases and 361,937 controls. The previously reported c.-135C>G variant at chr19:11089414 was seen in 1 case and no controls so could not be formally entered into the initial logistic regression analysis. Three other variants which had previously been reported to affect expression were observed in the UK Biobank sample: c.-101T>C at chr19: 11089448, c.-121T>C at chr19:11089428, and rs17248720C>T at chr19:11087511. These were entered into multiple logistic regression analysis with hyperlipidaemia as the outcome and with sex and principal components as covariates. The first variant, chr19:11089448T>C, was carried by 6 cases and 14 controls and showed no statistically significant evidence for association with hyperlipidaemia, OR (95% CI) = 1.30 (0.49–3.50). Chr19:11089428T>C was carried by 107 cases and 247 controls and was significantly associated with increased risk of hyperlipidaemia, OR = 1.58 (1.25–1.99), p = 0.00011. The common variant, rs17248720C>T, had MAF = 0.107 in cases and MAF = 0.126 in controls and was associated with a small but highly statistically significant reduction in hyperlipidaemia risk, OR = 0.82 (0.81–0.83), p = 3.0e−135.

Aside from these variants, there were 627 SNVs and 77 INDELs in the promoter region and 86 SNVs and 3 INDELS in the 5′UTR. Weighted burden analysis was carried out in each of these regions using as covariates sex, principal components, and the genotypes for chr19:11089428T>C and rs17248720C>T. There was no evidence for an overall association of the INDEL, CADD, and GPN-MSA scores with hyperlipidaemia in the 5′UTR, chi-squared = 3.12, 3 df, p = 0.37, and none of the scores was individually associated. One case did carry the c.−35C>G variant recently reported by the 100,000 Genomes project, and we do not know whether this represents the same individual having also enrolled in UK Biobank [10]. In the promoter region, the scores were collectively associated with hyperlipidaemia, chi-squared = 11.38, 3 df, p = 0.0099. This result was driven entirely by the CADD scores, beta = 0.021 (0.008–0.033), p = 0.00097, while there was no association with either the INDEL scores, beta = 0.0001 (−0.0086–0.0088), or the GPN-MSA scores, beta = −0.0009 (−0.0046–0.0028). Inspection of the detailed results revealed that the association did not appear to be driven by a small number of variants with higher frequency in cases but rather arose from the cumulative effect of many variants which were mostly extremely rare. The full list of variants with their genotype counts in controls and cases is provided in online supplementary Table 1 (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553528). There were 79 variants with CADD scores exceeding 0.5, and these had allele counts of 3,475 in controls and 937 in cases, with an average MAF of 0.005. When dichotomised in this way and without any weighting for frequency, their estimated effect size was small and not formally statistically significant, with carriage of a high-scoring variant having OR = 1.07 (0.99–1.15). It is possible that using different thresholds would result in different estimates for the effect size.

Discussion

In this sample, we find no statistically significant evidence of association of the promoter variant c.-101T>C at chr19:11089448 with hyperlipidaemia. This variant was initially identified in a single patient meeting the criteria for FH and was included in the LDLR Gene FH Variant Database following in silico analyses which concluded that it was probably pathogenic [21]. In subsequent functional studies using a luciferase assay, this variant was reported to show significantly reduced mean (±SD) luciferase activity (64 ± 8%, p < 0.001) [7]. Electrophoretic mobility shift assays identified suggestive allele-specific differential binding of liver (hepatoma) nuclear proteins. These results were judged to suggest that the variant was pathogenic. In the UK Biobank sample, it is carried only by 6 cases and 14 controls, and although one cannot exclude the possibility that it has some effect on hyperlipidaemia risk, the confidence interval obtained from the logistic regression analysis means that any effect is so small that this variant should not be regarded as causative of FH in the same way as is exemplified by LOF LDLR variants which have a near-Mendelian effect, with autosomal dominant transmission and high penetrance for severe disease.

The promoter variant c.-121T>C at chr19:11089428 was originally identified in a patient with possible FH [22]. Functional studies showed significantly reduced mean (±SD) luciferase activity (72 ± 8%, p < 0.001) and differential allele-specific binding of liver nuclear proteins [7]. This variant is significantly associated with increased risk of hyperlipidaemia in the UK Biobank participants. However, the effect size is only moderate and too low for this variant to be regarded as causative of FH, with OR = 1.58 (1.25–1.99). We replicate the previously reported finding that the common variant rs17248720C>T, which results in increased transcriptional activity, is associated with reduced risk of hyperlipidaemia, although again the effect size is modest [8].

We show that rare variants with high CADD score in the promoter region of LDLR are associated with increased risk of hyperlipidaemia but do not detect any signal using GPN-MSA or INDELs and do not detect an association for variants in the 5′UTR. The effect we detect is modest, and the statistical significance is less than that obtained from nonsynonymous coding variants using the same phenotype and the same statistical methodology [1]. The statistical significance for the promoter variant CADD score is p = 0.00097, whereas coding variants annotated with PolyPhen achieved a significance of p < 10−9. The effect size for the noncoding variants with CADD > 0.5 is estimated at OR = 1.07 (0.99–1.15), whereas coding variants annotated as probably damaging by PolyPhen had OR = 1.61 (1.39–1.86). While the results for promoter variants annotated by CADD may well indicate a real effect, these results can be only deemed statistically significant because a single gene was tested, and this effect would not be picked up in the context of a genome-wide analysis applied to all genes. The fact that this association signal is driven by the cumulative effect of a large number of extremely rare variants rather than by 1 or 2 common variants supports the idea that it probably represents a direct effect of these noncoding variants and is unlikely to be due to them being in linkage disequilibrium with functional variants nearby, perhaps in the coding region.

It is of note that c.-101T>C and c.-121T>C are associated with at most moderate increases in the risk of hyperlipidaemia, with OR no greater than 3.5, in spite of appearing to have quite marked effects on transcription, with reported reductions to 64% and 72%, respectively, compared to normal luciferase activity. By comparison, LOF variants in LDLR, which are causative of FH and with OR = 23.5 for the hyperlipidaemia phenotype, result in haploinsufficiency and a reduction to 50% of normal activity of the LDLR protein [23]. If the experimentally measured reduction of transcription translated directly to reduced protein activity, then one might expect to observe much more pronounced effects on risk from these 2 noncoding variants. However, the disproportionately large difference in phenotypic effects between these noncoding variants and LOF variants relative to their apparent effects on transcription is in line with the notion that traditional expression studies may not always accurately reflect the biological consequences of DNA variants [24].

Although the association we report here between LDLR promoter variants with high CADD scores and hyperlipidaemia in the general population is novel, this finding is not unexpected and arguably does not add much to our understanding of pathophysiology. The variants which contribute to this finding are individually so rare that it is not possible to determine which ones are responsible for the overall effect. Cumulatively, they are so rare that they would make only a small contribution to the risk at the level of the population and, given this and their small effect size, would have little utility in assessing risk for an individual.

There are very few other examples of common phenotypes for which rare coding variants in a gene exert a large effect on risk, such that it might be reasonable to attempt to detect a similar association with promoter variants. If methods of annotating noncoding variants improved, then this could increase the power to detect such associations by better highlighting a priori those variants most likely to have an effect. Nevertheless, these findings and those from more extensive studies support the view that in terms of gene discovery and better understanding of pathophysiology, it seems that the most valuable information might be largely confined to the coding regions of genes [25].
