The small-cage induced sedentariness in male young rats: evidence from energy expenditure and glucose uptake
Authors: L M Liang, X X Zhang, H Chi, J Y Liu, L Y Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent studies have frequently employed small-cage housing as a method to induce sedentary behavior (SED) in animal models. However, no research has been conducted to prove that the small cage can limit energy expenditure (EE) below the established SED threshold of 1.5 METs. This study demonstrated the EE of small-cage animals across 24 h and detected alterations in glucose uptake after sedentariness in young male rats. After an 8-weeks period, energy consumption of the animals housed in small-cage environments (S group) stayed below 1.5 METs in 24-h. In contrast, the rats in control group had significant variance in energy expenditure throughout 24-h, with energy consumption exceeding 1.5 METs in over half of the cases. Besides, a significant reduction in body weight was observed in rats in S group compared to controls after 2 weeks. Metabolic assessments demonstrated persistent elevations in fasting blood glucose (FBG) and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels in the S group throughout the study duration. Relatively muscle weight (wet muscle weight/ body weight) of gastrocnemius and soleus in S group was also decreased. The biochemical analysis indicated reduced muscle glycogen content in the gastrocnemius after both 4-week and 8-week time points. Histopathological examination through HE staining demonstrated disorganized arrangement of muscle fibers in the gastrocnemius of S group animals. Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) revealed a significant divergence in glucose levels between groups at 8 weeks, with control animals demonstrating improved glucose clearance, particularly reaching statistical significance at 15 min. Similarly, the area under the curve (AUC) value for the S group was significantly higher than that in the control group. Overall, our findings provide empirical evidence that small-cage housing effectively reduces EE below the established SED threshold of 1.5 METs. Introduction

Sedentary behavior (SED), characterized as any activity during the waking state that involves sitting or lying down and an EE ≤ 1.5 Metabolic Equivalent of Task (METs)1–5. The significance of regulating SED has been increasingly recognized in global public health researchers. Current epidemiological evidence has established significant associations between SED and multiple health outcomes, including mental health disorder6,7, obesity8–10, bone health11–13, muscle Characteristics14,15 and glucose metabolism16,17. However, the majority of existing research on SED is limited to observational studies examining correlations between SED and various health parameters. Currently, there are very few experimental investigations exploring the mechanisms underlying the associations between SED and health outcomes—likely due to the persistent ambiguity surrounding valid animal models of SED. Current experimental approaches—including cast immobilization18,19, hind-limb unloading20,21, and denervation models, fail to meet the operational criteria for a SED, as they do not replicate the characteristic low-energy expenditure (≤ 1.5 METs) that defines SED. A metabolic equivalent (METs) is a standardized unit for quantifying physical activity intensity and metabolic rate relative to resting energy expenditure (RMR).

To address these limitations, the primary goal of this study is to explore the effects of long-term SED on EE and glucose absorption in rats raised in small-cage. Our fundamental hypothesis is small-cage confinement can effectively induce a sedentary state meeting the SED criteria in rats, leading to measurable alterations in glucose uptake.

Discussion

As previously mentioned, SED has increasingly attracted the attention of scholars. However, existing research predominantly remains observational, partly because of limited experimental validation through animal models that accurately replicate human SED. The main reason for this is the lack of a unified animal model that can accurately replicate human SED. To address this key limitation, the primary goal of this study was to explore the effects of long‑term SED on EE and glucose absorption in rats housed in small cages. Our fundamental hypothesis was that small‑cage confinement can effectively induce a sedentary state that meets the standard criteria of SED in rats. To date, several animal models have been proposed to mimic SED, yet each carries notable limitations that compromise their reliability and applicability. Kakehi, et al.18 applied 24-h utilized a 24-h hind-limb cast immobilization technique on mice to simulate mice physical inactivity, and assessing intra-cellular lipids and the insulin signaling in the soleus muscle. Similarly, Tomiga, et al.22 used the same experimental paradigm to model physical inactivity. In addition to limb immobilization, alternative methodologies such as hind-limb unloading or denervation have been employed to simulate SED. While these approaches seek to restrict limb mobility or reduce lower extremity loading via distinct mechanisms, they fail to align with the operational definition of SED. According to established criteria, SED is defined by waking behaviors with EE ≤ 1.5 METs while in a sitting, reclining, or lying posture23. This definition underscores that SED encompasses a range of low-energy physical activities, rather than merely involving restricted physical movement. Notably, conventional models of physical inactivity often introduce non-specific stress in animals, whereas the novel small‑cage model used in the present study was designed to avoid such confounding stress responses24.

In contrast, recent studies advocate for small-cage confinement as a more ecologically valid model. Marmonti, et al.25 reported that the animals showed a significant decrease in soleus muscle mass, grip force and cross-sectional area, together with a decrease after reduced space designed to restrict their locomotion movements and allow them to drink and eat easily, without generating physical stress. Siripoksup, et al.26 further propose that small-cage more reliably recapitulates skeletal muscle metabolic perturbations associated with SED. Supporting this, Yoshihara, et al.27 found that the serum corticosterone level and adrenal weight, which were markers of stressors, did not differ between the inactivity group and control groups. Therefore, compared to the immersion model, bed rest, limb immobilization, and other models, the “small-cage” can provide better adequate free-living environments without unnecessary restraint stress27,28.

Even so, previous studies have demonstrated that reducing an animal’s living space by using small-cage can mimic SED. But none have validated its alignment with the quantitative SED criterion of EE ≤ 1.5 METs. Therefore, to better clarify the translational relevance of small-caged animal models to SED, we adopted and modified the experimental protocols established by Tyganov, et al.28 and Marmonti, et al.25. In brief, male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats were housed individually in small Plexiglas cages for 8 consecutive weeks. Using a 24‑hour whole‑body energy expenditure monitoring system, we demonstrated that rats subjected to small‑cage housing consistently maintained EE below 1.5 METs, thereby satisfying the core metabolic threshold for defining SED. RER was determined as an auxiliary parameter for the calculation and interpretation of EE, providing supportive information for the overall assessment of energy metabolic status. In addition to verifying the metabolic criterion, we further evaluated multiple SED-related physiological alterations, with a focus on glucose metabolism and skeletal muscle homeostasis. Our results revealed that 8 weeks of SED exposure led to loosely organized myofibers in the soleus muscle, significant reductions in skeletal muscle mass and glycogen storage, elevated FBG, and markedly impaired glucose metabolic function. These observations are in good agreement with previous studies: Piyarat, et al.26 reported mitochondrial metabolic inflexibility in skeletal muscle following small-cage-induced physical inactivity; Tyganov, et al.28 observed pronounced reductions in the mass of the soleus and extensor digitorum longus muscles after 21 days of restricted movement; similarly, Alabi, et al.24 documented significant increases in serum glucose and insulin levels after just 8 h of sedentary conditions.

Collectively, these findings confirm that our small-cage model successfully recapitulates key features of SED by meeting its core metabolic criterion and inducing representative SED-associated physiological phenotypes, including disrupted skeletal muscle glucose metabolism, insulin resistance, and impaired systemic glycemic regulation.

Notably, this study has certain limitations that should be noted, which also point to directions for future research. First, the current study only included male SD rats, without involving female rats or other animal species (e.g., mice). This may limit the generalizability of the findings, as physiological responses to SED might vary by gender and species. Future research could expand the scope of subjects to include different genders and species to enhance the external validity of the results. Second, the intervention duration of the small-cage model was fixed at 8 weeks, and the dynamic changes of SED-induced physiological effects over different time courses (e.g., short-term and long-term) remain to be explored. Subsequent studies may set up multiple time-point interventions to clarify the time-dependent characteristics of SED’s impacts. Third, this study mainly focused on phenotypic changes in skeletal muscle structure and glucose metabolism, without in-depth investigation into the underlying molecular regulatory mechanisms. Future work could further explore key signal pathways, gene expression, and epigenetic modifications related to SED-induced metabolic disorders, to comprehensively elucidate the pathological process of SED-related health hazards.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

Sprague–Dawley (SD) male rats were obtained from the Guangxi Medical University Laboratory Animal Center (Nanning, Guangxi Province, China). All experimental protocols were approved by the animal ethics committee of Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee of Guangxi Normal University (GXNU-202303-029). Six-week-old rats were divided into 2 groups (n = 14): vivarium control (C group); SED (S group). C Group: Control animals were housed in standard polycarbonate cages (dimensions: 31.0 × 23.0 × 36.0 cm; floor area ≈713 cm2), with wood shavings provided as bedding. Animals were maintained in groups of 6 rats per cage. S group: Rats subjected to SED were individually housed in restrictive small Plexiglas cages (dimensions: 20.0 × 9.0 × 10.0 cm; floor area ≈ 180 cm2; see Supplementary Figure S1). These cages were specifically designed to severely limit both movement and social contact. Animals were maintained in groups of one rat per cage. Within the housing rack, seven SED cages were placed side-by-side in a single uninterrupted row, ensuring visual and auditory contact between adjacent rats but preventing any physical interaction due to the solid cage walls.

All animals, irrespective of group assignment, were maintained within the same environmentally controlled vivarium room. Ambient temperature was regulated at 22–24 °C, with relative humidity maintained between 50 and 60%. A standardized 12:12-h light–dark cycle (lights on at 07:00 h) was imposed. Standard laboratory rodent chow (specify diet, e.g., Purina LabDiet 5001; containing approximately 23% protein, 4.5% fat, 6% fiber) and tap water were provided ad libitum to all rats throughout the experimental period. Bedding material (wood shavings) was changed twice weekly, and food/water replenished as needed.

Following 4 or 8 weeks of the respective housing regimen, rats were anesthetized with deep sedation via intraperitoneal injection of tribromoethanol at a dose of 400 mg/kg body weight—consistent with the safe dose range for rodents in short-term experimental procedures (200–400 mg/kg). This anesthetic was selected for its minimal interference with key metabolic indicators and suitable duration (40–60 min) to facilitate muscle tissue sampling. Upon confirmation of deep anesthesia (assessed by absence of pedal reflex), animals were subsequently euthanized by decapitation for tissue dissection.

Metabolic monitoring of rats

Whole-body metabolic parameters were assessed using respirometry cages (ZS-EMemrV, Multiplexed Respirometry Cage, Zhong Shi Technology, China) over a 24-h period. Sample measurements of VO2, CO2, RER, food intake (g), water intake (g), sum locomotor distance (m) and locomotor patterns (axis breaks) were recorded at 1-min intervals. We collected RER and EE within 60 min and take their average value. EE was calculated by software in real-time using the equation (EE = (3.815 + 1.232 × RER) × VO2 /lean mass)29. Values of EE and locomotor activity exceeding ± 3 interquartile range (calculated on all 3-min intervals for all rats) were excluded from calculations of the mean. Selection of Resting Metabolic Value: The energy expenditure data obtained over 24 h were arranged in ascending order, and the lowest value is selected as the resting metabolic value. Calculation of metabolic equivalent: METs = Hourly energy expenditure value / Resting metabolic value. SED state was defined when METs ≤ 1.5.

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA)

At 4 and 8 weeks, body composition was assessed using an INSIGHT VET DXA scanner (Osteosys). To ensure adequate immobilization, mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (40 mg/kg) and medetomidine (0.8 mg/kg). Lean tissue mass and fat mass were quantified by the scanner, consistent with established protocols30.

Biochemical analysis

FBG concentration was determined from tail vein blood samples using a glucometer (GA-3, SANNUO, China). Each blood sample was centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min, and the serum supernatant was collected into a new centrifuge tube. Serum insulin levels were quantified using a Rat Insulin ELISA Kit (ER1113, FineTest, Wuhan, China). HbA1c levels were measured with a Rat HbA1c ELISA Kit (ER1030, FineTest, Wuhan, China). Gastrocnemius muscle glycogen content was assessed using a Muscle Glycogen Assay Kit (JianCheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China).

Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) and insulin tolerance test (ITT) measurements

Following a 12-h overnight fast, rats were administered a 50% glucose solution (2 IU/kg body weight) via oral gavage. Blood glucose levels were measured at baseline (0 min) and at 15-, 30-, 60-, 90-, and 120-min post-administration. The area under the curve (AUC) for the OGTT was calculated to quantify glucose tolerance.

For the ITT, animals were similarly fasted for 12 h prior to intraperitoneal injection of recombinant human insulin (2 U/kg body weight; P3375-400 IU, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Glycemia was assessed at identical time intervals (0, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min) following insulin administration. The the area under the curve (AUC) for the ITT was subsequently computed to evaluate insulin sensitivity.

Soleus histomorphometry

After 8 weeks of intervention, soleus muscle samples from rats in each group were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. The isolated soleus muscles were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution at 4 °C for 24 h, followed by dehydration through a graded ethanol series. Subsequently, the specimens were embedded in paraffin wax, sectioned into 4 μm-thick paraffin slices, and dried at 60 °C. Prior to H&E staining, the slices were deparaffinized and rehydrated sequentially, using a commercial H&E staining kit (GP1031, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). After staining, the slices were mounted with neutral balsam. Images were captured using a Nikon Eclipse Ci microscope (Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a digital camera for subsequent analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± SD based on three independent experiments. All data were analyzed using Prism software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). Intergroup differences were assessed by two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups utilized one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Conclusion

In this study, we increased the evidence of EE in small-caged animal models to meet SED criteria, paving the way for future studies on the mechanism of SED. However, our study has several limitations. Due to different animal sizes, there is no precise, consistent cage parameter in the current research of many small-cage animal models. The variations in EE between various housing spaces and animal size ratios were not covered in this study. Thus, future researches are still needed.
