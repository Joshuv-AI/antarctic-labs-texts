A Measurement Model to Quantify Systemic Bacilli Load in Severe HIV-Associated Tuberculosis
Authors: Bianca Sossen, Charlotte Schutz, Linda Boloko, Tobias Broger, Amy Ward, Abulele Bekiswa, Avuyonke Balfour, Muki Shey, Graeme Meintjes, David Adam Barr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPathogen load is considered fundamental to infection pathobiology but is unobserved or inadequately measured. We used latent variable modeling to characterize HIV-associated tuberculosis mycobacterial load in 519 inpatients. Tests on urine and blood, not sputum, showed substantial covariance. Summarizing covariance via measurement models estimated pathogen loads with stronger correlation with host inflammation and mortality than traditional measures such as blood culture time to positivity.Keywords: biomarker, dissemination, latent class analysis, pathogen load, tuberculosis

Pathogen load is thought to be a major determinant of host response and outcome in infection [1]. Examples from clinical studies include viral infections where polymerase chain reaction cycle threshold (Ct) values have been associated with disease severity and outcome [2, 3] or percentage parasitemia in Plasmodium falciparum infection [4]. However, in most bacterial infections, reliable measures of pathogen are not readily available. Time to positivity (TTP) of blood cultures is the most studied putative pathogen load measure in bacteremia but has been inconsistently associated with clinical outcomes in large well-designed studies [5]. Culture-independent quantification of bacteria has been more convincingly linked with clinical phenotype and mortality—including lipopolysaccharide levels in meningococcal disease [6], genomic bacterial load in Streptococcus pneumoniae [7, 8], and Staphylococcus aureus infection [9]. It may be that as a readout for pathogen load, TTP is subject to significant measurement error, being influenced by multiple factors other than number of bacteria in the sample. Consequently, bacterial load can be considered an unobserved latent variable, largely unreported in clinical studies, or indirectly assessed by imperfect indicator variables such as TTP.

The idea of unobserved latent variables, reported via imperfect but observable indicator variables, is a foundation of many fields outside biomedical research, such as computational biology, natural language processing, social sciences, and psychometrics. Statistical “measurement models” are used to compose summary measures of proposed latent variables from observed data. A motivation for this approach is that information can be combined from multiple “noisy” variables to construct an underlying variable with reduced measurement error, since covariance in the observed variables is likely to represent the effect of an underlying shared process rather than random noise.

Conceptualizing systemic bacilli load as a latent variable in severe HIV-associated tuberculosis (TB), we hypothesized that covariance in Mycobacterium tuberculosis detection assays, captured via a measurement model, would provide a robust readout of pathogen load. We assessed external validity of this bacilli load latent variable, as compared with alternative measures, by strength of association with clinical variables and ability to predict mortality.

METHODS

Study Design, Patients, and Procedures

This analysis involves a previously reported prospective cohort study recruiting participants admitted to hospital in Khayelitsha, Cape Town, with a new diagnosis of HIV-associated TB [10]. In brief, participants were adults with CD4 counts <350 cells/μL and had sputum (with induction if needed), urine, and blood samples collected prospectively for TB testing and storage of neat urine and whole blood. Descriptions of the sampling and laboratory processing methods are available in the Supplementary material. Only participants with microbiologically confirmed TB (including via Xpert MTB/RIF; Cepheid), mycobacterial culture, or urine Determine Alere Antigen (AlereLAM; Abbott) were included in the present analysis.

Patient Consent Statement

The study was approved by the University of Cape Town Human Research Ethics Committee. As previously published, participants documented informed consent per their capacity and in line with the ethics approval [10].

TB Detection Assays Used as Indicator Variables

Each of the 7 systematically obtained TB detection assays (sputum-Xpert, urine-Xpert, blood–Xpert Ultra, FujiLAM, AlereLAM, blood culture, and sputum culture) generates a semiquantitative readout when positive (TTP, Ct value, or optical density [OD]). Results were therefore converted to a 4-level ordinal scale: negative results were assigned 0 and semiquantitative readouts were divided into 3 tertiles, from 1 (highest Ct/TTP, lowest OD) to 3 (lowest Ct/TTP, highest OD). Missing assay results were imputed via multivariate imputation by chained equations (Supplementary Figures 1 and 2).

Statistical Analysis

Ordinal scale polychoric correlations between assays were calculated and patterns of covariance assessed by hierarchical clustering and exploratory factor analysis. Evidence for number of dimensions was assessed by scree plotting, parallel analysis, and bayesian information criterion. Extracted dimensions were assessed for relationship to clinical phenotype via network graphing. A graded response model (GRM) was fit by the R Multidimensional Item Response Theory package (version 1.43) [11]. GRM is latent variable measurement model, applicable when “item responses” (in this case, TB assay results) are reported on an ordered polytomous scale. In GRM, a latent variable (theta) is related to observed item results by a logistic curve with a slope parameter and thresholds that identify boundaries on an ordered outcome. Items (in this case, TB detection assays) with good discrimination for theta (ie, better signal to noise) have higher slope parameters, but items can also vary in the threshold of theta at which they probabilistically move from one ordinal category to the next. Items with poorer discrimination may therefore still give information at levels of theta not well reported by higher discrimination items. The analysis was performed in R (version 4.5.2) with all packages listed in Supplementary Table 1.

RESULTS

Data from 519 participants with confirmed HIV-associated TB were included (Supplementary Table 2). Of the 7 TB detection assays in 519 individuals, 85% of assay results were available; most missing results were from nonproduction of sputum (Supplementary Figure 3). The distribution of semiquantitative readouts (OD, TTP, and Ct) were, in general, skewed with evidence of ceiling and floor effects (Figure 1A, Supplementary Table 3). Assays showed collinearity by anatomic compartment: the strongest correlations were blood culture with blood-Xpert and sputum culture with sputum-Xpert (Figure 1B and 1C). Urine assays were also significantly collinear, but urine-Xpert was more closely related to blood-Xpert than urine lipoarabinomannan (LAM) positivity (Figure 1C).

Parallel analysis suggested that covariance in the 7 assays could be summarized in 3 dimensions (Figure 1D). In a 3-factor model, the first dimension predominantly represented blood-Xpert and blood culture variance but also loading urine-Xpert and urine LAM (Figure 1E). Dimension 2 exclusively represented variation in sputum assays, and dimension 3 loaded residual variation in urine LAM and urine-Xpert that was not captured on dimension 1.

Dimension 1 (blood and urine assay variance) was highly correlated with multiple host response variables, showing high centrality in a network correlation graph (Figure 1F). Dimension 3 (residual urine assay variance) was more peripheral, while dimension 2 (sputum assays) was almost completely unrelated to any clinical variables.

We postulated that dimension 1 was most in keeping with a readout of systemic bacilli load, while dimensions 2 and 3 summarized variance specific to sputum and urine compartments. A GRM comprising the 5 blood and urine assays was fit as a measurement model for systemic bacilli load. This replicated dimension 1 from the previous factor analysis, estimating a latent variable, theta, which was most strongly related to blood–Xpert Ultra and blood culture variance but again also loading covariance of the 3 urine assays (Figure 2A and 2B).

IL-1ra was chosen a priori to represent a host response variable hypothesized to be an important mediator of pathophysiology in prior work. Discrimination for day 28 mortality (bootstrapped receiver operating characteristic [ROC] area under the curve [AUC]) is higher at 0.67, as opposed to 0.55, 0.60, 0.62, and 0.63 for logistic regression models relating theta, sputum-Xpert cycle threshold ordinal scale, blood culture TTP ordinal scale, sum score, or ordinal score to day 28 mortality, respectively. Mortality prediction calibration, assessed by bootstrapped calibration slope closer to 1, and overall model fit, assessed by lower Akaike information criterion (AIC) value, are also better for theta than the other measures.

We compared theta from the GRM model with 3 other measures of systemic or blood bacilli load: blood culture positivity and TTP; a simple sum score totaling the number of positive blood and urine assays (range, 0–5); and an ordinal score summing the ordinal category values (range, 0–15). Sum score, ordinal score, and theta were all highly correlated (rho > 0.88 for all 3 pairwise comparisons). Correlation varied with IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1ra), which was chosen as the most central node in the correlation network and because of prior work [10] (Figure 2C). Blood culture positivity and TTP explained the least variance in IL-1ra (17%) while theta explained the most (34%). Similarly, theta had the greatest predictive accuracy (highest area under the curve, best calibration, and lowest Akaike information criterion) for day 28 mortality (Figure 2D).

DISCUSSION

We found that TB detection assays show patterns of covariance in keeping with underlying systemic bacilli load and formally defined this bacilli load latent variable using a measurement model. We previously used sum scores of TB assays to define bacilli load [10, 12]. The latent variable measurement approach described here outperformed sum scoring, as evidenced by increased variance explained in the host response biomarker IL-1ra and improved discrimination and calibration for mortality prediction. This closer association with important clinical variables external to the measurement model can be interpreted as evidence of reduced measurement error and increased external validity.

A limitation of this study is that some of the TB tests used in the analysis are no longer available (ie, the first-generation FujiLAM assay), precluding direct validation in future studies. However, one advantage of the item response theory model—in contrast to, for example, sum scores—is that estimation of the underlying latent variable is still possible when an individual item is not observed. Relating new diagnostic assays to existing ones through item response theory modeling could be a useful additional way to characterize their utility, by describing assay location and discrimination with respect to bacilli load. Additionally, while we aimed to collect all TB detection assays and host biomarkers in close temporal alignment, this was not always feasible, which could influence our findings. Finally, our results cannot be generalized to other populations, such as those who are ambulatory or HIV negative.

In TB, bacilli load has been thought to have a dose-response relationship with host pathology, at least since the rabbit inoculation models of Robert Koch [13]. Classical descriptions based on postmortem pathology held that in postprimary TB (encompassing all extrapulmonary disease, distinct from cavitary reactivation pulmonary TB), “the number of bacilli reaching the blood stream and multiplying in the tissues is the main concern” governing disease phenotype [14]. Detection of M tuberculosis by blood culture is common in severe HIV-associated TB and is a predictor of mortality [10, 15] but has imperfect sensitivity [16], and other diagnostics can give additional quantitative information on bacilli load [17]. We found blood culture TTP to be a relatively weak predictor, but we show that combining multiple TB detection assays using latent variable modeling resulted in a pathogen load readout that related strongly to mortality and host response. This suggests that a weak association of blood culture TTP does not mean that pathogen load (the latent variable) is unimportant but rather that TTP is an indicator variable biased toward the null by measurement error. This may be true of other bacteremic infections, and our findings support calls for greater attention to pathogen load measurement in infection research [18] through novel laboratory and statistical methods.
