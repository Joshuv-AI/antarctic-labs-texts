Non-approval in the sunlight: FDA’s CRL disclosures, the limits of confidentiality, and lessons from the EMA
Authors: Gabriela Lenarczyk, Timo Minssen, Aaron S Kesselheim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issues complete response letters (CRLs) after completing a review when it determines that it cannot approve a drug or biological product application in its current form. Historically, the FDA has treated CRLs as confidential, leaving sponsors largely in control of how non-approval decisions are described publicly. This approach has been criticized for relying on an expansive view of confidentiality, which can obscure safety, efficacy, and quality-related concerns, and for enabling selective or incomplete sponsor communications. In 2025, the FDA shifted course. On July 10, 2025, the FDA posted more than 200 CRLs tied to applications that had subsequently been approved, consolidating information that was often already embedded in the approval action packages. On September 4, 2025, the FDA released 89 previously unpublished CRLs for pending or withdrawn applications, and committed to real-time disclosure of newly issued CRLs going forward. Using a comparative FDA-European Medicines Agency (EMA) lens, we assess what these initiatives changed at launch and how early implementation through May 2026 affects their durability, legal footing, and information value. By May 2026, the FDA’s CRL initiative had evolved into a functioning yet unsettled disclosure infrastructure: openFDA now offers searchable access to CRLs for approved and unapproved applications, while the FDA’s FY 2027 budget request simultaneously seeks clearer statutory authority to disclose certain information in CRLs. We argue that FDA CRLs and EMA assessment outputs are complementary, and we propose lessons that FDA can learn from the EMA to maximize the value of CRL disclosure: codifying timelines and redaction standards, adding neutral decisional summaries where redactions or technical language would otherwise obscure the basis for non-approval, and standardizing the classification and discoverability of prior-cycle CRLs in approval packages. These reforms can protect legitimate confidential commercial information while enabling learning from unsuccessful applications.Keywords: confidential commercial information, complete response letters, food and drug administration, regulatory transparency, freedom of information act, European medicines agency

I. INTRODUCTION

When the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) declines to approve a drug or biologic application, it communicates its reasons in a complete response letter (CRL).1 These letters are often the most direct statement of what, in the agency’s view, prevented approval: deficiencies in clinical evidence, safety signals, manufacturing and quality problems, or unresolved inspection findings. Yet historically, the public rarely saw them.2 Outside narrow circumstances, the FDA treated CRLs and the underlying application file as confidential unless and until the product was approved. In practice, this has meant that the public narrative about non-approval has been shaped primarily by sponsors, through press releases that may be brief, selective, or absent entirely.3

That information asymmetry matters because a substantial share of applications—about 37 per cent in the years 2018–22—received at least one CRL during the review cycle, meaning that opacity is not an edge case but a routine feature of the regulatory ecosystem.4 When FDA’s reasoning is not available, clinicians, researchers, other developers, patients, and investors must rely on sponsor accounts that may omit or soften key deficiencies. Empirical work has shown this pattern clearly: sponsor communications often capture only a small fraction of FDA-identified concerns, and serious issues such as mortality signals may go unmentioned.5 In a system in which regulatory decisions guide clinical practice, research and development strategy, and capital allocation, selective disclosure is a structural barrier to learning from failure.

In 2025, FDA took two steps impacting this landscape. On July 10, 2025, FDA posted more than 200 CRLs for drug and biologic applications submitted and approved between 2020 and 2024. Politically appointed FDA officials framed the step as ‘radical transparency’.6 Because many of these letters, or at least the information contained within them, were already accessible within approval action packages, the July release functioned mainly as a consolidation and searchability upgrade, lowering friction for those searching for that information but revealing relatively little that was genuinely new.7 A more consequential shift came on September 4, 2025, when FDA released 89 previously unpublished CRLs for pending or withdrawn applications and announced it would release newly issued CRLs promptly after issuance.8 If implemented consistently, this policy would, for the first time, make current and potentially terminal non-approvals visible rather than leaving them to sponsor discretion.

Early implementation suggests movement but also highlights why design details matter. The FDA has moved from promising CRL transparency to building a public-facing disclosure system. The openFDA CRL repository now describes the collection as covering CRLs associated with approved and unapproved NDAs and BLAs, and the public table is searchable by company, status, and year.9 The repository also includes post-announcement CRLs, including letters dated in 2026.10 At the same time, the system remains administratively and legally unsettled. FDA has not committed to a specific posting deadline, such as publication within 30 days of issuance, leaving the cadence and completeness of ‘prompt’ disclosure difficult to evaluate. The openFDA materials also contain mismatched coverage and update descriptions, including stale references to 2020–24 coverage even though later CRLs are publicly available. Most importantly, FDA’s current website asserts authority to publish redacted CRLs, while FDA’s fiscal-year 2027 budget justification asks Congress to provide explicit authority to disclose certain information in CRLs, including information about deficiencies in safety and efficacy data, for original new drug applications, original biologics license applications, and efficacy supplements.11 The discussion has therefore moved from whether CRLs should be disclosed to how FDA should stabilize and standardize its new disclosure practice.

Two questions follow. First, what distinct information does FDA-authenticated CRL disclosure provide that sponsor statements and other materials do not? Second, how can FDA make that disclosure timely, legally durable, and usable? This article identifies the distinct information value of the CRL as an agency-authenticated, deficiency-specific document, and uses the European Medicines Agency’s (EMA’s) more formalized system as a design comparator. We examine the informational value of the newly posted CRLs, including the extent to which redactions limit interpretability, and consider what additional steps would be needed to make the policy durable and administrable under existing legal constraints. Drawing on that comparative analysis, we propose a staged FDA disclosure pilot focused initially on high-salience original new drug and biologics license applications for unapproved products, with defined posting timelines, redaction standards, structured metadata, and neutral summaries when needed.

II. CRLS AND THEIR PUBLIC HEALTH SIGNIFICANCE

When a pharmaceutical company submits a new drug application or biologics license application, the FDA may determine after review that the drug cannot be approved in its present form. Instead of an outright rejection, the agency issues a CRL enumerating the deficiencies that preclude approval and often indicating what additional analysis, data, or changes are needed. CRLs frequently cite multiple concerns, such as inadequate evidence of efficacy, safety signals, manufacturing or quality problems, or the need for additional trials. A CRL is not an irrevocable denial: sponsors can address the cited issues and resubmit, sometimes over multiple review cycles.

Before FDA’s 2025 CRL releases, FDA’s confidentiality framework generally prevented public confirmation of unapproved applications and public disclosure of their contents. For drug applications, FDA regulations provided that the agency would not publicly disclose the existence of an NDA before approval unless the application had already been publicly disclosed or acknowledged; if the existence of an unapproved application had not been publicly disclosed or acknowledged, ‘no data or information’ in the application was available for public disclosure. Even when the application’s existence had been acknowledged before approval, the regulations generally made application data unavailable before approval, subject only to limited discretionary disclosure of summaries of selected safety and effectiveness data appropriate for public consideration of a specific pending issue, such as an open advisory committee.12 Parallel rules applied to biologics license applications. In practice, this meant that when an application generated a CRL, FDA generally did not confirm issuance unless the sponsor had already acknowledged the application, and the substance of the letter usually remained nonpublic before approval or another disclosure-triggering event.

As a basis for this stance, the FDA had cited statutory and regulatory nondisclosure obligations, including Freedom of Information Act (FOIA)’s Exemption 4, the Trade Secrets Act, FDCA § 301(j), and FDA’s regulations governing trade secrets and confidential commercial information.13 The onus of disclosure therefore fell largely on the sponsor: a company could state that it received a CRL or that its application was not approved, but any explanation of the FDA’s rationale ordinarily appeared only in sponsor communications. Many sponsors provided only terse summaries in press releases, or reported nothing at all, about why their drugs failed to pass FDA muster.14

When they did exist, sponsor statements became the primary public narrative around a CRL. Sponsors could portray the reasons for non-approval in the best possible light. In such situations, the FDA typically did not correct or clarify the record, apart from narrow exceptions (for example, to address false or misleading promotion or in materials prepared for an advisory committee). Evidence showed that this arrangement yielded systematically incomplete public information.15 A 2015 cross-sectional analysis comparing 61 CRLs (2008−13) with corresponding sponsor communications found that in 18 per cent of the cases, the company issued no press release about the FDA’s decision. When companies did make a public report, the vast majority of the FDA’s specific concerns were never mentioned. Press releases of sponsors matched only about 14 per cent of the factual statements contained in FDA letters. FDA letters frequently cited efficacy problems (67 per cent) and safety deficiencies (69 per cent), but sponsor announcements echoed only 16 per cent of the FDA’s efficacy concerns and 15 per cent of its safety concerns. In more than one-fifth of the cases, the sponsor’s communication did not overlap with any issues raised in the CRL. When FDA identified serious safety signals, including higher mortality in patients receiving the investigational drug, only one of seven corresponding sponsor statements mentioned the increased deaths. Sponsors were far more likely to state vaguely that ‘the FDA requested additional data’ than to report that a trial failed to demonstrate efficacy or raised a safety concern.

Such information gaps have practical consequences. Clinicians, patients, other developers, and investors make decisions in reliance on partial or selectively framed accounts of regulatory reasoning. Opacity around non-approvals also limits scientific learning from failed approaches, including avoidable trial design flaws, unanticipated safety signals, and recurring quality and manufacturing problems.16 Negative and intermediate results reduce the duplication of erroneous actions, potentially providing information for creating veritable roadmaps on how to navigate a complex terrain or finding new indications.17 Such information could also support future research on the development and evaluation of medical AI systems, although whether CRL data would improve model calibration or reduce bias remains to be established.

Sophisticated actors may draw adverse inferences from vague sponsor announcements, especially when a company discloses that FDA requested ‘additional data’ without elaboration. But adverse inference should not be a substitute for agency-authenticated reasoning. It does not reveal whether the issue involved lack of substantial evidence, a failed endpoint, a safety signal, manufacturing quality, facility inspection findings, assay validation, labeling, or statistical analysis, nor does it show how FDA framed the deficiency or what would be required to resolve it.

Recognizing these concerns, public health experts have long urged the FDA to shine more light on CRLs and non-approvals more generally.18 In 2010, the FDA’s Transparency Task Force recommended disclosing the existence and content of CRLs, arguing that informing the public when a drug is refused and why would yield public health benefits.19 The Task Force also noted that the EMA already publishes the reasons for denying marketing applications, and that US sponsors seeking EU authorization are subject to that regime. The proposal faced industry pushback, which has consistently viewed CRLs and underlying application data as confidential commercial information.20 Legal constraints also loomed large: disclosing CRLs would have required navigating FOIA Exemption 4 and FDA regulations providing that, for unapproved applications for which their existence had not been publicly disclosed or acknowledged, ‘no data or information’ in the application or biological product file was available for public disclosure; even when an application’s existence had been acknowledged, pre-approval disclosure was generally limited to selected summaries of safety and effectiveness data appropriate for public consideration of a specific pending issue.21

As a result, the 2010 recommendations were never implemented, even as criticisms of opacity in FDA decision-making continued.22

III. THE FDA’S 2025 TRANSPARENCY INITIATIVES

In two 2025 actions, the FDA broke from its past approach to CRLs and released the agency’s own account of non-approvals. The July 2025 release of about 200 CRLs for applications that were ultimately approved (2020−24) added limited new information, because most prior-cycle letters, or the information contained within them, had already been incorporated into approval packages published at the time of approval. As a practical matter, the release centralized dispersed documents and reduced the friction of locating CRLs embedded within lengthy approval materials, but it did not materially expand visibility into non-approvals that never reached the market.

Even this limited consolidation illustrates the informational value of CRLs. As shown in Table 1, recent CRL–announcement pairs display a consistent asymmetry: efficacy, safety, or manufacturing deficiencies identified by FDA are absent or softened in sponsor communications. The letters also make visible patterns across applications, including recurrent clinical and statistical issues. For example, a 2015 cross-sectional analysis found that nearly half of the 61 CRLs examined (29 of 61; 48 per cent) cited both efficacy and safety deficiencies.23 Other frequent issues included inadequate trial design, failure to meet endpoints, safety signals such as liver toxicity, and manufacturing shortfalls.24 Once public, these CRLs thus could function as a practical guide to common failure modes that sponsors, investors, and other developers can use to avoid repeating predictable errors.

New empirical work reinforces that CRLs are not marginal documents. Dilek and colleagues analyzed publicly available CRLs for 43 novel therapeutics ultimately approved by FDA between 2020 and 2024 and found that manufacturing-related deficiencies were most common, including facility deficiencies in 65 per cent and CMC deficiencies in 51 per cent of cases. Labeling deficiencies appeared in 44 per cent, efficacy and safety concerns each in 26 per cent, and new clinical trials were requested in 26 per cent of cases. These findings support treating the CRL corpus as a source of regulatory learning rather than as isolated corporate setbacks.25

However, the July batch was selective in that it covered only applications that ultimately succeeded. The class of decisions most valuable for public learning includes letters for applications that remain unapproved or were withdrawn, for which sponsor communications may be minimal and for which the public might otherwise never learn that FDA had important concerns. The September 2025 release of 89 previously unpublished CRLs for pending or withdrawn applications was therefore far more consequential. The tranche spans 58 new drug applications and 31 biologics license applications across 77 unique application numbers, including applications that were pending or withdrawn at the time of release.

In the September announcement, FDA also announced a purportedly forward-looking policy: all new CRLs will be made public ‘promptly’ after issuance, and when a drug is approved, any prior CRLs from earlier review cycles will be released as part of the approval package. Qualitatively, the September tranche of letters documented concrete and often correctable deficiencies, including inadequately validated assays, unresolved inspection findings, statistical analysis problems, and gaps in clinical evidence. The release also brought to light some cases when sponsor communications had been most lacking—for non-approvals that might never have been revealed. For example, if a company quietly withdrew its application after receiving a CRL, the public might otherwise never have learned of the CRL or FDA’s underlying concerns. FDA characterized the release as consistent with Executive Order 14303, which, subject to applicable law and protections for sensitive personal and confidential business information, directs agencies, to the extent practicable and within their authority, to release scientific and technological information that they reasonably assess will have a clear and substantial effect on important public policies or private-sector decisions.26

The FDA generally publishes individual letters without a case-specific agency-authored explanation, and some letters are heavily redacted. That pattern mirrors the redaction approach used in approval action packages and is, in part, the price of disclosure under current rules. Still, compared with sponsor press statements, CRLs can provide richer signals about what failed and why, which can reduce duplicative errors and sharpen future submissions. The categories of deficiencies themselves (eg whether the FDA’s concerns were clinical, safety-related, or manufacturing-related) is more insight than the public had before.

IV. HOW THE FDA NOW ALIGNS WITH THE EMA’S TRANSPARENCY REGIME

By contrast to the FDA, the European Union (EU) has built a routinized and proactive transparency architecture around new drug and biologic approvals. The EMA publishes decision rationales and supporting material through standardized public assessment reporting and trial transparency systems. The cumulative effect is a system designed for routine visibility into both outcomes and underlying evidence.

This creates structural differences in how the EMA treats marketing authorization application outcomes. The EMA publishes European Public Assessment Reports (EPARs) for medicines it evaluates whether the decision is positive or negative. If a drug is approved, the EMA releases an EPAR that includes the scientific assessment by its Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use (CHMP), explaining the evidence base, rationale for approval, and any conditions.27 If an application is refused, the EMA publishes a ‘refusal EPAR’ that summarizes the reasons for refusal and often includes the assessment report detailing why the drug’s benefit–risk profile was deemed unfavorable.28 Even if a sponsor withdraws an application before a formal refusal, the EMA usually publishes a public-facing Q&A or withdrawal assessment explaining, in general terms, the concerns that had been raised during review. There is no standardized FDA counterpart to an EMA refusal EPAR, and publicly released CRLs provide only a partial functional analog.

Policy instruments drive much of the EMA’s approach. Policy 004329 operationalizes EU access-to-document rules with a disclosure default: unless content is personal data or bona fide commercially confidential information (CCI), the content is generally releasable after the agency reaches a decision.30 Over the past decade, the EMA has released substantial volumes of review material and supporting files on request, and EU courts have upheld the agency’s approach to clinical data disclosure. As a result, there is no broad confidentiality blanket over clinical data and companies must show concrete harm to prevent release.31

Policy 007032 extends beyond case by case access requests to proactive publication of clinical study reports and related materials once a centrally authorized product reaches decision.33 Following its 2023 relaunch, the EMA clarified publication pathways for new active substances reaching a CHMP opinion and, in this phase, for applications withdrawn before opinion, bringing some negative or abandoned applications into public view. Europe’s approach therefore goes beyond explaining decisions to increasing visibility into underlying data for drugs that never reach the market. The EMA has coupled this with measures to protect genuinely confidential information. Companies may propose redactions for certain parts of reports, but EMAs default position is that very little data in a clinical study report qualifies as confidential once a drug’s development is complete. In its COVID-19 publication experience, EMA reported that only 21 pages—0.01 per cent of the pages published—contained any accepted CCI redactions.34

FDA and EMA also operationalize confidentiality differently. FDA rules separately protect trade secrets and confidential commercial or financial information; FDA’s regulation defines confidential commercial or financial information as valuable business information customarily held in strict confidence and not disclosed to the public.35 EMA’s Policy 0070 guidance uses CCI as a functional category: non-public information in clinical reports for which the disclosure may undermine the applicant’s legitimate economic interests. EMA also states that most clinical information in clinical reports falling within Policy 0070 should not be considered CCI and requires specific justification for proposed redactions. Thus, the systems are not identical. FDA’s approach is tied to FOIA, the Trade Secrets Act, FDCA restrictions, and FDA disclosure regulations; EMA’s is embedded in a proactive publication process with structured justification and agency review.

The Clinical Trials Information System (CTIS) extends transparency to the trial level. Under the Clinical Trials Regulation (EU) No 536/2014, CTIS requires public registration and disclosure of specified trial documents and results on defined timelines, with limited redactions when justified.36 The result is a predictable cadence of disclosure spanning registration through assessment, enabling independent scrutiny across the product life cycle. Europe’s experience suggests the feasibility of proactive transparency when coupled with principled redaction.37

Recent EMA materials reinforce the comparative point that EMA’s disclosure infrastructure is more proceduralized than FDA’s emerging CRL regime. EMA’s CTIS training materials state that clinical study reports must be submitted within 30 days after the relevant marketing-authorization decision, completion of the authorization procedure, or withdrawal of the application, and that the submitted information is made public on submission; users may also upload a non-public version when necessary to protect personal data. At the same time, EMA transparency is not absolute. In SD v EMA, the General Court upheld the Agency’s refusal of access to detailed technical information on the COVID-19 vaccine Comirnaty, emphasizing that the remaining redactions concerned highly technical data derived from vaccine manufacturer BioNTech’s know-how and that disclosure could give competitors concrete advantages.38

Nevertheless, recent evidence cautions against treating CTIS architecture as self-executing. A 2026 cross-sectional preprint examining phase II−IV drug trials registered in CTIS found that, among the first 234 trials legally required to report results, only 116 reported results on time, 20 reported results late, and 98 failed to make substantive results public.39 The study concluded that sponsor compliance with CTIS reporting requirements was weak and that current regulator monitoring and enforcement appeared insufficient. This reinforces the point that structured transparency rules require monitoring, quality control, and enforcement. EMA and national competent authorities should therefore tighten compliance checks and use available penalties when sponsors fail to submit usable results on time.

V. CASE EXAMPLE: THE TRAZIMERA (TRASTUZUMAB-QYYP) APPLICATION

Though they have distinct advantages, EMA materials cannot substitute for FDA CRLs. The two agencies may review different indications, data packages, facilities, manufacturing arrangements, endpoints, labels, and legal standards, and they may act at different times. Even for the same development program, an FDA CRL records US-specific review history and evidentiary objections, including whether FDA viewed a deficiency as previously communicated to the sponsor.

One case that illustrates the differences between FDA and EMA transparency concerns Pfizer’s Trazimera (trastuzumab-qyyp), a trastuzumab biosimilar reviewed by FDA in 2018. FDA issued a CRL that year, which at the time was kept confidential. Pfizer - the sponsor - said that FDA had requested ‘additional technical information,’ that the request did not concern safety or clinical data submitted in the application, and that it was “working closely with the FDA to address the contents of the letter.40 After approving Trazimera on March 11, 2019, FDA published the redacted CRL in the 2019 approval action package. Its later inclusion in openFDA’s centralized CRL repository improved discoverability and accessibility but did not make the letter public for the first time.41 In Europe, EMA’s CHMP adopted a positive opinion on May 31, 2018; EMA published the opinion summary on June 1 and the full assessment report on August 16, after EU authorization on July 26.42 This is not a like-for-like comparison of two non-approvals, because EMA approved the product while FDA initially did not. Even so, the documents illustrate a timing difference between FDA’s approval-linked retrospective disclosure and EMA’s structured publication around the regulatory decision. Going forward, such a gap should be avoided because FDA has pledged ‘prompt’ posting of new CRLs but has not committed to a specific deadline, such as 30 days after issuance.

Beyond timing, the regimes differ in how much information is revealed and what is held back as confidential. FDA’s CRLs are constrained by US laws, including FOIA Exemption 4 and the Trade Secrets Act. In the published Trazimera CRL, (b)(4) redactions conceal some proprietary values and process details. The visible text nevertheless identifies line-item product-quality deficiencies concerning cell-bank stability, fill-weight controls, shipping validation, potency and other assays, specification limits, storage conditions, sterility assurance, and microbiological controls. The document may not disclose all supporting evidence or proprietary particulars. Its tone is regulatory and technical because it was written to the sponsor, which can make it challenging to interpret without regulatory expertise.

EMA assessment reports are more narrative and synthesized. They describe a medicine’s quality, efficacy, and safety in a structured way, summarizing the agency’s interpretation and decision rationale. The Trazimera report states that CCI was deleted, but it presents a continuous public assessment of product quality, comparability, clinical evidence, and benefit–risk. EMA policies also generally treat clinical information in clinical reports as non-confidential unless a proposed CCI redaction is specifically justified.

These differences affect interpretability. FDA CRLs, even when redacted, can provide ‘forensic’ detail by itemizing deficiencies in technical terms and referencing specific data or sections of the application. This can be useful for experts seeking to understand exactly what FDA found lacking. EMA reports provide a curated summary of the agency’s assessment rather than reproducing each issue raised during review. In the Trazimera case, FDA’s published CRL identifies line-item product-quality deficiencies, whereas EMA’s 2018 report discusses the product’s quality and comparability in a synthesized framework and explains why the evidence supported authorization.

Thus, FDA’s CRL is raw and granular but redacted, whereas EMA’s report is polished and structured. By ‘raw,’ we mean that CRLs reproduce FDA’s operative deficiency language directed to the sponsor, rather than a public-facing assessment drafted for a general audience. That rawness is a strength for sophisticated users, including regulators, regulatory counsel, competitors, investors, and researchers studying review standards, because it preserves FDA’s categories, phrasing, and level of concern. It is a weakness for patients, clinicians, and non-specialist policymakers who may need context to understand the practical significance of a deficiency. The neutral summary proposed below is therefore not meant to convert CRLs into EMA-style assessment reports. It is a usability layer for cases when redactions or technical language would otherwise obscure the main basis for non-approval. Each has advantages: FDA letters provide insight into regulatory reasoning and wording, and EMA reports provide context and rationale in a more reader-friendly form. Accessibility also diverges. FDA’s CRL database is a major improvement in availability, but the letters are not consistently structured for aggregation and analysis (for instance, one cannot easily filter the database by therapeutic area or type of deficiency). Public companies may have securities-law incentives or obligations to disclose material CRLs, but sponsor disclosure is uneven and does not substitute for a standardized agency source that provides FDA-authenticated reasoning across public companies, private companies, foreign issuers, and abandoned or withdrawn applications. EMA materials are embedded in a more standardized publication and search environment, which allows users to search by medicine name and within documents. The CTIS separately makes trial-level information publicly accessible in a structured database. In practice, EMA’s standardized product pages make its assessment materials easier to navigate at the product level, while openFDA centralizes letter text and basic metadata but lacks fields for therapeutic area, deficiency category, review-cycle number, and posting date. The systems serve different functions: EMA product pages concern product assessment, CTIS concerns clinical trials, and openFDA centralizes decision letters. Making sense of the underlying documents still requires regulatory expertise.

VI. DISCUSSION

The FDA’s embrace of greater transparency may signal a recognition that hiding non-approvals was detrimental to public trust and scientific learning. However, making this change lasting and optimally useful will depend on implementation details. Delays and heavy redactions can blunt learning, and the policy’s durability is uncertain without clearer operational commitments. To consolidate these gains—and to approach the level of transparency that EMA’s system achieves—we recommend two further actionable steps for the FDA.

First, FDA should specify a disclosure timeline and redaction standards. FDA should commit, through guidance or regulation, to posting CRLs within a defined period after issuance. This would remove ambiguity and ensure ‘real-time’ means ‘timely.’ In tandem, the FDA should establish uniform redaction standards that track existing legal standards for trade secrets and confidential commercial information, with the goal of preserving the interpretability of deficiency descriptions. When redactions would leave the CRL functionally unreadable, FDA should provide a short, neutral summary stating the main reasons approval was not possible.

The FDA should also add structured metadata to the CRL repository. At minimum, each entry should identify application number, sponsor, product, center, therapeutic area, CRL date, posting date, review cycle, product status, deficiency categories, and whether the CRL is associated with an approved, pending, withdrawn, or abandoned application. The public openFDA table is already searchable by company name, status, and year, which is a meaningful improvement over scattered documents. But the application programming interface (API) page still describes coverage and updates in broad terms and does not provide the posting-date and update-log infrastructure needed for researchers to assess timeliness and completeness.

Operationally, FDA should use a bounded submitter-notice process,43 but the sponsor should bear the burden of identifying proposed redactions with page-and-line specificity and a short justification tied to trade secret, CCI, or personal-data protection. FDA should retain final decision-making authority. To reduce burden on review divisions, the process should be handled by a centralized FDA disclosure team, with review staff consulted only when technical context is needed to evaluate whether a proposed redaction would impair the public meaning of the deficiency. Notice should be framed as an administrative safeguard, not as a litigation-risk cure. A short notice-and-objection period would give sponsors a defined opportunity to identify CCI or trade secrets, create an administrative record for FDA’s redaction decisions, and reduce procedural objections to surprise disclosure. It would not prevent sponsors from challenging either the policy or particular redaction decisions.

Second, FDA should institutionalize retrospective transparency for approvals. Whenever the FDA approves a drug that had previous review cycles, all prior CRLs should be included in the public approval action package by default. Because FDA has often included prior-cycle CRLs in approval packages for approved products, the remaining problem is less the total absence of retrospective disclosure than inconsistency, discoverability, and lack of standardized classification. FDA should standardize the practice by requiring, for every approved product with a prior CRL, a cycle-history table that identifies the CRL date, public posting date, review cycle, principal deficiency categories, and whether the deficiency was resolved through new data, analysis, inspection remediation, labeling, or other changes. This would preserve the value of the July 2025 consolidation while making the approval record easier to search and compare. Along with the letters themselves, the FDA should add a brief FDA-authored summary table or cover note that categorizes the main deficiencies from each review cycle, for example, ‘Cycle 1— [principal deficiency category and disposition].’ This provides immediate context to readers and ensures that someone looking at the approval documents can clearly see the road the product took. It also helps researchers compile statistics on why drugs needed multiple cycles. By standardizing this practice, the FDA’s eventual approvals will come with a complete history of the application’s journey, rather than burying past issues. Not only does this fulfill the FDA’s own commitment (to release prior CRLs upon approval), but it would also align more closely with EMA practice, under which assessment documentation is published for refused and withdrawn applications as well as for authorized products, allowing comparisons across regulatory outcomes where the relevant records are available.

This sequencing targets core implementation challenges. A notice-and-objection process would create a procedural record, giving sponsors a defined opportunity to identify genuine confidentiality interests, and helping FDA tie redaction decisions to the trade secret and confidential commercial information standards it already applies.44 Workload is bounded by scoping the pilot to a small, well-defined subset of applications and by using templates for summaries and metadata, much as FDA does for advisory-committee dockets.45 Former leadership has flagged bandwidth—not industry resistance—as a key barrier to CRL disclosure.46 A pilot that focuses on a manageable subset of high-salience applications, uses templates, and reuses advisory-committee redaction checklists keeps the lift realistic and testable. Commercial harm is limited by redacting specific process know-how, proprietary analytical methods, and similar confidential commercial information while disclosing the high-level decisional rationale that clinicians, patients, and researchers need. The pilot should be limited at first to a defined subset of high-salience original NDAs and BLAs for unapproved products, prioritizing cases involving unmet medical need, accelerated approval, surrogate endpoints, single-arm or externally controlled trials, major safety concerns, or substantial public reliance on sponsor statements. FDA could expand the pilot after assessing posting timelines, redaction burden, sponsor objections, and user demand.

This staged approach is narrower than some recent transparency proposals. Ulrich and Lurie argue that FDA should eventually disclose marketing-denial communications across product types and should more openly acknowledge filings of applications for new and investigational products. Though these would also be positive steps, the proposal here starts from a more administrable premise: FDA should first stabilize the disclosure practice it has already begun, namely redacted CRL publication for drugs and biologics, before extending the model to adjacent forms of non-approval or non-filing communication.47

Transparency is not at odds with innovation or competitiveness if managed properly. The biopharmaceutical industry often worries that too much disclosure could expose proprietary strategies or deter investment. EMA’s experience suggests that a balance can be struck between extensive transparency obligations and protection of legitimate commercial interests. FDA’s use of (b)(4) redactions in published CRLs likewise shows that the disclosure initiative continues to protect information treated as confidential commercial information. In truth, the policy choice here is not a binary ‘secrecy versus innovation’ trade-off; it is about design - what information is protected versus what is shared, and when. By protecting proprietary elements, such as manufacturing trade secrets, but disclosing outcomes and non-confidential scientific reasoning, FDA can enhance public trust. Transparency and confidentiality can coexist with smart policy design.

VII. CONCLUSION

The FDA’s 2025 moves toward transparency, if sustained, help align US practice with the public interest in agency-authenticated explanations of non-approval. The July release primarily repackaged already-public documents, serving as a proof of concept that the FDA can publish decision letters en masse. The September initiative brought previously unpublished letters for pending or withdrawn applications into the open and committed the agency to prompt prospective publication. This begins to narrow the gap between FDA disclosure and the more routine publication of assessment materials at the EMA.

Still, as our analysis shows, there is room to make the FDA’s transparency more timely, interpretable, and user-friendly. By codifying clear timelines and redaction rules, and by institutionalizing the inclusion of prior CRLs in future approval packages, the FDA can turn what is now an episodic burst of transparency into a consistent, permanent feature of its regulatory process. In doing so, the agency would not only match the EMA in principle—acknowledging negative decisions – but could even exceed it in certain ways, by providing a rich database of ‘lessons learned’ from past applications. The EMA, for its part, should preserve and improve linkages between refusal and withdrawal documents, EPARs, CTIS records, and clinical data publications, and should ensure that public summaries retain enough technical specificity to allow expert users to understand the key evidentiary deficiencies. Recent CTIS reporting problems also suggest that the EMA and national competent authorities should monitor results-reporting compliance more actively and use enforcement tools, including penalties where available, when sponsors fail to make usable results public on time. The end goal is a smarter, more informed ecosystem where successes and failures alike contribute to biomedical innovation. When non-approvals see sunlight, patients, clinicians, scientists, sponsors, and regulators all benefit from the illumination.
