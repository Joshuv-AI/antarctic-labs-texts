Differences in admission to trauma centres by sex among adults with traumatic brain injury: a population-based cohort study
Authors: Natalia Alejandra Angeloni, Areti-Angeliki Veroniki, Federico Angriman, Damon C Scales, Neill K J Adhikari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Female patients face disparities in access to specialized health care, but whether similar disparities exist in access to trauma centres after traumatic brain injury (TBI) is unclear. We sought to examine the association between sex and admission to a lead trauma centre (a hospital designated as a level I or II trauma centre) among adults with TBI.Methods:We conducted a population-based cohort study using provincial health administrative databases from Ontario, Canada. We included adults (aged ≥ 18 yr) admitted to hospital with TBI between April 2009 and March 2020. Our main exposure was sex. The primary outcome was admission to a lead trauma centre. We used multivariable logistic regression, adjusting for age, Injury Severity Score, comorbidities, rurality, and income quintile. We reported odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We estimated marginal risk differences using standardization. Sensitivity analyses included quantitative bias analysis of outcome misclassification.Results:Among 55 606 patients (39.0% female), 26.1% of females and 38.3% of males were admitted to a lead trauma centre. In the overall cohort, female patients were older, had more comorbidities, and were less likely to have severe injuries than male patients. After adjustment, females had significantly lower odds of admission to a lead trauma centre (OR 0.74, 95% CI 0.71 to 0.78), corresponding to an adjusted marginal risk difference of −4.7% (95% CI −5.4% to −4.0%). Findings were consistent across sensitivity analyses.Interpretation:Female patients admitted to hospital with TBI were less likely than male patients to be admitted to specialized trauma centres, independent of clinical and socioeconomic factors. These results suggest inequities and underscore the need for system-level strategies to ensure equitable triage and transfer practices.

Access to health care is fraught with disparities by sex, with female patients facing disadvantages in accessing care for traumatic injury, myocardial infarction, kidney transplant, and critical illness.1–4 Access to specialized care for traumatic brain injury (TBI), the leading cause of trauma-related death and disability worldwide,5 may be similarly affected by such disparities.

Several factors may explain why patient sex affects access to health care. The historic underrepresentation of females in research studies has contributed to underappreciation of female-specific signs and symptoms,6–8 potentially leading to missed or delayed diagnosis.9,10 Sex-related bias — the negative evaluation of one sex group relative to the other — can influence clinical decisions. These biases are shaped by stereotypical appraisals of gender,11 where gender refers to the socially constructed roles, behaviours, expressions, and identities of individuals. 12 Such appraisals can affect how the severity and urgency of injuries are perceived, influencing triage and subsequent care, particularly in the context of sex-based differences in TBI epidemiology and injury mechanisms.13–15

Specialized care improves outcomes for patients with moderate-to-severe traumatic injuries.16,17 Evidence-based triage and transfer guidelines recommend referral to trauma centres based on physiologic status and injury severity;18 however, non-clinical factors also influence transfer decisions.19,20 A population-based study in Ontario, Canada, demonstrated that between 2002 and 2010, severely injured women had significantly lower odds of receiving care at a trauma centre than men, even after accounting for age, comorbidity, mechanism of injury, and severity of injury.21 Whether similar inequities exist among a more restricted population, namely, patients with TBI, is unknown. Additionally, although sex-specific differences in TBI and general trauma populations have been described, existing evidence and reviews have primarily examined predisposition, pathophysiology, and outcomes, with limited attention to access to care.10,22,23 We aimed to estimate the association between sex and the likelihood of transfer to a lead trauma centre among patients with TBI.

Methods

Setting

We conducted a population-based cohort study of adult patients with TBI in Ontario, Canada. Canadian citizens, permanent residents, convention refugees, and certain categories of temporary residents (with work or study permits) who make Ontario their primary place of residence are eligible for the Ontario Health Insurance Plan (OHIP). Coverage provides publicly funded access to medically necessary hospital and physician services. Individuals without OHIP coverage — such as visitors, tourists, and some recent arrivals — are not captured in Ontario’s population-based administrative health databases and were therefore excluded from this study.24,25

Data source and cohort creation

We created the study cohort using population-based provincial health administrative databases housed at ICES, an independent, nonprofit research institute. The specific data holdings used in this study are listed in Appendix 1, Table S1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.251721/tab-related-content. The data sets were linked using unique encoded identifiers and analyzed at ICES. The reporting of this study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement and the Reporting of Studies Conducted Using Observational Routinely Collected Health Data (RECORD) extension (Appendix 1, Section 3).26,27

We included adult patients (aged ≥ 18 yr) with a first admission for TBI (either as the primary diagnosis or in the context of multi-system trauma) in Ontario between April 2009 and March 2020. We required admission to last 2 calendar days or more to identify patients for whom decisions about admission to a trauma centre or interfacility transfer would reasonably be made, excluding patients with brief observational stays and early deaths. Patients admitted to a lead trauma centre directly or fewer than 2 days after initial hospitalization were included in the cohort if the total hospital stay was 2 days or longer. We limited the study period to years before the COVID-19 pandemic because pandemic-related system reorganization affected processes of trauma triage and admission.28–30 We identified TBI using previously used codes from the Canadian version of the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (Appendix 1, Table S2).31–34

Variables

Sex was the main exposure, which we obtained from the Registered Persons Database (RPDB).35 The RPDB captures self-reported sex at the time of health card registration, recorded as a binary variable (female or male) for administrative purposes. This variable reflects only legal or administrative sex and does not capture gender identity or gender expression, which encompasses socially constructed attributes, expectations, and experiences that may influence injury patterns, health-seeking behaviour, triage decisions, and clinical care.12 Because gender is not available in ICES data, our analyses were restricted to differences by sex.

The main outcome was admission to a lead trauma centre at any point during the TBI admission, defined as having a record in the Ontario Trauma Registry.36 In Ontario, a lead trauma centre is a hospital designated as a level I or II trauma centre that meets provincial standards.37

We recorded baseline characteristics, including age, income quintile, rurality (based on postal code), previous hospitalizations, Charlson Comorbidity Index, and comorbidities.38–44 We captured the Injury Severity Score (ISS) and Abbreviated Injury Score of the head region (AIS-head) using a validated algorithm. 45,46 To characterize the hospital stay, we recorded intensive care unit (ICU) admission, in-hospital death, and in-hospital events (i.e., sepsis, acute kidney injury, respiratory failure, and gastrointestinal bleed) (Appendix 1, Table S2).47,48

Statistical analysis

We summarized baseline and hospital characteristics by sex. We reported counts and proportions for categorical variables and medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables. We compared groups using absolute standardized mean differences, with values higher than 10% considered important.49,50

To evaluate the association between sex and admission to a lead trauma centre, we conducted multivariable logistic regression analysis, adjusted for clinically relevant variables, namely age, income, rural residence, ISS, and baseline comorbidities (Appendix 1, Figure S1). From this model, we derived adjusted predicted probabilities of admission to a lead trauma centre and plotted them across age. Age was modelled linearly after exploring potential nonlinearity using restricted cubic splines, which did not materially improve model fit.

We conducted a series of clinically relevant subgroup analyses by severity of head injury (AIS–head ≥ 3, the standard definition of severe head injury, v. < 3);51 by overall injury severity (ISS > 15, the conventional threshold for major trauma, v. < 15);51 by age (≥ 65 v. < 65 yr), consistent with commonly used definitions of geriatric trauma;21,52,53 and by place of residence (rural v. nonrural), given evidence of differential access to trauma centre care by rurality.20,54 We also explored a subgroup of patients with comorbid dementia because of dementia’s clinical relevance and substantial baseline differences between male and female patients.

To address differences in baseline covariates between male and female patients, we conducted 2 sensitivity analyses adjusting the modelling strategy. First, we conducted a logistic regression analysis in a 1-to-1 female-to-male propensity score–matched cohort.55,56 The second sensitivity analysis involved standardization using the plugin g-formula, with estimation of marginal odds ratios (ORs), marginal risks, and absolute risk differences (RDs) (Appendix 1, Section 2). 57 Since awareness of sex and gender considerations may have evolved over the study period, we conducted a post hoc sensitivity analysis comparing early (2009 to 2014) and later (2015 to 2020) periods by including a sex-by-period interaction term. We also conducted a quantitative bias analysis to account for potential outcome misclassification (i.e., false positives in the outcome definition), as some patients may have been entered into the Ontario Trauma Registry for procedures (e.g., interventional radiology, magnetic resonance imaging), without necessarily being admitted to a lead trauma centre (Appendix 1, Section 2).58

We reported ORs and RDs with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We used a complete-case analysis because there were no missing data points on the main exposure or outcome, and missing covariate data were less than 1%. We used a threshold of 0.05 for statistical significance. We performed all analyses using SAS Enterprise Guide version 7.1.

Ethics approval

ICES’ legal status under Ontario’s health information privacy law allows it to collect and analyze health care and demographic data, without patient consent, for health system evaluation and improvement. The use of data in this project was authorized under section 45 of Ontario’s Personal Health Information Protection Act, which does not require review by a research ethics board.

Results

A total of 55 606 patients were admitted to hospital for TBI in Ontario between April 2009 and March 2020. Of these, 21 719 (39.0%) were female (Figure 1). Overall, 18 650 patients were admitted to a lead trauma centre including 5666 (26.1%) of the female patients and 12 984 (38.3%) of the male patients. Table 1 presents patients’ baseline characteristics. Female patients were older (median age 78 [IQR 63 to 86] yr) than male patients (median age 67 [IQR 48 to 81] yr; standardized difference 0.49) and were more likely to have a comorbid condition (84.6% v. 73.8%; standardized difference 0.27), including dementia (20.4% v. 11.8%; standardized difference 0.23) and hypertension (68.8% v. 54.8%; standardized difference 0.29). Female patients were more likely to develop sepsis during admission (19.3% v. 14.8%; standardized difference 0.12). Male patients were more likely to have severe trauma (ISS > 15) (32.6% v. 24.8%; standardized difference 0.17) and to be admitted to an ICU during their hospital stay (46.0% v. 32.7%; standardized difference 0.28). We included 55 271 patients in the complete-case adjusted analysis. Female patients had lower odds of being admitted to a lead trauma centre (OR 0.74, 95% CI 0.71 to 0.78) (Figure 2 and Appendix 1, Table S3).

In subgroup analyses, the association between female sex and lower odds of admission to a lead trauma centre remained consistent across age groups (age < 65 v. ≥ 65 yr), injury severity (AIS-head ≥ 3 v. < 3, and ISS > 15 v. < 15), rural versus urban residence, and dementia status. In older patients and those with lower severity of head injury, the association between sex and likelihood of admission to a lead trauma centre was more pronounced (Figure 3). We did not observe a significant interaction between female sex and rural residence or dementia status (Figure 3).

The 1-to-1 propensity score matching procedure yielded 21 113 unique matched pairs, with all standardized covariate mean differences lower than 10% (Appendix 1, Table S4). The results from the matched logistic regression and the standardization with the g-formula were similar in direction and magnitude to those of the primary analysis, including for subgroup-specific adjusted RDs (Appendix 1, Tables S5 and S6). The adjusted marginal RD of admission to a lead trauma centre for female patients was −4.7% (95% CI −5.4% to −4.0%). We observed no evidence of differences in association between sex and admission to a lead trauma centre by study period (interaction p = 0.3). The quantitative bias analysis showed a consistent association between female sex and admission to a lead trauma centre across all levels of outcome misclassification (Figure 4).

Interpretation

In this population-based cohort study of adult patients admitted to hospital for TBI in Ontario, we found female patients were significantly less likely than male patients to be admitted to a lead trauma centre, after we accounted for injury severity, comorbidities, and socioeconomic characteristics. Several factors may explain these disparities. First, injuries in female patients are more often associated with lower-energy mechanisms, such as ground-level falls, that may attract less attention13,14 and may lead to lower prehospital priority.52 In our cohort, mechanism of trauma was systematically captured only for patients admitted to a lead trauma centre (i.e., those with an Ontario Trauma Registry record), which does not reflect the broader population of patients with trauma. Second, unconscious (implicit) sex-related bias may contribute to differential recognition of severity of injury. This concept is supported by simulation studies showing that identical volumes of bleeding are often underestimated in female patients and overestimated in males,59,60 and similar evidence regarding cardiopulmonary resuscitation from bystanders. 61 Third, the underrepresentation of female patients with trauma and TBI in research studies has resulted in a limited understanding of their presentation and outcomes.10 As a result, triage and transfer guidelines may not fully capture how TBI manifests in female patients, potentially leading to missed or delayed recognition of injury severity. Finally, transfers may be influenced by patient or family preferences and goals of care. For instance, families may decline transfer or a patient’s advance directive may limit escalation.62 If such preferences or decisions differ systematically by patient sex, these unmeasured factors could contribute to the observed differences.

Our research builds on a 2012 study that first identified sex-based disparities in access to trauma centres in Ontario among patients with severe trauma,21 with females less likely to receive trauma centre care when the main injured region was the head or lower extremity. By examining a more recent cohort and focusing on TBI, our study shows that these disparities have persisted over time and appear to be relevant across different clinical conditions. We also found disparities across all age and injury severity strata. Together, these studies provide complementary evidence that female patients experience inequities in access to trauma care, strengthening the case for system-level strategies to ensure equitable triage and transfer practices.

Similarly, our findings align with a recent study of severely injured geriatric patients, which reported lower rates of trauma system activation among female patients, despite comparable injury severity.63 In that study, female patients were older, more likely to sustain head and neck injuries, and more likely to present with apparently stable physiologic features (e.g., alert mental status, higher systolic blood pressure), characteristics that have also been associated with undertriage in other trauma populations. 52 In contrast, a cohort study from Europe reported no statistically significant differences by sex in trauma centre or ICU admission for patients with moderate-to-severe TBI but was limited by its grouping of moderate and severe TBI and may have been underpowered to detect differences within severity strata.64 In our population-based cohort, female patients across the full spectrum of injury severity were less likely to be admitted to a lead trauma centre. Our findings reinforce the plausibility of systematic underestimation of severity of injury among female patients in the context of lower-energy mechanisms of trauma and initially more stable physiologic presentations.

Future research should investigate the mechanisms underlying sex-based disparities in trauma care more directly, focusing on how triage and interfacility transfer decisions are made. In Ontario, triage performance is suboptimal,65 with high rates of both overtriage and undertriage, suggesting variability in decision-making, even when standardized guidelines are in place. Understanding how this variability interacts with sex and gender is critical. The role, if any, of conscious and unconscious bias in clinical decision-making in care of patients with TBI should be explored, as has been done for other clinical conditions;66,67 results should guide targeted interventions to reduce the disparities we have identified.

Limitations

Sex is a nonmodifiable and nonrandomizable exposure, and the observed associations may be influenced by unmeasured group differences, despite adjustment for a broad range of clinical and sociodemographic variables. These differences include baseline functional status and social factors such as living situation or availability of a caregiver or next of kin, which were not captured in administrative data. Although we adjusted for dementia, rurality, and neighbourhood income quintile as proxies for cognitive and social context, these measures do not fully capture functional independence or social support and may influence triage or transfer decisions if they differ systematically by patient sex. We were also limited to self-reported or phenotypical sex as recorded in administrative data, which included only binary categories (male or female) and were unable to account for gender identity or other gender-related constructs. We lacked access to a validated measure of functional TBI severity, such as the Glasgow Coma Scale, which is available only for patients recorded in the Ontario Trauma Registry. However, our stratified analysis comparing patients with high versus low scores on the AIS-head, an anatomic scale of TBI severity, yielded consistent results. In addition, TBI was identified using administrative diagnostic codes that have not been formally validated in this population, and some degree of misclassification is possible. Detailed information on patterns and mechanisms of injury was systematically available only for patients admitted to a lead trauma centre, limiting our ability to include these factors in the main analysis or to perform stratified analyses by injury type. We also lacked data on withdrawal of life support, advance directives, decisions to decline transfer, and patients’ ability to participate in those decisions. All these unmeasured factors may be relevant if they vary by patient sex. In addition, restricting the cohort to patients hospitalized for at least 2 calendar days excluded patients who were rapidly discharged or died early, and our findings may not generalize to the full spectrum of TBI presentations. Injury severity was measured via administrative codes using standardized algorithms, which may not fully capture nuanced clinical severity. Our study period did not include the most recent years; however, we found no evidence that our findings varied over the study period, and similar findings have been reported in Ontario-based studies using data collected a decade earlier,21 suggesting temporal stability in sex-based differences in admission to lead trauma centres. Ongoing analyses using contemporary data will be needed to determine whether these patterns changed after the COVID-19 pandemic. Finally, we focused on differences in access to a lead trauma centre rather than in-hospital mortality or functional outcomes.

Conclusion

Female patients with TBI in Ontario were significantly less likely than male patients to be admitted to a lead trauma centre, after we accounted for differences in sociodemographic factors and clinical severity. Our findings underscore the need to examine how sex-related factors influence triage and transfer decisions in trauma systems and support the call for more equitable policies in acute care delivery.
