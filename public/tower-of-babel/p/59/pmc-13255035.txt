Zinc oxide quantum dots as an alternative to conventional ZnO: Physicochemical characterization and its application in weaned piglets
Authors: Chan Liang, Xintao Wang, Daiwen Chen, Gang Tian, Jun He, Ping Zheng, Jie Yu, Junning Pu, Wendan Zhou, Bing Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-dose zinc oxide (ZnO) is effective in preventing diarrhea in piglets, but its use is limited by several concerns, including impaired intestinal barrier function, increased bacterial resistance, and the risks of heavy metal residues and environmental pollution. This study aimed to investigate whether zinc oxide quantum dots (ZnO QDs), a novel nanomaterial with diameters of less than 10 nm, could serve as an alternative to ZnO. In vitro, ZnO QDs were used to characterize the structure and evaluate antibacterial activity. In vivo, a total of 150 piglets (24 ± 1 d old, 7.45 ± 0.41 kg weight) were randomly divided into five groups: negative control group (CON), positive control group (ZnO; 1500 mg/kg Zn as ZnO), and 3 levels of ZnO QDs groups (250, 500, or 750 mg/kg Zn as ZnO QDs). Each group consisted of 10 replicates of 3 piglets for a 28-d trial. Results showed that ZnO QDs (size = 3.82 ± 0.38 nm, specific surface area = 17.65 m2/g) exhibited superior antibacterial activity in vitro due to their small size, large surface area, and high Zn release when compared to ZnO (size = 239.48 ± 78.02 nm, specific surface area = 0.91 m2/g). In vivo, the ZnO, ZnO QDs 250 and ZnO QDs 500 groups improved average daily gain (ADG; P = 0.003), while only the ZnO QDs 500 group decreased the feed to gain ratio (F/G; P = 0.038) compared with the CON group. Supplementation with ZnO QDs at 250 to 750 mg/kg also reduced diarrhea incidence in piglets (P < 0.05), with effects comparable to those observed with 1500 mg/kg ZnO. Dietary ZnO QDs supplementation mitigated Zn emissions and prevented hepatic Zn accumulation compared with high-dose ZnO (P < 0.001). Moreover, both ZnO QDs and ZnO improved the apparent digestibility of dry matter, organic matter, crude protein, and ether extract, enhanced digestive enzyme activities, and upregulated SGLT1 mRNA expression in the jejunal mucosa (P < 0.05). In addition, supplementation of 250 and 500 mg/kg ZnO QDs promoted jejunal development, and 500 mg/kg ZnO QDs increased microbial α-diversity indices and enriched beneficial bacteria such as norank_f_Muribaculaceae in the colon (P < 0.05). Conversely, high-dose ZnO hindered intestinal development and decreased Lactobacillus abundance and increased Escherichia-Shigella and Treponema abundances (P < 0.05), all of which are detrimental to the intestinal health of piglets. In conclusion, dietary ZnO QDs at 250 and 500 mg/kg effectively promoted growth, prevented diarrhea, reduced Zn emissions, improved nutrient utilization, and regulated gut microbiota, suggesting their suitability as an alternative to high-dose ZnO. This study provides a novel approach to prevent diarrhea in piglets.Keywords: Zinc oxide quantum dot, Antibacterial activity, Piglet, Diarrhea, Zinc metabolism, Intestinal microbiota

Introduction

Early weaning is widely adopted to maximize sow productivity (Tang et al., 2024b), but it predisposes piglets to multisystem stresses, including psychological, environmental, and managerial challenges (Han et al., 2024), which often result in post-weaning diarrhea (PWD) (Zhou et al., 2024). Particularly, PWD within the first two weeks after weaning, is the primary inducement to piglet morbidity and mortality (Larsen et al., 2023), imposing substantial economic losses on the global swine industry (Hetényi, 2023; Tang et al., 2024a). In recent decades, pharmacological doses (2000–3000 mg/kg) of zinc oxide (ZnO) have been widely recognized as an effective strategy to prevent PWD and promote growth in weaned piglets (Grilli et al., 2015; Poulsen, 1998; Xia et al., 2017). However, the use of high-dose ZnO has led to various issues such as animal intestinal barrier dysfunction, bacterial resistance, heavy metal residues, and environmental pollution (Satessa et al., 2020; Su et al., 2023), which have increasingly sparked concerns regarding environmental protection and public health. Consequently, it is imperative to develop novel alternatives to high-dose ZnO that are both effective in promoting growth performance and preventing PWD and safe for animal production and environmental sustainability.

Recently, zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) have received widespread attention as an alternative to high-dose ZnO due to their small particle size and large surface area (Oh et al., 2022). Few studies have shown that low-dose (200 or 600 mg/kg) nano-ZnO reduced diarrhea incidence and improved growth performance in weaned piglets, demonstrating an effect comparable to that of conventional-dose ZnO (Oh et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2019). Furthermore, other beneficial effects of nano-ZnO supplementation, such as antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, reproductive protection, and organ protection, have been reported in animals (Mohamed and Abdelrahman, 2019; Mohammad et al., 2019; Rani et al., 2018). However, concerns remain regarding their application in animal production due to uncertainties about their stability and safety (Meiβner et al., 2014). Indeed, a particular material may exhibit toxicity when reduced to the nanoscale, a phenomenon potentially related to the ability of nanoparticles to penetrate and accumulate in cells and organisms (De Stefano et al., 2012; Ispas et al., 2009). The toxicity of nanoparticles is negatively correlated with their particle size. Previous research has shown that nanoscale ZnO with one-dimensional structure exhibits greater toxicity than spherical ZnO, while the effect of size on toxicity appears to be minimal (Peng et al., 2011). Moreover, in vivo fluorescence imaging experiments has revealed that quantum dots with a hydrodynamic diameter less than 5.5 nm can be completely eliminated from the body (Soo Choi et al., 2007). Thus, zinc oxide quantum dots (ZnO QDs), a novel nanomaterial with diameters ranging from 1 to 10 nm in all dimensions, not only retain the distinctive properties of ZnO NPs, but are also cost-effective and non-toxic (Pan et al., 2011), which may be a promising candidate to replace high-dose ZnO in the feed industry. Currently, there is limited research on the application of ZnO QDs in animal production.

In this study, an alternative form of ZnO (ZnO QDs) was investigated to first characterize its structure and evaluate its antibacterial activity in vitro. Subsequently, an in vivo experiment was investigated to assess its effects on diarrhea, Zn metabolism, nutrient digestion, and intestinal microbiota in piglets. This study aimed to investigate whether low-dose ZnO QDs could serve as a substitute for high-dose ZnO and provide a novel insight for preventing diarrhea in weaned piglets.

Materials and methods

Materials and solution preparation

ZnO QDs and ZnO were provided by Sichuan Chelota Biotech Co., Ltd. (Guanghan, Sichuan, China). Escherichia coli (serotype O149:K91, K88ac; CVCC225) was purchased from the National Center for Veterinary Culture Collection (Beijing, China). Salmonella (CVCC2141) and Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC25923) were kindly provided by the College of Veterinary Medicine, Sichuan Agricultural University (Chengdu, Sichuan, China). All experimental chemicals used in this study were of guaranteed reagent grade. Fresh particle suspensions were prepared for each experiment, employing ultrasonic dispersion on ice for 20 min (130 W, 20 kHz, work for 9 s, stop for 3s) (Lee et al., 2016).

Physicochemical characterization and solubility analysis

Morphological structures of ZnO and ZnO QDs were observed by scanning electron microscope (SEM; TM-1000, Hitachi High-Technologies, Tokyo, Japan), transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEM-2100F, JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), and high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HR-TEM; JEM-2010, JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Particle sizes were measured using Digital Micrograph software (version 3.7, Gatan Inc., Pleasanton, CA, USA) according to their images. The specific surface area, total pore volume and average pore size of ZnO and ZnO QDs were analyzed via nitrogen (N2) absorption using the multipoint Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method and calculated by Novawin software (version 11.02, Quanta Chrome Instruments, Inc., Boynton Beach, FL, USA). The hydrodynamic size and zeta potential of ZnO QDs were obtained through the dynamic light scattering (DLS) method using an ELS-8000 (Otsuka Electronics Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) under varying pH conditions (pH 3.8, 6.8, and 7.8). In the solubility study, firstly, the dissolved states of ZnO QDs and ZnO (1000 mg/L) in deionized water were observed after a standing period of 5 min. Subsequently, the Zn2+ concentration in deionized water and phosphate buffer saline (PBS; pH 7.4) was determined using the method of Su et al. (2023). Briefly, ZnO and ZnO QDs solutions at various concentrations (10, 20, 50, 100, 200, and 500 mg/L) were prepared, and then incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for 24 h, followed by centrifugation at 4 °C and 2500 × g for 10 min. Thereafter, 10 mL supernatant was collected from each concentration using a 0.22-μm filter membrane to determine Zn2+ release ability via flame atomic absorption spectrometry (ContrAA 700, Analytik Jena AG, Jena, Germany).

Antibacterial activity test

In vitro, E. coli, Salmonella, and S. aureus were used to investigate the antibacterial activities of ZnO and ZnO QDs through the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) tests. The operation method was based on a previous study with minor modifications (Wu et al., 2019). Notably, a pre-experiment was necessary to roughly determine the antibacterial concentration of the samples. Based on the pre-experimental results, the samples were then diluted with Luria–Bertani (LB) medium to the desired concentrations. Specifically, 100 μL bacterial suspension in the logarithmic growth phase was mixed with 9.9 mL different concentrations of samples in each sterile tube, followed by incubation for 24 h at 37 °C and 200 rpm. The optical density (OD) values of 600 nm each bacterial solution were recorded hourly to plot a bacterial growth curve and determine the MIC. Based on the MIC results, sample concentrations were set at 0.5 ×, 1.0 ×, 1.5 ×, 2.0 ×, 2.5 ×, 3.0 × and higher multiples of the MIC. Similarly, 100 μL diluted bacteria suspension (approximately 1 × 105 CFU/mL) was mixed with 9.9 mL different sample concentrations in a sterile tube, and incubated for 3 h at 37 °C and 200 rpm. The mixture of each tube was then diluted 1000 times with sterile saline. Subsequently, 100 μL the diluent was applied to solid LB medium and cultured at 37 °C for 24 h, to observe the colony growth and determine the MBC.

Animal, experimental design, and diets

The animal care protocols during the current study were approved by the Animal Welfare Care and Use Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University (Chengdu, Sichuan, China), with approval number SYXK (Sichuan)-2019-187. A total of 150 Duroc × (Landrace × Yorkshire) piglets (half barrows and half gilts, weaned at 24 ± 1 d, weighing 7.45 ± 0.41 kg) were randomly divided into five groups in a randomized complete block design based on body weight (BW) and gender. Five groups were the negative control group (CON; basal diet), positive control group (ZnO; basal diet + 1500 mg/kg Zn as conventional ZnO), and 250, 500, or 750 mg/kg Zn as ZnO QDs groups (ZnO QDs 250, ZnO QDs 500, or ZnO QDs 750). Each group consisted of 10 replicates (5 pens of barrows and 5 pens of gilts) with 3 piglets per pen, and this study was conducted in two phases (d 1–14 and 15–28). From d 1 to 14, piglets were fed their respective treated diets, and then all piglets were fed the same basal diet from d 15 to 28. The basal diets (Table 1) were formulated to meet the recommendations of the NRC (2012) and were free of ZnO. Piglets were housed in a temperature-controlled room at 26 to 28 °C, and had ad libitum access to water and feed throughout the experiment. From d 1 to 28, feed intake for each pen was recorded daily to calculate the average daily feed intake (ADFI). Piglets fasted for 12 h were weighed at both the beginning and end of the trial to determine the average daily gain (ADG). Meanwhile, diarrhea scores were recorded daily at 08:00 and 20:00. Fecal scores were evaluated according to the previous method as follows: 0, normal; 1, soft; 2, semiliquid and 3, liquid (Liang et al., 2023a). A fecal consistency score ≥ 2 was considered indicative of diarrhea, and the diarrhea rate was determined as previously described (Sun et al., 2019). Dry matter (DM; method 934.01), crude protein (CP; method 976.05), ether extract (EE; method 973.18), and crude ash (method 942.05) were analyzed in the basal diet according to the method of AOAC (2000). The CP content was analyzed by a Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer (K1270, Hanon Future Technology Group Co., Ltd., Jinan, Shandong, China), and the crude ash was determined by a muffle furnace (SK2-7-17TPC4, Henan Brother Instrument Co., Ltd., Zhengzhou, Henan, China). The organic matter (OM) content was calculated using DM minus crude ash content. The levels of neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and acid detergent fiber (ADF) in the diet were analyzed according to GB/T 20806–2022 and GB/T 13885–2017 (China National Standard, 2017, 2022) using an automatic fiber measuring instrument (A2000I, Ankom Technology Corp., Fairport, NY, USA). The Zn content were analyzed according to NY/T 1459 2022 (Ministry of Agriculture of the People’s Republic of China, 2022) using a flame atomic absorption spectrometer (ContrAA 700). Other nutrient levels were calculated according to the NRC (2012).

Sample collection

From d 11 to 14, fecal samples were collected. Fresh feces from each pen (containing 3 piglets) were collected immediately after defecation at 08:00, 14:00, and 20:00 daily. Samples were preserved in sealed bags with 10 mL of 10% H2SO4 and 2 drops of toluene per 100 g feces, mixed, and stored at −20 °C. After the 4-d collection, all feces from each pen were homogenized and dried to a constant weight at 65 °C. The dried samples were then ground through a 40-mesh sieve and stored at −20 °C for subsequent analysis of the apparent total tract digestibility (ATTD) of nutrients. On d 15, eight replicates with average BW were selected from each group (4 pens of barrows and 4 pens of gilts). Subsequently, one piglet of medium BW was chosen from each of these eight pens for sample collection. Blood samples from each selected piglet were collected to obtain serum, as previously described (Liang et al., 2023b). The forty piglets were anesthetized with 0.1 mL/kg a combination of tiletamine hydrochloride and zolazepam hydrochloride (Zoletil 50, Virbac S.A., Carros, France) administered via intramuscular injection and subsequently euthanized for sample collection. Liver and rectal digesta were collected to determine Zn content. The mucosa from the middle portion of the jejunum was scraped for subsequent analysis of digestive enzyme activity and gene expression. Colonic contents were collected for intestinal microbiota analysis. All samples were immediately frozen with liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for later analysis.

Zn content measurement

Zn contents in feed, serum, liver, feces, and rectal digesta were measured by flame atomic absorption spectrometry (ContrAA 700) according to GB/T 13885–2017 (China National Standard, 2017). Prior to measurement, samples were pre-treated as described by He et al. (2019). In brief, samples were weighed and placed into digestion tubes containing a 10-mL mixture (the proportion of 8 nitric acid to 2 perchloric acid), and soaked for 12 h. Subsequently, samples were dissolved on the graphite digestion instrument for 2 h at 160 °C, followed by the addition of another 10 mL mixed acid to obtain a colorless liquid with a volume of 0.05 mL. These dissolved samples were diluted using 0.5% nitric acid to determine Zn content.

Digestibility and digestive enzyme activity measurement

The analyzed methods of DM, OM, CP, and EE were the same in diets and fecal samples. The ATTD of nutrients was determined using acid-insoluble ash (AIA) as the endogenous indicator (Song et al., 2021). The activities of digestive enzymes including trypsin (A080-2-2), lactase (A082-1-1), and lipase (A054-2-1) were measured using the commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Data were acquired and analyzed using SoftMax Pro software coupled with a SpectraMax 190 microplate reader (Molecular Devices Corp., San Jose, CA, USA).

RNA extraction and gene expression analysis

Total RNA of the jejunal mucosa sample (approximately 0.1 g) was extracted using RNAiso Plus reagent (No. 9109, TaKaRa Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Dalian, Liaoning, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The integrity of RNA was analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis, and subsequently the concentration and purity of RNA were assessed with a NanoDrop ND-2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). For each sample, 1 μg total RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA‌ (cDNA) using PrimeScript FAST RT reagent kit containing gDNA Eraser (RR092A, TaKaRa Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Dalian, Liaoning, China). The gene expressions of Zn transport, nutrient transport, intestinal development factor and aquaporin in jejunal mucosa were determined using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II FAST qPCR kit (CN830A, TaKaRa Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Dalian, Liaoning, China) with a QuantStudio 5 Flex System (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). The cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 10 s. Primer pairs for β-actin (ACTB), as the housekeeping gene, and target genes were listed in Table 2 and full names were provided in Table S1. The relative mRNA expression level was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001).

Intestinal microbiota analysis

Total microbial DNA from the colonic content of a piglet was extracted and identified. The V3–V4 regions of 16S rRNA gene were amplified using the bacterial-specific primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). The 16S rRNA sequencing was performed using the Illumina MiSeq platform (PE300, Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The sequencing data were analyzed on the Majorbio Cloud platform (www.majorbio.com) and deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (BioProject ID: PRJNA1259570).

Statistical analysis

In this study, data were analyzed using SAS software (version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and presented as mean and the standard error of the mean (SEM). The pen was used as an experimental unit for growth performance, diarrhea rate, and nutrient digestibility data (n = 10), whereas the individual piglet was the experimental unit for other data (n = 8). Diarrhea rate was analyzed by the Chi-square test using the PROC GLIMMIX procedure of SAS 9.4. Other data were subjected to one-way ANOVA analysis using the PROC MIXED procedure of SAS 9.4, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison to identify significant differences among the five groups. Furthermore, the data were analyzed using orthogonal polynomial contrasts to evaluate the linear and quadratic effects of ZnO QDs levels among the four groups (CON, ZnO QDs 250, ZnO QDs 500 and ZnO QDs 750). To estimate the minimum effective level (effective dose) of ZnO QDs for anti-diarrhea, broken-line regression analysis was conducted using the PROC NLIN procedure of SAS 9.4. The optimal level (optimal dose) of ZnO QDs for anti-diarrhea was evaluated by quadratic-curve regression analysis using the PROC MIXED procedure of SAS. In addition, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used to analyze the data of microbes and linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis was used to identify distinct microbiome profiles among three groups (CON, ZnO and ZnO QDs 500), which were analyzed on the Majorbio Cloud platform (www.majorbio.com). Statistical significance of all data was considered at P < 0.05, with 0.05 < P < 0.10 representing a trend.

Results

Physicochemical characterization and solubility

From the images (Fig. 1A) and Table 3, ZnO QDs predominantly comprised spherical particles with an average diameter of 3.82 ± 0.38 nm and an interplanar spacing of 0.25 nm, whereas conventional ZnO consisted of irregularly shaped particles or fragments with an average size of 239.48 ± 78.02 nm. Compared to conventional ZnO, ZnO QDs exhibited better dispersibility in water, as evidenced by the rapid precipitation of ZnO at the bottom of the bottle (Fig. 1B). Notably, it was observed ZnO QDs had greater solubility in H2O relative to ZnO (Fig. 1C). Further assessment of the physicochemical characteristics of ZnO QDs and ZnO was summarized in Table 3. In comparison to ZnO, ZnO QDs exhibited a larger surface area and total pore volume. Furthermore, DLS measurements revealed that ZnO QDs formed agglomerates in simulated gastric (pH 3.8), small intestinal (pH 6.8) and colonic (pH 7.8) fluids, with hydrodynamic diameters of 343.50 ± 51.14, 275.00 ± 47.46, and 271.10 ± 56.80 nm, respectively. The zeta potential of ZnO QDs was measured as 22.40 ± 6.75 mV at pH 3.8, 18.00 ± 4.93 mV at pH 6.8, and 14.50 ± 5.73 mV at pH 7.8.

Antibacterial activity

Both ZnO QDs and ZnO exhibited antibacterial activities. For ZnO QDs, the growth of S. aureus, Salmonella, and E. coli was completely inhibited at concentrations of 40, 300, and 400 mg/L, respectively (Fig. 2A). While the MICs for ZnO against S. aureus, Salmonella, and E. coli were respectively 60, 1250, and 1250 mg/L (Fig. 2B), which were higher than those observed for ZnO QDs. As shown in Fig. 2C−E, when the concentration of ZnO QDs reached 400, 750, or 480 mg/L, there were no E. coli, Salmonella, or S. aureus on the culture plate. Compared to ZnO QDs, ZnO was less effective, requiring concentrations of 3750, 3750, and 1080 mg/L to kill E. coli, Salmonella and S. aureus, respectively. These results were summarized as MICs and MBCs in Table 4.

Growth performance

As shown in Table 5, piglets in the ZnO, ZnO QDs 250, and ZnO QDs 500 groups showed higher ADG than those in the CON group (P = 0.003). Additionally, the ZnO group had a higher final BW compared with the CON group, while the ZnO QD 500 group had a lower feed to gain ratio (F/G; P = 0.038). Increasing supplementation of ZnO QDs resulted in a quadratic dose-dependent increase in ADG (P = 0.003) and final BW (P = 0.019), accompanied by a quadratic dose-dependent decrease in F/G (P = 0.013).

Discussion

Despite the wide exploration of numerous alternatives to high-dose ZnO (Sun et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2022; Jang et al., 2023), none have yet demonstrated the ability to fully replace it in practical applications (Tang et al., 2024b). Consequently, there remains an urgent need to develop and validate novel ZnO replacement strategies for PWD in piglets. The transition of ZnO to a nanoscale regime endows it with multifunctional properties, including antibacterial activity, antioxidant capacity, photocatalytic performance, and enhanced biocompatibility (Ali et al., 2017, 2018). These properties predominantly arise from the reduced size and increased specific surface area, providing a compelling rationale for concentrating research efforts on ZnO QDs with dimensions smaller than 10 nm (Verma, 2022). In this study, the ZnO QDs particles were nano-sized (diameter = 3.82 ± 0.38 nm), which were substantially smaller than conventional ZnO (diameter = 239.48 ± 78.02 nm). The specific surface area of ZnO is negatively correlated with its particle size and positively correlated with its bioavailability (Lallo da Silva et al., 2019). In this study, ZnO QDs exhibited a larger surface area due to their smaller particle size, a characteristic that might indicate a potential for high bioavailability and merits further research. Compared to ZnO, ZnO QDs demonstrated superior dispersibility in H2O This finding aligned with a previous study that 30-nm ZnO NPs had poor stability and dispersibility in water compared to 4- and 10-nm ZnO NPs (Bai et al., 2015). The hydrodynamic diameter data indicated a tendency for ZnO QDs to agglomerate in liquid, however, this did not mean that ZnO QDs lost their properties as nanomaterials. The weak bonds between nanoparticles in agglomerates were believed to be able to dissociate upon interaction with biological substances such as proteins (Kim et al., 2012). Research indicated that the surface charge of nanoparticles was a key factor influencing their interactions with biological substances (Merhi et al., 2012; Mura et al., 2011; Kim et al., 2012). However, the zeta potential of ZnO nanoparticles was easily influenced by various factors. In this study, the zeta potential of ZnO QDs varied under different pH conditions, so that needs further experiments to understand it. Moreover, ZnO QDs exhibited higher solubility in H2O when compared to ZnO, which was consistent with Rupasinghe’s (2011) findings on 4-nm ZnO NPs. Collectively, these data indicated that ZnO QDs might possess favorable biological properties (such as antibacterial activity), due to their small particle size, large surface area and high Zn release.

The use of ZnO for preventing diarrhea in weaned piglets is closely associated with its antibacterial activity (Lei and Kim, 2020). The MIC and MBC are the critical indicators for evaluating the ability of materials to inhibit and kill bacteria, respectively (Wu et al., 2019). The present study found that both ZnO QDs and ZnO exhibited antibacterial activities against both bacterial types. Furthermore, low-dose ZnO QDs (400, 300, and 40 mg/L) demonstrated inhibitory effects on E. coli, Salmonella, and S. aureus compared to high-dose ZnO (1250, 1250, and 60 mg/L). A similar phenomenon was observed in MBC results, where ZnO QDs respectively killed E. coli, Salmonella, and S. aureus at concentrations of 400, 750, and 480 mg/L, while ZnO was at concentrations of 3750, 3750, and 1080 mg/L. These findings provided substantial in vitro evidence that the antibacterial activity of ZnO QDs was superior to that of conventional ZnO.

Based on the excellent properties of ZnO QDs in vitro, their effects in vivo were subsequently investigated. Sun et al. (2019) reported that nano-ZnO (23 nm) at 500 and 600 mg/kg significantly improved ADG and reduced diarrhea incidence of piglets; meanwhile, adding 600 mg/kg nano-ZnO achieved the same diarrhea-preventive effect as 2000 mg/kg ZnO. Similarly, piglets with the addition of 250 and 500 mg/kg ZnO QDs improved ADG and final BW, and decreased F/G, exhibiting a similar effect of 1500 mg/kg ZnO administration. Notably, the increase in ADG and decrease in F/G both exhibited a quadratic dose-dependent manner with increasing supplementation of ZnO QDs, suggesting that appropriate doses of ZnO QDs are more conducive to promoting growth in piglets. In addition, the diarrhea rate in piglets fed 250−750 mg/kg ZnO QDs was significantly decreased and comparable to that in piglets fed ZnO (1500 mg/kg). Moreover, broken-line and quadratic-curve analyses of piglets diarrhea rates during the first two weeks post-weaning showed that dietary ZnO QDs exceeding 246 mg/kg effectively prevented diarrhea, with the maximal anti-diarrhea effect achieved at 522 mg/kg. These results indicated that low-dose ZnO QDs have the potential to replace high-dose ZnO in promoting growth and preventing diarrhea in piglets, probably related to their small particle size, large surface area, and superior antibacterial activity.

At present, the application of high-dose ZnO in piglets faces significant limitations, primarily due to its low bioavailability and extensive excretion, leading to environmental pollution (Tang et al., 2024b). In this study, dietary Zn increased Zn content in rectal digesta and feces in a dose-dependent manner, indicating that reducing dietary Zn supplementation could directly and effectively reduce Zn emissions. Therefore, with comparable efficacy in promoting growth and preventing diarrhea in piglets, low-dose ZnO QDs were more environmentally friendly than high-dose ZnO. Zn homeostasis is constantly maintained in the body and tissues (King et al., 2000). Currently, the results revealed a significant elevation in serum and hepatic Zn levels in the ZnO QDs 750 and ZnO groups compared to the CON group, which may indicate Zn overload. This observation was consistent with previous findings, which demonstrated that Zn concentrations in the liver and serum of piglets increased in parallel with the level of dietary ZnO supplementation (Dalto et al., 2023). In addition, the extremely high Zn level in the liver was found in the ZnO group, suggesting that high-dose ZnO could increase Zn accumulation and potentially cause liver damage (Villeneuve et al., 2025). Metallothionein (MT) is a heavy metal-binding protein that protects against heavy metal toxicity and participates in protective stress responses due to its cysteine-rich binding capacity (Liu et al., 2020; Ruttkay-Nedecky et al., 2013). The increased intestinal MT1 mRNA expression in the ZnO group also implied that high-dose ZnO might induce heavy metal toxicity in piglets. Of importance, these adverse effects have not been observed in piglets supplemented with 250−750 mg/kg ZnO QDs. Overall, the addition of low-dose ZnO QDs not only decreased Zn emission pollution, but also protected animals from heavy metal toxicity. Two families of Zn transporters, ZIP (solute carrier 39A, SLC39A) and ZnT (solute carrier 30A, SLC30A), play important roles in regulating Zn homeostasis in the body (Kimura and Kambe, 2016). The ZIP family facilitates the mobilization of Zn from extracellular or organelles/vesicles to the cytosol, thereby increasing cytoplasmic Zn concentration. Conversely, ZnT family mediates Zn efflux or sequestration into organelles/vesicles, reducing cytoplasmic Zn concentration (Wang and Zhou, 2010). In this study, ZnO reduced jejunal ZIP4 mRNA expression and promoted ZnT1 mRNA expression, suggesting that high-dose ZnO caused Zn overload, thereby decreasing intracellular Zn concentration. The DMT1 is mainly involved in the uptake of divalent metal ions, and its gene expression level is up-regulated with increasing Zn content in the intestinal cells (Yamaji et al., 2001). High-dose ZnO promoted DMT1 expression, however, ZnO QDs did not alter the expression of genes related to Zn transport in this study. Moreover, it has been reported that cationic nanoparticles (such as QDs, metallic, and dendrimers) are capable of penetrating cell membranes (Verma and Stellacci, 2010). Thus, it is possible that ZnO QDs can enter cells directly without the need for transport carriers, which warrants further investigation.

For piglets, the underdeveloped gastrointestinal tract can result in reduced ability to digest and absorb nutrients (Tang et al., 2022). Given that undigested and unabsorbed feed can easily predispose piglets to malabsorption and diarrhea (Engelsmann et al., 2023), improvements of nutrient digestion and absorption play a crucial role in promoting growth and preventing diarrhea. Several studies have revealed that high-dose ZnO administration can improve nutrient digestibility (Lei and Kim, 2018; Lipinski et al., 2014; Oh et al., 2022), and this research also confirmed these findings. Furthermore, ZnO QDs improved the ATTD of DM, OM, CP, and EE in piglets in a quadratic dose-dependent manner, particularly in the ZnO QDs 250 group. Notably, the ATTD of OM, CP, and EE coincided with an increase in DM digestibility, which is expected since organic nutrient digestibility is inherently correlated with that of DM through a shared digestive stream (Noblet and van Milgen, 2004). Thus, the improvement of digestive function in piglets by ZnO QDs could be attributed to an overall enhancement of gut health. Concomitant increases in the activities of digestive enzymes (such as trypsin and lipase) following ZnO QDs treatment further supported the finding that ZnO QDs enhanced the digestive ability of piglets. In addition, piglets fed with ZnO and ZnO QDs up-regulated the mRNA expression of SGLT1, indicating an increased capacity for glucose absorption (Han et al., 2022). These results partially explained the improvement in nutrient digestibility, ultimately enhancing the growth performance. Each glucose molecule absorbed is co-transported along with 2 Na ions and 260 water molecules (Wang et al., 2016), potentially contributing to the reduced frequency of diarrhea in pigs receiving ZnO and ZnO QDs. A study has highlighted the critical role of aquaporins (AQPs) in regulating water transport across the gastrointestinal cell membrane (He et al., 2017). Several AQPs, such as AQP3, AQP4, and AQP8, are downregulated in various types of diarrhea (He et al., 2017; Zhu et al., 2017). Treatment with 500 mg/kg ZnO QDs ameliorated AQP3 mRNA expression, contributing to the maintenance of water homeostasis and the reduction of diarrhea. In contrast, high-dose ZnO treatment resulted in a decrease of AQP1 mRNA expression, indicating that perhaps the ability of conventional ZnO to prevent diarrhea in piglets might depend on the convergence of its own molecules rather than the improvement of intestinal function. Indeed, ZnO QDs promoted the expression of genes related to intestinal development, while high-dose ZnO reduced in this study, suggesting ZnO QDs are beneficial for intestinal development and health in piglets. Collectively, both low-dose ZnO QDs and high-dose ZnO could improve the digestion and absorption ability of piglets, thereby increasing nutrient digestibility and preventing diarrhea. However, the underlying mechanisms may differ, as evidenced by their distinct effects on intestinal development, and further research is needed to explore their impact on intestinal health.

Although ZnO QDs have shown excellent antibacterial activity in vitro, their effects on gut microbiota in vivo are still unclear. In this study, modifications of colonic microbiota in the ZnO and ZnO QDs 500 groups, reflected by different OTUs, increased richness and diversity, as well as changed taxonomic composition. In accordance with LEfSe analysis, there were distinct advantageous microbiota in each group, but a comparison exhibited that Lactobacillus decreased significantly following high-dose ZnO supplementation. Firmicutes and Bacteroidota were identified as dominant bacteria in colonic digesta, which was similar to previous reports (Buiatte et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2024). Furthermore, both ZnO and ZnO QDs 500 could alter the gut microbiota. ZnO QDs 500 enhanced the abundance of Verrucomicrobiota, which were considered as beneficial bacteria involved in the regulation of intestinal homeostasis and immune response (Breyer et al., 2024). Conversely, ZnO increased the abundance of Spirochaetota, potentially pathogenic bacteria associated with microbial dysbiosis and intestinal inflammation (Zhang et al., 2024). Lactobacillus is a common probiotic that contributes greatly to preventing pathogen infections, maintaining intestinal health, and stimulating immune responses (Feng et al., 2024; Saha et al., 2024). Earlier studies reported that conventional ZnO reduced the Lactobacillus spp. in both the ileum and colon of piglets (Hu et al., 2013; Vahjen et al., 2011). Similarly, a notable reduction in the abundance of Lactobacillus was observed in piglets fed ZnO. In parallel, the abundances of harmful bacteria such as Escherichia-Shigella and Treponema were enhanced by ZnO. These changes in the genera of microbiota reflected pathogenic infection and even dysbiosis in the gut of piglets fed with high-dose ZnO. More importantly, no obvious changes in Lactobacillus and Treponema were observed in piglets after ZnO QDs intervention. Additionally, ZnO QDs 750 enhanced norank_f_Muribaculaceae abundance that could inhibit the growth and colonization of pathogenic bacteria and mitigate intestinal mucosal damage (Zou et al., 2023). Similarly, a previous study has shown that ZnO QDs reduced the counts of Salmonella pullorum in the cecum of chicks, contributing positively to intestinal health (Li et al., 2021). However, research on ZnO QDs in vivo remains limited. Consequently, further investigations are required to elucidate the specific roles of ZnO QDs on gut microbiota and intestinal health.

Conclusions

The ZnO QDs (3.82 ± 0.38 nm) exhibited superior antibacterial activity in vitro compared with conventional ZnO (239.48 ± 78.02 nm), likely due to their small size, large surface area, and higher Zn release. Low-dose ZnO QDs (250 and 500 mg/kg Zn) effectively improved growth performance, prevented diarrhea, reduced Zn emissions, improved nutrient utilization, and regulated gut microbiota. Conversely, high-dose ZnO increased Zn emissions and liver Zn accumulation, hindered intestinal development, and disrupted microbiota balance by reducing the Lactobacillus population. This study proposes a novel strategy to substitute high-dose ZnO and suggests a promising application for ZnO QDs in animal production (Fig. S1). Presently, there is a severe paucity of research data regarding the use of ZnO QDs in living animals. Given the public’s caution and skepticism towards nanoparticles, comprehensive research and toxicity assessments are essential to validate their efficacy and safety.
