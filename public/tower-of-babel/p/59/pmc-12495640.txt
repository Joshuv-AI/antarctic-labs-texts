Tetanus toxoid vaccination coverage and associated factors among pregnant women in East Africa: systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Birhan Ambachew Taye, Belyu Yehualashet Weldearegay, Bantie Getnet Yirsaw, Aychew Kassa Belete
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTetanus is a public health concern in East Africa, impacting pregnant women and infants since vaccination rates are below acceptable levels. Despite several studies examining tetanus toxoid vaccine coverage in East Africa, the total pooled vaccination coverage rate remains unknown, and the results of these studies are related to these variables. The purpose of this meta-analysis is to estimate the pooled tetanus toxoid vaccination coverage rates among pregnant women in East Africa and identify contributing variables.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Scopus, Embase, and Google Scholar for studies published between March 10, 2015, and March 10, 2025. This study adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) criteria. The study quality was assessed using a modified version of the Newcastle-Ottawa quality assessment tool. The data were retrieved separately by two authors using Microsoft Excel and analyzed with Stata version 17. A random effects model was used to calculate the pooled vaccination coverage and related covariates. Publication bias is assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test, while heterogeneity is evaluated through Cochran’s Q test and the I² statistic, helping to ensure the reliability of meta-analytic findings. The PROSPERO registration number for this evaluation was CRD420251008125.ResultThe meta-analysis included 25 articles and 70,592 participants. Pregnant women in East Africa had at least two doses of tetanus toxoid vaccination, with a pooled estimate of 47% (95% CI: 41%-54%). The meta-analysis revealed that maternal education (OR = 0.63), media exposure (OR = 1.14), ANC service follow-up (OR = 1.06), place of delivery (OR = 1.17), wealth index (OR = 1.25), family planning use (OR = 0.55), number of ANC visits (OR = 3.12), place of residence (OR = 0.88), and need for indexed birth status (OR = 1.36) were significant predictors of tetanus toxoid vaccination coverage.ConclusionThe findings indicate that receiving at least two doses of tetanus toxoid immunization coverage among pregnant women in East Africa was low. Furthermore, various factors were significantly associated.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24687-9.Keywords: Tetanus toxoid, Immunization, Pregnant women, East africa, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Tetanus is a bacterial disease caused by the Clostridium tetani microorganism. It is characterized as a deadly, non-communicable illness that results from the effects of a toxin [1, 2]. The condition of maternal and neonatal tetanus develops when parents maintain unsanitary delivery practices [3]. Immunization coverage rates in sub-Saharan Africa remain unacceptably low, at under 50%, while high-income countries achieve rates exceeding 85% [1]. Failure to receive a tetanus immunization increases your risk of experiencing severe muscular spasms and lungs that fail to function. Unvaccinated individuals in low-income countries face a 50% risk of death, as over 30,000 cases are reported annually [4].

The region of East Africa represents different economic situations, healthcare structures, and local ways of life, which create distinct barriers to reaching high vaccination coverage levels among pregnant women [5]. Multiple factors shape pregnant women’s decisions to get Tetanus toxoid (TT) vaccinations, including their schooling levels, medical service access conditions, social status, and knowledge regarding vaccine advantages. The adoption of tetanus toxoid vaccinations depends on social attributes and healthcare service access, together with cultural values and education levels [4]. Studies report that a higher educational background enables females to better comprehend vaccination benefits and to seek medical care, according to research [6]. The reduction of maternal and neonatal mortality, along with morbidity in East Africa, depends on increasing TT vaccination coverage [3, 7]. The Targeted sub-national areas in Kenya face serious obstacles to vaccination rates due to insufficient health facilities, financial challenges, and vaccine misinformation [8]. The identification of tetanus toxoid vaccination determinants supports public health initiatives to create tailored solutions that will enhance vaccination program productivity [9].

Research exists to show the vital importance of tetanus toxoid vaccinations, yet knowledge remains inadequate about particular factors that determine pregnancy-related coverage rates in East Africa. The existing studies about tetanus toxoid vaccination provide either nation-specific perspectives or insufficient regional analysis, which results in insufficient synthesized data for policy development. Hence, this systematic review and meta-analysis aims to assess the current state of tetanus toxoid vaccination coverage among pregnant women in the East and also to identify key factors associated with vaccination uptake. By synthesizing existing data, this study seeks to provide actionable insights for policymakers, ultimately contributing to improved maternal and neonatal health outcomes in the region. The complete review will offer public health officials precise intervention techniques to increase immunization rates and improve maternal and new-born health outcomes across East Africa.

Methods

Study protocol and registration

This study was a systematic review and meta-analysis of Tetanus toxoid vaccine coverage and related variables among pregnant women in East Africa, done by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis guidelines. The protocol for this systematic review and meta-analysis was filed at Prospero under the registration number (CRD420251008125).

Searching strategies and sources of information

We searched electronic databases including PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Science Direct, and others for research published between March 10, 2015, and March 10, 2025. Keywords such as tetanus toxoid, vaccination coverage, determinants, and East Africa, together with their accompanying Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) phrases, were searched using Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT). For example, the advanced PubMed search strategy was: ((Tetanus toxoid) AND (Utilization OR coverage OR vaccination uptake OR immunization status) AND (Factors OR determinants OR risk factors OR correlates) AND (East Africa OR Eastern Africa OR Eritrea OR Ethiopia OR Comoros OR Djibouti OR South Sudan OR Rwanda OR Uganda OR Burundi OR Sudan OR Tanzania OR Kenya OR Somalia OR Seychelles)) and Filters applied: from 2015 to 2025.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

This meta-analysis included studies that met the following criteria: research conducted solely in East Africa, full-text articles and abstracts written entirely in English, studies published in peer-reviewed journals, all observational studies reporting two or more doses of Tetanus Toxoid (TT2+) vaccination coverage among pregnant women, and publication dates ranging from March 10, 2015 to March 10, 2025.

Exclusion criteria

Studies with distinct outcomes of interest, data from letters and conference abstracts, duplicated data, research done in languages other than English, thesis or dissertations, and studies conducted before March 10, 2015, and after March 10, 2025, were excluded from this study.

Study selection and quality assessment

All examined studies were transferred to EndNote 21 software. After removing duplicates, two writers (BYW and AKB) independently examined the studies for inclusion criteria. Finally, studies meeting the inclusion criteria were included. The quality of the studies was assessed using modified Newcastle-Ottawa quality assessment techniques, which are commonly used for cross-sectional and cohort studies [10]. We based this criterion on established quality assessment tools, such as the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) and the NHLBI Quality Assessment Tool, which are widely recognized for evaluating observational studies. The studies’ quality was independently assessed by two writers (BYW and AKB), and discrepancies were resolved by consulting a third author (BAT). Finally, we considered high-quality studies with at least 8 out of a possible 9 points for both cross-sectional and case-control studies [11, 12]. Our decision to use an 8-point threshold was intended to ensure that only studies with minor limitations were included, thereby enhancing the reliability of our findings (ST1 in the supplementary file).

The outcome of the study

The dependent variable in this study is tetanus toxoid vaccination coverage (TT2+) among pregnant women, defined as the percentage of women who have received at least two doses of the vaccine. Tetanus toxoid (TT) vaccination coverage was calculated as the percentage of pregnant women who received at least two doses of the vaccine. Data were extracted from relevant studies, and coverage was computed using the formula:

Coverage (%) = (number of vaccinated women/total surveyed) × 100.

The primary goal of the study was to determine the pooled tetanus toxoid vaccination coverage among pregnant women in East Africa, and the secondary goal was to identify the determinants associated with tetanus toxoid vaccination coverage among pregnant women in East Africa. The study aims to identify significant disparities in vaccination rates and to examine variables that influence vaccine coverage, including educational background, healthcare accessibility, and cultural attitudes.

Operational definitions

Vaccinated women are those who have received at least two doses of the tetanus toxoid vaccine during their current pregnancy or in previous pregnancies. Women who have received fewer than two doses are classified as uncovered.

Data extraction process

Two authors (BAT and BYW) extract all relevant data using the data extraction form. Both extractors first reviewed the data in question together to discuss their interpretations. If they could not reach a consensus, a third independent reviewer (BAT) was brought in to evaluate the data and provide a final determination. This form was prepared in Microsoft Excel and contains the following variables: The author’s name, publication year, study setting or country, study period, study design, sample size, tetanus toxoid vaccination coverage rate, and adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with a 95% confidence interval for associated factors of tetanus toxoid vaccination coverage among pregnant women in East Africa were extracted.

Statistical analysis

The data was analyzed with Stata version 17. The pooled proportions of tetanus toxoid vaccination coverage were calculated using the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation to stabilize the variances of the proportions. and its related determinants were investigated using a random effects model [13]. We utilized sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness of the results, subgroup analysis to explore variations among different groups, and meta-regression analysis to identify potential sources of heterogeneity in the data. Heterogeneity was evaluated using the Statistic. The Statistics showed variance between investigations, with values of 25%, 50%, and 75% indicating low, medium, and high heterogeneity, respectively [14]. Finally, the funnel plot, Egger’s regression test, and Begg’s test were employed to determine the presence of publication bias among the studies [15].

Ethics considerations

The study does not require ethical approval (HREC) because it is based on a significant literature review and meta-analysis. All data were identified and pooled to guarantee casuistic data ownership.

Results

Search outcomes and characteristics of included studies

A total of 842 studies were searched from various databases, with all research on Tetanus toxoid vaccine coverage included. The data was then exported to EndNote 21 Reference Manager for screening, and a total of 817 studies were removed from the analysis due to irrelevant (unrelated) titles, duplicate findings, and disparate outcomes of interest. Finally, 25 studies with a total of 70,592 women who matched the inclusion criteria were selected for systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

The pooled rate of tetanus toxoid vaccination coverage (TT2+) among pregnant women in East Africa

From a total of 25 studies, the pooled estimate of receiving at least two doses of tetanus toxoid immunization coverage among pregnant women in East Africa is 47% (95% CI: 41% − 54%), with observed heterogeneity ( = 91.4%; p-value = 0.000) (Fig. 2).

Sub-Group analysis

Tetanus toxoid vaccine coverage varied from 20 to 52% in the research locations; Tanzania had the highest coverage, while South Sudan had the lowest, at 20% (95%CI: 14%−29%). The research done in Ethiopia showed the most heterogeneity (I^2 = 91.99%).

When looking at the year of publication, the coverage of tetanus toxoid vaccinations among pregnant women was at its lowest in 2018 at 28% (95%CI: 20%−37%) and at its greatest in 2022 at 60% (95%CI: 50%−69%). The research carried out in 2024 showed the largest heterogeneity (I^2 = 94.67%) (Table 2).

Publication bias or small study effects

The Egger’s test and funnel plot were used to determine whether there were small study effects or publication bias across the studies. The funnel plot displays an uneven distribution of data points, with more on the right, and the faux 95% confidence limits are not symmetrical, indicating a potential publishing bias toward higher, positive impact sizes. However, an Egger test is required to demonstrate the existence and magnitude of this bias (Fig. 4).

Funnel plot of tetanus toxoid vaccination coverage with standard error

Egger’s regression analysis shows a substantial negative slope and positive bias, meaning that smaller trials report lower effect sizes. The low p-value offers strong evidence against the null hypothesis of no small-study effects, suggesting that this meta-analysis may contain publication bias (Table 3).

Uneducated women had a pooled odds ratio (OR) of 0.63, indicating that they are less likely to receive at least two doses of tetanus toxoid immunization coverage than literate women, a statistically significant finding (p = 0.008). Women exposed to media had a higher vaccination rate odds ratio of 1.14 compared to unexposed women, with a statistically significant p-value of 0.000, despite research variability (I² = 94.0%). In addition, those who had ANC follow-up had an OR of 1.06 compared to those who did not receive it. Similarly, women who give birth in a health facility are more likely to obtain vaccines (OR = 1.17), compared to those who give birth at home. Women who do not use family planning services are less likely to acquire the TT vaccine (OR = 0.55) than their peers. Women who had 1–3 or ≥ 4 ANC visits were more likely to obtain vaccines than those who did not have any. The location of residence also has an influence; rural women had an OR of 0.88 compared to urban women, indicating a reduced likelihood of immunization (p = 0.000). Finally, Women with wanted pregnancy were more likely to receive vaccination (OR = 1.36) than those who did not, with a significant p-value of 0.040(Table 4).

Discussion of results

The major goal of this study was to determine the pooled estimate of pregnant women in East Africa who had at least two doses of tetanus toxoid vaccine and other associated characteristics. Our final meta-analysis included 25 studies with 70,592 women, 47% of whom were vaccinated. This conclusion is consistent with earlier research done from 2016 to 2023 [18, 22, 37–39] and indicates that there is a core level of comprehension. However, this analysis revealed that it is lower than findings published in high-income nations (85%) [1], studies conducted in Ethiopia [16, 19, 23, 25, 28, 32, 34], and greater than those reported in studies [3, 8, 17, 20, 21, 27, 29, 36, 37, 40, 41]. This disparity might be attributed to variables such as poor healthcare access, varied education levels, cultural attitudes, and disparities in research methodology.

According to this study, maternal education significantly increased tetanus toxoid vaccination coverage among pregnant women in East Africa. The pooled OR value shows that illiterate women had a lower vaccination rate than literate women. This study is backed by research conducted by [42, 43]. This might be owing to higher vaccination rates among educated women, who have a better grasp of the need for immunizations.

Pregnant women who were exposed to the media were more likely to acquire vaccinations than those who did not interact with the media. This report is consistent with investigations conducted by [44]. The rationale might be that media participation boosts vaccine awareness until it reaches a high level of acceptance, resulting in increased vaccination coverage. Furthermore, pregnant women who had an ANC follow-up were more likely to obtain the TT immunization than those who did not. This study is backed by research conducted [45–47]. This might be related to sustained healthcare interaction, which leads to higher vaccination uptake rates. Furthermore, women who do not utilize family planning are less likely to be inoculated against TT than those who do. This report has been backed by [48]. This might be because family planning involves more comprehensive healthcare involvement since frequent trips to provider facilities result in higher vaccine access.

Similarly, this study found that women who had 3–4 or more ANC visits were more likely to obtain TT immunization than those who did not have visits. This might be because women with limited healthcare engagement encounter a variety of vaccination barriers since they do not have consistent access to healthcare facilities. This study also found that rural women were less likely to get immunized than urban women. This report is consistent with investigations conducted by [49]. This might be because people in rural areas have less access to healthcare than their urban counterparts. Furthermore, women who wanted to become pregnant were more interested in vaccinations than those who did not. This report has been supported by [50]. This might be because women who desire to become pregnant actively seek healthcare services, including vaccines.

Furthermore, the paper makes specific recommendations, such as increased healthcare services, media participation, and educational activities. Eastern African countries need to address these issues to improve vaccination acceptance and enhance health outcomes for mothers and new-borns. Similarly, this study underscores the need to implement educational campaigns to inform pregnant women about the benefits of immunization. Regular antenatal care (ANC) is essential for pregnant women. By addressing these concerns, we can increase tetanus toxoid immunization rates, which will significantly enhance maternal and new-born healthcare outcomes across East Africa.

Conclusion

The data indicate that the pooled estimate of obtaining at least two doses of tetanus toxoid vaccine coverage among pregnant women in East Africa is low. Consequently, public health officials face a critical challenge, as inadequate vaccination of women can lead to an increase in maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality. Furthermore, the study found that maternal education, media exposure, ANC service follow-up, place of delivery, wealth index, family planning use, number of ANC visits, place of residence, and need for indexed birth status were all significant predictors of tetanus toxoid vaccination coverage among pregnant women in East Africa. To improve tetanus toxoid vaccine coverage in East Africa, health systems must be strengthened via facility infrastructure improvements and healthcare staff training. Community participation through outreach activities can help to improve knowledge about the vaccine’s relevance, especially among pregnant women. Increased accessibility through mobile vaccination units, as well as integration into mother and child health efforts, can help to improve uptake even further. Furthermore, strong monitoring methods to track immunization rates are critical. Future studies should concentrate on finding societal obstacles to vaccination, assessing long-term health effects, and comparing the cost-effectiveness of different delivery modalities. Investigating vaccine reluctance and the involvement of non-governmental organizations (NGOs) in immunization initiatives can also yield useful insights for increasing coverage.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study has several key strengths: The systematic review and meta-analysis examined a wide range of studies to discover large gaps in tetanus toxoid vaccination coverage among pregnant women in Eastern Africa. Nonetheless, there are important limitations, such as research quality variability, the likelihood of publication bias, and the fact that all evaluated publications are from East Africa, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, all of the included studies employed a cross-sectional design, limiting the capacity to show causality and study temporal changes. These strengths and limitations can help guide future research into this important aspect of maternal health.
