Salmonella vector creates de novo parvovirus that reduces solid tumors and forms antitumor immune memory
Authors: Shradha Khanduja, Vishnu Raman, Christopher L. Hall, Lars M. Howell, Nele Van Dessel, Neil S. Forbes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe have created a Salmonella vector that delivers oncolytic viruses (OVs) into solid tumors. Despite their potential, OVs are cleared after systemic injection and are not effective against internal tumors. When injected intravenously, virus-delivering Salmonella (VDS) is safe and colonizes tumors 50 million times more than clearance organs. After colonization, VDS invades cancer cells, releases a virus-encoding plasmid, and initiates virion formation. Bacterial delivery of the H-1 parvovirus reduces both hepatocellular and pancreatic tumors, increases survival, and triggers the formation of tumor-specific splenocytes that prevent re-implantation. Treating with VDS increases dendritic cells, infiltration of CD8 T cells, and polarized macrophages. Intravenous injection of VDS produces the same responses as an intratumoral injection, and outperforms direct injection of H-1 parvovirus (H-1PV), which minimally affects immune responses and tumor volume. Combining bacteria and OVs creates a therapy that activates the immune system, generates antitumor immunity, and provides a promising platform for treating solid tumors.

Introduction

Oncolytic viruses (OVs) have great potential as cancer therapeutics but have limited efficacy against solid internal tumors.1,2,3,4 When OVs are injected intravenously, they are cleared by anti-viral immunity or neutralizing antibodies, and, typically, only 0.001% reach the tumor microenvironment.5,6,7,8,9,10,11 Because of these limitations, many OVs are injected intratumorally.12,13 An engineered bacterial vector could overcome this delivery limitation and carry OVs directly into tumors. When injected intravenously, non-pathogenic, therapeutic Salmonella colonizes tumors at least 3,000 times more than healthy tissue.14,15 In addition, Salmonella naturally invades epithelial cells16,17 and senses the intracellular environment to express effectors encoded by pathogenicity island 2 (SPI2).18,19,20

To utilize these properties, we created VDS, a bacterial vector that carries OVs into internal solid tumors (Figure 1A). After invading into cells (step 1), we designed VDS to release the viral genome into the intracellular environment (step 2). Once the viral genome is transported to the nucleus, the cell’s machinery translates the viral DNA into non-structural and capsid proteins (step 3). Assembly of these proteins into functional virions lyses the cells (step 4) and releases virus particles into the local environment to infect neighboring cells (step 5). The OV induces lytic death of cancer cells that recruits immune cells (macrophages and T cells) to tumors (step 6) and initiates an antigen cascade that forms antitumor immunity.21,22,23,24 The modularity of this design enables the delivery of any OV that can be encoded on a plasmid, including both double-stranded and single-stranded DNA and RNA viruses.Figure 1Salmonella delivers functional H-1PV virions that are cytotoxic to cancer cells(A) Mechanisms of bacterial delivery of viruses to cancer. Several other strategies have been explored to overcome the difficulty of delivering OVs to internal solid tumors. Capsid proteins have been modified to target viruses to cancer cells, but this engineering compromises virus formation after secondary infection25,26 and the capsid would have to be uniquely engineered for each tumor.27,28 Alternately, viruses could be carried by autologous cells (e.g., T cells, myeloid cells, and stem cells).9,11 These carriers, however, have less than 10% accumulation rates in tumors,9,11 which is orders of magnitude smaller than the accumulation rates of intracellular-delivering (ID) Salmonella.14,15 One recent approach delivered the single-stranded RNA genome of Senecavirus A into cancer cells, demonstrating the potential of bacterial delivery.29

H-1 parvovirus (H-1PV) is a small (∼5 kb), single-stranded DNA rat virus of Protoparvovirus genus.30,31,32 It is naturally oncolytic, replicates autonomously, and is not pathogenic to humans.25,33,34,35 H-1PV selectively infects cancer cells while sparing normal cells because it utilizes cellular proliferation machinery to produce viral DNA and proteins.30,36,37 Because of its small size, H-1PV diffuses easily and is readily dispersed through tumor tissue.38 The H-1PV genome contains two regions that encode for two non-structural proteins, NS1 and NS2, and three structural capsid proteins, VP1, VP2, and VP3.39 After parvoviruses infect cells, host polymerases produce NS1 from a double-stranded template of the viral DNA.40,41,42,43 NS1 is the initiator protein that creates a site-specific nick in single-stranded DNA and serves as a helicase to initiate DNA replication.41,42,44,45 This DNA template is used to produce more single-stranded viral genomes that are assembled in the nucleus into new virus particles.31,46 In addition to this essential role in virus replication, NS1 also kills cells by nicking DNA and hijacking protein production.37,47

In general, H-1PV controls tumors by the same immune stimulatory mechanisms as other OVs.31,40,48 In mice, OVs regress tumors and clear many intractable cancers.49,50,51 The lysis of tumor cells by OVs initiates the formation of antitumor immunity, which eliminates cancer cells throughout the body (the abscopal effect).52,53,54,55 OVs function by two main effects: direct lysis of tumor cells and activation of the immune system.4,40,53,55,56,57 In tumors, H-1PV induces immunogenic cell death by a cathepsin-mediated pathway48,58 that stimulates the release of tumor-associated antigens, cytokines, and danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs).36,48,54,56,59,60,61,62 These responses recruit macrophages that reform the tumor microenvironment.63 When activated, macrophages produce cytokines that attract and activate T cells.63 Cross-presentation of tumor antigens forms central memory T cells that are the principal components of a long-term antitumor immune response.48,60,64,65 After intratumoral (i.t.) administration, H-1PV has been shown to clear tumors31,50,66 and generate tumor-specific, T cell memory.60,61 Despite this potential, OVs do not have any inherent tumor tropism and are often sequestered to the liver and spleen.8,9,25,40 Creating an effective carrier is necessary to deliver viruses and activate immune memory in internal solid tumors.5,7,9,11

Unlike other cancers, there are few treatment options for patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) or pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PDAC), and no curative treatments exist.67,68,69 H-1PV is particularly effective against hepatoma cells with null or mutated p5369,70,71,72 and replicates in PDAC cells that express SMAD4.40,73 HCC and PDAC are the third and fourth leading causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide, and projections indicate that incidence will rise over the next decade.69,74,75 PDAC has a dismal prognosis, with nearly 80% of patients dying in the first year following diagnosis and only 10%–20% meeting the resection criteria for curative treatment.74,76 HCC is similarly dismal with almost 75% of patients being diagnosed at intermediate or late stages and only a minority being eligible for liver transplantation.69,77,78 Standard therapies for HCC (transcatheter arterial chemoembolization or sorafenib) and PDAC (gemcitabine plus nab paclitaxel or the FOLFIRINOX regimen) have had little effect on disease prognosis and only moderately increase survival.69,79,80 In addition to HCC and PDAC, H-1PV has also been shown to be effective against glioblastoma.36,37,81

The goal of this work was to create VDS to induce antitumor immune responses in HCC and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (Figure 1A). We hypothesized that (1) VDS delivers H-1PV into the cytoplasm of cancer cells in tumors; (2) the resultant viral infection causes cell death, induces immune cell infiltration, and triggers the formation of antitumor immunity; and (3) VDS delivery of H-1PV reduces tumors, increases survival, and prevents tumor re-implantation. To test these hypotheses, we deleted recombinase genes from the Salmonella genome to maintain virus viability and transformed the strain with an autonomous lysis system to enable gene delivery into cancer cells. We tested the efficacy of VDS by treating multiple cancer types (HCC, breast, and pancreatic) in vitro and measuring NS1 production, virus formation, and overall cytotoxicity. To measure virus formation in vivo, we injected VDS into tumor-bearing mice, measured the production of NS1, and measured the infectivity of newly formed virions in the tumor lysate. We also measured the safety, maximum tolerable dose, and biodistribution of the virus-delivering bacteria. To quantify efficacy, we measured tumor volume reduction, overall survival, and the activation of innate and adaptive immune cells. To determine the benefit of this approach, we compared bacterially delivered H-1PV to direct intravenous (i.v.) administration of H-1PV. To quantify immunological memory against tumors, we adoptively transferred splenocytes from VDS-treated mice into naive mice and measured tumor implantation. By combining intracellular delivery with Salmonella and immune induction with H-1PV, we have created a promising platform for treating solid tumors that could expand the number of tumor types that can be treated with OVs.

Results

Salmonella delivers functional H-1PV virions

To stably deliver OVs, we created a Salmonella strain (BB′CDF, Table S1) that does not degrade essential hairpins in the H-1PV genome (Figure 1B). Two palindromic regions on either end of the linear genome initiate replication and are necessary for virion production in cells.82 The BB′CDF strain is deficient in several genes, recB, sbcB, sbcC, sbcD, and recF, that are components of the RecBCD and RecFOR DNA repair systems.83,84 We also created an additional strain (designated CDS) that only has one repair deletion (sbcCD). SbcCD is a nuclease that cleaves double-stranded hairpins.85

With these deletions in homologous recombination genes, BB′CDF stably maintained the H-1PV plasmid and formed virus particles in cancer cells (Figures 1C and 1D). To test stability, we transformed BB′CDF and CDS with a plasmid containing the H-1PV genome (Table S1) and compared these strains to the SURE-2 (Stop Unwanted Rearrangement Events) strain, which is an endonuclease- and recombination-deficient strain of E. coli that is used to maintain parvovirus hairpins.86 Digestion with SSPI and EcoRI produced two 4,402- and 3,150-bp fragments in the plasmids maintained in all three strains (Figure 1C). When cleaved, a 97-bp section is deleted from the right-end hairpin, which reduces the first fragment to 4,305 bp.82 For all three strains, the two bands were the same size, indicating that the hairpins were retained (Figure 1C). To determine their activity, the H-1PV plasmids that were maintained in BB′CDF, CDS, and SURE-2 were transfected into human embryonic kidney (HEK293T) cells (Figure 1D). All of these transfected cells expressed the viral initiator protein NS1, which indicates the beginning of functional virus formation and shows that all three plasmids were active (Figure 1E).

To create a strain that would release the viral genome into cancer cells, we transformed Salmonella with a plasmid containing the PsseJ-lysE circuit (Figures 1F and 1G). The PsseJ promoter controls the expression of an SPI2-associated gene and is activated after cell invasion.15 In this circuit, it is coupled to the lysin gene E (lysE) from bacteriophage ΦX174 that rapidly lyses bacteria after expression and spills their contents into the local environment.87 To test molecular release, parental Salmonella were transformed with a plasmid that contains PsseJ-lysE and constitutively expresses GFP. A control strain was created that only expresses GFP but does not contain the PsseJ-lysE circuit. When the PsseJ-lysE strain was co-cultured with cancer cells, it invaded the cells and released GFP (green, white arrow) after sensing the intracellular environment and triggering the PsseJ promoter (Figure 1F, left). In comparison, the control strain invaded cells (red, black arrow), but did not release any GFP, which was not visible after permeabilization (Figure 1F, right). Over multiple cultures, the PsseJ-lysE strain delivered molecules to more cells than the control strain (p = 0.014, Figure 1G), showing the efficacy of the PsseJ-lysE for intracellular delivery.

To generate VDS, we transformed BB′CDF with the plasmid containing the H-1PV genome and a second plasmid containing the PsseJ-lysE intracellular delivery circuit (Table S1). We also created an additional delivery strain (designated VDS-C) by transforming CDS with the H-1PV and the PsseJ-lysE plasmids. To enhance delivery, the CDS strain has an additional deletion (ΔsseJ, Figure S1).88 The ΔsseJ deletion was not added to VDS because the extra deletion weakened this strain and reduced delivery (Figure S1).

Salmonella delivery of H-1PV is cytotoxic to cancer cells

VDS delivered H-1PV and significantly decreased cell viability in four human and two mouse cancer cell lines (Figures 1H–1Q). To measure cytotoxicity, VDS and VDS-C were co-cultured (Figure 1H) with three human liver cells (HuH7, Hep3B, Sk-Hep-1), human breast cells (MCF-7), syngeneic mouse liver cells (Hepa1-6), and mouse pancreatic cancer cells (KPCY). After 2 h of infection, extracellular bacteria were removed with gentamicin (Figure 1H). Administering VDS or VDS-C to HuH7 cells induced the formation of NS1 and phosphorylated-NS1 (NS1P, Figure 1I). This induction matched the direct transfection of HuH7 cells with the H-1PV plasmid (Figure 1I). The production of this protein is the beginning of virus production and shows that the bacteria released the H-1PV plasmid into cells, where it translocated to the nucleus and was expressed.82 When applied to Hepa1-6 mouse hepatoma cells (Figure 1J) and KPCY mouse pancreatic cancer cells (Figure 1K), VDS delivered the H-1PV plasmid and generated the NS1 protein. VDS-C did not deliver as many viral plasmids as VDS (Figures 1J and K).

Administering VDS to HuH7 cells reduced cell viability 70% compared to bacterial controls (p = 0.0011; Figure 1L, left). For these controls, cells were treated with the BB′CDF strain that invades and lyses, but does not deliver the H-1PV plasmid. Treatment with VDS-C had a similar effect, but only reduced viability by 25% (p = 0.0003; Figure 1L, right). Direct transfection with the H-1PV plasmid also considerably reduced viability, showing that this virus is effective against HuH7 cells (p < 0.0001, Figure 1L). The number of bacteria added to the cultures did not affect viability (Figure S2), further indicating that virus replication is a critical component of this mechanism.

VDS delivered H-1PV and induced cell death in the five other cell types (Figures 1M–1Q). The delivery of H-1PV significantly reduced cell viability in both Hepa1-6 (p = 0.017; Figure 1M, left) and KPCY (p = 0.021; Figure 1N, left) cells, compared to bacterial controls. VDS-C had less effect on the viability of Hepa1-6 cells than VDS (Figure 1M, right), but did reduce the viability of KPCY cells (p = 0.011; Figure 1N, right). Similar to HuH7 cells, administration of VDS also reduced the viability of two human liver cancer cells, Hep3B (p = 0.0025; Figure 1O, left) and Sk-Hep-1 (p < 0.0001; Figure 1P, left), and MCF7 breast cancer cells (p = 0.0045; Figure 1Q, left). In these cells, VDS-C had a similar effect as VDS (Figures 1O–1Q). In addition to native H-1PV, VDS delivered modified mH-1PV, showing the ability to deliver multiple viral types (Figure S3). Viewed together, these data demonstrate that VDS stably maintains the H-1PV plasmid. Delivery of this plasmid produces active virus particles that kill both human and mouse cancer cells.

Five days after injecting the bacteria, the viral initiator protein NS1 was produced in tumors, but not in uninfected control tumors (Figure 2B). Viruses delivered by VDS are functional and able to initiate de novo infections (Figures 2C–2F). Five days after tumor-bearing mice were injected with VDS, tumors were homogenized to harvest newly formed virions (Figure 2C). The tumor homogenate was treated with gentamicin to kill the bacteria and was added to cultures of HEK293T cells (Figure 2C). Forty-eight hours after adding the homogenate from Hepa1-6 tumors, HEK293T cells produced NS1 (Figure 2D). Cells incubated with the homogenate from uninfected control mice did not produce NS1 (Figure 2D). Although VDS-C delivered H-1PV, it was not as effective as VDS (Figures 2D and 2E). When the homogenate from mice treated with VDS-C was applied to HEK293T cells, some NS1 was produced (Figure 2D). However, the amount of NS1 produced after treating cells with VDS-C tumor homogenate was 24% less than VDS homogenate (p = 0.018, Figures 2E and S4B). In addition, there was no phosphorylated NS1 (NS1P, Figure 2D), which is critical for the function of H-1PV.82,89 Using a similar protocol (Figure 2C), nude mice with human Hep3B tumors were injected with VDS or VDS-C (Figure S4C). The homogenate from these tumors induced HEK293T cells to produce two H-1PV capsid proteins (VP1 and VP2), indicating the production of fully functional virions (Figure S4C). Similarly, the homogenate from human HuH7 tumors, treated with VDS or VDS-C, induced HEK-293T cells to form NS1 and phosphorylated NS1P (Figure S4D).

Virus-delivering Salmonella is safe and has enhanced colonization

BB′CDF has greater tumor colonization than parental Salmonella (Figures 3A–3C). To determine the biodistribution of virus-delivering bacteria, we injected BB′CDF into mice with Hepa1-6 tumors (Figure 3A). Seventy-two hours after injection, BB′CDF colonized tumors significantly more than healthy organs (p < 0.0001, Figure 3B). The colonization in tumors was over 50 million times higher than in the liver and spleen (Figure 3B). The engineered BB′CDF strain did not colonize healthy organs as much as the parental strain (Figure 3C). Colonization of BB′CDF in the liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, brain, and heart was minimal and only 0.6% to 3% of the parental strain (Figure 3C). The safety of VDS was due, in part, to the safety of the base BB′CDF strain (Figures 3F–3H). The genes that were deleted from this strain (recB, sbcB, sbcCD, and recF) prevent cleavage of the H-1PV genome,82 but they also increased the safety of the strain. Five days after the last injection, splenocytes were extracted from donor mice and injected intraperitonially into naive recipient mice (Figure 6A). Two weeks after the splenocyte injection, the recipient mice were challenged with Hepa1-6 cells (Figure 6A). Tumors formed in 75% of naive control mice that were not injected with splenocytes (Figure 6B). In these mice, tumors started to form between days 28 and 42 (Figure 6C). By day 66, all of the tumors were palpable and most had reached maximum tumor burden (Figure 6C). VDS increased the number of innate immune cells in viable tumor tissue (Figures 7C–7J). The presence of Salmonella-delivered H-1PV in tumors increased macrophages 1.76 times (p = 0.032, Figure 7D) compared to control mice. Within the population of macrophages, treatment with VDS increased the number of cells expressing the co-stimulatory CD80 surface molecule and decreased the number expressing the mannose receptor (CD206, Figure 7E). VDS increased the number of activated macrophages that express CD80 3.1 times compared to BB′CDF controls (p = 0.014, Figure 7F). Treatment with VDS increased the ratio of CD80- to CD206-expressing macrophages 2.03 times compared to controls (p = 0.047, Figure 7G), indicating a shift from tumor-promoting M2 macrophages (CD206+) to tumor-inhibiting M1 macrophages (CD80+).90,91 CD80 and CD206 were gated using fluorescence-minus-one controls for both BB′CDF and VDS (Figure S6). In these mice, VDS increased the number of tumor-resident dendritic cells by 10% (Figure 7H). Although VDS increased innate immune cells, it generally decreased the total number of all immune cells (p = 0.031, Figure 7I). This drop was due to a decrease (p = 0.015, Figure 7J) in the number of neutrophils (CD45+ CD3− F4/80− NKp46− CD11c−). Together, these effects on innate immune cells show that VDS shifted the tumor microenvironment from immunosuppressive to immunostimulatory.

In addition to stimulating innate cells, bacterial delivery of H-1PV activated the adaptive immune system (Figures 7K–7R). The presence of viruses in the tumors considerably increased the number of T cells (CD3+, red boxes, Figure 7K). Mice injected with VDS had 2.4 times more CD3+ T cells than bacterial (BB′CDF) controls (p = 0.011, Figure 7L). Of these infiltrating T cells, most were cytotoxic lymphocytes ([CTLs], CD8+) and not regulatory T cells ([Tregs], CD4+ CD25+; Figure 7M). Treatment with VDS increased the number of CD8+ T cells 1.8 times (p = 0.027, Figure 7N) and increased the ratio of CTLs to Tregs 12-fold (p = 0.040, Figure 7O). Of the CD8+ CTLs that had infiltrated into the tumors, three times more were antigen experienced (CD44+, Figure 7P) in the group treated with VDS (p = 0.041, Figure 7Q), reflecting prior antigen encounter and differentiation into effector/memory-like states.

With time, the immune composition of the tumors shifted. In the mice that survived for 93 days beyond the injection of VDS (Figure 4F), the number of T cells (CD45+ CD3+) decreased 61% compared to the number in tumors 17 days after starting treatment (Figure S7A). At 93 days, most of the CD8 T cells in tumors were exhausted and 97% expressed PD-1 (Figure S7B).

VDS outperforms direct injection of H-1PV, which is only minimally effective

Compared to the direct injection of H-1PV, bacterial delivery with VDS decreased tumor volume and increased both adaptive and innate immune responses (Figure 8). Mice with KPCY pancreatic tumors were treated with two injections of VDS, H-1PV virus alone, or BB′CDF bacterial control (Figure 8A). The i.v. administration of VDS reduced tumor growth and was significantly more effective than H-1PV alone (Figure 8B). This reduction corresponded to the lowest tumor growth rate (p = 0.0019 for BB′CDF and p = 0.0009 for H-1PV, Figure 8C). Compared to H-1PV, VDS enhanced myeloid activation and strengthened adaptive immunity (Figures 8D–8G). In the tumors treated with VDS, there was a trend toward increased macrophages (Figure 8D) and a significant increase in activated macrophages (F4/80+CD80+, p = 0.0070, Figure 8E). Total T cell infiltration (CD3+) was significantly elevated (p = 0.0043, Figure 8F). Of the infiltrating T cells, most were cytotoxic lymphocytes (CTLs, CD8+), compared to both H-1PV and BB′CDF controls (p = 0.0070, Figure 8G). VDS also increased the total number of dendritic cells (CD11c+, p = 0.046, Figure 8I) and there was a borderline increase in the number of activated dendritic cells (p = 0.063, Figure 8I). Together, these findings demonstrate, in another tumor model, that VDS suppresses tumor growth and promotes a more activated and effector-dominant immune landscape, while H-1PV virus alone fails to confer measurable antitumor or immunological benefit.

Discussion

We have created a bacterial vector that delivers and launches an H-1PV infection in cancer cells in tumors. When injected intravenously, this strain colonizes tumors preferentially and clears rapidly from healthy organs (Figures 3B and 3C). After colonizing tumors, VDS invades cells, releases the virus-encoding plasmid, and induces the formation of functional virions (Figure 2). Bacterial virus delivery reduced volume in multiple tumor models, including HCC and PDAC (Figures 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8); increased overall survival (Figures 4 and 5); and considerably outperformed direct injection of H-1PV (Figure 8). The delivery of H-1PV by Salmonella activated both innate and adaptive immunity and converted the tumor microenvironment from immunosuppressive to immunostimulatory (Figure 7). VDS caused infiltration of tumor-specific CD8 T cells (Figures 7 and 8) and prevented the implantation of tumors (Figure 6). We found that VDS therapy is safe, causes minimal effects on mouse weights, and does not induce the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Figure 3).

The reduction of tumor volume and extension of survival was due in large part to the engagement of the immune system. The direct cytotoxic effect of VDS was small and did not kill all cells (Figure 1). When VDS was administered to Hepa1-6 cancer cells, the improvement in cytotoxicity over the bacterial vector alone was modest (Figure 1J). In contrast, when VDS was injected into tumor-bearing mice, we observed a marked reduction in tumor volume (Figures 4, 5, and 8), indicating that immune activation in addition to direct oncolysis was responsible for the enhanced antitumor effect. Delivery of H-1PV into tumors with VDS recruited macrophages (Figure 7D) and T cells (Figures 7L and 8F). In addition, VDS polarized macrophages to the tumor-inhibiting M1 phenotype (Figures 7G and 8E), recruited antigen-experienced cytotoxic CD8 T cells (Figures 7M–7Q and 8G), and induced the formation of tumor-specific memory T cells (Figure 6). CD8-depletion studies are required to determine if this enrichment in CD8 T cells directly caused the observed tumor reduction. The parallel induction of both innate and adaptive immune responses was due to the synergistic coupling of bacterial and viral therapies. Bacterial therapies recruit innate immune cells to the tumor microenvironment,92 and OVs stimulate adaptive immune responses.1,52,53,56,57

Using bacterial delivery overcomes critical limitations of OVs and enables the treatment of internal solid tumors. When administered with an i.v. injection, VDS induced the same effect on tumor volume as an i.t. injection (Figure 5G). This equal response shows that the bacteria protected the viral genome while it was in the blood and prevented the formation of anti-viral immunity. In addition, using a bacterial carrier enables repeat dosing (Figures 4 and 5). One problem associated with OVs is the development of neutralizing antibodies and anti-viral T cells after the first dose reduces the efficacy of subsequent doses.8 When VDS was repeatedly administered to mice, we saw a continued reduction in tumor volume (Figures 4 and 5) and an increased immune response (Figures 6 and 7). Because the bacteria only carry the genome and no viral proteins, immune cells do not encounter viral antigens until virus particles are formed by cancer cells in tumors.

The removal of genes to make the base BB′CDF strain greatly increased the performance of the strain. We originally deleted these genes to enable Salmonella to carry the parvovirus genome without cleaving essential hairpins.82 These modifications, however, had unexpected benefits. In mice, BB′CDF had improved safety and tumor colonization than the parental strain (Figure 3). This advantage was caused by a doubling in tumor colonization and increased elimination from healthy tissues. In healthy organs, the recB deletion in BB′CDF increased its clearance by M1 macrophages.93,94 In Salmonella, the RecBCD pathway is required to repair double-stranded breaks created by reactive oxygen species in the phagosomes of macrophages.94,95 In tumors, by comparison, M2 macrophages in the immune-suppressive environment would not clear BB′CDF, enabling it to grow 100 times more than the initial dose (Figure 3B). The deletions in BB′CDF reduced the pro-inflammatory cytokines, especially IL-6 and TNF-α, that were produced after systemic injection (Figures 3I and 3J). This reduced toxicity increased the maximum dose of bacteria that could be injected from 1 × 107 CFU (for the parental strain) to 1 × 108 CFU (for BB′CDF, Figures 3G and 3L).

VDS has several distinct advantages over other therapeutic strategies. Because of its innate characteristics, Salmonella is uniquely suited to deliver viruses. As a universal platform, VDS has the potential to deliver many virus types, including double-stranded (dsDNA and dsRNA) and single-stranded (ssDNA and ssRNA) viruses. Unlike other bacterial species, Salmonella naturally detects the intracellular environment of mammalian cells.15 When the PsseJ promoter in VDS detects the intracellular environment, it triggers lysis and releases the viral genome (Figures 1F and 1G). Compared to previously explored virus carriers, which include T cells, monocytes, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and neural stem cells,9,11 VDS has significantly higher accumulation rates. For these cells, tumor accumulation is rate limiting, and most have rates below 10%.9,11 Compared to other bacterial systems designed to deliver viruses,29 VDS has several advantages, including the ability to deliver any virus that can be encoded on a plasmid (as opposed to only single-stranded RNA viruses), coordinated induction of both innate and adaptive immune responses (Figures 7 and 8), and increased safety due to intracellular lysis and the recB deletion (Figure 3). Compared to other bacterial therapies, only a few bacteria are required to initiate a tumor-localized virus infection, suggesting that the number of colonized bacteria do not affect efficacy. Ultimately, the release mechanisms built into VDS make it non-toxic and self-limiting. When Salmonella with the PsseJ-lysE circuit is injected into mice, intracellular lysis eliminates the bacteria and prevents unwanted infections.15

Bacterial delivery could also reduce the manufacturing costs associated with OVs. To make a fully functional virus, mammalian cells are infected with viral particles or transfected with a plasmid containing the viral genome.96,97 After a few days, cells are harvested and lysed to release viruses.96,97 DNA and protein contaminations are removed and incomplete virus-like particles are removed using an iodixanol gradient.96,97 Throughout the separation, the virus titer is determined by plaque assay on mammalian cells or by quantitative reverse-transcription PCR.96,97 The multiple intricate steps in this process make it long, tedious, and costly. Transforming the viral plasmid into Salmonella circumvents these problems. Culturing bacteria is easier and less expensive than mammalian cells. To manufacture virus-delivering bacteria, virus particles do not have to be formed or purified, as the virus is completely encoded on a bacterial plasmid.

Here, we have demonstrated that delivery of OVs with engineered bacteria can induce antitumor immune responses (Figures 6, 7, and 8). Using Salmonella to deliver parvovirus couples the benefits of both modalities and creates a therapy that is more effective than either alone. The combination of these two microbes circumvents the problems associated with each. After i.v. injection, Salmonella protects the virus, carries it to tumors, and deposits it in cancer cells (Figure 2). After initial colonization, bacteria grow rapidly, dramatically increasing the dose in tumors (Figure 3B). Similarly, viral replication is a key component of VDS treatment. Once virus particles are formed, they expand the infection by invading neighboring cells, inducing lysis, and releasing more viruses (Figure 2). This replication increases the size of the affected region and amplifies the therapeutic effect. To complement the activation of innate immune response by Salmonella, H-1PV recruits adaptive immune cells (Figures 7 and 8) and promotes the discovery of tumor antigens (Figure 6). All of these effects are aided by the base virus delivery strain (BB′CDF), which accumulates in tumors 50 million times more than healthy tissue (Figure 3). Rapid clearance of this strain from healthy organs ensures that VDS is safe (Figure 3) and can be administered multiple times (Figures 4 and 5). Together these characteristics suggest that VDS has clinical potential. Ultimately, the synergy between Salmonella and H-1PV creates a therapy that activates the immune system, generates anti-tumor immunity, and has the potential to treat patients with many tumor types.

Limitations of the study

This study provides evidence that Salmonella-mediated delivery of the OV H-1PV reduces tumors and generates antitumor immunity. There are several limitations. All in vivo experiments were performed in subcutaneous tumor models, which, while experimentally tractable, do not fully recapitulate the extracellular matrix architecture, stromal density, or immune contexture characteristic of orthotopic tumors. Another limitation is that although CD8+ T cells were enriched and activated following treatment, we did not determine their contribution to tumor regression. We did not calculate the virion production rate and the virus replication rate in newly infected cells, obscuring a precise determination of effect per virus particle. Finally, off-target viral delivery and replication in non-tumor tissues were not assessed, leaving the broader biodistribution of bacterially delivered H-1PV uncharacterized.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

Seven mammalian cell lines were used throughout: HEK-293T human embryonic kidney cells (RRID:CVCL_0063; ATCC, Manassas, VA), HuH7 human hepatocellular carcinoma cells (RRID:CVCL_0336; Cytion), Hep3B human hepatocellular carcinoma cells (RRID: CVCL_0326; ATCC, Manassas, VA), Sk-Hep-1 human liver adenocarcinoma (RRID: CVCL_0525; ATCC, Manassas, VA), Hepa1-6 mouse hepatoma cells (RRID:CVCL_0327; ATCC, Manassas, VA), MCF-7 human breast adenocarcinoma cells (RRID:CVCL_3397; ATCC, Manassas, VA), and KPCY murine pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells (RRID:CVCL_YM32; Kerafast, Boston, MA). Five of the cell lines (HEK-293T, HuH7, Hepa1-6, MCF-7 and KPCY) were maintained in low glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) with 3.7 g/L sodium bicarbonate (pH 7.4) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Cytiva, Marlborough, MA) at 37°C and 5% CO2. The other two cell lines (Hep3B and Sk-Hep-1) were maintained in minimum essential medium (MEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) supplemented with 1.5 g/L of sodium bicarbonate and 10% FBS at 37°C and 5% CO2. The cell lines were authenticated by Short Tandem Repeat (STR) analysis. All cells were negative for mycoplasma contamination.

Bacterial strains

Four bacterial strains were used throughout, two derived strains (BB’CDF and CDS), parental Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium (VNP20009; ΔmsbB, ΔpurI, Δxyl), and the SURE 2 (stop unwanted rearrangement events) strain of E. coli. All bacterial cultures were grown in LB media containing 10 g/L tryptone, 10 g/L sodium chloride, and 5 g/L yeast extract. Resistant strains of bacteria were grown in the presence of 100 μg/mL of carbenicillin, 33 μg/mL chloramphenicol, or 50 μg/mL of kanamycin.

Plasmids

The H-1PV and mH-1PV plasmids were provided by Dr. Jürg Nüesh (German Cancer Research Center, Heidelberg, Germany) and contains ampicillin resistance. The p27 plasmid contains the Lysin E gene from ΦX174 bacteriophage under control of the intracellular responsive PsseJ promoter.88 The PsseJ-lysE gene circuit induces bacterial lysis after cell invasion and delivers the viral plasmid into cells.82 The p27 plasmid also contains the asd gene, chloramphenicol resistance, green fluorescent protein (GFP) under the ptac promoter, and a p15A origin of replication.88 The asd gene maintains the plasmid in vivo in Δasd bacterial strains without antibiotics.15 For immunocytochemistry plasmid used contains the Lysin E gene from ΦX174 bacteriophage under control of the intracellular responsive PsseJ promoter,88 the asd gene, chloramphenicol resistance, green fluorescent protein (GFP) under the plac promoter, and a ColE1 origin of replication.

Animal models

Three animal tumor models were used: Hepa1-6 tumors and KPCY tumors in C57BL/6J mice (Mus musculus); HuH7 tumors in NU/J 002019 mice; and Hep3B tumors in NU/J 002019 mice. Mice were ordered from Jackson laboratories (Bar Harbor, ME) and kept under a 12 h light/dark cycle at room temperature of 72°F and relative humidity of 60%. For all models, tumors were formed by injecting 1×106 cells (Hepa1-6, KPCY, HuH7 or Hep3B) subcutaneously in 5–6 weeks female mice (C57BL/6J or NU/J 002019). Delivery of the viral plasmid and the immune responses to VDS were determined after intratumoral injections. The efficacy and biodistribution of VDS was determined after intravenous injections. Toxicity and serum pro-inflammatory cytokine levels were determined in tumor-free mice. All animal experiments were approved by the University of Massachusetts, Amherst, Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Experiments were performed under IACUC protocol 4212.

Method details

Bacterial gene deletions

The BB’CDF and CDS strains were derived from parental Salmonella. BB’CDF has four deletions of recombinase genes (recB, sbcB, sbcCD and recF) to prevent homologous recombination of the virus genome.82 It also has a deletion of an essential gene, asd, to prevent plasmid loss without the use of antibiotics.99 This gene is required for bacterial cell wall synthesis.99 In the absence of asd, Salmonella spontaneously lyses. Lysis can be prevented by incorporating asd on plasmids or supplementing media with diaminopimelic acid (DAP, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA).99 The CDS strain has one recombinase deletion (sbcCD), an asd deletion, and a sseJ deletion to prevent escape of the Salmonella from intracellular vacuoles and increase plasmid delivery.88 An alternate BB’CDF-S strain was created by deleting sseJ from BB’CDF.

All deletions were performed sequentially starting with parental Salmonella. Lambda red recombineering100 was performed to delete each gene. Starting strains were transformed with pKD46 (Yale CGSC E. Coli stock center), grown to OD600 = 0.1, and induced with 10 mM arabinose. When the OD600 reached 0.6–0.8, the bacteria were transferred to a pre-cooled 50 mL conical tube and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min. Once pelleted, the supernatant was removed and 50 mL of distilled, ice-cold water was added. Bacteria were gently pipetted up and down until completely resuspended and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min. This procedure was repeated one more time and the final bacterial pellet was resuspended in 500 μL of distilled ice-cold water. A PCR product for the in-frame deletion of the gene was amplified from the pKD4 plasmid (GenBank: AY048743.1) using primers for recB, sbcB, sbcCD and recF,82 or asd and sseJ.15 The PCR product, which contains 50 bp homology for the gene, was transformed into Salmonella using electroporation and recovered for 2 h in 1 mL of LB. The recovery was plated on kanamycin plates (50 μg/mL) and grown overnight. Colonies were screened for deletion by colony PCR of the junction sites of the inserted PCR-amplified products. Successful knockouts were grown overnight at 43°C to eliminate pKD46. To remove the kanamycin resistance, the bacteria were transformed with the pCP20 plasmid and recovered at 30°C. The colonies were then grown at 43°C to remove the pCP20 plasmid. Cultures were diluted and streaked on hektoen, kanamycin, and chloramphenicol agar plates to (1) confirm the species (Salmonella are dark green on hektoen), (2) confirm removal of kanamycin resistance in the genome, and (3) confirm removal of the chloramphenicol-resistant pCP20 plasmid.

Virus-delivering Salmonella

To generate VDS, BB’CDF was transformed with a plasmid containing the H-1PV genome and a second plasmid (p27) that controls delivery. A second virus-delivering strain (VDS-C) was formed by similarly transforming CDS with p27 and the H-1PV plasmid. Plasmids were transformed into Salmonella strains by electroporation using 1 mm cuvettes at 1800 V and 25 μF with a time constant of 5 msec.

Hairpin stability

To test hairpin stability, the H-1PV plasmid was isolated from VDS and VDS-C. As a control, H-1PV was transformed into supercompetent SURE 2 using heat shock. Plasmids were extracted from the Salmonella and E. coli strains using the ZymoPURE plasmid miniprep kit per the manufacturer’s instructions (Irvine, CA). To measure hairpin stability, isolated plasmids were digested with the SSPI and EcoRI restriction enzymes (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA) and sequenced (Massachusetts General Hospital CCIB DNA core facility). To measure virus production, isolated plasmids were transfected into HEK-293T cells to measure the NS1 protein. Plasmid was transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 following the manufacturer’s protocol (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA). Thirty-six hours after transfection, cells were scraped from flasks. Cells were collected and pelleted by centrifugation at 1000×g for 5 min at 4°C. The media was removed, and cells were washed once with PBS and resuspended in Laemmli SDS 6x sample buffer (Alfa Aesar, Haverhill, MA). Samples were boiled for 10 min at 95°C, loaded onto NuPage 4–12% Bis-Tris gels (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) and separated by electrophoresis. Proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA) that were blocked with 5% BSA in Tris-buffered saline solution with Tween 20 (TBS-T) for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were probed using mouse monoclonal anti-NS1 (gift from Dr. Kinjal Majumder) at a 1/500 dilution in blocking buffer overnight at 4°C. Blots were washed with TBS-T 2X and incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (R&D Systems, Catalog # HAF007; RRID:AB_357234; 1/1000 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature. The blots were washed again two times with TBS-T and visualized using Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP substrate (Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA) on an ImageQuant LAS4000 imaging system (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL).

Bacterial invasion, immunocytochemistry, and microscopy

Murine cancer cells were grown on coverslips and infected with Salmonella transformed with the PsseJ-lysE gene circuit at an MOI of 10 for 2 h at 37°C. Control cultures were incubated with Salmonella without the PsseJ-lysE circuit. Two hours after infection, cells were washed five times with PBS and incubated with media containing 50 μg/mL of gentamicin to remove extracellular bacteria. Fifteen hours after infection, the media was aspirated, and the cells were fixed with 10% formalin for 10 min at room temperature. The cells were then blocked with an intracellular staining buffer (PBS with 0.1% Tween 20, 1 mM EDTA, and 2% bovine serum albumin) for 30 min. Tween 20 permeabilizes mammalian cell membranes, while leaving bacterial membranes intact. Coverslips were stained with FITC-conjugated rabbit anti-Salmonella polyclonal antibody (Abcam, catalog # ab69253; RRID:AB_1270507;1:100 dilution), rat anti-myc monoclonal antibody (Chromotek, catalog # 9e1-100; RRID:AB_2631398;1:200 dilution) for one hour. After adding the primary antibody, the coverslips were washed twice with intracellular staining buffer. Sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 568 goat anti-rat secondary antibodies (ThermoFisher, catalog # A11077; RRID:AB_2534121) for one hour and then washed twice with intracellular staining buffer. Following the washes, the coverslips were counterstained with DAPI-containing mountant (ThermoFisher, catalog #P36962). Samples were imaged on a Zeiss Axio Observer Z.1 microscope. The fixed cells on the coverslips were imaged with a 20X objective. Automated analysis was performed in MATLAB (Mathworks) with the threshold calculated as two standard deviations over the mean of the background fluorescence intensity.

Virus delivery

To measure virus production, VDS and VDS-C were co-cultured with HuH7, Hepa1-6 and KPCY cells. The bacterial strains were grown in LB with carbenicillin, chloramphenicol, and kanamycin antibiotics. After the culture reached an OD600 between 0.8 and 1.0, the bacteria were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 1 min. The supernatant was removed and the pellet was resuspended in PBS. The bacteria were added to cultures of HuH7, Hepa1-6 and KPCY cells which were 70–80% confluent at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100 for two hours. After 2 h of invasion, cells were washed with PBS three times and extracellular bacteria were removed with 50 μg/mL of gentamicin. For HuH7 cells, the H-1PV plasmid was transfected into naive cells with Lipofectamine 3000 as a positive control. Uninfected cells were used as negative control. Thirty-six hours after infection or transfection, cells were scraped from the flasks. Cells were collected and pelleted by centrifugation at 1000×g for 5 min at 4°C. The media was removed, and cells were washed with PBS once and resuspended in Laemmli SDS 6x sample buffer (Alfa Aesar, Haverhill, MA). Samples were boiled for 10 min at 95°C, loaded onto NuPage 4–12% Bis-Tris gels (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) and separated using electrophoresis. Proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA). The membranes were blocked with 5% BSA in tris-buffered saline solution with Tween 20 (TBS-T) for one hour at room temperature. The membranes were probed using mouse monoclonal anti-NS1 (gift from Dr. Kinjal Majumder) at a 1/250 dilution in blocking buffer overnight at 4°C. Blots were washed with TBS-T twice and incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (R&D Systems, Catalog # HAF007; RRID:AB_357234; 1/500 dilution) for one hour at room temperature. The blots were washed again two times with TBS-T and visualized using Immobilon Western chemiluminescent HRP substrate (Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA) on an ImageQuant LAS4000 imaging system (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL). For HuH7 cells, the membranes were probed with polyclonal anti-NS1 (gift from Dr. Jürg Nüesh) at 1/500 dilution and then incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Invitrogen, Catalog # 31466; RRID: AB_2533967; 1/1000 dilution).

Cell viability

Cell viability was determined by administering VDS and VDS-C to cultures of HuH7, Hepa1-6, KPCY, Hep3B, Sk-He-1, MCF7 cells. Four bacterial strains, VDS, VDS-C, BB’CDF and CDS (as bacterial controls) were co-cultured with cells that were 78% confluent for two hours. Cells transfected with the H-1PV plasmid were used as positive controls. Bacteria were co-cultured with cells at different MOIs ranging from 25 to 150. Two hours after bacterial infection, the cells were washed with PBS three times and extracellular bacteria were removed with 50 μg/mL of gentamicin. Seventy-two hours after infection/transfection, cells were incubated with 5 mg/mL MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) solution (Themo Fisher, Waltham, MA). After two hours, the supernatant was discarded and the cells were solubilized using 500 μL of isopropanol and 25 N HCl solution. Absorbance was calculated at 570 nm (Spectramax ID3). Three wells (biological replicates) were used for each condition, and three technical replicates were used from each well. Cell viability was calculated relative to the appropriate bacterial controls (BB’CDF or CDS).

Virus delivery in vivo

Three mouse models (Hepa1-6, HuH7 or Hep3B) were used to measure delivery of H-1PV by VDS and VDS-C. Tumors were formed by subcutaneously injecting 1×106 cells/mouse into female C57BL/6J or NU/J 002019 mice. Cultures of VDS and VDS-C were grown to OD600 between 0.8 and 1.0. One mL of bacteria was centrifuged at 1000×g for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded and the bacteria were washed twice with PBS. The final pellet was resuspended in PBS so that 25 μL contained 4 × 107 CFU bacteria. These bacteria (4 × 107 CFU of VDS or VDS-C) were injected intratumorally. Saline (25 μL) was injected intratumorally into control mice. Every day, mice injected with VDS and VDS-C were injected subcutaneously with 10 mg of ampicillin sodium salt (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA) suspended in sterile water. Ampicillin injection ensured maintenance of the H-1PV viral plasmid in the Salmonella. Five days after bacterial injection, mice were euthanized and tumors were excised. Tumors were flash frozen with liquid nitrogen and crushed using mortar and pestle. Radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer was prepared using the cold spring harbor protocol.101 Phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF; serine protease inhibitor, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA) reconstituted in 100% ethanol was added to the RIPA buffer at a concentration of 1 mM. One microliter of RIPA buffer with PMSF was added per 1 mg of tumor. One microliter of benzonase (Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA) was added to 1 mL of tumor slurry. The tumor slurry was placed on a shaker for two hours at 4°C and constant agitation. After two hours, the suspension was centrifuged at 17,000×g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was removed and placed in a fresh tube kept on ice. The pellet was discarded. Supernatant was mixed with Laemmli SDS 6x sample buffer. Samples were boiled for 10 min at 95°C, loaded onto NuPage 4–12% Bis-Tris gels and separated using electrophoresis to measure NS1 and VP1.

To measure the formation of functional virions, the homogenate from injected Hepa1-6 tumors were applied to HEK-293T cells. Half of the frozen and crushed tumor powder was suspended in 1 mL PBS. The tumor-PBS solution was dounced five times using a loose dounce tissue grinder. Once the tumor completely turned into a slurry, it was put in a fresh tube and centrifuged at 5000×g for 10 min at 4°C. After two freeze thaw cycles, the slurry was again centrifuged at 5000×g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected, and the pellet was discarded. One milliliter of supernatant was mixed with fresh DMEM and added to HEK-293T cells with 50 μg/mL of gentamicin. Forty-eight hours after adding the tumor homogenate, the cells were evaluated for NS1 using the immunoblot procedure described above.

Tumor colonization and biodistribution

To measure tumor colonization and its biodistribution, BB’CDF and the parental Salmonella strain were injected into mice with Hepa1-6 tumors. Both the BB’CDF and parental strains were transformed with the p27 plasmid and grown in LB to an OD600 between 0.8 and 1.0. One milliliter of each bacterial culture was centrifuged at 1000×g for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded and the pellet was washed twice with PBS. The final pellet was resuspended in PBS so that 100 μL contained 1 × 107 CFU of bacteria. Mice with subcutaneous size-matched 500–600 mm3 Hepa1-6 tumors were intravenously injected with 1 × 107 CFU BB’CDF or parental Salmonella. Seventy-two hours after injection, mice were euthanized and the tumors, livers, spleens, kidneys, hearts, lungs, and brains were excised. All the organs and tumors were weighed, equal volumes of PBS were added (1μL/mg), and the tissues were homogenized. The homogenates were diluted by 10-fold serial dilutions, and 100 μL was added to agar plates containing 33 μg/mL chloramphenicol. The following day, colonies were counted and the dilution which had more than 10 colonies was used for calculating colonization. Bacterial density (CFU/g) was calculated using the formula 2×(# of colonies)×(dilution factor). Fold change was calculated as the ratio of bacteria in tumors to those in the liver or spleen, whichever is higher.

Toxicity and maximum tolerable dose

To evaluate safety, BB’CDF and VDS were injected into tumor-free mice. VDS was compared to saline to determine its overall safety. BB’CDF was compared to the parental Salmonella strain to measure the beneficial effects of the added gene deletion. For all bacterial conditions, 1 × 107 CFU were injected intravenously in 100 μL of saline. Mice injected with VDS were injected with 10 mg of ampicillin sodium salt solution subcutaneously every day to maintain the H-1PV plasmid. Mice were weighed before the injection (day 0) and every day until seven days after the injections. After day 7 days, blood was collected using cardiac puncture and the mice were euthanized. Blood was centrifuged in heparin tubes at 1500×g for 10 min at 4°C. Serum was collected and analyzed for pro-inflammatory cytokines by ELISA (samples sent to Eve technologies, Calgary).

To determine the maximum tolerable dose (MTD), 5×107 and 1 × 108 CFU of the BB’CDF and parental strains were intravenously injected in tumor-free mice. Saline (100 μL) was injected intravenously as a negative control. Mice were weighed before the injection (day 0) and every day until seven days after the injections. On day 7, a second dose of 5×107 or 1×108 was administered and mouse weights were measured until day 12. During the study, if the weights dropped more than 20%, the mice were euthanized as stipulated by the protocol.

Tumor volumes and mouse survival

The efficacy of the strategy was determined by injecting VDS into tumor-bearing mice. Two separate procedures were performed following either intratumoral (IT) or intravenous (IV) injections of 4 × 107 CFU of VDS into C57BL/6J mice with subcutaneous hepatoma (Hepa1-6) tumors. The efficacy of virus delivery was determined by comparison to BB’CDF bacterial controls, which lack the H-1PV plasmid. In the intravenous procedure, a third group of mice were intraperitonially injected with 10 mg/kg of Sorafenib. To prepare the bacteria, BB’CDF and VDS were grown in LB to an OD600 between 0.8 and 1.0. One milliliter of bacterial culture was centrifuged at 1000×g for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the bacteria were washed twice with PBS. The final pellet was resuspended in PBS so that final volume (25 μL for intratumoral and 100 μL for intravenous injection) contained 4 × 107 CFU of bacteria. Mice treated with VDS were subcutaneously injected with 10 mg of ampicillin sodium salt solution daily. Tumors were measured by caliper every three days and the volumes were calculated using the formula (length)∗(width)2. All tumor measurements were blinded: an independent researcher measured tumor volumes without knowing the treatment that the mice had received. Mice were removed from the studies when tumor volumes were greater than 1000 mm3. Injections of VDS, BB’CDF and Sorafenib were given every six days until the last control mice died. Tumor growth rates were calculated by fitting individual tumor volumes to a time-varying exponential function.

Adoptive transfer of splenocytes

To measure the formation of tumor-specific immunity, splenocytes from VDS-treated mice were adoptively transferred into naive mice and tumor growth was measured after implanting tumor cells. VDS was prepared by growing in LB to an OD600 between 0.8 and 1.0. One milliliter of bacterial culture was centrifuged at 1000×g for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the bacteria were washed twice with PBS. The final pellet was resuspended in PBS so that the injected volume (25 μL) contained 4 × 107 CFU of bacteria. C57BL/6J mice with subcutaneous Hepa1-6 tumors were intratumorally injected with three doses of 4 × 107 CFU of VDS, six days apart (on days 0, 6 and 12). Mice were subcutaneously injected with 10 mg of ampicillin sodium salt solution every day to maintain the H-1PV plasmid. Five days after the last injection, mice were euthanized and spleens were excised. Using the end of a syringe plunger, spleens were gently pulverized in in a six-well plate containing 5 mL of DMEM with 10% FBS. The cell suspension was filtered through a 100 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 500×g for 10 min. After removing the supernatant, the cell pellet was resuspended in 4 mL of complete DMEM. The cell suspension was gently added to the top of 4 mL of density gradient media (Ficoll Paque, density 1.084 g/mL; Cytiva, Marlborough, MA) in a 15 mL conical tube. The tubes (containing cell, media and ficoll) were centrifuged at 500×g and 20°C for 20 min. The acceleration was set to low and brake was turned off. After the centrifugation, the peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) layer was gently isolated, washed with PBS, and injected intraperitonially into naive C57BL/6J mice. Fourteen days after splenocyte injection, 2.5×106 Hepa1-6 cells were injected subcutaneously and monitored for tumor growth every seven days for 56 days. After 56 days, mice were rechallenged with another 2.5×106 of Hepa1-6 cells on the opposite flank and tumor growth were monitored on both flanks for another 40 days.

Tumor dissociation, flow cytometry, and immune analysis

Tumor-bearing mice were injected with VDS to determine the immune response to virus delivery.

Bacteria (BB’CDF and VDS) were prepared by growing in LB to an OD600 between 0.8 and 1.0. One milliliter of bacterial culture was centrifuged at 1000×g for 10 min, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in PBS so that the injection volume (25 μL) contained 4 × 107 CFU of bacteria. Two separate procedures were performed with either subcutaneous Hepa1-6 (hepatoma) tumors or subcutaneous KPCY (pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma) tumors. Mice with subcutaneous Hepa1-6 tumors were intratumorally injected three times with 4 × 107 CFU of VDS or control BB’CDF six days apart (on days 0, 6 and 12). Mice with subcutaneous KPCY tumors were intravenously injected two times with (1) 4 × 107 CFU of VDS, (2) 4 × 107 CFU of BB’CDF (bacterial control), or (3) 4×107 plaque-forming units (PFU) of H-1PV (viral control) six days apart (on days 0 and 6). Every day, VDS-treated mice were subcutaneously injected with 10 mg of ampicillin sodium salt. Five days after the last injection, mice were euthanized and tumors were excised.

Tumor tissue was minced with a sterile knife in 5 mL of DMEM with 10% FBS. For Hepa-16 and KPCY tumors, necrosis was removed prior to mincing the tumors. Tumor tissue was pulverized using the end of a syringe plunger, until most of the tissue was between 1 and 2 mm on its longest diameter. The tumor and media slurry was mixed with collagenase D solution to a final concentration of 1 mg/mL. One microliter of benzonase was added to the slurry. The mixture was placed in a douncer with a loose pestle and dounced five times until slurry was broken into pieces smaller than 2 mm in diameter. The slurry was incubated for two hours at 37°C and 5% CO2. The tumor slurry was then filtered through a 100 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 500×g and 4°C for 10 min. The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was washed twice with PBS. Cells were fixed with 2% formaldehyde, 1 mM EDTA in PBS for 15 min at room temperature and constant shaking. After 15 min, the cells were centrifuged at 1000×g for 10 min. After the supernatant was removed, cells were washed with 1 mM EDTA in PBS. Cells were blocked overnight in extracellular staining buffer (2% BSA, 1 mM EDTA in 1X PBS) using Fc block (CD16/CD32 bispecific antibody; 1:200, RRID: AB_312800).

Cells were stained for extracellular markers overnight using extracellular staining buffer and one of three antibody panels. To measure innate immune cells in Hepa1-6 tumor model, the dissociated tumor cells were extracellularly stained with anti-CD45 (APC, 1:500, RRID: AB_2876537), anti-F4/80 (APC-Cy7, 1:200, RRID: AB_893489), anti-CD11c (FITC, 1:200, RRID: AB_313774), anti-NKp46 (brilliant violet 421, 1:500, RRID: AB_2563104), anti-CD80 (brilliant violet 605, 1:500, RRID: AB_11126141), and anti-206 (PE Dazzle 594, 1:500, RRID: AB_2565931). To measure adaptive immune cells, the dissociated tumor cells were extracellularly stained with anti-CD45 (APC, 1:500, RRID: AB_2876537), anti-CD3 (brilliant violet 421, 1:500, RRID: AB_10900227), anti-CD8 (Alexa Flour 488, 1:200, RRID: AB_493423), anti-CD4 (APC-Cy7, 1:200, RRID: AB_312699), and anti-CD25 (brilliant violet 605, 1:500, RRID: AB_11126977). To measure the activation of CD8 T cells, the dissociated tumor cells were extracellularly stained with anti-CD45 (APC-Cy7, 1:200, RRID: AB_312980), anti-CD3 (brilliant violet 421, 1:500, RRID: AB_10900227), anti-CD8 (APC, 1:500, RRID: AB_312750), and anti-CD44 (FITC, 1:200, RRID: AB_2941437). To measure immune response in the KPCY tumor model, dissociated tumor cells were extracellularly stained with anti-CD45 (APC/Cyanine7, 1:200, RRID:AB_312980), anti-F4/80 (brilliant Violet 570, 1:200, RRID: AB_3106088), anti-CD11c (brilliant Violet 785, 1:400, RRID: AB_11219204), anti-CD80 (PE/Cyanine5, 1:400, RRID: AB_313132), anti-CD3 (brilliant violet 421, 1:400, RRID: AB_10900227), and anti-CD8 (PE, 1:400, RRID: AB_312746). Gating was performed by using florescence minus ones (FMOs). All antibodies are from Biolegend.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in GraphPad Prism 9.2.0. Comparisons of two populations were made with two-tailed, unpaired t-tests with Welch’s correction, not assuming equal standard deviations. Multiple conditions were compared using ordinary one-way ANOVA with either Bonferroni or Dunnett’s correction. Outliers were removed using Grubbs' test, with an alpha of 0.05. To compare survival for two populations, the log rank test (Mantel-Cox) was used. To compare survival between multiple populations, the log rank test (Mantel-Cox) with Bonferroni correction was used. All measurements were taken from distinct samples. Values are reported as means ± standard error of the means (SEMs). Statistical analyses are described in the corresponding figure legends. A p value <0.05 was considered significant. Significance levels are denoted as: ∗ (p < 0.05), ∗∗ (p < 0.01), ∗∗∗ (p < 0.001), and ∗∗∗∗ (p < 0.0001).
