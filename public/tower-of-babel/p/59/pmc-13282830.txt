Upper-Extremity Deep Vein Thrombosis in Cancer
Authors: António Araújo, José Costa, Ana Rodrigues
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Cancer-associated thrombosis (CAT) remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in oncology, representing a major challenge at the interface between cancer biology and vascular medicine. While lower-extremity deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism have traditionally dominated research and guidelines development, upper-extremity deep vein thrombosis (UEDVT) has remained comparatively understudied despite its increasing incidence in contemporary oncology practice. In this issue of JACC: CardioOncology, Espitia et al.1 provide important insights into the clinical course of cancer-associated UEDVT, challenging the longstanding perception of this condition as a relatively benign or predominantly catheter-related complication.

Using data from the RIETE registry, the authors demonstrate that cancer-associated UEDVT is associated with substantially higher risks of recurrent venous thromboembolism and bleeding compared with non-cancer UEDVT.1 More importantly, they show that these risks are not homogeneous across malignancies. Lung cancer was associated with the highest recurrence risk, whereas genitourinary malignancies carried the greatest bleeding burden. These observations reinforce a growing body of evidence suggesting that CAT should not be viewed as a single clinical entity, but rather as a spectrum of tumor-specific vascular phenotypes.2,3

This concept is particularly relevant in UEDVT. Historically, UEDVT has frequently been interpreted through a largely mechanical framework centered on central venous access devices, endothelial injury, and venous stasis. However, the findings by Espitia et al.1 suggest that tumor biology may be at least as important as catheter-related factors in determining clinical outcomes. The persistence of tumor site as an independent predictor of recurrence and bleeding after adjustment for catheter-related thrombosis supports the notion that UEDVT may represent the vascular expression of systemic cancer biology rather than merely a localized thrombotic complication.

The markedly elevated recurrence observed in lung cancer is biologically plausible. Lung tumors are characterized by strong prothrombotic properties mediated through tissue factor expression, inflammatory cytokine release, platelet activation, and circulating procoagulant microparticles.4 Conversely, the higher bleeding rates observed in genitourinary malignancies likely reflect a combination of mucosal vulnerability, local tumor invasion, and frequent instrumentation. Together, these competing risks illustrate the central therapeutic dilemma in CAT: Patients at greatest risk of thrombosis are often simultaneously at greatest risk of bleeding.

Importantly, the study also identifies anticoagulation duration as a major determinant of post-treatment recurrence. Patients receiving anticoagulation—particularly beyond 180 days—experienced significantly lower recurrence after treatment discontinuation.1 At first glance, these findings may support extended anticoagulation strategies in selected high-risk patients, particularly those with lung cancer. However, caution is warranted before interpreting these observations as evidence of causality.

As with all observational registry analyses, residual confounding remains a major concern. Patients able to continue anticoagulation for prolonged periods likely represent a biologically and clinically selected population with lower competing mortality, better performance status, improved cancer control, and greater tolerance to anticoagulant therapy. Consequently, the apparent protective effect of longer anticoagulation may partly reflect patient selection rather than treatment duration itself. In this context, the findings should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing.

Another important consideration relates to temporal heterogeneity within the RIETE cohort. The study spans more than 2 decades, during which the management of CAT evolved profoundly, particularly with the increasing adoption of direct oral anticoagulants.5 Although contemporary guidelines now recognize direct oral anticoagulants as acceptable alternatives to low-molecular-weight heparins in selected patients, bleeding risk remains a major concern in gastrointestinal and genitourinary malignancies.5 The absence of granular analyses comparing anticoagulant strategies across tumor types limits the ability to determine whether the observed risk gradients were influenced by treatment evolution over time.

Nonetheless, the clinical implications of this work are considerable. First, the study strongly supports incorporation of tumor type into routine thrombotic and bleeding risk assessment. Current clinical decision-making in CAT still relies heavily on generalized approaches that insufficiently account for tumor-specific biology. The present data reinforce the need for a more individualized framework integrating cancer type, bleeding susceptibility, catheter status, treatment response, and competing mortality risk.

Second, the findings highlight the importance of dynamic reassessment throughout the anticoagulation course. Decisions regarding continuation or discontinuation of therapy should not be based solely on arbitrary treatment durations, but rather on evolving oncologic status and individualized risk-benefit evaluation. This is particularly relevant in cardio-oncology, where thrombotic, hemorrhagic, and treatment-related cardiovascular risks frequently intersect.

Finally, this study also raises broader conceptual questions regarding the nature of CAT itself. Increasingly, thrombotic events in cancer appear less as isolated vascular complications and more as integrated manifestations of tumor-host interaction. From this perspective, UEDVT may represent another dimension of systemic cancer biology, shaped by inflammation, coagulation activation, endothelial dysfunction, and treatment-related factors. Understanding these interactions will be essential for developing truly precision-based thrombosis care.

The work by Espitia et al. represents an important step toward that objective. It challenges the traditional view of UEDVT as a relatively low-risk condition and instead portrays a biologically heterogeneous and clinically consequential disease state in which thrombosis and bleeding coexist in dynamic tension. The next challenge for the field is not merely to define the optimal duration of anticoagulation, but to develop a precision-based approach capable of identifying which patients truly benefit from extended therapy and which are more likely to be harmed by it.

In the evolving landscape of cardio-oncology, the challenge is no longer merely to treat the clot, but to understand the cancer that drives it.
