Opening the black box of co-creation: exploring process delivery, stakeholder experiences and perceived outcomes in the development of a family-based healthy weight development intervention for infants
Authors: Lene Kierkegaard, Camilla Thørring Bonnesen, Rikke Rothkegel Carlsson, Trine Pagh Pedersen, Mette Rasmussen, Nanna Gram Ahlmark, Rikke Fredenslund Krølner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCo-creation has gained prominence in public health intervention research, as stakeholder involvement is expected to strengthen implementation and effectiveness. Its growing popularity highlights the need for a critical examination of how co-creation is conceptualized and operationalized. This study examines: (1) the delivery of the co-creation process; (2) stakeholders’ experienced involvement; and (3) perceived outcomes, including how the process shaped the intervention design and stakeholders’ perceived value of participation. The co-creation centred on developing The Bloom Study, an early intervention to promote healthy weight development amongst infants integrated in the Danish community health nurse system.MethodsThe co-creation activities comprised six workshops and eight group meetings with a Participatory Planning Group of community health nurses, researchers and facilitators, supplemented by four parent group interviews. The evaluation followed a qualitative research design, combining multiple data sources: transcripts from co-creation activities, a joint oral evaluation and individual interviews with the Participatory Planning Group participants. Analysis was guided by the PROcesS Evaluation framework for CO-creation (PROSECO) framework.Results(1) The co-creation process was delivered as intended, though parental involvement was challenging. (2) Participants perceived themselves as highly involved, but still viewed the intervention primarily as the researchers’ project. The Participatory Planning Group demonstrated strong motivation and constructive group dynamics. Despite initial uncertainty about their roles, they felt they were able to meaningfully influence the intervention. (3) The Participatory Planning Group valued the co-creation process as a reflective space that enabled knowledge exchange. The intervention was shaped through iterative negotiation, integration of research and practice knowledge, and adaptation to contextual constraints. Although parental involvement was limited, their input helped refine materials and address parental needs.ConclusionsThis study provides empirical insight into how co-creation unfolds within a health system context. Meaningful stakeholder involvement was constrained by structural and temporal conditions tied to the research project, including fixed timelines. Despite these limitations, the process supported contextually grounded adaptations, strengthened stakeholder ownership and enhanced responsiveness to local needs. The critical application of the PROSECO framework helped “opening the black box” of co-creation and offers implications for health systems, particularly regarding how participatory approaches can inform implementation practices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12961-026-01486-1.

Background

Co-creation has become a buzzword within public health intervention research, aiming to address complex issues such as overweight, low well-being and social inequality in health [1–3]. This is reflected in, amongst other things, funders and journals increasingly requesting involvement of stakeholders such as implementers and the target group in research projects [4, 5]. Furthermore, systematic planning approaches, for example, the Intervention Mapping (IM) protocol [1] and the Medical Research Council (MRC) framework for complex interventions [2], as well as the new Declaration of Helsinki [6], highlight the necessity of engaging stakeholders in the planning, development, implementation and evaluation of interventions [1, 2, 7].

Research on the value of co-creation in intervention development continues to grow [8–12]. Co-creation with the target group may improve the reach and appeal of intervention components, as they can be tailored to the target group’s specific needs [13–18], thereby increasing effectiveness of the intervention and its potential to reduce health inequalities [16, 18]. In addition, there is an increased risk of adverse unintended intervention outcomes if the problem definitions and their solutions do not consider the needs of the target group and implementers [1, 2, 15, 17]. Further, involving implementers in the co-creation process increases the likelihood of successful implementation, sustainability and scalability of interventions [8, 13, 19, 20].

The terminology and operationalization in intervention studies are diverse, with terms such as co-creation, co-production, co-design and participatory action research often used interchangeably, which challenges comparisons of results across studies [21]. In this article, we draw on the definition of co-creation by Vargas et al.: “Co-creation refers to the collaborative approach of creative problem solving between diverse stakeholders at all project stages” (2022, p. 2). Thus, we approach co-creation as an umbrella term for applying a broad range of participatory methods to make collaborative decisions in different phases of intervention development. This also encompasses co-design, which primarily refers to the collaborative design of an intervention that is tailored to the target group’s needs, expertise and knowledge, as well as co-production, which focusses on collaboration around implementation strategies and how to allocate resources [22, 23].

Whilst co-creation in intervention development is considered a promising strategy for tackling complex health challenges, such as childhood overweight and obesity, it is also a challenging approach to adopt [11]. It requires collaboration and consensus between researchers, target groups and implementers who may represent different perspectives, agendas, needs and institutional logics [8]. Conflicting priorities between researchers and stakeholders can challenge co-creation, potentially resulting in a power imbalance and undermining shared ownership [13, 19]. Furthermore, there is a risk of tokenism, in which stakeholders are only superficially involved and have limited influence over decision-making and minimal power to shape the intervention [21, 24].

Co-creation processes may reinforce social inequities, as individuals with greater resources and availability are more likely to engage, potentially resulting in interventions that disproportionately reflect the needs and perspectives of more advantaged groups [25]. Another challenge is its resource-intensive nature, which emphasizes the need for sufficient time and funding allocated to intervention development [19]. These challenges are particularly relevant when involving parents in infant weight‑related interventions, where recruitment, retention and discussions about sensitive issues can be a barrier [26–28]. Time pressures, stigma concerns and discomfort with weight‑related topics may therefore limit parental engagement, thereby challenging meaningful co‑creation.

There are no agreed-upon standards or recommendations for co-creation methods. They vary across nonacademic and academic fields, limiting the establishment of best practices [24]. Furthermore, studies are often not transparent in their facilitation of the co-creation process [3, 24, 29]. These inconsistencies in co‑creation research are also relevant in the early life context. Clarifying when and how parents can contribute meaningfully is essential to designing interventions that reflect family needs and are feasible in real‑world settings [26, 28].

Stakeholders may be involved in co-creation to varying degrees and at different points in the process. Moreover, it might not always be meaningful for every type of stakeholder to contribute to the same degree [10]. Flexibility and creativity are therefore needed during co-creation processes to consider when and how co-creation is possible [30].

The popularity of co-creation underscores the need to critically evaluate how the concept is understood and applied in practice, including its benefits and limitations. This includes examining how and to what extent different stakeholder groups are involved [5, 8, 12, 17], and the difference between the intentions of co-creation and what is realistic to achieve in real-life intervention contexts.

This study aims to critically explore how co-creation unfolds in practice in the development of a public health intervention, the Bloom Study. The overall aim of the Bloom Study is to develop, implement and evaluate healthy weight development amongst infants and toddlers of first-time parents through an early intervention integrated into the Danish community health nurse (CHN) system in Denmark. Specifically, we aim to investigate: (1) whether the co-creation process was delivered as intended; (2) how stakeholders perceived and experienced their involvement; and (3) perceived outcomes of the co-creation process, including stakeholders’ perceived value and costs of participation and the design and content of the final intervention.

Materials and methods

Empirical setting: the Bloom Study

The Bloom Study was based on the need for early, family-centred support that promotes healthy weight development during infancy. Building on multifactorial evidence that the first year of life is a critical period in which feeding practices, sleep routines, parental responsiveness and movement and motor skills shape long-term growth trajectories [27, 31, 32], the study addressed the challenges many families face in navigating these developmental tasks. In this study, we use the intervention development phase of the Bloom Study to explore the practical applications and implications of co-creation processes. The Bloom intervention was systematically developed over 3 years (2021–2023), guided by the six steps of the IM protocol [1], integrating co-creation in each step: (1) needs assessment, (2) program theory and matrices, (3) theoretical approaches and practical applications for modifiable determinants, (4) intervention tools, (5) program implementation plan, and (6) evaluation plan. For a detailed description of the scientific rationale for the Bloom Study and the use of IM, please see the protocol paper [33].

The Bloom intervention is integrated into the well-established, universal CHN system in Danish municipalities. According to CHN regulations, the standard service provided to families with an infant includes a minimum of five home visits during the child’s first year of life. It is estimated that almost all families receive visits by the CHNs, and data from 33 of the 98 Danish municipalities document that 93.2% of families accept the visit in the first month, and 85.5% of families receive a visit at 8–10 months [34]. The Bloom intervention includes additional home visits, telephone consultations, parent materials and parent groups. It is universal, with an integrated focus on reaching families across social diversities, including those of low socioeconomic position (SEP) and/or with an ethnic minority background. The primary target group is first-time parents and their children [33]. The effectiveness of the intervention will be tested in a cluster-randomized controlled trial (RCT) and combined with a comprehensive mixed-methods process evaluation [33].

The study object: overall co-creation process applied in the Bloom Study

The Bloom Study’s co-creation process aimed to develop and tailor the intervention to the needs, capacity and opportunities of first-time families and the CHN setting.

The co-creation process was inspired by the iterative approaches proposed by Leask et al. [8] and Hawkins et al. [19] and evolved over time, where information from previous interactions with stakeholders informed the following co-creation activities. Therefore, we did not have a predefined protocol for the entire co-creation process, and the protocol for each co-creation activity was developed between meetings. In the beginning of the co-creation process, we applied the Problem, Objective, Design, (end-) Users, Co-creators, Evaluation and Scalability (PRODUCES) framework to define the overall aim, identify stakeholders and plan details (see Appendix 1 online) as proposed by Leask et al. [8]. Additionally, we followed other principles by Leask et al. [8], such as stakeholder sampling, how to foster ownership and the relevance of stating both the overall and short-term aims.

On the basis of the framework by Hawkins et al., we applied the action research cycle, in which we, together with stakeholders: (a) reflected on findings from the needs assessment including literature review and interviews; (b) discussed ideas for intervention materials, tools and implementation plans; (c) agreed on ideas; (d) produced drafts of material; (e) reviewed drafts and received feedback; and (f) revised drafts as needed [19].

Certain elements, such as a focus on healthy weight development, a universal approach and the targeting of first-time parents, had already been determined a priori to the start of the co-creation process.

Selection, recruitment and involvement of stakeholders for the co-creation process

We initially conducted a stakeholder analysis using the IM protocol and Leask et al. [1, 8]. The target group was chosen, and CHNs identified as key stakeholders to understand their context, skills and daily routines.

Implementers: involving community health nurses

Following the IM protocol, we established a Participatory Planning Group (PPG) for the primary co-creation process. The main stakeholders were CHNs from five Danish municipalities, selected to represent variation in geographical location, urbanization, population size, SEP and ethnicity, with most reporting a high prevalence of overweight amongst children starting school. We aimed to involve both managing and practising CHNs in the co-creation process, which was achieved in four municipalities. In the last municipality, a health consultant participated in the PPG, whilst one practising CHN only participated in the last co-creation activities. The composition changed during the co-creation process due to participants’ retirement or change of workplace. Four municipalities were recruited through telephone and email contact with the managing CHNs, whilst one municipality independently reached out to the research group after attending a seminar with a colleague of the research group. The practising CHNs were appointed by the managing CHNs, for example, due to their roles in overweight clinics. The municipalities were not guaranteed inclusion in the final trial.

Additionally, a representative from a Danish University College was invited to shape the development of a CHN training course, which may be adapted into a standard training module at the CHN education for future CHN students following the Bloom Study evaluation. In addition, a health economic researcher participated to gain insights for planning the health economic assessment of the Bloom Study. Whilst all stakeholders engaged in the co-creation process; we consider the CHNs the primary participants of the PPG and therefore our analysis centres on their participation, experiences and perspectives.

Overall, the PPG consisted of approximately 13 members, which is considered a suitable size for group work and discussions [8, 20]. All stakeholders were economically compensated for the time spent (see details in Appendix 2 online).

Co-creation activities and facilitation: Six members of the Bloom research group (L.K., C.T.B., R.R.C., T.P.P., and two colleagues), from the fields of public health intervention, social epidemiology and child health, participated in the co-creation process, with various roles as researchers, meeting facilitators and intervention developers. Health promotion consultants from the Centre for Health Promotion in Practice (CHPP) at Local Government Denmark, an organization representing Danish municipalities, assisted the research group in organizing and facilitating the workshops. They are experienced in running workshops, building capacity and providing implementation support within municipalities. We organized six workshops and eight online group meetings with the PPG. Below, we first describe the workshops, followed by the group meetings.

The workshops lasted 3–5 h, and were primarily conducted in person, except for the first and fourth workshop, which were conducted online due to coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) restrictions. In the workshops, everyone was encouraged to participate. Workshop scripts were prepared before each session, outlining the goals of the day, detailed timelines, facilitation roles and materials to bring. To align expectations, each workshop with the PPG began with (1) stating the overall goal of the co-creation process and the specific aim of that workshop; (2) providing updates on recent developments and changes to the intervention, such as new project collaborations based on PPG input from previous workshops; and (3) presenting the six IM steps and two co-creation frameworks [1, 8, 19] aligned with the workshop objectives (see Fig. 1). Each workshop concluded with a brief outline of the next steps. Typically, workshops began with researchers presenting new evidence from literature and empirical data, using PowerPoint slides, figures and images to visualize the project and foster shared understanding. Moreover, the research group’s prior experience with CHNs proved valuable, providing insights into this stakeholder group and supporting the use of evidence-based knowledge. Throughout the workshops, a range of more creative co-creation methods was used, including cards, Post-its, brainstorming, ranking, voting, walk-and-talk sessions and others (see Appendix 2 online). Prototypes of intervention components were tested and refined iteratively [8].Fig. 1Overview of the different elements included in the co-creation process of the Bloom Study. The eight group meetings were conducted online and lasted 1.5 h. These meetings were designed to ease participation from members located across the country. PPG members could choose to attend approximately half of the meetings. The CHPP did not participate, and typically only two members of the research group were present. In the group meetings, small PowerPoint presentations were used, and specific topics derived from the workshops were discussed in more detail with the participants.

Across both workshops and group meetings, the aim was to explore the CHNs’ needs and barriers to participating in the Bloom intervention, brainstorm potential intervention activities, verify and challenge the research group’s initial ideas and discuss concerns and ideas regarding the implementation plan. We continually analysed and reflected on the findings from each co-creation activity with the PPG members, guided by the action research cycle. The process was iterative and nonlinear, as we frequently revisited earlier data to deepen our understanding [8, 19].

Target group: involving parents

We involved parents in a parallel co-creation process to ensure that the intervention was designed, planned and implemented to reach and appeal to first-time families across social diversities [14, 15]. We engaged parents through group interviews with mother and father groups. In Denmark, there is a strong tradition of mother groups, where mothers of infants living in the same area meet regularly, often organized by CHNs. Father groups are not as common, but play groups for fathers without commitment are becoming more common across Danish municipalities, reflecting that an increasing proportion of fathers are taking paternity leave. We used a purposeful sampling strategy [35] to recruit parents from diverse family types through CHNs and our network. The participating parents were 23–40 years old and represented a broad mix of ethnic backgrounds (with origins in, for example, Denmark, Turkey, Syria, Pakistan and Bulgaria). Approximately one third had a non-Western background, and the group represented a range of SEP, with more than half of the participants having low levels of formal education. We aimed to include a diverse group of parents to ensure perspectives from different positions within the CHN setting and their needs in an intervention. All parents were on parental leave with their infant, who was under 12 months old. The parents did not receive reimbursement for their participation.

Co-creation activities and facilitation: We conducted four group interviews to explore parents’ preferences regarding receiving visit from a CHN and intervention types and materials: (1) a mother group specifically for young mothers (N = 4), (2) a group for ethnic minority mothers (N = 7), (3) a group for mothers participating in a parent group (N = 4) and (4) a fathers’ playgroup (N = 5). Within mother groups, the mothers knew each other well from previous interactions, whereas the fathers did not. To make participation as user-friendly as possible, the group interviews were conducted either at locations provided by the municipality or in a quiet local café, where we could speak undisturbed. In all parent group interviews, infants were present. In two of the group interviews, a CHN was briefly present, as these sessions were part of a mother group setting. L.K. and R.R.C. facilitated the sessions, which lasted 50–70 min.

The parent group interviews were conducted in Danish or English, depending on participant preference. We used semi-structured interview guides with overarching themes and optional probing questions to ensure that issues important to parents were explored and also allowed for the interview to take unexpected directions. The themes and core questions were generated and refined within the research group, reviewed after each completed interview and adjusted for subsequent sessions as deemed relevant. The interview guides focussed on two main areas: (1) parents’ experiences with visits from CHNs and (2) their needs when becoming a parent, as well as their wishes for an intervention to support a healthy start in life, including perceived needs and preferences regarding visit content, materials and other forms of support. Whilst both topics were addressed in all interviews, the depth of discussion varied according to the natural flow of the conversation. During parent group interviews, the co-creation activities were primarily the use of visual aids, such as displaying materials, using cards with various components and providing Post-its for participants to write on.

Information from the workshops and group meetings with the PPG helped inform the subsequent group interviews with parents and vice versa. An overview of the co-creation process is shown in Fig. 1.

Study design for the process evaluation of the co-creation

We conducted a qualitative process evaluation of the co‑creation approach applied in the development of the Bloom intervention. A qualitative approach is particularly relevant for evaluating co-creation because it enables an in-depth exploration of participants’ perspectives, interactions and the evolving processes that shape the development of the intervention [8].

Data material

The study data includes transcripts of recordings of workshops, group meetings and parent group interviews (described above). To deepen our understanding of PPG’s perception of the co-creation process, we used data from an oral evaluation and individual qualitative interviews (described below). The parents did not evaluate their participation in these activities.

Oral evaluation: At the end of the final workshop, the PPG evaluated the co-creation process orally in plenary. Participants were introduced to Arnstein’s ladder of citizen participation [36] and asked to select the step that best represented their perceived level of involvement throughout the co-creation process. Arnstein’s ladder has eight rungs: manipulation, therapy, informing, consultation, placation, partnership, delegated power and citizen control. The first two rungs are classified as nonparticipation, the next three as degrees of tokenism and the last three as degrees of citizen power [36]. This oral evaluation was recorded and transcribed.

Qualitative interviews: We invited all participants in the PPG (regardless of whether they participated fully, partially, or not at all in the workshops and group meetings) to take part in individual semi-structured online telephone interviews. Everyone accepted the invitation, and L.K. conducted the interviews, which lasted 15–47 min. Leask et al. provide principles for evaluating the co-creation process, including assessing retention, perceived engagement, satisfaction and ownership, which inspired our interview guide [8]. Therefore, the interviews were designed to explore participants’ perceptions of their level of involvement in the co-creation process, motivation for participation, trust in the group, potential power conflicts and perceived value and cost of participation. All interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Analytical approach

L.K. analysed transcripts and field notes using thematic analysis and Nvivo (version 14), along with collaborative analyses with co-authors [37], which is described below.

Analytical framework

We used the recently published PROcesS Evaluation framework for CO-creation (PROSECO) framework as an analytical framework [38]. It aims to help researchers evaluate co-creation processes in public health and includes 37 components organized within 5 dimensions (see Fig. 2): delivery (whether the process and methods were implemented as intended), participation (the level of engagement amongst those involved), experiential (how participants perceived and evaluated their experience), context (the broader societal, cultural and political conditions shaping the process) and impact (whether the process achieved its intended outcomes and its effects). To enhance the trustworthiness of the analysis [42], we incorporated several procedures throughout the analytical process. The trustworthiness was strengthened through multiple authors involved in comparing interpretations and refining themes. Throughout the analysis, the first author kept notes to document analytical decisions and emerging reflections. Finally, thick description and the use of illustrative quotes allow readers to assess how interpretations are grounded in the empirical material. Ethical considerations

The development of the Bloom Study was conducted in accordance with the Danish Code of Conduct for Research Integrity [43]. The project is registered with the Research and Innovation Office at the University of Southern Denmark (reference number: 10.828) and has been approved by the Research Ethics Committee at the University of Southern Denmark (approval ID: 21/30422). Before consenting to participate, parents received comprehensive verbal and written information about the study, including purpose and terms of participation, that participation is voluntary, that withdrawal from the study is possible at any time and that data are handled confidentially. CHNs and other stakeholders also received comprehensive verbal and written information about the study and the terms of participation. The data collected from workshops, group meetings and interviews have been pseudonymized.

Throughout the study, we attended to situated and relational ethics [42], acknowledging the sensitive nature of healthy weight development and the vulnerability of early parenthood. We were especially mindful when engaging parents from diverse SEP and ethnic backgrounds, striving to communicate respectfully and avoid reinforcing stigma. In interviews, we used open, neutral questions and created space for participants to guide the conversation. Across all workshops and group meetings with the PPG, we approached them with ethical care and worked to minimize power imbalances by fostering dialogue and establishing a safe, respectful environment. These considerations also guided the analysis and writing of the study.

Positionality

As researchers, the authors’ professional backgrounds in public health and other health-related fields shaped our approach and interpretation of the study. Our social position as highly educated, majority ethnic women may especially have influenced how the parents perceived the interviewers during group interviews, and what they felt comfortable sharing. We focussed on remaining reflexive throughout the research process, acknowledging how our pre-understandings, on, for example, the importance of health, shaped by our professional roles, could influence both data generation and interpretation. This included continually examining the subtle power dynamics inherent in co-creation settings, where researchers and stakeholders contribute under different conditions of authority and expertise. By actively questioning our own influence and engaging in ongoing critical dialogue within the research group, we worked to remain open and attentive to perspectives that diverged from our own, and to ensure these were represented meaningfully in the analysis. Given our close collaboration with the CHNs, we were attentive to the risk that rapport and shared goals could soften our critical stance. To counter this, three co-authors who had not participated in the co-creation activities served as “critical friends”, providing independent scrutiny and helping us identify potential courtesy bias (such as favouring the CHNs views) arising from our proximity to the field.

Results

On the basis of our analysis, we identified seven overarching themes, which could be situated within four of the five dimensions of the PROSECO framework (see Fig. 3). Whilst the fifth dimension, context, is not represented as a standalone theme, it cuts across and interacts with several of the themes. The parents’ perspectives only informed two themes, co-creation in practice and negotiating and shaping the intervention, as they were only interviewed about the intervention content and implementation, not their perceptions on the co-creation process. Delivery

In this study, the delivery dimension of the PROSECO framework was explored on the basis of the intention of the co-creation process and the reality [38], focussing specifically on the components fidelity, transparency and adaptation, but also on other components from the framework’s other dimensions, which influenced whether the co-creation process was delivered and unfolded as planned (contextual influences and recruitment). In this section, we examine the co-creation activities, the involvement of parents and material used during co-creation.

Co-creation in practice: navigating intentions and realities

Initially, we planned five 5-hour workshops, however, the co-creation activities were changed in the beginning of the process. Due to COVID-19, two workshops were online. Furthermore, during the co-creation process we added an extra workshop, plus the online group meetings. The PPG expressed varying preferences: some wanted more meetings; others were content given their busy schedules. A few wished for more time outside workshops to collaborate with PPG members or colleagues within their municipalities. To ensure a shared understanding of the goal, we emphasized the project’s aim at each meeting. We continuously reminded the PPG that the target group was first-time parents, and the focus was on healthy weight development, not overweight management. We promoted transparency by sharing information and using visual aids, such as Fig. 1, to keep participants informed. We regularly shared materials on the basis of discussions from past workshops to validate our understanding.

The involvement of parents was adopted along the way. Our initial goal was to involve the same group of parents in multiple co-creation activities and bring parents and CHNs together in joint sessions to ensure diverse perspectives. However, this proved unfeasible due to time constraints and limited resources. It became clear that parents often have limited time and capacity to engage in ongoing projects, and we had limited resources to conduct a more thorough parent involvement. We initially involved parents when their child was around 4–6 months old and on parental leave, which facilitated participation. However, parental leave typically ends when the child reaches 10 months, and sometimes it is shared between parents, challenging continued involvement by the same individuals. Our initial plan to use visual material and co-creative methods with parents proved difficult, as children played with cards and Post-its, making ranking difficult. In the final parent groups, we mainly showed and discussed material without ranking. Children’s presence limited discussion time, and interviews were sometimes interrupted by crying or breastfeeding children.

Participation

As part of the participation dimension of the PROSECO framework, we explored the motivation for participation and the co-creation environment. In the following analysis we explore participants’ motivation for participation guided by the components motivation and attendance and the co-creation environment inspired by the components group dynamics and impartial collaboration.

Motivations for participation: what drove CHNs to join the co-creation process

The CHNs’ motivations for participating in the co-creation process were reflected through their explicit statements of motivation during the first workshop, the individual interviews and their attendance at workshops and group meetings. At workshop 1, participants shared their reasons for engaging in intervention development in plenary. Across municipalities, the CHNs wished to contribute to develop systematic methods for promoting healthy weight development in children, as they “missed evidence, knowledge, and methods”. They also experienced “many gaps” in the current practice and emphasized the need for greater coherence to ensure continuity beyond the first year of life.

The participation patterns reflected varying motivation and prioritization. Some CHNs attended nearly all workshops and group meetings, whilst others participated more selectively. One CHN did not attend, explaining that she had to prioritize other municipal tasks. In contrast, others emphasized that high attendance was crucial to influence the intervention. Workshop attendance was stable (N = 11–13), except for one workshop where only seven attended due to other obligations. Group meeting participation was lower, partly because we did not require full attendance and partly because of the CHNs having other responsibilities. These patterns show motivation was high but also affected by practical constraints and priorities.

The co-creation environment: group dynamics and trust

The PPG was established at the outset, and its composition remained largely stable, with changes occurring only due to participants’ job shifts or retirement. As mentioned above, one CHN never participated, and another CHN was assigned to join during the final workshop and group meeting. Although the PPG mainly consisted of CHNs, other participants, such as the representative from the University College, contributed on equal terms in the co-creation process. Overall, the group composition was viewed as constructive, as one managing CHN stated: “… I was a bit surprised that there needed to be so many representatives from management, but once we got started, I could see that it made really, really good sense in many ways. And then I think that that mix of people gives you a sort of bouquet of expertise, which often makes good sense because we support each other, and it sparks some good discussions...” (stakeholder 2, individual interview).

Initially, we were concerned that including both managing and practising CHNs in the same workshops would suppress practising CHNs’ open dialogue due to power imbalances. This concern was carefully discussed within the research group and with our co-facilitators. To promote open discussion, workshops were sometimes organized with separate groups for practising and managing CHNs. The individual interviews revealed that disagreements could be shared without tensions. As one participant reflected: “The things we’ve disagreed on in this process are actually mostly because we come from such different municipalities and have such different conditions that we haven’t perhaps agreed as community health nurses. But I think we’ve had some good conversations with you researchers, and we’ve ended up with some results we can be comfortable with. The decisions that have been made are good” (stakeholder 6, individual interview). None of the practising CHNs expressed feeling limited in their freedom to speak when addressing potential power imbalance.

Only a few participants in the PPG knew each other beforehand. The fact that the first workshop was online was not an ideal start since informal interactions were limited. Later in-person workshops strengthened social bonds and networks, creating a safe space for co-creation. Participants regarded the atmosphere as trustworthy, as one practising CHNs expressed: “It’s also that psychological comfort – when you’re like ‘Oh, I’ve seen her before and I remember her from last time’ […] It also has an impact in terms of when you meet on Teams, but also when you meet in person, that you think there’s something recognizable in the people you’re with […] it’s just important in relation to reflections and things like that” (stakeholder 9, individual interview). The participants’ experience of psychological safety was shaped by the other group members’ ways of being and the facilitators’ approach, which was perceived as friendly and responsive. This helped foster a comfortable and inclusive space where participants felt heard, respected and valued.

Experiential

Within the experiential dimension of the PROSECO framework, we explore the PPG’s perceived involvement and whether the co-creation process was clear to the PPG.

Degrees of involvement: expectations, experiences and power balance

In this theme, the analysis is especially guided by the components expectation, experience, perceptions, acceptability and satisfaction.

Co-creation seeks to delegate power, and we aimed for high involvement of the PPG on Arnstein’s ladder [36], but within predefined intervention aspects regarding a universal approach to promote healthy weight development amongst infants of first-time parents. At the final workshop, two participants chose delegated power when asked to describe their perceived involvement. One participant argued for this choice by referring to a previous negotiation of intervention content between the PPG and the research group: “Well, at first I also thought [to describe the involvement as] ‘partnership’, but then I remembered that we had a big hurdle called ‘parent course’, and then I thought you were very keen on having that and you were hard to persuade, but we actually achieved a majority and it was overruled” (stakeholder 1, workshop 6). This reflects how the PPG members, based on their practice knowledge, refined the initial ideas of the researchers, which were based on literature reviews of previous interventions. The remaining nine participants chose partnership, though some expressed a preference for placing themselves between the two rungs of partnership and delegated power. They argued that their voices were being heard and acknowledged, for example, when the intervention theme family sense of security was defined and developed. Some chose partnership over delegated powerbecause the research group decided not to include a parent telephone hotline in the intervention, as suggested by some CHNs (see further in Negotiating intervention components section). Most felt highly involved, more than in previous projects, and did not feel the researchers made decisions they disagreed with.

Although the researchers did not involve the PPG in determining the intervention’s aim, the PPG valued that the purpose was established beforehand, as they found it meaningful. As one stated: “I kind of think that it’s great to be consulted by someone who wants to create something but, like, only when they know a bit more about what it is they want to create, because I think our everyday is hectic […] I’ve received some enquiries before that were a bit too fluffy, and then you think ‘no, I’ll be spending too much time on this’. I always prefer to say that if someone takes the lead on this, we will contribute with the professional element […] that’s why I sometimes think that if we are to have the energy to participate, I think it’s good to have a platform to work from that has been described” (stakeholder 2, individual interview). Another CHN described how the co-creation process also changed her view on what we, the PPG and researchers, were doing in the co-creation process, as the project went from an initial, idealistic vision to a more pragmatic and realistic intervention, tailored to the available resources and setting of the project: “I think we’ve kind of been through a process where we thought we were going to revolutionize the whole world and that something more was going to happen […]. But now that we’ve worked our way through it, it makes perfect sense that this is actually a realistic project, and I think we’re going to succeed in making some changes. I think we believed we were going to do something wild” (stakeholder 6, workshop 6). This highlights that the goal of the co-creation process might not have been clear from the beginning.

PPG members generally found the collaboration with researchers to be flexible and meaningful, and appreciated the researchers’ suggestions and feedback. During the plenary oral evaluation, they described the process as enjoyable, noting researchers were “attentive” and proactive in engaging municipalities. One managing CHN said, “Well, I actually think we’ve had quite a lot of influence, and you’ve also been out investigating … we’ve sent you lots of material – and it was clear that you’d also read it and used some of the things in some of what you prepared as drafts that we could then develop further. So I actually think we’ve had a lot of influence because I think we’ve actually had the influence we wanted to have. But you’ve been really open to … you could say you weren’t like – which you can experience in some projects – that you almost know the direction is already decided before you even start. […] In that sense, we have had a lot of influence, I definitely think so” (stakeholder 2, individual interview). In general, most participants felt more genuinely involved compared with previous experiences. Another stakeholder also highlighted that the collaboration was easy to engage in, as we, researchers, primarily developed the intervention materials on the basis of discussions and input from the PPG: “What has made it easy to be involved in this project is that you guys have done all the work. We have just turned up, shared our thoughts, and given our input. I think it’s been a really good process, and I feel that I’ve been listened to. It was also clear that when you guys presented something the next time or the next time again, I could recognize the things we had talked about” (stakeholder 8, individual interview). Thus, some of the CHNs viewed the project as researcher-led rather than fully shared. This demonstrates that the participants viewed their role as providing knowledge to inform the intervention, resembling consulting on Arnstein’s ladder of participation, a level lower than the PPG stated themselves. Although the collaboration was generally perceived as constructive, this also shows that the power dynamic between the researchers and PPG may have been uneven in decision-making. Some stakeholders viewed the project as mostly the researchers’ rather than a shared project, as a CHN noted: “I also think it’s your project too. You’re also the ones who will be running it and make it grow and so on” (stakeholder 9, workshop). Thus, the final decisions ultimately rested with the researchers, but without the PPG feeling overruled. However, one notable disagreement between the PPG and the researchers concerned whether the PPG municipalities could be included in the final intervention. This issue was discussed during the co-creation process, as it had implications for the final evaluation design. The PPG advocated for their inclusion, and in the end, they were incorporated into the randomization on equal terms with other municipalities.

Clarity and comfort in co-creation: navigating roles, expectations and process openness

The experiential dimension also includes the component clarity and expectations regarding the different roles in the co-creation process. In this theme, the analysis is also inspired by readiness for change from the context dimension and transparency from the delivery dimension.

In the co-creation process, it is essential to have clarity about the aim of the process and the stakeholders’ roles and matching expectations across researchers, facilitators and stakeholders [38], which is also reflected in the transparency component from the PROSECO framework. The purpose of the co-creation process and the PPGs’ role was unclear to some participants in the beginning. During the evaluation, some found the initial workshops “frustrating” or felt “being lost” due to the open-ended nature of the exploration. Whilst this initial approach was intended to foster creativity, it was perceived as rather abstract by some participants. This was also highlighted in the roles of the participants, which might not have been defined explicitly by us in the beginning, as was highlighted by a managing CHN: “So I think a clearer description of the roles or functions to clarify them. Because I think sometimes, when we showed up at these [workshops], it wasn’t until a bit later that the penny dropped about the program theory and what we had to discuss. But I do think, you know, I would also say that I think the workshops were a success, and once we got to know the topic and found out what we were going to talk about, it was really well organized with the processes...” (stakeholder 3, individual interview). Thus, initially, some found it challenging to understand their role and to translate the theoretical aspects of the program theory into something to discuss in the CHN setting. This might be due to CHNs being very “result-oriented”, as one participant stated during an interview, which can contrast with the co-creation process, often involving open-ended discussions. Meanwhile other participants delved into and enjoyed the creative process as described by one participant: “I think I just thought of it as an aeroplane, it doesn’t fly from here to there, it flies according to the wind, we always knew that there was some kind of goal, but it didn’t matter if we landed at this or that airport, as long as it was in the same country. I think it’s just been – I was quite comfortable with just trusting the process. And it’s okay to be frustrated sometimes, because that’s when you get that extra bit of creativity” (stakeholder 7, workshop 6). Thus, participants differed not in how they engaged, but in how meaningful they found the activities within the co-creation process. Whilst some participants appreciated the open, exploratory nature of the early workshops, and were more process-oriented and comfortable with ambiguity of co-creation, others struggled to see the relevance or value, preferring clearer direction and tangible outcomes.

Impact

In this final result section, we analyse the outcomes of the co-creation process which relates to the impact dimension of the PROSECO framework, for example, components that assess the extent to which the co-creation process achieved its desired outcomes. Co-creation can bring unexpected outcomes. An intervention is not only produced through the co‑creation process; participants also acquire various forms of learning, experiences and outcomes, both positive and negative.

Stakeholders’ perceived value and costs of participating in co-creation

In this theme, we especially explore the components of personal change and capacity building. We identified five subthemes, which are outlined below.

Creating space for reflection in a busy practice

Participants described the co-creation process as an opportunity to pause and reflect, something that is otherwise difficult to prioritize in their busy everyday work. This reflective space allowed them to consider both their own practices and broader strategic goals. The PPG appreciated the chance to meet across municipalities and engage in meaningful dialogue. As one stakeholder said: “I think it’s been incredibly rewarding that, with the questions you’ve brought, which we’ve really been able to immerse ourselves in and been able to reflect on some topics that you don’t really get to do in your everyday working life. It’s been really good” (stakeholder 7, workshop). Thus, the workshops and group meetings created a space for reflection that was not only rare in participants’ daily routines, but also valued as a meaningful part of the co-creation process. Some participants also experienced that the co-creation generated renewed energy that spilt over into their everyday work, which was highlighted by a CHNs: “I think the whole process has been interesting, so it’s kind of given me energy that I then use when I go back [to the daily work]” (stakeholder 3, individual interview).

Co-creation as mutual learning, inspiration and networking

The transition of knowledge in the collaboration was clearly bidirectional. The PPG contributed insights into municipal practices, whilst the researchers brought evidence-based knowledge into the dialogue. One participant reflected: “There have been some great discussions about it. And it’s been really cool to see that the next time we met, we could see that you had learned something, and we had also learned something. And then ‘Ahh okay, we’re going in that direction’, you know, where it’s been slowly but surely adjusted more and more, that’s where we are. I think it’s been really interesting to see, and that goes both ways” (stakeholder 7, individual interview). As the research group developed a deeper understanding of the CHN setting, the PPG members also gained new knowledge and skills, not only from the researchers, but also from each other. Another participant noted: “...you also get something out of hearing the other participants’ thoughts and how things are done in other municipalities, so it also involves some networking and inspiration. So in that way, I also got something out of it that I could take away with me. So it has gone both ways, I think” (stakeholder 1, individual interview). The social connection amongst participants was evident, as they enjoyed the interaction and actively exchanged knowledge and experiences, fostering mutual inspiration.

Research matters: reframing the role of research through co-creation

Some participants expressed that their involvement in the co-creation process had changed how they viewed research. Working closely with researchers gave them a new appreciation for the role of research in driving meaningful change. One participant reflected: “…I did write ‘Research Matters’ because I just feel it in a different way than I’ve ever done before. That this is just how you make a difference” (stakeholder 7, workshop). This statement illustrates how co-creation not only generates new knowledge, but also transforms attitudes towards research and its role in practice. Participants perceived the collaboration between research and practice as a meaningful way to develop new and improved practices.

The need for closure in co-creation

At the final workshop, some participants expressed a sense of incompleteness, as the intervention development was still ongoing. One CHN reflected: “…I find myself thinking ‘Are we done now?’ […]. Because I’m actually thinking that after the next round of processing by those of you who work with it, and then the feedback on the whole thing, I kind of think that for me, that would be the end. […] because you use a lot of words and write a lot of things, but getting to the core, what needs to be included and getting rid of things, that’s important. That’s probably my method, that’s how I often work. So I have to keep my critical lens all the way to the end, and I think this group would also have been good for this […] You know, that final detail, so that you feel that now it’s spot on” (stakeholder 3, workshop). This reflection highlights the importance of closure in the co-creation process. Whilst openness and iteration are key features of co-creation, prolonged engagement without a clear endpoint or tangible outcome can leave participants feeling disengaged. Ensuring that participants are involved until the end of the co-creation process and that the process culminates in a clear, shared sense of completion is essential for maintaining engagement and reinforcing the legitimacy of co-creation.

Co-creation is fluffy and time-consuming

Some participants described moments in the co-creation process when activities felt “fluffy” or lacked direction. This perception often arose when the overall aim, or the purpose of specific sessions, was unclear, and when discussions became overly focussed on abstract elements, such as program theory. For some participants in the co-creation process, the demands of involvement, abstract discussions and high time investment may have outweighed the perceived benefits. As one participant critically reflected on the experience: “I remember that workshop, the first in-person one, I think that was very long compared to what we got out of it. At least what we got out of it, and it’s also a balance when you are developing an intervention. I think you definitely got a lot out of it, but our feeling after the workshop in terms of what the outcome was for us, it can sometimes be a bit hard to see. So I think it’s important to constantly relate it to the goal or constantly link it to what’s in it for us as a municipality when three people spend a whole day on this. It might sound better to us when we can see, ‘OK, that’s what this goal relates to’. Because I remember it as a long day, and I didn’t really learn much. That’s just the way it is when you’re part of those projects where we feed into your agenda […] It can be difficult, of course, because often it’s only when you start getting down to the nitty–gritty of the interventions or figuring out what the municipalities are actually able to do, then it starts to get a bit more substance and useful” (stakeholder 4, individual interview). This participant was only part of the process in the beginning due to a change in workplace and may therefore not have experienced the full scope and coherence of the co-creation process.

Negotiating and shaping the intervention

Finally, the impact dimension also highlights the components of knowledge integration and evidence and objective alignment, which are explored in the following analysis. The Bloom intervention was shaped through a dynamic co-creation process, in which stakeholders, both CHNs and parents, contributed contextual insights, practical experience and preferences. Their input influenced several aspects of the intervention, including the selection of the target group, the intervention content and the implementation strategies. However, the process also involved negotiation, as researchers or stakeholders occasionally rejected suggestions that conflicted with the evaluation design or practical constraints. This interplay between stakeholder influence and researchers’ consideration reflects a co-creation process that was both inclusive and selective. Identifying the target group

The co-creation process played a central role in shaping the understanding of who the intervention should target. As stated in the Method section, some elements of the intervention were predefined before the co-creation process, for example, reaching all first-time parents, and these elements were both supported and contested during stakeholder discussions.

In the early workshops (see Fig. 4), CHNs strongly advocated for a universal approach. They emphasized that vulnerabilities are often invisible and only become apparent through sustained relationships with families. As one CHN explained: “There are just so many parents who lack basic parenting skills, and it’s by no means only families we would describe as challenged from the outset, it’s also people where we think, they do have some resources, but many parents have never held a baby in their arms until they have their own” (stakeholder 2, group meeting 3). However, not all participants agreed. In the individual evaluation interview, one stakeholder argued for a more targeted approach focussed on high-risk families: “We have different needs and expectations and also different professional preferences in relation to … it’s a bit of a professional hot topic, really, whether you should go for the universal option or go for something more targeted. You have to choose something and I just have a preference here, but … I’m well aware that, you know, there’s not one single truth, right” (stakeholder 10, individual interview). Through these discussions, it became evident that families in need of “extra support” needed to be defined more broadly than traditional categories, such as low SEP or minority ethnic background. At the same time, stakeholders recognized the difficulty of defining fixed criteria for who should receive additional support. Consequently, the final decision was to leave this assessment to the professional judgment of the CHNs within the intervention.

Father involvement

Father involvement emerged as another key theme. Whilst some CHNs felt they already had effective strategies for engaging fathers, others, particularly in rural areas, reported ongoing challenges. Fathers themselves expressed a strong desire to be included in CHN visits. As one father noted: “Also, it’s good to hear things as a first timer [father]. If I have to hear it from my wife, then I have to hear what she says and what she remembers – leaving it for the mother, ‘like this is your job” (father 1, group interview) and another continued: “it’s a good support, and you are more active on what’s going on [when participating in the visits]” (father 2, group interview). These insights underscored both the participating fathers’ wish to be actively involved and their shared parental responsibility in childrearing. The co-creation process revealed a continued need to strengthen fathers’ inclusion in the CHN setting.

As a result, the intervention was designed to support families across a wide range of social diversities on the basis of CHNs’ professional judgment and with a special focus on involving fathers. These aspects are embedded in the training courses, implementation guide and materials offered to parents.

Negotiating intervention themes: family sense of security

One of the intervention themes that evolved significantly during the co-creation process was family sense of security (see further description in the study protocol [33]). Initially, the theme was included in the projects program theory on the basis of literature pointing to the limited effect of behavioural interventions on healthy weight development [44] and theoretical links between sense of security and obesity risk [45]. However, following discussions with the PPG, the theme was redefined to fit the CHN setting better. The PPG perceived family sense of security as a multifaceted construct encompassing factors such as economic or housing instability and lack of social networks. They emphasized the importance of responding to the needs of families facing social adversities. This shifted the focus from an abstract theoretical construct towards a more action‑oriented understanding rooted in CHNs’ everyday encounters with families across social diversities.

A key strategy in addressing family sense of security that emerged was early interaction with families, particularly through a pregnancy home visit, which was not standard in all municipalities. The PPG highlighted this visit as a crucial opportunity for the CHN to build relationships and understand family circumstances before birth. Inspired by existing practices in municipalities and other research projects in Denmark (e.g. [46, 47]), we developed the Bloom Wheel, a visual dialogue tool with a question guide to support CHNs in tailoring their approach during the pregnancy home visit to the individual needs and circumstances of the families.

Meanwhile, the idea of the Bloom Wheel also generated concerns in the PPG. They warned that the visit could be perceived as intrusive, underscoring the need for a respectful approach. As one noted: “I think we have to make sure that it doesn’t become a cross-examination that ends up with people shutting down instead of opening up. So I also think it’ll be more beneficial to take it easy and take a deep breath, rather than being overly pushy with these kind of intrusive questions. Because I think we run the risk that some of them, where it’s actually important, that they might clam up like an oyster instead. […] time and calm and an extra visit, I think, is far more important than [talking about all the questions]. I think it’s necessary for us to create a relation and trust and knowledge about us. […] In relation to the duty of notification but also the duty of confidentiality […] and as a bridge builder in relation to the municipality’s department for families with children. All those things, if that’s what it takes. That’s much, much more important than mapping everything” (stakeholder 9, workshop). This illustrated a central dilemma: whilst gaining insight into family circumstances was seen as essential for supporting the parents, overly structured questioning risked undermining trust or other unintended outcomes. To resolve this tension, the use of the Bloom Wheel was created as a flexible tool, allowing each CHN to decide how and when to apply it on the basis of their professional judgment.

Negotiating intervention components

Throughout the co-creation process, various intervention components were negotiated. To clarify how consensus was reached during the co-creation process, we describe two examples in which stakeholders and researchers initially disagreed. The research group introduced ideas on the basis of national and international literature, such as a parent course (e.g. [48, 49]), whilst the PPG suggested additional components on the basis of practical knowledge, including a parental telephone hotline. These suggestions triggered concrete disagreements about feasibility, target-group relevance and alignment with CHN practice.

First, the parent course became a central point of contention. CHNs rejected it on the basis of two concerns: (1) their experience that families across social diversities rarely engage in group-based formats and (2) their preference for one-on-one relational work. As one CHN explained: “Well, in my opinion, the more specific you can be in how you target the individual in the family, where they are, where they need it. I think we'll get the furthest with this approach. If it becomes too generalized, I think sometimes it becomes too difficult for the family to take it in. And this, well, at least that’s our experience from when we did it many years ago” (stakeholder 6, workshop). On the basis of this practice-based knowledge, the parent course was excluded. This illustrates how experiential insights directly challenged assumptions derived from the existing evidence and shaped the intervention in ways that would not have emerged through literature alone.

A second example concerned the proposal for a telephone hotline. This was discussed across three workshops and a group meeting in an iterative process: the PPG proposed the idea, the research group examined feasibility and proposed it in a parent group interview and the issue was revisited in joint reflection in the PPG. The telephone hotline proposal, used in some regions of Denmark, proved unfeasible due to constraints linked to the evaluation design, such as risks of spillover effects to control municipalities, and the resource demands it would place on intervention municipalities. Across both examples, consensus emerged through iterative dialogue, joint reflection, and transparent negotiation of practice-based, methodological and feasibility concerns. Whereas the parent course revolved around appropriateness for families and the CHN practice, the hotline negotiation centred on practical and methodological limitations.

Parental influence on design of intervention materials

The participating parents had a substantial influence on the design of family-focussed materials. They stressed the importance of offering all parents multiple format options, rather than matching specific materials to particular groups. For instance, they challenged the researchers’ assumptions, on the basis of literature, that videos were mainly relevant for parents with linguistic challenges, or that long written materials primarily suited parents with higher SEP. Their input shifted the process away from such assumptions and towards a more flexible, inclusive strategy that allows parents to choose the format that best fit their preferences and circumstances.

Co-creating implementation strategies

During the workshops, the PPG emphasized the need for CHNs to be thoroughly introduced to the Bloom Study, its rationale, aims and intervention logic before implementation. In response, a 5-day training course was developed. One full day focussed specifically on reaching families across social diversities, aiming to strengthen CHNs’ awareness of varied family circumstances and support reflection on their own positionality when entering new family contexts (see Fig. 4 – Workshop 5). The structure and duration of the course were discussed with the PPG. Some managing CHNs argued for a shorter format due to competing obligations, whilst practising CHNs called for more extensive training. The final design represented a compromise reflected a balance between comprehensive enough to support implementation, yet manageable within organizational constraints. As one CHN put it: “So that feeling, that we’ve really been a part of it, […] also when we can see it applied in practice, that we can see that there is a practice-based perspective in it. That makes it much, much easier to work with than ‘Just go out there and implement this’ when there haven’t been any community health nurses involved who could say that this doesn’t actually make sense in our daily workflow” (stakeholder 9, workshop). This illustrates how the co-creation process enhanced implementation feasibility by ensuring that training strategies were grounded in practical realities of CHN’s everyday work rather than imposed from above.

Discussion

This study critically explored how co-creation unfolds in the development of a public health intervention using the Bloom Study as a case example. We specifically explored process delivery, stakeholder experiences and outcomes of the co-creation process. Three overarching findings emerged.

Firstly, whilst the co-creation process was delivered largely as intended, we encountered several challenges. Most notably, we were unable to engage parents to the extent originally envisioned. The PPG participants’ motivation was reflected in both professional commitment and recognition of the opportunity to influence practice, and the co-creation environment was characterized by positive group dynamics amongst participants and a safe space.

Secondly, the PPG reported feeling highly involved in the co-creation process but the PPG perceived the intervention as the researchers’ project, viewing their own role mainly as providing input and feedback. They nevertheless stated that they held some decision-making power and could change the researchers’ initial ideas, which corresponded with the extent of influence they desired. The PPG initially found the aim of the co-creation process unclear, reflecting unclear role descriptions and had a preference for outcome-focussed processes over open-ended, exploratory approaches.

Thirdly, the co-creation process affected both the PPG participants and the design and content of the final intervention. Overall, the PPG valued the process as a creative space for meaningful knowledge exchange that energized them and generated insights for their own practice. Perceived costs of participation included feeling excluded from the later development stages and time costs, especially when the purpose of the process was unclear. The co-creation process entailed negotiations between the PPG and researchers and resulted in changes to target group definitions, intervention content and implementation strategies. The final intervention reflected research and practice knowledge, which informed the theme of family sense of security, the inclusion of specific intervention activities (e.g. a pregnancy visit) and the development of intervention materials and implementation strategies (e.g. the Bloom training course for CHNs).

Even though parents were involved to a lesser degree than initially planned, they provided valuable feedback and insights into which materials were most relevant to parents, helping us better understand the families’ needs.

The reality of facilitating co-creation – Challenges and lessons learned

The delivery of the co-creation process was, as in many other co-creation projects within academia, through the conduction of workshops, group discussions and interviews with stakeholders [24, 50–53]. As Agnello et al. [24] argue, it is essential to adopt a creative approach and draw inspiration from the grey literature on co-creation [24]. Access to a broader range of co-creation methods might have helped us to be more innovative throughout the process and further foster creativity, social bonding and motivation. Moreover, involving the stakeholders more actively in developing the co-creation process itself, by incorporating their preferences and ideas, might have provided new insights [24]. However, the workshop format may have been particularly well suited to the CHNs, as many preferred its result-oriented structure, and it was therefore selected to fit the participants [3]. The theoretical framework underpinning our workshops largely followed the IM steps, which sometimes felt overly academic for the PPG. Discussions of program theory and mechanisms were abstract and progressed slowly, not always aligning with the PPG’s more practical orientation.

In a true co-creation process, the overall goal is defined in collaboration with participants [23]. In this study, the research group predetermined the overall aim of promoting healthy weight development, which is often the case within research [46, 53], as studies are often funded with a specific aim in mind [3]. However, the goal of the Bloom Study aligned with a need identified by most participants in the PPG, and some appreciated that it had already been established. Some participants in the PPG were more process-oriented and comfortable with ambiguity, whilst others needed clearer direction and tangible outcomes. These differences highlight the importance of aligning expectations and clarifying roles throughout the process to ensure that all participants can see the purpose and potential impact of their involvement.

To better support this alignment, clearer communication about expectations and roles at the outset and throughout would have been beneficial [8, 54]. For example, we could have jointly developed a brief, shared agreement that outlined which elements of the intervention were open to co-creation, which were constrained by funding or research requirements, and what types of decisions the PPG would have direct influence over. Regularly revisiting these agreements at the beginning of each workshop could have helped maintain a shared understanding as the process evolved. In addition, making even more explicit the different forms of expertise in the room (e.g. professional, experiential, methodological) may have supported participants in understanding why their contributions were essential and how they complemented those of the research team. In hindsight, we as researchers could have been more thorough in clarifying the PPG’s role in the process. This could have been done by explicitly outlining their responsibilities in each workshop and by continuously discussing these roles with them. To engage meaningfully in the co-creation process, participants need adequate preparation; it is essential to learn who the group of stakeholders involved are, both the target group and implementers, as well as their circumstances, and the delivery must be adapted to this and be context sensitive. Ensuring that facilitation, pacing and tasks were sensitive to these contextual realities would likely have supported stronger role clarity and fostered a more even distribution of participation [54].

The co-creation process was experienced as time-consuming. The iterative cycle of planning, coordinating and follow-up required for each meeting was resource intensive for the research group, demanding significant coordination and effort. These time constraints also limited parental involvement, as we were unable to explore alternative recruitment and engagement strategies. Because of the fixed project timeline and the need to initiate the main trial, extending the co-creation period was not possible. This highlights the importance of an adaptive, context-sensitive approach to co-creation: initial plans may prove unrealistic, and flexibility is essential, yet can be constrained by grant conditions and other external requirements.

Engaging in co-creation: involvement of both implementers and target group

Most PPG members perceived their involvement as situated between the rungs of participation and delegated power, which corresponds to a level of citizens’ power on Arnstein’s ladder [36], and thereby a high degree of involvement. The partnership with the PPG succeeded in fostering cross-municipality dialogue, although not all stakeholders experienced a complete sense of ownership, nor did they perceive that their influence reached the highest level, citizens’ control, on Arnstein’s ladder [36].

Regarding the parents, it became evident that our approach resembled researching on them rather than with them, as the engagement largely remained one-way and offered limited opportunities for mutual exchange of knowledge. Consequently, parents’ involvement corresponded to the consulting level on Arnstein’s ladder [36]. As demonstrated in previous studies, stakeholders often differ markedly in their needs, expectations and capacities for participation, making it essential to account for these variations through a genuinely bottom-up approach [55]. Our findings reflect this dynamic: whilst the PPG’s higher placement on Arnstein’s ladder enabled them to identify more strongly with the project’s purpose and to engage in a reciprocal exchange of knowledge, parents’ predominantly consultative role offered far fewer opportunities for such mutual learning. This discrepancy illustrates how variations in participation levels can shape both the quality and quantity of collaboration and may also affect the different stakeholder groups feeling of ownership of the co-creation process.

The challenge of achieving true co-creation, where the target group are active collaborators rather than mere informants, is well recognized [24]. We aimed to involve families continuously as experts to tailor solutions to their needs [56], but we were also attentive to potential risks, including the possibility of involving them in a merely symbolic way, that is, tokenism, and the ethical considerations around the time commitment we requested from participants [57]. Our initial goal was to bring parents and CHNs together in joint sessions to ensure diverse perspectives. This was thoroughly discussed but we decided against it, due to the risk of creating a power imbalance and failing to achieve genuine co-creation. More extensive parental involvement might have been achieved by conducting more group interviews either with the same parents or in other settings and by providing reimbursement for participation in the co-creation activities to increase parental prioritization of these activities [58]. Furthermore, although reimbursement is widely recognized in the co-creation literature as both a practical facilitator and an ethical obligation [58], particularly for engaging underrepresented groups, we did not compensate parent participants despite reimbursing professionals. This may raise questions about equity and whether differences in valuing participants’ time influenced recruitment and sustained involvement. Another way to foster ownership amongst parents could have been achieved by engaging a more dedicated group of parents, for example, by including peer researchers employed in the research group, as seen in other research areas [59] or by involving parental organizations. This approach could, however, increase inequity within the intervention, as participants with higher SEP are more often engaged [13–15].

Although we had hoped for sustained parental involvement throughout the intervention development period, we found that less demanding formats, such as single group and individual interviews, were more feasible for parents than repeated interviews and workshops-based activities. Similarly, researchers involved in the Face-It study, a study on mothers with gestational diabetes mellitus, their partners and health professionals [52], found that less demanding co-creation approaches increased families’ participation. In another study [53], parents of preschool children with emerging overweight were involved in consecutive workshops; however, not to the intended level due to recruitment difficulties and busy family lives [53]. The study’s success in involving the same parents continuously may reflect that parents find co-creation more relevant when they personally experience the issue being addressed [53]. In our study, the included parents had not yet experienced weight-related issues with their infant; therefore, the focus on healthy weight development in infancy reflected a need primarily identified by CHNs and researchers rather than by parents themselves. As a result, engaging parents in co-creation on this topic may be more challenging, as their immediate motivation to participate is likely lower. In the Bloom Study, co-creation activities with parents were also constrained by the children’s presence, and their interaction with the co-creation materials. Whilst greater parental involvement might indeed have contributed additional perspectives, the practical challenges encountered suggest that achieving such involvement may not be feasible in this context. Therefore, success criteria that reflect a more limited level of parental involvement, compared with that of implementers, may be more realistic for similar co-creation processes.

Co-creation is a balancing act that requires careful consideration of stakeholders’ capacity, individual preferences and time to participate, whilst seeking input from as many stakeholders as possible without overburdening them. Overall, the analysis suggests that keeping a clear focus on what different stakeholders gain from participation is key to sustaining motivation and engagement. Whilst co-creation aims to foster inclusive and meaningful collaboration, it requires careful design and facilitation to ensure participants’ time and contributions are respected and the process remains purposeful [3].

The co-creation also needs to be designed to accommodate varying levels of comfort with uncertainty, balance creativity with clarity and provide sufficient orientation so that all participants can find meaning in their involvement. Had participants been involved from the very beginning, higher ownership and empowerment might have been achieved – although this would have required a more intensive involvement process. However, the overall sense of ownership was evident amongst most PPG participants, who continued to share news from the CHN setting and maintained an active interest in the project’s progress, even after the development phase. Furthermore, it is crucial to be explicit about and recognize how stakeholders perceive the primary goal of their participation – whether it is focussed on research and theory development or on practical design outcomes only. Understanding these perceptions is essential for achieving and maintaining a shared understanding amongst all participants.

Impact through co-creation: on participants and intervention design

The co-creation process benefitted both PPG participants and the final intervention design. We found that the co-creation process was perceived as a creative space that facilitated knowledge transition, a finding that has also been reported in previous studies [60]. In a review, Agnello et al. [24] found that co-creation enhances collaboration, stimulate creativity and builds trust, but also emphasizes the resource-intensive nature of co-creation, both regarding funding, time spent on co-creating and human resources needed [24]. These factors may have implications for the longer‑term maintenance of similar collaborative processes and for the scalability of co‑created interventions across settings.

Other studies using co-creation have developed similar interventions in early life. In the Face-It study, co-creation was conducted with the target group and healthcare professionals, who negotiated and adapted the intervention, for example, by developing a digital coaching app and creating tailored visits from CHNs. Similar to our findings, they found the need to understand vulnerability in early parenthood as situated rather than determined by SEP, and emphasized the need to tailor the intervention to the needs of each family [52]. The Face-It study was not effective on the primary endpoint, but on some of the secondary outcomes [61], and was seen as useful and motivating [62]. In general, limited knowledge exists on whether co-creation actually improves the health outcomes of the intervention, and Grindell et al. call for robust evaluation to ascertain this [63]. They recommend a systematic review to assess whether co-approaches actually produce better knowledge and outcomes than methods not using co-creation [63]. Although we do not yet know the effect of the Bloom intervention on primary or secondary outcomes, our comprehensive evaluation design, combining a RCT effectiveness study with a mixed‑methods process evaluation, will allow us to assess whether the co‑creation process succeeded in developing an effective, implementable intervention that appeals to parents across social diversities and implementers. The upcoming evaluation of the Bloom Study will further clarify whether co‑creation has led to solutions that resonate with parents and implementers and whether the initiative has had a measurable impact, either overall or on secondary outcomes [2, 64].

Methodological consideration

Strengths of this study include a detailed and reflexive account of the co-creation process by being transparent about the co-creation methods applied (Appendix 2 online) and having recordings of workshops and group meetings with the PPG from the entire co-creation process. Furthermore, everyone in the PPG participated in the individual evaluation interviews, including a participant who never showed up and a participant who retired before the process ended. In addition, we applied the PROSECO framework to comprehensively evaluate the co-creation process and conducted collaborative analysis within the author group, including researchers not involved in the Bloom Study or the co-creation process. This collaborative analytic approach enhanced the methodological rigour by bringing multiple interpretive perspectives into the analysis, reducing individual bias and enabling critical scrutiny of emerging themes. Involving researchers with varying degrees of proximity to the study facilitated both insider and outsider interpretations, strengthening the credibility and depth of the analytical claims [42].

Our process evaluation of co-creation was limited by not being integrated into the Bloom Study initially, as co-creation was primarily viewed as a tool for intervention development and not as a black box to unpack. Thus, we did not have a process evaluation protocol, theoretical framework or observation guide in place from the outset as suggested by recent studies [20, 24, 38, 65, 66]. This meant that we did not systematically collect insights into the co-creation process itself. However, all workshops and group meetings with PPG were recorded and transcribed, allowing us to unpack the co-creation process after completion using the PROSECO framework [38]. Integrating a formative evaluation into the co-creation process, including evaluation of each co-creation activity with participants, could have provided an in-depth understanding of the acceptability and perceived value of the specific methods used and enabled adjustments to the process along the way to strengthen involvement and output of each activity. Instead, we evaluated the PPG’s experiences of involvement in the end, but lacked the parents’ perspectives on being involved in group interviews.

During the final workshops with the PPG, L.K. devoted time to conducting observations. However, this proved challenging due to her concurrent role as a project facilitator. This dual role may also have influenced participants’ willingness to speak freely in the individual evaluation interviews, given the sensitivity of evaluating both the research process and the collaboration. However, participants reported that they felt able to speak openly.

Involvement of implementers

The PPG was homogeneous in relation to similar educational backgrounds. Including a broader group of stakeholders beyond implementers, for example, decision-makers within municipalities or representatives from other municipal departments, would have enabled a fuller understanding of competing interests within the municipal field. Their participation would have provided insights into cross-sectoral collaboration within the process. This represents a drawback of the current co-creation setup, as different priorities across municipal departments and the potential for budget reductions in other sections are well-known factors that must be acknowledged and addressed during intervention development. Recognizing these dynamics is important for understanding the broader political and municipal agendas that shape the context in which CHNs operate.

Involvement of parents in co-creation

Parents who were already part of a parent group may also have introduced a selection bias towards those who are familiar with, and generally satisfied with, the CHN system. We successfully engaged parents from different ethnic backgrounds; however, because the four groups were consulted at different stages, it remains unclear whether this diversity strengthened the co-creation or introduced variability in how perspectives were incorporated. It should also be noted that the fathers who participated may be more engaged than the average, having actively sought out a playgroup, and may therefore be more resourceful and more involved in childrearing. Their preferences may thus differ from those of the wider father population.

Implications for future research and the PROSECO-framework

We conducted this co-creation in a municipal setting, however, our insights apply to public health and health promotion more broadly. Future researchers are encouraged to create a thorough plan for the delivery of the co-creation process. Still, they also need to plan for the unexpected and remain adaptable to emerging contextual conditions, as things rarely unfold exactly as intended. When involving both implementers and target groups, understanding their initial motivations for participation is key. It is crucial to consider not only the time required for planning and development, but also the time costs, roles, capacity and responsibilities of the stakeholders and target group who will commit to the study. Therefore, before the co-creation process, it is essential to understand how and when to engage the different stakeholders. It is also important to recognize that not all participants seek full decision-making authority; therefore, clarifying and aligning expectations at the outset, and revisiting them throughout the process, is essential. Finally, funders must allocate dedicated resources to support this process.

The use of the PROSECO framework

By applying the PROSECO framework to our analysis of the co-creation process in the Blooms Study, we respond directly to the authors’ invitation to contribute to its refinement. This is one of the first studies to apply the PROSECO framework [38]. It served as a valuable analytical lens, helping us structure and communicate the thematic findings coherently and systematically. However, our application of the framework also revealed areas where further conceptual development could enhance its usability for researchers. Notably, the dimension of context permeated all themes, suggesting that it may function more effectively as a cross-cutting element rather than a standalone category. Additionally, several of the 37 components appeared difficult to distinguish conceptually, with overlapping definitions that challenged consistent coding. For example, some components, such as acceptability and experience, are closely related in meaning, making it difficult to distinguish them clearly in practice. We see potential to refine conceptual clarity, possibly by merging some components or sharpening their distinctions.

Moreover, some components appeared better suited to alternative dimensions than those initially assigned, for example, transparency, as we also ended up doing in the analysis, or could plausibly belong to multiple dimensions depending on the empirical context. Finally, the component personal change seems to focus mainly on the benefits of co-creation participation, whilst overlooking potential costs or burdens. The components maintenance, system integration and mechanisms of impact can be assessed only after the intervention has been evaluated, and not just after the co-creation process. By testing the framework in practice, we have identified opportunities for further conceptual refinement to make it more useful for researchers.

Conclusions

Whilst co-creation is often cited as a key principle in intervention development, the actual processes often remain a black box. In this study, we contribute empirical insight into how co-creation unfolds in practice within a health system context by opening the box and examining the benefits and challenges of co-creating the Bloom intervention in Denmark. Despite intentions to involve stakeholders meaningfully, practical issues such as parental recruitment and time constraints hindered ideal co-creation. Researchers aimed for broad power delegation, but the predefined scope and methodological commitments of research projects often restricted the extent of stakeholder impact. Intervention providers felt highly involved but preferred clear structure over open-ended processes. Nonetheless, they valued being heard, gaining knowledge, expanding networks and seeing their expertise used. Stakeholder involvement was crucial in refining the intervention, which underwent significant changes. Systematic evaluation of co-creation is essential to developing best practices, identifying effective engagement strategies and improving processes for researchers and stakeholders. Such assessments clarify approaches that foster inclusivity and impact, thereby contributing to an evidence base for refining co-creation methods across settings. Finally, the critical application of the PROSECO framework contributes to unpacking the complexities of co-creation and offers important insights for health systems, particularly regarding how participatory approaches can inform and strengthen implementation practices.
