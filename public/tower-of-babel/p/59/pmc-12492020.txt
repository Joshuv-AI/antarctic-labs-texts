Ultrasound-mediated fabrication of chickpea protein nanoparticles for stabilizing Pickering emulsions
Authors: Bingbing Cui, Yixue Zhang, Chenyan Zhu, Ke Li, Yu Wang, Yuntao Wang, Wuchao Ma, Yanhong Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Emulsions represent one of the most prevalent colloidal systems in food products, comprising two immiscible liquid phases[1]. These systems are primarily classified into oil-in-water (O/W) emulsions and water-in-oil (W/O) emulsions[2]. Pickering emulsions, which utilize solid particles irreversible absorption for stabilization[3], exhibit distinct advantages over conventional surfactant-based emulsions, including enhanced stability and reduced toxicity[4]. These advantages make Pickering emulsions promising for food applications. Nevertheless, current research on Pickering emulsions lacks systematic evaluation of their stability under typical food processing conditions, with critical factors such as thermal processing and freeze–thaw cycle being notably under-evaluate. Meanwhile, while several biomacromolecules, including milk proteins[5], chitosan[6] and zein[7], have proven effective as Pickering stabilizers, the limited variety of available food-based stabilizers hinders their broader utilization in the food industry. Consequently, advancing food-grade Pickering stabilizers demands urgent research attention.

Chickpea protein isolate (CPI), containing all eight essential amino acids with particularly high lysine content − an amino acid typically deficient in cereal proteins[8] combined with its low allergenicity and high digestibility[9], positions CPI as a promising functional food-grade ingredient. The amphiphilic nature of proteins, arising from their hydrophobic and hydrophilic amino acid sequences, makes CPI a promising candidate for utilization as a stabilizer for Pickering emulsions[10]. However, like most plant proteins, CPI’s capacity to adsorb at the air–water interface is constrained by their dense structure[11]. This limitation was corroborated by Hei et al. in their comparative evaluation of plant protein isolates (soybean. pea, mung bean, chickpea, and chia seed) for stabilizing Pickering emulsions and ice cream[12]. They indicated that CPI demonstrated the least favorable interfacial stabilization performance among the tested protein systems. Although nutritionally valuable, the practical application of CPI as a standalone emulsifier remains challenging due to its propensity to induce emulsion aggregation and flocculation, which ultimately destabilizes the systems.

As a novel food physical processing means, the core mechanism of action of ultrasonic (US) originates from the vibration of mechanical waves carrying a frequency higher than 20 kHz[10]. In recent times, US treatment has gained great interest as a safe and effective food processing technology, particularly low-frequency high-intensity ultrasound which is predominantly employed in various food industry[13]. Our previous work demonstrated that high-intensity ultrasound treatment at 300 W for 5–20 min resulted in a notable improvement in solubility and foaming capacity of CPI, with foaming ability increasing by 2.2-fold in comparison with the untreated samples[14]. Similarly, Bi et al. observed that US treatment at varying power (60–300 W) and duration (15–30 min) efficiently decreased particle size and improved dispersion uniformity of chickpea protein aggregates[15], while Zhang et al. demonstrated that high-intensity ultrasound (100–500 W, 20 min) concurrently increased surface hydrophobicity, decreased interfacial tension and enhanced interfacial properties of CPI[16]. Nevertheless, the capacity of US-modified CPI as an O/W Pickering emulsion stabilizer remains unexplored. Notably, there exists a significant research gap regarding the thermal and freeze–thaw stability of Pickering emulsions stabilized with US-modified CPI, representing a critical area for future investigation.

Therefore, this research investigated the potential of US-treated CPI in enhancing the Pickering emulsion stability. We systematically evaluated the structural modifications of CPI induced by varying US power intensities (0, 150, 300, 450, and 600 W) and subsequently examined their impact on O/W Pickering emulsion stabilization. The emulsion characteristics were comprehensively assessed through droplet morphology analysis, while the thermal and freeze–thaw stability were quantitatively determined using macroscopic visualization coupled with particle size measurements. These integrated analytical approaches elucidated the factors governing CPI's functional enhancement under varying US power levels and rigorously evaluate its potential as an effective Pickering emulsion stabilizer under different processing conditions.

Materials and methods

Materials

Kabuli chickpea (Cicer arietinum L.) seeds were provided by the Zhonghejianyuan Biotechnology Co (Heze, Shandong, China), with a chemical composition including 14.1 % protein, 2.7 % fat, 48.1 % starch, and 17.6 % dietary fiber. Commercial Jinlongyu soybean oil (Yihai Kerry Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was bought from a local supermarket (Zhengzhou, Henan, China). Nile Red, Nile Blue A, 8-Anilino-1-naphthalenesulfonic acid (ANS) and sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Disodium hydrogen phosphate and sodium dihydrogen phosphate were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. (Shanghai, China). Reagents employed in the current investigation were of analytical grade or higher.

Preparation of CPI

The protocol followed Zhang et al. with minor modifications[17]. Whole chickpeas with intact grains were selected, mechanically crushed, and the resulting powder was collected. The powder was then passed through an 80-mesh sieve to ensure uniformity. Subsequently, the sieved powder was dried in a 50 °C oven until a constant weight was achieved (final moisture content: 3.85 %), yielding the crude protein powder. The defatted powder samples were obtained through solvent extraction, followed by storage at appropriate conditions for further analysis.

Defatted chickpea powder was hydrated (1:8, w/v, mg/mL) in deionized water, and the pH was acidified to 9.0 using 2 mol/L NaOH. A 100 mL alkaline suspension was aliquoted to a 150 mL double-walled beaker and subjected to ultrasonication (20 kHz, 400 W) (SCIENTZ-IID, Ningbo Xinzhi Bio-Tech Co., China) with a 13 mm probe immersed at 1.5 cm depth. Pulsed ultrasonication (2 s on, 4 s off) was applied for 10 min of effective treatment time, with temperature controlled below 20°C via ice-water bath. Real-time temperature monitoring was conducted with an integrated probe.

The ultrasonicated samples were subjected to centrifugation at 6000 r/min for 15 min using a refrigerated high-speed centrifuge (himac CR21N, Hitachi, Japan) to eliminate insoluble components. After acidification to pH 4.9 using 1 mol/L HCl, the supernatant was centrifuged (6,000 r/min, 15 min) to remove insoluble substances, supernatant pH was neutralized to 7.0 for dialysis. The dialyzed sample was freeze-dried under vacuum (Lab-1–50, Beijing Boyikang Experimental Instrument Co., Ltd., Beijing China), and the purified CPI was stored in airtight bags at 4°C for further analysis.

Ultrasound treatment

Proteins were dissolved in phosphate buffer solution (PBS) at a mass concentration of 20 mg/mL, which was processed using magnetic stirring for 2 h at 25 °C, followed by ultrasonication. The experimental procedure was based on Li et al. with minor modifications[18]. A 100 mL CPI suspension was transferred into a 150 mL double-walled beaker with a 13 mm ultrasonic probe immersed about 1.5 cm under the liquid and kept at a fixed distance from the temperature sensor (SCIENTZ-IID, Ningbo Xinzhi Bio-Tech Co., China), and the whole process was performed with continuous ice-water cooling to control the temperature below 20℃. US treatments were performed under the following conditions: 20 kHz, 0, 150, 300, 450 and 600 W, effective time 10 min, and 2 s on/4 s off. All treatments were performed in triplicate on separate occasions to ensure repeatability.

Particle size and zeta potential of CPI

The protein dispersion was adjusted to 1 mg/mL with PBS. For particle size determinations, 1 mL aliquots of the prepared sample were transferred to a standard colorimetric cuvette[19]. Place the sample in the Malvern zeta potential sample cell DTS1070 to measure the zeta potential. Triplicate measurements were assessed at 25 °C with a Malvern Nano-ZS90 laser particle size analyzer (Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, UK).

Structural characteristics of CPI

Spectroscopic methods

Circular dichroism (CD) analysis

The method was adapted from Kim et al., incorporating several modifications[20]. Briefly, the CPI solution was prepared at 10 mg/mL with PBS and centrifuged (10,000 r/min, 15 min) to remove insoluble matter. Following concentration measurement, supernatant was aliquoted to 0.1 mg/mL for subsequent analysis. Circular dichroism (CD) spectra were recorded using a Chirascan spectropolarimeter (Applied Photophysics Ltd., Surrey, UK) with the parameters set as follows: bandwidth of 1.0 nm and scan range of 190–260 nm. Contents of secondary protein structural elements were calculated using the CD Spectra Deconvolution (CDNN) software. Each sample was measured in triplicate.

Fluorescence spectroscopy

Intrinsic fluorescence spectra of US-modified CPI were measured using a Hitachi F-7000 fluorescence spectrophotometer (Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan)[21]. Samples were prepared by dissolving CPI in PBS at a concentration of 0.1 mg/mL. Fluorescence spectra were acquired (λex = 290 nm; λem = 290–430 nm) with 5 nm slit widths and 1200 nm/min scanning speed. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate.

Ultraviolet absorption spectroscopy

US-modified CPI was diluted to 1 mg/mL in PBS, and ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectra (200–450 nm) were recorded using a TU-1810 spectrophotometer (Purkinye General Instrument Co., Beijing, China) with buffer as blank. Measurements were conducted in triplicate.

Surface hydrophobicity (H0)

Surface hydrophobicity of US-modified CPI was assessed with ANS (8 mmol/L in PBS, pH 7.0) as a fluorescent probe[22]. CPI solutions (0.02, 0.04, 0.06, 0.08 and 0.1 mg/mL) were vortexed with 100 μL ANS in 4 mL aliquots in the dark, and fluorescence intensity was assessed (F-7000, Hitachi, Japan) at 370 nm excitation and 490 nm emission. Triplicate measurements were conducted for each sample.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM)

The morphological alterations of CPI induced by ultrasonic treatment were investigated using field emission scanning electron microscopy (Regulus 8100, Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The obtained freezing dried CPIs were mounted on the conductive tape of the SEM sample table, and then observed after gold spraying treatment. SEM conditions are set as follows: acceleration voltage 10 kV, magnification factor 500 × .

Pickering emulsions preparation

The Pickering emulsions were fabricated according to the approach of Kim et al. with minor modifications[20]. The ultrasonically treated protein dispersion was employed as the aqueous phase, and the soybean oil was mixed with the protein dispersion in the ratio of 1:1 (v/v, mL/mL), and dispersed at high speed for 2 min using a high-speed homogenizer (Ultra T25, IKA, Staufen, Germany) at an invariable speed of 10,000 r/min to obtain the Pickering emulsions. All operations were played in triplicate using independently prepared Pickering emulsions.

Droplet size and zeta potential of Pickering emulsions

The mean particle size of the Pickering emulsions was measured using a laser particle sizer (LS13320/ULM2, Beckman Coulter, California, USA) at 25℃ according to Ma et al. and the results were showed as volume-weighted mean diameter (d4,3)[20]. The refractive indices of the oil phase and water phase are 1.47 and 1.33, respectively. Measurements were conducted in triplicate for each treatment.

Zeta potential of Pickering emulsions was measured in accordance with Bi et al.'s method[15]. Following a 100-fold dilution with deionized water, 1 mL aliquots were recorded using a Malvern Nano-ZS90 zeta potential analyzer (Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, UK). Every measurement was conducted at 25 °C in triplicate to ensure credibility.

Emulsifying activity index (EAI) and emulsifying stability index (ESI) determination

The emulsification properties were analyzed following a modified protocol adapted from Wang et al.[14]. A 10 mg/mL US-modified CPI suspension was blended with soybean oil (4:1, w/v, g/mL) and homogenized (10,000 r/min, 2 min) with an Ultra T25 homogenizer (IKA, Staufen, Germany). For analysis, 50 μL aliquots from the bottom phase were aliquoted 100-fold in 0.1 % SDS (w/v, g/mL) and the absorbance (A0) was immediately recoded at 500 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Purkinye General Instrument, Beijing, China). After 10 min of quiescent standing, the absorbance (A10) was remeasured. Both EAI and ESI were determined using established formulas.(1)EAIm2/g=(2×2.303×N×A0)/(10,000×ω×C)(2)ESImin=A0/(A0-A10)×10Where N is the dilution factor of 100; C is the mass concentration of CPIN, g/mL; ω is the oil volume fraction, 20 %.

Turbiscan stability index (TSI) determination

A freshly prepared Pickering emulsion (20 mL, method detailed in Section 2.7) was loaded into a dedicated Turbiscan vial and stability was assessed via Turbiscan LAB Expert analyzer (Formulaction, Toulouse, France). Scanning was performed at 60 s intervals over 3600 s, with all measurements conducted in triplicate.

Determination of Pickering emulsions microstructure

Optical microscopy

Pickering emulsions morphology was visualized via optical microscopy (PH50-2A43L-A, Phenix, China). Briefly, 10 μL of Pickering emulsions were deposited on microscope slides, covered, and subsequently imaged in triplicate for analysis.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM)

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (FV3000, Olympus, Japan) was performed following the methodology outlined by Tao et al.[23]. Pickering emulsions were dual-stained with 0.1 % (w/v, g/mL) Nile red (dissolved in ethanol, λex = 488 nm) and Nile blue (dissolved in water, λex = 633 nm) for 30 min in darkness. For each sample, triplicate images were captured at a magnification of 20×, with one representative image presented.

Pickering emulsion stability evaluation

Place 10 mL of Pickering emulsion in a sealed centrifuge tube and store at low temperature (4 °C, 24 h) to obtain a macro image as a control group for comparison with freeze–thaw treatment and heat treatment.

Determination of thermal stability

The thermal stability of Pickering emulsion was evaluated according to Wu's method with appropriate modifications[24]. Pickering emulsion samples (10 mL) in sealed glass tubes were heated at 90 °C for 30 min, then right away cooled in an ice water bath to 25℃. After homogenization using a vortex mixer (MX-S, Dalong Xingchuang Experimental Instrument (Beijing) Co., China) at low speed, the mean particle size was determined in triplicate by laser diffraction (LS13320/ULM2, Beckman Coulter, USA).

Determination of freeze–thaw stability

In the evaluation of freeze–thaw stability, 10 mL Pickering emulsion in sealed glass tubes were subjected to freezing (−20°C, 24 h) followed by thawing (25 °C). Prior to conducting triplicate measurements of particle size, the samples were vortex-mixed at a low speed to achieve homogeneity.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently carried out in triplicate on multiple occasions. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were subjected to analysis using one-way ANOVA (SPSS 21.0, IBM). Significant differences (p < 0.05) were subsequently assessed through Duncan's multiple range test.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of CPI

Particle size and zeta potential of CPI

The stability of Pickering emulsions is strongly influenced by particle size. For protein-based delivery systems, nanoparticles with dimensions less than 250 nm are particularly effective in promoting nanoscale emulsification. As depicted in Fig. 1, CPI exhibited a pronounced reduction in mean particle size (p < 0.05) upon ultrasonication at varying powers compared to the untreated control. Size characterization revealed CPI particles in the nanometer range, hereafter referred to as CPIN. Notably, treatment at 450 W reduced the particle size from 557.46 ± 25.28 nm to 150.13 ± 0.94 nm, corresponding to a 73 % reduction (p < 0.05). This significant reduction could be ascribed to the disruptive influence of ultrasound cavitation, microstreaming, and turbulence forces, which facilitate the breakdown of CPI aggregates, increase surface area, and enhance interparticle interactions. Importantly, the energy input from ultrasonication induces structural rearrangements without hydrolyzing peptide bonds, thereby leading to a considerable decline in particle size[15]. Nevertheless, at increasing ultrasonication power (600 W), an augment in CPI particle size was detected. This phenomenon may result from the faster movement of protein molecules and increased opportunities for collisions between protein molecules caused by high-power US, leading to the re-aggregation of protein molecules into larger particles[25]. CPIN displayed significantly elevated (p < 0.05) absolute zeta potential values following ultrasonication (Fig. 1), with a distinct optimum power intensity observed. The peak increase in absolute potential (from 19.15 mV to 28.1 mV) occurred at 450 W, consistent with earlier research on ultrasonicated sesame protein[26]. This enhancement in surface potential arises from ultrasound-induced cavitation, which facilitates the reorientation internal polar groups towards the protein surface, thus increasing the overall ionic character of the solution[27]. The resultant increase in electrostatic repulsion substantially prevents protein coalescence, thereby leading to superior CPIN dispersion stability. However, an excessive power input exceeding 450 W led to a decrease in absolute potential values, possibly due to protein reaggregation masking surface charge groups[28].

Circular dichroism spectroscopy has demonstrated notable changes in the secondary structure of CPIN induced by ultrasonication (Table 1). An increase ultrasonic power, ranging 0 to 450 W, was found to significantly enhance α-helix content from 16.21 % to 20.25 %, while concurrently decreasing the β-sheet content from 25.34 % to 22.98 % (p < 0.05). No significant variations were observed in the β-turn or random coil contents (p > 0.05). These observed trends align with previously reported effects on peanut protein[29]. Increased α-helix and β-sheet fractions indicate CPI structural dissociation, presumably mediated by cavitation-driven cleavage of non-covalent bonds[30]. This leads to the de-folding of β-sheets, which may result in a transformation to α-helix[15], and the diminished random coil content reflects improved structural organization. Fluorescence spectra

Intrinsic fluorescence spectra of CPIN revealed power-dependent alterations in tertiary structural (Fig. 2A). At 450 W, the maximum fluorescence intensity exhibited a 36.4 % increase (from 6054.5 to 8261.5) with concomitant redshift of 2 nm in maximum emission wavelength. The observation suggests an exposure of tryptophan residues and an augmentation in the polarity of the microenvironment. Such modifications reflect partial protein unfolding and subsequent aromatic amino acid (particularly tryptophan) solvent exposure, which in return increases the quantum yield[31]. This trend correlates with surface hydrophobicity measurements, confirming structural rearrangement. Strikingly, excessive sonication (600 W) made fluorescence intensity decrease, indicative of protein reaggregation and chromophore burial[15], a manifestation that has also been documented in yeast protein systems[32]. Therefore, it can be concluded that optimal ultrasonication (≤450 W) effectively modulates protein conformation, thereby enhancing its functional properties.Fig. 2Effect of different ultrasonic powers on intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy (A), ultraviolet absorption spectroscopy (B) and surface hydrophobicity (C) the of CPIN. Ultraviolet absorption spectroscopy

UV absorption at 280 nm, which is indicative of aromatic residues including tyrosine, phenylalanine, and tryptophan, revealed that ultrasonication induces structural alterations in CPIN (Fig. 2B). The absorption intensity exhibited a biphasic response to increasing ultrasonic power, initially decreasing before subsequently increasing. This trend suggests that low-power ultrasonication may facilitate protein unfolding and expose hydrophobic groups, thus attenuating aromatic amino acid absorption[33]. At 600 W ultrasonic power, a notable rise in UV absorption intensity was recorded. This effect may be attributed to excessive ultrasonication, which could enhance protein cross-linking, potentially trapping hydrophobic moieties and aromatic amino acids within an aggregated structure. These findings are consistent with complementary intrinsic fluorescence (Fig. 2A) and surface hydrophobicity (Fig. 2C) measurements, collectively indicating structural reorganization.

Surface hydrophobicity

Protein surface hydrophobicity plays a critical role in maintaining tertiary structure stability and determining interfacial functionality[15]. As evidenced by Fig. 2C, a progressive rise in ultrasonic power significantly affected the surface hydrophobicity of CPI. Specifically, the group subjected to 450 W ultrasound treatment shows a remarkable enhancement in surface hydrophobicity (p < 0.05), approximately 20.29 % greater than the untreated group. However, a continuous elevation in ultrasonic power to 600 W caused a decline in surface hydrophobicity (p < 0.05), but was still higher than that of the control group. In agreement with current results, Zheng et al. researched that surface hydrophobicity of yeast proteins treated with ultrasound treatment (20 kHz, 100–600 W) also exhibited a parabolic trend with increasing ultrasonic power, peaking at 450 W before diminishing[32]. The initial increase in surface hydrophobicity at the lower power range likely results from the dissociation and rearrangement of certain protein aggregates or subunit structures mediated by the ultrasound-induced cavitation. This process can expose originally hidden hydrophobic groups in the interior, thereby enhancing the accessibility of active regions on protein surfaces. Consequently, these exposed hydrophobic groups can be more easily combined with ANS, which resulted in increased protein surface hydrophobicity[34]. Additionally, some hydrophobic groups that were initially located on the surface of soluble proteins may have migrated, further leading to an augment in the hydrophobicity of the protein surface[15]. Enhanced hydrophobic group exposure facilitates interfacial interaction with lipid phases, significantly improving the emulsification performance of proteins[35]. This observation can be explained by effective decrease in protein particle size due to US treatment, consequently increasing the accessible molecular surface area[36]. Conversely, the decrease in surface hydrophobicity at ultrasonic power above 450 W may be related to protein structural rearrangement during denaturation/re-aggregation, resulting in hydrophobic residue burial, minimizing their interaction with hydrophobic probes used in the assay.

SEM

SEM results demonstrated notable microstructural changes in CPI following ultrasonication, as evidenced by Fig. 3. Untreated CPI exhibited large and irregularly shaped aggregates, with the majority the proteins being stabilized by robust intermolecular interactions, resulting in an insoluble aggregate state[17]. Ultrasonication (450 W) resulted in the fragmentation of lamellar structures, accompanied by an increase porosity and homogeneity, which corresponded with observed trends in particle size reduction. This structural reorganization is presumably attributable to the impact of sonication on CPI, wherein collapse of bubbles caused by ultrasonic cavitation leads to the formation of transient high temperatures as well as shear forces generated by microfluidics and shockwaves. These phenomena disrupt the stabilizing forces between the CPI peptide chains, leading to denaturation of the protein and opening of the structure, thereby facilitating the formation of abundant and homogeneous nanoparticles of CPI proteins[22]. In contrast, excessive treatment (600 W) led to irregular reaggregation due to oxidation and excessive localized heating despite external cooling, which was consistent with findings from prior studies[37,38].Fig. 3Effect of different ultrasound powers on the microstructure of CPIN. Mean particle size and zeta potential of Pickering emulsions

The average droplet diameter represents an essential indicator of emulsion droplet aggregation and nanoparticle functionality in Pickering emulsions. As demonstrated in Fig. 4, emulsions stabilized with native CPIN exhibited relatively large droplet sizes (34.44 ± 0.15 μm), whereas ultrasonic treatment at 450 W significantly reduced the average particle size to 27.91 ± 0.01 μm (p < 0.05). On the contrary, an augment in droplet size was displayed with excessive sonication at 600 W, which aligns with findings of optical microscopy (Fig. 9A). This phenomenon is attributed to cavitation-induced protein particle size reduction, structural unfolding, and enhanced interfacial adsorption efficiency, leading to smaller emulsion droplets[39]. Similar behavior was reported for walnut protein-stabilized emulsions, where smaller nanoparticles yielded reduced emulsion droplet sizes[40]. The improved emulsification capacity and stability observed at 450 W reflect enhanced interfacial homogeneity and adsorption, ultimately resulting in superior emulsion stability[41].Fig. 4Effect of different ultrasound powers on the average particle size and zeta potential of Pickering emulsions. Zeta potential, a key parameter of emulsion stability, reflects inter-droplet electrostatic interactions in emulsion systems. Elevated absolute values (>30 mV) typically indicate robust stabilization against droplet aggregation, minimizing flocculation and coalescence[15]. As evidenced by the data in Fig. 4, zeta potential of CPIN-stabilized Pickering emulsions exhibited a pronounced (p < 0.05) biphasic response to ultrasonic power, peaking at 450 W (50.53 ± 0.35 mV), mirroring trends in particle size. This behavior likely stems from ultrasound-induced structural reorganization of CPIN, which enhances intermolecular interactions (electrostatic and hydrophobic) by exposing previously buried functional groups[15]. These findings align with colloidal stability theory, wherein zeta potentials exceeding ± 30 mV confer sufficient electrostatic repulsion to stabilize emulsions[15]. Comparable effects have been reported in ultrasonicated pea protein isolates, where reduced zeta potentials coincided with improved emulsification performance[42]. Further corroboration comes from Silva et al., who observed that extended ultrasonication elevated zeta potential magnitudes, directly improving emulsion stability[43].

EAI and ESI

EAI and ESI serve as critical indicators of protein functionality, with EAI denoting the interfacial adsorption capacity and ESI reflecting the resistance to droplet coalescence. As evidenced by the data in Fig. 5A, EAI and ESI of CPIN-stabilized Pickering emulsions exhibited a bell-shaped response to ultrasonic power (0–600 W), peaking at 450 W (p < 0.05). Specifically, EAI exhibited a marked increase from 18.72 ± 0.09 m2/g to 26.67 ± 0.19 m2/g, while ESI showed comparable enhancement. This optimal performance at moderate ultrasonic power (450 W) can be attributed to two primary factors: (1) conformational rearrangement of protein that exposes hydrophobic domains, thereby improving interfacial adsorption[15]; and (2) a reduction in particle size through cavitation-induced disruption of protein aggregates, enabling higher interfacial coverage and formation of denser protective membranes[44]. Supporting this mechanism, Liu et al. observed that 400 W ultrasound treatment reduced pepper seed protein particle size by 51 % with concomitant 2.31-fold EAI improvement[45]. However, excessive power (>450 W) led to detrimental effects including: (1) protein over-aggregation due to excessive cross-linking, which compromises molecular flexibility and interfacial stability[46]; and (2) oxidative damage caused by ultrasound-generated free radicals (e.g., ·OH), which detrimentally influences emulsification performance[17].Fig. 5Effect of different ultrasound powers on the EAI, ESI (A) and TSI (B)of Pickering emulsions. TSI

TSI provides a quantitative measure of emulsion stability, with lower values signifying superior droplet dispersion and enhanced resistance to phase separation. Fig. 5B revealed that CPIN-stabilized Pickering emulsions displayed characteristic time-dependent TSI increases (0–3600 s) when subjected to various ultrasonic treatments (0–600 W). A pronounced U-shaped response to ultrasonic power was identified (p < 0.05), with 450 W treatment achieving maximum stability (TSI = 3.654 compared to 6.418 for untreated controls), a result that is further substantiated by ESI analysis. This stabilization phenomenon originates from the controlled protein denaturation mediated by optimal ultrasonication. At 450 W, CPIN undergoes partial conformational changes that: (1) expose hydrophobic domains, thereby enhancing interfacial activity; (2) accelerate adsorption kinetics; and (3) facilitate formation of smaller, more thermodynamically stable droplets through interfacial tension reduction[47]. These mechano-acoustic effects align with established theories of emulsion stabilization, which posit that a decrease in interfacial tension is directly consistent with improved system stability[48]. Also, parallel observations by Li et al. regarding wheat germ protein emulsions further substantiate the stabilization mechanism facilitated by ultrasonication[48].

Microstructure of freshly prepared Pickering emulsions

The emulsion stability is inversely correlated with droplet size, as larger oil droplets are prone to aggregation, whereas smaller droplets facilitate enhance system stability. As illustrated in Fig. 6A, Pickering emulsions stabilized by CPIN demonstrated a notable decrease in droplet diameter and enhanced size homogeneity when compared to the untreated control. An increase in ultrasonication power resulted in a larger population of emulsion droplets that were more uniform in shape, evenly distributed, and significantly reduced in size. These observations substantiate the assertion that ultrasonic treatment enhances the stability of CPIN-stabilized Pickering emulsions. Emulsions prepared with untreated CPI contained larger, irregularly sized droplets and displayed pronounced aggregation. This instability likely arises from pronounced interfacial tension at soybean oil-aqueous, which impairs emulsion integrity[49]. Ultrasonication mitigates this issue by modifying protein conformation, promoting structural unfolding, and reducing CPI into smaller nanoparticles. These changes enhance protein anchoring at lipid-aqueous boundaries, lowering interfacial tension and suppressing droplet coalescence, thereby improving emulsion stabilization[50].Fig. 6Effect of different ultrasonic powers on freshly prepared Pickering emulsion optical microscopy (A) and confocal laser scanning microscopy (B).

CLSM imaging was utilized to characterize colloidal microstructure of CPIN-stabilized Pickering emulsions subjected to varying ultrasonic powers (Fig. 6B). The analysis revealed a distinct, tightly adsorbed particle interface at the droplet periphery, confirming presence of an O/W emulsion structure[20]. The oil and aqueous phases were fluorescently labeled with Nile red (green) and Nile blue (red), respectively[23]. Complementary to the mean droplet size analysis (Fig. 6), CLSM visualization provided evidence that ultrasound treatment facilitates the generation of smaller droplets relative to the untreated control. Notably, 450 W ultrasound treatment resulted in emulsions characterized by smaller and more homogeneous droplet dispersion in contrast to the untreated samples. This phenomenon can be explained by ultrasonication-induced effects, which includes (1) protein particle fragmentation, (2) structural unfolding of proteins, and (3) an increase in nanoparticle formation within the continuous phase. The resulting protein aggregates exhibited rigid interfacial behavior, which impeded the dynamic adsorption behavior at fluid interfaces and restricted the reorganization of the interfacial film. As a result, adjacent oil droplets coalesced, bringing about the generation of a homogeneous, spherical emulsion systems[51]. However, the excessive power (>450 W) increased droplet size, likely due to weakened interfacial adsorption caused by electrostatic repulsion, which aligns with the findings from TSI results.

Heat and freeze–thaw treatment of Pickering emulsions

Thermal stability is an essential characteristic for emulsion-based food products, as inadequate heat resistance can lead to phase separation and structural degradation during processing or storage. As illustrated in Fig. 7A, thermal stability of Pickering emulsions stabilized by ultrasonicated CPI was assessed via droplet size analysis following heat treatment. Although all emulsions exhibited an increase in particle size upon heating, those stabilized by CPI subjected to higher ultrasonication power (up to 450 W) demonstrated significantly less growth in droplet size. This observation indicates that moderate ultrasonication effectively enhances heat resistance of protein-based emulsion systems. The improvement may be attributed to ultrasound-induced protein unfolding, which uncovers both hydrophobic and hydrophilic groups. This structural modification enhances adsorption of protein particles at lipid-aqueous interface, generating a more resilient interfacial architecture which withstands thermal perturbation[52]. Cavitation effect generated during ultrasonication facilitates this process by promoting molecular rearrangement without excessive fragmentation. However, excessive ultrasonication power (>450 W) undermined these benefits, likely due to the protein over-processing that compromises their interfacial functionality. At higher power, excessive fragmentation or irreversible structural deformation may impair the protein’s emulsifying capacity, leading to weaker interfacial films and larger emulsion droplets[52]. Freeze-thaw stability is a critical determinant of shelf life for emulsion-based food products, particularly since freezing is frequently employed during transportation and storage. However, traditional emulsions often exhibit poor stability under freezing conditions, where crystallization of the oil phase can lead to sedimentation, creaming, and eventual phase separation[54]. In accordance with the results demonstrated in Fig. 7B, Pickering emulsions stabilized by CPIN showed markedly reduced emulsion breakdown and smaller average particle sizes in contrast to the untreated samples, with a 41.87 % reduction in emulsion size after freeze–thaw treatment. The experimental findings indicate that ultrasonic treatment significantly enhances freeze–thaw stability of CPIN-stabilized emulsions. The instability of emulsions during freezing arises primarily from the crystallization of water, which occurs prior to the oil crystallization of oil. This process displaces and concentrates emulsion particles in unfrozen water regions, promoting droplet aggregation and the formation of large fat clusters[51,55]. Moreover, ice crystal growth can mechanically disrupt the protein interfacial membrane, leading to emulsion breakdown upon thawing. Ultrasonication alleviates these adverse effects by inducing structural unfolding of CPIN, which enhances protein adsorption at oil-aqueous interface and fortifies hydrophobic interactions among interfacial protein molecules. This results in a more resilient and cohesive interfacial layer that is better equipped to withstand mechanical damage from ice crystals and oil droplet deformation. Furthermore, development of the compact network throughout the emulsion matrix aids in preventing oil–water separation, thereby improving freeze–thaw performance[56].

Conclusion

This research investigated the impacts of ultrasonic power (0–600 W) on CPIN structure and its Pickering emulsions stabilization. The key findings of this study can be summarized as follows: moderate ultrasonication at 450 W was identified as the optimal condition for reducing the particle size of CPI, while concurrently preserving a balanced protein secondary structure—as reflected in the α-helix and β-sheet content—and modifying surface properties. This structural modulation resulted in significantly improved emulsification performance: emulsions prepared with 450 W-treated CPI exhibited smaller droplet sizes, enhanced physical stability, and superior resistance to both heat and freeze–thaw stresses compared to those stabilized with native CPI. These functional enhancements are primarily attributed to the synergistic mechanisms of nanoparticle formation, improved interfacial anchoring capacity, and increased electrostatic repulsion between droplets. Although these findings support the potential application of ultrasonicated CPI as a fat substitute in meat products, it is important to note that the optimal ultrasonication power may vary depending on the protein source or specific equipment used. Future research should focus on establishing quantitative models that link processing parameters to structural and functional properties, as well as validating the scalability of this treatment for industrial applications. The identified 450 W ultrasonication protocol offers a practical approach for engineering CPI into effective emulsifiers and potential bioactive delivery vehicles.
