A compliant, morphing gripper for handling diverse leafy vegetables in vertical farming systems
Authors: Jiajun Liu, Xing Yu Chen, Huu Duoc Nguyen, Wenjie Lai, Joel Ming Rui Tan, Soo Jay Phee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobal food security faces growing pressure from climate change and limited resources. In land-constrained Singapore, vertical farming is expanding to improve resilience. However, this expansion has a bottleneck: many varieties of delicate leafy vegetables still rely on manual harvesting. The task is labor-intensive, and hard to scale. We address this need with a two-finger pinching gripper that has compliant, morphing fingertips. The fingertips collapse to enter narrow gaps between densely packed plants, expand to create soft contact surfaces, and reconfigure to accommodate different plant geometries. They also pinch to secure a firm grasp when needed. We validated the approach through bench tests and a trial at a local farm. The gripper consistently grasped multiple leafy-vegetable types and completed lettuce harvesting when used with a root trimmer. It maintained plant integrity and no immediate visible damage was observed. The fingertips also handled real-world variations, off-center growth, size changes, and irregular shapes, without re-tuning. These results establish a practical first step toward automated harvesting in Singapore’s vertical farms and highlight next steps in perception, motion planning, and system-level line integration.Keywords: Soft gripper, Morphing gripper, Agriculture, Vertical farm systemSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Engineering, Environmental sciences, Plant sciences

Introduction

Food supply disruptions are rising worldwide due to climate change and resource constraints. In response, Singapore launched the “30by30” initiative1 to build local capability and capacity to produce 30% of the nation’s nutritional needs by 2030 in a productive, resource-efficient, and climate-resilient way.

Because land is limited, Singapore emphasizes vertical farming systems (VFS) for sustainable urban food production. VFS stack production facilities to use indoor space efficiently and maintain fine-tuned environments for optimal yields2. This approach uses less land and water and provides climate resilience, though it requires additional electrical energy and careful chemical management. Custom subsystems regulate nutrient concentrations in recirculating irrigation, as well as lighting, temperature, and humidity3. With this fine control, VFS can target product qualities such as taste, aroma, appearance, nutritional content, and even pharmaceutical value4.

VFS infrastructure is modular and standardized in function, size, and location, which favors automation. Robotic systems with dedicated end-effectors are being developed for tasks across the cultivation stages, including seeding5, seedling handling6, transplanting7, pruning8, and harvesting9. Among these, harvesting remains the most manual step in many VFS. It is time-critical, must be synchronized with upstream and downstream processes, and presents challenges in worker safety and training, reachability, crop diversity, damage control, and contamination control. Manual harvesting is flexible across crop types, but it is costly and labor-intensive4.

Harvest strategies also differ by the edible portion. Some crops are consumed as a whole; others are separated into leaves, stems, roots, or fruits. Depending on growth habits, leftover plant parts may be retained for regrowth or discarded to free the related facilities. These differences in consumable parts and growth patterns drive distinct harvesting methods and tool requirements for each vegetable.

Locally, farms prefer leafy vegetables that offer strong consumer acceptance, long post-packaging shelf life, compact form, and neat, regular appearance - traits that improve economic yield. Demand varies by type, but total volume is substantial, so many Singapore VFS run mixed production of multiple leafy varieties. Our collaborating VFS farm produces several leafy vegetables at different throughputs in a dry-hydroponic system10. Major products include crystal lettuces, kailan, naibai, and xiaobaicai, with lettuce demand far exceeding the rest. Crops are arranged on fixed-size floaters. To maximize output and resource use, plants are grown until they nearly cover each floater as shown in Fig. 1a. This creates crowded canopies with leaves touching, leaving very limited space for a gripper to approach and grasp targets. In this study, we therefore focus on whole-plant harvesting of leafy vegetables.

Whole-plant harvesting is labor-intensive in the local setting. Workers first remove the plant from the floater, then trim away the substrate to collect the marketable portion. Loose or yellow leaves are removed before packaging. Handling steps vary slightly by crop. For xiaobaicai, kailan, or naibai, each graspable with one hand, the plant is lifted out and the substrate is cut with scissors. For lettuce shown in Fig. 1a, one-hand grasping risks leaf damage. Workers flip the head out of the floater to expose the substrate and then use a knife to cut it. Fig. 1b shows a side-view of lettuces on a floater; the yellow dashed triangle marks the narrow access space at the side. In practice, farmers must harvest several leafy types in one session, switching techniques and tools as needed.

Several robotic solutions have been proposed for leafy-vegetable harvesting. Liu et al. developed a rigid gripper designed to hold the kailan stem in VFS, concentrating contact on the tougher stem skin to protect fragile leaves11,12. Ma et al. presented a pneumatic gripper for top-down grasping of iceberg lettuce in VFS, using a soft body and controlled pressure to deliver adequate force with reduced damage13. That top-access strategy is feasible when sufficient space exists between plants. In our partner farm, however, plants are grown to the floater’s limits, so neighboring leaves touch and top access is constrained. In open-field conditions, Birrell et al. engineered a robotic harvester for iceberg lettuce that used vision and machine learning to choose cutting height, then lowered a cage-shaped tool to contain and cut the plant before transfer14. In some cases, the tool contained rather than tightly gripped the head and moved it to a conveyor - an insight that informs gentle handling. While these systems show potential, their end-effectors are tailored to specific crops with similar shapes or weights.

Successful gentle handling requires soft contact and a careful balance between contact and holding forces. Soft contact can be achieved with compliant structures15, soft or flexible materials16, pneumatic interfaces17, or combinations thereof18–24. These contacts have been integrated with rigid or soft gripper bodies and various actuators (pneumatic25, jamming26, cable-driven27, etc) to improve adaptability and payload28. Sensors further enhance performance: Liu et al. used a contact-sensor array to estimate contact count and regulate grip force29; soft-interface force sensors enable closed-loop control of contact forces30; slip sensors detect incipient slip so controllers can raise holding force and prevent loss31. However, more sensing and degrees of freedom increase design complexity, size, and control burden, and may reduce robustness.

Jin et al. combined conveying and grasping in a harvesting system and demonstrated clamping and transport of crops such as cabbage, spinach, and celery32. The passive design depended on pre-measured surface friction, compression behavior, and stiffness of the targets. Performance can degrade when conditions change, such as wet leaves or variations in size, weight (e.g., due to harvest timing), or shape.

In summary, there is a clear need in VFS for a gripper that can stably and robustly grasp diverse leafy vegetables with minimal crop damage, despite limited approach space and real-world variations: including off-center growth, size differences, and shape irregularities.

Results

Design of the proposed gripper

Here, we propose a parallel pinch gripper with two rotatable fingers as shown in Fig. 1c&d. Each finger carries a morphable fingertip that switches between a collapsed, reduced-size state as Fig. 1d and an expanded, compliant state as Fig. 1e. The detailed design of the finger is presented in Fig. 1f. Using lettuce as an example, the collapsed fingertip can enter the narrow gaps between densely packed heads as Fig. 1a&b. The cable-driven fingertip enters the confined space as Fig. 1g, and expands without snagging neighboring plants as Fig. 1h. In the expanded state, the fingertip provides a soft, conformal contact that limits leaf damage. Finally, a pitching motion closes the gripper and secures the target as Fig. 1i.

The gripper uses a rack-and-pinion parallel mechanism. The distance between the two finger rotation centers is allowing adaptation to different plant sizes and container layouts. Each finger rotates by , from vertical to horizontal. This added degree of freedom extends the grasp taxonomy from pure pinching as Fig. 1j to a grasp like ”hand scooping” as Fig. 1j in which the proposed gripper wraps partially around the crop from beneath. Please note that the crack indicated by the grey arrow in Fig. 1k was created when performing pinching. For ball-shaped vegetables (e.g., lettuce), a angle allows scooping for stable support. For umbrella-shaped plants (e.g., xiaobaicai, naibai, kale), a up-facing angle can contact the leaves, while a angle can contact the stem area. These settings provide practical options to manage off-center growth, size variation, and shape irregularity.

Fingertip morphing is driven by a movable backbone (MB) actuated by two antagonistic cables, Cable 1 and Cable 2. Both cables terminate at the same end fixtures; their opposite ends are wound on a common drum, with Cable 1 in counter-clockwise direction at top layer and Cable 2 in clockwise direction at second layer, respectively. Cable 1 is routed over the finger, as shown in Fig. 1e,f, to generate an extending action on the MB. When the drum pulls Cable 1, it simultaneously releases Cable 2 by an equal length, and vice versa. Pulling Cable 1 extends the MB as indicated by black arrows in Fig. 1e. As the MB protrudes, it tensions a flexible sheet (FS), which bends the fixed backbone (NB) and forms a bow-shaped compliant structure at the fingertip. This structure creates a wide, soft contact surface that conforms to delicate leaves. Reversing the drum pulls Cable 2 and releases Cable 1. The MB retracts, FS tension drops, and the bow collapses to a flat, low-profile pad Fig. 1d,f. This antagonistic layout yields fast, repeatable shape change with simple position control. The small collapsed profile improves reachability on crowded floaters. The expanded, compliant surface spreads contact pressure to reduce bruising and slip. The adjustable geometry (via and ) allows scooping grasp, to let the same tool accommodate different cultivars and growth states, and to further reduce the damages to the produce. We first assess the compliant fingertip to establish the proposed gripper’s performance.

While the FS material is fixed in our design, leafy vegetables exhibit different surface textures and mechanical properties, leading to variability in the effective friction coefficient. This two-step procedure improved repeatability and provided a robust operating window across targets with different diameters while maintaining gentle contact and minimizing risk of sliding.

Discussion

We selected cable-driven actuation for fingertip morphing due to its simplicity and compact packaging. However, the cable experiences wear as it slides over the rigid fingertip, and installing cables to precise lengths is difficult when fixing them to the drum and end fixture. Excess length produces slack, creating an unwanted dead zone whenever the movable backbone (MB) reverses direction. For example, in Fig. 7d a consistent delay appears between the 1 s tick and the first dot-dash line: the controller commands motion at , but the MB moves at , yielding a dead zone that remained stable over five repeats. The dead zone can be reduced by adding an in-line tensioner or preloading spring. We also measured that MB motion consumed less than of the full motor load and remained stable under fingertip compression. This suggests that alternative drives (e.g., a compact linear actuator) could further minimize dead zone while maintaining the same load capacity.

During payload testing, a 3D-printed sphere was used as a surrogate for lettuce. While we acknowledge that the sphere differs from organic produce in friction, mechanics, and compliance, it was instrumental in characterizing the deformation behavior of the compliant fingertip and narrowing down the effective ranges for the control parameters ( and ).To minimize tissue damage, our primary objective is to avoid excessive contact stress. Consequently, our tuning strategy prioritizes adjusting the rotation angle () first, followed by the clamping distance (). For instance, when handling an 80 mm, 200 g lettuce, an angle of with a clamping distance mm proved optimal. As illustrated in the force diagram in Fig. 4d, this configuration prioritizes vertical lifting force over horizontal compressive force when increasing the finger rotation angle (), thereby minimizing target deformation and reducing reliance on friction. Fig. 4c indicates that the maximum holding force is highly sensitive to parameter changes. This sensitivity stems from the varying contact mechanics shown in Fig. 3b, d, e. At smaller angles, the flexible sheet is steeply inclined, causing the upper edge to contact the target first. This leads to localized stress concentration and substantial deformation, potentially damaging the crop. Conversely, larger angles present a more horizontal contact surface, distributing the load more evenly.Finally, using empirically tuned parameters for each specific crop, the system achieved a 100% success rate (9 out of 9 attempts), successfully grasping and lifting three samples each of lettuce, Nai Bai, and Kailan.

Although the compliant design adapts across shapes, weights, and grasp locations, this trial-and-error process will not be scaled to the full range of sizes present in VFS operations. The paper explains the rationale behind the scooping grasp, but we still need a quantitative mapping from target shape/size to gripper control parameters (, ) that guarantees successful, low-damage grasps. Practically, this requires a vision-guided controller to estimate geometry, select holding/cutting locations, and compute and automatically.

We intentionally used slow, constant-speed motions. This avoids (i) impacts on tall vegetables (e.g., kailan, kale) that could induce tilting and grasp failure; (ii) poking interactions in crowded heads (e.g., lettuce, xiaobaicai) that risk leaf damage; and (iii) sudden squeeze events that can bruise leaves or stems. The trade-off is longer cycle time. Future work will: (1) optimize the station layout to simplify motion planning and reduce travel; (2) add vision-in-the-loop control at the gripper to proactively avoid hits and pokes; and (3) run additional pinch-parameter studies32 to identify safe, high-throughput operating points for each crop.

To further reduce slippage, the gripper’s camera provides a clear, in-gripper view of the target. High-frame-rate tracking of relative movement between fingers and target can indicate incipient slip; this feedback can command a small reduction in to raise holding force. Complementarily, a flexible contact-sensor array on the FS could close the loop on contact force to prevent slip while maintaining gentle, steady pressure during transport. These additions would improve robustness and adaptability without sacrificing delicacy.

As shown in Fig. 7, the fixed-position root trimmer requires added alignment mechanisms, which impose two constraints: (1) only small lateral/rotational misalignment is tolerable for inserting the substrate into the trimmer, and (2) the bottom of the gripper must remain at least above the substrate to avoid collisions with the trimmer. These constraints restrict both parameter choices and feasible grasp locations. A future alternative is to mimic the human procedure by adding a second robot with scissors, using vision to align the cut dynamically.

While this study conducted preliminary small-sample tests to demonstrate the usability and adaptability of the proposed gripper, the overall system efficiency was not quantitatively analyzed. In this context, efficiency depends on two factors: produce shelf-life and harvest cycle time. In our experiments, motor speeds were intentionally reduced to prevent abrupt impact and minimize tissue damage, as mechanical trauma directly accelerates post-harvest degradation. Furthermore, ensuring extended shelf-life requires the periodic sanitization of gripper contact surfaces to eliminate microbial pathogens. Consequently, future work must optimize the trade-off between increasing harvesting speed and maintaining produce quality. As the current prototype represents an early-stage proof of concept, we aim to collaborate with agricultural partners to validate the design in a complete workflow—incorporating cleaning and packaging stages—to achieve a balance of low cycle time, minimal damage, and maximum shelf-life.

We also tested everyday objects as Fig. 8. In Fig. 8a–c, a half-filled PET bottle, a cuboid plastic box, and a small aluminum can were grasped using the pinching taxonomy. The FS deformed differently in each case, generating sufficient friction to hold the objects, similar to other pinching grippers. Note that FS edges can hook into surface features (e.g., the bottle’s ribs), which greatly increases payload; this is acceptable for rigid objects but undesirable for fragile produce. In Fig. 8d–g, the gripper lifted a masking tape at multiple fingertip locations (middle/near top edge, distal/near top edge, proximal/near top edge, and middle/near bottom edge) using the same pinching distance, demonstrating spatial adaptability. In Fig. 8h, the extending/expanding fingertip was used to pick up a towel from a table. The fingertip could hold a potato chip without propagating an existing crack as Fig. 8i by just pinching. For a scooping grasp on a soft, heavy, low-friction item such as a wet jelly pudding as Fig. 8j, the grasp succeeded but produced notable squeezing; this highlights the need for better friction-aware control and shape-aware control when handling extremely delicate foods.

Methods

3D printing of parts

Custom rigid components for the gripper, root trimmer, and testbeds were 3D printed on a Bambu Lab X1C using Bambu Lab Polylactic acid (PLA) Basic (default printing parameters33 in Bambu Studio ). Specifically, two boards and four color markers were printed in orange and green, respectively; supports and the rigid finger were printed in black and orange. Soft components (the flexible sheet, FS) were printed on a Prusa MK3S equipped with a DropEffect OmniaDrop V2 extruder, using Filaflex Thermoplastic Polyurethane (TPU) 60A (default Filaflex settings34).

Fabrication of the finger

The finger assembly comprised two long end fixtures, one short end fixture, a rigid finger, a DYNAMIXEL XL430-W250-T motor, the FS, a set of movable backbones (MB), a set of fixed backbones (FB), a cable, and a drum. The FB and MB elements were cut from off-the-shelf superelastic nitinol strips (width , thickness ). FBs were bonded into distal slots on the rigid finger; a long end fixture was glued to their proximal ends. MBs were placed into dedicated channels; a long end fixture was glued to their distal ends and a short end fixture to their proximal ends.

A multi-strand, PTFE-coated cable (ASAHI-INTECC, diameter) was knotted around a stainless crimp bead (inner diameter ) and the proximal MB short end fixture, then crimped to lock the knot. The cable side routed along the MB is denoted Cable 1; the return side is Cable 2. Cable 1 was routed through a tip channel on the rigid finger and wound on the drum’s top layer counter-clockwise . Cable 2 was routed through a mid-finger hole and wound on the drum’s bottom layer clockwise. Both cables were fixed to the drum; additional stainless crimp beads ( inner diameter) served as anti-slip end stops at the drum’s locking features. The drum was mounted to the motor output, and the FS was bonded to the long end fixtures of the MB and FB sets.

Gripper assembly

The gripper consisted of a main frame, a pinion, two racks, two finger adapters, two completed fingers, and three DYNAMIXEL XM430-W350-T motors. Two motors were fastened to the racks; a third motor was fixed to the main frame and coupled to the pinion. Each completed finger was attached to a finger adapter on the rack-mounted motors. The racks were then inserted into the frame to mesh with the central pinion, forming the parallel pinching mechanism. Motors were connected to a DYNAMIXEL U2D2 interface, providing power and PC communication. A Python script commanded position and velocity profiles.

Root-trimmer assembly

The trimmer included two low-profile microtome blades (), a main frame, blade holders, two racks, two pinions, a long-tail limit switch, an Arduino microcontroller, two DYNAMIXEL XL430-W250-T motors (for bottom and front alignment), and two DYNAMIXEL XM430-W350-T motors (for blade motion). The XM430 motors were mounted left/right to drive opposing blades via rack-pinion stages. The XL430 motors actuated a bottom alignment block and a front alignment block. The long-tail limit switch at the trimmer entrance was wired to the Arduino to detect substrate contact. Two stainless shims () were glued to a rotary element to form the bottom alignment block; the front block was 3D printed. Blades were secured to their holders with screws and cyanoacrylate, then the holders were mounted on the racks and engaged with the motor pinions.

Characterizing fingertip expansion

The coordinate origin was defined at the rigid-finger base (Fig. 2a). All parts were mounted on a flat aluminum tooling plate. A camera (Canon R50) recorded fingertip expansion from top-view. Python code synchronized motor commands and motor-load logging. After recording started, the sequence was: 1-s waiting; motor-drum rotation to 2500 encoder steps (4096 steps/rev) at constant speed (profile speed ); 2-s waiting; retraction to the initial pose at the same speed; 1-s waiting. Five cycles were executed.

Video frames were extracted and the image origin and axes recorded. Frames were converted to grayscale using the green channel, thresholded to binary, and green fiducials were detected. Fiducial pixel centers were mapped to local coordinates, and fingertip angles were computed from these coordinates using the equations defined in the main text.

Farm demonstration: lettuce

The UR5E robotic arm, harvesting station (with floater), root trimmer, and collection bin were arranged as in Fig. 1. All motors interfaced through a DYNAMIXEL U2D2. A RGB-D camera (Intel RealSense d435i) was placed at the top of the floater to capture the floater top-view. A camera and a phone documented the process from front and side respectively; a web camera on the gripper captured in-hand video. The floater was transferred from the production stacks; two random heads in the first row were removed to mimic missing heads in the production; the entire second row was removed to facilitate video taking.

The perception pipeline segmented lettuce heads using SAM2 on the top-view image and computed projected areas. A user-set threshold on area selected targets for harvest. Selected heads were processed left-to-right. For each head, the gripper grasped, transferred it to the trimmer for root removal, and then placed it in the collection bin. Perception, motion planning for this fixed pick-and-place, UR5E control, and gripper control were integrated in a ROS 2 program.

Farm demonstration: other vegetables

For naibai, xiaobaicai, kailan, and kale, the robot and harvesting station layout matched the lettuce demo. Selected vegetables were placed into designated floater slots. The ROS 2 program executed the grasping sequence and a vertical lift (base ) to validate stable handling.
