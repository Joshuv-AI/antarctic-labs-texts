Antifungal Effects of Non-Thermal Atmospheric Pressure Plasma In Vitro and Ex Vivo
Authors: Hye-Jin Ahn, Jin-Woo Lee, Woo Yeon Hwang, Byung Su Kwon, Ki-Heon Jeong, Min Kyung Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-thermal atmospheric pressure plasma (NTAP) generates reactive oxygen species, reactive nitrogen species, and ultraviolet radiation, which can inactivate microorganisms. Onychomycosis treatment is challenging, and its prognosis is poor owing to mixed infections and dermatophytosis. Although NTAP has shown in vitro antifungal effects against dermatophytes and yeast, its efficacy against non-dermatophyte molds (NDMs) and in clinical or nail model studies remains poorly understood.ObjectiveWe evaluated the effects of NTAP on fungi, including NDMs, and infected nail plates.MethodsFor the in vitro experiments, Trichophyton rubrum, Candida albicans, Aspergillus fumigatus, and Fusarium oxysporum strains were exposed to NTAP. After NTAP exposure (2,4 and 6 minutes), growth curve, cell viability, and biofilm biomass were assessed by absorbance wavelength of 600 nm, XTT assay, and crystal violet staining, respectively. For the ex vivo experiments, infected nail plates were analyzed using a scanning electron microscope.ResultsT. rubrum and C. albicans showed greater growth inhibition with increasing NTAP exposure time, whereas A. fumigatus showed enhanced growth after 6 minutes exposure. Many fungal elements within the subungual hyperkeratosis of the ex vivo specimen were all damaged following NTAP exposure.ConclusionNTAP has antifungal effects on dermatophytes, yeast, and NDMs. We suggest that the intensity and time of NTAP application should be adjusted according to each strain and can be more effective when NTAP directly reaches the hyphae on the nail bed or subungual hyperkeratosis.

INTRODUCTION

Onychomycosis accounts for approximately 50% of all nail diseases worldwide. Treatment of onychomycosis is challenging; complete cure was achieved in only 25%–50% of patients receiving standard courses of therapy12. The recurrence rate is reported to range from 10%–53%3; treatment difficulties include mixed-infections with dermatophyte and non-dermatophyte molds (NDMs), biofilm formation, and host factors, including poor peripheral circulation, co-infection of tinea pedis, and thick nail plate. Mixed infections have been reported in 3%–11% of onychomycosis cases. However, the rate could be higher4. The most frequently isolated NDMs globally are Fusarium, Aspergillus, Scopulariopsis brevicaulis, Neoscytalidium dimidiatum, and Acremonium4. There have been increased reports of Aspergillus infection in Asia, where Aspergillus sp. were the most frequently isolated NDMs, followed by Scopulariopsis brevicaulis, Alternaria, and Fusarium 25. NDMs invade the nail plate and survive on unkeratinized intercellular cement previously damaged by trauma, an existing nail disorder, or a dermatophyte infection4. Several studies suggest that, unlike dermatophytes, these fungi respond poorly to systemic antifungal agents, and some, such as Fusarium sp., possess intrinsic resistance6. Some nails retained NDMs after therapy successfully eliminated dermatophytes7.

Fungal organisms may group to form biofilms in the nail rather than acting as independent spores and hyphae8. The sessile biofilm microbial community, encased within the extracellular matrix (ECM), is considered the primary virulence factor contributing to resistance against antifungal agents and evasion of host immune responses910. Biofilm-forming cells were at least 1,000 times more resistant to antifungals than planktonic cells11. The nail plate is an impediment to treatment effectiveness. Its densely packed architecture confers resistance to penetration by external molecules. A dermatophytoma is a hyperkeratotic fungal mass in the subungual space that appears as a dense, white, or yellowish structure. Because it has fewer connections to the nail bed, it may not receive adequate drug exposure12. Furthermore, the rise in oral antifungal resistance emphasizes reassessment of current strategies for treating onychomycosis13.

Plasma lasers generate various reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, including ozone, hydroxyl radicals, and nitric oxide, which exert antifungal properties14. Previously, non-thermal atmospheric-pressure plasma (NTAP) inhibited the growth of dermatophytes and Candida in vitro1516. Previous studies on plasma suggest that plasma treatment inhibited mycelial growth and spore germination in a time-dependent manner17. However, research on the effect of NTAP against NDMs and biofilms is lacking, with only a few clinical studies18 and those on the nail plate model1920. Thus, this study evaluated the effects of NTAP on dermatophytes, yeasts, and NDMs in vitro and ex vivo in infected nail plates to verify the potential of NTAP as a therapeutic option.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

In vitro experiments

1) Fungal strains

Trichophyton rubrum (National Culture Collection for Pathogens [NCCP] 22456), Candida albicans (NCCP 32557), Fusarium oxysporum (NCCP 32677), and Aspergillus fumigatus (NCCP 32663) were obtained from the national culture collection for pathogen in the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA). The isolates were inoculated onto Sabouraud dextrose agar plates (Bandio Inc., Pocheon, Korea). The primary cultured strains were incubated in Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium (RPMI) 1640 (pH 7.0 with 3-[N-morpholino] propanesulfonic acid, 4-morpholinepropanesulfonic acid [34.53 g/L]), a suspension culture medium, and cultured at 28°C for T. rubrum and at 37°C for C. albicans, A. fumigatus, and F. oxysporum. The clumped fungal suspensions were then filtered through a cell strainer (VWR, Radnor, PA, USA) (pore size 100 μm) and 200 μl of fungal suspension was equally seeded into the 96-well flat-bottom microtiter plate.

2) NTAP

The NTAP (IBMsol, Seoul, Korea) was a jet plasma; the feeder gas was helium, and the flow rate was 2 L/min. The distance from NTAP to the specimen was fixed at 2 mm. The power was set to 11.2 W (0.7 kV, 21.7 mA), the frequency was 5 kHz, and the jet was emitted for 2, 4, and 6 minutes. The temperature was maintained at 24°C–25°C (room temperature) during NTAP generation, as there was no heat generation. The irradiation area corresponds to the region contacted by the transient purple flame, measuring approximately 3 mm in diameter. The experiments were repeated four times for each exposure duration. In an additional experiment of A. fumigatus, the power was increased to 12.5 W (0.76 kV, 23.3 mA), with the jet emitted for 30 seconds, 1 minute, and 2 minutes.

3) Growth curve of the fungus

Fungal strains were incubated following NTAP exposure, and the growth curves were measured over time. Untreated control of each fungal strain was measured in the same manner. The growth curve for each fungus was obtained using a Tecan Spark 10M microplate reader (Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland) at an absorbance wavelength of 600 nm.

4) Cell viability of T. rubrum and A. fumigatus

Cell viability assays for T. rubrum and A. fumigatus were conducted using previously described methods using the CyQUANT™ XTT Cell Viability kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA)21. Twenty-four hours after NTAP exposure on T. rubrum suspension, 0.3 mg/mL solution of XTT (sodium 3′-[1-{phenylaminocarbonyl}-3,4-tetrazolium]-bis[4-methoxy6-nitro] benzene sulfonic acid hydrate) was added to each NTAP-exposed well and incubated at 38°C for 2 hours in the dark. In A. fumigatus suspension, 0.3 mg/mL of XTT was added to each NTAP-exposed well immediately, 24, 48, and 72 hours after NTAP treatment and incubated at 38°C for 2 hours in the dark. Colorimetric changes due to formazan production by metabolically active cells were measured using an E-max microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) at an absorbance wavelength of 450 nm and a reference wavelength of 650 nm. Untreated control was also measured by cell viability assay using the same method.

5) Biofilm biomass of T. rubrum assay

As described by Kwasny and Opperman22, the biofilm formation assay was performed with the following modification. Biofilm reduction after NTAP exposure and NTAP untreated control was evaluated using crystal violet (CV) staining. A fungal biofilm of T. rubrum was formed in a 96-well flat-bottom microtiter plate. T. rubrum suspension was pipetted onto a microtiter plate. The well plate was incubated for 3 hours at 37°C without agitation for the adhesion phase. After being washed with 250 μl of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), 200 μl of RPMI medium was pipetted into each well. For the development phase, the suspension was incubated for 72 hours at 37°C without agitation and then gently washed thrice with PBS. Subsequently, NTAP were exposed to NTAP for 1, 2, or 4 minutes. The remaining adherent biofilms were fixed at 60°C for at least 120 minutes and stained with 0.1% CV solution for 5 minutes. After the plates were washed with deionized water, CV-stained biofilms were solubilized in 33% acetic acid for 1 hour. The amount of CV bound to each well was quantified by measuring the absorbance of the wells at 600 nm using a Tecan Spark 10M microplate reader.

6) A. fumigatus ultrastructure imaging by transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

TEM (HT7800, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) was used to investigate the effects of NTAP on the microbial structure of A. fumigatus. The suspensions on solid medium were exposed to NTAP for 2, 6 minutes, or without NTAP and incubated at 37°C for 1 day. Samples were initially fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde and 2.5% glutaraldehyde in PBS. After preprocessing the block samples, ultrathin sections were mounted on copper grids with 1% uranyl acetate solution and stained with lead citrate solution. After rinsing with distilled water, the grids were air-dried before imaging.

7) Ex vivo experiments on infected nail plates

Informed consent was obtained for using the nail plates of 3 patients diagnosed with onychomycosis (Kyung Hee University Hospital Institutional Review Board of KHUH 2024-04-033). Immediately after removing the yellow spike area from the infected nail plate, the area was divided into two pieces. For scanning electron microscopy (SEM) examination, NTAP was applied for 6 minutes to the dorsal layer of the toenail plate in patient 1, to the ventral layer of the fingernail plate in patient 2, and to subungual hyperkeratosis on the ventral layer of the toenail plate in patient 3. The samples were freeze-dried for 24 hours and coated with platinum using an ion sputterer before SEM imaging (TESCAN S8000 UHR FE-SEM; TESCAN FRANCE, Fuveau, France).

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance was set at p<0.05 for all analyses. For parametric data, one-way analysis of variance followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test was used to compare NTAP exposure groups (2, 4, and 6 minutes) against the untreated control group at each time point. All treatments were performed in quadruplicate. For correlation analyses between NTAP exposure time and measured outcomes (cell viability, biofilm biomass), Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) was calculated due to the ordinal nature of the exposure time variable and to account for potential non-linear relationships. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

Growth curve of the fungus

All NTAP exposure time groups of T. rubrum were significantly inhibited on days 0–6 compared to the control (p<0.005) (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Table 1). Exposure to NTAP for 4 and 6 minutes significantly inhibited the growth of T. rubrum for up to 10 days (p<0.001). However, the 2-minute exposure did not show significant differences after 6 days. The 2- and 4-minute exposure times groups of A. fumigatus were significantly inhibited (p<0.05) (Fig. 1B). However, the 6-minute exposure time showed a higher growth curve than the control after 24–120 hours, but with no statistical significance. In an additional experiment, when the power was increased to 12.5 W for 30 seconds, 1 minute, and 2 minutes, the cell growth curve of A. fumigatus showed no differences compared to that of the control, except at 48 hours in the 2-minute exposure group (Supplementary Fig. 1). C. albicans was also inhibited after NTAP, but the effect on C. albicans lasted only until 77 hours in all exposure time groups (p<0.05) (Fig. 1C). The growth curve of F. oxysporum showed that the 6-minute exposure group exhibited significant inhibition until 72 hours. The 2- and 4-minute exposure groups also showed growth inhibition compared to the control, but the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 1D).

A. fumigatus ultrastructure imaging by TEM

In the TEM images of the control, the cell membrane (blue arrow) was observed as a distinct, electron-dense lipid bilayer closely attached to the inner surface of the fungal cell wall (yellow arrow, Fig. 3A and B), which induced disruption of the cell membranes. The cell wall and cell membrane structures were severely damaged in samples exposed to NTAP for 6 minutes (Fig. 3G and H). According to the literature, A. fumigatus conidia are globose with 2.0–3.0 µm diameter, and the average cell wall thickness is 133 nm2324. The conidia exhibited an average diameter of approximately 2.0 µm. The cell wall thickness was reduced after NTAP exposure (Fig. 3C and F). After 6 minutes of NTAP exposure, the conidia appeared swollen (Fig. 3I), and filamentous structures extending toward the cytoplasm and the appearance of protuberances were identified (white arrow, Fig. 3I).

Ex vivo experiments on infected nail plates

In Case 1, no structures resembling hyphae or fungal spores were found (Fig. 4A), and no significant changes were observed following NTAP exposure on the dorsal layer of the toenail plate (Fig. 4C). In Case 2, fungal hyphae were observed between the keratin filaments (Fig. 4B). After NTAP exposure of the ventral layer of the fingernail plate, the hyphae shrank and had a reduced diameter (Fig. 4D), and fungal hyphae and spores were not visible. In Case 3, many cave-like spaces were observed deep inside the subungual hyperkeratosis (Fig. 4E). The entrance of the spaces was approximately 150–200 µm (yellow line, Fig. 4F). Fungal structures were barely visible on the outer surface but formed clusters in deeply recessed spaces (Fig. 4G and H). All fungal structures in the plasma-exposed specimens were damaged (Fig. 4I and J).

DISCUSSION

Organisms responsible for onychomycosis include dermatophytes, NDMs, and yeasts. Most dermatophyte nail infections (60%–70%) are caused by T. rubrum and Trichophyton mentagrophytes 25. Yeast onychomycosis is most likely caused by Candida spp. and is more likely to be causal in ngernails, especially in individuals whose hands are frequently immersed in water26. The role of NDMs in mixed infections involving dermatophytes remains poorly understood27. The treatment of NDMs and mixed-infection onychomycosis has not been investigated as extensively as dermatophyte infection, especially regarding recurrence2528. Furthermore, recent research highlights the emergence and global spread of antifungal resistance among Trichophyton species2930. Owing to the limitations of current treatments, there has been a demand for effective alternative modalities to treat onychomycosis. Among non-medical devices, the 1,064-nm Nd:YAG laser was first approved by the Food and Drug Administration. The most influential hypothesis for the antifungal effect of the 1,064-nm Nd:YAG laser is inactivation by heat. Carney et al.31 showed that heat produced a fungicidal effect for T. rubrum at 50°C with 15-minute exposure using a 1,064-nm Nd:YAG laser in vitro. In contrast, temperatures under 45°C can stimulate germination with Trichophyton spp.32. Moreover, damage to normal collagen and skin necrosis can be seen at temperatures exceeding 45°C and 50°C, respectively33. However, the antifungal effects of plasma do not depend on heat. The antifungal effect of NTAP has been reported in dermatophytes and yeasts151617. In the present study, the effect of NTAP differed for each strain, being most effective on T. rubrum, C. albicans, and F. oxysporum in a time-dependent manner (in that order). However, A. fumigatus did not exhibit any time-dependent effects. Applying NTAP on T. ruburm was the most effective, lasting up to 10 days. C. albicans was inhibited, but the duration of the effect was short. We hypothesize that differences in growth rates influenced the results. T. rubrum is known to grow slowly, and incubating dermatophyte cultures for at least 4 weeks is recommended34. In contrast, yeast and NDMs grow rapidly. C. albicans can achieve maximal doubling times under 1 hour35. Thus, the slow-growing T. rubrum has a longer duration of NTAP effect, while fast-growing C. albicans has a shorter duration.

Interestingly, our study found that treating NDMs with the same protocol used for T. rubrum or C. albicans may be ineffective or have opposite effects. The degree of inhibition of F. oxysporum was lower than that of T. rubrum and C. albicans, although F. oxysporum showed a time-dependent effect. The response of A. fumigatus to NTAP differed from that of other fungal strains. We further evaluated A. fumigatus under different NTAP power and time conditions. However, no significant difference in the growth curve was observed when the NTAP power was increased compared to the control. In the cell viability assay, immediately after NTAP exposure, a longer exposure duration was associated with a greater reduction in cell viability, showing a trend similar to that observed in other fungal species. However, from 48 hours post-exposure onward, cell viability paradoxically increased in the groups exposed to NTAP for 6 minutes. Several characteristics of A. fumigatus may have influenced these results. The A. fumigatus genome is rich in specific enzymes such as catalases, superoxide dismutases, and glutathione transferases to detoxify reactive oxygen species (ROS)24. A previous study reported that treating fungal spores of Aspergillus oryzae with micro dielectric barrier discharge plasma using nitrogen gas for 2 and 5 minutes significantly increased the germination percentage36. Another study comparing A. fumigatus conidia during germination and in the ungerminated state found that the conidia underwent a significant increase in volume by swelling, and the diameter of the conidia increased approximately 2-fold compared to that of the resting conidia37. The appearance of a small protuberance on the conidial cell wall followed the swelling of the conidia37. We found visible structural changes in the conidia in the samples exposed to 6 minutes of NTAP. NTAP produces a variety of reactive species that can have a wide range of effects on microorganisms, from inactivation to activation, depending on the applied dosage26; however, the interaction of NTAP with living cells is poorly understood. ROS play a critical role in the host’s immune defense against fungal pathogens. Thus, fungus employs enzymatic and regulatory antioxidant systems. In addition, a growing body of evidence supports that ROS act as physiological signaling molecules in aerobic organisms, with a particular relevance of H2O23839. The diversity of responses to ROS depending on the fungal species may partially explain the results showing that although all were initially suppressed, the growth curves varied over time.

Meanwhile, in a study evaluating the antifungal activity of NTAP against F. oxysporum present in paprika, NTAP treatment resulted in complete inhibition of mycelial growth and spore germination, particularly at 1,000 W for 90 seconds40. Similarly, in vitro study of A. fumigatus demonstrated a time-dependent reduction in fungal growth following repeated cold atmospheric-pressure plasma exposure when the electrode was powered by a 12 kHz pulsed 15 kV DC high-voltage supply41, indicating that a stronger energy source was employed compared to our study. Considering these points, NDMs may require higher energy levels than dermatophytes or Candida species for effective inactivation. Therefore, applying the NTAP exposure intensity and duration for each strain is important.

Biofilms offer some advantages for the growth and survival of the fungus, as they are surrounded by the ECM25. Biofilm formation could contribute to the treatment resistance seen in onychomycosis. Because of the persistence of fungal spores in chronic infections127, a high antibiotic dosage is necessary for effective treatment once a mature bacterial biofilm is established4243. ECM has been implicated in limiting the penetration of antimicrobial agents43. Increased cell density has been associated with reduced antifungal efficacy in mature biofilms4244. In addition, drug efflux pumps are commonly found in biofilms and have been implicated in azole resistance4546. Therefore, we suggest that a combination of NTAP has biofilm-disrupting effects, and antifungal agents may enhance their efficacy.

We conducted an ex vivo nail experiment to evaluate its efficacy on infected nails. In onychomycosis, subungual hyperkeratosis contains more fungal elements than the nail plate47. A previous study reported that numerous periodic acid-Schiff-positive hyphae are present and located mainly in the subungual hyperkeratosis48. One of the challenges in treating onychomycosis is the presence of arthroconidia. Fungal growth in the stratum corneum of the skin, nails, and hair typically occurs as hyphae may later develop into arthroconidia. Arthroconidia may be more prevalent in moist skin lesions and nail infections, and may contribute to treatment resistance49.

Most dermatophytes first invade the ventral layer as they attach to the nail bed, and the intermediate layer is involved in the infection process. However, the dorsal layer of the nail plate is rarely infected, except in white superficial onychomycosis50. In a previous plasma study of the nail plate model, plasma was applied to the dorsal nail plate covered with the fungal suspension medium1920. However, if the plasma cannot directly reach the hyphae or spores due to the nail plate, the antifungal effect of NTAP is unlikely to be effective. In our ex vivo study, numerous cave-like spaces were observed when the area of subungual hyperkeratosis was magnified. Fungal structures proliferated within the cave-like spaces with narrow entrances, whereas only a few fungal structures were observed on the nail plate. In the existing literatures5152, hyphae were smooth prior to damage; however, after exposure to plasma or antifungal agents, a reduction in diameter, wrinkles and twisting was reported. Consistent with these findings, structural destruction of fungal elements was found after NTAP treatment. Our findings showed that NTAP was effective against fungal structures exposed beyond the nail, particularly in the ventral layer of the nail plate and cave-like spaces within the subungual hyperkeratosis.

Although this study had limitations, including a small number of infected nail samples, we found an antifungal effect of NTAP against NDMs, biofilms, and spores associated with subungual hyperkeratosis, which is considered a contributing factor to onychomycosis recalcitrance. These findings indicate the potential of NTAP as a therapeutic agent.

In conclusion, applying NTAP using an optimized protocol for each fungal species is necessary. In combination with antifungal agents, NTAP may be effective against infected nails and biofilms.
