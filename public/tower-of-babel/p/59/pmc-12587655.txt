The impact analysis of energy industry structure optimization on urban low-carbon transition
Authors: Qiao Fan, Xinyu Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccelerating urban low-carbon transition is vital for high-quality economic growth and sustainable development. The energy industry, a key driver of socioeconomic progress, is the main source of carbon emissions and is crucial for decarbonization. Optimizing its structure is essential for urban low-carbon transformation. Based on the panel data of 284 prefecture-level cities in China from 2003 to 2022, this study decomposes the optimization of the energy industry structure into basic structure optimization and spatial structure optimization, uses green total factor productivity (GTFP) to measure the level of urban low-carbon transformation, and uses the super-efficiency SBM model to calculate it, deeply explores whether and how the optimization of the energy industry structure affects the low-carbon transformation of cities and analyzes the mechanism effect of the level of green technology innovation in it. The results show that both the optimization of the energy industry basic structure and the optimization of the energy industry spatial structure can promote the low-carbon transformation of the city, and the results are still valid after the endogenous test and robustness test. The level of urban green innovation will play a positive moderating effect on the optimization of the energy industry structure on its low-carbon transformation and development. The relationship between energy industry structure optimization and urban low-carbon transformation is obviously heterogeneous in different regions, not only in different geographical locations, but also in different levels of economic development.Keywords: Optimization of energy industry structure, Low-carbon transformation, Green total factor productivity, Double-fixed effects model

Introduction

Under the intensifying challenges of global climate change, the low-carbon transition has emerged as a fundamental strategic pillar and core element in international economic development. As the world’s second-largest economy, China has achieved rapid economic expansion, but it has also exacerbated environmental pressures and climate problems. The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report unequivocally identifies human activities as the primary driver of carbon emissions. Cities, as the primary venues for human activities, are the main sources of high energy consumption, severe pollution, and significant emissions. Effectively promoting the low-carbon transformation of cities is an inevitable choice for implementing China’s low-carbon transition strategic deployment and actively addressing climate change issues. It is also a key approach to practicing the construction of ecological civilization [1].

The energy industry has played a pivotal role in China’s rapid economic expansion, yet it simultaneously engenders substantial environmental issues characterized by high energy intensity and significant pollution outputs [2]. As the fundamental material backbone of socioeconomic development, urban energy systems account for approximately 67% of global energy consumption, according to International Energy Agency reports, fueling industrial production, infrastructure development, and residential activities through intensive energy demands. Against the backdrop of evolving global power dynamics and intensifying geopolitical tensions, energy security has emerged as a critical constraint on China’s economic trajectory. However, this strategically vital industry represents both the predominant source of urban carbon emissions and the primary focus area for low-carbon transition initiatives. Currently positioned at a critical juncture of high-quality development, China’s synchronized pursuit of ecological civilization and the Beautiful China initiative presents unprecedented opportunities for energy sector transformation [3]. As the world’s largest energy producer and consumer, China’s path to low-carbon transformation lies in deeply advancing the energy industry revolution to promote the low-carbon and efficient use of energy. The optimization of the energy industry structure has become an important step and measure for the low-carbon transformation of cities in China.

In the current international and domestic academic fields, there are many studies on the optimization of energy industry structure and urban low-carbon transformation, and the relationship between the two. However, most scholars start from the three industries and energy classifications to conduct research related to low-carbon energy transformation. Few scholars have linked the energy industry with urban low-carbon transformation, and there is still a lack of research on urban low-carbon transformation from the perspective of the energy industry, and this research field has received less attention.

To comprehensively investigate the specific influencing factors and mechanisms through which energy industry structure optimization facilitates urban low-carbon transition, this study establishes an Energy Industry Basic Structure Optimization Index from the energy industry perspective and innovatively employs the spatial angle index method to quantify its spatial structure optimization level. The super-efficiency SBM model is adopted to measure urban green total factor productivity, serving as a crucial evaluation indicator for urban low-carbon transformation. A two-way fixed effects model is constructed to empirically examine the intrinsic relationship between these variables, aiming to provide novel insights for optimizing urban energy industrial structures and advancing low-carbon development. The potential marginal contributions of this research are twofold: First, it pioneers the investigation of how China’s prefecture-level cities optimize their energy industrial structures to drive low-carbon transitions, a research perspective rarely explored in the existing literature. Second, this paper makes methodological innovation by originally introducing the spatial angle index method to assess energy industry spatial structure optimization, establishing a novel measurement framework for evaluating urban energy structure optimization in Chinese cities.

Literature review and theorized mechanisms

Literature review

Research on the optimization of energy industry structure

As a foundational industry underpinning the development of the national economy, the energy industry has consistently been a focal point of attention for urban development, shouldering the dual responsibility of fueling urban growth and driving green transformation. Nevertheless, the traditional structure of the energy industry largely falls short of meeting the demands of the urban low-carbon transition, thereby rendering the exploration of optimization pathways for the energy industry structure a significant research priority within the academic community [4].

The optimization of the energy industry structure is not a task that can be accomplished overnight; A comprehensive understanding of the development history and structural optimization research process of the domestic energy industry is an essential prerequisite. Some scholars posit that China’s energy industry has experienced four distinct stages of development: initial growth, steady improvement, energy transition, and energy revolution [5]. The energy industry should progressively achieve higher levels of industrial sophistication [6]. Currently, China’s energy industry is in a period marked by intense reform of the energy system, the advancement of the implementation of the "dual carbon" strategy, and the phase of climbing towards high-quality economic development. It confronts multiple challenges, including overcapacity, low economic efficiency, and an unoptimized structure [7].

The optimization of the energy industry structure is the core strategy to deal with energy security, climate change, and sustainable development, and its research process is mainly divided into three stages: early exploration, medium-term deepening, and later integration. In the early research, the academic community mainly focused on the relationship between energy consumption structure and economic transformation, and mainly focused on macro empirical evidence, revealing the fact that the excessive proportion of energy consumption in China’s industrial sector has increased energy intensity [8], quantifying the contribution of industrial restructuring to the decline of China’s energy intensity, and emphasizing the catalytic role of market-oriented reform [9]. However, most of the research at this stage is mainly limited to correlation analysis, and the energy structure optimization method is relatively simple. With the deepening of research, the multidisciplinary integration characteristics of energy industry structure optimization have been enhanced, and constraints and policy tools have been introduced, focusing on optimization paths such as industrial restructuring, technological upgrading, and policy intervention [10, 11]. Recently, research on the optimization of the energy industry structure has gradually broadened the research scale, realized the optimization of multi-scale structures such as countries, industries, and enterprises, and emphasized the importance of clean energy by applying tools such as big data, artificial intelligence, and policy collaboration [12, 13].

In addition, there are many studies in the field of measurement methods for the optimization of energy industry structure. A majority of scholars have used the proportion of clean energy consumption [14] and the proportion of primary, secondary, and tertiary energy consumption [15] as research perspectives. They have carried out measurements of energy structure optimization and put forward corresponding countermeasures and suggestions. Some scholars have constructed an index system for measuring the optimization degree of the energy structure based on single indicators, providing a comprehensive assessment of the level of structural optimization [16]. In addition, other researchers have focused on the field of new and traditional energy substitution. Starting from enhancing energy efficiency, developing new energy sources, and leveraging carbon pricing mechanisms, they have proposed optimization paths that involve substituting traditional fossil-based energy with new energy sources [17]. From the perspective of the research process of the entire energy industry structure optimization, most of the relevant studies are based on the low-carbon transformation of energy itself, and few studies directly start from the perspective of the energy industry to study its impact on urban low-carbon transformation, which leaves a large gap for the research content of this paper.

Research on urban low-carbon transformation

In recent years, with the proposal of the national green and low-carbon development strategy, the relevant research on urban low-carbon transformation has also gradually developed. In 2006, the first systematic demonstration of the economic necessity of low-carbon transformation was demonstrated, and the central role of cities as the main battlefield for emission reduction was emphasized. Subsequently, some scholars compared international cases and proposed that energy structure optimization, transportation greening, and building energy conservation are the core elements of urban low-carbon transformation, which have become the theoretical basis of early research in China. On the basis of the maturity of the previous theory, most scholars have conducted empirical exploration on the development of urban low-carbon transformation, which is mainly divided into two sections: measurement and influencing factors of urban low-carbon transformation.

In the field of measurement methods for urban low-carbon transformation, the early literature mainly considered that carbon dioxide gas is the core driving factor of low-carbon transition, so most studies at this stage mainly use intuitive indicators such as total carbon emissions and carbon emission intensity as measurement standards [18]. With the deepening of research, scholars have found that urban low-carbon transformation not only needs to consider carbon emissions, but also incorporates urban economic development into the measurement system. Therefore, many scholars in this stage of research include green total factor productivity in the scope of urban low-carbon transformation.

Green total factor productivity, which is an extension of the traditional concept of total factor productivity, takes into account energy consumption, unintended outputs, and environmental protection, among other factors [19]. The measurement methods for green total factor productivity can be categorized into two types: firstly, Parametric methods, also known as the Distance Function Approach, primarily involve calculating the shadow prices of undesirable outputs through distance functions to construct a productivity index. These methods estimate green total factor productivity by combining parametric techniques, including the Tornqvist index method based on output distance functions [20], the Malmquist productivity index method based on output distance functions [21], and the stochastic frontier analysis method based on output distance functions. However, the effectiveness of these methods is highly dependent on the appropriate specification of the functional form, and their strict data requirements may limit their applicability. Secondly, Non-parametric methods, mainly comprising Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) and its hybrid variants. Current measurement methods include the DEA-ML model [22, 23], the SBM-ML model [24], and the EBM-GML model. A majority of scholars employ non-parametric methods to measure the green total factor productivity of cities and various industrial sectors. Many researchers combine traditional DEA methods with ML or modified GML indices, which offer significant referential value.

There are many influencing factors for urban low-carbon transformation, and most studies believe that green technology innovation, government policy and governance, industrial structure upgrading, urban scale, and spatial structure are important factors affecting urban low-carbon transformation. Among them, the improvement of green innovation technologies such as clean energy technology and energy efficiency is the core driving force [25], the efficiency of government resource investment affects the transformation effect, and the misallocation of resources may exacerbate the "resource curse" [26], and the industrial structure needs to break the "dependence on energy-intensive industries" [27] and promote the low-carbon transformation of cities through the extension of the green industrial chain. In addition, some scholars also believe that environmental regulations, technological progress, and digital economy are important influencing factors for urban low-carbon transformation [18].

Research on the impact of energy industry structure optimization on urban low-carbon transformation

The impact of energy industry structure optimization on urban low-carbon transformation cannot be underestimated. Accelerating the optimization of the energy industry structure is conducive to promoting the low-carbon transformation process of China’s cities and promoting the development and upgrading of our country’s green productivity. However, most of the existing literature focuses on the impact of energy structure optimization and adjustment, energy consumption, and overall industrial structure optimization and upgrading on regional low-carbon transformation [28].

At present, most studies on the impact of energy structure optimization and energy consumption adjustment on urban low-carbon transformation adopt single-model analysis methods [29]. Based on these single-model approaches, some scholars have discovered that the traditional Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) is insufficient to account for domestic phenomena fully. As a result, they have employed simultaneous equation models to empirically examine the relationship between energy structure transformation, factors such as carbon dioxide emissions, and economic growth. Their findings reveal a U-shaped relationship between energy structure transformation and economic growth and an inverted "U" - shaped relationship between carbon dioxide emissions and economic growth [30]. Previous research has largely adopted a planar unit perspective with limited consideration of spatial effects. Current scholars have applied the Spatial Durbin Model to investigate the direct and indirect impacts of economic structural adjustments and green technological progress on carbon emission performance. They have found that industrial structure upgrading, energy consumption structure optimization, and the reduction of capital stock per labor all exert a "structural dividend" effect, which contributes to the improvement of carbon emission performance [1]. Building on numerous studies of single economies, some scholars have expanded the data and research scope, conducting empirical research with panel data from the global economy. They have proposed that there is a significant double-threshold effect between energy structure optimization and Green Total Factor Productivity [31].

In the field of industrial structure optimization and upgrading, some scholars have proposed the theory of "carbon lock-in of industrial structure", revealing that the industrial structure led by heavy industry locks in the path of high carbon emissions through infrastructure investment, and the low-carbon transformation of cities needs to break the industrial inertia [32]. In addition, some studies have confirmed that the rationalization and upgrading of industrial structure can reduce energy consumption per unit of GDP, and put forward a reasonable and convincing theoretical basis from the data [33]. Some scholars have found that the contribution rate of industrial structure to carbon emission reduction is higher than that of the simple development of renewable energy, but supporting industrial transfer policies are needed to avoid employment shocks [34].

The existing literature provides rich research results on the relationship between industrial structure optimization, energy consumption, structural upgrading, and urban low-carbon transformation. However, there is a lack of research on the integration of energy and industry and the impact of energy industry structure optimization on urban low-carbon transformation, which is also the basis of this study.

Theorized mechanisms and research hypothesis

The direct impact of energy industry structure optimization on urban low-carbon transformation

In recent years, China has vigorously promoted the optimization and transformation of the energy industrial structure, issuing a series of policies and measures to ensure the optimization of the energy industrial structure. As a major energy producer and consumer, China’s urban development primarily relies on energy as the power source. However, since the founding of the People’s Republic of China, energy consumption has mainly been dominated by traditional fossil fuels such as coal, oil, and gas, which have high carbon content. Urban development inevitably results in a significant generation of greenhouse gases like due to the consumption of conventional energy, leading to high pollution and high energy consumption in most of China’s energy industry. The optimization of the energy industrial structure implies a reduction in the consumption of fossil fuels and the promotion of the birth and development of clean energy industries, such as new energy sources, increasing the usage of clean energy like hydropower and wind power to truly achieve the renewal and iteration of old and new energy sources. Consequently, this can greatly reduce urban carbon emissions. Moreover, the optimization of the energy industrial structure is not a simple process. The completion of structural optimization is necessarily accompanied by technological innovations related to energy, which not only promote urban technological innovation but also address the high energy consumption issues of the existing industries. Through the emergence of new clean technologies and emerging clean industries, the consumption of traditional fossil fuels is reduced, and the efficiency of urban energy utilization is improved, thereby reducing urban carbon emissions and effectively promoting the low-carbon transformation of cities.

China boasts a vast territory with a wide span of latitude and longitude, leading to pronounced differences in topography and geomorphology across the country. Different cities exhibit distinct geographical locations, demographic characteristics, and levels of economic development. The optimization of the energy industrial structure is highly dependent on urban policy formulation, population, and industrial scale, as well as regional economic development levels. The emphasis on the optimization of the energy industrial structure varies among different cities, as do policy orientations and resource allocations, resulting in significant differences in the transformation of the energy industrial structure across cities. Although the low-carbon transformation of cities has been highly prioritized by the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China, and a series of policies and measures for urban low-carbon transformation have been issued to accelerate the comprehensive green transformation of economic and social development, it is an important task for the current low-carbon transformation of cities to coordinate the relationship between development and emission reduction, the whole and the part, the present and the long term, and the government and the market, while properly preventing and resolving the internal and external risks and challenges faced by green transformation. The implementation of low-carbon transformation policies is region-specific, and the optimization of the energy industrial structure also exhibits significant differences under the backdrop of various cities. Enhancing the optimization level of the urban energy industrial structure according to local conditions to promote urban low-carbon transformation is a crucial measure for high-quality development. Based on the above analysis, this paper proposes the following research hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2: The optimization of the energy industrial structure exhibits significant heterogeneity in promoting urban low-carbon transformation.

The moderating effect on the impact of energy industry structure optimization on urban low-carbon transformation

With the enhancement of China’s economic power, the drawbacks of traditional industries have become increasingly evident, characterized by high energy consumption and heavy pollution, which no longer align with the theme of green development in today’s world. The model of China’s economic development has shifted from factor-driven to innovation-driven [35]. The optimization of the energy industrial structure is a gradual process, and its long-term transformation and optimization require substantial technological innovations in the energy sector as support. These innovations may involve improvements in energy efficiency or the fostering of new energy industries. There is no doubt that the optimization of the energy industry structure is accompanied by corresponding technological innovations, especially green technology innovations related to low-carbon transformation. On the other hand, green technological innovation serves as one of the effective means to address environmental pollution and promote the achievement of the "dual carbon" goals. The enhancement of a city’s green innovative capacity can effectively drive its low-carbon transformation and empower China’s green development [36]. Innovation capacity, as a significant indicator for evaluating cities, has always been a focus of government attention. Sufficient innovation capabilities can trigger a technological revolution in cities, among which green technology innovation can not only promote the green transformation and upgrading of urban industries, but also promote the allocation of urban resources and people’s lifestyles to a greener, low-carbon, and reasonable direction, so as to reduce carbon emissions from the source. Through the "induced effect," the greening of urban residents’ consumption can be promoted, thus advancing the low-carbon transformation of cities. Therefore, the process of optimizing the energy industrial structure is inseparable from green technological innovation, which also drives the low-carbon transformation of cities.

Variable description

Dependent variable

The degree of urban low-carbon transformation is the dependent variable in this paper, and its appropriate measurement is of great importance. Most current studies use Green Total Factor Productivity (GTFP) as the main indicator to measure the extent of urban low-carbon transformation. In the context of environmental sustainability, enhancing green total factor productivity is seen as a key measure to stimulate internal economic dynamics. To accurately assess GTFP during the low-carbon transformation process, the super-efficiency model is introduced as an effective tool to quantify the level of GTFP.

For the measurement of GTFP, traditional evaluation models typically employ the models BCC and CCR. However, both models are radial efficiency evaluation models, and when there is non-zero slack in inputs and outputs, the improvement in slack is not reflected in the efficiency measurement. Consequently, using the BCC and CCR models can overestimate the efficiency of decision-making units [37]. Based on this, this paper adopts the super-efficiency SBM model to measure GTFP [38]. Based on the aforementioned methods, this paper measures the optimization levels of both the basic structure and spatial structure of the energy industry across 284 prefecture-level cities in China. Moderating variable

The level of urban green technology innovation (GIL) refers to a city’s demonstrated capabilities in green innovation and technological creativity, covering green innovation levels in various industries and sectors. Concerning the methodological research conducted by Wang Xin [41], Xu Jia [42], and others, considering that patents can intuitively and effectively represent the ability of scientific and technological innovation, the natural logarithm of the sum of the number of green invention patent applications and green utility model patent applications in prefecture-level cities (avoiding zero logarithm plus 1) is used as an index to measure the green innovation level in cities.

Control variable

Financial Development Level (FIN): Under the traditional financial system, an extensive development model may lead to increased urban carbon emissions and decrease the level of urban low-carbon transformation. This paper represents the financial development level as the proportion of the balance of loans from financial institutions at the end of the year to the regional GDP for each city [43].

Human Capital (HC): The accumulation of human capital helps to improve labor productivity, drive innovation and research and development, and facilitate policy formulation and implementation. This paper quantifies human capital by selecting the proportion of educational expenditure in public fiscal expenditure [44].

Industrial development level (PIE): The level of urban industrial development is closely related to the level of urban low-carbon transformation. In order to measure the level of urban industrial development, this paper uses the proportion of profits of industrial enterprises above a designated size to regional GDP.

Sample selection and data sources

The data used in this paper consists of panel data spanning from 2003 to 2022 for 284 prefecture-level cities in China. The energy industry classification follows the “Classification of National Economic Industries” (GB/T 4754-2017) issued by China in 2017 and is further refined by incorporating the “Statistical Classification of Energy-saving, Environmental Protection, and Clean Industries (2021) published in 2021. From the 96 industries classified in the national economic system, 15 energy-related industries were selected and manually compiled to form the energy industry dataset. Benchmark regression

During the model selection process, it is necessary to compare the pooled Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) model, the random effects model, and the fixed effects model. The individual effects test yields a P-value of 0, indicating that the fixed effects model outperforms the pooled OLS model. Similarly, the time effects test results in a P-value of 0, suggesting that the random effects model also surpasses the pooled OLS model. Finally, the Hausman test showed that the result P was less than 0.01, so the fixed-effect model was better at the significant level of 0.01. Furthermore, upon examining both individual and time factors, the results indicate the presence of both individual and time factors in the panel data of this study. Therefore, this paper selects a two-way fixed effects model with both individual and time-fixed effects for regression analysis to reduce omitted variable bias and more accurately estimate the relationships between variables.

Endogenous test

After analyzing the two-way fixed effects model, endogeneity issues may arise: firstly, there may be other unobservable factors that affect the low-carbon transformation of cities, leading to the problem of omitted variables; secondly, the measurement of green total factor productivity takes into account energy inputs, which is not entirely exogenous to the measurement of optimization in the energy industrial structure, potentially leading to a bidirectional causal relationship. In this paper, the instrumental variable method is used to test whether the model is endogeneity. Considering the correlation and exogenousness of instrumental variables, this paper will use the lagging period ( and ) of the core explanatory variables as instrumental variables for regression. Since most of the economic variables are dynamically persistent, the lagging variables are often highly correlated with the core explanatory variables. In addition, the lagging period variable reflects past information and is usually independent of the error term, so it satisfies the exogenous condition.

Previously, possible endogenous problems were analyzed from the theoretical level, and then examined whether the model has endogenous problems from an empirical perspective. Table 5 shows the empirical results of the instrumental variable validity and endogenousness test. From the regression results of columns 1 and 2, it can be seen that the lag period of the explanatory variable has a strong correlation as an instrumental variable, and the Kleibergen-Paap Wald rk F statistic is 2272.57, and the Cragg-Donald Wald F statistic is 5083.19, indicating that the instrumental variable selected in this paper is a strong instrumental variable. Since the number of instrumental variables selected in this paper is exactly the same as the number of endogenous variables, there is no need to perform an over-identification test. In addition, because the benchmark regression coefficient is similar to that of the instrumental variable, the difference is only 0.0056, which is much lower than the significant difference in the usual manifestation of endogenous problems, and the results of the Durbin-Wu-Hausman test show that in the instrumental variable regression the significance of the test results is greater than 0.1, so we cannot reject the null hypothesis that the core explanatory variable is an exogenous variable, so we judge that the benchmark model does not exist endogenous, and use the fixed-effect model as the benchmark result in the subsequent discussion.

For the western region, as an important energy base in China, the fossil energy industry accounts for a relatively high proportion of the industrial structure. Optimizing the energy industry basic structure in this region can effectively promote the improvement of urban low-carbon transformation level; It is worth noting the “high carbon lock-in” trap in optimizing the spatial structure of the energy industry. It is precisely because the western region, as China’s energy base, often requires the construction of various types of high-energy consumption and high carbon emission facilities and bases. However, most of the energy produced in the western region will be exported. These measures lock the western region onto a high-carbon development path, significantly increasing the absolute carbon emissions and intensity of the region, offsetting or even exceeding the emission reduction benefits of local renewable energy development, and having a significant negative impact on the low-carbon transformation of cities in the region. Hypothesis 2 is thus validated.

Urban economic development

The economic development levels of different cities vary significantly, and they may also adopt different low-carbon transformation policies, which can affect the results of the benchmark regression. Therefore, this paper calculates the median of the per capita gross domestic product (GDP) for 284 cities each year and divides the entire sample into two sub-samples based on this metric: those with lower and those with higher levels of economic development, to conduct a regression analysis. The results are presented in Table 7.

The research results indicate that for cities with lower levels of economic development, optimizing the energy industry basic structure has a positive impact on their low-carbon transformation, while optimizing their spatial structure has a weak negative impact, but this impact is not significant. For cities with high levels of economic development, optimizing the basic structure of the energy industry has a significant positive impact on the low-carbon transformation of the region, but the positive impact of optimizing its spatial structure is not significant. The regression results of cities with different levels of economic development show significant heterogeneity, thus re-verifying hypothesis 2.

The specific reasons are as follows: cities with lower levels of economic development have limited financial resources, weaker ability to absorb innovative advanced technologies, and more difficult financing capabilities and channels. Therefore, the speed of promoting the optimization of the energy industry basic structure is slow, the optimization efficiency is low, and it is difficult to produce significant scale emission reduction effects. The optimization of the spatial structure of the energy industry involves industrial transformation and upgrading, but cities with lower levels of economic development may lack sufficient governance and regulation capabilities, and are prone to problems such as low optimization efficiency and improper planning, which have a negative impact on the low-carbon transformation of cities. In addition, cities with lower levels of economic development often rely more on traditional energy to support economic growth, and most of these cities are located in underdeveloped areas such as the central and western regions and the northeast, which is consistent with the regression results of geographical heterogeneity mentioned earlier. Similarly, cities with higher levels of economic development have stronger financial resources, technology, talent, and market mechanisms, which enable them to more effectively implement energy industry basic structure optimization projects. However, the energy systems of these cities are relatively mature, and the degree of industrial cleanliness is higher. The cost of further optimizing the low-carbon transformation of basic structure has increased. Therefore, the regression results show a significant positive impact, but the impact coefficient is much lower than that of central and other regions. The optimization of the spatial structure of the energy industry also faces the same problem. If a relatively complete system is further optimized, there may be a risk of cost increase, and although the regression coefficient is positive, it is not significant.

Mechanism test

Based on the theoretical analysis presented earlier, it is evident that urban green technological innovation plays a pivotal role in urban low-carbon development. Innovation capacity, as a crucial evaluation indicator of cities, has always been a focus of government attention. Sufficient technological innovation can trigger a technological revolution in cities, which not only drives the green upgrading of urban industries but also promotes more rational and low-carbon changes in government resource allocation and people’s lifestyles, thereby reducing carbon emissions at the source. Therefore, this paper posits that green technological innovation exerts a positive moderating effect on the optimization of the energy and industrial structure’s impact on urban low-carbon transformation. However, the aforementioned analysis of this impact mechanism has been conducted from a theoretical perspective. Consequently, this paper necessitates an empirical test of this impact chain. In the analytical process, referring to Jiang Ting’s elaboration on moderating variables, this paper first centralizes the selected moderating variables to provide a more intuitive meaning to the regression coefficients [45]. Subsequently, the paper gradually introduces the moderating variables and interaction terms into the regression analysis to examine the significance of the coefficients’ signs. Policy recommendations

The low-carbon transformation and upgrading of cities have been highly prioritized development strategies in China in recent years. The measurement indicator, green Total Factor Productivity (GTFP), is a novel efficiency concept based on traditional TFP, incorporating various factors such as labor, economy, and green development for calculation. Given the current development status of heavy industry and high energy consumption in Chinese society, the energy industry, as a vital sector in the strategy for urban low-carbon transformation, urgently requires structural optimization. This paper selects research data from 284 Chinese prefecture-level cities spanning the years 2003-2022, and collects the enterprise survival data of 15 energy industries and input-output indicators of urban green Total Factor Productivity. Building on the measurement of the optimization of the energy industry’s basic structure, the paper innovatively introduces the spatial angle index method to measure the optimization of the energy industry’s spatial structure. Additionally, the super-efficiency SBM model is employed to calculate the urban green Total Factor Productivity, yielding the core explanatory and dependent variables of this study. The paper then utilizes a two-way fixed effects model at the time and city level to explore the impact and underlying mechanisms of the optimization of the energy industry’s structure on urban low-carbon transformation.

The research findings indicate that the optimization of the energy industry’s structure plays a positive role in promoting urban low-carbon transformation. After various endogenous and robustness tests, this conclusion is still valid. In addition, the impact of energy industry structure optimization on urban low-carbon transformation is obviously heterogeneous. For cities with high economic development levels, such as the eastern region, their economic level and industrial structure are relatively perfect, and it is difficult to have a significant impact on the low-carbon transformation of such regions, whether it is the optimization of the energy industry basic structure or the optimization of the spatial structure. For the central region and the northeast region, due to the regional economic and resource characteristics, the optimization of the energy industry basic structure and the optimization of spatial structure can significantly promote the low-carbon transformation level of the region. The most noteworthy is the western region, which has fallen into the trap of “high carbon lock-in”, and the optimization of basic structure has a significant role in promoting, but the optimization of spatial structure has a significant negative impact. Mechanism tests reveal that green technological innovation acts as a positive moderator in the impact of the optimization of the energy industry’s structure on urban low-carbon transformation.

The research conclusions of this paper provide beneficial insights for accelerating urban low-carbon transformation and promoting the green, low-carbon, and circular economic development of cities in the new era:

First, attach importance to the optimization and development of the urban energy industry structure, and promote the low-carbon transformation of cities with green total factor productivity as the starting point. Whether it is the optimization of the basic structure of the energy industry or the optimization of the spatial structure, it plays a significant role in promoting the level of low-carbon transformation in cities at the national level. China is a major energy producer and consumer, and the energy industry is also a pillar industry for its economic development, so it is of great significance to promote low-carbon transformation through the optimization of the energy industry structure. Improving the level of energy industry structure optimization should become a key focus area of national and government work.

Second, promote the optimization of the basic structure and spatial structure of the energy industry according to local conditions. When local governments promote the optimization of the energy industry structure in cities with different geographical locations and economic backgrounds, their priorities should be significantly different. For regions with high economic levels, such as the east, the focus of low-carbon transformation should be on fundamental system changes and technological breakthroughs. For regions with relatively weak economic capabilities, such as the central and western regions and the northeast, the government should choose spatial structure optimization measures according to local conditions on the basis of promoting the optimization of the energy industry basic to adapt to the development status of the local economy and industry.

Third, enhancing urban technological innovation capabilities and establishing collaborative innovation platforms to drive energy structure optimization and urban low-carbon transformation through technological innovation. Improving urban technological innovation capabilities is a core task in promoting high-quality urban development. Regions must prioritize the research, development, and application of cutting-edge technologies, driving profound transformations in energy production and consumption patterns, and using technological innovation to advance the optimization process of the urban energy industry’s structure. Additionally, the government should build a diversified and collaborative system of innovation platforms, ensuring that technological innovation plays a pivotal role in the transformation of the energy industry’s structure, breaking down barriers within the energy industry, accelerating the industrialization and scaling of scientific and technological achievements, realizing green and intelligent energy utilization, and providing strong technological support for urban low-carbon development, promoting a shift towards a low-carbon, efficient, and sustainable direction.
