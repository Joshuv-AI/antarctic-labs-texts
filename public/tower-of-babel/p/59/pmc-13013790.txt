Harnessing the power of the gut microbiome: a review of supplementation diagnosis and therapy for liver cirrhosis
Authors: Yuanqing Zhu, Wenkang Gao, Mingjian Cheng, Xuyang li, Kailin Cai, Liuying Chen, Huikuan Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Liver cirrhosis is an end-stage pathological process caused by various chronic liver diseases, whose incidence is increasing and represents a serious public health burden. The onset of liver cirrhosis is related to various factors such as alcohol, autoimmune, steatosis, and viral infections. Recent research suggests that the gut microbiota also participates in the progression of liver cirrhosis and that supplementation with probiotics improves the progression of liver diseases. We have reviewed the latest studies on the mutual influence between the gut microbiota (including bacteria, fungi and virus) and cirrhosis, and summarized the changes in the gut microbiota, the immune and metabolic mechanisms, and the application of gut microbiota in the clinical management, hoping to provide new strategies for the diagnosis and treatment of liver cirrhosis.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

The liver cirrhosis (LC) represents an end stage of chronic liver diseases (CLD), where the typical liver structure is supplanted by pseudolobules. It is characterized by extensive fibrosis throughout the liver and an increase in blood vessels, both within the liver tissue and in the surrounding areas. The global mortality rate of LC has increased by 47.15%. LC caused 1 million deaths over the 20-year period from 1997 to 2017. There are approximately 1.5 billion patients with CLD worldwide, and CLD has become the third leading cause of death among those aged 45–64 years. The 2019 Global Burden of Disease Study by the World Health Organization indicated that LC results in 560.4 age-standardized disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) per 100,000 individuals globally, which exceeds the 151.1 DALYs attributed to liver cancer [1–6]. However, the only therapeutic method for treating LC is liver transplantation. Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore the mechanisms of LC occurrence and development, as well as to generate appropriate diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Most cases of LC usually develop from various CLD, such as viral hepatitis, alcohol-related liver disease (ALD), nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), autoimmune liver disease, cholestatic diseases, and inherited metabolic liver disease (disorders of iron or copper metabolism) [6], the first three of which are recognized as the principal drivers of LC [2]. Typically, most cases of LC are attributed to a single cause, but some cases are affected by multiple factors simultaneously, such as hepatitis B (HBV) and hepatitis C (HCV) coinfection and long-term heavy alcohol consumption in patients with viral hepatitis. In addition, the factors driving the progression of LC are indeed multiple and complex, such as obesity and insulin resistance, and certain drugs (acetaminophen, isoniazid methotrexate, etc.) can also contribute to LC. In short, etiological treatment is usually the conventional method of slowing the progression of cirrhosis, but its effectiveness does not appear to meet clinical needs.

Current investigations suggest that the gut microbiota significantly influences the development of LC. In the fecal microbiota of LC patients, there is an abnormal enrichment of Fusobacteria, Proteobacteria, Enterococcaceae, and Streptococcaceae and a reduction in Bacteroidetes, Ruminococcus, Roseburia, Veillonellaceae, and Lachnospiraceae [7–10]. The severity of LC is correlated with the above microbial alterations. This review discusses the changes in the gut microbiota in LC patients as well as microbe-related mechanisms and applications in LC prevention, diagnosis, and treatment.

Barriers to the gut‒liver axis

With respect to the gut microbiota and CLD, the gut‒liver axis must be mentioned, which was first proposed by Volta et al. [11]. The enterohepatic system communicates with each other through the portal vein, which transports nutrients, bile acids, and other products from the intestine to the liver. The liver also secretes bile, which enters the intestine through the major papilla of the duodenum and carries antibodies from the liver to the intestine [12]. The intestinal barrier and the hepatic barrier are vital parts of the gut‒liver axis, which form a complex structure and strongly affect biological processes such as metabolism, immunity, and endocrine functions. The gut‒liver axis was previously described in detail [13, 14], thus we skip the preliminaries and get straight to the point.

Alteration of the microbiota

Recent research has confirmed that the microbiome changes in terms of abundance and category at different stages of CLD. Moreover, changes in the microbiota in patients with liver diseases of varying etiologies are usually different [15, 16]. In some cases, microbial changes even occur before liver damage [15]. Here, we briefly summarize the alterations in the gut microbiota, including bacteria, fungi, and viruses, in patients with different CLD and with LC.

Alterations of bacteria in CLD

Patients with NAFLD or nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) have decreased proportions of Faecalibacterium, Lactobacillus, Ruminococcaceae, Methanobrevibacter, Prausnitzii, and Coprococcus and increased abundances of Fusobacteria, Escherichia, and Fusobacteriaceae [17–20]. Patients with ALD or alcoholic hepatitis (AH) exhibit a decrease in the proportions of Peptococcus, Clostridiaceae, Lactobacillus, Bacteroides, and A. muciniphila and an increase in the abundances of Oxalobacter, Ruminococcaceae, Adlercreutzia, Clostridiales, Paraprevotella,

Barnesiellaceae, Phascolarctobacterium, Butyricimonas, and Sutterella [21–23]. Patients with HBV-CLD have decreased proportions of Blautia, Escherichia-Shigella, Bifidobacterium, Klebsiella, Parasutterella, E. hallii, Collinsella, Erysipelotrichacea, and Lactococcus and increased abundances of Fecalibacterium, Streptococcus, Sutterella, Lachnospiraceae, and Ruminiclostridium [24]. Patients with primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) exhibit a decrease in the proportions of Sutterella, Oscillospira, and Faecalibacterium and an increase in the abundances of Haemophilus, Veillonella, Clostridium, Lactobacillus, Streptococcus, Pseudomonas, and Klebsiella [25]. Alterations of bacteria in the LC

In patients with LC, the diversity of the gut microbiota is reduced, characterized by a decrease in α diversity, and there are significant differences in β diversity among the microbial communities [26]. As CLD progresses to LC, the changes in the gut microbiota converge again, especially in the decompensated stage of LC [7, 8, 21]. These bacteria are characterized by increases in Proteobacteria, Fusobacteria, Streptococcaceae, and Enterococcaceae and decreases in Bacteroidetes, Veillonellaceae, Ruminococcaceae, Akkermansia, Lachnospiraceae and Roseburia [27, 28]. Moreover, studies claim that these changes are independent of the etiology of LC [7, 10, 27].

Streptococcus is a common type of pyogenic cocci bacteria enriched in patients with LC, and it has been reported that Streptococcus, which is capable of encoding lantibiotics, is a risk factor for patients with LC [29]. Enterococcaceae are a group of facultative pathogens that are enriched in LC patients, and intestinal colonization by Enterococcaceae is associated with the progression of primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) [30]. Enterococcus. Fecalis, a member of the Enterococcus genus, has strong tolerance and colonization capabilities and has been reported to exacerbate PSC in animal models [31]. Akkermansia is a beneficial bacterial genus, and its abundance decreases in LC caused by different etiologies [27, 28]. The alteration of the gut microbiota generally manifests as a decrease in beneficial bacteria (such as Akkermansia) in the gut, an increase in potentially pathogenic bacteria (such as Streptococcus and Enterococcus), and the emergence of small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) [32].

Fungal alterations

In addition to bacteria, fungal diversity also decreased in LC patients treated with antibiotics and proton pump inhibitors, and over time, the changes in the mycobiome decreased and tended to stabilize [33]. Interestingly, in another study of patients with HBV-related LC, the abundance and diversity of fungi were greater than those in healthy subjects [34]. In HBV-related LCs, increased abundances of C. parapsilosis, C. tropicalis, C. glabrata, S. cerevisiae, Saccharomyces and Candida were detected [35, 36]. Similarly, in patients with NAFLD-related fibrosis, there is also a disturbance in the gut mycobiome. In patients with severe fibrosis, the abundances of C. albicans, Mucor sp., Cyberlindnera jadinii, Saturnispora sp., Cladosporium, Paecilomyces, and Babjeviella in the gut are increased and related to the progression of fibrosis, whereas the abundances of Pulvinula and Micropachyspora are negatively correlated with the progression of fibrosis [37, 38]. Similar to the results obtained in patients with HBV-related LC, multiple studies have shown an enrichment of Saccharomyces and Candida with the progression of fibrosis. However, owing to insufficient samples, it remains uncertain whether the gut mycobiome of patients with LC undergoes a consistent change similar to that observed in the gut bacterial community. The increased presence of harmful fungi increases the likelihood of infection in LC patients. The mortality rate of LC patients infected with Candida is 55%, which is 4.4 times higher than that of LC patients not infected [39].

Viral alterations

Gut virome alterations have also been reported in LC. Changes in phages that target bacteria that produce neuroactive metabolites are related to cognitive changes in patients with LC and hepatic encephalopathy (HE) [40]. Phages targeting urease-producing Streptococcus are associated with HE in patients with LC [41]. Another study conducted an 8-week treatment with rifaximin for patients with decompensated LC and subsequently reported the loss of phage functions targeting Streptococcus, Pseudomonas, and Enterobacteriaceae spp [42]. , which may promote the enrichment of those potentially pathogenic bacteria. Another study on patients with NAFLD-related fibrosis revealed that as fibrosis progresses, there is a significant decrease in the abundance of the phages Lactococcus and Leuconostoc, whereas there is a significant increase in the abundance of the Lactobacillus phage phiAT3 [43]. Current studies have focused on changes in the gut virome, but how changes in the gut virome affect the progression of LC is currently unclear.

The possible mechanisms by which the gut microbiota promotes liver fibrosis

The mechanism of the intestinal microbiota in CLD and LCs has been a research hotspot, and the current mainstream view is that the gut microbiome may be involved in the progression of liver disease by influencing intestinal barrier function, regulating gut and liver immunity, and changing the level of small molecule metabolites produced by microbes, such as short-chain fatty acids and bile acids.

Disruption of intestinal barrier function

The intestinal barrier is crucial for preventing pathological bacterial translocation and maintaining immune homeostasis. In patients with LC, the structure of the intestinal barrier undergoes abnormal changes, and the entire gastrointestinal tract shows increased intestinal permeability in both patients and animal models [44, 45]. Patients with LC have abnormalities in the intestinal epithelial tight junctions (TJ), leading to increased permeability compared with that of healthy controls [46]. In general, intestinal barrier damage parallels the progression of LC, and when LC progresses to the decompensated stage, with complications such as ascites and esophageal-gastric varices, intestinal barrier damage becomes particularly severe [47]. The destruction of the intestinal barrier in the LC not only increases the number of pathways through which large molecules (including lipopolysaccharide (LPS) or bacterial deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA)) enter the systemic circulation but also promotes the occurrence of LC-related complications such as gram-negative bacteremia [48]. The abnormal function of the intestinal barrier in the LC is related to changes in the intestinal structure, including thinning of the mucus layer, enlargement of the interepithelial space, shortening and widening of microvilli, submucosal edema, and reduced expression of the tight junction proteins occludin and claudin-1 [48–52].

The mechanisms of intestinal damage caused by LCs of different etiologies are also not entirely the same. Ethanol and its metabolite acetaldehyde can directly damage intestinal TJs and increase intestinal permeability in ALD. A reduction in the levels of protective metabolites such as butyrate can also lead to barrier damage. Weakened TJs promote the paracellular transport of bacterial metabolites, triggering an immune response in the body. Bacterial translocation may involve transcytosis, but the detailed mechanisms are not fully understood. Studies have shown that subclinical intestinal inflammation caused by changes in the gut microbiota can exacerbate barrier function damage in advanced LC, indicating that a dysregulated microbiota plays an important role in the process of intestinal barrier damage.

Intestinal immunity is disrupted by the gut microbiota

In the LC, the integrity of the intestinal barrier is disrupted, leading to severe gut bacteria translocation (GBT) [48]. In the context of an imbalanced gut microbiota, the first response is the innate immune system. Excessive pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) from microbial communities stimulate lymphocytes, leading to an inflammatory pattern of immune dysregulation in intraepithelial lymphocytes and lamina propria lymphocytes, including increased lymphocyte activation and increased production of Interleukin 17 (IL-17) and interferon γ (IFN-γ) [48, 49]. As the microbial community contacts intestinal dendritic cells (DCs), they are activated and start producing tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α). Excessive TNF-α damages the intestinal barrier in patients with LC, ultimately leading to more extensive GBT and creating a vicious cycle [53]. In addition, TNF-α increases the activation of intestinal macrophages through the TNF-α/tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 (TNFR1) signaling pathway and promotes the secretion of cytokines such as IL-8, C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2), IL-6, and nitric oxide (NO) [54]. Moreover, increased expression of Claudin-2 was observed in experimental models, which together contributed to damage to the intestinal barrier [55]. Other changes in innate immune cells include the proliferation of γδT cells and natural killer T (NKT) cells secreting IFN-γ [56]. A reduction in IL-22 produced by group 3 innate lymphoid cells leads to a decrease in the expression of regenerating islet-derived protein 3 gamma (Reg3γ) in the intestine, which can damage barrier function [57]. As LC progresses to the decompensated stage, due to the stimulation of excessive antigens, the characteristics of the intestinal immune system in experimental models shift to a T helper 1 (Th1) regulatory pattern, while resident Th17 cells are depleted [48] (Fig. 1).

Other components of the intestinal mucosal immune system are also altered. The release of RegIIIβ/γ and secretory Immunoglobulin A (IgA) is reduced, indicating impaired antibacterial functions of neutrophils, epithelial cells, and B cells, which is consistent with reports of patients with LC [58, 59]. Interestingly, another study reported that patients in the NASH stage are subjected to overactivation of the gut microbiota, which subsequently activates metabolic T cells and promotes fibrosis through IgA-Fc fragment receptor (FcR) signaling. Depletion of gastrointestinal B cells prevents or reverses NASH-induced fibrosis [60]. T-cell function is also disrupted, and the translocated microbiota increases the expression of IFN-γ/IL-10, leading to the suppression of T-cell immunity in bile duct ligation (BDL)-mice [61].

Imbalances in the gut microbiota cause a breakdown of both innate and adaptive immune reactions within the intestinal lining, which in turn encourages bacteria to stick, settle, and increase gut permeability. As the LC transitions from a compensated to a decompensated state, the disrupted mucosal immune system results in the disintegration of epithelial tight junctions, a decrease in AMPs, and a decrease in DC phagocytosis. Consequently, this leads to more bacteria and their products entering the bloodstream. The gut’s effector immune cells, along with inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, PAMPs, and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), travel together in the circulation, triggering a widespread inflammatory reaction. This causes issues with the circulatory system and the proper functioning of distant organs. The restoration of gut immune normalcy and a reduction in intestinal permeability through the reorganization of microbes by antibiotics indicate the significant contribution of bacteria to disease development [47, 48, 56, 57, 62–64].

Liver immune damage mediated by intestinal bacteria

Disruption of the intestinal barrier and an imbalance of the gut microbiota indirectly damages liver function. In response to these disease-causing conditions, liver cells and the immune cells that reside in the liver might shed their typical functions and take on a profile that fosters inflammation and tissue scarring, thus increasing the severity of LC (Fig. 2).

Hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) are the main precursors of myofibroblasts in the process of liver fibrosis and play a leading role in LC [65]. In LC, LPS from the gut can stimulate Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) in quiescent HSCs and activate them via a myeloid differentiation primary response protein 88 (MyD88)-nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB)-dependent pathway, leading to fibrogenic transformation and the development of liver fibrosis [66]. Furthermore, in animal models, ligands (such as β-glycans, double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), single-stranded RNA (ssRNA), and high-mobility group box 1 protein (HMGB1)) from different microbial communities activate TLRs (TLR2, TLR3, and TLR5. TLR7 and TLR9), which can activate HSCs through the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and NF-κB pathways, producing profibrotic factors such as α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and exacerbating liver fibrosis [67–71].

Hepatocytes also express rich pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) and play a role in the progression of LC. Nucleotide binding oligomerization domain contain 1 (NOD1), when stimulated by its ligands in hepatocytes, can activate the NF-κB and MAPK pathways, inducing the production of C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1 (CXCL1) and CCL5, which promote wound healing and fibrosis [72]. Under the stimulation of gut microbiota antigens, hepatocytes undergo significant endoplasmic reticulum stress, causing the activation of the stimulator of interferon genes (STING)-interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) pathway and inducing a mitochondria-dependent apoptotic pathway, as well as the activation of α-SMA and collagen type I alpha 2 chain (COL1A2) expression in HSCs, collagen deposition, and the progression of fibrosis [73].

Hepatic macrophages consist of various subsets, with kupffer cells (KCs) and macrophages derived from monocytes being crucial for preserving immune balance. In the LC, excessive GBT can deplete the clearance capacity of macrophages, leading to the expression of IL-10. Simultaneously, these macrophages exhibit elevated MER tyrosine kinase expression, which dampens the reaction to PAMPs, consequently reducing antibacterial action [74, 75]. When macrophages are activated, they release inflammatory factors such as TNF, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and ROS. They also promote the activation and survival of HSCs and myofibroblasts via the action of transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-β1) and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) [74]. In addition, inflammatory bodies induced by GBT and tissue damage from macrophages also contribute to liver inflammation and injury [76].

Liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs) and macrophages constitute the main clearance system within the liver. LSECs preserve immune balance by fostering tolerance to benign antigens originating from the diet or symbiotic bacteria, as well as by maintaining the quiescent state of HSCs [77]. However, during the process of LC, LSECs undergo a transformation characterized by the loss of fenestration and the formation of a basement membrane, known as capillarization. When LPS from the gut contacts LSECs, it induces an excessive inflammatory response, leading to LSEC injury and capillarization [78, 79]. Moreover, this transformation appears to be mediated by KCs [79]. When LSECs undergo capillarization, they fail to supply enough oxygen to the hepatocytes beneath them, resulting in cell death by apoptosis and necrosis and eventually the emission of DAMPs [80]. Signals from LSECs, including DAMPs and TGF-β, can stimulate fibrogenic HSCs, enhancing collagen synthesis and LC.

In addition to macrophages and LSECs, other parts of the liver immune system also undergo extensive changes. Neutrophils are affected by dysfunction of the gut‒liver axis, leading to liver cell damage associated with inflammation and the IL-17-dependent activation of HSCs [81]. Moreover, in patients with acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF), neutrophils in the liver exhibit high expression of C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 1 (CXCR1) and CXCR2, which are crucial for the recruitment of neutrophils and the production of inflammatory mediators such as IL-8, IL-6, and IL-23 [80].

In summary, the impaired intestinal barrier and subsequent inflammation lead to the extensive release of PAMPs and DAMPs, fundamentally altering the immune environment of the liver. The tolerance observed in a healthy state is replaced by the immunogenic and fibrotic nature of the cirrhotic environment, characterized by the expansion of proinflammatory cells and cytokines, as well as the deposition of the extracellular matrix (ECM). The activation of HSCs is a common and core mechanism induced by various cells to promote the progression of LC. During this transition, the innate immune system appears to play a primary role, whereas the adaptive immune system has a certain protective effect.

Microbiota-associated metabolic pathways

Short-chain fatty acids

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), including butyrate, propionate, and acetate, are the end products of microbial fermentation and are involved in a variety of physiological functions [82]. SCFAs participate in maintaining the integrity of the intestinal mucosa [83], maintaining the innate gut barrier function of mucus levels [84], regulating the immune system and inflammatory responses [85, 86], and promoting the secretion of gut hormones [87].

In patients with LC, the abundance of representative bacteria that produce SCFAs in the gut, including Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Clostridium cluster IV, is reduced. A decrease in SCFA content leads to increased intestinal permeability, resulting in an increase in toxic and proinflammatory factors in the circulation, which exacerbates the progression of LC and triggers complications [88]. The deficiency of SCFA-related bacteria may be due to the use of antibiotics and can be restored by fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from donors rich in Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae, which increases the abundance of SCFAs [89].

The levels of SCFAs can regulate the progression and complications of LC. Circulating butyrate levels in patients with LC are negatively correlated with portal hypertension, endotoxemia, and systemic inflammation [90]. The decrease in intestinal SCFA levels caused the downregulation of the SCFA receptor G-protein-coupled receptor (GPR) 43, leading to reduced expression of myogenic markers and ultimately exacerbating muscle atrophy in cirrhotic mice [91]. Antibiotic pretreatment of multidrug-resistant 2−/− (MDR2−/−) mice colonized with Roseburia reduced liver fibrosis, inflammation, and pathogen translocation, whereas Roseburia, which produces SCFAs and purified SCFAs, reduced fibrosis [31].

Bile acids

Bile acids are integral to bile and are synthesized in the liver. Stored in the bile ducts or gallbladder, these compounds are deployed to the small intestine following digestion, where gut microbes further process them. They then return to the enterohepatic circuit, with the majority—approximately 95%—being reabsorbed in the ileum before entering the hepatic portal vein. Finally, they are absorbed by hepatocytes as they filter through the sinusoids [92, 93]. With the progression of liver disease to LC, total bile acid levels are reduced because of ongoing deconjugation. However, the production of secondary bile acids remains relatively rare, and the specific mechanism driving this phenomenon remains unclear. This may be due to the significant decrease in the abundance of bacteria such as Lachnospiraceae, Roseburia, and Ruminococcaceae, which can metabolize bile acids, in cirrhosis patients. A reduction in these bacteria leads to a decrease in the levels of secondary bile acids.

Farnesoid X receptor (FXR), which is predominantly found in the liver, intestine, and gallbladder, is essential for controlling bile acid production, release, and recycling. Moreover, FXR contributes to the maintenance of intestinal vascular barrier integrity [55]. The gut microbiota can interfere with the regulation of FXR through interactions with bile acids, thereby affecting LC. Alterations within the gut microbiota result in the weakening of bile acid absorption in the ileum, often caused by interference with the function of the apical sodium-dependent bile acid transporter (ASBT). This leads to a decrease in the expression of nuclear FXR and fibroblast growth factor 19 (FGF19) in intestinal epithelial cells, along with a reduction in the levels of primary conjugated bile acids in the colon. A decrease in FXR expression leads to a reduction in the secretion of AMPs [94], whereas the negative feedback regulatory mechanism of FXR-FGF19 is disrupted, resulting in increased bile acid synthesis [95]. The abnormal composition of the gut microbiota in patients with cirrhosis may lead to an increase in circulating unbound bile acids, ultimately causing damage to intestinal barrier function and exacerbating cirrhosis [96, 97]. Notably, this is a possibility, and the mechanism by which an abnormal gut microbiota affects bile acid metabolism in patients with cirrhosis remains to be explored.

The liver might have a more difficult time dealing with scarring when FXR levels decrease. FXR in HSCs can help prevent liver scarring by turning on genes that fight against it [98]. The mechanism may involve FXR negatively regulating collagen deposition by suppressing the TGF-β/SMAD3 and Jun D/AP1 signaling pathways in an SHP-dependent manner [99]. In addition, FXR-FGF19 in the gut can improve liver inflammation by regulating the activity of NF-κB inhibitors [100].

HE is a common neuropsychiatric complication during the progression of LC and is characterized mainly by hyperammonaemia. In patients with LC, the levels of serum conjugated bile acids are elevated. During HE, elevated levels of bile acids in the bloodstream can lead to the accumulation of these acids in the brain. Given their detergent-like properties, bile acids have the potential to increase the permeability of the blood‒brain barrier, resulting in damage to the brain [101]. After the occurrence of GBT, there is an overgrowth of proinflammatory pathogenic bacteria, especially those in the Enterobacteriaceae family, which release inflammatory markers in the gut and exacerbate necroinflammatory changes in liver tissue. This initiates a self-reinforcing loop that results in additional inhibition of bile acid synthesis [102].

LPS and fungal metabolites

The gut microbiota and its metabolites can also have negative effects on the body. Dysbiosis caused by the overgrowth of pathogenic bacteria or reduced microbial diversity due to LC may lead to an imbalance in the gut microbiota. Combined with impaired intestinal barrier function, this can result in the translocation of microbes or microbial products into systemic circulation, leading to the occurrence of endotoxemia [103, 104].

LPS is a glycolipid component of the outer membrane of gram-negative gut bacteria. It consists of a carbohydrate moiety and a lipid A part. The circulating concentration of LPS is very low, typically less than 20 pg/ml [105]. Upon breaching the intestinal barrier and gaining entry into the portal venous system, LPSs are metabolized by liver-specific enzymes, such as alkaline phosphatase, or are eliminated into bile via the action of scavenger receptors [106]. Under normal conditions, KCs can also rapidly clear LPS [107]. However, during gastrointestinal bleeding and liver dysfunction associated with sepsis, hepatocytes cannot completely metabolize endotoxins into bile. This not only leads to liver damage and further exacerbates the progression of LC but also results in the occurrence of mild endotoxemia [108]. This mild endotoxemia caused by LPS can be detected in the liver portal vein circulation and may trigger the formation of portal vein thrombosis (PVT), a life-threatening complication [109].

The impact of fungal metabolites is equally important [41]. Candidalysin is an exotoxin secreted by Candida albicans, and together with hemolysins, it can directly damage hepatocytes, potentially leading to the deterioration of liver function and affecting the prognosis of patients with AH [110]. Previous studies have reported an enrichment of Candida albicans in patients with LC, and invasive candidiasis is associated with negative outcomes in cirrhotic patients [33, 39]. Therefore, the impact and mechanisms of action of C. albicans on LC warrant further exploration.

In summary, during the progression of LC, changes in bile acids and a reduction in SCFAs can exacerbate the disease process, whereas bacterial metabolites (such as LPS) directly lead to serious complications of LC. The interaction between microbial metabolites and the host organism needs further exploration. This complex interplay underscores the importance of the gut microbiota in liver health. The balance of microbial metabolites, such as bile acids and SCFAs, plays a crucial role in maintaining the integrity of the intestinal barrier and modulating the immune response. Disruptions in these balances, potentially due to changes in the gut microbiota composition, can have profound effects on liver function and disease progression. Understanding these mechanisms can provide insights into the development of targeted therapies for managing LC and its associated complications (Fig. 3).

The clinical application of the gut microbiota in LC

Diagnosis

The early stage of LC often lacks obvious symptoms, drawing the patient’s attention only when it reaches the decompensated stage. Moreover, fewer than one-third of patients are aware of their condition [111]. Currently, noninvasive methods for fibrosis assessment are increasingly replacing liver biopsy. Common noninvasive diagnostic tools include serological testing (fibrosis indices based on four factors (FIB-4) and the aspartate aminotransferase-to-platelet ratio index (ARPI)), imaging-based indicators (ultrasound (US), computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)), and elastography techniques (vibration-controlled transient elastography and magnetic resonance elastography) [112]. The determination of LC severity on the basis of the gut microbiota and its metabolites deserves attention.

A study that used metagenomics and machine learning techniques developed an LC prediction method. They identified dozens of disease signatures associated with cirrhosis (such as Veillonella parvula, Ruminococcus gnavus, and Veillonella atypica). When combined with patient age, this model can accurately detect NAFLD-related LC (area under the curve (AUC) = 0.88) [10]. Similar results were also obtained in different cohorts of patients with various etiologies of LC in China and Italy [10]. Another study developed an interpretable neural network, including the metabolic activities of different bacterial species, phylogenetic relationships, and bacterial community structure, for predicting human host phenotypes. By taking the species abundance of human gut microbiome samples as input, this neural network can predict LC, achieving an AUC of 0.969 [113]. Chinese researchers reported that the enrichment of Streptococcus and Lactobacillus in tumor tissues is positively correlated with LC in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). These bacteria can serve as potential biomarkers for LC. Multiple logistic regression revealed that the presence of Streptococcus as a biomarker can significantly predict LC in HCC patients, with an AUC of 0.73. When combined with the abundance of Romboutsia, the AUC can be increased to 0.78 [114]. Other studies have shown that by selecting a combination of five operational taxonomic unit (OTU)-based biomarkers, including Lactobacillus reuteri, Lactobacillus mucosae, Lactobacillus rhamnosus, Ruminococcus gnavus, and Neisseria mucosus, the occurrence of LC complications such as spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) can be predicted [115].

In addition to bacteria, gut fungi and viruses can also be used to diagnose LC decompensation. A longitudinal study revealed that, compared with patients who further decompensate, patients who experience initial decompensation have greater decreases in α/β diversity, bacterial changes (increases in Lactobacillus and Streptococcus and decreases in Ruminococcaceae and Faecalibacterium), viral changes (increases in phages and decreases in microRNA viruses), and archaeal changes (decreases in short-chain methanogens) [116]. The similarity between groups and within groups suggests that these changes may be important predictive factors, but further research is still needed.

Prognostic prediction

The prognosis of LC is typically associated with age at diagnosis and extrahepatic complications. For example, the overall in-hospital mortality rate after variceal bleeding is approximately 14.5% [117]. The median survival time after a significant episode of HE is 0.92 years [118]. The presence of ascites in patients with LC is related to a median survival period of 1.1 years [119]. The commonly used prognostic system for LC at present is the child–turcotte–Pugh (CTP) score and the model for end-stage liver disease with sodium (MELD-Na) score. However, the CTP score and MELD-Na score pose certain challenges. The CTP score is a nonobjective indicator for the assessment of ascites and hepatic encephalopathy; however, the accuracy of the MELD-Na score has declined in recent years, and it fails to accurately predict outcomes in several groups of patients [120, 121]. This may be due to a greater proportion of patients with ALD and NAFLD rather than viral hepatitis in the current era.

In recent years, with the advancement of gut microbiome research, many novel models for predicting the prognosis of LC have emerged. For example, the fecal DNA and RNA profiles and microbial composition can predict the hospitalization of patients with LC, independent of clinical variables [122]. Phascolarctobacterium predicts the recurrence of acute HE episodes and predicts the recurrence of HE once combined with Staphylococcus. Additionally, in acute episodes of overt HE, the relative abundances of three bacterial species (Alistipes, Bacteroides, and Phascolarctobacterium) and five OTUs (Clostridium-XI, Bacteroides, Bacteroides, Lactobacillus, and Clostridium-sedis) were associated with the recurrence of HE and overall survival during the subsequent one-year follow-up [123]. It has also been reported that biomarkers of dysbiosis caused by the use of proton pump inhibitors can be used to predict the survival of patients with LC and are related to intestinal barrier dysfunction. Specifically, the abundance of Streptococcus salivarius is associated with gut inflammation and permeability, and the abundance of Veillonella parvula is correlated with liver function. Both can independently predict LC-related mortality [124].

In addition to the bacteria themselves, virulence factors (VFs), which are produced by gut microbes and are related to colonization and adhesion, are associated with the prediction of death and hospitalization in patients with LC. Many of these VFs are encoded by Enterobacteriaceae, Staphylococcus, and Enterococcus, which are known pathogens and have been found to be associated with poor prognosis in patients with LC [81, 125–127]. Moreover, patients with severe LC and a history of complications have higher levels of VFs related to iron metabolism, adhesion, and biofilm formation, which are related to death and hospitalization and decrease after FMT (Table 2) [128].

In conclusion, there are many prediction or diagnostic models for LC or LC complications, but the clinical popularity of these newly constructed models is very low, and clinicians are still accustomed to using classical indicators, such as alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), albumin, and coagulation function, to evaluate patients’ disease progression. It is necessary to promote the popularization of these detection methods in the future. However, these are mostly correlational results, and confirming that certain microbes can affect the levels of circulating proteins or secreted metabolites in vivo could help to deepen the understanding and application of the gut microbiome in LC.

Targeting the gut microecology to treat LC

After the diagnosis of LC, comprehensive treatment should be initiated as early as possible, with an emphasis on etiological treatment. Controlling the etiology, such as inhibiting hepatotropic virus duplication and abstinence, slow the progression of LC, improve the prognosis, and even ameliorate the stages of fibrosis. For patients in the decompensated stage, treatment strategies should focus on preventing further decompensation rather than treating complications when they occur. Liver transplantation is the only effective method for treating decompensated LC, but unfortunately, owing to a shortage of liver donors and a heavy economic burden, it cannot be widely applied to all patients. We will introduce several new strategies for the treatment of LC on the basis of the gut microbiota and its derivatives.

Antibiotics in cirrhosis

Currently, the main method of regulating the gut microbiota is the use of antibiotics, which alleviates LC and its complications by reducing intestinal permeability, inhibiting circulating antigen levels, and increasing the levels of neurotoxins such as ammonia.

When SBP is suspected in patients with cirrhosis, empirical antibiotics should be used, typically third-generation cephalosporins, except in cases where there are risk factors for multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs), in which case the drug of choice is piperacillin/tazobactam. To prevent this condition, norfloxacin and ciprofloxacin are preferred as initial and subsequent preventative medications, with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole being the next option in the treatment sequence [129–133].

For patients with cirrhosis who have upper gastrointestinal bleeding, intravenous antibiotics are recommended for short-term prophylaxis, as this can reduce the risk of infection and improve survival rates [134, 135]. Additionally, a recent meta-analysis revealed that prophylactic use of antibiotics, such as quinolones and cephalosporins, improved the prognosis of LC patients with upper gastrointestinal bleeding [136].

In patients with HE, the use of long-term antibiotics is common. Historically, metronidazole, neomycin, and vancomycin have been employed in the treatment of HE. However, owing to their associated side effects and the rise in multidrug-resistant bacteria, these medications have fallen out of favor in current practice [137]. In contrast, rifaximin has become a choice for the treatment of HE [129]. It is the first nonaminoglycoside intestinal antibiotic with broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, and it has been reported to have good antimicrobial activity against enterotoxigenic E. coli, Shigella, and Salmonella [138]. Rifaximin reduces the risk of recurrent overt HE [139] and is significantly associated with lower hospitalization rates, length of hospital stay, emergency department visits, and 30-day readmission rates [139–141]. The reported mechanisms of rifaximin include the ability to reduce circulating levels of LPS [142], exert anti-inflammatory effects [143], decrease cytokine expression [144], increase the expression of tight junction proteins [140], reduce bacterial adhesion [145], decrease bacterial virulence [146], selectively promote beneficial bacterial groups [147], increase beneficial metabolites [148], and increase the nonmicrobiota-dependent reduction in intestinal ammonia [144]. Currently, rifaximin promotes the proliferation of only beneficial bacterial groups at the genus level, and changes at the species level still need to be explored. Studies on whether rifaximin can reduce blood ammonia levels are somewhat conflicting [144, 146, 149], but studies using germ-free mice have shown that rifaximin reduces the production of intestinal ammonia through glutaminase in the gut. However, further exploration is still needed regarding the mechanism of action of rifaximin.

The role of fungi in the progression of liver disease has received increasing attention, but little is known about how fungi regulate this process. In some CLD, antifungal drugs control SIBO and alleviate liver damage [38, 150]; however, given that some antifungal drugs are hepatotoxic, the role of antifungal drugs in the treatment of LC still needs to be explored.

Notably, the administration of antibiotics, particularly those with broad-spectrum activity, has the potential to disturb the equilibrium of the gut microbiota. The application of antibiotics diminishes the diversity of microbes, modifies the functions of the microbial community, and may result in the emergence and selection of antibiotic-resistant strains. This can render the host more vulnerable to infections from pathogens such as Clostridioides difficile [151]. Furthermore, in the gut microbiota of patients with cirrhosis, there is an enrichment of antibiotic resistance genes, which may be associated with the extensive use of antibiotics [152].

Recently, a new antibiotic that targets gram-negative bacteria called lolamicin has been reported. It has shown good efficacy in the treatment of septic mice without harming the symbiotic gut microbiota of the mice and prevents secondary infections by Clostridium difficile [153], making it a potential candidate for antibiotic treatment in LC. Aside from developing smart antibiotics with a high degree of specificity, the use of bacteriophages also allows for the targeted removal of specific bacteria without affecting other microbiota, which will be described later.

Probiotics, prebiotics and synbiotics

Bacterial probiotics

Probiotics are bacteria that reside in the host’s gut and can promote the health of the host. Compared with the control, probiotics significantly reverse minimal hepatic encephalopathy (MHE) and improve HE, and the incidence of serious adverse events associated with probiotics is lower, demonstrating greater safety and tolerability. Probiotics potentially improve liver function by reducing the MELD score and result in favorable changes in quality of life and the gut microbiota [154].

The most commonly used probiotics currently include Lactobacillus acidophilus, Lactobacillus casei, Bifidobacterium and the Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 strain [155]. VSL#3, composed of eight strains, is a probiotic that is specifically used for the treatment of LC [156]. In a double-blind trial conducted in India, patients with LC who were administered VSL#3 for a period of six months experienced a notable decrease in hospitalization risk, CTP score, and MELD score compared with the control group that received a placebo [156]. Another randomized controlled trial suggested that probiotics have a certain effect on patients with HE, but their efficacy is not as good as that of traditional therapies [157]. Compared with propranolol therapy, VSL#3 has been reported to reduce the serum and hepatic venous TNF-α levels in patients with esophageal and gastric varices; moreover, it can further reduce the hepatic venous pressure gradient [158]. Similarly, a clinical trial showed that the use of Bifidobacterium infantis combined with Clostridium butyricum in patients with MHE can significantly improve cognitive function. In the group treated with probiotics, there was a significant increase in beneficial bacterial species such as Clostridium cluster I and Bifidobacterium. Concurrently, there was a marked decrease in potentially harmful bacteria such as Enterococcus and Enterobacteriaceae. Additionally, probiotic intervention correlated with a substantial decrease in ammonia levels in venous blood [159].

Although most probiotic therapies for LC have been proven to be effective, their mechanisms have only recently begun to be revealed [160]. In patients with LC, neutrophil function can be restored by increasing the production of reactive oxygen species in neutrophils through supplementation with a probiotic mixture of Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Lactococcus, thereby reducing the incidence of infections [161]. In animal models, VSL#3 can prevent increases in intestinal permeability by maintaining the expression of TJs and inhibiting apoptosis [162]. B. pseudocatenulatum CECT7765 has the ability to stimulate a shift toward an anti-inflammatory cytokine profile in intestinal lymphocytes and macrophages through the regulation of TLR2. This action enhances the integrity of the intestinal barrier, reduces the translocation of bacteria, decreases endotoxin levels, and diminishes the bacterial antigen load in the liver. These effects have been validated both in patients with cirrhosis and in experimental models [163, 164]. Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG can also reduce the synthesis of bile acids in the liver by enhancing the negative feedback effect mediated by FGF15 [165]. Lactobacillus salivarius Li01 reduces acute toxin-induced liver injury and hyperammonaemia in mice by stabilizing the intestinal barrier and restoring intestinal bacterial symbiosis [166, 167]. A deeper understanding of probiotic mechanisms could help to better utilize them in the treatment of LC.

Fungal probiotics and phages

Recent studies have shown that therapies targeting fungi and viruses can also improve LC. S. boulardii is a beneficial yeast strain that has been utilized in clinical settings as a probiotic. It offers a significant therapeutic approach for the prevention and management of NAFLD by modulating the gut mycobiome [168, 169]. In a blinded, randomized, placebo-controlled trial, the therapeutic impact of a probiotic containing Saccharomyces boulardii on SIBOs in patients with decompensated LC was assessed. After a three-month treatment period, a significant difference was observed, with 80.0% of the patients in the probiotic group being free of SIBO compared with only 23.1% in the placebo group (p = 0.002). Patients who successfully resolved SIBO experienced a reduction in the frequency of ascites and HE, along with an increase in platelet and albumin levels. Additionally, there was a decrease in total bilirubin, serum LPS, and systemic inflammation levels compared with pretreatment values [170, 171]. However, there was no change in the intestinal barrier marker Claudin3, which is consistent with the findings of another study on the effects of S. boulardii [172]. Notably, there have been previous reports of fungemia caused by S. boulardii in immunocompromised patients [173], and potential probiotic fungi should be approached with caution, especially in patients with LC. In addition, edible mushrooms such as Agaricus bisporus have also been reported to decrease the levels of ALT and AST in a dose-dependent manner; reduce hepatocyte degeneration, necrosis, inflammatory infiltration, and liver damage; enhance the antioxidant status; and improve lipid metabolism in mice with CCl4-induced liver fibrosis [174, 175]. Thus, potential fungal probiotics may offer new perspectives for the treatment of LC (Table 3).

Trials in which probiotics were used in LC

Given that phages are natural killers of harmful bacteria, they could theoretically ameliorate liver disease. However, research on the treatment of LC with bacteriophages is still in its infancy. In one study, in germ-free mice, receiving feces from AH patients with cytolysin-positive Enterococcus faecalis exacerbated ALD, and treating these mice with specific bacteriophages targeting the bacterial strain through oral gavage reversed the worsening of liver disease [176]. These results suggest that phage therapy targeting specific bacterial strains plays an important role in the treatment of LC.

Prebiotics

Prebiotics are indigestible food components that induce the growth or activity of beneficial microorganisms in the gut. These oligosaccharides include bifidogenic, indigestible oligosaccharides (inulin, its hydrolysis product oligofructose, and (trans-) galactooligosaccharides), etc [177]. These dietary components serve as nutrients for the gut microbiota, and their fermentation byproducts, such as SCFAs, are absorbed into the bloodstream. These SCFAs influence not only the gastrointestinal tract but also other organs in the body. Indigestible disaccharides such as lactulose and lactitol are recommended for first-line therapy in the context of HE and have been extensively utilized in the management of LC [178]. A systematic review indicated that individuals who consume nonabsorbable disaccharides have a lower mortality rate than those receiving a placebo or no treatment. Furthermore, these individuals have a decreased likelihood of experiencing severe complications, including liver failure, bleeding, and infections. Nonabsorbable disaccharides are also effective in preventing the onset of HE and in increasing recovery rates among those affected by it [179].

Additionally, prebiotics with improved effects on LC have also been identified in animal models. Garlic polysaccharide can ameliorate liver damage in carbon tetrachloride (CCL4)-treated mice and increase the abundance of probiotics while reducing potential pathogenic bacteria [159]. Polysaccharides from G. frondosa can inhibit oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in fibrotic mice and slow the progression of liver fibrosis by inhibiting the TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway [180]. Dendrobum officinale polysaccharide has been reported to improve the fibrotic process and reduce intestinal mucosal damage [181].

Many studies are currently exploring prebiotics that could be utilized in the treatment of liver diseases, aiming to develop effective methods for the treatment of liver injury and liver conditions [182]. The molecular mechanisms by which prebiotics alleviate LC are not yet clear, and relevant clinical studies are still incomplete and require further exploration.

Synbiotics

Synbiotics are combinations developed to combine the advantages of probiotics and prebiotics [183]. Early reports on HE used mixtures of P. pentosaceus, Leuconostoc mesenteroides, L. paracasei, and L. plantarum with beta glucan, pectin, and resistant starch, and the results revealed that the CTP functional classification improved in nearly 50% of the cases [184]. More recent reports on HE used mixtures of L. paracasei, L. plantarum, L. mesenteroides, and P. pentosaceus with oat bran, pectin, resistant starch, and inulin, and the results revealed no improvement in cognitive levels in HE patients [185]. Several other synbiotic studies targeting NAFLD also suggest limited effects on the improvement of liver fibrosis [186, 187]. The role of synbiotics in the treatment of LC has been preliminarily explored, but the results seem to be contradictory, which may be related to different formulation components, and the development of new synbiotic combinations is still awaited.

Fecal microbiota transplantation

FMT is an emerging nontargeted treatment approach for liver diseases aimed at altering the gut microbiota by transplanting fecal matter from a healthy donor into the patient [188]. In recent years, the number of clinical studies on the impact of FMT on LC has increased. However, the definitive role of FMT in the diagnosis of LC is not entirely convincing. However, the existing data still provide a promising outlook for this pioneering treatment method (Table 4) [189]. FMT may affect the pathogenesis of LC through several potential mechanisms: the production of SCFAs, changes in the structure of the microbial community, bile acid metabolism, and reduced ammonia production [190]. The exact mechanisms are not fully understood and require further exploration.

Trials of FMT in LC

In clinical trials involving patients with LC and HE, combination therapies of lactulose or rifaximin with traditional FMT methods have shown good safety and efficacy [191, 192]. In an open-label randomized trial, 20 outpatients with LC and recurrent HE were randomly divided into two groups, with the experimental group receiving broad-spectrum antibiotics, lactulose, and rifaximin treatment for 5 days before FMT and the control group receiving the same treatment except for FMT [193]. In the placebo group, half of the patients experienced a relapse of HE, whereas none of the patients in the FMT group experienced a recurrence. Additionally, the FMT group demonstrated a reduced rate of hospitalization and a more significant improvement in cognitive function. Compared with those of the control group, the safety and efficacy of FMT can last up to one year, with a significant reduction in the frequency of HE and hospitalization [194]. A major concern about FMT in patients with LC is the risk of infection. Owing to immune function disorders associated with LC, these patients are prone to infections. Studies have reported the risk of SBP and the transfer of multidrug-resistant bacteria after FMT [191, 195].

Conclusion and future perspectives

We summarized the changes in the gut microbiota in patients with LC, the immune and metabolic mechanisms by which the gut microbiota influences the progression of LC, methods for diagnosing and predicting LC via the gut microbiota and its metabolites, and methods for treating LC via regulation of the gut microbiota. In particular, we highlighted the changes in gut nonbacterial components in LC and their roles, methods for diagnosing and predicting LC via the microbiota, and novel therapies for LC involving bacterial and nonbacterial probiotics.

Several issues deserve further exploration. With respect to changes in the gut microbiota, more detailed longitudinal and in-depth analyses of the microbial community on a large scale are still needed. Future experiments should interpret results cautiously, taking into account demographic factors (geographical location, sex, and diet) and disease-related factors (etiology, medication, and treatment). Further research is needed to study the roles of other altered components of the gut microbiota of LC patients, such as fungi, viruses, and archaea. The interactions between the gut microbiota, their metabolites, and the host immune system still need further clarification. In addition to common PAMP‒PRR interactions, other components and metabolites of these species may have unique ways of communicating with the host. In the context of the LC, complex interactions and intercellular communications occur among different immune cells. Finally, new treatment methods, such as probiotics and FMT, are needed. Despite the considerable therapeutic potential demonstrated by new methods such as FMT, there are areas that require refinement. For example, FMT has been reported to facilitate the spread of MDROs and cause secondary infections. The implementation of FMT in cirrhosis patients with compromised immune function requires stricter donor selection criteria and further processing to eliminate potential sources of infection. The gut‒liver axis microbiota in cirrhotic patients is closely related to the progression and survival of patients. Future research is needed to explore the cellular and molecular mechanisms of the gut microbiota in cirrhotic patients.
