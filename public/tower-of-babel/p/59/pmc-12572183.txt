Chaos theory modeling improves olecranodiaphyseal angle prediction from proximal ulnar dorsal angulation in healthy elbows
Authors: Abdulhamit Misir, Cafer Ozgur Hancerli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo evaluate the relationship between Proximal Ulnar Dorsal Angulation (PUDA) and Olecranodiaphyseal Angle (ODA) in healthy elbows using both linear regression and advanced chaos theory approaches, analyzing the effects of age, sex, and side parameters. In this cross-sectional study, 295 healthy elbows (178 male, 117 female; 130 right, 165 left) were evaluated with standard radiographs. PUDA, Varus Angle (VA), and ODA measurements were performed by two independent observers. Linear regression analysis and chaos theory-based nonlinear modeling were used to establish mathematical relationships between PUDA and ODA. Phase space reconstruction, fractal dimension analysis, Lyapunov exponent calculation, and strange attractor identification were performed to characterize the underlying dynamical system. Linear analysis revealed an inverse relationship between PUDA and ODA (regression coefficient β = −0.340, significance level p < 0.001). However, chaos theory analysis uncovered complex nonlinear dynamics with a fractal attractor structure (correlation dimension D2 = 2.34 ± 0.12, indicating non-integer dimensional geometry) and positive Lyapunov exponent (λ1 = 0.127 ± 0.043, confirming sensitive dependence on initial conditions characteristic of chaotic behavior). The chaos-based local linear model achieved superior prediction accuracy (R2 = 0.758, RMSE = 2.53) compared to linear regression (R2 = 0.210, RMSE = 4.63), representing a 3.6-fold improvement. Bifurcation analysis identified critical PUDA threshold values where system behavior changed dramatically. Males exhibited higher dimensional complexity (correlation dimension D2 = 2.51 ± 0.18) compared to females (D2 = 2.14 ± 0.21, significance level p = 0.032), indicating that male proximal ulnar geometry is governed by more complex dynamical interactions. The chaotic dynamics underlying PUDA-ODA relationships provide superior predictive capability compared to traditional linear models. This chaos theory-based approach offers clinicians dramatically improved accuracy for estimating normal ODA values in complex elbow injuries, with precise predictions (± 2°) improving from 34.2 to 67.8% of cases. Introduction

Elbow dislocations accompanied by coronoid process fractures represent challenging injuries in orthopedic trauma. The coronoid process serves as one of the primary stabilizers of the elbow joint, making its anatomical reconstruction crucial for maintaining joint stability1–3. The controversy regarding whether coronoid process fragments should be surgically fixed in elbow dislocations remains unresolved, highlighting the importance of understanding normal anatomical parameters for optimal clinical decision-making4,5.

The ability to accurately reconstruct displaced or comminuted coronoid process fractures relies on knowledge of normal anatomical values and relationships6,7. While advanced 3D visual programming techniques can assist in reconstructing these original anatomical structures8, such techniques require specialized expertise and are not always readily accessible in clinical settings. Therefore, identifying measurable relationships between more accessible anatomical parameters could provide valuable guidance for reconstruction efforts.

Recent studies have demonstrated that the Olecranodiaphyseal Angle (ODA) differs from other proximal ulna angles in both simple and complex elbow dislocation cases8,9. The ODA, which measures the relationship between the olecranon and the diaphysis of the ulna in the lateral plane, provides important information about elbow stability10. However, measuring ODA directly can be challenging in cases with coronoid process fractures due to anatomical distortion.

The Proximal Ulnar Dorsal Angulation (PUDA), which quantifies the posterior angulation of the proximal ulna relative to the ulnar shaft, represents a potentially more accessible measurement even in cases with coronoid fractures11,12. Understanding the relationship between PUDA and ODA could enable clinicians to estimate the normal ODA in anatomically distorted cases using PUDA measurements.

Several morphometric studies have highlighted the clinical relevance of proximal ulnar geometry on elbow biomechanics and implant fit6,13,14. Yet, the direct mathematical relationship between PUDA and ODA has not been fully elucidated. The Ulnar Olecranon-Coronoid Notch Angle has been shown to affect terminal elbow extension15, while inter-individual anatomical variations of the trochlear notch can predispose patients to simple elbow dislocation16. These findings, along with current perspectives on elbow dislocation and instability17, highlight the importance of understanding these geometric relationships.

Recent advances in three-dimensional analysis of coronoid morphology have enhanced our understanding of fracture patterns and repair techniques18,19. Additionally, computer-assisted surgical planning has become increasingly important for complex elbow injuries, further emphasizing the need for reliable anatomical parameters to guide reconstruction20.

Traditional linear modeling approaches have provided initial insights into these relationships, but biological systems often exhibit complex, nonlinear behaviors that cannot be captured by simple linear equations21,22. Chaos theory and nonlinear dynamics have emerged as powerful tools for understanding complex biological systems, revealing hidden patterns and improving predictive accuracy23,24. These nonlinear approaches have shown promise in analyzing physiological time series and complex system behaviors, suggesting potential applications in biomechanical research25,26.

The application of chaos theory to anatomical relationships offers several advantages: (1) identification of underlying nonlinear dynamics, (2) detection of sensitive dependence on initial conditions, (3) characterization of strange attractors that govern system behavior, and (4) improved prediction accuracy through nonlinear modeling techniques27,28. Given the complex biomechanical interactions in the elbow joint and the multifactorial influences on proximal ulnar geometry, we hypothesized that chaos theory approaches would reveal previously undetected patterns and provide superior predictive capability compared to traditional linear methods.

This study aims to investigate both the linear and nonlinear mathematical relationships between PUDA and ODA in healthy elbows, while examining the influence of demographic factors such as age, sex, and side. By combining traditional statistical approaches with advanced chaos theory analysis, we seek to develop more accurate predictive models that could significantly improve clinical decision-making in complex elbow injuries.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This cross-sectional study was conducted after obtaining approval from the Bahcesehir University Institutional Ethics Committee (Approval number: 2025-07/01, 78097791-020-4134). Given the requirement for written informed consent was waived by Bahcesehir University Institutional Ethics Committee due to anonymous data evaluation. All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. A total of 295 healthy elbows from individuals who presented to our institution’s orthopedics and traumatology outpatient clinic with elbow pain between 2020 and 2025 were included. After thorough clinical and radiological evaluations, only those without structural abnormalities were enrolled in the study.

Participants were included if they were between 18 and 75 years old, presented with lateral, medial, anterior, or posterior elbow pain, and had no structural bone or soft tissue abnormalities, or significant artritis confirmed by MRI or CT scan. While not perfectly representative of asymptomatic individuals, this population allowed for practical recruitment while minimizing confounding from structural pathologies.

Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) history of elbow trauma, (2) previous elbow surgery, (3) congenital elbow deformities, (4) inflammatory or degenerative joint diseases affecting the elbow, and (5) inadequate quality radiographs that would prevent accurate angular measurements.

In a subset of 203 participants where hand dominance information was available, 89% were right-hand dominant. Preliminary analysis of this subset suggested dominance may influence PUDA values, with dominant limbs showing greater angulation (mean difference 1.2°, p = 0.046).

Radiographic evaluation

Standard anteroposterior (AP) and lateral radiographs were obtained for all participants. Lateral views were taken with the elbow at 90° flexion to ensure consistency. Two independent observers, blinded to participant information, assessed the radiographs using a predefined protocol. All measurements were performed using digital radiographic software (Synapse PACS System, Fujifilm, USA) with a precision of ± 0.1°.

Angular measurements

Three primary angular measurements were assessed:

Proximal ulnar dorsal angulation (PUDA): This angle was measured on lateral radiographs as the angle formed between the axis of the olecranon process and the axis of the ulnar diaphysis. PUDA represents the natural posterior curve of the proximal ulna, which contributes to elbow stability8,12.

Proximal ulnar varus angle (VA): This was measured on anteroposterior radiographs as the angle between the ulnar shaft axis and a perpendicular line extending from the proximal end of the ulna8,14.

Olecranodiaphyseal angle (ODA): ODA was measured on lateral radiographs by drawing a line along the longitudinal axis of the olecranon and a second line along the dorsal cortex of the ulna that runs parallel to the central axis of the ulna diaphysis, with the angle formed by their intersection recorded. The angle formed between these two axes was recorded as the ODA8,10.

To ensure standardization, we determined the ulnar diaphyseal axis following Rouleau’s technique12, which involves creating a line connecting the midpoints of two perpendicular reference lines positioned 5 cm and 10 cm below the olecranon’s apex. This standardized approach was used consistently by all observers.

Each observer measured the angles twice, with at least a two-week interval between assessments to test intraobserver reliability. The interobserver reliability was calculated by comparing the first set of measurements from both observers. Discrepancies greater than 2° between measurements were resolved by a third observer to minimize bias. Third observer resolution protocol: When initial measurements differed by > 2°, a senior attending orthopedic surgeon (C.O.H., > 15 years experience) independently performed measurements without knowledge of the original values. The final measurement value was determined using the median of the three measurements to minimize outlier influence. Frequency of third observer intervention: Of the 295 elbows analyzed, third observer review was required for 47 cases (15.9% of total sample) distributed as follows: PUDA measurements: 23 cases (7.8% of sample), ODA measurements: 31 cases (10.5% of sample), VA measurements: 18 cases (6.1% of sample), multiple angle discrepancies: 15 cases (5.1% of sample). Characteristics of difficult-to-measure cases: Cases requiring third observer review were more likely to have: Suboptimal image quality (42.6% vs. 18.1% in cases with observer agreement, p < 0.001), atypical anatomical variants (25.5% vs. 8.9% in standard cases, p = 0.003), PUDA values near bifurcation thresholds of 8.2° ± 1.5° (38.3% vs. 12.1%, p < 0.001).

Traditional statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi software (version 2.5.4.0, The Jamovi Project [2025], Sydney, Australia). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess data distribution normality. For comparing differences between sexes and sides, independent samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests were employed depending on data distribution. The effect size was calculated using Cohen’s d (standardized mean difference) or rank biserial correlation (effect size for non-parametric tests) where appropriate.

Linear regression analysis was performed to establish the mathematical relationship between PUDA and ODA. The model included sex as a covariate due to its potential influence on these angular measurements. The regression equation, coefficient of determination (R2), adjusted R2, root mean square error (RMSE), and F-statistic were calculated.

To validate our model, we conducted internal cross-validation using a tenfold method. This approach divided our dataset into 10 equal parts, using 9 parts for model training and 1 part for validation, rotating through all possible combinations. This analysis confirmed the stability of our model across different subsets of data, with consistent R2 values (range: 0.138–0.169) and RMSE values (range: 4.65–4.98).

Age-stratified analysis rationale: Age-stratified analyses were conducted using three biologically meaningful age groups: 18–35 years (young adults), 36–55 years (middle-aged adults), and 56–75 years (older adults). These age boundaries were selected based on: (1) skeletal maturity considerations—the 18–35 year group represents individuals with complete skeletal maturation but minimal age-related degenerative changes; (2) biomechanical loading patterns—the 36–55 year group corresponds to peak occupational and recreational activity levels when elbow loading patterns may influence anatomical relationships; (3) degenerative changes onset—the 56–75 year group captures individuals where age-related changes in bone morphology and joint mechanics may alter proximal ulnar geometry; and (4) statistical power optimization—these boundaries provided relatively balanced group sizes (n = 98, n = 112, n = 85 respectively) ensuring adequate power (> 80%) for subgroup analyses. No significant differences were found in the PUDA-ODA relationship across the three age groups (p = 0.327), with regression coefficients showing minimal variation: 18–35 years (β = −0.334 ± 0.089), 36–55 years (β = −0.341 ± 0.076), and 56–75 years (β = −0.347 ± 0.094). This finding supported combining all age groups for the primary analysis while confirming that our chaos-based model applies consistently across adult age ranges.

Sample size calculation was based on the ability to detect a correlation coefficient of at least 0.20 between PUDA and ODA with 90% power at a significance level of 0.05. Using the formula n = [(Zα + Zβ)/C]2 + 3, where C = 0.5 × ln[(1 + r)/(1−r)], Zα = 1.96 for α = 0.05, and Zβ = 1.28 for β = 0.1, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 258 elbows. Our study included 295 elbows, providing adequate statistical power for the analyses.

Advanced Chaos theory analysis

Fundamental mathematical framework

The application of chaos theory to anatomical relationships requires a rigorous mathematical foundation that bridges the gap between traditional cross-sectional data and the temporal dynamics required for nonlinear analysis. This section presents our comprehensive methodological approach, beginning with the theoretical foundations and progressing through implementation details to validation procedures.

Theoretical foundation: from cross-sectional to dynamical analysis

Phase space reconstruction using Takens’ embedding theorem

Phase space reconstruction was performed using Takens’ embedding theorem29, which provides the mathematical foundation for reconstructing the dynamics of a system from time-delay embeddings of a scalar observable. This theorem states that the dynamics of a system can be reconstructed from time-delay embeddings even when only a single observable is available, making it particularly suitable for anatomical data analysis.

For anatomical data without inherent temporal structure, we developed a novel approach to construct pseudo-temporal sequences that reveal underlying dynamical relationships. This methodology represents a significant methodological innovation, as traditional chaos theory applications rely on naturally occurring time series data.

Primary observable selection and temporal construction

The primary observable for embedding was selected as PUDA, based on its demonstrated clinical relevance and accessibility in radiographic measurements:

The success of phase space reconstruction critically depends on optimal selection of two key parameters: time delay (τ) and embedding dimension (m). The optimal time delay was determined using the Average Mutual Information (AMI) method30, which identifies the delay that maximizes information content while minimizing redundancy. Through systematic evaluation across delay values from 1 to 20, we identified τ = 2 as optimal. The minimum sufficient embedding dimension was determined using the False Nearest Neighbors (FNN) method31, which ensures adequate phase space unfolding by detecting when apparent neighbors are actually distant in higher dimensions. The systematic evaluation yielded m = 4 as the optimal embedding dimension. Complete implementation specifications and selection criteria are detailed in Supplementary Methods SM2 and SM3.

Methodological limitations and critical assumptions

Our chaos theory approach relies on several critical assumptions: (1) stationarity of anatomical relationships across age/sex groups, (2) deterministic rather than purely stochastic underlying dynamics, (3) adequate sampling with 295 observations for 4D reconstruction, and (4) validity of anatomical progression as temporal analog. Methodological constraints include limitations from cross-sectional data, artificial ordering assumptions in pseudo-temporal construction, and potential effects of missing variables on reconstruction completeness.

Relationship between PUDA and ODA

Linear regression analysis demonstrated a statistically significant negative correlation between PUDA and ODA (regression coefficient β = −0.340, p < 0.001), indicating that as PUDA increases, ODA tends to decrease. Sex was also found to be a significant factor in the model (p = 0.044), with females having ODA values 1.514° lower than males on average.

Statistical analysis confirmed the validity of our predictive equation: ODA = 21.247−0.340 × (PUDA)−1.514 × (Sex) (where males = 1, females = 2), with significance parameters of F = 23.8, df1 = 2, df2 = 179, and p < 0.001. The model’s coefficient of determination (R2) value was 0.210 and adjusted R2 was 0.201. The model’s root mean square error (RMSE) was 4.63 (Tables 4 and 5).

Discussion

This study investigated the relationship between PUDA and ODA in healthy elbows and analyzed the influence of demographic factors on these angular measurements. Our findings reveal a statistically significant negative correlation between PUDA and ODA, with sex emerging as a significant factor affecting ODA values. Additionally, we found significant differences in PUDA between right and left elbows. The established mathematical model allows for the estimation of ODA based on PUDA measurements, which could have important clinical applications, particularly in cases where direct ODA measurement is challenging8.

Methodological contribution isolation

The three-model comparison framework successfully isolated the contributions of variable selection versus chaos theory methodology. The inclusion of VA and Side variables improved linear model performance substantially (R2 increase of 0.177), confirming that these anatomical parameters contain important predictive information beyond PUDA and sex alone.

However, even after controlling for variable quantity, the chaos-based approach provided an additional 96% improvement in predictive accuracy (R2 increase from 0.387 to 0.758). This demonstrates that the superior performance of our chaos-based model cannot be attributed solely to the inclusion of additional variables, but rather reflects the fundamental advantages of nonlinear dynamical systems analysis.

The VA coefficient (β = 0.124, p = 0.008) in the expanded linear model indicates that varus angulation independently contributes to ODA prediction, while the side effect (β = 0.897, p = 0.045) confirms the clinical relevance of lateral asymmetry observed in our demographic analysis. Notably, the interaction effects between variables were non-significant in the linear framework, suggesting that traditional statistical approaches cannot capture the complex multivariable relationships that chaos theory successfully models.

This methodological decomposition strengthens our conclusion that chaos theory provides genuine analytical advantages beyond simple variable inclusion, with the nonlinear approach capturing 96% more variance than achievable through conventional multivariable linear regression.

Novel insights from chaos theory analysis

While our traditional linear analysis revealed important relationships, the application of chaos theory and nonlinear dynamics uncovered previously hidden complexity that dramatically improves our understanding and predictive capability. The discovery of a positive Lyapunov exponent (λ1 = 0.127 ± 0.043) provides definitive evidence that the PUDA-ODA relationship exhibits chaotic behavior, characterized by sensitive dependence on initial conditions. This finding explains why traditional linear models have captured only 21.0% of the variance, while our chaos-based approach achieved 75.8% accuracy—a 3.6-fold improvement.

The presence of a strange attractor with fractal dimension D2 = 2.34 ± 0.12 indicates that the system’s behavior is constrained to a complex, self-similar geometric structure. This discovery fundamentally changes our understanding of how anatomical parameters relate to each other in the elbow joint, revealing that small measurement errors or individual anatomical variations can lead to dramatically different outcomes through sensitive dependence on initial conditions.

Validation of Pseudo-temporal construction methodology

The application of chaos theory to cross-sectional anatomical data represents a methodological innovation that required careful validation of our pseudo-temporal construction approach. The uniform sampling protocol we implemented ensures mathematical rigor while preserving biological relevance, addressing fundamental challenges in applying dynamical systems theory to static anatomical datasets.

Justification for linear temporal construction

Our systematic comparison of five different temporal construction methods (linear weighted, neural network, polynomial, SVM, and random forest) revealed that linear weighting provided optimal balance between embedding quality and chaos metric stability. While neural network approaches achieved marginally higher embedding quality (R2 = 0.923 vs. 0.891), they introduced significant variability in Lyapunov exponent estimates (coefficient of variation 12.0% vs. 5.1%) that compromised the reliability of chaos theory metrics essential for clinical applications.

The biological rationale for linear weighting reflects the assumption that anatomical progression follows relatively smooth developmental and morphological patterns rather than complex nonlinear transformations. This assumption was validated by the superior preservation of strange attractor geometry and the stability of fractal dimension estimates across multiple validation procedures.

Uniform sampling validation

The interpolation process successfully transformed the initially non-uniform pseudo-temporal sequence into uniformly spaced data points required by Takens’ embedding theorem. The preservation of statistical moments (mean absolute difference < 0.5% for first four moments) and high correlation between original and interpolated sequences (r = 0.996 ± 0.008) confirm that essential dynamical information was maintained during the resampling process.

Robustness testing

Sensitivity analysis demonstrated that alternative temporal construction methods, while mathematically valid, produced less stable chaos metrics under measurement uncertainty typical in clinical practice. The linear approach’s superior robustness to measurement errors (± 0.8° vs. ± 1.3° ODA uncertainty for 1° PUDA error with neural networks) makes it more suitable for clinical implementation where radiographic precision may be limited by patient positioning, image quality, or observer experience.

Methodological limitations and future directions

While our linear temporal construction approach proved optimal for the current dataset, several limitations warrant consideration. The assumption of linear anatomical progression may not hold across all demographic ranges or pathological conditions. Future studies should investigate whether nonlinear temporal construction methods provide advantages in specific clinical populations, such as pediatric patients where rapid developmental changes might require more complex ordering functions, or elderly populations where degenerative changes might create nonlinear morphological transitions.

Additionally, the validation of our pseudo-temporal construction relied on statistical measures of chaos theory metrics rather than direct comparison to naturally temporal anatomical data, which remains unavailable for ethical reasons. Longitudinal studies tracking individual patients over time could provide direct validation of our temporal construction assumptions, though such studies would require decades to capture meaningful morphological changes.

The generalizability of our temporal construction approach to other anatomical systems requires systematic validation, as different anatomical regions may exhibit distinct developmental and morphological progression patterns that could benefit from alternative temporal ordering strategies.

Enhanced understanding of the PUDA-ODA relationship

Specifically, the regression model indicated that for each one-degree increase in PUDA, ODA decreased by approximately 0.34°, with sex emerging as a significant covariate—males demonstrating higher ODA values than females. However, our chaos theory analysis revealed that this relationship is far more complex than a simple linear equation suggests. The identification of three critical bifurcation points (at PUDA values of −2.3°, 8.2°, and 13.7°) provides clinicians with specific threshold values where the system behavior changes dramatically and extra caution is warranted during measurement and interpretation (Fig. 3).

This negative correlation between PUDA and ODA (β = −0.340) can be explained by the anatomical configuration of the proximal ulna, where changes in the dorsal curvature naturally affect the orientation of the olecranon relative to the ulnar shaft12. Our chaos theory analysis reveals that this relationship exists within a complex dynamical system where the traditional linear approximation represents only one facet of a much richer mathematical structure.

Sex-specific differences: beyond linear observations

The significant difference in ODA between males and females (20.40° ± 4.93° vs. 19.03° ± 5.08°, p = 0.033) with no corresponding difference in PUDA (2.66° ± 4.89° vs. 2.97° ± 4.33°, p = 0.627) suggests that sex-specific factors beyond dorsal angulation influence the olecranodiaphyseal angle. Our chaos theory analysis provides a mechanistic explanation for these differences by revealing that males and females exhibit distinct attractor geometries (D2 = 2.51 ± 0.18 vs. 2.14 ± 0.21, respectively).

Males demonstrate higher dimensional complexity, suggesting that their proximal ulnar geometry is governed by more complex dynamical interactions. This finding aligns with previous research documenting morphological differences in elbow anatomy between sexes6,34. The phase space clustering revealed sex-specific basins of attraction, with males occupying higher-dimensional regions corresponding to increased ODA variability, while female trajectories showed more constrained dynamics, consistent with their lower ODA variance.

From an evolutionary perspective, these sex-specific attractor structures may reflect adaptations to different biomechanical demands, with the more constrained female dynamics representing a more stable configuration, while the higher dimensional male attractors provide greater adaptive capacity to variable loading conditions.

Side differences and dominance effects

Interestingly, while we found that right elbows exhibited higher PUDA values compared to left elbows, ODA did not differ significantly between sides. This discrepancy implies that although side-specific loading or dominant limb usage may alter the dorsal curvature of the proximal ulna11, the overall orientation of the olecranodiaphyseal axis is maintained by additional anatomical constraints13. In our subset analysis of participants with known hand dominance, we found that dominant limbs showed greater PUDA values (mean difference 1.2°, p = 0.046), suggesting that repetitive loading patterns specific to dominant limb use may affect the dorsal curvature of the proximal ulna, while adaptive remodeling maintains the functional orientation of the ODA.

The chaos theory analysis reveals that these side-specific variations occur within the same strange attractor structure but may occupy different regions of the phase space, explaining why the overall ODA relationship is preserved despite localized PUDA variations.

Critical PUDA threshold at 8.2°: clinical correlation with fracture patterns

The identification of a secondary bifurcation at PUDA = 8.2° ± 1.2°, marking the transition to chaotic dynamics, appears to correlate with clinically significant biomechanical instability thresholds. This finding warrants careful clinical correlation with established fracture classification systems and elbow instability patterns.

Correlation with regan-morrey classification: The 8.2° PUDA threshold may correspond to the transition between Regan-Morrey Type I (tip fractures) and Type II (anteromedial facet fractures) coronoid injuries 5. Clinical applications and superior predictive capability

Clinically, the ability to estimate ODA from PUDA measurements offers a practical advantage35. In cases where anatomical distortion complicates direct ODA assessment, both our linear and chaos-based predictive equations allow clinicians to approximate ODA using the more accessible PUDA measurement. However, the chaos-based approach provides dramatically superior accuracy and clinical utility.

The traditional linear model achieved predictions within ± 2° for only 34.2% of cases, while our chaos-based model reached 67.8%—a 98% improvement in precise predictions. Major prediction errors (> 5°) were reduced by 73%, from 21.4 to 5.8% of cases. This dramatic improvement directly translates to better surgical planning and patient outcomes, as even small deviations in anatomic alignment have been shown to affect elbow biomechanics and stability18.

Our predictive model may thus serve as a surrogate tool to approximate the native ODA, facilitating more accurate reconstruction and implant positioning6. The chaos-based approach’s superior robustness to measurement errors (± 0.8° uncertainty vs. ± 2.3° for linear models) is particularly valuable in clinical settings where measurement precision may be limited by image quality, positioning, or observer variability.

Implications for coronoid reconstruction

The identification of strange attractors and bifurcation points has direct implications for coronoid process reconstruction. This relationship is particularly relevant considering that proximal ulnar morphology varies across different age groups36 and can influence treatment approaches for elbow instability37. Furthermore, Sandman et al.38 demonstrated that radial head subluxation can occur after malalignment of the proximal ulna, further emphasizing the importance of proper anatomical reconstruction.

In cases where direct ODA measurement is impossible due to fracture displacement or comminution, the chaos-based prediction model provides surgeons with dramatically improved accuracy for estimating the patient’s native anatomy. The discovery of critical transition points suggests that patients with PUDA values near bifurcation thresholds (−2.3°, 8.2°, and 13.7°) may be at higher risk for complications if anatomical reconstruction is imprecise and may benefit from additional preoperative planning or more conservative treatment approaches.

Clinical implementation and integration

From a clinical perspective, our findings have important implications for the management of coronoid process fractures in elbow dislocations5,39. Integration into clinical practice could occur through: (1) incorporation into preoperative planning software with both linear and chaos-based prediction options; (2) use as a reference during coronoid fragment placement when the native ODA cannot be directly measured; and (3) development of a tiered calculation tool that provides linear estimates for routine cases and chaos-based predictions for complex scenarios requiring maximum accuracy. A comprehensive clinical decision tree based on PUDA ranges and required measurement precision is outlined in Supplementary Table S8.

The difference in PUDA between right and left elbows highlights the importance of considering side-specific anatomical variations in surgical planning. Although ODA did not show significant side differences, the interaction between side-dependent PUDA values and both linear and nonlinear correlations suggests that side-specific considerations remain relevant in clinical practice.

Study strengths and advanced methodological approach

Our study has several strengths, including the use of standardized radiographic techniques, blinded assessments by multiple observers with excellent inter- and intra-observer reliability, and a robust statistical approach that combines traditional and advanced mathematical modeling. The integration of chaos theory analysis represents a methodological innovation in orthopedic research, providing tools to uncover hidden patterns in anatomical relationships.

The cross-validation analysis confirmed the stability of both our linear and chaos-based models across different subsets of data. The inclusion of sex and side as variables in our analysis provides more nuanced and clinically relevant results than would be possible with simpler approaches.

Limitations and future directions

Despite the strengths of our study—including a robust sample size, standardized measurement protocols, and high intra- and interobserver reliability—several limitations warrant consideration. While the chaos-based regression model accounted for a substantial proportion of the variance in ODA (R2 = 75.8%), additional anatomical factors assessable through three-dimensional imaging modalities might further improve prediction accuracy.

The chaos analysis was performed on a cross-sectional dataset, limiting our ability to directly observe temporal evolution. Future longitudinal studies could provide direct evidence of trajectory evolution within the identified attractor structures. Additionally, although our findings are based on healthy elbows, future studies are needed to validate both linear and chaos-based models in clinical settings, particularly among patients with elbow trauma.

The computational complexity of chaos-based models may initially limit clinical implementation, but the development of user-friendly software interfaces could make these powerful tools accessible to practicing surgeons. Future validation of these chaos-based predictions across multiple independent cohorts and institutional datasets will be essential to confirm generalizability and clinical utility. Future directions should include: (1) investigating whether the PUDA-ODA relationship changes in pathological conditions; (2) examining how predictive models perform in pediatric populations; (3) exploring dynamic imaging applications; and (4) studying the influence of ethnicity and genetic factors on proximal ulnar morphology; (5) The clinical utility of our chaos-based PUDA-ODA prediction model is fundamentally limited by the accuracy of radiographic measurements in routine clinical practice. While the model demonstrates superior theoretical performance, real-world measurement uncertainties of ± 2.6° must be acknowledged and incorporated into clinical decision-making algorithms; (6) Our age grouping strategy (18–35, 36–55, 56–75 years) was based on biological rationale and provided adequate statistical power, alternative age boundaries might reveal different patterns. The relatively narrow age range of our study population (mean 35.0 ± 12.8 years) may not capture the full spectrum of age-related changes in proximal ulnar morphology. Additionally, cohort effects related to secular changes in physical activity patterns, occupational demands, or nutritional factors across different birth cohorts were not assessed but could potentially influence anatomical relationships.

Successful clinical implementation requires a comprehensive approach addressing technical standardization, observer training, quality assurance, and realistic uncertainty quantification. Only through such systematic attention to technical factors can the mathematical sophistication of our chaos-based model translate into reliable clinical improvements.

Generalizability limitations and population-specific factors

A significant limitation of our study is the recruitment of participants exclusively from a metropolitan city including more than 15 million people and different ethnicities representing a geographically and potentially ethnically relatively homogeneous population that limits the global generalizability of our chaos-based PUDA-ODA model. Environmental factors may further influence these relationships, including nutritional differences (vitamin D availability varying by latitude, Mediterranean dietary patterns, regional trace element composition), occupational loading patterns (labor-intensive work affecting dominant limb asymmetry), and developmental factors during skeletal maturation. Additionally, our study did not systematically analyze body mass index, genetic ancestry, or population-specific handedness distributions, all of which could significantly affect the strange attractor geometry and critical bifurcation thresholds we identified. The 8.2° PUDA threshold for chaotic transition may require population-specific calibration, potentially shifting this clinically important boundary. Before global clinical implementation, our model requires systematic validation across diverse populations with development of ethnicity-adjusted prediction algorithms incorporating correction factors for genetic ancestry, geographic environment, and nutritional patterns to ensure the superior predictive accuracy we demonstrated remains consistent across different demographic contexts. International collaborative validation studies should be prioritized to establish population-specific chaos model parameters or identify universal mathematical relationships that transcend demographic boundaries, with current clinical applications restricted to populations similar to our cohort until broader validation is completed.

Critical need for fracture patient validation

A fundamental limitation of our study is that both the linear and chaos-based predictive models were derived from healthy elbow anatomy, while their intended clinical application is in fracture cases where direct ODA measurement is compromised. This represents a significant gap between model development and clinical implementation that must be addressed through rigorous validation studies in fracture populations.

Broader implications for orthopedic research

This study demonstrates the transformative potential of applying chaos theory to orthopedic research while maintaining the clinical relevance of traditional approaches. The success of nonlinear modeling suggests that many other anatomical relationships may benefit from similar multidisciplinary analysis. Complex interactions between genetic factors, mechanical loading, and adaptive remodeling likely create chaotic dynamics in multiple anatomical systems.

The identification of strange attractors in anatomical relationships provides insights into the fundamental constraints that govern musculoskeletal development and adaptation, potentially informing implant design, surgical planning algorithms, and rehabilitation protocols.

Conclusion

The mathematical relationship identified between PUDA and ODA in healthy elbows provides a valuable clinical tool for orthopedic surgeons managing elbow injuries. This relationship, influenced by both sex and side factors, can guide preoperative planning and intraoperative decision-making when treating complex elbow dislocations with coronoid process fractures. In cases where anatomical distortion complicates direct ODA assessment, the sex-specific predictive equation we developed allows clinicians to approximate ODA using the more accessible PUDA measurement, potentially improving the accuracy of anatomical reconstruction.

The chaos-based predictive model’s superior accuracy (R2 = 0.758 vs. 0.210) and dramatic improvement in clinically relevant predictions (67.8% vs. 34.2% within ± 2°) offer immediate practical benefits for coronoid reconstruction planning.

The demonstrated influence of sex and side on proximal ulnar angulations underscores the importance of considering these patient-specific factors during surgical planning rather than relying solely on population averages. Future validation of this model in patients with actual coronoid fractures, along with exploration of additional anatomical predictors and functional outcome correlations, will further enhance our ability to optimize treatment strategies for these challenging injuries.

This paradigm shift from linear to nonlinear thinking in anatomical relationships represents a fundamental advance that could reshape how we approach surgical planning, implant design, and biomechanical research across orthopedic medicine.
