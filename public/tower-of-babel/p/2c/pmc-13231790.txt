A first-in-class bifunctional antibody targeting CD20 and CD37 remodels the immune microenvironment in relapsed or refractory B-cell malignancies
Authors: Li Wang, Jiaying Liu, Keshu Zhou, Zengjun Li, Yun Zeng, Ying Qian, Yuping Sun, Hui Wu, Lindsay Roerker, Peter Tan, Chen Ma, Liwei Zhou, Xianlin Duan, Xiaoyan Kang, Wei-Li Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBispecific antibodies engaging CD3 have transformed the treatment landscape of B-cell malignancies but remain constrained by T-cell overactivation and cytokine release. Here, we describe PSB202, a first-in-class bifunctional antibody co-targeting CD20 and CD37 to deplete malignant B cells independently of T-cell engagement.MethodsIn this multicenter, open-label phase Ia trial (NCT05003141), adults with relapsed or refractory (R/R) CD20⁺ B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma (B-NHL) received escalating doses of PSB202 from 12 to 300 mg. Primary endpoints were dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) and maximum tolerated dose (MTD). Secondary endpoints were safety, pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and efficacy.ResultsFifteen heavily-pretreated patients were enrolled, with a median age of 67.7 years (range, 54–78). Background

Therapeutic targeting of CD20 revolutionized the treatment landscape of B-cell malignancies, but relapse and refractory disease remain inevitable in most patients despite rituximab-based therapy [1–3]. Although chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy has extended survival in patients with relapsed/refractory (R/R) B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma (B-NHL), its broader clinical application remains limited by the risks of cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and neurotoxicity, as well as the complexity of manufacturing and high costs. Emerging bispecific antibodies (bsAbs) that recruit T cells via CD3 engagement reproduce the efficacy of CAR T-cell therapies [4–6], while markedly attenuating the incidence and severity of CRS, neurotoxicity, and manufacturing complexity—thereby underscoring the distinct advantages of antibody-based approaches [7–10]. Thus, the development of dual blockade therapies for synergistic tumor eradication is imperative, offering a strategy to mitigate the toxicities associated with chemotherapy and CAR-T cell therapy.

CD20 and CD37 are integral membrane proteins co-expressed on mature B cells and B-cell lymphomas [11–14]. CD20 mediates calcium influx and survival signaling [11], while CD37 participates in lymphocyte adhesion and apoptosis regulation [15–18]. Preclinical findings indicated the physical and functional interaction between CD20 and CD37, as well as the robust synergy in complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC) upon dual inhibition [19, 20]. In addition, co-targeting CD20 and CD37 has been shown to augment macrophage-mediated effector functions, supporting efficient B-cell clearance [21]. Therefore, we hypothesized that simultaneous blockade of CD20 and CD37 would achieve potent B-cell depletion and reshape the tumor microenvironment (TME), providing a rational and feasible approach.

PSB202 is a first-in-class bifunctional antibody consisting of engineered anti-CD20 and anti-CD37 monoclonal antibodies at a 1:1 configuration. In preclinical models, PSB202 induced antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) and CDC as well as enhanced immune-mediated clearance of B-cell maligancies [22], highlighting the therapeutic potential of dual blockade of CD20 and CD37.

Here we report a first-in-human, multi-center phase Ia trial of PSB202 to evaluate its efficacy, safety, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamic characteristics in patients with R/R B-cell malignancies.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study (PSB202-01) was a multicenter open-label trial conducted in six centers across China, Australia, and the United States (Supplementary Appendix), consisting of a phase Ia dose-escalation followed by a phase Ib dose-expansion across three previously treated cohorts.

Adult patients with histologically confirmed CD20+ indolent B-NHL or mantle cell lymphoma who experienced relapsed or refractory disease (as defined previously [23]) after treatment with at least two prior lines of standard therapy were enrolled in this study. Patients were required to have measurable lesions, an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0–2, and adequate hematological, hepatic, and renal functions. Exclusion criteria mainly were CAR T-cell therapy or autologous stem cell transplant within 180 days or anticancer therapy within 4 weeks or five half-lives of the drug prior to the first dose of PSB202, central nervous system lymphoma, and active graft-versus-host disease. A washout period of > 4 weeks was required following the last dose of any anti-CD20 antibody. Full eligibility criteria are provided in the Protocol.

This study was done in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, the international standards of good clinical practice, and applicable local laws and regulations. The study protocol and all amendments were reviewed and approved by the institutional review board or independent ethics committee at each participating site. All patients provided written informed consent before enrollment. A safety review committee provided oversight of safety. This study was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT05003141.

Procedure

Following the accelerated titration design, this trial (PSB202-01) initially adopted single-patient dose escalation until the completion of the first three dose cohorts (12, 36, and 100 mg), observed grade 2 or higher adverse events (AEs) in the dose-limiting toxicity (DLT) assessment period that could not be reasonably attributed to the patient’s underlying diseases, concurrent medical conditions, or concomitant medications, subsequently converting to a 3 + 3 design with modified Fibonacci design. The top escalated dose level was set at 2500 mg. The Safety Review Committee (SRC), composed of investigators and the sponsor, evaluated the safety data generated in the study prior to each dose escalation to determine whether the trial should proceed to the next dose level. This procedure was implemented to ensure patient safety and could lead to trial termination before reaching the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) if warranted by the available data. DLTs were evaluated in a 28-day observation period and defined as treatment-emergent AEs (TEAEs) per the National Cancer Institute-Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI-CTCAE) version 5.0 and hematologic events in chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) patients according to the iwCLL guidelines, including grade ≥ 3 non-hematologic and hematologic AEs, grade 5 fatal events, grade 3 or 4 CRS, and any events leading to dose discontinuation, dose reductions, and less than 75% of the planned PSB202 dose. The MTD was defined as the highest dose at which less than 2 of 6 patients experienced DLTs.

Patients received intravenous PSB202 at a loading dose on days 1, 8, and 15 in cycle 1 and on day 1 thereafter. Treatment was given in a 21-day cycle for up to 9 cycles until disease progression (PD), unacceptable toxicities, or other discontinuation criteria prespecified in the protocol. Premedication with corticosteroids, montelukast, acetaminophen, antihistamine, and glucocorticoid was permitted prior to the first dose of PSB202. Dose modifications were allowed to manage toxicity, with a maximum of two dose reductions. Safety was monitored during the study until 3 weeks after the last dose of PSB202. AEs were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities version 24.1 and graded by the NCI-CTCAE version 5.0. The hematologic toxicities in CLL patients were graded on the basis of the iwCLL guidelines. The tumor response was assessed by the investigator using computed tomography with or without positron emission tomography (PET-CT) or magnetic resonance imaging at baseline, every 3 cycles during the first 6 months, and every 12 weeks after 9 cycles of PSB202, as per 2018 iwCLL guidelines, criteria from Eighth International Workshops on WM (IWWM8), and Lugano 2014 criteria when appropriate. The bone marrow aspirate and biopsy, and PET-CT were required to confirm the first documented complete response (CR).

Blood samples were collected at the indicated time points to determine pharmacokinetics. Plasma concentrations of PSB202 were evaluated using the validated Enzyme Linked Immunosorbent Assay and pharmacokinetic parameters, including maximum concentration (Cmax), time to reach maximum concentration (Tmax), area under the concentration–time curve (AUC), and elimination half-life (T1/2), were calculated using the non-compartmental analysis method.

In vitro cytotoxicity was assessed by incubating human Burkitt lymphoma cell lines (WSU-DLCL2 [RRID: CVCL_1880], Ramos [RRID: CVCL_0597], Raji [RRID: CVCL_0511], and Daudi [RRID: CVCL_0008]) with indicated antibodies in complete RPMI-1640 media for 7 days at 37 °C and quantified by a colorimetric WST-8 assay. ADCC was assessed using Raji cells co-cultured with human natural killer cells isolated from human peripheral blood mononuclear cells. After a 4-h incubation, cytotoxicity was quantified by measuring lactate dehydrogenase release using a Cytotoxicity Detection Kit (Roche). Antibodies were incubated with Raji cells and 5% human complement (Sigma-Aldrich) for 2 h to measure CDC. Cell viability was determined by an Alamar Blue assay (Life Technologies). Cell line-derived xenograft studies were approved by the institutional ethical committee of Shanghai Ruijin Hospital.

Full guidance on the study schedule in the dose-escalation and expansion parts, the detailed definition of DLTs, treatment decisions including dose modification criteria, toxicity management, permitted concomitant treatments, and sampling are provided in the Protocol.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was the identification of DLTs and determination of the MTD and recommended dose for further studies; the secondary endpoints were safety, pharmacokinetics, investigator-assessed overall response rate (ORR), and pharmacodynamic biomarkers. Detailed information on outcomes is provided in the Protocol.

Tumor dissociation and single-cell RNA/single-cell T-cell receptor sequencing (scRNA-seq/scTCR-seq)

The scRNA-seq/scTCR-seq sequencing was performed in six tumor biopsy samples were collected from 3 patients of the 300 mg cohort at specific time points, including Patient 03 (baseline: cycle 1 day 1 [C1D1]; post-treatment at partial response [PR] and at PD), Patient 09 (baseline: C1D1), and Patient 07 (baseline: C1D1; post-treatment at PR). Samples were immediately dissociated through mechanical mincing followed by enzymatic digestion with collagenase IV (1.5 mg/mL; Worthington) and DNase I (20 U/mL; STEMCELL) in RPMI-1640 medium at 37 °C for 30 min. The resulting suspensions were filtered through 70-μm strainers (Falcon) and washed with phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.04% bovine serum albumin. Cell viability was confirmed to be > 90% by trypan blue exclusion (Countess II, Thermo Fisher). Single-cell libraries were prepared using Chromium Single Cell 5’ Kit v2 (10 × Genomics) for transcriptomes (n = 6 libraries) Chromium Single Cell V(D)J Enrichment Kit v2 for TCR repertoire (n = 5 libraries) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Library quality was verified by Qubit dsDNA HS Assay (Thermo Fisher) and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer High Sensitivity DNA kit. Paired-end sequencing (150 bp) was performed on Illumina NovaSeq 6000. scRNA-seq and scTCR-seq FASTQ files were preprocessed using Cell Ranger v6.0.1 (10 × Genomics) with default parameters.

scRNA-seq analysis

Downstream bioinformatic processing was conducted in R v4.4.1 using the Seurat package v4.4.0. Initial quality control implemented gene-level filtering (removing features detected in < 3 cells) and cell-level exclusion criteria (discarding cells with Unique Molecular Identifier [UMI] counts > 20,000 and gene counts < 200, gene counts > 6,000 and gene counts < 200, or mitochondrial content > 15%). Count matrices underwent log-normalization, followed by cell cycle phase scoring. Highly variable genes were identified through vst method with subsequent removal of ribosome- and mitochondria-associated genes. Data scaling was performed, regressing out mitochondrial percentage and S/G2M phase scores. Dimensional reduction was achieved through principal component analysis with batch correction implemented via Harmony v1.2.0 [24]. Cell clustering employed k-nearest neighbor graphs and Louvain optimization, visualized in two-dimensional Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) embeddings. Canonical marker-based cell type annotation was performed, with doublet exclusion based on co-expression of lineage-specific markers [25]. Specialized analyses included: 1) Copy number variation (CNV) estimation using InferCNV v1.20.0 [26]; 2) Differential gene expression detection via FindMarkers (thresholds: |avg_log2FC|> 1.0, adj. p < 0.05) with Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment (clusterProfiler v4.12.6); 3) Intercellular communication network inference through CellChat v2.1.2 [27] including pathway information flow comparisons via rankNet. The CNV score was calculated using the infercnv R package (v1.20.0) with T cells as the normal reference group. For each cell, we computed a CNV score as the total number of genes whose normalized expression deviated from the diploid baseline (1.0) by more than 0.1 (absolute difference > 0.1). This metric reflects the overall CNV burden per cell. The CNV scores were then added to the Seurat object metadata for downstream visualization and statistical comparisons.

scTCR-seq analysis

Processed TCR sequence annotations (filtered_contig_annotations.csv), generated as standard outputs from CellRanger pipelines, served as primary inputs for subsequent clonotype analyses. We then sequentially intersected the filtered contig annotations of each sample with T cells using scRepertoire (v2.0.7). Clonotypes were defined based on identical CDR3 amino acid sequences of paired α/β chains. Cell-level clonotype information was integrated with transcriptomic data [28]. For each group, three complementary diversity indices (Shannon index) were computed using the vegan package (v2.6–8).

Statistical analysis

Approximately 32–40 patients were scheduled in the phase Ia dose-escalation study based on the design of up to 8 dose cohorts, which provided a sufficient basis to determine the MTD and recommended dose for subsequent trials. The safety population included all enrolled patients who received at least 1 dose of PSB202. Efficacy was analyzed in the full analysis set (FAS), defined as patients with measurable diseases at baseline among the safety population. All data reported here were based on the results of phase Ia dose-escalation. Baseline characteristics and safety data were summarized using descriptive statistics.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between March 1, 2022 and November 5, 2024, 15 patients with R/R B-NHL were enrolled in the four dose levels (12 mg: n = 1; 36 mg: n = 2; 100 mg: n = 9; 300 mg: n = 3) of the phase Ia dose-escalation part across six international centers in China, Australia, and the United States. The median age was 67.7 (range, 54–78) years, and 60.0% of patients were male. Most of the patients were diagnosed with marginal zone lymphoma (6/15 [40%]) and mantle cell lymphoma (3/15 [20%]), with 86.7% (13/15) of patients having stage III-IV disease. Patients received a median of 3 (range, 2–7) prior systemic therapy. None of the patients received prior CD20 × CD3 bispecific antibodies. Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Safety and tolerability

PSB202 was well tolerated across all dose levels from 12 mg up to 300 mg. DLT of grade 4 neutropenia with fever occurred in one patient at the 100 mg dose level. The MTD was not reached. The most common TEAEs of ≥ grade 3 were leukopenia and neutropenia (both 60.0%) (Fig. 1A). No CRS or neurotoxicity occurred (Table 2). Four patients (26.7%) had serious AEs (SAEs), including neutropenia (one case of neutropenic fever and three cases of grade 4 neutropenia) and platelet count decreased (n = 2, 13.3%). Seven (46.7%) patients required dose interruptions because of TEAEs, including hematologic toxicities in 2 patients (28.6%; grade 4 neutropenia: n = 1; grade 4 neutropenia and grade 4 thrombopenia: n = 1), and non-hematologic AEs in 5 patients (71.4%; grade 1–2 infusion-related reactions [IRRs]: n = 4; grade 3 lung infection: n = 1). No TEAEs leading to treatment discontinuation or death occurred.

After the occurrence of DLT in the 100 mg cohort, the protocol was amended to include a 36 mg priming dose administered 7 days prior to the target dose. The 300 mg dose level (with a priming dose of 36 mg) was chosen for confirmation in part 2 of this study on the basis of collective safety, efficacy, pharmacokinetic, and pharmacodynamic data.

Efficacy

One patient at a 300 mg dose level achieved a CR, and two had a PR (one at 100 mg and one at 300 mg). In the FAS (n = 15), the ORR per investigator was 20.0%. Of 10 evaluable patients, the ORR was 30.0%. Two patients had stable disease, and the disease control rate (DCR) was 33.3% in the FAS and 50.0% in the efficacy-evaluable patients (Fig. 1B).

Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics

The sampling and data analysis were performed as shown in Fig. 2A. The plasma concentration–time curves and pharmacokinetic parameters of PSB202 are summarized in Table 3 and Fig. 2B. Among 13 patients evaluable for pharmacokinetics, the plasma exposure of PSB202 after a single intravenous infusion increased with escalating doses, peaking from 4 to 11.7 h. The exposure (Cmax and area under the concentration–time curve from time zero to time t [AUC0-t]) showed great between-patient variability. The PSB202 appeared to be eliminated with a half-life of approximately 53 to 154 h. Overall, pharmacokinetic parameters of PSB202 exhibited dose-dependent, non-linear behavior, with an increase in exposure and half-life at 300 mg upon repeated administration. At a 300 mg dose level, the average concentration at steady state exceeded the preclinically estimated EC90 for human B-cell depletion, likely suggesting adequate pharmacologic exposure to support target engagement.

In the phase Ia part, biomarker data were obtained from all 13 patients dosed with PSB202 12 to 300 mg. Following administration of PSB202 at the dose level of 12 mg or higher, a long-term eradication of circulating CD19+ cells up to median cycle 5 (range, 1–9) was observed (Fig. 2C). An increase in CD19⁺ cells from cycle 2 was observed in patient 13 of the 100 mg cohort; however, the clinical relevance of this change could not be determined. Besides, PSB202 infusion resulted in a transient increase followed by a significant and sustained depletion in CD19+ cells in 2 patients from the 100 mg and 300 mg cohorts; this might be explained by the decrease of total peripheral lymphocyte counts after infusion, leading to a relative increase of the proportion of CD19+ B cells. In the 300 mg cohort, significant IFN-γ release was observed in all 3 patients, and other cytokines (IL2, IL4, IL6, IL10, IL17A, and TNF-α) were not changed from baseline (Extended Data Fig. 1). In 2 cases that IgM, IgG, IgA, free light chain κ and λ, and κ/λ ratio from baseline were collected, no apparent change was observed from baseline (Fig. 2D); however, due to the small sample size, the effect of PSB202 on the antibody formation and immune surveillance requires further assessment in a larger patient population, which was beyond the scope of the current study.

Preclinical models

PSB202 exhibited dose-dependent cytotoxicity across four types of human lymphoma cells, with a stronger effect on Ramos cells (refractory to anti-CD20 antibodies) compared to the control group (Extended Data Fig. 2A). Besides, PSB202 showed a dose-dependent ADCC (Extended Data Fig. 2B). Both PSB202 and its components (an-CD20 PSB102 or anti-CD37 PSB107) demonstrated CDC (Extended Data Fig. 2B), with PSB202 exhibiting increased CDC activity compared to any individual component, suggesting possible combinatorial effects. PSB202 significantly inhibited the growth of the Raji cell-xenograft (CDX) model and showed a comparable antitumor activity to Rituximab (Extended Data Fig. 2C). Besides, PSB202 combined with lenalidomide exhibited a synergistic effect against the Raji CDX model (Extended Data Fig. 2C).

scRNA-seq reveals immune remodeling in responders

The scRNA-seq of 6 tumor samples from 3 patients in the 300 mg cohort captured 634,914 high-quality cells (Fig. 3A), with cell populations annotated based on canonical marker genes [29, 30] (Extended Data Fig. 3A). Given the limited sample size (two responders and one non-responder), the following analyses should be considered exploratory and descriptive. Analyses of baseline samples revealed that responders had a lower B‑cell proportion but a higher T‑cell proportion compared to the non‑responder. Furthermore, in responders, the post‑treatment samples showed a significant decrease in B‑cell proportion and a concomitant increase in T‑cell proportion relative to their own baseline (Fig. 3B). Responders showed increased intercellular communication compared to non-responders, and the treatment further enhanced it (Fig. 3C). The non-responder sample exhibited relatively stronger immunosuppressive signaling patterns (e.g., CD23, BTLA, FLT3), whereas immunostimulatory signals (e.g., CD86, CXCL, MHC-II) appeared more prominent in responders (Fig. 3D). Compared with pre-treatment samples, analysis in the post-treatment samples showed a transition from immunosuppressive (GALECTIN/BAFF/CD137) to immunostimulatory (FASLG/LIGHT/CD48) signaling profiles (Fig. 3E).

Within the tumors of these patients, B cells represent the predominant cellular source of CD37, owing to both their relative abundance and their elevated expression levels (Fig. 4A). We also observed that B-cell CD37 expression was higher in responders than in the non-responder at baseline, and decreased after treatment (Fig. 4B). However, given the limited number of patients, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive observations. Regarding B‑cell subclustering, we observed pronounced inter‑individual heterogeneity in the B‑cell subset composition across patients (Extended Data Fig. 3B-C). The higher baseline B-cell CNV scores from responders likely reflect the presence of aberrant or tumor-associated B-cell clones, with their subsequent marked reduction following PSB202 treatment possibly indicating effective depletion of these genomically unstable malignant clones, consistent with the encouraging clinical responses observed (Fig. 4C). GO enrichment analysis of B cell differential expressed genes (Supplementary Material) revealed baseline enrichment of antigen signaling and T-cell regulation pathways in responders, whereas metabolic and translational programs were more prominent in the non-responder; these patterns may suggest that response to PSB202 was predicated on a pre-existing immune-active state. PSB202 treatment further induced pathways associated with immune activation and leukocyte interaction in responders, consistent with a decrease of activation in B cell (Fig. 4D).

Given the marked changes in T-cell abundance, we proceeded to subcluster the Tcell population (Fig. 5A, Extended Data Fig. 3D). The responders had a markedly more active T-cell compartment than the non-responders, characterized by higher CD4+ and CD8+ proportions (Fig. 5B), enrichment of multiple T-cell subsets (Fig. 5C), greater cytotoxic (Fig. 5D) and exhaustion scores (Fig. 5E) of CD8+ T-cells, and augmented TCR clonal diversity (Fig. 5F). PSB202 treatment also expanded CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells, anti-tumor subset, and TCR clonal diversity, suggesting a robust T-cell response (Fig. 5B-F). However, these observations are descriptive and should be interpreted cautiously.

Collectively, these exploratory analyses may suggest that patients responsive to PSB202 exhibit higher CD37 expression levels on tumor B cells, coupled with increased T-cell infiltration, enhanced T-cell cytotoxic potential, and a more immunostimulatory TME. However, due to the limited number of patients included, these findingsrequire further validation in adequately powered studies.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first-in-human study of a first-in-class bifunctional product comprising two engineered monoclonal antibodies targeting CD20 and CD37. This phase Ia study indicated a manageable safety profile, along with preliminary signals of antitumor activity (ORR, 30.0%). Given the limited sample size and exploratory nature of this study, these findings should be interpreted with caution and warrant further investigation in larger studies. However, PSB202 may represent a potential off-the-shelf therapeutic approach for patients with R/R B-NHL.

DLT occurred in one patient (grade 4 neutropenia with fever) at 100 mg. PSB202 was well-tolerated across all dose levels up to 300 mg, with MTD not reached. No new or unexpected safety signals were identified. The most common grade ≥ 3 TEAEs were leukopenia and neutropenia (both 60%), which were consistent with selective B-cell depletion rather than myelosuppression of progenitors. Notably, CRS or neurotoxicity, adverse effects frequently associated with CD3-engaging bsAbs, did not occur. Hematologic toxicities, particularly leukopenia, neutropenia, and lymphopenia, were reported frequently independent of escalated dose, as expected in the R/R B-NHL settings [31–33]. Importantly, most events were manageable via dose modifications and growth factor support, with no treatment-related deaths. The rapid kinetics of cytopenias, where hematologic events mostly occurred within the first three treatment cycles, may be attributed to a direct effect of PSB202 on mature peripheral blood cells rather than hematopoietic progenitors. Besides, the high incidence of grade 3/4 infections was probably associated with the increased hematologic toxicity, as well as preexisting immunosuppression from the underlying malignancy and exposure to prior cytotoxic therapies, which were typically reversible without clinical sequelae. The occurrence of IRRs underscored the need for IRR management strategies, including optimized premedication, infusion rates, and monitoring. An extended follow-up and larger cohort studies will provide further characterization of its long-term safety.

Responses (ORR 30.0%) were observed in the absence of CD3 engagement, which may be associated with direct B-cell depletion and immune modulation through ADCC and CDC. Exploratory scRNA-seq revealed that responders tended to exhibit higher baseline CD37 expression, likely suggesting a potential association between target expression and treatment response. Besides, higher baseline CNV scores were seen in responders, suggesting that genomic instability, while a hallmark of malignancy, may confer therapeutic vulnerability. Such unstable clones may be dependent on survival pathways targeted by PSB202; however, such hypothesis requires further validation. Despite preliminary antitumor activity, the small sample size and heterogeneous patient population preclude us from drawing any definitive conclusions on the benefit of PSB202 in heavily-pretreated R/R B-NHL.

PSB202 exposure increased with dose, showing Cmax peaks between 4 to 14.5 h and a half-life of 53 to 154 h. At 300 mg, steady-state concentrations exceeded 90% of the effective threshold for B-cell depletion. The high inter-patient variability in Cmax likely reflected heterogeneous tumor burden and CD37 expression, suggesting target-mediated drug disposition. Pharmacodynamic analyses showed long-term depletion of circulating CD19⁺ cells and stable immunoglobulin levels (IgG, IgA, IgM), indicating preserved immune surveillance. Furthermore, CD37 expression was heterogeneous across the patient population, and a subset of patients lacked detectable CD37 expression, indicating potential target-negative resistance mechanisms. The pharmacologic feasibility of sustained target engagement without immune suppression may suggest a favorable property for chronic administration; however, the clinical implications of these observations require further investigation.

In vitro and in vivo analyses demonstrated dose-dependent cytotoxicity across four human lymphoma cell lines. PSB202 showed enhanced ADCC and CDC activity compared with rituximab or its monospecific components (anti-CD20 or anti-CD37). Dual epitope binding may promote Fc clustering and complement activation, potentiating effector cell–mediated cytotoxicity. In xenograft models, PSB202 suggested tumor growth inhibition comparable to rituximab and showed combinatorial effects when combined with lenalidomide. Overall, these findings may suggest functional cooperativity between CD20 and CD37 blockade and support the further investigation of PSB202 as a novel therapeutic entity.

Consistent with prior studies in R/R indolent B-NHL [31–33], all patients enrolled in this study had received multiple prior lines of therapy, reflecting a heavily-pretreated and refractory population representative of real-world clinical settings. Although affected by patient population heterogeneity in treatment sensitivity, the observed preliminary efficacy signal (ORR 30.0%) was generally consistent with the observed in vitro cytotoxicity Ramos cells refractory to anti-CD20 antibodies and in vivo antitumor effects in the Raji CDX model and may at least be associated with the dual-target design of PSB202, enabling concurrent targeting of CD20 and CD37. Preclinical data showed enhanced CDC activity of PSB202 compared with its individual components, suggesting potential functional cooperativity. In addition, ADCC may also contribute to the observed activity. Exploratory translational analyses further suggested that PSB202 treatment could induce enhanced T-cell infiltration and cytotoxicity in responders, accompanied by significant IFN-γ release, as well as modulation of B-cell phenotypes toward a more anti-tumor state. These changes may facilitate interactions among B cells, T cells, and NK cells, potentially contributing to a shift from an immunosuppressive to a more immunostimulatory tumor microenvironment. However, these findings remain hypothesis-generating and require further validation. Under such immune surveillance, responders may derive clinical benefit, as immunosuppressive TME has been linked to poor prognosis in indolent B-NHL [34] however, given the limited sample size and exploratory nature of this study, the association with long-term outcomes should be interpreted with caution. Notably, contextualizing the resistance to anti-CD20 antibodies in R/R settings due to CD20 loss [35], PSB202 may serve as a promising backbone for combination strategies with other novel therapies, given that CD19⁺ B-cell depletion by PSB202 across different lymphoma subtypes was largely mediated by CD37, whose expression remains relatively stable before and after treatment. However, given that all enrolled patients in this study were CD20-positive, the theoretical advantage of PSB202 in settings where CD20 downregulation occurs should be confirmed in future studies.

Single-cell transcriptomic profiling of six tumor samples (three patients from the 300 mg cohort) provided exploratory insights into potential immune changes associated with treatment response. Compared with non-responder, responders showed decreased malignant B-cell fraction, increased CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, expanded TCR clonal diversity. Cell–cell interaction analysis revealed a shift from immunosuppressive (BTLA, FLT3) to immunostimulatory (FASLG, LIGHT, CD48) signaling networks post-treatment. Collectively, these findings may suggest that PSB202 treatment may be associated with modulation of the tumor microenvironment, CD37 expression, and T-cell activation. However, these observations are exploratory and require further validation in larger cohorts to assess their potential as hallmarks of therapeutic response.

This study was limited by the single-arm design, limited sample size, exploratory nature of correlative analysis, and heterogeneous patient population, aligning with previous phase Ia studies. These limitations make our study more concept-generating than practice-defining. More future efforts on efficacy asseessments and biomarker analyses in larger homogeneous cohorts are warranted. Future studies with integrated immunophenotypic and longitudinal clinical outcome data are warranted to clarify the potential association between peripheral CD19⁺ cell dynamics and treatment response. Though MTD was not being reached, several factors contributed to the decision to terminate dose escalation at 300 mg. In vitro studies demonstrated a mean plasma concentration of 24,870 ng/mL under repeated administration at 300 mg, which was sufficient to sustain > 90% receptor occupancy for CD20 and CD37 and 95% cytotoxic activity across all tested cell lines. Consistently, three patients treated at the 300 mg dose level in this trial exhibited complete B-cell depletion and marked increases in serum IFN-γ, suggesting that PSB202 at 300 mg had achieved the desired pharmacodynamic effects. This pattern was in line with the CD3-independent mechanism of action of PSB202 and likely reflected early immune activation followed by pharmacodynamic saturation after rapid B-cell depletion, rather than cumulative immune amplification or immune exhaustion. However, grade ≥ 3 toxicities, rash, and fever were also observed at a dose of 300 mg. Given the minimal likelihood of additional efficacy gains and increased toxicity at higher doses, the SRC decided to halt further dose escalation at 300 mg.

In conclusion, single-agent PSB202 demonstrated a manageable safety profile and preliminary efficacy signals, possibly suggesting its treatment potential in R/R B-NHL.
