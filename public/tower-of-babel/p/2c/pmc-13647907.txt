Spatiotemporal brain-state dynamics delineate executive function subtypes in school-aged autism: evidence from co-activation patterns and a four-year follow-up
Authors: Zenghe Yue, Jinyi Zhu, Yuxuan Wang, Luyang Guan, Shengjian Yin, Hui Fang, Xiaoyan Ke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAutism spectrum disorder (ASD) is characterized by profound clinical and biological heterogeneity. The neurodynamic profiles associated with divergent developmental trajectories of executive function (EF) during the critical transition from late childhood to early adolescence remain poorly understood. This study aimed to determine whether heterogeneity in EF development is associated with distinct neurodynamic profiles.MethodsIn a longitudinal study, 68 children with ASD (aged 6–9 years) and 50 age-matched typically developing (TD) controls underwent baseline resting-state fMRI. The ASD group was followed for approximately 4 years. EF was assessed using the Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function (BRIEF), alongside follow-up depression, anxiety, and sleep outcomes. Longitudinal EF trajectory clusters were identified to characterize developmental heterogeneity. In parallel, baseline neurofunctional subtypes were derived from ALFF using a normative-deviation framework, with fALFF used for sensitivity analysis. CAP analysis was then applied to examine brain-state dynamics across these complementary stratification approaches.ResultsEF declined longitudinally in the ASD group, particularly in behavioral regulation domains, and these changes were associated with depressive symptoms and sleep problems at follow-up. Three longitudinal EF trajectory clusters were identified, but baseline CAP dynamics showed minimal differences across these groups. In contrast, ALFF-derived neurofunctional subtypes exhibited distinct CAP dynamic profiles, with Subtype 1 showing greater engagement of visual-related states and Subtype 2 exhibiting enhanced transitions among DMN/FPN-related control states. fALFF-based analyses yielded similar subtype assignments, supporting the robustness of the neurofunctional stratification. Critically, similar EF deterioration was associated with distinct neurodynamic profiles, as reflected by subtype-specific state-transition patterns that showed opposite associations with EF changes.LimitationsFirst, the TD group was not followed longitudinally, limiting precise quantification of deviation from normative developmental pathways. Second, the sample size and attrition during follow-up may affect the stability of subtype assignment. Finally, as inferences were based on resting-state fMRI, task-based or ecologically valid measures were not available to validate functional interpretations.ConclusionThese findings provide evidence for neurodynamic heterogeneity in ASD, suggesting that similar clinical outcomes may be associated with divergent brain-state profiles. This work supports the move toward neuro-subtype–informed precision stratification and targeted intervention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13229-026-00728-x.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a complex neurodevelopmental condition characterized by core deficits in social communication and the presence of restricted, repetitive behaviors or interests [1]. Notably, ASD exhibits substantial heterogeneity across multiple levels—including genetic architecture, neurodevelopmental pathways, and behavioral phenotypes—which poses major challenges to precision diagnosis and personalized intervention strategies [2–4]. Converging evidence further indicates that individuals with ASD commonly exhibit executive function (EF) impairments, encompassing higher-order cognitive domains such as cognitive flexibility and emotional control, and these difficulties often persist across developmental stages [5, 6]. Importantly, EF deficits in ASD have been associated with more severe emotional and behavioral problems (e.g., anxiety and depression) and may be further exacerbated by frequent comorbidities such as sleep disturbances [5, 7]. Despite these observations, how EF changes longitudinally with age in ASD—particularly during the critical window from late childhood to early adolescence—remains insufficiently understood. Existing reviews and meta-analyses suggest that EF impairments in autistic children are relatively persistent; however, whether the developmental trajectories of EF differ from those in typically developing individuals remains inconsistent across studies [8]. This inconsistency highlights the need for high-quality longitudinal evidence to characterize EF developmental trajectories and their potential neurobiological correlates in ASD.

In neuroimaging research, the amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF) has been widely used as a key index of intrinsic neural activity in children with ASD. Prior studies have reported atypical ALFF patterns in ASD, such as reduced ALFF in regions including the anterior cingulate cortex, parietal cortex, and prefrontal areas, which may reflect altered baseline neural activity underlying higher-order social and cognitive functions [9, 10]. Beyond ASD, ALFF-based markers have also been leveraged to identify clinically meaningful subtypes in major depressive disorder and bipolar disorder, with potential implications for precision treatment [11, 12]. However, such ALFF-informed subtyping approaches remain largely unexplored in neurodevelopmental conditions such as ASD. Moreover, as ALFF is typically regarded as a regional marker of spontaneous brain activity, alterations observed at the large-scale network level may reflect broader neurobiological organization beyond focal regional abnormalities. These observations motivate further investigation of ASD subtypes from a broader network-level perspective, aiming to delineate the neurophysiological architecture underlying ALFF-defined heterogeneity.

Large-scale brain networks reflect the intrinsic organizational architecture of brain connectivity. Recent evidence suggests that spontaneous brain activity exhibits temporally coordinated patterns, which may arise from underlying neuronal population events [13–15]. Accumulating findings have also indicated that large-scale networks supporting executive function are substantially altered in ASD. Specifically, converging evidence from both task-based and resting-state fMRI studies has demonstrated atypical recruitment and connectivity within executive control–related circuits in ASD, particularly involving the frontoparietal network (FPN), cingulo-opercular networks, and their interactions with other salience-related systems such as the default mode network (DMN) [16–18]. These observations underscore that executive dysfunction in ASD is unlikely to reflect isolated regional activation abnormalities; rather, it may arise from widespread and system-level dysregulation in dynamic interactions and coordination across large-scale networks. Therefore, examining spontaneous activity within a large-scale network framework may provide deeper insights into the pathophysiology of ASD and its neurobiologically distinct subtypes. However, most existing work has relied on static metrics or task-evoked measures, with limited integration of longitudinal clinical changes and time-varying network dynamics. This gap constrains a comprehensive understanding of EF developmental trajectories and their associated neural profiles.

Compared with other dynamic functional connectivity approaches such as sliding-window methods, co-activation patterns (CAPs) enable framewise characterization of large-scale network interactions and transient brain-state dynamics [19, 20]. Recently, a two-layer model of brain function has been proposed, suggesting that fluctuations in subcortical–cortical projections and body–brain coupling may first manifest as changes in CAP activity, which in turn shape global signal fluctuations [21]. These findings provide a potential window for non-invasively probing large-scale neuronal population events using fMRI. To date, CAP-based measures have been increasingly considered as promising neuroimaging markers for neurodevelopmental conditions such as ASD. For example, individuals with ASD have been reported to spend more time in CAP states associated with the ventral attention network (VAN), but less time in CAP states related to the frontoparietal network (FPN) and dorsal attention network (DAN) [22]. Moreover, CAP differences between ASD and typically developing individuals appear to be developmentally modulated: adolescents with ASD tend to dwell longer in VAN-related states, whereas adults with ASD show greater occupancy of FPN-related states [23]. Building on these observations, CAP analysis may provide a useful framework for characterizing ASD-related neurodynamic alterations, particularly when integrated with static ALFF measures to refine neurobiological interpretations of EF-related subtypes in ASD [24].

The pronounced heterogeneity of ASD has long been recognized as a major barrier to precision diagnosis and individualized intervention. Emerging evidence increasingly suggests that such heterogeneity is not merely random noise, but may instead reflect systematic differences in underlying neurobiological mechanisms across multiple levels. For instance, leveraging large-scale ASD cohorts integrating genetic and phenotypic data, Litman and colleagues identified four robust clinical subtypes and further indicated that their biological foundations are tightly linked to developmental stage, providing substantive support for advancing precision medicine in ASD [25]. However, as individuals with ASD transition into late childhood and early adolescence, the roles of neurofunctional regulation and person–environment interactions become increasingly prominent in shaping cognitive and behavioral outcomes. In this developmental window, genetic liability or static phenotypic descriptions alone are unlikely to fully account for the substantial individual variability in EF trajectories. Critically, the key question is not simply whether ASD is associated with brain abnormalities, but whether distinct individuals show different neurodynamic profiles that are associated with divergent longitudinal outcomes.

Therefore, we adopted a longitudinal follow-up design to characterize EF developmental heterogeneity in school-aged children with ASD and to examine its relationship with baseline resting-state brain dynamics. Conceptually, we treated longitudinal EF trajectory clusters and baseline neurofunctional subtypes as complementary levels of heterogeneity rather than as competing classification schemes. The EF trajectory clusters were used to capture clinically meaningful developmental variability, whereas ALFF-derived normative-deviation subtypes were used to identify latent baseline neurofunctional variation that may not be fully observable from conventional clinical measures. CAP analysis was then applied to test whether these stratification approaches were associated with distinct patterns of transient brain-state engagement and switching. This framework allowed us to examine whether heterogeneous EF outcomes in ASD are associated with separable clinical trajectories and baseline neurodynamic profiles.

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited from the specialized autism cohort at the Brain Hospital affiliated to Nanjing Medical University. A longitudinal follow-up design was adopted. At baseline, 68 children with ASD (aged 6–9 years) were enrolled from the outpatient clinic of the Department of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry, where they had received a clinical diagnosis of ASD. Age-matched typically developing (TD) children were recruited from surrounding communities. Baseline demographic information and clinical characteristics are presented in Supplementary Table 1. The ASD cohort was subsequently followed up longitudinally. Written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of all participants. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Brain Hospital affiliated to Nanjing Medical University (Approval No. 2017-KY098-01).

All ASD participants met the diagnostic criteria for autism spectrum disorder as defined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5). Diagnoses were independently confirmed by two senior child and adolescent psychiatrists (associate chief physician level or above). Exclusion criteria were: (1) major physical illnesses (e.g., diabetes, hypertension, or infectious diseases); (2) neurological disorders (e.g., head injury, cerebrovascular disease, or brain tumors); (3) other neurodevelopmental disorders (e.g., attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder or tic disorders); (4) intellectual disability (IQ < 70); and (5) contraindications to MRI (e.g., severe claustrophobia or metallic implants). Throughout the follow-up period, none of the ASD participants reported a history of medication use specifically targeting core ASD symptoms.

Core autism symptoms were assessed using the Autism Diagnostic Interview–Revised (ADI-R) based on parent interviews [26]. EF was evaluated using the parent-rated Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function (BRIEF) [27]. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Depression Self-Rating Scale for Children (DSRSC) [28], and anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Screen for Child Anxiety Related Emotional Disorders (SCARED) [29]. Sleep problems were evaluated using the Children’s Sleep Habits Questionnaire (CSHQ) [30].

MRI data acquisition and preprocessing

MRI data were acquired at baseline, with all ASD and TD participants undergoing a single scanning session. Imaging was performed on a Siemens 3.0-T MAGNETOM Verio MRI scanner equipped with a standard 32-channel head coil. Resting-state functional images were collected using a gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 2000 ms, echo time (TE) = 30 ms, flip angle = 90°, matrix size = 64 × 64, field of view (FOV) = 220 × 220 mm², number of slices = 30, slice thickness = 3.5 mm, total volumes = 140, resulting in a total acquisition time of 280 s.

Functional images were preprocessed using Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 (SPM12; http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm) and the Data Processing Assistant for Resting-State fMRI (DPARSF; http://www.restfmri.net/forum/DPARSF) [31]. The pipeline comprised removal of the first five volumes, slice-timing correction, six-parameter rigid-body head-motion realignment, spatial normalization to the EPI template in MNI space using nonlinear transformations, resampling to 3 × 3 × 3 mm³ isotropic voxels, and spatial smoothing with a 6-mm FWHM Gaussian kernel. Because dynamic functional connectivity and CAP analyses are highly sensitive to head motion and physiological noise, we adopted an expanded, benchmark-supported confound-regression strategy: the Friston-24 head-motion parameters [32, 33], the mean white-matter and cerebrospinal-fluid signals, and the global mean signal (GSR) were regressed from the time-series. Nuisance regression, linear detrending, and temporal band-pass filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz) were performed in a single step to avoid frequency-dependent reintroduction of nuisance variance [34]. This 24-parameter + WM + CSF + GSR configuration is among the most effective strategies for suppressing motion- and physiology-related artifact in resting-state benchmarking studies [35–39]. For motion-based quality control, participants with a mean framewise displacement (FD) > 0.3 mm were excluded; to preserve the continuous temporal structure required for CAP frame assignment, no volume-wise scrubbing was applied, and mean FD was instead included as a nuisance covariate in all group-level analyses. Of the 78 ASD participants and 56 TD controls initially recruited, 10 ASD and 6 TD participants were excluded due to excessive head motion, yielding final samples of 68 ASD participants and 50 TD controls.

Co-activation pattern (CAP) analysis

CAP analyses were conducted using the open-source NeuroCAPs toolbox (https://github.com/donishadsmith/neurocaps) [45]. First, resting-state fMRI time series were extracted for all 118 participants at baseline using the Schaefer 400 parcellation, yielding a two-dimensional matrix of T time points × N ROIs for each participant. The time series of each ROI were then z-scored to standardize signal amplitude across regions. Notably, all time points were retained for spatial similarity-based clustering. This strategy avoids subjective threshold selection and ensures that low-amplitude fluctuations are also considered when defining brain states.

To construct a common state space applicable across groups, individual z-scored time-series matrices were concatenated along the temporal dimension to form a group-level aggregated matrix. K-means clustering was then applied to this aggregated matrix to identify recurring co-activation patterns. The number of CAP states was evaluated across K = 2–12 using WCSS, Silhouette score, Davies–Bouldin index, and Calinski–Harabasz index. K = 5 was selected because it corresponded to the WCSS elbow and the global minimum of the Davies–Bouldin index, whereas Silhouette and Calinski–Harabasz indices monotonically favored lower-K solutions, a known tendency in high-dimensional clustering [46] (Supplementary Fig. 4). CAP spatial maps were generated by averaging all time points assigned to each cluster and were subsequently z-transformed for visualization. Next, each time point of each participant was assigned to one of the five CAP states according to the highest spatial correlation with the corresponding cluster centroid. To characterize the functional relevance of the identified states, the 400 ROIs were mapped onto the canonical Yeo-7 functional networks, and mean signal intensities within each network were computed for further analyses.

A single common state space was derived from the concatenated time series of all participants so that the temporal dynamic metrics of every participant were defined relative to the same set of reference states, a prerequisite for valid between-group comparison. To verify that this pooled solution did not bias the state definitions toward normative (TD) patterns, we performed a sensitivity analysis in which the five CAP states were re-derived from the ASD sample alone (Supplementary Figs. 5–7).

Temporal dynamic metrics of CAP states

To characterize temporal dynamics in ASD and TD participants, each time point of an individual’s ROI time-series matrix was matched to one of the five identified CAP states based on maximal spatial correlation. Temporal dynamic features were then quantified for each CAP state, including occurrence (occupancy rate), mean persistence (dwell time), and transition probability, as described previously [45, 47]. Occurrence (occupancy rate) reflects the temporal predominance of a given brain state across the scan and was defined as the proportion of frames assigned to that CAP state: (Occupancy = TState / TTotal), where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{T}_{\mathrm{state}}$$\end{document}denotes the number of frames classified into a specific CAP state, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{T}_{\mathrm{total}}$$\end{document}denotes the total number of frames in the time series. Mean persistence (dwell time) reflects the stability of a given state before switching to another and was calculated as the average duration of consecutive frames spent in that CAP state: (Persistence time = Mean (TState × TR)), where TR denotes the fMRI repetition time (2.0 s). Transition probability captures the flexibility of network reconfiguration and was defined as the probability of switching from one CAP state to another between successive time points. Specifically, the transition probability from state A to state B was computed as: Transition probability = NA to B / TState, where NA to B denotes the total number of transitions from state A to state B across the time series.

Statistical analysis

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power 3.1.9.7. Based on the 4-year longitudinal follow-up design, we assumed an effect size of 0.40 (small-to-moderate), a significance level of α = 0.05, statistical power of 80%, and an expected attrition rate of 20%. Under these assumptions, at least 49 ASD participants were required at follow-up, and at least 62 ASD participants needed to be recruited at baseline.

To compare baseline demographic and clinical characteristics between ASD and TD groups, independent-samples t-tests were used to examine group differences in age, IQ, core symptom severity, and primary caregiver education level. For categorical variables, chi-square tests were applied to compare the distribution of sex and primary caregiver occupation. To assess potential attrition bias, baseline demographic, IQ, BRIEF, and clinical characteristics, as well as neurodynamic subtype distribution, were compared between completers and participants lost to follow-up using independent-samples t-tests and chi-square tests. Linear mixed-effects models were used to examine longitudinal changes in EF and its subdomains from baseline to follow-up within the ASD group. To account for potential confounding factors, baseline EF scores, baseline age, sex, and IQ were included as covariates. To investigate the clinical relevance of EF longitudinal dynamics, change scores were calculated for each BRIEF domain (T2 – T1). Partial Spearman correlation analyses were then conducted to assess associations between EF change scores and follow-up clinical outcomes, while controlling for baseline EF scores and demographic covariates. To further characterize heterogeneity in EF development, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on multidimensional EF change scores to identify distinct developmental trajectory patterns. Kruskal–Wallis H tests were used to compare the severity of depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and sleep problems across the three trajectory subgroups. Where a significant main effect was observed, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted. To control the family-wise risk of Type I error due to multiple comparisons, all p values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) procedure (Benjamini–Hochberg).

For neuroimaging analyses of static ALFF maps, two-sample t-tests were conducted to compare each ASD subtype with the TD group. Multiple comparisons were corrected using Gaussian random field (GRF) theory, with a voxel-wise threshold of p < 0.001 and a cluster-level threshold of p < 0.05. To characterize the network-level topographic distribution of ALFF abnormalities, the resulting t-statistical maps were averaged within regions defined by the Yeo-7 network parcellation, yielding subtype-specific network-level summaries.

Group differences across the three groups (Subtype 1, Subtype 2, and TD) in continuous variables (e.g., age, IQ, CAP occupancy rate, CAP persistence time, and CAP transition probabilities) were examined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests. Significant group effects were followed by post hoc pairwise comparisons using FDR. For longitudinal clinical trajectory analyses within the ASD cohort, a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was performed with group (Subtype 1 vs. Subtype 2) as the between-subject factor and time (T1 vs. T2) as the within-subject factor. This model was used to test group × time interaction effects on BRIEF domain scores, thereby capturing subtype-dependent developmental trajectories. Finally, to examine the relationship between neurodynamic measures and longitudinal clinical changes, partial correlation analyses were conducted to examine associations between changes in CAP metrics and changes in follow-up clinical scores while controlling for baseline scores. Spearman’s correlations were applied when variables deviated from normality; otherwise, Pearson’s correlations were used, and consistency across correlation methods was reported. Because head motion can influence both ALFF and dynamic measures, mean FD was compared between the ASD and TD groups (Mann–Whitney U test) and entered as a continuous nuisance covariate in all group-level analyses of CAP metrics (occurrence, persistence, and transition probabilities). Group-level static ALFF comparisons across Subtype 1, Subtype 2, and TD were also re-examined at the Yeo-7 network level using one-way ANCOVA, with mean FD entered as a continuous nuisance covariate. Group effects were FDR-corrected across the seven networks. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with the significance level set at p < 0.05. All brain–behavior correlation analyses were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure, and all reported p values are FDR-adjusted.

In response to the conceptual question of whether longitudinal EF trajectory groups themselves captured baseline neurodynamic heterogeneity, we conducted a supplementary analysis comparing baseline CAP dynamics across the three EF trajectory clusters and TD controls. CAP fractional occupancy, persistence time, and transition probabilities were compared using one-way analysis of variance when distributional assumptions were met; otherwise, non-parametric tests were used. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. This supplementary analysis was used to determine whether clinically defined EF trajectory groups showed distinct baseline CAP dynamic profiles.

Spatial similarity and stepwise regression analyses

To examine the spatial correspondence between dynamic brain states and static neurophysiological subtypes, we first extracted mean signal intensities from the five CAP state maps and the two subtype-specific ALFF statistical maps (Subtype vs. TD) using the Schaefer-400 parcellation. Spatial similarity between each CAP state and each ALFF map was then quantified by computing Pearson correlation coefficients across the 400 ROI-wise values.

In addition, to further investigate the relationship between CAP states and ALFF statistical maps, we implemented a stepwise linear regression model to reconstruct each ALFF statistical map using CAP states as predictors. Specifically, ROI-wise signals from the five CAP states (400 values per state) were treated as candidate independent variables, whereas ROI-wise values from the subtype-specific ALFF statistical map (Subtype vs. TD; 400 values) served as the dependent variable. A bidirectional stepwise selection procedure (forward inclusion and backward elimination) was applied, iteratively adding or removing CAP predictors according to statistical criteria. This approach optimized model fit while reducing overfitting, enabling identification of the dynamic states that contributed most strongly to explaining subtype-specific static ALFF patterns. Ultimately, the ALFF statistical map for each subtype could be approximated as a weighted linear combination of the most relevant CAP states.

Results

Four-year clinical follow-up

As shown in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 2, the mean follow-up duration was 3.77 ± 1.54 years. Thirteen participants were lost to follow-up, yielding an attrition rate of 19.1%. A baseline comparison between completers and participants lost to follow-up revealed no systematic attrition bias (Supplementary Table 3). After controlling for baseline scores, age, sex, and IQ, linear mixed-effects models demonstrated a significant overall decline in executive functioning (β = 3.57, p = 0.018). Notably, significant deterioration was observed in behavioral regulation–related subdomains, including Inhibit, Shift, and Emotional Control (all p < 0.001), whereas metacognition-related subdomains did not show statistically significant changes over time (all p > 0.05). This pattern indicates a clear dissociation, with longitudinal EF decline being more pronounced in behavioral regulation than in metacognitive components.

Five recurring CAP states identified by clustering analysis

To identify fundamental co-activation states, we performed k-means clustering on all frames from the baseline resting-state data of 118 participants (68 ASD and 50 TD), without imposing any a priori temporal, anatomical, or intensity-based constraints. The optimal number of clusters was selected as K = 5 based on the elbow criterion and further supported by three quantitative validation metrics calculated across K = 2–12, including the Silhouette score, Davies–Bouldin index, and Calinski–Harabasz index. The within-cluster sum of squares showed a clear reduction in the rate of improvement at K = 5. In addition, the Davies–Bouldin index, which is less affected by the tendency of magnitude-based indices to favor overly simple low-K solutions in high-dimensional spaces [46] — reached its lowest value at K = 5 (Supplementary Fig. 4). As shown in Fig. 3A, four CAP states formed two pairs of spatially opposing configurations: CAP1 vs. CAP3 and CAP2 vs. CAP4. Specifically, CAP1 was characterized by activation in the SMN and VAN accompanied by deactivation in the DMN and FPN, whereas CAP3 exhibited the inverse pattern. CAP2 showed activation in the VIS with deactivation in the FPN, whereas CAP4 displayed the opposite configuration. Network-level mean signal intensities of the five CAP states, quantified using the Yeo-7 parcellation, are presented in Fig. 3B. Notably, CAP5 was primarily characterized by activation in the DAN. Together, these CAP states captured distinct instantaneous interaction patterns among large-scale networks and recurrently reappeared and transitioned throughout the resting-state scan.

CAP states derived from the ASD sample alone were nearly identical to those from the pooled solution (all five states: cosine similarity ≥ 0.99; mean r = 0.99; Supplementary Figs. 5–7), confirming that the identified states are robust and not driven by pooling with TD participants.

Baseline ALFF-derived neurofunctional subtypes and fALFF sensitivity analysis

Based on ALFF patterns at baseline, the 68 children with ASD were stratified into two subtypes relative to the 50 TD children: Subtype 1 (n = 32) and Subtype 2 (n = 36) (Supplementary Table 5). The two subtypes did not differ significantly in core ASD symptom severity (all p > 0.05). Subtype-specific ALFF alterations relative to TD were identified using two-sample t-tests with GRF correction (voxel-wise p < 0.001; cluster-level p < 0.05), revealing distinct spatial patterns across the two subtypes (Fig. 4A, B).

In Subtype 1, ALFF was significantly increased in the occipital cortex (VIS) and precentral gyrus (SMN), but decreased in the orbitofrontal cortex (LIM), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (FPN), and middle temporal gyrus (DMN), compared with TD. In Subtype 2, ALFF was significantly decreased in the occipital cortex (VIS) and precentral gyrus (SMN), whereas increased ALFF was observed in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (FPN), relative to TD. All overall network-level ALFF group effects that were significant in the original analysis remained significant after including mean FD as a covariate, including the Visual, Somatomotor, Limbic, Frontoparietal, and Default networks (all p_FDR < 0.001). The Dorsal Attention and Ventral Attention networks remained non-significant. Mean FD was not a significant covariate for the Frontoparietal or Default networks, and although it was significantly associated with limbic ALFF, the limbic group effect remained significant after FD adjustment (Supplementary Table 6).

Notably, subtype-specific ALFF t-statistical maps also showed significant spatial correspondence with CAP state maps (Fig. 4C). In Subtype 1, the ALFF t-map was negatively correlated with CAP3 (r = − 0.53) and strongly positively correlated with CAP4 (r = 0.60). Conversely, in Subtype 2, the ALFF t-map was positively correlated with CAP3 (r = 0.59) and negatively correlated with CAP4 (r = − 0.51). All correlations were statistically significant (all p < 0.001). To further examine the relationship between CAP states and static ALFF deviations, we employed a stepwise linear regression model to reconstruct the ALFF t-maps using CAP state spatial features (Fig. 4D). The CAP-based predicted maps showed strong linear correspondence with the empirical ALFF t-maps (Subtype 1: r = 0.6061, p < 0.001; Subtype 2: r = 0.5945, p < 0.001). These results suggest that static ALFF abnormalities may not arise in isolation, but rather reflect cumulative effects of biased engagement of specific CAP states over time.

Temporal dynamics of CAP states across ASD subtypes

Using similarity-based assignment, all frames from Subtype 1 and Subtype 2 were matched to the five predefined CAP states. CAP temporal dynamics were characterized by three metrics—occupancy rate, persistence time, and transition probability (Fig. 5)—which together describe recurring state patterns from overall prevalence to inter-state relationships. These metrics were computed for each participant and compared across Subtype 1, Subtype 2, and TD groups using ANOVA with post hoc tests. To rule out a motion confound, all group-level CAP analyses were repeated with mean FD as a nuisance covariate. The ASD and TD groups did not differ in mean FD (p = 0.35; Supplementary Fig. 10).

Occupancy rate summarizes the proportion of each CAP state across the entire time series (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Table 5). In the TD group, the five states occurred at comparable rates: CAP1, 17.2 ± 5.1% (mean ± SD); CAP2, 22.3 ± 5.0%; CAP3, 16.4 ± 4.5%; CAP4, 22.7 ± 4.8%; and CAP5, 21.5 ± 4.5%. Subtype 1 showed increased occupancy of CAP4 (25.7 ± 5.2%) relative to TD, whereas no significant occupancy differences were observed between Subtype 2 and TD. We next examined persistence time, reflecting the mean duration that a CAP state was maintained before switching to another state (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Table 5). Consistent with prior reports that CAP states typically persist for approximately 4–8 s before transitioning [47, 48]mean persistence times in the TD group were similar across states: CAP1, 5.35 ± 1.2 s; CAP2, 5.19 ± 1.2 s; CAP3, 5.14 ± 1.0 s; CAP4, 5.01 ± 0.9 s; and CAP5, 5.15 ± 1.0 s. In Subtype 1, persistence was again prolonged for CAP2 (6.10 ± 1.4 s) and CAP4 (6.10 ± 1.4 s) compared with TD. In contrast, persistence times in Subtype 2 did not differ significantly from those in the TD group.

To further characterize within- and between-state dynamics, we quantified transition probabilities, defined as the probability of switching from one state to another between adjacent frames (Fig. 5C). In line with previous studies, all five CAP states exhibited strong self-maintenance tendencies, with self-transition probabilities typically ranging from ~ 58% to 70%. In Subtype 1, the self-transition probability of CAP2 (65.8 ± 7.3%) was significantly higher than that in the TD group (59.8 ± 9.6%). Subtype 1 also showed an increased probability of transitions from CAP5 to CAP4 (12.6 ± 6.1%) compared with TD (9.9 ± 4.3%), whereas transitions from CAP2 to CAP3 and from CAP3 to CAP1 were significantly reduced relative to TD (Fig. 5C). By contrast, Subtype 2 exhibited a more pronounced pattern of between-state reconfiguration: the transition probability from CAP3 to CAP5 (16.5 ± 8.1%) was significantly higher than that in TD (12.1 ± 6.8%). Similar to Subtype 1, Subtype 2 also showed an increased CAP5-to-CAP4 transition probability (13.0 ± 6.3%) relative to TD (9.9 ± 4.3%). Together, these findings provide a detailed view of state-to-state switching processes that may account for subtype-specific differences in CAP occupancy and persistence. Overall, Subtype 1 exhibited a prominent “sensory stickiness” pattern characterized by prolonged engagement of the VIS-related CAP2 state, whereas Subtype 2 showed a “control instability” profile characterized by excessive transitions across brain-network states.

Analysis of baseline CAP dynamics across EF trajectory groups

To directly examine whether the longitudinal EF trajectory clusters corresponded to distinct baseline neurodynamic profiles, we compared CAP temporal metrics across the Declining, Stable, Improving, and TD groups. CAP fractional occupancy and persistence time were compared across groups using one-way ANOVA or non-parametric tests, followed by FDR-corrected post hoc pairwise comparisons. This analysis revealed only one significant pairwise difference after correction: CAP2 persistence time differed between the TD and Stable groups (p = 0.032), with the Stable group showing marginally longer CAP2 persistence than TD controls. All other pairwise comparisons were not significant after correction (all p > 0.05). These findings suggest that although the EF trajectory clusters capture clinically meaningful longitudinal outcome heterogeneity, they do not by themselves fully account for baseline CAP dynamic heterogeneity (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Associations between longitudinal EF changes and CAP transition dynamics

To further examine the relationship between CAP transition dynamics and longitudinal changes in EF domains, we first compared baseline and follow-up differences in Inhibit, Shift, Emotional Control, and the BRIEF T score between the two subtypes (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Table 7). Subtype 2 showed significant longitudinal changes across all four domains (all p < 0.05), whereas Subtype 1 exhibited significant changes only in Shift and Emotional Control (both p < 0.05). After accounting for group and time effects, two-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed no significant group × time interactions for any of the four domains (all p > 0.05). Together, these results indicate that although both subtypes exhibited comparable degrees of longitudinal EF decline, Subtype 2 (the “control-instability” subtype) may be associated with a broader range of EF impairments.

Next, given that abnormal CAP2 self-transitions and aberrant CAP3→CAP5 transitions represented subtype-specific features, whereas abnormal CAP5→CAP4 transitions were shared across both subtypes, we tested whether similar clinical trajectories were associated with distinct neurodynamic profiles. To this end, we performed partial correlation analyses between subtype-relevant abnormal CAP transition metrics and EF change scores while controlling for baseline EF levels (Fig. 6B). In Subtype 1, CAP2 self-transition probability was positively correlated with changes in Shift (r = 0.435, p = 0.034), CAP3→CAP5 transition probability was positively correlated with changes in Emotional Control (r = 0.548, p = 0.005), and CAP5→CAP4 transition probability was positively correlated with changes in T score (r = 0.493, p = 0.012). By contrast, in Subtype 2, CAP3→CAP5 transition probability was negatively correlated with changes in Emotional Control (r = − 0.456, p = 0.011), and CAP5→CAP4 transition probability was negatively correlated with changes in T score (r = − 0.379, p = 0.039). These findings suggest that longitudinal EF deterioration may be associated with opposing neurodynamic patterns across ASD subtypes, supporting the notion that similar behavioral outcomes may be linked to distinct underlying brain-state profiles.

Discussion

In this study, we used a co-activation pattern (CAP) framework to characterize instantaneous brain-state dynamics in two data-driven ASD subtypes in school-aged children. By focusing on CAP temporal properties and integrating a four-year longitudinal follow-up of executive function (EF), we provide new insights into large-scale network alterations associated with subtype-specific EF development in ASD, and offer preliminary neurobiological evidence with potential implications for future precision-oriented intervention studies. Specifically, we highlight three major contributions: (1) heterogeneity in EF development represents a key source of heterogeneity in long-term clinical outcomes in ASD; (2) static ALFF and dynamic CAP provide complementary and spatially convergent markers of intrinsic brain dysfunction; and (3) subtype-specific CAP dynamics may be associated with differential patterns of EF impairment.

Heterogeneity in EF trajectories and links to later clinical outcomes

Although individuals with ASD commonly exhibit impairments across multiple executive function (EF) domains that persist across development [49], several longitudinal studies have reported that age-related changes in EF do not always differ significantly between autistic and typically developing (TD) children [5, 50–52], and may even appear to follow “similar trajectory” patterns. One explanation may relate to measurement tools and assessment contexts: highly structured, goal-directed laboratory tasks are more likely to capture an upper bound of performance under external support, whereas real-world EF relies heavily on self-initiated control, contextual integration, and emotion regulation, such that “being able to do” does not necessarily translate into “being able to do consistently.” A second explanation is methodological: conventional longitudinal designs often rely on group-mean comparisons or a single trajectory slope, which can obscure substantial within-ASD heterogeneity and allow distinct developmental pathways to cancel out at the statistical level, thereby yielding non-significant group differences. Consistent with this view, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis highlighted the inconsistency across prior findings and emphasized subgroup heterogeneity and measurement variability as major sources of divergence [8].

Against this background, our trajectory-based stratification further suggests that EF development is not simply a matter of “improvement versus decline,” but may reflect heterogeneous patterns of regulatory functioning that are associated with broader clinical outcomes. In ASD, sleep problems are highly prevalent, and early childhood sleep disturbances have been associated with worsening EF trajectories in school-aged children, supporting the notion that sleep may contribute to shaping the development of executive control [7]. Accordingly, insufficient EF stability may be associated with greater depressive burden, possibly through links with cognitive flexibility, inhibitory regulation, fatigue, and rhythm-related disruptions. In contrast, anxiety may be driven more strongly by mechanisms such as sensory overload and intolerance of uncertainty, and may not couple linearly with EF changes; for example, Normansell-Mossa and colleagues suggested that intolerance of uncertainty may intensify anxiety via heightened sensory sensitivity, implicating a sensory–affective pathway rather than a unitary EF-driven mechanism [53]. Taken together, these lines of evidence indicate that describing EF development in ASD solely using mean-level change or a single slope may systematically underestimate its clinical significance, whereas a trajectory-centered stratification framework may better identify individuals at elevated mental health risk and clarify heterogeneity in long-term outcomes.

The supplementary CAP analysis across EF trajectory groups further refines this interpretation. Although the Declining, Stable, and Improving clusters were clinically meaningful and differed in later depressive and sleep-related outcomes, they showed only limited differentiation in baseline CAP dynamics after correction for multiple comparisons. This suggests that developmental EF trajectories may be shaped by multiple interacting factors, including baseline neural organization, developmental compensation, environmental influences, and later-emerging clinical burden. Therefore, clinical trajectory stratification alone may be insufficient to identify latent baseline neurofunctional profiles, supporting the need for a complementary neuroimaging-based stratification approach.

Complementary clinical and neurofunctional stratification: spatial convergence of ALFF/fALFF and CAP dynamics

The EF trajectory clusters provide a longitudinal clinical classification based on developmental changes in everyday executive functioning. By contrast, the ALFF/fALFF-derived subtypes provide a baseline neurofunctional classification based on deviation from the TD reference distribution. The limited CAP differentiation across EF trajectory groups, together with the more robust CAP differences across ALFF/fALFF-derived subtypes, suggests that neurofunctional heterogeneity may not be fully reducible to longitudinal clinical trajectory labels.

By establishing spatial correlations and linear reconstruction models between CAP states and ALFF maps, our study provides evidence of spatial correspondence between dynamic brain-state configurations and static intrinsic activity deviations. Specifically, the two ASD subtypes exhibited opposing ALFF deviation patterns between higher-order control networks (DMN/FPN) and primary sensory networks (VIS/SMN). This antagonistic topography closely aligned with the spatial configurations of the identified CAP states and was consistent with prior dynamic findings [54]. Such correspondence suggests that subtype-specific ALFF abnormalities may not reflect isolated regional deficits alone, but may be related to biased engagement of instantaneous brain states over time—namely, over-representation or repeated recurrence of specific CAP configurations that could contribute to stable ALFF deviations.

Moreover, CAP states differentiating the two subtypes often formed mirror-opposite patterns (e.g., CAP2 vs. CAP4), and subtype-specific ALFF deviation maps showed significant correlations with these states in opposite directions. This “state-to-trait” relationship further supports the interpretation that static regional activity abnormalities in ASD may represent a long-timescale footprint of aberrant transient network dynamics, rather than independent focal dysfunctions. Similar CAP–ALFF coupling has been reported in both animal and human studies, where transient co-activation events were shown to shape the frequency distribution and power of spontaneous BOLD fluctuations within the 0.01–0.1 Hz band, thereby modulating ALFF intensity [55].

In previous ASD and developmental neuroimaging studies, opposing resting-state activity changes between prefrontal control regions and occipital sensory regions have often been interpreted as markers of symptom severity or developmental delay. However, our observation that this prefrontal–occipital antagonism is embedded within instantaneous brain states suggests that such relative activation patterns may reflect a recurring organizational feature of large-scale networks rather than merely a downstream correlate of ASD. Accordingly, our findings indicate that regional ALFF abnormalities are more likely an outward manifestation of underlying network-level dysfunction—expressed through altered brain-state dynamics and reconfiguration—rather than a primary deficit localized to single regions [56].

Coupling between subtype-specific brain-state dynamics and longitudinal EF changes

In this study, we identified two neurofunctional subtypes in school-aged children with ASD characterized by distinct profiles of instantaneous large-scale network dynamics, suggesting that EF impairment in ASD does not necessarily arise from a single neurobiological pathway. Specifically, Subtype 1 tended to remain excessively in sensory–perceptual configurations, reflecting a relatively rigid organization of brain states, whereas Subtype 2 exhibited more frequent reconfiguration and cross-state switching, indicative of an unstable mode of dynamic regulation. Similar abnormalities in brain-state persistence have been repeatedly reported in ASD dynamic connectivity studies, highlighting that core differences in ASD may lie not only in static connectivity strength, but also in how transient network states are maintained, switched, and integrated across development [54, 57].

An important consideration is that the ASD and TD groups differed significantly in IQ at baseline. Although IQ was included as a covariate in the longitudinal EF models and relevant group-level analyses, the possibility that some of the observed neurodynamic differences partly reflect general cognitive ability cannot be fully excluded. This issue is particularly relevant for alterations involving the FPN and DMN, as frontoparietal systems have been consistently implicated in intelligence, cognitive control, and flexible goal-directed behavior, and developmental studies further suggest that FPN–DMN connectivity varies with intelligence [58, 59]. Therefore, FPN/DMN-related CAP and ALFF differences in the present study should not be interpreted as purely ASD-specific neural signatures. Rather, they may reflect the combined influence of ASD-related atypical network organization and individual differences in general cognitive ability. Future studies with larger samples, more closely IQ-matched control groups, or stratified analyses across cognitive-ability levels will be necessary to clarify the extent to which these neurodynamic profiles are specific to ASD versus shared with broader cognitive-developmental variation.

These two dynamic profiles may correspond to different patterns of intrinsic network organization that are relevant to executive-control difficulties. For individuals showing a “sensory stickiness” pattern, prolonged engagement of sensory-related states may reflect reduced flexibility in shifting from externally oriented processing toward internally guided goal maintenance and rule updating. This interpretation is consistent with network models emphasizing impaired top-down modulation and atypical salience filtering in ASD, in which the ventral attention network (VAN) and its hub regions (e.g., anterior insula) play a key role in information selection and switching between control-relevant networks; dysfunction within this system may render attentional resources more susceptible to being hijacked by non-salient stimuli, thereby reducing control efficiency [60]. In contrast, the “control instability” subtype may reflect less stable engagement of control-related network configurations over time, rather than a simple failure to recruit control networks, resulting in frequent switching accompanied by inefficient regulation. This view aligns with network neuroscience accounts of EF, which argue that effective executive control is not equivalent to stronger activation, but instead depends on frontoparietal–insular control systems acting as adaptive hubs to support cross-network coordination and stable integration; when such hub function is compromised, increased network reconfiguration may resemble “noisy resets” rather than efficient flexibility [18].

Longitudinally, our findings suggest that although both neuro-subtypes may converge on a common pattern of EF deterioration, the associated neurodynamic profiles are not identical, consistent with a form of equifinality whereby similar phenotypes are associated with distinct neurodynamic profiles. Notably, prior ASD studies have reported inconsistent directions of alterations within the DMN, FPN, and VAN—ranging from hyperconnectivity to hypoconnectivity—which may be partly attributable to averaging across unstratified heterogeneity, leading to cancellation of subgroup-specific effects [61, 62]. Thus, interpreting EF trajectories through a neuro-subtype lens may have potential translational implications: future interventions targeting EF may benefit from not assuming a uniform neurobiological target, and from considering strategies aligned with subtype-specific dynamic features—a possibility that should be tested in future stratified intervention studies.

Comparison with traditional classification

Traditional ASD classification has largely relied on behavioral phenotypes, such as stratification by core symptom severity, language and intellectual functioning, comorbidity profiles, or dimensions capturing social communication and restricted/repetitive behaviors. While such approaches are clinically practical for assessment and service allocation, their intrinsic limitation is that they primarily describe similarities and differences at the level of overt phenotype, which may not map onto mechanistic homogeneity. Consequently, when the goal shifts toward explaining inconsistent neuroimaging findings, predicting long-term outcome divergence, or informing precision intervention, symptom-based classification alone often lacks sufficient mechanistic resolution. Lombardo and colleagues have emphasized that ASD heterogeneity spans multiple levels (behavioral, cognitive, neural, and genetic), and that single-axis traditional stratification may obscure cross-level dissociations and parallel developmental pathways, thereby limiting mechanistic understanding and translational progress [63].

In contrast, the neuro-subtype differences identified in the present study suggest that even among individuals with comparable clinical presentations, markedly distinct modes of large-scale network organization and neurodynamic regulation may exist. This aligns with the growing movement toward neurobiologically informed stratification, in which ASD is subdivided based on brain functional or structural features to reduce mechanistic mixing within diagnostic labels. Increasing evidence indicates that ASD includes reproducible neurobiological subtypes that may reflect distinct underlying pathways. For example, Rasero and colleagues identified relatively stable functional connectivity subgroups characterized by hyper- versus hypoconnectivity in a large sample, and further showed that excitatory/inhibitory imbalance–related gene-expression signatures were prominent in only one subtype, suggesting that superficially similar ASD diagnoses may conceal divergent pathophysiological mechanisms [64].

Moreover, recent large-scale genetic studies support the notion that differences in ASD outcomes may reflect traceable pathway-level divergence. Litman and colleagues decomposed phenotypic heterogeneity in ASD and found that distinct clinical outcomes were linked to different genetic and molecular programs, with variant-category–specific developmental time windows aligning with outcome differentiation [25]. Together, these findings indicate that although symptom-based classification remains clinically necessary, it may be insufficient for distinguishing “mechanistically distinct ASDs.” Integrating clinical phenotypes with neuroimaging-based subtyping may therefore provide a more actionable framework for enhancing neurobiological interpretability, improving long-term risk prediction, and generating hypotheses for precision intervention studies.

Precision intervention and clinical translational implications

The neuro-subtype findings of this study may have potential implications for future EF intervention studies in ASD: individuals presenting with comparable EF impairments may show distinct neurodynamic profiles, which could inform hypotheses about different intervention targets and formats. For the subtype characterized by “sensory stickiness,” the observed neurodynamic profile may be consistent with difficulty disengaging from lower-level processing modes, switching rules efficiently, and updating goal representations under changing contexts. Accordingly, interventions that prioritize cognitive flexibility and de-stucking training, while explicitly promoting transfer from structured training to real-world contexts, may represent plausible candidates for future subtype-stratified testing. For example, Kenworthy and colleagues developed the context-embedded EF curriculum Unstuck and On Target, which has demonstrated efficacy in randomized controlled trials by improving flexibility, planning, and everyday EF in autistic children, with meaningful generalization across school and home settings—consistent with the hypothesized switching-related vulnerability of this subtype [65].

In contrast, the subtype characterized by “state instability/control dysregulation” may reflect insufficient stability in control-network engagement, manifested as difficulty sustaining goal-directed processing and suppressing excessive reconfiguration into task-irrelevant states. For this subtype, intervention should not merely aim to “increase switching,” but rather to enhance the persistence and stabilization of control processes. Two mechanistically targeted pathways may be considered. First, structured EF training programs explicitly targeting sustained executive control may be beneficial; for instance, a randomized controlled trial by Faja and colleagues showed that computerized EF training combined with metacognitive coaching could improve executive-related neural responses and yield partial generalization benefits, suggesting potential suitability for individuals with impaired control maintenance [66]. Second, neuromodulation strategies targeting key control-network hubs may provide a candidate approach for testing subtype-specific modulation of control-network dynamics. Notably, real-time fMRI neurofeedback targeting the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) has been explored in ASD and has shown the capacity to modulate relevant brain activity and EF-related indices, supporting neurofeedback-based modulation as a promising intervention avenue for the control-instability subtype [67].

Taken together, our findings suggest that the goal of “precision intervention” should not be limited to increasing overall EF scores, but rather to mapping subtype-specific control-failure components (switching constraints vs. maintenance instability) onto actionable intervention targets and outcome metrics. This framework provides a clear direction for future neuro-subtype–informed stratified trials, which may reduce the heterogeneity of treatment response commonly observed in ASD interventions and improve clinical reproducibility.

Limitation

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the TD group was not followed longitudinally; thus, EF trajectories and neurodynamic changes could not be directly compared between ASD and typical development on the same timescale, limiting precise quantification of deviation from normative developmental pathways. Second, although we identified subtype-specific brain-state dynamics and their associations with longitudinal EF changes, these findings remain observational and cannot establish a causal chain. Resting-state fMRI captures intrinsic patterns of spontaneous brain activity, but it does not directly measure executive-control processes. Therefore, labels such as “sensory stickiness” and “control instability” should be understood as descriptive summaries of CAP dynamic profiles rather than direct evidence of impaired cognitive operations. Future interventional studies (e.g., neuromodulation, neurofeedback, or mechanism-guided EF training) are needed to test subtype-specific plasticity and treatability and to further validate a “subtype–target matching” precision-intervention framework. Third, the relationship between longitudinal EF trajectory clusters and baseline neurofunctional subtypes could not be fully resolved in the present sample. Although we performed a supplementary analysis comparing baseline CAP dynamics across the Declining, Stable, and Improving EF trajectory groups, only limited CAP differences survived correction for multiple comparisons. This may reflect limited statistical power, the multifactorial nature of EF development, or a partial dissociation between clinical trajectory heterogeneity and baseline neurofunctional heterogeneity. Future studies with larger longitudinal samples should examine how clinical trajectories and neuroimaging-derived subtypes jointly predict later functional outcomes and treatment response.

In addition, although completers and participants lost to follow-up did not differ in baseline executive function, autism symptom severity, or neurodynamic subtype distribution, participants lost to follow-up had somewhat higher baseline Full-scale IQ. This suggests that the retained longitudinal sample may slightly under-represent higher-functioning individuals with ASD. Because IQ was included as a covariate in the longitudinal models, this difference is unlikely to fully account for the observed longitudinal EF associations; nevertheless, residual selection bias related to cognitive ability cannot be completely excluded. Future studies with larger and more cognitively diverse longitudinal samples are needed to confirm the generalizability of these findings.

Conclusion

In summary, leveraging spatiotemporal neurodynamic analyses of resting-state brain networks in school-aged children with ASD together with a four-year follow-up, this study revealed marked heterogeneity in EF development and its potential neural substrates. We identified clinically meaningful longitudinal EF trajectory clusters and, in parallel, baseline ALFF/fALFF-derived neurofunctional subtypes characterized by distinct CAP dynamic profiles. These findings suggest that developmental clinical heterogeneity and baseline neurofunctional heterogeneity represent complementary dimensions of ASD, and that similar EF outcomes may arise from partially distinct neurodynamic profiles. These findings provide a novel integrative framework for understanding within-ASD heterogeneity and offer a translatable basis for neuro-subtype–informed risk stratification and precision intervention strategies.
