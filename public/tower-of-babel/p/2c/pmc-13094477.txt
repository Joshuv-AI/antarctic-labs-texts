Knowledge, readiness to help, and attitudes toward epilepsy: A comparative study among Palestinian university students in health and non-health majors
Authors: Hala Nujedat, Israa Mustafa, Ahmad Igbariah, Ahmad Abuhassan, Taysir Alsadder, Mohammad Jaber, Ramzi Shawahna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Epilepsy stigma persists among youth, impacting safety and social inclusion.•This study compared knowledge, readiness, and attitudes in health vs. non‑health majors.•Students in health majors showed higher knowledge and readiness to help compared to non-health majors.•Students in non‑health majors expressed more favorable attitudes toward people with epilepsy compared to health majors.Keywords: Epilepsy, University students, Knowledge, Readiness to help, Attitudes, Stigma

Introduction

Epilepsy is one of the most common chronic neurological disorders, with an estimated 51.7 million people (95% UI 44.9–58.9) affected worldwide in 2021, corresponding to an age-standardized prevalence of 658 per 100,000 population (569–748), the majority of whom reside in low- and middle-income countries [1]. Epilepsy is characterized by recurrent seizures resulting from sudden, abnormal electrical discharges in the brain, which can manifest as convulsions, loss of consciousness, or subtle behavioral changes [2], [3]. Because seizures often occur unpredictably, people with epilepsy face significant health risks, including injuries, accidents, and social disruption, underscoring the critical need for timely medical care and appropriate first aid during seizure episodes [4], [5]. Simple measures, such as ensuring safety by removing nearby hazards, avoiding harmful practices like inserting objects into the mouth, and seeking urgent medical attention for prolonged seizures, can be lifesaving [6], [7]. Yet, despite advances in treatment and the availability of effective seizure control for most patients, epilepsy continues to be surrounded by stigma. Misconceptions, fear, and lack of knowledge about the condition contribute to discrimination and social exclusion, often leaving people with epilepsy marginalized and misunderstood in their communities [8], [9], [10].

Among young people, including university students, epilepsy represents a particularly challenging condition because it often emerges during adolescence and early adulthood, critical stages for education, social development, and career planning [11], [12]. Students with epilepsy may require urgent assistance during seizures, yet studies consistently show that peers often lack adequate knowledge of seizure first aid and may respond with fear or inappropriate actions [13], [14], [15]. Misconceptions about epilepsy remain widespread in youth populations, contributing to stigma, social distancing, and discriminatory attitudes [16]. Such stigma can negatively affect academic performance, social integration, and mental health, reinforcing the need for supportive environments within universities. Evidence suggests that students in health-related majors generally demonstrate higher levels of knowledge and more positive attitudes toward people with epilepsy compared to their peers in non-health disciplines [17], [18]. However, readiness to help and willingness to engage socially with affected individuals vary considerably, highlighting the importance of targeted educational interventions across all faculties to reduce stigma and foster inclusive attitudes.

In Palestine, several studies have highlighted the persistence of misconceptions and stigma toward epilepsy among students and healthcare professionals. These studies reported that undergraduate nursing students demonstrated moderate knowledge but mixed attitudes toward people with epilepsy, with gaps in understanding seizure first aid and social integration [19]. In pharmacy practice, knowledge of epilepsy pharmacotherapy was found to be limited, underscoring the need for enhanced training and awareness among future healthcare providers [20]. More recently, population-based surveys confirmed that misconceptions remain prevalent, with many respondents, attributing epilepsy to supernatural causes or expressing reluctance to engage socially with affected individuals [8], [9], [21]. These findings emphasize that stigma and inadequate readiness to help are not confined to the general population but extend into academic environments, where differences between health-related and non-health majors may shape attitudes and supportiveness toward peers with epilepsy.

In Palestine, recent investigations have begun to shed light on how university students perceive epilepsy, yet important gaps remain. While these findings underscore the importance of improving knowledge among health-related disciplines, little is known about how students in non-health majors perceive epilepsy or how their attitudes compare with those of their peers in health-related majors. Moreover, the dimension of readiness to help, whether students feel prepared to provide support or intervene during seizure episodes, has received little attention, despite its critical role in fostering inclusive and safe university environments. Addressing these gaps, the present study aimed to explore and compare the knowledge, readiness to help, and attitudes toward epilepsy among Palestinian university students enrolled in health-related and non-health majors, thereby identifying areas where targeted educational interventions may reduce stigma and enhance support for people with epilepsy.

Methods

Study design and settings

This study adopted a cross‑sectional design and was conducted in strict adherence to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist, which is provided as Supplementary Table S1. The research was carried out in universities located in the northern region of the West Bank, Palestine, encompassing diverse faculties to ensure representation of both health‑related and non‑health academic majors. The locations of the universities in the northern region of the West Bank, Palestine are presented in Supplementary Fig. S1, and a detailed list of the health‑related and non‑health academic majors included in this study is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

This setting was chosen to capture the perspectives of students from varied educational backgrounds within a culturally and socially relevant context. Data collection was conducted during the academic year 2025, from April 25 to December 24, 2025, allowing coverage across different academic semesters and ensuring adequate participation from students in varied stages of their university programs.

Study population, inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study targeted undergraduate and postgraduate students enrolled in universities located in the northern region of the West Bank. Eligible participants were those aged 18 years or older, actively enrolled in either health‑related or non‑health academic majors, and willing to provide written informed consent. Students with a self‑reported diagnosis of epilepsy, those who had already graduated, or individuals who had participated in similar studies within the past year were excluded to avoid bias and repeated data inclusion. In addition, students who declined participation or provided incomplete responses were not included. This approach ensured that the study population represented a diverse and academically relevant sample of university students while maintaining methodological rigor.

Sample size and sampling technique

The sample size was determined using an online sample size calculator (Raosoft Inc., Seattle, WA; available at www.raosoft.com). Based on a university student population exceeding 20,000, a 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and an assumed prevalence of 50% to maximize variability, the minimum required sample size was approximately 377 students. To ensure adequate statistical power of 80% for comparisons between health‑related and non‑health majors and to account for potential nonresponse, the target sample was increased, with balanced representation across the two strata. Stratified sampling was employed by academic discipline, and within each stratum, participants were recruited through classroom announcements and online invitations disseminated via student channels. This approach ensured feasibility while maintaining diversity across faculties, years of study, and demographic characteristics.

Data collection tool and procedures

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire developed from previously validated instruments and adapted to the Palestinian context [8], [9], [16], [18], [19], [22]. The questionnaire was divided into four main sections. The first section captured sociodemographic and background information, including age, sex, place of residence, academic discipline, year of study, awareness of epilepsy, prior exposure to seizure episodes, acquaintance with people with epilepsy, and prior training in seizure first aid. The second section assessed knowledge about epilepsy (8 items). Each item was presented as a statement or question with multiple choice options. These items covered the nature of epilepsy, causes, driving eligibility, contagion, treatment, seizure duration, first aid practices, and participation in sports. The third section measured readiness to help (4 items). Students were asked about their willingness to assist a colleague with epilepsy, provide emotional support, report bullying, and share awareness information on social media. Each item had three response options (yes, no, I am not sure), coded to reflect positive, negative, or uncertain readiness. The fourth section evaluated attitudes toward people with epilepsy (10 items). These items explored perceptions of anxiety, work efficiency, fear, pity, employment, marriage, mental functioning, willingness to help, friendship, and beliefs about living a normal life. Responses were rated on a five‑point Likert scale (strongly disagree to strongly agree). Higher scores indicated more favorable attitudes. The study questionnaire is provided as Supplementary Table S3.

The internal consistency of the questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which exceeded 0.70 for all constructs, indicating acceptable reliability. Test-retest reliability was evaluated in a subsample of participants who completed the questionnaire twice within a two‑week interval, yielding a Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) of 0.91, confirming excellent stability of the instrument.

Data analysis

Data were entered, cleaned, and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 21.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables such as age were summarized using means and standard deviations (SD), while categorical variables such as sex, place of residence, academic discipline, and year of study were presented as frequencies (n) and percentages (%).

Composite scores were generated for each of the three main domains: knowledge, readiness to help, and attitudes toward epilepsy. Knowledge items were scored as correct (1 point each) or incorrect/I don’t know (0 point each), and the total score was expressed as a percentage. Readiness to help items were coded as positive (1) for “yes” responses and 0 for “no” or “I am not sure,” with the overall score expressed as a percentage. Attitude items were rated on a five‑point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), and the mean score was calculated for each participant, then standardized to percentages for comparability.

Comparisons of mean scores across groups (e.g., sex, place of residence, academic discipline, year of study, prior exposure to epilepsy, and sources of information) were performed using independent samples t‑tests for two‑group comparisons and one‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for comparisons involving more than two groups. A two‑sided p‑value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Variables that showed significant associations in bivariate analyses were subsequently entered into multiple linear regression models to identify independent predictors of each construct (knowledge, readiness to help, and attitudes). Model diagnostics were conducted to ensure validity of the regression assumptions. Variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance values were examined to assess multicollinearity, with VIF values < 5 and tolerance values > 0.2 considered acceptable. Residual plots were inspected to confirm linearity and homoscedasticity, and normality of residuals was verified using standardized residual distributions.

Results

Descriptive characteristics of the study participants

A total of 449 students participated in the study, with the majority being female (n = 285, 63.5%) and residing in villages (n = 230, 51.2%) or cities (n = 208, 46.3%) (Table 1). The mean age of students was 21.2 years (SD = 3.7). Students were nearly evenly distributed across academic disciplines, with 221 (49.2%) enrolled in healthcare‑related majors and 228 (50.8%) in non‑health majors. Representation across years of study was balanced, with the largest proportion in the fourth year (n = 114, 25.4%). Most students reported awareness of epilepsy (n = 419, 93.3%), yet only a minority had attended a formal course on epilepsy (n = 141, 31.4%) or received training in seizure first aid (n = 71, 15.8%). About one‑third personally knew someone with epilepsy (n = 147, 32.7%), and 195 (43.4%) had witnessed a seizure episode, though only 72 (16.0%) had ever assisted during one.

Knowledge about epilepsy

Overall, students demonstrated modest levels of knowledge about epilepsy, with a mean score of 53.9% (SD = 16.2). Several misconceptions were evident. The majority correctly identified epilepsy as a brain illness (n = 382, 85.1%), yet a notable minority misclassified it as a mental illness (n = 46, 10.2%) or reported uncertainty (Supplementary Table S4). When asked about causes, head injury or trauma was most frequently cited (n = 187, 41.6%), followed by genetic factors (n = 93, 20.7%) and brain tumors (n = 66, 14.7%), while nearly one‑quarter (n = 103, 22.9%) reported not knowing. Knowledge gaps were particularly evident in practical aspects. Only a small proportion recognized that people with well‑controlled epilepsy may be allowed to drive, with most responses indicating uncertainty (n = 226, 50.3%) or outright denial (n = 160, 35.6%) (Supplementary Table S4). Misconceptions about first aid were also common: although most students correctly rejected the unsafe practice of inserting objects into the mouth during a seizure (n = 322, 71.7%), nearly one‑quarter (n = 98, 21.8%) still believed it was safe. Similarly, while most students acknowledged that epilepsy is not contagious (n = 422, 94.0%), fewer recognized that seizures can last longer than 30 min (n = 247, 55.0%), and only about half believed epilepsy can be treated with medical care (n = 217, 48.3%).

Readiness to help

Overall, students expressed a high level of readiness to help peers with epilepsy, with a mean score of 79.5% (SD = 24.7). The vast majority reported that they would assist a colleague during a seizure (n = 403, 89.8%), and most were willing to provide emotional support to affected students (n = 380, 84.6%) (Supplementary Table S5). Similarly, a large proportion indicated they would report bullying directed at a student with epilepsy (n = 380, 84.6%). However, readiness was less consistent when it came to advocacy behaviors. While more than half of the students were willing to share epilepsy awareness information on social media (n = 265, 59.0%), a substantial proportion were either unwilling (n = 61, 13.6%) or uncertain (n = 123, 27.4%) (Supplementary Table S5).

Attitudes toward people with epilepsy

Students’ attitudes toward people with epilepsy were mixed, with an overall mean score of 65.0% (SD = 11.1). While many expressed supportive views, ambivalence and stigma were also evident. More than half disagreed with avoiding friendships with people with epilepsy (n = 251, 55.9%), yet nearly one‑fifth remained neutral (n = 87, 19.4%) and a small proportion agreed with avoidance (n = 38, 8.5%) (Supplementary Table S6). Similarly, although over half strongly agreed or agreed that they would help someone with epilepsy if given the opportunity (n = 268, 59.6%), a sizable minority reported uncertainty or disagreement. Perceptions of capability and normalcy were less favorable. Only a small proportion strongly agreed that people with epilepsy can live a normal life (n = 17, 3.8%), while the majority either disagreed (n = 184, 41.0%) or remained neutral (n = 104, 23.2%). Attitudes toward employment were also cautious, with less than one‑fifth agreeing or strongly agreeing that they would recommend a person with epilepsy for a job (n = 79, 17.6%), while most remained neutral (n = 188, 41.9%) or disagreed (n = 182, 40.5%) (Supplementary Table S6). Emotional reactions revealed persistent stigma. A considerable proportion reported feeling anxious if a classmate had epilepsy (n = 195, 43.4%) or scared when witnessing a seizure (n = 234, 52.1%) (Supplementary Table S6). Pity was also common, with more than one‑third expressing agreement (n = 174, 38.7%).

Discussion

Epilepsy remains one of the most stigmatized neurological disorders worldwide, and understanding how young adults perceive the condition is critical for shaping supportive educational and social environments [10], [23]. This study set out to assess and compare the knowledge, readiness to help, and attitudes toward epilepsy among Palestinian university students in health‑related and non‑health majors. Unlike previous investigations that focused narrowly on single faculties or professional groups [15], [16], [18], [19], [20], this research is unique in its breadth, encompassing diverse academic disciplines and systematically linking knowledge, attitudes, and readiness to help within one instrument. By integrating these three constructs, the study provides a more holistic picture of how future professionals and community members may respond to peers with epilepsy. The most notable findings highlight both strengths and gaps. Students demonstrated moderate knowledge overall, with persistent misconceptions about causes, treatment, and seizure first aid. Encouragingly, readiness to help was high, with most students willing to assist or provide emotional support to peers, though advocacy behaviors such as sharing awareness information were less common. Attitudes were mixed, revealing willingness to form friendships and help, but also lingering anxiety, fear, and doubts about employability and normal life for people with epilepsy. Importantly, healthcare majors scored higher in knowledge and readiness, yet non‑health majors expressed slightly more favorable attitudes, underscoring the complexity of stigma and support. These findings are particularly informative for educators, health professionals, and policymakers who design curricula, awareness campaigns, and student support systems. They highlight the urgent need to strengthen epilepsy education across all faculties, while also addressing emotional reactions and stigma that persist even among well‑informed students. By identifying both the areas of competence and the domains of misconception, this study provides actionable insights for universities, ministries of health, and advocacy organizations seeking to foster inclusive and safe environments for students with epilepsy.

Knowledge about epilepsy among university students in this study was moderate, reflecting both awareness and persistent misconceptions. This pattern is consistent with reports from other low‑ and middle‑income countries, where students often recognize epilepsy as a neurological condition but remain uncertain about its causes and management [5], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19]. For example, studies in Ethiopia and Saudi Arabia found that while most students rejected supernatural explanations, many still misidentified epilepsy as a mental illness or were unaware of appropriate first aid practices [14], [15]. The persistence of misconceptions in our cohort, particularly regarding seizure duration, treatment, and driving eligibility, suggests that general awareness campaigns alone are insufficient. Instead, structured educational interventions embedded within university curricula are needed to provide accurate, practical knowledge [8], [23]. Importantly, the finding that healthcare majors scored higher in knowledge than non‑health majors echoes previous work in Turkey and Jordan [17], [24], [25], yet the overall scores remain lower than expected for future health professionals. This indicates that current training may emphasize pharmacological or theoretical aspects of epilepsy while neglecting everyday management and first aid [18], [19], [20]. Building on these insights, universities could integrate case‑based learning, simulation exercises, and patient narratives into health curricula to bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge and practical competence. For non‑health majors, targeted awareness programs delivered through campus events or peer‑led initiatives may be more effective, ensuring that all students, regardless of discipline, are equipped to respond appropriately to seizures. Thus, the knowledge findings highlight a dual need as strengthening epilepsy education within health faculties to ensure professional competence, and extending awareness beyond health disciplines to foster inclusive and supportive campus environments. These recommendations are particularly relevant for curriculum developers, student affairs offices, and ministries of health seeking to reduce stigma and improve safety for students with epilepsy.

The consistently high readiness to help observed among students is encouraging, as it suggests that empathy and willingness to support peers with epilepsy are embedded in the student culture. Similar findings have been reported in studies from Turkey and Ethiopia [15], [17], where students expressed strong intentions to assist classmates during seizure episodes. However, the weaker engagement in advocacy behaviors, such as sharing awareness information on social media, reflects a gap between interpersonal support and broader community action. This distinction is important because while immediate willingness to help ensures safety during seizures, advocacy is critical for dismantling stigma and promoting long‑term inclusion. The reluctance to engage in awareness‑raising may stem from uncertainty about the accuracy of information, fear of social backlash, or limited confidence in discussing epilepsy publicly. Previous studies in Saudi Arabia and Jordan have noted similar hesitations, suggesting that students often lack the tools or training to act as effective advocates [15], [26]. Building on these insights, universities could empower students by integrating epilepsy awareness into peer‑education programs, providing evidence‑based materials for dissemination, and encouraging student‑led campaigns. Such initiatives would not only strengthen readiness to help but also transform supportive intentions into proactive community engagement. Ultimately, the readiness findings highlight that students are willing to act when confronted with epilepsy directly, but require structured opportunities and confidence‑building to extend their support into advocacy. This has direct implications for student affairs offices, health educators, and advocacy organizations aiming to cultivate a culture of inclusion and awareness on campuses.

Attitudes toward people with epilepsy in this study were mixed, reflecting both supportive intentions and lingering stigma. The coexistence of willingness to form friendships and provide help alongside anxiety, fear, and doubts about employability mirrors findings from other Middle Eastern and African contexts, where stigma remains entrenched despite moderate knowledge levels [4], [9], [10], [13], [15], [17], [19], [24], [25]. For example, studies in Jordan and Ethiopia reported that students often expressed empathy but simultaneously endorsed restrictive views on marriage, work, and social integration [15], [25]. Our finding that non‑health majors expressed slightly more favorable attitudes than healthcare majors is particularly noteworthy, as it contrasts with the assumption that greater knowledge automatically translates into more positive attitudes. This suggests that professional training may inadvertently reinforce perceptions of epilepsy as a disabling condition, emphasizing risks and limitations rather than capabilities. These mixed attitudes highlight the complexity of stigma as it is not simply a product of ignorance, but also of cultural norms, emotional reactions, and professional framing. Interventions therefore need to go beyond factual education, incorporating empathy‑building strategies such as patient testimonies, peer interaction, and narrative‑based learning. Evidence from Turkey and Saudi Arabia shows that exposure to people living successfully with epilepsy can significantly reduce fear and pity, fostering more inclusive attitudes [6], [14], [17]. For Palestinian universities, embedding such approaches into both health and non‑health curricula could help shift perceptions from deficit‑focused to strength‑based, ensuring that students see people with epilepsy as capable of leading normal, productive lives.

Taken together, the findings reveal a complex interplay between knowledge, readiness to help, and attitudes. Higher knowledge and exposure were linked to greater readiness, yet attitudes did not always align with knowledge gains, underscoring that stigma is not simply a matter of ignorance. This divergence suggests that educational interventions must be multidimensional as factual instruction alone may improve competence but does not necessarily reduce fear or negative perceptions. Conversely, positive attitudes among non‑health majors, despite lower knowledge, highlight the role of cultural and social framing in shaping empathy. The synthesis of these constructs points to a clear recommendation: universities and health authorities should design integrated programs that simultaneously build accurate knowledge, strengthen practical readiness, and challenge stigmatizing attitudes. Such programs could combine didactic teaching with experiential learning, peer‑support initiatives, and exposure to role models living successfully with epilepsy. By addressing all three dimensions together, interventions can move beyond isolated improvements to foster a genuinely inclusive environment. These insights are particularly valuable for curriculum developers, student affairs offices, and advocacy organizations, who must recognize that knowledge, readiness, and attitudes are interdependent and require coordinated strategies to achieve meaningful change.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study has several notable strengths. First, the study drew on a large and diverse sample of students, exceeding the minimum required for statistical power and ensuring representation across different faculties and years of study. Second, by including both health‑related and non‑health majors, the study captured meaningful contrasts between disciplines, a feature that distinguishes it from many previous investigations limited to single faculties. Third, the use of a validated and culturally adapted questionnaire strengthened methodological rigor and ensured relevance to the Palestinian context. Fourth, the instrument demonstrated robust reliability for all constructs and excellent test–retest stability. Fifth, the multidimensional approach allowed simultaneous assessment of knowledge, readiness to help, and attitudes, providing a holistic understanding of student perceptions. Sixth, rigorous statistical analyses were applied, including both bivariate and multivariable regression models, with diagnostic checks confirming validity and ruling out major biases such as multicollinearity.

On the other hand, the following limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross‑sectional design restricts causal inference, meaning that observed associations cannot be interpreted as directional effects. Second, reliance on self‑reported data introduces the possibility of social desirability bias, particularly in items assessing readiness to help and attitudes. Third, the geographic restriction to universities in the northern West Bank may limit generalizability to other Palestinian regions or international contexts. Fourth, the knowledge section contained only eight items, which may not fully capture the breadth of epilepsy knowledge. Fifth, although the sample size was adequate, nonresponse bias remains possible, as students who declined participation or provided incomplete responses may differ systematically from those included. Sixth, the socioeconomic status as a comprehensively assessed as a variable in this study. While the place of residence was included, this measure alone does not adequately capture socioeconomic differences. In Palestine, certain health-related majors such as medicine, dentistry, and pharmacy require substantially higher tuition fees compared to non-health majors. This financial barrier may influence the selection of students into specific fields and could correlate with the differences observed in knowledge and attitudes toward epilepsy. Future studies should incorporate socioeconomic indicators to better account for these potential confounding factors. Seventh, although prior studies in the region have reported that epilepsy is sometimes attributed to supernatural causes [9], [10], [15], [16], [17], [18], [21], [23], [24], [25], [26], our questionnaire did not assess this item. Given that the Palestinian community is predominantly religious, the omission of this item may have limited our ability to fully capture misconceptions related to cultural or spiritual beliefs. Future research should consider including such items to provide a more comprehensive assessment of epilepsy-related misconceptions. Finally, advocacy in this study was assessed primarily through participants’ willingness to share epilepsy awareness on social media. While useful, this represents only one dimension of advocacy. Other forms, such as in‑person engagement, community education, or institutional initiatives, were not captured. Limiting the assessment to social media may therefore overlook participants who prefer alternative advocacy approaches, which is particularly relevant in communities where stigma remains a major issue.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of Palestinian university students’ knowledge, readiness to help, and attitudes toward epilepsy, revealing both encouraging strengths and persistent gaps. While students demonstrated high willingness to assist peers and provide emotional support, misconceptions about epilepsy and mixed attitudes toward social integration remain evident. Healthcare majors showed stronger knowledge and readiness, yet non‑health majors expressed slightly more favorable attitudes, underscoring the complex relationship between information, empathy, and stigma. The findings highlight the urgent need for integrated educational strategies that combine factual instruction with empathy‑building and advocacy training. Universities, ministries of health, and student affairs offices can leverage these insights to design curricula and awareness programs that not only improve seizure first aid competence but also challenge stigma and foster inclusive environments. By addressing knowledge, readiness, and attitudes simultaneously, such interventions can empower students to become informed allies and advocates, ultimately contributing to safer, more supportive communities for people with epilepsy.
