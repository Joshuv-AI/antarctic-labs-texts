The perceptions and reactions to COVID-19 among offspring of African immigrants and young adults who migrated to the U.S․
Authors: Titilayo A Okoror, Abi Fapohunda, Kelechi Ibe-Lamberts, Daudet Ilunga Tshiswaka, Jade Oghoanina, Deanna Keenan, Mmekom Udosen, Yayra Akolly, Danita Hingston
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•African immigrants and COVID-19 perceptions.•Health disparities in Black immigrant communities.•Cultural identity and healthcare experiences in the U.S.•Misinformation and social media influence on COVID-19 awareness.•Spirituality, traditional healing, and pandemic coping strategies.Keywords: African, Immigrants, Offspring, Young adults, Covid-19, Health, Black, Vaccine, Cultural self-identity

Introduction

In 2019, Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) pandemic, also known as COVID-19, took over the world, and has continued to cause significant sickness and deaths globally, disrupting daily lives. It has upended family lives, economic and social pursuits, besides the untold suffering and death. The coronavirus disease negatively affects the respiratory system and is transferred via direct transmission through contact made with respiratory droplets from an infected person (World Health Organization 2023). As of February 2023, there have been a total of about 102.74 million cases in the United States and about 1110,363 deaths that were caused by COVID-19 (Center for Disease Control and Prevention 2023). A total of 269.2 million people has had at least one dose of the vaccine, while 229.8 million people are fully vaccinated (69.2 % of population), and about 52.5 million have received the updated (bivalent) booster dose in the U.S (Center for Disease Control and Prevention 2023). With Black (non-Hispanic) people making up only 12.5 % of the US population, they are disproportionately reported as having the third highest number in COVID-19 cases with a total of 7970,885. The same goes for the rates in death by COVID-19 with a total of 103,1271 (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2023). While the percentage of Blacks that have received at least one dose of COVID-19 vaccine has increased (90.1 % in comparison to 86.4 % in Whites), and 85.1 % have completed the primary series of the vaccine versus 83.8 % in Whites, they are less likely to have received the updated bivalent booster dose in comparison to Whites (23 % versus 34.9 % in Whites) (Center for Disease Control and Prevention 2023). Adjusted data accounting for differences in age by race/ethnicity shows that Blacks along with other minority groups have had higher rates of infection and death than White people for the most part of the pandemic (Nambi et al., 2022). Data on COVID-19 by age group in the U.S. show people between the ages 18–29 as having the most cases of COVID-19 (19,439,382) but had the sixth lowest cases of death (6768) by COVID-19 (CDC, 2023) (Center for Disease Control and Prevention 2023). Furthermore, people between the ages 18–29 were the fourth lowest age demographic to be fully vaccinated (13,225,517) or have at least one dose (16,436,879) (Center for Disease Control and Prevention 2023).

Though focus has been on the unequal burden of the impact of COVID-19 on people of color, specifically Blacks, the monolithic view of the Black communities has undermined efforts to improve vaccine uptake among them, and created a gap in vaccine research among the population. Factors that impact vaccine uptake, such as culture, history, religion, immigration status, language, racial discrimination, type of employment, access to healthcare or lack thereof, are not examined in vaccine promotion programs, though these factors are experienced differently in the black communities. For example, while immigration status will not be a pertinent issue for vaccine hesitancy among African Americans (i.e. American Descendants of Slavery [ADOS]), or Africans that arrived in the US as refugees, it may be a salient factor for African immigrants (specifically sub-Saharan Africans). As such, though these populations are classified as Blacks, their experiences and interactions with COVID-19 pandemic varies and has impact on vaccine uptake or lack thereof.

It should be noted that the terms ‘immigrant’ and ‘refugee’ has implications for immigration status (documented or undocumented); and therefore, access to resources or lack thereof (Matlin et al., 2022; McFadden et al., 2022; Sudhinaraset et al., 2022). A refugee has valid immigration status, while an immigrant may be documented or undocumented. In addition, while sub-Saharan African immigrants (which includes refugees) make up 4.5 % of the US 44.7million immigrants, research indicates that they make up a significant number of the healthcare and essential workforce in America, with 3.4 percent of all healthcare workers as black immigrants; 9.4 % working as health aides; 3.9 % as nurses, and 2.3 % of all food production workers (Echeverria-Estrada and Batalova, 2019; New American Economy 2020) These types of employment further increase their risk of exposure to COVID-19, and the potential to bring the virus home to families and communities.

Research among Blacks in general, and sub-Saharan African immigrants in particular, shows that relationships and perceptions of family and community members have a bearing on health decisions; and beliefs and decisions surrounding COVID-19 vaccination is not an exception (Airhihenbuwa et al., 2020). African immigrant’s children or offspring perceptions and reactions to the COVID-19 pandemic is of importance in efforts to improve vaccine uptake. It is estimated that black immigrants and their children make up 18 % of the overall black population in the US; 11 % of all black children in America are in black immigrant families; and the numbers are going rapidly, with African immigrants poised to become the largest Black immigrant group (Tamir, 2022; Capps and Fix, 2012). As indicated above in the COVID-19 vaccination data among young adults, of which African immigrants’ offspring are included, understanding factors that influence their vaccine decision-making is salient in efforts to reduce the spread of the pandemic and increase vaccine uptake among them.

Research indicates that offspring of African immigrants and young adults who migrated to the U.S. are usually quite different from their parents. Similar to their contemporaries, they tend to be quite savvy in the use of the internet and social media, consuming and disseminating digital contents, while processing information differently from their parents (Yazykova and McLeigh, 2015; Premazzi and Ricucci, 2013). By the same token however, they have created complex and hybrid identities fused from “African-based traditions and cultures with American sensibilities that aligned more closely with their realities” (Amoah, 2014; Chacko, 2019; Emeka, 2019). One of the implications of the complex hybrid identities among African immigrants’ offspring is an amalgamation of American and African cultures that serve as lens in interpreting their experiences; and thereby, influencing their perceptions, knowledge, and behaviors. Therefore, using the Social Ecological Model (SEM), the purpose of this study is to examine the knowledge, perceptions and reactions to COVID-19 among offspring of African immigrants and young adults in the US.

Theoretical Framework: The Social Ecological Model (SEM) posits that the interactions of the intrapersonal, interpersonal, organizational, community, and public policy broadly influences health behaviors and decisions. The model recognizes that health decisions and behaviors are not solely based on individual preferences and choices, but rather they are influenced by the broader social and environmental context. The intrapersonal level refers to demographic factors such as race, ethnicity, age, gender, and individual’s knowledge and skills; and the interpersonal level which examines the influence of a person’s relationships with others, such as family, friends, peers, and social networks. The organizational level refers to the social institutions and organizations, while community level indicates the relationships between organizations. The public policy level examines national, state, local laws, and regulations. For this study, a modified form of the SEM focusing on three levels: (1) the intrapersonal, (2) interpersonal, and (3) organizational (social institutions)/community levels was utilized (Latkin et al., 2021). The SEM was selected as it allows for an exploration of the interactions across multiple levels, and account for factors that influence individual’s health behaviros and decisions, such as family and community in shaping individual’s reactions and responses.

Methodology

This is part of a larger project exploring how African immigrants and refugees in the US are reacting and responding to the COVID-19 pandemic. Participants were recruited through flyers shared on social media, and through community organizations, and associations. Community organizations that serve African immigrants and refugees, along with national and ethnic associations and groups of African immigrants and refugees were contacted through social medias, such as Facebook and Twitter, and through personal networks of investigators on the project. Emails about the study along with recruitment letter were sent to organizations and associations’ leaders to share with their members. Based on pilot testing and feedback, the term “discussion” instead of “interview” was used in the recruitment letter to reduce anxiety that may occur with contemplation of participation. The recruitment letter was translated into three languages: English, French, and Swahili – the three most common languages spoken by African immigrants and refugees in the US. The letter contained a link for potential participants to access the study information. Using snowball approach, participants in the study were encouraged to spread the word about the project to reach a broader audience within the African immigrant and refugee communities. The use of social media for recruitment, and zoom for data collection allowed for open recruitment across the US irrespective of state of dwelling despite snow-ball approach. Many participants were able to share the study links across their networks, which was then shared across other networks allowing for heternogenity of participants across multiple spaces and places. Data collection started six months into the first lockdown (August 2020).

Data collection

Data was collected using Qualtrics online platform, semi-structured interviews and focus group discussions. Through Qualtrics, interested participants completed the informed consent; demographic survey; and selected preferred method of participation (zoom video one-on-one; telephone interview using zoom; or zoom focus group discussion) in their chosen language (English, Swahili, or French). Following the selection, participants received a prompt that took them to a new page to input their contact information. This is to ensure confidentiality that their completed survey was not linked to their contact information. Research has validated the use of zoom videoconferencing for data collection as cost-effective with relative use of ease by respondents (Archiblad et al., 2019; Lobe et al., 2020). All study materials (inform consent, survey and interview questions) were translated into Swahili and French; back translated into English and verified by independent researcher for accurate translations of contents and ideas. The demographic survey contained questions on gender, age, employment, marital status, country of origin, number of languages spoken, religion/faith, means of communication with family abroad, whether refugee or immigrant, and health insurance status. It should be noted that all participants were classified as immigrants though the survey question included an option for participants to indicate if they were refugees. Discussions were facilitated by one of the investigators in participant’s chosen language (English, Swahili, or French). Focus groups discissions were arranged by participants’ age groups to ensure opneness during discussions, while also acknowledging the respect accorded to age-based seniority and elders, as practiced in a number of African cultures. Cultural ethos of respect was also employed by interviewer, especially when participants appeared older. Pseudonyms were assigned to participants during group discussions, and all were encourage to use the assigned pseudonmyns to ensure confidentiality.

The Social Ecological Model (SEM) guided the development of interview and focus group questions, based on literature reviews. Focus group discussions and interviews were guided by five opinion questions that explored participant’s knowledge/perceptions of COVID-19; impact of COVID-19 on self and community; community members reactions to the disease; trustworthiness of COVID-19 messages; and vaccination. Discussions lasted an average of 45 min to 1.5 h, and were recorded via zoom, following the reiteration of the consent form. The University Institutional Review Board approved the study.

Data analysis

Transcripts were downloaded from Zoom and verified for accuracy by research assistants, and translated to English if discussions took place in French or Swahili. Sonix.AI software was used for language translation. Two investigators fluent in French and Swahili reviewed transcripts to verify content of English translation, and all transcripts were uploaded into NVivo software 12 (QRS International) for data management. Deductive and inductive approaches were used in coding the transcripts, using open and axial coding. Deductive approach was guided by discussion questions based on literature reviews, while inductive approach was used in searching for themes, patterns or ideas that emerged from the data (Braun and Clarke, 2012). We followed the six-phases outlined by Braun & Clarke in using thematic analysis method: reading and rereading of transcripts for data immersion; generating initial codes, which we used deductive and inductive approach; searching for themes by reviewing the coded data and identifying similarity and overlaps; quality checking of identified themes to ensure it captures patterns described in the data; defining and naming of identified themes; and generating the report (Braun and Clarke, 2012; Braun and Clarke, 2006). Each transcript was coded by two coders, and disagrements on codes were resolved through discussion. Data saturation was achieved when no new themes emerged from discussions and interviews of participants who identify as offspring and young adults of African immigrants (Mwita, 2022).

Results

Over 100 respondents have participated in the study, however only 27 interviews with participants that identified as offspring of African immigrants are included in this analysis. Participants were mostly female (67 %), with an average age of 28 years. Participants were equally distributed in marital status: 44 % (married & single), while 12 % reported being in a relationship. Participants were mostly Christian (88.9 %), employed (66 %), and had health insurance (81.5 %), with over 95 % reporting access to healthcare services, when they need it. Almost half of the participants were bilingual (48 %), had family living in Africa (96 %), and communicated with family abroad using two or more electronic platforms (88.9 %). Using the modified SEM, with particular focus on three levels, there are four major themes from the data analysis: 1) Knowledge of COVID-19; (2) Sources of Information about COVID-19; (3) Cultural Self-Identity & U.S. Healthcare System; and (4) Spirituality, Healing and COVID-19.

Knowledge of COVID-19: On the intrapersonal level, most participants demonstrated adequate knowledge about COVID-19, explaining how the virus can be transmitted, symptoms of infection, preventive measures such as wearing facemasks, handwashing, social distancing, quarantining, and isolation, while also avoiding large gatherings:

“I think it's super important to wear masks and socially distance and wash your hands more often than you normally would and you know if hand washing isn't an option and to use hand sanitizer and stuff like that.”

“I know it's like flu-like symptoms. So, like cough…I know like diarrhea or other gastrointestinal issues. Um, I don't know a lot. I just know mostly like if you have symptoms of a cold like that's, that could be an issue.”

“I'm just staying away staying away from each other, you know, we're so used to gathering, you know, and that's the thing you know just, you know, that was tough. Right, especially when you know, we know with African ceremonies.”

“I feel like people are thinking not going to church and rather doing it over zoom and over teleconference and more seriously…”

Participants discussed how family members have avoided visiting them, and/or not hugging when visiting to avoid spreading the virus. Though some reported confusion as to the ‘truth’ about how the virus spreads, there is the understanding that many people coming together can spread the virus. It must be stated that the confusion about the virus was evident in the earlier interviews conducted six months into the pandemic, while interviews in late 2021 demonstrated an improved knowledge and awareness:

“.. I think education does play a role in it …. in terms of the African community … some people just don't have as much resources as others so it could be most people the same age but depending on where they live - this person knows way more about like science and biology than the other person. And I think that understanding the pandemic - it comes from your understanding of how viruses work in general.”

“Because people don't understand why, they just wear facemask because everyone is wearing facemask, but they don't understand why they're putting on a face mask. So, education I would say does play a huge wider role in telling them the reason why behind what you're telling others to do. So, it does play a huge role and education opens your mind to think critically think, that's how you get the understanding.”

Sources of Information about COVID-19: The interconnectedness with family and friends was exemplified at the interpersonal level, with participants reporting receiving information from trusted family, friends, and peers, especially those in the healthcare system:

“Well, for me personally, I just have more privileged information through a long list of uncles and aunts that were in the medical field. And that's where my trusted information will only come from, the news can say what they want…”

“Either the WHO or my mother because my mother is a doctor.”

“… probably my siblings, when it first started getting popular, my siblings, we all like had this group call where we all talked about oh we have to stay safe. We need to stay indoors”.

On the social institutions and community levels, many participants reported trusting health institutions like the Center for Disease Control and/or World Health Organization for information regarding COVID-19:

“The disease surveillance organizations like the WHO and the CDC were in tracking how quickly and how pervasive the disease was…”

“I think like people in general and probably trust the CDC, the most just because, like, that's their job.”

“I get most of my news surrounding COVID from the CDC and the World Health Organization's like I make it a priority and I tell all my family this, don't listen to politicians, because you're not supposed to get your public health information from politicians, so I could try not to listen to them for COVID stuff”.

On the other hand, participants reported that they received information from news media with skepticism. Many reported that news coverage during the height of the pandemic led to confusion on reliable information, misinformation, and information overload:

“It’s through experiences, and pigging back a little bit on, you know, from the CDC, the World Health Organization, with just like previous participants stated, so many information, and then it getting to overload and it's overload with fear, anxiety, it gets to confusion. So, so we really sit back to analyze and reason ourselves and see what works around us. Even without being in the medical field you see really what works around you.”

“I don’t trust what I see in the news to be honest about the pandemic because it’s changed so much over time, so, yeah, it’s, I think right now, every, any information, I get is something I, my first thought is, this people really know what they’re saying or are they just guessing right now?”

Some participants reported experiencing anxiety or fear due to the news overload, and react by limiting their exposure:

“Maybe some friends, school, school children, there was a lot of fear and anxiety because maybe the news people aren't doing it on purpose, but because it's news they report it, and you hear that same story about numbers, and you know they put up graphics and it was very scary. So, at some point, we had to unplug from watching the news. Okay, we'll just listen to you know 15 min of news to see what's happening in the neighborhood and then unplug. Maybe listen to, you know, a government, a government agent, a government official…”

“Um, yeah, I'd like to chime in on that the part about the anxiety and the fear from information overload. Personally, that kind of happened in my household, because you know with the news on and you know it was something new in the world. So, we realized after a while. My wife and I, we'd have to cut off the TV.”

Social media apps such as WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram were reported as main form of communication with family, friends and acquaintances, while they are also major sources of misinformation about COVID-19 and spreading false information about the vaccine. Many reported the types of messages they saw on social media:

“COVID-19 is “just a flu”’

“A healthy diet is all that’s needed to prevent COVID-19, not a vaccine.”

“The vaccine is deadly for your body.”

Some participants reported their concerns in general about misinformation via social media:

“I think, first of all, just making sure that all the facts are out about the coronavirus since, you know, we talked about with WhatsApp and how this is so much misinformation and confusion going around”.

“…. in terms of like WhatsApp and Facebook, I think it would be really important that going forward, especially, … throughout this pandemic realize that this is not just an African thing, it's a problem that misinformation traveling through WhatsApp and Facebook is very prominent and a lot of countries”.

Cultural Self-Identity and US Healthcare System: The interactions between the three levels: intrapersonal, interpersonal and institution/community were obvious in this theme. Participants’ cultural self-identity and their relationships influence their perceptions, experiences, and interactions with the US healthcare systems. While racial identity as black seems to be salient in their interactions (intrapersonal level), their country of origin and/or parents’ country of origin (interpersonal level) which informed their cultural identity served as lens in describing and/or interpreting their interactions with the US healthcare system (institution/community level). Participants who self-identify as African or African descent versus African Americans reported understanding ADOS community’s historical encounters with the US healthcare system and medical professionals, and the resulting weariness and distrust as articulated in the quotes below:

“I guess my, my cultural identity being black in America, but also a Nigerian American and not an African American who's had generations here. So, like my cultural group, I should say my clan hasn't gone through the same levels of or doesn't have the same level of distrust or mistrust, one of those two towards healthcare professionals, non-white or non-people of color. Yeah, I will just say non people of color, but yeah basically they don't have that same level of weariness towards white professionals like healthcare professionals, the way African-Americans do.”

“I can say the same for myself, I'm aware of the unfortunate sordid, history that has travailed across time. I’ve read[about] the medical apartheid like it's real… but at the same time because I've had positive experiences with [health care]. Then again all of my physicians have been relatively like either if they’re white they were European and if they were non-white they were like just non-white.”

Some participants reported that they could not recall a time when they were discriminated against in the healthcare system, which they attributed to it not occurring or they not being attentive to racism in that aspect:

“So, I have obviously interacted with the healthcare system here, and I had both, I guess I’ve had overall really good experiences. But I guess I’m aware of the fact that, as a black person, I’m, like the healthcare I’ll receive is more likely going to be of a lower, like it wouldn’t be as good as the healthcare that a white person would receive.”

“But this is not something that I was personally aware of. I'm sure I've been treated differently, and I didn't notice I'm like very blind to racism, because I mostly grew up outside of America. Um, so I my mind doesn't instantly go to racism, for the most part, but it [pandemic] just has made me question a lot more like times that I've been neglected, um as a black woman. I'd already started to go to people of color for health care because I don't know [sigh] I just somehow feel like if you're a person of color, you understand what we go through more as opposed to like a white doctor, for instance, or even Asian doctors. I prefer to go to doctors who are from, who are black from Africa - African doctors would be the best for me because I'm African, lived in Africa [and if I] have issues that would be related to someone who's come from Africa.”

By the same token, however, some reported that the COVID-19 pandemic has exposed the racial disparities within the healthcare system, making them more aware than before, and they do not want to be hospitalized for COVID-19 due to fear of discrimination of not being priority to healthcare workers because they are black:

“Um, I think it just, I kind of like just took it for granted that doctors would be professional and would treat everyone equally. Um, so yeah, it was, it was all very shocking and sad to hear that, especially in New York City, where the hardest hit - that black people were being affected more and passing away more during the pandemic, so yeah, my eyes were actually literally open during this pandemic in some ways…”

“So, I guess the bottom line is, yes, I think you can get good health care. Here I have gotten great health care here in the past, but I wouldn't want to fall sick right now because I feel that if push comes to shove, I don't know if I would be on the priority. I don’t know where I would fall basically on the priority line.”

At the time most of these discussions took place, many participants expressed that they were planning to get the vaccine but were initially hesitant because the vaccines were not FDA approved. Also, social media posts detailing people who had negative reactions to the vaccine (skin rashes; severe allergic reactions; death) created concerns and questions. It should be noted that when participants were asked if they knew of anyone who died after receiving the vaccine, they reported none. Most participants reported that they were unsure of how many people in their community were vaccinated, due to members been private about health matters, though some members of their immediate family were vaccinated. Participants that were fully vaccinated stated it was due to work requirements (working in healthcare field), school and seeing loved ones that have recovered from COVID-19 or have passed away.

Spirituality, Healing & COVID-19: While participants discussed at length the role of religious leaders as potential conduit of information about COVID-19, and providing guidance as necessary (interpersonal), many were quick to point out that they take autonomy over their own health and do not depend on their religious leaders for such. Many reported prayers and reading of religious texts, among others (intrapersonal) as central to maintaining their health and coping with the COVID-19 pandemic. At the same time, they disagreed with the views some members in their community had that faith and prayer was all that was needed to prevent contracting COVID-19:

“Africans, we always believe the pastor, or the person in charge of the religion we practice, whether you're Muslim or Christian. Whoever is in charge of the religion you are in, or who is seen as the guide, or the shepherd, or the Father.”

“…so definitely you know prayers is a tool that we use for like everything. So yeah, there's been a lot like especially from my church, like there's been prayers, like every single night like over zoom um, fasting, everything is in holy water, I mean, holy oil. So yeah, we've been like, there's been a lot of focus on like prayer and spiritual warfare and all of that.”

“But the problem that I'm having with the church is that…they use prayer and they think that you should not just have wisdom anymore. Like they think that …because I now prayed that I'm gonna do whatever I want, even though it’s a pandemic, because I prayed. Jesus is going to cover me, and everything's gonna be okay…I feel like that's not good spiritually, because we all read the Bible, and we saw that we not supposed to tempt to God. And I think that's what Christians are doing like …I know there's a pandemic but I’m gonna go outside with no mask, I’m gonna cough all over the place, I’m gonna eat whatever I want and God's gonna protect me just because I said so…”

Participants further discussed the use of traditional herbs, recipes, teas, and vitamins as remedies used in strengthening their immune system against COVID-19, and sharing of such information through social medias like WhatsApp:

“It’s very, very funny and interesting is because my mom is a physician assistant, but she still sends me those things and I had the discussion…I said, Is there a cure? Can you get cured? And she said ‘No, it doesn't get cured but what is does is to help clear your system a bit, and limits chances. It does not mean that you won't get it, if it's in your body you’re gonna get it.’ It doesn't prevent it but it does help your body as a whole, clear up your body,” and she told me to keep on steaming, taking ginger, honey, lemon, that it helps clear your sinuses, it helps cleanse your system. So it's not bad thing to say, but when people go to the dangerous line of saying it cures it, it prevents it, that's when there's a problem.”

“I was definitely on lockdown, like there was no reason for me to leave the house, and I could even come up with an excuse because like my parents would just be like no, you're going stay home. You have no reason to be outside. So, we've really been socially distancing, making sure to take Vitamin C, definitely like we've been taking it every single day since like March. Yeah, we've been really cracking down on I think the African communities over here, we're very serious about it.”

“Sharing recipes for teas that are supposed to prevent you from getting COVID-19″

“Sharing certain types of herbal steam inhalations that are supposed to help prevent or heal from COVID-19″

Discussion

This study examined the knowledge, perceptions and reactions of African immigrant’s offspring and young adults living in the US to the COVID-19 pandemic. The data suggest that participants have adequate knowledge about the SAR-CoV-2 modes of transmission, symptoms, and preventive actions. Most participants explained what to look out for if one is infected with the virus (symptoms), though they indicated the similarity between COVID-19 symptoms and influenza and/or cold. While the question of what is the ‘truth’ about the virus (the science about the virus), was central to participants’ responses to questions about their knowledge of the virus in most of 2020, interviews conducted in late 2021 showed clearer understanding of the virus, and less comparison to cold or flu symptoms. It is possible that this may be due to more information becoming available about the virus, and participants’ experiences of infected friends and family members recovering from the virus. Participants’ willingness to avoid large gatherings including participating in religious activities, speaks to the impact of the pandemic in disrupting daily lives and routines. For African immigrants, participation in such activities is central, not just to their identities, but also to their survival. Studies have demonstrated that immigrants, and African immigrants in particular, engage in social gatherings to derive meanings to their lives which is essential for health and wellbeing (Ekwonye et al., 2021).

The disparity within the African immigrants’ communities in terms of lack of awareness about the virus and access to resources is of note in participants’ comments about the value of education. While some members of the communities are well informed about the virus and engage in preventive actions such as wearing facemasks because they understand why, others engage in preventive behaviors “because everyone is wearing facemasks…they don’t understand why they’re putting on a face mask.” This is telling as it speaks to the role of community members in shaping behaviors. There is no indication of peer pressure being a factor in this context to influence preventive behaviors, rather it seems the African cultural context of community identity holding sway over individual identity maybe at play in influencing behaviors (Jakub et al., 2018). Jakub and colleagues reported that dietary behaviors among young adult offspring of African immigrants were heavily influenced by emotional connection to family and home, and “their exercise patterns have familial and generational influences” (Jakub et al., 2018). Research among Somali refugees in Minesota demonstrated the power of the community in shaping behaviors during the MUMPS outbreak in 2017 due to lack of vaccine uptake in the community (Bahta and Ashkir, 2015; Banerjee et al., 2017; Hall et al., 2017). Engaging community members was central in efforts to increase vaccine uptake among the Somali refugees and stop the outbreak (Banerjee et al., 2017; Hall et al., 2017). The role of the community is further emphasized in participants’ sources of information about COVID-19. Primary sources identified were family members and friends they trust, and at the same time, these same sources can be a source of misinformation about the virus. While participants did discuss other sources like health organizations (CDC, WHO), they were quick to indicate that family and friends’ sources hold sway: “privileged information through long list of uncles and aunts that were in the medical field.” Despite the multicultural sensibilities demonstrated by African immigrants’ offspring and young adults, the importance attached to information from friends, family and community members indicate that they (friend, family and community members) can serve as resource tools that can be utilized by public health policy in designing and implementing vaccines campaigns, as exemplified in this study.

The use of social media, such as WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram, to name a few as communication tools with family and friends, while also serving as tool of misinformation cannot be downplayed. The COVID-19 pandemic and subsequent global lockdown meant more people depended on social media platforms (SMPs) to stay connected (Goel and Gupta, 2020; Tsao et al., 2021). While participants valued their interconnectedness to family and friends through social media, especially with those family and friends living in the continent, they were quick to point out that social media is also a source of misinformation about the virus, a fact borne out by multiple studies on the impact of social media on health information (Bridgman et al., 2020; Frenkel et al., 2020; Tsao et al., 2021). Some participants suggested that “going forward, it would be really important that [the misinformation] traveling through [social media] is not just an African thing, [rather] it’s very prominent and [in] a lot of countries,” and efforts should made to reduce them. Addressing public health misformation, must be paramount in public health agenda if the efforts are to be successful in managing publc health emergencies. There is a need to enagage communities using channels and mediums they are most comfortable with, and have ready access to. African immigrants’ offspring and young adults, while similar to their contemporaries in the US, are also more likely to have strong connections to family and friends in the continent. As such, efforts to engage them must take into accounts the connections they hold dear, and the channels they use to maintain those connections. While social media, such as Whatsapp, Facebook, Instagram, Telegram, Twitter, to name a few, does have its drawbacks because of its potential for misinformation, public health vaccine campaigns utilizing them may go a long way for wider reach to this population, and make it easy for to share such information across multiple platforms (Kanchan and Gaidhane, 2023).

Contrarily, the constant coverage by news media (which some viewed as reputable sources) also presented its own challenges to participants. Some reported anxiety and fear due to overload of information about the pandemic, while some were skeptical and questioned the validity of the information they are receiving: “…[do] this people really know what they are saying or are they just guessing right now?” Fear and anxiety ensuing from news coverage of the pandemic was also reported in the studies among African immigrants (Ekwonye and Truong, 2022). The need to disconnect from the news and only access it when needed is of note because it suggests the need for personal control, similar to findings from Ekwonye & Truong on how African immigrants make meaning of the COVID-19 pandemic (Ekwonye and Truong, 2022). Participants understand the need to stay informed and be aware of what’s going on around them, and yet disconnect for their peace of mind. However, unlike participants in Ekwonye & Truong’s study, participants in this study did not compare their situations as better off than others, in what is known as “downward comparisons” (Ekwonye and Truong, 2022). Rather participants in the study seem to believe in the idea of personal autonomy to decide when to engage and disengage from the news.

The idea of personal autonomy is well demonstrated in how participants described the role of spirituality and religion in helping them to cope with the pandemic. Similar to findings from other studies (Ekwonye and Truong, 2022; Ekwonye et al., 2021), participants discussed the value of prayer and reading religious texts as important tools in coping with the pandemic. However, they were quick to point out that they have personal autonomy over their health though they may attribute ‘some’ value to what the churches or mosques’ ministers may say. They do not agree with the idea of praying only and not following public health preventive measures to protect their health, and condemn those who believe praying only is the way to go without following preventive measures: “…they use prayer and they think you should not just have wisdom anymore…they think…because I now prayed that I’m gonna do whatever I want, even though it’s a pandemic…[sigh].” While participants did not report that the pandemic drew them closer to God as in other studies (Ekwonye and Truong, 2022; Ekwonye et al., 2021), they emphasized that it drew them closer to family and friends. Families and friends are the sources of information about traditional herbs, recipes, and drinks, which many in the study reported using to augment their immune system against COVID-19. The use of such remedies is well documented in research among immigrants across the board in the US (Barimah and Van Teijlingen, 2008; Venters and Gany, 2011), and even among mainstream Americans (e.g., use of chicken soup for managing cold). The challenge though is that there is limited research on the validity of such remedies in preventing COVID-19 infection, although the use of honey, lemon and ginger does show promises in managing the severity of symptoms (Hossain et al., 2020; Jafarzadeh et al., 2021; Orisakwe et al., 2020), and these three items were listed by participants. The value of such cultural remedies was important to participants as it enabled them to feel connected to family and friends, especially since they could not gather together.

Cultural identity, specifically, how people see themselves must play a central role in public health programs, especially in efforts to increase vaccine uptake among African immigrants’ offspring and young adults. The data suggest that while participants can identify with being Black in America, they do not see themselves as African Americans (ADOS), similar to the findings from other studies (Clark, 2008; Kebede, 2019; Sall, 2019). Their cultural self-identify influence their perception and interactions with the US healthcare system. Participants acknowledged the history of medical racism against ADOS, and its impact on the health of the Black population. However, they delineated that their personal interactions and experiences with the healthcare system may not necessarily be through the lens of racism, or they may have experienced it and not be aware of it: “I’m aware of the unfortunate sordid history that has travailed across time…medical apartheid…but at the same time…I’ve had positive experiences with [health care].” Most of the participants reported having good experiences overall with the US healthcare system, although COVID-19 has now lay bare the disparities in the system: “I took it for granted that doctors would be professional and would treat everyone equally…it was all very shocking and sad to hear that …black people were being affected more…my eyes were actually open during this pandemic in some ways…” The above words of a participant captured the awareness many African immigrants’ offspring and young adults are coming to terms with. It should be noted that participants understand that being white carries privileges that being black does not conferred, as some participants expressed the weariness of what it will mean to become sick during the pandemic and not be a priority for care from health care professionals: “…I wouldn’t want to fall sick right now because I feel that if push comes to shove, I don’t know if I would be on the priority. I don’t know where I would fall basically on the priority line.”

The follow-up interviews and focus group discussions in late 2021 showed that this idea is still alive in the African immigrant communities: fear of not receiving optimal care from healthcare providers because of the color of their skin. Research among African Americans and other minority groups also confirm this weariness and fear of not receiving the best care from healthcare providers and/or being less of a priority. Fear of not receiving optimal care or being less of a priority to receive care is listed as one of the reasons why minorities do not participate in organ donor program (Morgan, 2006; Siminoff et al., 2006; Williamson et al., 2019), and with the new generation of Africans in the diaspora, there is a need to address these concerns. African immigrants’ offspring and young adults pulling from the community memory of what ADOS had experienced and in many cases, continue to experience, has implications for healthcare providers’ trustworthiness. This has serious implications for efforts to increase vaccine uptake in the African immigrant communities.

Limitations and implications

There are limitations to this study. Participants’ reactions and perceptions cannot be generalized due to the sample size included in this analysis. However, the varied locations of participants across multiple states in the US (MD, IL, NY, PA & TX) lends support to the shared commonality of their experiences and reactions. While research among immigrants’ offspring has demonstrated that age of arrival in the US and length of stay in the US has impact on their assimilation and acculturation into US mainstream culture (Li and Wen, 2015), this study did not explore this effect among participants. Participants openly discussed “how they arrived in the US” and compare their experiences in the US to where they migrate from, however, they did not say what impact, if any, it has on their experiences and reactions. It is possible that their multicultural identities may be a product of their acculturation and assimilation in the US.

Conclusion

As an evolving virus with more information becoming available, Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) has proven to be a virus that many are learning to contend with and manage better with more biomedical advancements. The implication of contending with the virus is that more people now believe that it’s not any different from influenza, or maybe the common cold virus. While the pandemic has leveled off, the virus itself continues to and will remain part of the public health normative. Life managing this “new normal” remains a work in progress among underserved communities, including African immigrants and their offspring. Children of African immigrants will play a very crucial role in efforts to increase COVID-19 vaccine uptake as they are able to straddle between two worlds: their parents and the American world they now live in They have shared values with their parents because of their upbringing, and yet they are different from their parents because they are more likely to be acculturated in the American social fabric, developing their own sensibilities which is more representative of their own realities.

The World Health Organization Director-General Tedros Ghebreyesus warned in his speech at the 2024 World Governments Summit that “the next pandemic is a matter of when, not if” (World Health Organization 2024). There is an urgent need to prepare the African immigrant communuities now by engaging them to (1) identify communication mediums they find trustworthy; (2) creating, tailoring, and testing publc health messages to identify which they will respond to; and (3) establishing connections that can be engaged and utilized when there is another pandemic. It is crucial for public health efforts to take multicultural values into consideration when developing initiatives to promote vaccine uptake in these communities for the sake of posterity and, most importantly, with the intention to shape a new reality where COVID-related mortalities are miniscule.
