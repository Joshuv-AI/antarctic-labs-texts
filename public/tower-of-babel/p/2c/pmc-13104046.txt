Photon-Counting Detector Computed Tomography Depicts a Ventricular Tachycardia Substrate
Authors: Ryo Tateishi, Takuro Nishimura, Susumu Tao, Kota Yokoyama, Hiroto Hada, Shinsuke Miyazaki, Tetsuo Sasano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecent studies have demonstrated that delayed enhancement using contrast-enhanced computed tomography corresponds with late gadolinium enhancement on cardiac magnetic resonance imaging. There is growing evidence that late iodine enhancement (LIE) on computed tomography (CT) corresponds to late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) on cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR), thereby enabling characterization of myocardial scarring.1 Photon-counting detector (PCD)–CT has recently been developed, offering a higher resolution than conventional energy-integrating detector CT.2 However, the clinical impact of LIE on PCD-CT for scar-related ventricular tachycardia (VT) ablation has not been evaluated. A nonischemic interventricular septal scar is recognized as a challenging arrhythmogenic substrate, and localized re-entrant VTs can originate within the periaortic septal region.3 Although the arrhythmogenicity of septal scar has been evaluated using CMR, assessment of the periaortic septum remains difficult owing to its relatively thin structure and complex anatomical configuration.

This report describes the intramural VT substrate based on an assessment of the periaortic septal LIE detected by PCD-CT in a patient with cardiac sarcoidosis. We evaluated the spatial relationship between the LIE localization and functional substrate with abnormal electrograms recorded in both ventricles.

Case Presentation

A 49-year-old man with cardiac sarcoidosis presented to our hospital with sustained monomorphic VT. He had presented 17 years earlier with frequent premature ventricular contractions and an impaired cardiac function. Coronary artery disease had been ruled out by coronary angiography. Positron emission tomography/CT demonstrated abnormal uptake within the left ventricle (Supplemental Figure 1). In addition, given the occurrence of sustained VT and thinning of the basal ventricular septum on echocardiography, the patient was diagnosed with cardiac sarcoidosis.4 Although inflammation was controlled with continuous prednisolone therapy (initiated at 30 mg daily and gradually tapered), his left ventricular ejection fraction remained low at 30%. A transvenous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) was placed for secondary prevention. Given amiodarone-induced hyperthyroidism, sotalol (160 mg daily) was prescribed for VT suppression. Nine years ago, he had received ICD shock therapy owing to monomorphic VT, and catheter ablation had been performed. During this initial procedure, radiofrequency energy was delivered to the basal anterior region of the left ventricle. VT was not observed for several years thereafter. However, a recent VT episode requiring ICD shock therapy led to the current hospitalization for a second ablation session.

The clinical VT exhibited a left bundle branch block pattern with a left inferior axis and V3 transition, suggesting an exit site in the superior septal region (Figure 1). Before the procedure, enhanced PCD-CT imaging (Naeotom Alpha, Siemens Healthineers) was performed to evaluate the ventricular substrate. A delayed-phase CT scan was performed 7 minutes after a contrast agent injection. No significant coronary artery stenosis was observed. Late scans showed LIE within the outflow tract bounded by the periaortic septum and right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT). The edge of the LIE was observed as touching the RVOT and periaortic septum, respectively (Figure 2A). A three-dimensional reconstruction using the ADAS 3D system (ADAS LV Medical) showed an extensive LIE with a 10.2-mm width and 7.1-mm height in the superior septal region (Figure 2B). Although no prior studies have established dedicated thresholds for PCD-CT analysis, we explored multiple boundary settings for ADAS 3D reconstruction to visualize LIE (Supplemental Figure 2). Among these, the 40% to 60% boundary—commonly applied in CMR—demonstrated the best spatial concordance with the LIE identified on PCD-CT. However, as this observation is based on a single case, further studies are required to establish a generalized threshold for ADAS-based LIE delineation. CMR could not be performed owing to ICD incompatibility caused by an elevated shock lead threshold.

After the initiation of the electrophysiological study, a substrate map was created using a grid-shaped multielectrode catheter (HD Grid Advisor, Abbott) during sinus rhythm. In the left ventricle, abnormal voltage areas were observed from the periaortic region to the midseptum. An abnormal voltage in the right ventricle was observed in the RVOT and on the tricuspid annular septum. On the isochronal late activation map (ILAM), a line of conduction block with split potentials and a distinct isoelectric segment (>20 ms) was delineated on both the periaortic septum and in the RVOT, suggesting a high arrhythmogenicity (Figure 3).5 Interestingly, simultaneous pacing from the periaortic septum and septal RVOT produced a higher correlation score (86%) than pacing from either site alone (periaorta: 81%, RVOT: 80%). This phenomenon suggested that the exit of the re-entrant circuit may not have been confined to a single cardiac surface, and that the exit wavefront may have involved both the RVOT and periaortic regions simultaneously (Figure 4).

Radiofrequency ablation was performed using a TactiFlex catheter (Abbott) with a temperature limit of 45 °C and an irrigation flow rate of 13 to 30 mL/min. Lesions were created along the line of the conduction block in both the periaortic region and RVOT. Ablation was performed with 40 W in the periaortic region and 30 to 35 W in the RVOT. After the procedure, VT was no longer inducible, indicating a successful ablation. No significant changes were observed on electrocardiogram before or after the procedure (Supplemental Figure 3). The patient was discharged on postprocedure day 4 without any complications, and although there was 1 episode of antitachycardia pacing, the VT burden decreased and he has remained free of ICD shocks for 1 year after the procedure.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating the potential utility of PCD-CT–based LIE for precise localization of myocardial scar and identification of an arrhythmogenic substrate. Unlike conventional energy-integrating detector CT, which detects x-rays through an indirect process, PCD-CT counts each x-ray photon directly and can distinguish its energy. This allows for sharper images, less electronic noise, and reduced blooming artifacts.6

LGE on CMR, a reference standard of the characterization of myocardial scar, is a useful tool for assessing arrhythmogenic substrates in nonischemic cardiomyopathy.7 However, CMR is not always possible in patients with ICDs owing to device incompatibility. In the outflow septal region, the myocardium in our patient was thin, making it challenging to evaluate the LGE. This case demonstrated the size and location of the LIE and highlighted the potential of PCD-CT for identifying VT substrates. This was also supported by the elevated extracellular volume fraction (Figure 5). In addition to the elevated extracellular volume fraction observed in the lateral region of the FDG (18F-fluorodeoxyglucose) uptake on positron emission tomography/CT (shown in Supplemental Figure 1), elevated extracellular volume fraction was also seen on the basal septum in this case, colocalizing with the LIE.

In the present case, the LIE within the intramural septal region suggested the potential formation of a three-dimensional re-entrant VT circuit. This might also be supported by simultaneous pacing from both the periaortic region and RVOT, which yielded the highest pacing score. The three-dimensional re-entrant circuit of periaortic VTs has been reported to be associated with a higher VT recurrence rate after catheter ablation compared to cases with only two-dimensional, planar circuits.3 The strategy for catheter ablation of hemodynamically unstable VTs involves modification of the arrhythmogenic substrate.8 In the present case, ILAM revealed a line of conduction block with split potentials, localized to a confined region in both ventricles that corresponded to the area of the LIE. Preprocedural evaluation with PCD-CT may be useful for enhancing the visualization of the substrate for periaortic VTs. Previous studies have reported that LIE on PCD-CT correlates with scar areas identified by electroanatomical mapping.9 In the present case, the line of conduction block detected by ILAM also showed a spatial correlation with the LIE. These findings suggested that LIE on PCD-CT may be useful for functional substrate characterization. As a control for comparison, we provide PCD-CT images from a patient without structural heart disease and preserved cardiac function (Supplemental Figure 4); no periaortic LIE was observed, supporting the periaortic LIE as a notable finding in the present case. While electroanatomical mapping remains indispensable for VT ablation, this image integration may serve as a practical tool to potentially predict arrhythmogenic substrates before the procedure. This approach may add significant value to procedural planning, although further validation in larger cohorts is required.

Conclusions

We report a case in which an LIE on PCD-CT was instrumental in predicting the periaortic intramural VT substrate. This case underscores the potential for PCD-CT to serve as an alternative to CMR in patients with non–MRI-conditional ICD implants, offering a practical means for a detailed substrate prediction.
