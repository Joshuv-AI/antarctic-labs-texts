From barrier to guide: Exploiting disease-specific hemodynamics for enhanced nanodrug targeting in cardiovascular diseases
Authors: Zirui Wang, Xinran Chen, Yihui Li, Siyu Zhai, Zihan Guan, Wenli Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain the leading cause of global mortality. Conventional systemic drug administration is plagued by issues such as suboptimal bioavailability and off-target effects. As an emerging vehicle, nanoparticles offer the potential to enhance therapeutic efficacy and reduce side effects through their targeted delivery properties. The hemodynamic characteristics under pathological conditions directly determine the success of targeted delivery by regulating the distribution, retention, and extravasation of nanoparticles within the circulation. This review systematically summarizes the pathological characteristics and disease-specific hemodynamic features of common CVDs: atherosclerosis, aneurysms, phlebitis, and venous thromboembolism. It thoroughly examines their critical influence on the delivery behavior of nanoparticles in blood and summarizes three targeted delivery strategies: passive targeting achieved by regulating nanocarrier properties, active targeting realized through the design of stimuli-responsive carriers, and homing effects attained via biomimetic modifications. Additionally, it summarizes relevant computational, experimental, and in vivo imaging methodologies for simulating blood flow dynamics and monitoring nanocarrier behavior. This review aims to provide a systematic perspective for understanding the role of hemodynamics in targeted delivery of nanomedicines, and to offer theoretical foundations and research paradigms for designing more efficient and lesion-selective cardiovascular nano-delivery systems.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain the leading cause of global mortality. Despite significant advances in pharmacological and interventional therapies, achieving precise drug delivery to vascular lesions remains a formidable challenge [1,2]. Conventional systemic drug administration often suffers from suboptimal bioavailability, off-target effects, and inadequate accumulation at pathological sites, ultimately compromising therapeutic efficacy [3,4]. Thus, the imperative to develop lesion-specific delivery strategies capable of circumventing hemodynamic constraints has become increasingly prominent. Nanoscale drug carriers, with their tunable physicochemical properties, can overcome hemodynamic barriers, reduce off-target effects, and enhance drug bioavailability, thereby exhibiting tremendous potential in the targeted delivery of CVDs [5]. However, a critical yet underexplored barrier to effective vascular targeting lies in the complex hemodynamic microenvironment inherent to CVDs.

Pathological alterations in vascular structure profoundly disrupt physiological blood flow patterns, generating aberrant hemodynamic forces. Emerging evidence highlights that the hemodynamic landscape varies significantly across distinct CVDs. Atherosclerotic plaques create localized high-shear zones at stenoses and flow recirculation downstream, while aneurysms exhibit intra-saccular stagnation and complex vortical flows [6,7]. Phlebitis and venous thrombosis are characterized by profound flow stagnation and venous hypertension [8,9]. These forces directly dictate the fate of intravenously administered drug carriers, influencing their stability, margination, adhesion, cellular internalization, and drug release kinetics [10].

This review systematically analyses the interplay between pathological hemodynamics and nanoparticles behaviors in cardiovascular diseases. It outlines disease-specific hemodynamic features and their impact on carrier stability, immune evasion, vascular targeting, endothelial interaction, and drug release. Strategically approaches are evaluated to overcome hemodynamic barriers and improve specific delivery. Relevant computational and experimental methodologies for modeling blood flow and monitoring carrier dynamics are also summarized. The core significance of this review lies not only in profoundly revealing hemodynamics as a key determinant of the in vivo fate of nanoparticles, but also in establishing a systematic cognitive framework linking hemodynamic characteristics, carrier behaviors, and delivery strategies. This provides a robust theoretical foundation and forward-looking technical pathway for overcoming traditional delivery bottlenecks and advancing next-generation intelligent nanoparticles with adaptive capabilities to disease microenvironments.

Pathology of common vascular diseases and special hydrodynamic characteristics of their pathological state

Atherosclerosis

Atherosclerosis is a chronic progressive disease characterized by the formation of plaques within the arterial wall, leading to luminal narrowing and impaired blood flow. The pathogenesis of this disease entails multifactorial mechanisms, including lipid metabolism dysregulation, inflammatory responses, and vascular endothelial dysfunction [11].

Hemodynamic factors play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis and progression of atherosclerosis. Aberrant shear stress serves as a hallmark feature of both atherosclerosis-prone regions and the periplaque microenvironment [12]. Specifically, LSS promotes endothelial activation, inducing a pro-inflammatory and pro-thrombotic gene expression profile, thereby exacerbating vascular inflammation and plaque development [[13], [14], [15]]. Oscillatory shear stress (OSS) contributes to atherosclerosis initiation by disrupting endothelial planar cell polarity, which enhances vascular permeability and leukocyte recruitment, are the key events in early lesion formation [16].Additionally, OSS drives abnormal endothelial cell proliferation, further accelerating disease progression [17]. Atherosclerotic plaque-induced obstruction significantly modifies hemodynamic characteristics in adjacent vascular segments [18].The luminal narrowing caused by plaques leads to markedly accelerated flow velocities within stenotic regions, while downstream of the constriction, substantial reorganization of coronary flow distribution occurs. Within the stenotic segment, the increased flow velocity substantially elevates wall shear stress (WSS) at the lesion site, exhibiting pronounced pulsatile cyclicity [19]. In the post-stenotic region, the sudden increase in cross-sectional area leads to flow separation, generating low shear stress zones and recirculation regions, which are the primary contributors to vortex formation [20]. Particularly in the dilated downstream segment, unstable primary vortices form preferentially at vascular wall indentations, exhibiting periodic detachment and downstream propagation [21]. Notably, in curved or bifurcated stenotic arteries, complex secondary flows and helical flow patterns emerge, substantially enhancing regional hemodynamic intricacy and shear stress heterogeneity [22]. Aneurysm

Aneurysm is a pathological dilation of the vascular wall that may develop in arteries throughout the body, among which cerebral aneurysms warrant particular clinical attention given their propensity to precipitate catastrophic neurological sequelae [23]. The pathogenesis of aneurysms is a complex process involving the interplay of multiple factors, including genetic predisposition, the mechanical and material properties of the vascular wall, and the hemodynamic environment.

Hemodynamic factors play a pivotal role in the formation and progression of aneurysms. Within the aneurysm sac, particularly in regions of flow stagnation are dominated by LSS [24]. Substantial experimental evidence demonstrates that persistently low WSS induces endothelial dysfunction and inflammatory activation, which collectively mediate vascular remodeling, ultimately elevating the risk of aneurysm growth and rupture [25]. In contrast, the aneurysm neck, branch sites, and flow impingement zones are mostly characterized by high WSS, which can damage the vascular wall and contribute to the initiation and expansion of aneurysms. [26].Large or irregularly shaped aneurysms frequently exhibit localized turbulent or quasi-turbulent flow regimes, along with vortex formation [27]. At the aneurysm inlet or neck region, blood flow velocity may exhibit localized acceleration or highly unstable flow, generating vortices and turbulence [28]. Additionally, within the aneurysm sac, blood flow velocity typically decreases markedly, resulting in prolonged relative residence time of blood within the sac. Flow separation and recirculation zones commonly develop within the aneurysm sac, typically accompanied by low flow velocity and low WSS which are strongly associated with thrombus formation [29].These hemodynamic changes are critically important for understanding aneurysm pathogenesis, progression, growth, and rupture risk assessment, and may also significantly influence carrier targeting.

Phlebitis

Phlebitis is an inflammatory vascular disorder characterized by pain, swelling, and redness at the affected venous site, which may be accompanied by fever and chills in severe cases. It is typically induced by infection, trauma, or chemical irritation.

Phlebitis-induced vascular remodeling is a complex process arising from the interplay between vascular inflammation and hemodynamic abnormalities. This process directly alters blood flow characteristics via structural alterations in the vessel wall, such as reduced blood flow, abnormal flow velocity and shear stress, and increased vascular resistance. Inflammatory stimuli promote proliferation, migration, and phenotypic switching of vascular smooth muscle cells, along with excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition. These cellular and matrix changes collectively drive vascular wall thickening and stiffening [30]. Additionally, another manifestation of remodeling is vascular stenosis and dilation. Vascular lumen narrowing (inward remodeling) occurs primarily due to inward encroachment of proliferating vascular wall cells and ECM accumulation, which is the most common scenario. Conversely, in certain cases, inflammation and hemodynamic alterations may also lead to vascular lumen dilation (outward remodeling), as seen in the adaptive remodeling in arteriovenous fistulas to accommodate high blood flow [31]. When vascular wall thickening and luminal narrowing coexist, the resultant luminal stenosis and vessel stiffening significantly elevate vascular resistance. Such pathological conditions directly elevate relative residence time and promote the formation of stasis zones, consequently facilitating platelet activation and thrombosis [32]. Concurrently, the localized acceleration of blood flow and luminal diameter reduction induce pathologically elevated WSS at the stenosis site. Distally, beyond the stenosis or at branching points, flow patterns frequently become disturbed (turbulent or recirculating), creating regions of low or oscillating wall shear stress [33].

Venous thromboembolism

Venous thromboembolism is a complex vascular disorder whose pathogenesis is primarily defined by Virchow's triad of venous stasis, hypercoagulability, and endothelial injury [34]. Venous thromboembolism comprises deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, and is among the major cardiovascular diseases worldwide [35].

Under the pathophysiological conditions associated with venous thrombosis, thrombus formation mechanically obstructs the vessel lumen. This obstruction significantly alters hemodynamic characteristics, including blood flow velocity, WSS and vortex formation in perivascular regions. A primary consequence is an exacerbation of flow stagnation, as thrombus development directly impedes normal blood flow. Research indicates that such venous stasis extends beyond the immediate vicinity downstream of the thrombus, potentially encompassing distal vascular segments. Moreover, venous thrombi can induce localized turbulent flow. Although venous blood flow is predominantly laminar, thrombus-induced luminal narrowing and geometric distortion trigger a shift from laminar to turbulent flow [36].This localized turbulence generates aberrant shear stress, disrupting endothelial cell function. Moreover, following venous thrombosis, significant alterations in shear stress occur at the thrombus site. Specifically, WSS tends to decrease proximal to the thrombus and in perithrombotic regions, while markedly increasing at the thrombus margins and within areas of luminal constriction [37].Such aberrant shear stress induces endothelial dysfunction, promotes inflammatory responses, and may trigger the coagulation cascade.

Collectively, these four prevalent cardiovascular disorders share fundamental alterations in vascular wall structure and function. Endothelial dysfunction represents a shared central pathological characteristic, while inflammatory cell infiltration and cytokine release further exacerbate vascular wall injury and remodeling. Specifically, low shear stress induces pro-inflammatory gene expression in endothelial cells in atherosclerosis, correlates with aneurysm growth and rupture risk in aneurysms, and is linked to blood stasis and thrombus formation in phlebitis and venous thrombosis, respectively.

The influence of complex hydrodynamic conditions on the fate of carriers

Accurately predicting the behavior of blood components​under complex hemodynamic conditions is a significant challenge. Blood demonstrates complex non-Newtonian rheology, properties such as shear-thinning, viscoelasticity, and thixotropy, especially pronounced at low shear rates [38,39]. Upon entering the bloodstream, drug delivery carriers undergo dynamic interactions with plasma proteins, blood cells, and other blood constituents. These interactions can modify carrier surface properties, stability, and aggregation behavior [40]. Therefore, an in-depth analysis of how complex hemodynamic conditions act on carriers, and a clear identification of their specific impacts on carrier stability, distribution, targeting efficiency, and drug release kinetics, constitute the core prerequisite for overcoming the current predicament of targeted drug delivery in cardiovascular diseases and designing tailored optimization strategies(Fig. 2).Fig. 2Schematic overview of pathological hemodynamic influences on nanodrug carrier fate, including stability, immune clearance, margination, target engagement, and intracellular delivery.

The intrinsic stability of carriers

Particle size is one of the key factors affecting the stability of nanocarriers. Small-sized carriers (on the nanoscale) generally exhibit superior permeability, which facilitates the delivery of drugs to target tissues. However, under high blood flow velocity, small-sized carriers may suffer from low margination efficiency. This issue impairs their targeted transport and leads to abnormal distribution in the circulatory system.

The shape of carriers also significantly influences their stability under complex hydrodynamic conditions. Non-spherical carriers display more unpredictable behaviors in complex geometric environments, which may reduce their stability in the bloodstream [50]. Such behavioral uncertainty can cause the carriers to undergo abnormal collisions or stress in specific flow fields, thereby triggering deformation, aggregation, and other phenomena that ultimately compromise their stability and in vivo distribution. In pathological conditions such as atherosclerosis and venous thrombosis, the low-flow environment upstream of the lesion site increases the collision frequency between carriers, making them more prone to aggregation. After aggregation, when passing through uneven regions such as vascular stenosis or plaque surfaces, the aggregated carriers are likely to be physically trapped or blocked, forming microemboli that affect carrier stability. When carriers pass through high-flow, high-shear-stress regions such as narrow blood vessels, larger carriers may deform under strong shear forces [51]. Although deformation may help them pass through narrow regions, it can also alter their structural integrity [52]. In severe cases, this deformation may result in disintegration, premature drug release, or the formation of non-targeted fragments.

The immune clearance issue of carriers in blood vessels

Lesions give rise to the issue of carrier immune clearance. Low blood flow velocity and low shear stress reduce the convective transport efficiency of nanocarriers, prolonging their retention time in the circulatory system. During this process, carrier aggregation leads to increased particle size. This larger size further raises the probability of the carriers being recognized and phagocytosed by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) in organs like the liver and spleen, ultimately shortening their blood circulation time [53] (Fig. 3A). The inherent characteristics of carriers, such as particle size and shape, are also closely related to their immune clearance under specific hydrodynamic conditions [50]. Carriers with larger particle sizes, e.g., exceeding 200 nm, are more likely to be recognized and cleared by the MPS, which shortens their blood circulation time [56]. Additionally, when passing through narrow blood vessels, larger carriers may increase the risk of vascular occlusion, leading to non-specific deposition on the vessel wall. This deposition further enhances their recognition by the MPS and raises the possibility of immune clearance. In terms of shape, changes in stability or abnormal movement states caused by shape can alter the recognition and phagocytosis of carriers by the MPS, thereby linking the impact of inherent carrier characteristics on stability to immune clearance [57].

Carrier margination stage

Pathological hemodynamic conditions can significantly impair the margination efficiency of drug carriers toward target sites, such as atherosclerotic plaques. Margination refers to the lateral drift of suspended particulates toward the vessel wall in flowing blood. This phenomenon is driven by red blood cells (RBCs). Due to their high concentration, RBCs establish a cell-free layer near the vascular center, propelling larger particulates such as leukocytes, platelets and nanocarriers, toward the endothelium, thereby facilitating margination [58]. In large and medium arteries, larger microparticles (0.5–5 μm) experience significant inertial lift forces that drive them toward the vessel wall, a phenomenon exploited for atherosclerotic targeting [42,43]. In this regime, particle size, shape, and density relative to blood are the dominant determinants of margination efficiency. Conversely, in microcirculatory vessels and capillaries, inertial effects are negligible, and margination is governed primarily by RBC exclusion and hydrodynamic interactions within the cell-free layer [58,59]. Here, nanocarrier margination is highly sensitive to RBC deformability, aggregation state, and hematocrit, rather than particle inertia.

The process of margination is a critical prerequisite for enabling drug delivery systems to interact with the diseased vascular endothelium. However, near pathological sites like atherosclerotic plaques, low-shear regions frequently develop. These zones are characterized by diminished flow velocity, disruption of laminar flow patterns, and the potential development of complex vortices or recirculation zones. Although research on margination indicates that elevated flow velocity and shear stress do not markedly suppress carrier margination, it is widely acknowledged that margination efficiency is governed by multiple parameters, including carrier size, density, and the hemorheological effects of RBCs within the flow conduit [58,59]. Atherosclerotic plaques often protrude to cause irregular stenosis of the vascular lumen. This change in geometric morphology disrupts the normal laminar flow pattern and induces the formation of complex eddy currents, recirculation zones, or low-shear regions. In low-shear regions, the blood flow velocity decreases significantly, which disrupts the ordered arrangement of RBCs and makes it difficult to form a stable cell-free layer in the central part of the blood vessel [60]. In contrast, eddy currents and recirculation zones cause carriers to move randomly with the blood flow, preventing their directional lateral migration toward the vessel wall. Even under locally elevated shear conditions in lesion areas, the irregular structure of plaque surfaces further exacerbates blood flow disturbance, which weakens the drifting ability of carriers toward the diseased endothelium and ultimately reduces margination efficiency.

Abnormal interactions between carriers and blood cells represent another key factor hindering margination. The inflammatory response in lesion areas activates platelets and leukocytes, leading to increased expression of adhesion molecules on their surfaces. This makes them more prone to non-specific binding with nanocarriers. Such binding not only alters the physical properties of carriers (e.g., particle size and density) but also causes carriers to be encapsulated by blood cells, losing the momentum to move toward the vessel wall [61]. Meanwhile, in the context of atherosclerosis, RBCs exhibit reduced deformability and increased aggregation, which impairs the normal hemorheological properties of blood [62].

The preceding discussion of carrier margination assumes healthy RBC rheology, wherein flexible, deformable erythrocytes migrate toward the vessel center, establishing a well-defined cell-free layer that facilitates the lateral drift of nanocarriers toward the vascular wall. However, cardiovascular diseases are frequently accompanied by profound alterations in RBC deformability and aggregation propensity, which disrupt this hydrodynamic architecture and, consequently, nanocarrier margination efficiency.

Under pathological conditions such as hyperglycemia, chronic inflammation, and oxidative stress, RBCs undergo significant biomechanical deterioration. In diabetes mellitus, chronic hyperglycemia promotes non-enzymatic glycation of membrane proteins, leading to the formation of advanced glycation end-products that cross-link cytoskeletal components and reduce membrane fluidity. Oxidative stress further exacerbates this stiffening through lipid peroxidation and protein carbonylation. These modifications collectively impair RBC deformability, transforming normally flexible biconcave cells into rigid, poorly deformable entities. Gutierrez et al. demonstrated that as the proportion of stiffened RBCs increases, the fluorescent platelet signal at the channel wall progressively decreases, indicating impaired margination [48]. Their simulations revealed that the inclusion of as little as 30% stiffened RBCs is sufficient to reduce CFL thickness and suppress margination. Notably, individually stiffened RBCs have been observed to marginate toward vessel walls themselves in capillaries and microchannels, further disrupting the exclusion-based transport of nanocarriers [63].

In contrast to stiffening, pathological RBC aggregation exhibits a more complex influence on nanocarrier margination. In inflammatory states, infections, and thrombotic disorders, plasma fibrinogen levels can increase up to fivefold, dramatically enhancing RBC aggregability and producing robust, shear-resistant aggregates [46]. Enhanced RBC aggregation can actually promote the radial migration and wall adhesion of platelet-sized microparticles at low shear rates [64]. Guilbert et al. showed that hyper-aggregating RBCs increased microparticle adhesion to vessel walls by approximately 2–3-fold compared to non-aggregating controls [65]. This phenomenon is particularly relevant in venous and low-flow pathological environments, such as venous thrombosis, aneurysm sacs, and post-stenotic recirculation zones, where shear rates fall below 100 s−1 and RBC aggregates persist [66]. Experimental studies by Coutinho et al. provided direct experimental evidence for near-wall accumulation mechanisms of platelet-size particles in pressure-driven RBC suspension flows, corroborating the aggregation-enhanced margination hypothesis [67].

However, this aggregation-mediated enhancement of margination is not universally beneficial. In arterial flows or at higher shear rates, pathological aggregates may disaggregate incompletely, creating turbulent flow disturbances that disrupt laminar margination patterns. Moreover, excessively large RBC aggregates can increase apparent blood viscosity, elevate flow resistance, and promote non-specific trapping of nanocarriers within the aggregate core rather than facilitating their transport to the wall [46].

Interaction stage between carriers and targets

In stenotic regions where plaques form, elevated blood flow velocity significantly reduces the residence time of drug carriers. For effective therapeutic action, drug carriers typically require sufficient time to undergo margination from the central flow stream, achieve proximity to the vascular endothelium, and subsequently undergo processes such as adhesion, endocytosis, or therapeutic payload release [68] (Fig. 3B). However, excessive flow velocities can rapidly sweep carriers away before they adequately approach or establish specific binding interactions with the plaque microenvironment. Consequently, this diminished residence time severely impairs these critical downstream processes.

Regions characterized by low shear stress and low-flow conditions, such as recirculation zones within atherosclerotic plaques or aneurysms, often prolong carrier residence time, which generally facilitates nanoparticle adhesion. However, this may simultaneously reduce effective contact between carriers and endothelial cells. Conversely, elevated shear stress can rapidly dislodge carriers, diminishing their accumulation at pathological sites and increasing the risk of detachment from the vessel wall, thereby reducing adhesion efficiency. Furthermore, pathologically altered shear stress can modulate the expression of target receptors on endothelial cells, which in turn impairs the ligand-receptor binding affinity of targeted nanocarriers [42,54,69].

Moreover, complex hemodynamic conditions such as turbulence and vortices can profoundly alter carrier transport pathways and spatial distribution at pathological sites. Within aneurysm sacs, flow is typically highly disordered and unstable, characterized by vortices, recirculation zones, and regions of flow stagnation. Vortices may entrap carriers within the aneurysm sac, whereas recirculation zones prolong residence time, enabling enhanced interactions with the vascular wall [70].Additionally, disordered flow patterns can cause heterogeneous carrier distribution within the aneurysm, impeding spatially uniform drug delivery. In the vicinity of venous inflammation and thrombosis lesions, nanocarriers can be entrapped or decelerated within vortex-dominated zones, enhancing opportunities for interaction with the endothelium. However, excessively strong vortices can also promote nanocarrier aggregation or deposition, impairing uniform distribution and targeting efficiency. Turbulent flow can propel carriers rapidly through target regions, significantly reducing residence time and limiting the window for effective binding. Additionally, intense turbulent shear forces can compromise nanocarrier structural integrity or accelerate therapeutic payload release, thereby diminishing targeting efficacy.

For structurally complex venous thrombi or plaques, carriers also encounter significant challenges during the penetration process into the thrombus interior. Venous thrombi exhibit a dense, complex three-dimensional network structure, composed predominantly of fibrin networks, platelets, red blood cells, and leukocytes [71].This intricate architecture severely restricts carrier diffusion, particularly hindering the penetration of larger-sized carriers into such dense matrices [72].Furthermore, drugs can undergo nonspecific binding to fibrin, platelet surface receptors, or other thrombus constituents, resulting in drug sequestration at the thrombus surface. This thereby reduces the quantity of drug available for diffusion into the thrombus core, ultimately leading to subtherapeutic local drug concentrations and compromised therapeutic outcomes.

Internalization and release stages of carriers

For nanocarriers engineered for intracellular activity following targeted delivery, cellular internalization is a critical step in drug efficacy, determining the accessibility of therapeutic agents to their intracellular targets. Fluid shear stress, particularly at low levels, modulates nanocarrier internalization through multiple mechanisms. However, the "acute/chronic" properties and "high/low" magnitudes of shear stress often exhibit complex correlations under different physiological and pathological conditions, and these correlations further affect their regulatory effects on nanocarriers. For instance, the high and acute shear stress commonly found in healthy arteries, the low and stable chronic shear stress typical of atherosclerotic-prone regions, and the extremely high and acute shear stress present at severe vascular stenoses may all impact the interaction between nanocarriers and vascular endothelial cells. Endothelial cell endocytosis of nanocarriers is also regulated by shear stress: acute shear stress in healthy arteries or stenotic areas can either enhance or suppress nanocarrier internalization, while chronic shear stress in atherosclerotic regions may induce phenotypic adaptation in endothelial cells, thereby modulating their uptake efficiency of nanocarriers [73].Studies have demonstrated that low shear stress can enhance endocytosis. For example, low-intensity ultrasound, which generates localized low-shear microenvironments, promotes drug uptake in tumor cells predominantly through the clathrin-mediated endocytic pathway [74]. Although the precise mechanisms are still being elucidated, low shear stress may facilitate receptor-mediated endocytosis by altering cellular membrane mechanics or modulating nanoparticle interactions with cell surface receptors.

Nanocarriers utilize diverse endocytic pathways for cellular entry, including clathrin-mediated endocytosis, caveolae-mediated endocytosis, and macropinocytosis. Shear stress can modulate the specific endocytic pathway utilized by cells, and carrier morphology may significantly influence endocytosis. For instance, Wang et al. employed atomic force microscopy (AFM) to elucidate the dynamic internalization kinetics of rod-shaped nanostructures, highlighting the pivotal role of shape, and shear stress may further modulate these kinetics [75]. Indirectly, low shear stress can impact nanocarrier internalization and distribution through its effects on endothelial cell function. OSS is widely recognized as detrimental to endothelial health, promoting inflammatory responses and upregulating cell adhesion molecules [76].These alterations can indirectly impair nanocarrier binding and internalization. Research has demonstrated that compared to unidirectional shear flow, OSS may alter nanocarrier uptake by modulating endothelial cell mechanotransduction pathways [77].

For drug delivery systems utilizing site-specific release and two-step targeting strategies, careful consideration of hemodynamic influences is required on the release process. Distinct from carriers requiring cellular internalization for therapeutic efficacy, these systems retain the therapeutic payload within nanocarriers until triggered by specific stimuli, enabling spatiotemporally precise release within the target site or in close proximity to target cells [44]. For therapeutics targeting the vascular endothelium or thrombi, the subsequent diffusion and binding of released drugs are critically influenced by the pathological hemodynamic environment. After being released from the carrier, the drug undergoes diffusion, which is a process significantly slower than convective transport by blood flow. This disparity is particularly pronounced in high-flow regions near thrombi or plaques. Consequently, drugs risk convective clearance before achieving therapeutic local concentrations. Moreover, pathological conditions like atherosclerosis are frequently associated with elevated blood viscosity, which further diminishes the drug diffusion rate [78].Furthermore, many therapeutic agents require binding to cell surface receptors for efficacy. However, high-flow velocities and elevated shear stress can significantly hinder drug-receptor binding kinetics and promote desorption of bound drugs via shear-induced detachment.

Furthermore, pathologically elevated shear stress or interactions with blood constituents can trigger premature nanocarrier rupture or degradation, thereby compromising precise drug release [55] (Fig. 3C). Aberrant blood flow velocities, whether excessively high or low, can influence the diffusion of small molecules and their approach to target sites. In stenotic or pathological vessels, blood flow often transitions to turbulent regimes, characterized by vortices and irregular flow patterns. This flow instability promotes heterogeneous distribution of therapeutic agents [54].For carriers designed for intrathrombotic or intraplaque drug release, alterations in the local microenvironment can significantly modulate drug release kinetics and stability, ultimately diminishing therapeutic efficacy at the target site.

Targeting strategies for different diseases under special hemodynamic conditions

Pathological hemodynamics profoundly alter nanocarrier circulation, vascular margination, lesion adhesion and on-site drug release, creating multiple bottlenecks for cardiovascular targeted delivery. Given that atherosclerosis, aneurysm, phlebitis and venous thromboembolism present distinct flow patterns and vascular microenvironments, a universal delivery strategy cannot adapt to all disease scenarios. Accordingly, this section categorically summarizes three mainstream optimization approaches: regulating carrier physical properties, constructing stimulus-responsive systems and developing biomimetic carriers to tackle hemodynamic obstacles, and discusses their application characteristics and design principles for each cardiovascular disease, aiming to realize precise lesion targeting.

Atherosclerosis

The progression of atherosclerosis is closely associated with LSS and OSI, as these hemodynamic abnormalities induce endothelial dysfunction, inflammatory cell infiltration, and plaque formation [79]. In high shear stress regions (e.g., stenotic sites), elevated blood flow velocity reduces the residence time of drug carriers, whereas low shear zones downstream of plaques are prone to flow separation and vortex formation, impairing carrier penetration and retention. Besides direct hemodynamic impacts on endothelial cells, endothelial extracellular vesicles (EVs) are key amplifiers of shear stress-related pathological signals [80]. Abundant surface adhesion proteins promote receptor-mediated endocytosis, and low shear-induced oxidative stress further boosts cellular endocytosis. This EV-driven positive feedback loop fueled by abnormal hemodynamics accelerates atherosclerosis, supporting the development of EV-targeted delivery systems [81,82]. Targeting these characteristics, the following strategies have been extensively investigated.

Passive targeting achieved by regulating the physical properties of carriers

Particle size and shape, as critical physical attributes of both nano- and microparticles, exert significant influence on their stability under complex hemodynamic conditions, such as those near atherosclerotic plaques and thrombi, as well as on their targeted delivery efficiency. Specifically, the immune clearance of carriers targeting atherosclerotic plaque sites is significantly influenced by particle size. However, margination demands a contradictory size selection principle compared to immune clearance. To bridge these two different scenarios, it is essential to recognize that they impose contrasting requirements on particle size.

For immune clearance, carriers exceeding 200 nm in diameter are preferentially recognized and eliminated by the MPS in the liver and spleen, resulting in abbreviated circulation [49]. Although sub-200-nm particles exhibit reduced hepatic clearance and extended circulation times, they undergo nonspecific adsorption under high shear stress [83]. Moreover, ultrafine particles (<10 nm) experience rapid renal clearance via enhanced glomerular filtration, leading to extremely short half-lives [50].Accordingly, empirical evidence from studies suggests that maintaining a particle size of approximately 100 nm generally achieves an optimal balance between circulation persistence and targeting efficiency [49].

In atherosclerosis, particle size distinctly affects margination. Under laminar flow, micrometer-scale carriers (500 nm–2 μm) move from flow core to vessel periphery via inertial forces for enhanced margination, while smaller particles dominated by Brownian motion show poor margination and weak endothelial targeting. Experiments show 2 μm spherical particles have 5 times higher margination efficiency than 100 nm particles [34]. Two size-related mechanisms account for this difference. First, lateral migration relies on the ratio of particle diameter (d) to red blood cell-free layer thickness (δ). Effective margination starts at d/δ ≈ 0.2 and rises sharply when d/δ > 0.5. The 2 μm particles (d/δ ≈ 0.2–1.0) meet the threshold, whereas 100 nm particles (d/δ ≈ 0.01–0.05) stay within the erythrocyte-rich core. Second, particles ≥2 μm have a critical wall shear rate (WSRcrit), above which detachment prevails over adhesion. WSRcrit declines with larger particle size: 1000 s−1 for 2 μm, 500 s−1 for 5 μm and 200 s−1 for 10 μm. In atherosclerotic plaques (WSR ≈ 200–500 s−1), only 2 μm particles remain adhesion-dominant. Together, these quantitative relationships explain why 2 μm represents the optimal particle size for targeting atherosclerotic vasculature [43].

However, smaller particles may be advantageous for targeting macrophages within atherosclerotic plaques. A study of dexamethasone-loaded liposomes showed that 200-nm liposomes outperformed both 70-nm and 500-nm liposomes in terms of delivery efficiency to atherosclerotic lesions and anti-atherosclerotic efficacy [84,85]. This suggests that smaller carriers penetrate the endothelial barrier more readily and are more efficiently internalized by plaque-resident cells. Overall, optimal particle size selection requires balanced consideration of specific therapeutic goals and the pathophysiological characteristics of the target vascular lesions. Microparticles may be superior for applications demanding firm adhesion to the vascular endothelium, while nanoparticles are better suited for traversing biological barriers and facilitating intracellular delivery.

Beyond particle size, carrier geometry profoundly influences its behavior in blood flow and targeting efficiency within atherosclerotic plaques [42,86]. Non-spherical particles exhibit unique flow interactions, resulting in altered circulation profiles, margination capacity, and cellular internalization compared to spherical particles. Additionally, plate-like, rod-shaped, and discoidal particles typically demonstrate higher margination propensity and stronger vascular adhesion than their spherical counterparts [87].

For margination efficiency, computational modeling in three dimensions demonstrates that oblate spheroidal microparticles exhibit the greatest propensity for vascular wall accumulation and stable adhesion, whereas prolate spheroidal microparticles, despite frequent wall contact, show minimal enrichment [47,88]. Animal studies corroborate these findings, showing that microspheres and microrods achieve better delivery outcomes in atherosclerotic vasculature than nanocarriers, with microrods outperforming microspheres. Regarding adhesion capacity, under the low shear conditions characteristic of atherosclerotic plaques, elliptical discs exhibit approximately 6.5-fold greater adhesion at microchannel bifurcations compared to spherical particles [89]. When the long axis of elliptical discs is parallel to flow, drag force decreases by 40–60% and contact area increases 2–3 times, yielding a far higher adhesion-detachment force ratio than spherical particles. At vascular bifurcations with flow separation and recirculation, particle residence time is inversely related to local flow velocity and declines under high shear. Spherical particles show drastically reduced adhesion due to ineffective near-wall migration. By contrast, non-spherical particles keep moving toward vessel walls via lateral forces from tumbling, retaining advantages at high shear. Accordingly, their adhesion superiority over spheres at bifurcations grows from 6.5-fold (low shear) to 11-fold (high shear), with shape-induced differences always more prominent here than in straight channels.

However, research indicates that while platelet-inspired geometries demonstrate enhanced targeting capability, they may concurrently increase the risk of mechanically destabilizing plaques. In contrast, brick-shaped particles show the most favorable safety-efficacy profile, with minimal risks of plaque damage and moderate targeting performance [90]. Consequently, the design of drug carriers should carefully balance targeting efficiency and potential biomechanical risks, selecting the most appropriate geometric shape based on therapeutic requirements.

Active targeting realized via responsive carriers

Responsive nanocarriers can sense and respond to specific biophysical or biochemical signals, enabling on-demand drug release at target sites, which provides crucial support for targeted disease therapy [91]. Among these, the shear stress-responsive property exhibits unique potential in the treatment of atherosclerosis. Atherosclerotic lesion areas exhibit abnormally altered local shear stress due to vascular stenosis and disturbed blood flow, resulting in regions of high shear stress peaks or low shear stress. This pathological feature serves as a key target for the design of shear stress-responsive carriers [52,92]. Karaz and Senses noted that as a fundamental hemodynamic force, the abnormal changes in shear stress within lesion areas can be utilized to construct shear-sensitive drug carriers, laying a theoretical foundation for relevant research and development [52]. Specifically, liposomes have emerged as promising materials for constructing shear-sensitive carriers, attributed to their soft deformability, high biocompatibility, drug controlled-release capability, and shear stress sensitivity. The significantly increased local shear stress at vascular stenosis caused by atherosclerosis can trigger structural changes in liposomes and induce drug release, thereby achieving precise drug delivery [52].

Saeedreza et al. confirmed that shear-responsive nanocarriers undergo conformational transitions in high-shear-stress regions associated with vascular diseases [69]. This addresses the increased bleeding risk caused by the systemic administration of traditional anticoagulants, further verifying their application value. Given that atherosclerotic lesions are mainly concentrated in arterial regions with disturbed blood flow (e.g., vascular bifurcations, curved segments), shear stress-responsive carriers designed for these sites hold substantial research and development potential. Meanwhile, the inflammatory microenvironment induced by low shear stress can be combined with shear stress responsiveness to construct multi-responsive systems. Low shear stress promotes the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) via the Piezo1-mediated mechanosensitive pathway, exacerbating atherosclerosis. Targeting this biological change enables indirect targeted delivery to low-shear-stress regions, expanding the functional dimensions of the carriers [14] (Fig. 4A). For instance, the discoid nanoparticles constructed by Zhou Jing et al. demonstrated significantly superior accumulation capacity compared to spherical nanoparticles in low-shear-stress turbulent regions such as vascular bifurcations, both in in vitro and in vivo blood flow shear stress models. Besides shear stress-responsive delivery systems, molecular targeting peptides are a well-established strategy for atherosclerosis active targeting. These 5–30 amino acid sequences screened via phage display specifically bind biomarkers overexpressed on damaged endothelium [95], and have been widely used in the targeted therapy of tumors, heart failure and atherosclerosis [96,97]. The VHPK peptide series is the most commonly used VCAM-1-targeting ligand. Dosta et al. modified pBAE nanoparticles carrying anti-miR-712 with VHPK, and verified its in vivo targeting in a murine carotid ligation model [98]. This work first confirmed the nanoparticles act exclusively at hemodynamically disturbed vascular sites in one animal model. Differently, E-selectin is rapidly mobilized on inflamed endothelium after pro-inflammatory stimulation, making it ideal for initial capture under high shear. Zukerman et al. adopted a microfluidic model to test E-selectin-targeted nanoparticles across shear stress of 40–300 dyn/cm2 [99]. The results proved such peptides maintain stable ligand-receptor binding at 100–300 dyn/cm2, the typical high wall shear stress in atherosclerotic stenosis.

Homing effect attained through bionics

The biomimetic carrier strategy endows nanocarriers with excellent biocompatibility, prolonged circulation time, immune evasion capability, and active targeting ability by mimicking the properties of natural cells or their components in living organisms [100,101]. In atherosclerosis, commonly employed strategies include the RBC-hitchhiking strategy and immune cell-mimetic strategies [102,103]. Hydrodynamic conditions play a pivotal role in the design and in vivo behavior of biomimetic carriers. RBCs can serve as a "hitchhiking" platform for nanocarriers, significantly enhancing the delivery efficiency of nanocarriers to specific organs [102,104]. Brenner et al. reported an RBC-hitchhiking strategy, where nanocarriers are adsorbed onto the surface of RBCs. Leveraging the inherent property of RBCs to flow toward the center of blood vessels, the nanocarriers are transported over longer distances. Studies have demonstrated that intravenous injection of RBC-hitchhiking liposomes increases liposome uptake by approximately 40-fold compared to free nanocarriers, and by about 10-fold compared to delivery achieved using affinity ligands [105]. Under specific hydrodynamic conditions such as vascular bending, bifurcation, or stenosis, nanocarriers dissociate from the RBC surface and marginate toward the vascular wall [40,106]. The disturbed blood flow and local changes in shear stress within atherosclerotic lesion areas can further facilitate this dissociation and translocation process, ultimately enhancing the targeting effect.

Another important strategy involves nanocarriers mimicking immune cells (e.g., leukocytes) [100,107,108]. Leukocytes interact with corresponding receptors on inflamed endothelial cells to achieve rolling, adhesion, and transendothelial migration [103]. Within atherosclerotic lesion areas, endothelial cells are activated under low shear stress and express high levels of adhesion molecules, which provides an ideal environment for the targeting of leukocyte-mimetic nanocarriers [15]. These carriers can utilize the unique expression pattern of adhesion molecules at inflammatory sites to achieve more effective capture and adhesion in disturbed blood flow, thereby promoting the delivery of drugs into the interior of plaques.

Unlike biomimetic carriers, the cell carrier strategy utilizes cells themselves or cell-derived natural. By virtue of their biocompatibility, inherent targeting potential, or modifiability, these carriers precisely deliver therapeutically active substances or functionally active molecules they inherently carry to lesion sites, thereby achieving therapeutic effects [109]. Hydrodynamic conditions exert a significant influence on the targeting process. Additionally, a research team designed therapeutically enhanced EVs as biologically derived nanoparticles. They loaded microRNA-145 (miR-145) into these EVs using ExoMotifs. Combined with the effects of hydrodynamic factors such as blood flow velocity and vascular wall shear stress, this strategy effectively alleviates atherosclerosis [94] (Fig. 4C).

Aside from modifying EV cargos and surfaces, biomechanical preconditioning of source cells is another effective approach to produce potent therapeutic EVs. Li et al. treated endothelial cells with physiological laminar shear stress (15 dyn/cm2) to induce secretion of EVs rich in immunomodulatory miR-34c-5p [81]. To improve targeting, they conjugated hyaluronic acid (HA) to EVs via click chemistry for binding CD44 on inflammatory macrophages in plaques. In ApoE−/− mice, HA-modified LSS-EVs reduced plaque area from 31.84% to 13.37% and accumulated more abundantly in aortic arch and abdominal aorta [80,110,111]. Notably, fluid shear stress serves a dual function in this paradigm: it induces the production of therapeutic EVs during the preparative phase and, in vivo, governs the hemodynamic microenvironment that modulates the deposition and retention of EVs at target lesion sites.

Although biomimetic and stealth modifications greatly optimize delivery efficiency, their long-term safety and repeat administration performance cannot be ignored. The immune evasion advantages of the aforementioned biomimetic modification strategies are largely based on short-term experimental data following single-dose administration [112]. In fact, PEGylated nanocarriers can induce the production of anti-PEG IgM antibodies by splenic marginal zone B cells upon repeated intravenous injection; upon subsequent administration, these antibodies activate the classical complement pathway, triggering the accelerated blood clearance (ABC) phenomenon [113,114]. To address these challenges, Liu et al. pioneered the use of branched PEG lipid derivatives (DSPE-mPEG) as an alternative to conventional linear PEG [53]. Xiao et al. developed high-density brush-shaped polymer lipids that significantly reduce anti-PEG antibody binding by increasing steric hindrance [115]. Additionally, the use of alternative stealth polymers such as polyglycerol, polysarcosine, or zwitterionic polymers to replace PEG [116], as well as pre-infusion with high molecular weight free PEG prior to the first therapeutic dose to neutralize circulating anti-PEG antibodies [117], have also proven effective in circumventing the ABC phenomenon. For cell membrane-coated biomimetic nanocarriers, although "self" signals such as CD47 can achieve immune evasion to a certain extent, the introduction of exogenous membrane proteins still carries the potential risk of inducing immune responses [118,119]. Therefore, the use of autologous cell-derived membrane materials, elimination of denatured membrane proteins through bioengineering approaches, or construction of double-membrane coating structures to further shield immunogenic recognition sites represent important directions for enhancing the clinical applicability of biomimetic carriers [120].

Aneurysm

The hemodynamics within an aneurysm are characterized by blood flow recirculation, low WSS, and localized turbulence, with significantly prolonged residence times particularly in saccular aneurysms [28,121]. These hemodynamic features may not only promote thrombus formation but also offer unique opportunities for targeted drug delivery strategies.

Passive targeting achieved by regulating the physical properties of carriers

Particle size exhibits a dualistic function in aneurysm targeting, its selection is dictated by the specific type of therapeutic agent to be delivered. This phenomenon arises because within the aneurysm sac, diminished flow velocities and vortical flow patterns promote preferential retention and deposition of larger particles in localized regions [122]. Unlike high-shear stenotic regions in atherosclerosis, aneurysmal dilatations harbor flow stagnation zones that facilitate nanocarrier accumulation. However, effective therapy ultimately requires nanocarriers to achieve transmural penetration targeting vascular smooth muscle cells or inflammatory cells within the aneurysm wall. Although pathological wall remodeling may enhance permeability, particles exceeding 500 nm face significant penetration barriers. Consequently, size optimization must balance intra-saccular retention against transmural penetration capacity. If the goal is to embolize the aneurysm or deliver coagulants, larger particles are more likely to deposit in low-velocity regions. Conversely, if the objective is to repair the aneurysm wall or deliver anti-inflammatory drugs, smaller nanoparticles may be required to enhance penetration.

Hydrodynamic rules for carrier design in low-shear recirculating flow of aneurysms differ greatly from those for atherosclerosis. Epshtein et al. found 2 μm particles reached a localization index of 0.86 in aneurysm sacs, while 200 nm particles showed no targeted deposition [123]. Larger particles have a Stokes number of 0.1–1, allowing efficient trapping inside stable vortices. Singh et al.’s CFD results revealed that at helicity H > 40 m2/s2, particle normalized residence time (NRT) was 0.71–0.78, much higher than the NRT below 0.2 in vortex-free narrow-neck models [124]. Particles also need near-blood density for neutral buoyancy: polystyrene particles (ρ = 1.05) distributed evenly in sacs, yet PLGA particles (ρ = 1.3) settled only on gravity-dependent sides, as large density differences impair uniform distribution [125]. Collectively, these quantitative relationships indicate that aneurysm-targeting carriers should preferentially adopt a design featuring micrometer-scale dimensions, neutral buoyancy, and a Stokes number in the 0.1–1 range, so as to fully exploit the natural retention effect of recirculating vortices.

Nanocarrier geometry critically determines hemodynamic behavior and deposition patterns within aneurysms. In recirculation zones and vortical flows, non-spherical particles may exhibit distinct orientation and motility patterns compared to spherical particles [126]. For instance, rod-shaped or disc-shaped particles may be more readily captured or aligned in specific flow fields, thereby increasing their contact probability with the aneurysm wall. Notably, Jiang et al. demonstrated that spindle-shaped nanoparticles, owing to their high curvature characteristics, exhibit advantages in overcoming pressure differentials and enhancing tissue penetration, potential benefits for drug delivery requiring aneurysm wall penetration [127]. Nevertheless, the intricate 3D hemodynamic complexity of aneurysms complicates precise prediction of non-spherical particle behavior, necessitating high-fidelity computational fluid dynamics simulations for morphology optimization design [122]. Cooley et al.’s computational and experimental studies have further elucidated the quantitative relationships underlying this difference. In shear flow, the tumbling of non-spherical particles produces an effective migration coefficient 3–5 times greater than that of spherical counterparts. Additionally, for particles with an aspect ratio above 2–3, their effective contact area with the vessel wall rises by roughly 2 to 4 times, causing a non-linear increase in the adhesion-to-detachment force ratio [127].

Surface modification of carriers is another strategy in addition to particle size and shape optimization. To prolong the circulation time of nanocarriers in the bloodstream and reduce nonspecific clearance, stealth modifications such as PEGylation can be employed [50]. Extended circulation enhances the opportunity for nanocarriers to deposit at the target site, particularly in aneurysm regions with relatively low blood flow velocity.

Active targeting realized via responsive carriers

The hemodynamics inside an aneurysm is highly complex. Local regions of high shear stress exist at the aneurysm neck or flow impingement points, while regions of low shear stress or stagnation are present within the aneurysm lumen [92]. Leveraging this complex hemodynamic environment, several targeting strategies have been developed, primarily falling into three categories: shear-targeting, hydrogel embolization, and integrin targeting.

The first strategy involves designing responsive nanocarriers sensitive to the mechanical stress of blood vessel walls to target the local high shear stress distribution in aneurysm regions with a high risk of rupture. Some studies have constructed idealized three-dimensional geometric models for aortic aneurysms and used these models to investigate shear-activated targeted nanoparticle drug delivery strategies [54]. By converting hemodynamic parameters from hindering factors into activation mechanisms, the accumulation of drugs at lesion sites is enhanced. Gazendra Shakya et al. designed microbubbles to rupture or release drugs under specific shear stress, achieving targeted therapy for abdominal aortic aneurysms.

A second approach utilizes injectable biomaterials such as shear-thinning hydrogels (STHs), which are constructed with gelatin as the matrix and exhibit the typical property of shear thinning and static recovery. Under the action of external force, their system structure changes and presents a fluid state similar to a solution. After reaching the aneurysm, the removal of external force enables the hydrogel to quickly recover its solid structure, which specifically fills the aneurysm lumen and achieves precise localization at the lesion site [128].

Lastly, the integrin targeting strategy provides a new perspective for the treatment of diseases including aneurysms by regulating the bidirectional signal transmission between cells and the extracellular matrix (ECM). Luo et al. coated mesoporous Prussian blue nanoparticles with macrophage membranes, utilizing the ligand–receptor binding between integrin α4β1 on the membrane surface and VCAM-1 on activated endothelial cells to achieve active targeting to AAA [129]. At vascular bifurcations and curvatures, αv and β3 integrins bind to adapter molecules under shear stress, activating the Ras-MAPK pathway and thereby influencing cell behavior. Some drug or carrier designs based on integrin targeting utilize this mechanism to achieve targeting of pathological cells in these regions, providing a targeted strategy for aneurysm treatment [130].

Homing effect attained through bionics

Under the influence of fluid mechanics, the use of biomimetic carriers such as RBC membranes, platelet membranes, and leukocyte membranes for targeting aneurysms represents a cutting-edge strategy in current drug delivery and therapeutic research. By mimicking the flexibility and surface properties of natural cells, biomimetic carriers can better adapt to complex blood flow, reduce non-specific adsorption and immune clearance, and enhance enrichment efficiency in aneurysm regions. The core of biomimetic carriers lies in applying cell membrane coating technology to encapsulate nanoparticles with natural cell membranes, mimicking the surface properties of natural cells [131].

RBC membranes, through the binding of their surface CD47 protein to SIRPα on macrophages, send out "self" signals to achieve immune evasion. They maintain good stability in the fluid environment, extend blood circulation time, and increase the chance of reaching aneurysm lesions [132]. Meanwhile, RBC membrane coating substantially enhances the flexibility of nanocarriers, rendering their deformability close to that of natural RBCs and thereby altering their orientation and migration trajectories in shear flow fields. Studies have demonstrated that flexible nanoparticles possess superior near-wall accumulation capacity compared with their rigid counterparts, as they can more effectively respond to local flow field gradients and embed into the edges of the RBC layer [133]. Leukocyte membranes utilize their chemotaxis and infiltration capabilities in inflammatory responses to deliver drugs to the inflammatory microenvironment, targeting the pathological process of inflammatory cell infiltration in aneurysms [135]. In the fluid mechanics environment, blood flow shear stress, vascular structure, and the carriers' own size, shape, and surface properties all affect transport efficiency and targeting ability [132]. Existing experimental evidence indicates that in diseases such as abdominal aortic aneurysms (AAAs), inflammatory molecules like VCAM-1 can serve as targets [136]. Studies on the ability of platelet membrane carriers to target thrombi and damaged endothelium, microbubble-mediated miR-126 delivery for inflammation inhibition, and tea polyphenol nanoparticles targeting AAAs all highlight the potential of this strategy [137,138]. Notably, hybrid membrane strategies that integrate the advantages of different cell membranes are also widely explored [139].

Under specific fluid mechanical factors, cell carriers also constitute a highly promising cutting-edge strategy. Endothelial progenitor cells play a crucial role in vascular repair, angiogenesis, and endovascular therapy of aneurysms. EPCs can migrate to vascular injury sites and differentiate into mature endothelial cells to repair the inner vascular wall. Meanwhile, they can respond to changes in WSS, migrate to regions with low WSS, and repair damaged endothelial cells [140].

Phlebitis

Passive targeting achieved by regulating the physical properties of carriers

The hemodynamic profile of phlebitis is characterized by low wall shear stress, relatively reduced flow rates, and increased vascular permeability resulting from localized inflammation [139]. Particle size represents a critical factor in the context of targeted delivery for phlebitis. Optimizing particle size for phlebitis targeting requires navigating a trade-off between two key hemodynamic features: low shear stress and high vascular permeability.

Under low-shear conditions, nanoparticle margination is generally less effective compared to that in high-shear arterial flows. However, the slower bloodstream allows extended interaction time between larger carriers and the vascular wall [126]. Cooley et al. showed that in the presence of red blood cells, micron-sized non-spherical particles exhibit enhanced margination and adhesion compared to their nanoscale spherical counterparts. This implies that employing moderately larger carriers may improve contact with inflamed endothelium in the low-shear environment typical of phlebitis. Nevertheless, excessively large particles may still risk nonspecific clearance and capillary entrapment. Conversely, increased vascular permeability associated with phlebitis promotes enhanced extravasation of nanoparticles into inflamed tissues [141]. Hence, selecting an optimal particle size involves balancing efficient margination under low-flow conditions with the ability to extravasate effectively into target inflammatory sites.

The shape of nanocarriers also plays a critical role in their targeting efficacy within phlebitis-affected vessels. Under low-shear stress conditions, particle shape can influence near-wall behaviors such as orientation and rolling dynamics [89] (Fig. 5A). Specifically, non-spherical particles can enhance adhesion efficiency to inflamed endothelial cells by either increasing contact area with the vessel wall or altering their rotational dynamics in blood flow [126]. However, systematic studies examining how different nanocarrier shapes perform under the distinct hemodynamic environment of phlebitis are still scarce, underscoring the need for further research in this area.

Beyond particle size and shape, carrier stiffness is another key physical parameter, whose optimal selection is highly dependent on the local shear rate of the target vein. Fish et al. reported low-modulus particles (23 kPa) had much better adhesion than rigid ones (508 kPa) under low shear, with their in vivo venous adhesion 127% higher. The trend reversed under high shear, where rigid particles achieved 2.8–4.8-fold higher adhesion [142]. Deformable hydrogel microparticles can carry nanomedicines; their excellent margination delivers nanoparticles to vessel walls with dozens of times higher efficiency, and soft particles adhere for over 1 h [143]. For phlebitis targeting in low-shear venous environments, micron-sized soft particles (10–100 kPa) are optimal for effective margination and long-term adhesion.

Effective carrier modification for phlebitis involves two complementary approaches: enhancing circulation longevity and enabling active targeting. Similar to arterial systems, polyethylene glycol (PEG) modification prolongs systemic circulation by reducing clearance by the MPS. The surface charge of carriers should also be optimized to minimize nonspecific binding, with neutral or slightly negatively charged carriers typically exhibiting longer circulation durations [50]. Beyond these passive targeting strategies, the pathological environment of phlebitis itself can be leveraged for active targeting. Phlebitis is often marked by activated endothelium and upregulation of inflammation-related adhesion molecules such as P-selectin, E-selectin, ICAM-1, and VCAM-1 [141,144]. Conjugating carriers with ligands that specifically target these adhesion molecules enables selective binding to inflamed venous endothelial cells [44,141].

Active targeting realized via responsive carriers

Shear stress in veins is typically low and exhibits little variation. Therefore, the response to shear stress is less prominent in phlebitis treatment compared to atherosclerosis. However, if phlebitis is accompanied by thrombosis that causes local stenosis, regions with increased shear stress may occur, and shear-sensitive carriers could then play a role. For example, a shear-responsive nano-drug delivery system disclosed in Chinese Patent CN 111840527 B utilizes β-cyclodextrin (β-CD) to achieve responsive and sustained drug release under high shear stress at thrombus sites. By leveraging the host-guest inclusion interaction between β-CD and adamantane, fucoidan supramolecular self-assembled nanoparticles containing β-CD vesicles are prepared, and urokinase is encapsulated within them. This enables site-specific drug release at thrombus sites, which can be used for the treatment of thrombosis-associated phlebitis.

Stasis triggers rapid P-selectin exposure and VCAM-1 upregulation on endothelium, offering potential targets for peptide delivery. However, research on targeting peptides specifically for phlebitis remains nearly absent. Nonetheless, a few studies have provided proof-of-concept for the translation of targeting peptide strategies from the arterial to the venous system. Sakhalkar et al. modified PLA-PEG microparticles with a PSGL-1-mimetic peptide, yielding 10-fold higher adhesion to P-selectin in mouse post-capillary venules [145]. Furthermore, the mZD7349 peptide has demonstrated VCAM-1-dependent targeted endocytosis in human umbilical vein endothelial cells [146]. These preliminary lines of evidence suggest that VCAM-1- and P-selectin-targeting peptides, already well validated in the arterial system, hold potential for technological translation to the phlebitis indication, though their targeting efficiency and selectivity need to be further validated under venous-specific low-shear flow conditions.

Homing effect attained through bionics

For the treatment of phlebitis, two major biomimetic carrier strategies have been developed based on the functions of different blood cells, primarily categorized into leukocyte-mimetic systems, RBC-based carrier platforms, and therapies involving peripheral blood stem cells (PBSCs). The most critical biomimetic strategy for phlebitis focuses on nanocarriers capable of mimicking immune cells. Inflammation is the core of phlebitis, characterized by the recruitment and infiltration of immune cells to the inflamed site. Leukocytes possess an inherent ability to target inflamed blood vessels: through interactions between various adhesion molecules (e.g., LFA-1) on their surface and corresponding receptors (e.g., ICAM-1, E-selectin, P-selectin) on inflamed endothelial cells, they achieve rolling, adhesion, and transendothelial migration [107,108]. Exploiting this mechanism, Palomba et al. developed leukocyte-like vectors by transferring leukocyte membranes onto nanoparticles, endowing the nanoparticles with multiple key receptors that interact with endothelial cells on their surface. This mediates the accumulation of the vectors in the inflammatory microenvironment [107](Fig. 5C).

In contrast, nanocarriers coated with RBC membranes can also play a role in phlebitis treatment through a different mechanism. Although venous blood flow velocity is relatively low, RBC membrane coating enables nanocarriers to have a prolonged circulation time and reduced clearance by the MPS, thereby increasing their chance of accumulating in inflamed venous regions. A longer circulation time is particularly important for the treatment of chronic or persistent phlebitis. Beyond simple coating, despite the differences in hydrodynamic conditions between veins and arteries, the RBC-hitchhiking strategy is also applicable to phlebitis. RBC-Hitchhiking helps nanocarriers effectively avoid non-specific clearance before entering the inflamed region. Once reaching the inflamed region, the activation of inflamed endothelial cells and the expression of adhesion molecules may promote the dissociation of nanocarriers from the RBC surface and their deposition on the inflamed venous wall.

The therapeutic efficacy of PBSCs in phlebitis is realized through a synergistic combination of targeted carrier delivery and the cells' multifaceted repair mechanisms. Carriers achieve targeted enrichment and efficient release of loaded PBSCs by responding to the abnormal hemodynamic characteristics of the phlebitis region. PBSCs can further chemotax towards the damaged intima relying on their inherent homing ability, which synergizes with the hydrodynamic targeted delivery function of the carriers. Once delivered, PBSCs exert their therapeutic effects, primarily through their ability to differentiate into vascular endothelial cells, which is crucial for repairing damaged venous intima. Additionally, PBSCs can secrete a variety of pro-angiogenic factors through paracrine mechanisms, stimulating the proliferation, migration, and lumen formation of vascular endothelial cells, and improving blood supply and vascular density at the phlebitis site [147].

It should be emphasized that under repeated dosing conditions, PEGylation may also trigger complement activation-related pseudoallergy (CARPA) [148]. With the widespread use of PEG in COVID-19 mRNA vaccines and other products, the prevalence of pre-existing anti-PEG antibodies in the population reaches 24%-76%, and some patients may even experience ABC phenomena upon first-dose administration [149,150]. Anti-PEG antibodies can also compromise liposome membrane integrity via membrane attack complex formation, causing premature drug release. In response, Sanchez et al. employed poly(2-ethyl-2-oxazoline) lipids as PEG alternatives to avoid the ABC phenomenon [151]. Berger et al. utilized poly(vinylpyrrolidone) derivatives to achieve low-immunogenicity delivery [152].

Venous thrombosis

Venous thrombosis, which includes deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, is primarily associated with Virchow's triad: venous stasis, vascular wall injury, and hypercoagulability. From a hemodynamic standpoint, thrombi often develop in areas of very low or stagnant flow, such as valve sinuses or compressed venous segments. The extremely low shear stress in these regions, combined with the structurally complex nature of thrombi, poses significant challenges to the transport, penetration, and targeting of nanocarriers [29].

Passive targeting achieved by regulating the physical properties of carriers

In the field of thrombus drug delivery, nanoparticle design must holistically consider hemodynamic conditions, thrombus architecture, as well as particle size, morphology, and surface modifications to optimize targeting efficacy and penetration depth. In regions of stagnant or extremely low blood flow, particles have extended interaction time with the vascular wall or thrombus surface, making nanoparticle margination less of a limiting factor. However, thrombi exhibit a dense and heterogeneous internal structure consisting of fibrin meshes, aggregated platelets, and entrapped red blood cells. Effective drug delivery to the thrombus core requires nanocarriers to penetrate this complex architecture [29]. During this penetration process, carriers must not only be sufficiently small to traverse the pores but also overcome the physical resistance posed by the dense matrix, such as interstitial fluid pressure. Thus, smaller nanoparticles (typically under 100 nm) are advantageous for permeating the porous matrix within thrombi and achieving deep penetration. Nevertheless, excessively small particles may undergo rapid systemic clearance, underscoring the need to balance circulation persistence with penetration efficacy.

In regions of very low or stagnant flow, particle morphology significantly influences both deposition onto the thrombus surface and subsequent penetration into its interior [89]. Non-spherical particles, such as rod-like or disc-shaped particles, can improve penetration efficiency by modifying their movement through the porous structure of the thrombus. Cooley et al. suggested that non-spherical particles exhibit enhanced margination and adhesion under flow conditions, making them particularly suitable for targeting early-stage thrombi or peripheral regions of developing clots [126]. Jiang et al. reported that spindle-shaped nanoparticles show superior ability to overcome interstitial fluid pressure, offering relevant implications for penetrating dense thrombotic structures [127].

Stealth modifications like PEGylation, combined with neutral or slightly negative surface charges, reduce nonspecific interactions and extend the circulation half-life of nanocarriers for targeted venous thrombus delivery [50]. Venous thrombi are characterized by the exposure of fibrin, activated platelets, and thrombus-associated proteins [144]. Conjugating nanocarriers with ligands that target these thrombus markers such as fibrin-binding peptides, antibodies against platelet glycoprotein receptors, or inhibitors of coagulation factors enables highly specific binding to the thrombus.

Active targeting realized via responsive carriers

Shear stress within venous thrombotic regions is generally very low, limiting the utility of physical signal-based targeting strategies such as those responsive to shear or pressure. However, in cases where a thrombus causes complete vascular obstruction, a pressure gradient may develop proximal to the occlusion, thereby enabling the design of pressure-sensitive carriers. Alternatively, external physical stimuli such as ultrasound, have been investigated to trigger drug release from nanocarriers, offering a means to circumvent the restrictive hemodynamic conditions within thrombi. Lyu et al. highlighted the use of active synthetic micro-rotors for drug delivery, whose unique motion and associated vertical flow fields may enhance drug penetration and mixing inside thrombi [153]. Although this method does not directly exploit the intrinsic hemodynamics of thrombi, it provides a conceptual strategy for circumventing intrathrombotic fluidic barriers through external physical forces.

In the study of targeting peptides for venous thrombosis, the pathological process of blood stagnation provides two key targetable molecular targets: overexpressed P-selectin and GPIIb/IIIa on activated platelets, and fibrin accumulated within thrombi. Most relevant strategies focus on arterial thrombosis. Zhao et al. developed dual-targeting peptide MTCA-KKV; its self-assembled nanoparticles (<100 nm) bound activated platelets and inhibited arterial thrombosis in rats in a dose-dependent manner [154]. Similarly, Liu et al. utilized CREKA peptide-conjugated pH-responsive nanoparticles loaded with lumbrokinase to achieve fibrin-targeted imaging diagnosis and targeted thrombolysis in a FeCl3-induced mouse carotid artery thrombosis model [155]. These approaches are adaptable to venous thrombosis. Venous stasis rapidly exposes P-selectin on activated endothelium and platelets, acting as an early target. Fibrin, stably retained in venous thrombi, allows a longer targeting window. Notably, the CREKA peptide has demonstrated efficient targeting capability to fibrin deposits on the luminal surface of atherosclerotic plaques [156]. Given that fibrin is also present in venous thrombi as a targetable substrate, this suggests its potential applicability for targeting venous thrombosis.

Homing effect attained through bionics

In venous thrombosis targeting, biomimetic carrier strategies are primarily based on three cell types: platelets, RBCs, and immune cells. Platelet-mimetic carriers play a crucial role in the targeting of venous thrombosis. Platelets are core components in thrombus formation: they bind to fibrinogen and fibrin via multiple surface receptors (e.g., GPIIb/IIIa) to form thrombi, and they possess an inherent tendency to home to and bind with thrombi [157]. Platelet membrane-mimetic nanoparticles primarily mimic the surface markers of platelets, thereby replicating the structure and function of platelet membranes [158]. This ultimately enables targeted delivery of drugs to specific cells. Carriers can achieve targeting by modifying their surface with receptors such as glycoprotein Ib (GPIb) and integrin αIIbβ3. These receptors interact with adhesion molecules (e.g., VWF, fibrinogen) in vascular endothelial injury regions or plaques to facilitate targeting [159]. Meanwhile, under the action of shear stress, these receptor-ligand bindings may undergo dynamic activation or conformational changes, enhancing binding strength [160]. Furthermore, by encapsulating therapeutic agents within the nanoparticle core and coating the particle surface with platelet membranes, the resulting carriers can specifically bind to target cells in the bloodstream while effectively minimizing off-target effects [161].

Beyond platelet-mimetic carriers, nanocarriers coated with RBC membranes and the RBC-hitchhiking strategy can also function in venous thrombosis targeting. RBC membrane coating endows nanocarriers with prolonged circulation time and reduces clearance by the MPS, thereby increasing their chance of accumulating in thrombus regions [50,162]. Given the close synergistic relationship between inflammation and thrombosis, nanocarriers mimicking immune cells may also play a role in venous thrombosis treatment [144]. By leveraging the unique expression pattern of adhesion molecules at inflammatory sites, these carriers achieve more effective capture and adhesion in low-flow environments, thereby promoting drug delivery to thrombi or the surrounding inflammatory regions.

Besides the aforementioned carriers, EVs participate deeply in venous thromboembolism pathogenesis. Slow blood flow impairs splenic clearance of circulating EVs and facilitates their adhesion to endothelium. Hypoxia in venous valve pockets under stasis further boosts endothelial EV secretion [163]. Low shear stress and resultant oxidative stress enhance endothelial endocytosis, raising local EV uptake [80]. As the predominant circulating EVs, platelet-derived EVs express integrins and stably bind vessel walls under low shear, amplifying procoagulant signals [164]. However, it must be acknowledged that current EV research in VTE remains primarily focused on cargo characterization, while systematic studies on how hemodynamic forces regulate EV biogenesis, targeting, and uptake in the venous context are very limited, a gap that warrants further investigation.

It should be noted that the targeting efficiency of membrane-coated nanoparticles cannot be exclusively attributed to biological receptor–ligand interactions. Membrane coating simultaneously modifies multiple physical parameters of the carrier, including hydrodynamic diameter, surface charge, membrane fluidity, and particle deformability, all of which are known to influence margination dynamics, near-wall accumulation, and adhesion kinetics under blood flow conditions [165]. Recent studies on RBC-membrane-coated and platelet-membrane-coated nanoparticles have demonstrated that membrane-derived mechanical properties can substantially alter particle transport behavior independent of specific molecular recognition mechanisms [166]. For example, RBC membrane coating improves particle flexibility and circulation persistence, whereas platelet membrane coating modifies shear-dependent vessel wall interactions and near-wall drift behavior [[166], [167], [168]]. To avoid confounding these dual mechanisms, several complementary approaches can be employed to differentiate biological targeting from hydrodynamic enhancement. First, control systems using protein-denatured membrane-coated nanoparticles can preserve particle size, surface charge, and deformability while eliminating receptor functionality. Enhanced vascular accumulation observed in such systems would indicate a dominant contribution from physical transport mechanisms [169]. Second, microfluidic flow platforms lacking endothelial receptors can isolate pure hemodynamic effects, where increased wall accumulation of membrane-coated carriers reflects improved margination rather than biological binding. Third, quantitative analyses of margination velocity and adhesion–detachment kinetics under graded wall shear stress can distinguish size- and deformability-dependent transport from ligand–receptor-mediated adhesion, which typically exhibits shear-dependent saturation behavior. Finally, dual-fluorescence in vivo tracking combined with lesion hemodynamic mapping can reveal whether particle accumulation correlates primarily with disturbed-flow regions or with receptor-rich inflammatory endothelium. Collectively, these approaches provide a theoretical framework for decoupling biological and physical contributions to the targeting performance of biomimetic nanocarriers.

Summary and analysis of the aforementioned strategies

Taking a comprehensive view of the targeting strategies across the aforementioned diseases, the utilization of hemodynamics in current carrier design can be categorized into three progressive levels. The first level treats fluid mechanics as an obstacle, mitigating its adverse effects through strategies such as particle size optimization and PEGylation—this remains the design logic of most current approaches. The second level begins to actively exploit flow characteristics to assist delivery, the erythrocyte-hitchhiking strategy leverages the directional movement of red blood cells in the flow core to transport carriers, micron-sized carriers achieve recirculation retention through matching the Stokes number with vortex time scales, and carrier modulus is tailored according to the local shear rate at the target site. Hydrodynamic factors gradually transition from design constraints to design inputs. The third level directly utilizes hemodynamics as a functional trigger: shear-responsive liposomes undergo structural transformation at stenotic regions under high shear to achieve site-specific drug release, discoidal nanoparticles exploit resonance matching between their tumbling period and disturbed flow vortices to enhance accumulation, and shear-thinning hydrogels flow under injection shear and revert to a solid state upon entering the aneurysm sac to achieve embolization. These three levels collectively demonstrate that the role of hemodynamics in carrier design has progressively shifted from a passive barrier to an active guide, with the third level best exemplifying this core concept. The above design strategies all need to be verified and optimized through relevant simulation and experimental methods, which will be discussed in the next chapter.

Methods for simulating the behavior of nanocarriers under blood hydrodynamics conditions

Targeting strategies for various vascular diseases need to be based on their unique pathological and hydrodynamic characteristics, and optimization of carrier design is inseparable from advanced research methods. Hemodynamics and the study of carriers influenced by it are critical components in the field of targeted drug delivery, requiring a variety of methodologies such as computational fluid dynamics (CFD), modeling and simulation, and in vivo experiments. Through the comprehensive application of such methods, it is possible to simulate the special fluid dynamics conditions caused by various cardiovascular diseases in the human body to the greatest degree, thereby enabling the examination and analysis of the behavior of targeted drugs in the body and significantly improving the design and optimization capabilities of drug delivery carriers(Fig. 6).Fig. 6Simulation, modeling, and experimental imaging technology system for nanocarriers under hydrodynamic conditions.

Computational fluid dynamics models

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) models primarily rely on the Navier-Stokes equations to describe the flow of blood as fluid. In practical applications, researchers typically select different model optimization schemes based on the specific problem at hand [170].Non-Newtonian fluid models can more accurately describe the rheological properties of blood, which is crucial for understanding local hemodynamics. Saqr compared Newtonian, power-law, and quasi-mechanical blood viscosity models and confirmed the significant impact of model selection on the simulation of cerebral aneurysms [171].

Given the multi-component nature of blood containing blood cells, multiphase flow models must be introduced. The fluid-particle dynamics simulations developed by Umbarakar and Kleinstreuer provides new insights into studying thrombus formation and drug delivery. Considering the elastic properties of blood vessel walls, fluid-structure interaction models can simulate the interaction between blood vessels and blood. Tenderini and Deparis improved the computational efficiency of fluid-structure interaction through a space–time Galerkin reduced basis (ST–GRB) method [[172], [173], [174]].

Although three-dimensional models provide rich details, they are computationally expensive. Kroon et al. developed a simplified computational method combining one-dimensional (1D) wave propagation models for the blood vessels with zero-dimensional (0D) lumped models for the microcirculation to simulate vascular hemodynamics [175].The one-dimensional model simplifies the Navier-Stokes equations, maintaining computational efficiency while still capturing the primary characteristics of blood flow, particularly suitable for macroscopic simulations of vascular networks. High-quality vascular modeling techniques, such as Hong et al.'s implicit extrusion surfaces method and Qin et al.'s three-dimensional reconstruction technique for complex vascular systems, provide precise geometric foundations for numerical simulations [176,177].The integrated application of these methods has significantly enhanced the depth and breadth of computational fluid dynamics research.

However, one-dimensional models inherently average hemodynamic parameters over the cross-sectional area, sacrificing the ability to resolve local three-dimensional flow structures, such as secondary flows, helical patterns, and vortices generated at bifurcations, curvatures, and stenotic segments [178]. Consequently, these simplified mathematical models cannot accurately predict local WSS distributions, OSI, or flow recirculation zones, parameters that critically govern nanocarrier margination, adhesion, and drug release at lesion sites [179]. Furthermore, many CFD models still employ steady-flow or rigid-wall assumptions, overlooking the pulsatile nature of blood flow and the fluid-structure interaction between blood and compliant vessel walls. Such simplifications may introduce substantial deviations: steady-flow assumptions underestimate temporal WSS gradients and neglect the phase-lag between pressure and flow waves, potentially leading to inaccurate predictions of nanocarrier transport kinetics and endothelial uptake patterns in vivo.

Modeling and simulation

Microfluidic chip

Microfluidic chips are miniaturized experimental platforms based on microfabrication technology that enable precise control of fluid flow and reactions at the micrometer scale. In targeted drug delivery research, microfluidic chips can be used to construct biomimetic vascular networks that simulate the complex geometric structures, blood flow velocities, and shear stress distributions of in vivo blood vessels. They serve as an ideal platform for studying how hemodynamic environments, blood components, and particle characteristics collectively influence the interaction between drug carriers and vascular endothelium [133,180].

The specific applications of microfluidic technology in drug carrier performance are primarily realized through chip design. For conventional straight channels, researchers can culture vascular endothelial cells and manipulate shear stress by controlling fluid flow rate or waveform, thereby investigating the effects of shear stress on drug carrier performance [14]. For example, Toy et al. used an in vitro microfluidic model to assess the effects of nanoparticle shape, size, and density on their margination behavior in microcirculation, finding that smaller, flatter, and less dense particles exhibited higher margination rates, which is critical for particles to approach the vascular wall and interact effectively with the vascular bed [181].

Another significant advantage of microfluidic chips lies in their ability to simulate complex vascular structures and multi-cell co-culture environments. Costantini et al. proposed a valve-based flow-focusing (vFF) microfluidic chip combined with extrusion 3D printing, where the orifice geometry in the microfluidic junction can be dynamically adjusted by regulating pressure. This design achieves a remarkable one-order-of-magnitude variation (80-800 μm) in bubble diameter while maintaining a stable volume fraction (∼80%) of the dispersed phase and high monodispersity of the generated foam. It can simulate the heterogeneous pore characteristics of pathological sites and is of great high value for mimicking the complex porous microenvironment inside thrombi and preparing biomimetic vascular models [182].

3D printing technology is revolutionizing the fabrication of microfluidic chips, overcoming the limitations of traditional soft lithography in creating three-dimensional complex structures and achieving automated production [183].Miri et al. demonstrated a stereolithography-based bioprinting platform capable of rapidly switching between different bioinks through a microfluidic device, enabling layer-by-layer multi-material bioprinting while maintaining high spatial resolution. This approach offers potential for constructing more complex bionic vascular networks [184] (Fig. 7A).Similarly, Su et al. showcased the application of 3D-printed self-supporting elastic structures in multifunctional microfluidics, allowing direct integration of sensors and adaptation to curved surfaces, thereby further expanding the capabilities of microfluidic systems [187]. Additive manufacturing possesses the capability to fabricate complex nanoscale structures. Although microfluidic chips exhibit unique advantages in the field of vascular model construction, they still have numerous limitations that make it difficult to fully recapitulate the real vascular microenvironment in vivo. Beyond the issues of small channel sizes and limited cell seeding areas, such models generally suffer from the drawback of oversimplified vascular wall structures. Most can only construct a single layer of endothelial cells and are unable to simulate key physiological functions such as vasoconstriction and mechanical crosstalk between cells. Meanwhile, their hemodynamic simulation capability is insufficient: most rely on constant-flow pumps to provide stable fluid flow, making it hard to reproduce the pulsatile flow characteristics that change with the cardiac cycle in vivo and to recapitulate the hemodynamic shear stress gradients across different vascular regions, which limits the research on hemodynamic-related vascular pathologies such as atherosclerosis.

Parallel plate flow chamber

The parallel plate flow chamber system can be considered an improved microfluidic system, particularly suitable for studying the interaction between carriers and endothelial cells as well as the permeation behavior of carriers [171].It consists of two parallel plates forming a narrow flow channel between them, allowing precise regulation of shear stress within the channel by controlling fluid velocity and flow rate. Its core design lies in its ability to generate laminar flow, ensuring that the shear stress acting on cells is uniform and predictable [185] (Fig. 7B).

When studying the interaction between drug carriers and plaque sites, vascular endothelial cells are cultured on the bottom plate of the chamber, and fluid containing nanoparticles is perfused through the flow channel. By observing the adhesion of nanoparticles to endothelial cells under different flow rates and shear stresses, the targeting performance of the carriers can be evaluated. For instance, Han et al. utilized a parallel plate flow chamber to investigate how acute and chronic shear stress differentially regulate endothelial cell uptake of nanoparticles targeted to platelet-endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (PECAM-1) [73]. Simultaneously, simulating the non-Newtonian characteristics of blood in the parallel plate flow chamber, such as using a power-law rheological model, can more realistically reflect the behavior of drug particles in complex flow fields [185].

However, the models of parallel plate flow chambers typically employ simplified two-dimensional geometries and cannot fully replicate the complex three-dimensional morphological features of blood vessels in vivo, such as curvatures, bifurcations, and branched networks [189]. More importantly, the vast majority of parallel plate flow chamber studies rely on steady, unidirectional laminar flow, which fundamentally differs from the pulsatile, multidirectional, and often disturbed flow patterns observed in vivo. Pulsatile flow generates periodic variations in WSS magnitude and direction, which are known to modulate endothelial cell phenotype, mechanotransduction signaling, and nanocarrier internalization dynamics in ways that steady-flow experiments cannot replicate [190]. Furthermore, the simplified 2D geometry cannot generate flow separation, recirculation zones, or secondary flows characteristic of stenotic or bifurcated vessels [33]. These hemodynamic simplifications may lead to systematic overestimation of nanocarrier adhesion efficiency under uniform shear and fail to predict the heterogeneous distribution and aggregation behaviors observed in complex 3D lesion microenvironments [70]. Therefore, data derived from steady-flow parallel plate systems should be interpreted with caution when extrapolating to in vivo scenarios involving oscillatory or disturbed flow.

In vivo experimental and imaging research methods under fluid mechanics

Intravital fluorescence microscopy (IVM)

Intravital fluorescence microscopy, particularly intravital confocal microscopy (CM), is a non-invasive or minimally invasive technique that uses laser-based illumination to perform optical sectioning and quantitative analysis, capturing high-quality images and videos [189]. Currently, intravital confocal microscopy is widely applied to observe the microstructure of lesions and the targeting, accumulation, penetration, and cellular uptake of drug delivery carriers such as extracellular vesicles (EVs) within blood vessels [191,192].

The integration of intravital confocal microscopy with novel animal models has opened new avenues for studying carrier behavior in vasculature. Transgenic mouse embryos with visible vascular systems allow engineering of blood components and vessel structures via transgenic fluorescent labeling techniques. Transgenic zebrafish, as a model organism, offer transparent embryos and larvae that enable real-time observation of the vascular system through fluorescence imaging [193]. Numerous studies have adopted transgenic zebrafish embryos as the core model and integrated multiple technical approaches to conduct systematic investigations into the impact of hemodynamics on the in vivo distribution of nanoparticles. On one hand, by combining vascular 3D modeling, particle mapping technology, and computational fluid dynamics methods, quantitative evaluation of the distribution patterns of nanoparticles under the action of hydrodynamic forces is achieved. On the other hand, 3D models of vascular structures can be reconstructed based on the imaging results, while the correlation between the enrichment status of nanocarriers and various hemodynamic factors is also analyzed in depth.

In recent years, the combination of the near-infrared biological imaging window (NIR-II window) with intravital CM has enhanced tissue penetration depth, significantly expanding its application in deep-tissue imaging. Currently, several emerging advanced technologies, such as confocal scanning light field microscopy (csLFM) and confocal light field microscopy (cLFM), have also overcome the drawbacks of traditional confocal microscopy (CM), including slow imaging speed and insufficient resolution in deep tissues. These techniques can track neural activity and blood cells at depths of hundreds of micrometers in mouse brains with submicron resolution, providing unprecedented details for investigating vascular dynamics and nanoparticle behaviors [40,194].

Various ultrasound imaging technologies and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)

Doppler ultrasound, intravascular ultrasound (IVUS), super-resolution ultrasound, and other ultrasound-based technologies, as well as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), are all crucial non-invasive tools for monitoring vascular hemodynamics and the distribution of drug carriers. Among these modalities, Doppler ultrasound and MRI, as typical non-invasive techniques, also possess complementary advantages in such monitoring work, and together with the above-mentioned ultrasound technologies, they can effectively overcome the limitation of intravital fluorescence microscopy in terms of tissue penetration depth. Doppler ultrasound detects Doppler frequency shifts in blood flow to assess flow velocity and direction in real time. It is widely used in clinical diagnosis of vascular stenosis and thrombosis, as well as in studies of cerebrovascular reactivity (CVR) and regional cerebral blood flow (CBF). In drug delivery, Doppler ultrasound guidance enables real-time active intravascular delivery of nanoparticle microswarms [195].The advent of super-resolution ultrasound technology has further broken the traditional acoustic diffraction limit. Yu et al. developed a method that improves vascular imaging resolution by fivefold with significantly enhanced temporal resolution, capturing vascular details during cardiac motion and providing a new tool for studying atherosclerosis-related vasculopathy [196].Despite its advantages in real-time imaging, portability, and low cost, ultrasound resolution is limited by probe frequency and tissue penetration depth, and results can be operator-dependent.

Beyond doppler ultrasound, several imaging technologies developed based on its technical concepts and other emerging in vivo imaging technologies also show potential for investigating the effects of vascular hemodynamics on nanocarriers. Among them, raster-scanning optoacoustic mesoscopy (RSOM) and multimodal integration techniques, such as the combination of optical coherence tomography and intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) not only continue to expand the application boundaries of MRI but also exhibit unique value in vascular research. Photoacoustic imaging (PAI) combines optical contrast with ultrasonic penetration depth, enabling visualization of deep vascular networks and blood oxygen saturation. Buehler et al. used RSOM to achieve in vivo assessment of deep vascular patterns in mouse colitis [186](Fig. 7C). Optical coherence tomography (OCT) provides high-resolution cross-sectional images. Li et al. developed an ultrafast optical-ultrasound dual-modal imaging system and a miniature catheter capable of in vivo OCT-intravascular ultrasound synchronous imaging, rapidly generating volumetric maps of plaques and clearly identifying vulnerable plaques, which holds significant clinical value for atherosclerosis evaluation [197].

MRI, with its high spatial resolution, excellent soft tissue contrast, and absence of ionizing radiation, provides multidimensional structural and functional information. Herbert et al. and Ma et al. developed implantable wireless vascular electronic devices validated by MRI for real-time monitoring of arterial pressure and blood flow in rabbits, representing significant progress in continuous vascular disease monitoring [198,199]. In nanocarrier research, MRI offers distinct advantages. By labeling drug carriers with MRI contrast agents, their distribution and accumulation can be tracked in vivo. Fluorine-19 (19F) MRI, due to the absence of endogenous background signals, is ideal for multicolor imaging. Akazawa et al. prepared five types of 19F-labeled nanoprobes that produced three distinct color signals in mice, enabling evaluation of surface group effects on liver uptake [200]. Hyperpolarized xenon-129 (129Xe) MRI, enhanced by hyperpolarization techniques that increase sensitivity by hundreds to tens of thousands of times, has been reviewed by Yang et al. for applications in cell tracking and targeted molecular detection, offering new insights for nanodrug delivery monitoring [201]. High-field MRI further improves imaging precision. Boulant et al. used this technology to acquire mesoscopic-resolution images of living human brains, laying the foundation for studies of cerebral vasculature, neural networks, and evaluation of drug delivery for brain diseases [202].Despite its advantages, MRI faces challenges such as high equipment costs, long scanning times, limited dynamic process capture capability, and safety concerns related to specific absorption rate (SAR) in high-field systems.

Conclusions and perspectives

Cardiovascular diseases such as atherosclerosis and venous thromboembolism have unique pathological hemodynamic characteristics, which are closely related to the delivery effect of targeted nanomedicines. These studies have constructed a research framework linking hemodynamic characteristics, carrier fate, targeting strategies and technical support, and summarized optimization strategies for nanocarrier delivery challenges caused by hemodynamics. However, current research on nanocarriers for CVDs still has obvious limitations.

From bench to bedside: translational barriers that must Be addressed

The translation from bench to bedside is confronted with four intertwined barriers. First, the complex physicochemical nature of nanomedicines makes GMP-scale manufacturing extremely challenging, and batch-to-batch consistency in particle size, PDI, and encapsulation efficiency is difficult to guarantee. EMA data indicate that approximately 30% of marketing applications are rejected due to insufficient manufacturing data. Second, existing in vitro models such as microfluidic chips and parallel-plate flow chambers are overly simplified and fail to recapitulate in vivo pulsatile hemodynamics, vascular compliance, and disease-specific shear stress gradients. Although emerging technologies such as organ-on-chip hold promise for improving predictive capacity, they still lack standardized protocols and suffer from experimental artifacts such as protein corona adsorption [203]. Third, regarding safety, nanocarriers are prone to nonspecific clearance by the hepatic and splenic reticuloendothelial systems (RES), and approximately 20% of early-stage nanotherapies have failed for this reason [204]. Repeated administration may also trigger complement activation-related pseudoallergy and anti-PEG antibody-mediated accelerated blood clearance, with clinical statistics showing that PEGylated nanocarriers can induce severe hypersensitivity reactions in 0.46%–0.62% of patients [53,205]. Finally, at the regulatory level, the FDA and EMA currently evaluate nanomedicines primarily within conventional biologics frameworks, lacking dedicated guidance for nanomaterial similarity assessment. The opacity of current guidelines makes it difficult for industry to access clear pathways, underscoring the urgent need for science-based regulatory standards spanning the entire lifecycle and early adoption of the Quality by Design (QbD) approach to define critical quality attributes [162,206].

Crossing hemodynamic hurdles: concrete forward-looking guidance

To overcome hemodynamic barriers, future research should establish a closed-loop paradigm integrating computational design, biomimetic validation, intelligent delivery, imaging feedback, and regulatory synergy. First, patient-specific vascular imaging and CFD-based databases of lesion hemodynamics should be combined with machine-learning surrogate models to inversely optimize carrier size, shape, and surface functionality for flow-adaptive precision design [207]. Concurrently, organ-on-chip and 3D-bioprinted vascular networks featuring pulsatile flow, compliant walls, and multicellular co-cultures should be developed to recapitulate disease-specific shear stress gradients and bridge the in vitro–in vivo divide [208]. For low-flow, high-matrix environments such as thrombi or dense plaques, shape-morphing nanoparticles or externally powered nanorobots should be introduced to actively overcome interstitial pressure and fibrin barriers [127,153]. Finally, multimodal imaging combining NIR-II fluorescence, super-resolution ultrasound, and 19F/129Xe MRI should be employed to real-time couple carrier distribution with local hemodynamics, enabling imaging-guided individualized dosing [200,201,209]. Meanwhile, the QbD and DELIVER frameworks should be introduced at early preclinical stages to promote global regulatory consensus on nanomedicine characterization, biocompatibility, and endpoint design, thereby substantially shortening the cycle from innovation to approval [162].
