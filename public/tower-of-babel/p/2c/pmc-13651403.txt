Modelling geographical variation in the determinants of childhood malaria in Malawi using geographically weighted regression
Authors: Mary Magoya, Samuel Manda, Bedilu Ejigu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Malaria remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among children in Malawi with transmission exhibiting marked geographic disparities. While standard regression models estimate the association between childhood malaria and risk factors across the entire study area, they assume spatial stationarity, thereby masking local-area variations in estimated effects. We applied Geographically Weighted Regression, a spatial non-stationarity modeling approach to data from the recent 2024 Malawi Demographic and Health Survey to estimate district-specific associations between childhood malaria and selected risk factors, namely mosquito net use, place of residence, household wealth, maternal age, and maternal education. The overall malaria prevalence among children aged five or under was 36.3%. By applying Geographically Weighted Regression, the study demonstrated substantial spatial heterogeneity in the associations between childhood malaria and the selected predictors across Malawi, revealing locally varying effects that would have been obscured under conventional global modelling approaches. These findings highlight the value of locally estimated relationships for understanding contextual differences in the predictors of childhood malaria and provide district-specific evidence to support geographically targeted intervention strategies and more efficient allocation of prevention and treatment resources within Malawi’s National Malaria Control Programme.

Background

Across Sub-Saharan Africa, malaria remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality, with up to 200 million symptomatic cases annually [1]. Malaria risk is widespread across Malawi, with the highest incidence in low-lying, hot, and humid areas, particularly around Lake Malawi, the Shire River valley, and the central plains [2]. In contrast, cooler highland areas and the mountainous ridge along the southern part of the country, which forms part of the Great Rift Valley system, have the lowest malaria risk due to their higher altitudes and cooler temperatures. Malaria transmission in Malawi occurs year-round, with peaks during and shortly after the rainy season, which lasts from November to April. The entire population of Malawi is considered to be at risk [3, 4]. Malaria is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among children under five years of age, particularly among those living in the rural areas of central Malawi [5].

The Malawi government aims to have a malaria-free country by 2030, and various control programs are being implemented at the national level to achieve these goals. The National Malaria Control Program distributes long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs), conducts indoor residual spraying (IRS), and facilitates prompt access to artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) and preventive treatment during pregnancy [6–8]. In 2019, Malawi also started piloting RTS, S/ASO1 malaria vaccine in 11 districts [8]. There has been a shift toward a targeted approach, using subnational stratification to deploy resources where the burden is highest [9]. The current strategic plan aims to address geographical challenges and inequalities in accessing malaria services by extending service provision to hard-to-reach areas and providing malaria services free of charge at all service delivery points within the public-funded health care system.

Several studies have explained this burden by identifying key sociodemographic and environmental predictors of malaria infection among children. These studies employed spatial modeling and found substantive geographical variation in childhood malaria prevalence across the modeled areas. However, the factors themselves were not studied at the spatial level; the underlying models were logistic or otherwise, with spatial methods used to account for geographic context. At the individual level, they also identified significant associations with key predictors, including maternal education, consistent insecticide-treated net (ITN) use, and household wealth [3, 5, 10–12]. Importantly, these factors were not treated as area level aggregates, rather, the spatial models allowed their effects to vary across locations while preserving their individual level interpretation. This approach reveals that even when individual characteristics are important predictors, their influence on malaria risk is not constant across space.

In reality, the influence of these predictors is unlikely to be uniform across geographic areas. For example, the protective effect of maternal education might not be similar across all districts because education operates through context-specific pathways. Higher education may translate into improved malaria prevention practices such as consistent ITN use, timely healthcare-seeking, and adherence to treatment in districts where health facilities are accessible, malaria communication campaigns are active, and prevention tools are widely distributed. In districts where these systems are weaker, education alone may not produce the same level of protection [13, 14].

A similar pattern may occur with maternal age. Maternal age is often linked to education level, household wealth, and caregiving experience, which can influence practices such as mosquito-net use, health-care-seeking behavior, and overall childcare. Additionally, urban–rural differences, housing quality, and environmental conditions can affect children’s exposure to mosquitoes. Variations in access to malaria prevention programs, such as insecticide-treated nets, indoor residual spraying, and healthcare services, further shape the relationship between maternal age and malaria risk. As a result, the impact of maternal age on childhood malaria varies across districts and may increase, decrease, or remain negligible depending on district-specific contexts [15, 16].

Spatial variation may also be evident in the relationship between place of residence and malaria risk. The association between rural residence and malaria among children under five can vary across districts because rural environments and living conditions are not the same everywhere. Malaria transmission is influenced by ecological factors such as rainfall, temperature, altitude, and proximity to water bodies that support mosquito breeding, and these environmental conditions differ across districts. In some districts, rural areas may be located near lakes, rivers, or irrigated farmland where mosquito populations are higher, increasing malaria risk for children. In other districts, rural areas may be at higher altitudes or in drier environments where malaria transmission is lower, reducing the difference between rural and urban risk [17–19].

Despite these potential geographic differences, most previous studies rely on modelling approaches that assume uniform relationships across space. The global regression models used to determine these factors are valuable tools in statistical modelling; however, in many applications, they assume that relationships between covariates and malaria outcomes are spatially stationary, that is, the effect of a predictor is constant across all locations [20]. This assumption is often violated in real-world data, especially in geographically diverse settings such as Malawi.

To address this limitation, we applied Geographical Weighted Regression (GWR) to the 2024 Malawi Demographic and Health Survey (MDHS) data. GWR is a spatial extension of the ordinary least squares (OLS) regression model that allows relationships between predictors and outcomes to vary across space. Unlike global regression models, which assume a single set of coefficients, GWR estimates location-specific coefficients, thereby capturing spatial non-stationarity in the data.

GWR was developed in response to the recognition that regression models estimated over an entire study area may fail to capture important local variations [20, 21]. When the effects of predictors vary geographically, global models may mask these differences and hide important local patterns in the relationship between covariates and malaria prevalence among children. By allowing coefficients to vary across locations through a geographically weighted estimation procedure, GWR reveals how and where these relationships differ across districts.

Understanding such spatial heterogeneity is important for malaria control and public health planning. Analyzing risk factors using GWR can provide deeper insights into malaria epidemiology and help policymakers identify geographically specific drivers of malaria transmission [22]. This enables more targeted and efficient allocation of interventions at sub-national or district levels rather than relying on uniform national strategies. Recent studies suggest that malaria distribution in Malawi has become increasingly heterogeneous, with some districts experiencing a heavier burden than others [2, 23]. In such contexts, spatially explicit models like GWR are particularly useful for identifying localized predictors of malaria risk. This study compares traditional regression approaches with a spatially flexible model, GWR, to model local variations in the predictors of childhood malaria. We also compared GWR performance with that of the global regression model.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: the Methodology Section outlines the data and methodology used in the analyses; the Results section present the key findings of the study; and the Discussion provides the discussion and conclusions.

Methodology

Data and study design

The data were obtained from the recent 2024 Malawi Demographic and Health Survey, which the National Statistical Office of Malawi conducted in collaboration with the Ministry of Health and the Community Health Services Unit of Malawi. More details on the sampling procedure and design of the survey can be obtained from the 2024 MDHS report (NSO, 2024). The study analyzed data on 11,771 children under five years old who were alive and had responded to the fever question. Data collection was done in 769 enumeration areas (EAs) across the country. The sampled EAs were distributed across the country, and the sampling weight variable was incorporated into the data analysis software to ensure that all estimated parameters were appropriately weighted.

The outcome variable was whether a child had a fever (which is considered as proxy for suspected malaria) in the 2 weeks preceding the survey (0 = No fever; 1 = Fever) as reported by their caregiver/parent. The study did not use laboratory-confirmed malaria diagnosis (e.g., rapid diagnostic tests or microscopy) because such data is not available in the MDHS. Therefore, fever in the two weeks preceding the survey was used as a proxy indicator of possible malaria infection. Fever is an important symptom of malaria and serves as an important measure of the proportion of children under 5 who may need diagnosis or treatment. The number of these children with recent fever is used as a care-seeking indicator. However, this measure has limitations. It relies on reports from mothers or caregivers, which may not accurately reflect fever episodes in the two weeks before the survey. It may also overlook foster children or those without a caregiver [24–26]. The explanatory variables include Age of child in months(1 = 0–24, 2 = 25–35, 3 = 36–59), Sex of child (0 = male, 1 = female), Maternal age (1 = 15–19, 2 = 20–34, 3 = 35–49), Use of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), (0 = No, 1 = Yes), Mother's education level (0 = No/Primary education, 1 = Secondary, 2 = Higher), Type of residence (1 = Urban, 2 = Rural), Household wealth index (1 = Low, 2 = Middle, 3 = Higher).

Methods of data analysis

We first explored the associations between covariates and childhood fever, as well as among covariates using chi-square test of association, to identify those that were significantly correlated and avoid multicollinearity in the models. Covariates that were statistically significant at the 5% level were included in the models. Additionally, we selected some variables based on literature, specifically covariates that have been shown in previous studies.

Results

Participants characteristics

A summary of the characteristics of children who were alive and reported having a fever during 2024 DHS is presented in Table 1 for the 11,771 children studied. Among these, 33.6% (3,958/11,771) had reported to having fever 2 weeks preceeding the survey. Prevalence was the same between males and females (33%). Among the 11,771 children, only 1,415.0 (34.0%) children had slept under a mosquito net the night before the survey. Most children 81% (9564/11,771) were from the rural areas of Malawi. Model 1: unweighted binary logistic regression

The ordinary binary logistic regression model assumes spatial stationarity, where the association between childhood malaria and of all covariates is constant across Malawi. This model was fitted to obtain an overall relationship between malaria prevalence and covariates. In Table 2, children aged 25–35 months had slightly higher odds of malaria prevalence compared to those aged 0–24 months (OR = 1.12, 95% CI 1.00–1.25, p = 0.049). There was no significant difference in the odds of malaria infection by child sex (OR = 1.02, 95% CI 0.90–1.05, p = 0.466). Children of mothers aged 35–49 years had lower odds of having malaria infection compared to children of mothers aged 15–19 (OR = 0.90, 95% CI 0.76–1.05, p = 0.182). Higher maternal education was strongly protective, particularly for mothers with higher education (OR = 0.55, 95% CI 0.40–0.75, p = 0.019) compared to children of mothers who had no or some primary education. Rural residence increased the odds of malaria infection compared with urban residence (OR = 1.96, 95% CI 1.50–1.91, p < 0.001).

This model provides an average national association between childhood malaria and each covariate. However, by construction, it assumes that these associations operate uniformly across all districts. The AIC of 14,700 and BIC of 14,789.61 serve as baseline values for comparison with models incorporating spatial structure.

Null multilevel logistic regression model

The null multilevel logistic regression model indicated significant clustering of malaria infection at both the enumeration area (EA) and district levels (Appendix Table 6). The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) from the null model showed that 6.5% of the total variance in malaria infection was attributable to differences between EAs, while 8.1% was attributable to differences between districts. Overall, 14.6% of the variation in malaria infection was explained by higher-level clustering, confirming the appropriateness of the multilevel modeling approach.

Model 2: random effects model at the enumeration area (EA) level

We extended model 1 by adding a random effect at the EA level. The variance of the EA-level random effect was 0.46 with a standard deviation of 0.68, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and variance partition coefficient (VPC) of 0.123, indicating that approximately 12.3% of the total variation in childhood malaria was attributed to differences between enumeration areas. The reduction in AIC from 14,700 to 14,565 and BIC value from 14,789.61 to 14,652.9 indicates that unobserved community-level heterogeneity plays an important role.

After accounting for EA-level clustering, several associations remain similar; however, some coefficients changed in magnitude and significance. The protective effect of older maternal age became stronger (OR = 0.84, 95% CI 0.71–0.99, p = 0.035), suggesting that part of the variation previously attributed to individual-level factors may reflect clustering within communities. Higher maternal education continued to show a stronger protective effect (OR = 0.36, 95% CI 0.23–0.54, p < 0.001) and rural residence remained a strong risk factor (OR = 2.02, 95% CI 1.60–2.54, p < 0.001). High household wealth remained non-significant compared with low-income earning households (OR = 1.06, 95% CI 0.95–1.19, 0.324).

Model 3: random intercept model at the enumeration area and district levels

This model extended the random-effects structure by including random intercepts at both the enumeration area (EA) and district levels to account for hierarchical clustering of malaria infection. The estimated variance components indicated residual heterogeneity at both levels, with an EA-level variance of 0.21 and a standard deviation of 0.46, and a district-level variance of 0.22 and a standard deviation of 0.47. The ICCs and VPCs indicated that 5.6% of the total variation in childhood malaria was attributable to differences between EAs, and 6.0% to differences between districts. This confirms that clustering exists at both levels, with the district level slightly more influential than the EA level (Table 2). After accounting for this multilevel structure, the associations between covariates and malaria infection remained consistent with earlier models. Model 4: spatial random effects model

This model extended the multilevel modelling by incorporating a spatially structured random effect at the district level using a Matern correlation function to account explicitly for spatial dependence in malaria risk. The estimated variance components indicated moderate heterogeneity at all three levels: 0.27 at the enumeration area (EA) level, 0.17 at the district level, and ~ 0 for the spatial random effect. The ICCs and VPCs indicate that 7.1% of childhood malaria at the EA level and 4.6% at the district level are attributed to differences between EA and district (Table 2). Adding spatial coordinates in this model did not meaningfully capture additional variance, but EA and District still contributed to clustering.

In this model, rural residence and secondary and higher maternal education remain significant. However, maternal age (35–49 years) was no longer significant. The magnitude and significance of these covariates changed as spatial structure was incorporated. These shifts suggest that some relationships observed in global models reflect underlying geographic heterogeneity rather than uniform causal effects.

The improved fit of the spatial model, combined with the changing coefficients across specifications, supports the argument that the predictors of childhood malaria exhibit spatial non-stationarity. In other words, the strength - and in some cases the presence - of associations depends on district-level context. This spatial model showed the best overall fit (AIC = 13,716.12 and BIC = 13,860.38), indicating that accounting for spatial correlation at the district level captured residual variation not explained by the other models.

Model 5: geographical weighted regression

We extended the binary logistic regression model to allow the relationship between malaria prevalence and key covariates to vary spatially across districts using GWR. GWR generated location-specific coefficient estimates, which were subsequently mapped to examine spatial variation across districts.

This improvement suggests that accounting for spatial heterogeneity in the associations between childhood malaria and the selected predictors provides a better representation of the observed data. The GWR model also exhibited a substantially better fit, as indicated by markedly lower AIC (− 94.79 vs. 14,700) and BIC (262.55 vs. 14,789.61) values. Rural residence

There is substantial spatial variation in the association between rural residence and childhood malaria across districts (Fig. 2a). The coefficients are strongly positive in several northern districts, suggesting that living in a rural area is associated with markedly higher odds of malaria there. In contrast, the magnitude of this association weakens in parts of the central region and becomes small or even slightly negative in some southern districts. This pattern demonstrates clear spatial non-stationarity: the disadvantage associated with rural residence is not uniform nationwide. In certain northern districts, rural environments may amplify exposure due to limited health infrastructure or reduced access to preventive interventions. However, in some southern districts, rural residence appears less consequential, possibly reflecting better outreach services, smaller rural–urban disparities, or different settlement structures.

Use of mosquito net the previous night before the survey

Coefficients for the variable indicating whether a child slept under a mosquito net varied spatially (Fig. 2b). All coefficients are negative, indicating that sleeping under a net is associated with reduced malaria prevalence. The strongest protective effect is observed in the southern regions, shown in dark red, while central areas appear mostly neutral (white to light peach), suggesting minimal effect. Northern regions exhibit a moderate protective effect, represented by shades of orange and red. Overall, the map highlights clear spatial variation: nets are most effective in the south, moderately effective in the north, and least effective in central regions, emphasizing the importance of targeted interventions based on regional differences.

Figure 2b shows that the effect of a child sleeping under an insecticide-treated net (ITN) varies considerably across districts. The coefficients show a strong negative association in many southern districts, where ITN use substantially reduces the likelihood of malaria. In contrast, the protective effect is weaker in parts of the central region and more moderate in some northern districts. This suggests that the effectiveness of ITN use depends on local contextual conditions. In districts where the protective effect is strongest, ITN programs may be well implemented, usage may be consistent, or transmission intensity may be high enough for nets to confer substantial benefit. Where the association is weaker, factors such as inconsistent usage, insecticide resistance, net deterioration, or lower transmission intensity may reduce the observable impact. These findings reinforce the idea that malaria predictors do not operate uniformly across space; instead, their magnitude varies geographically, underscoring the importance of place-based intervention strategies rather than relying on nationally averaged effects.

Maternal education

Figure 2c, d shows substantial spatial heterogeneity in the association between secondary and higher maternal education and childhood malaria. Local coefficients range from strongly negative (− 5.12 to − 1.58) in several northern and selected southern districts to positive values (2.55 to 6.80) in parts of the central region.

In districts with strongly negative coefficients, higher maternal education is associated with markedly reduced odds of childhood malaria, indicating a strong protective effect. However, in parts of the central region, the association weakens considerably and, in some districts, becomes positive, suggesting that higher education is not uniformly protective and may even be associated with increased odds in specific contexts.

This clear geographic variability indicates spatial non-stationarity in the education–malaria relationship. The protective benefits of maternal education appear to depend on contextual conditions. In districts where health services, malaria prevention campaigns, and distribution of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) are functional and accessible, education may translate into improved preventive behaviors and timely care-seeking. Conversely, in districts where structural constraints limit the effectiveness of prevention systems, education alone may not yield equivalent protection. The presence of districts with positive coefficients underscores that national average effects mask important local differences in how education operates.

Household wealth index

Middle-income households show predominantly negative coefficients in the southern regions (Fig. 2e), with stronger negative magnitudes clustered in the southeast, while positive effects are observed in parts of the north and central areas. In contrast, high-income households display strong positive coefficients (Fig. 2f) concentrated in the northern regions, whereas negative effects are evident across several central and southern districts.

Local coefficients range from negative values (− 0.25 to 0.62) in several central and southern districts to strongly positive coefficients (2.85 to 4.75) in parts of the northern region.

In districts where coefficients are negative or near zero, high-income household wealth has limited influence on malaria risk. In contrast, in several northern districts, wealth is associated with substantially higher odds of malaria. This reversal in direction indicates that the household wealth–malaria relationship is not spatially constant and may operate through context-specific mechanisms.

The positive coefficients observed in some districts may reflect behavioral factors whereby wealthier households reside in areas with higher transmission intensity or in areas where housing improvements do not sufficiently mitigate exposure. Alternatively, district-level environmental or infrastructural conditions may reduce the protective advantages typically associated with wealth. While the global model suggests that wealth has little or no relationship with malaria, the GWR results show that this overall average hides important district differences. In some districts, wealth reduces malaria risk, while in others it is linked to higher risk.

This indicates that allowing coefficients to vary spatially provides a better fit to the data, capturing local variation in the associations between predictors and outcome that the global model cannot.

The observed spatial pattern suggests that the predictors of childhood malaria operate non-uniformly across districts, consistent with spatial non-stationarity. The presence of localised high-risk clusters highlights areas where children are predicted to have a substantially higher probability of malaria infection than the national average, even after accounting for relevant covariates in the GWR model.

Discussion

Our study aimed to apply Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR) to understand the district-level variation in in the effect of malaria predictors among children under 5 years of age in Malawi. Most studies in Malawi and throughout sub-Saharan Africa have used global regression models and spatial analyses of population-based health survey data to identify sociodemographic and environmental determinants of malaria infection among young children. We have found that the magnitude and direction of associations varied geographically, indicating that the influence of these factors is not uniform throughout the country. Thus, our study has provided a better understanding of local variations in malaria risk by identifying districts where predictors exert stronger or weaker effects. These results highlight the importance of incorporating spatially sensitive analytical approaches when investigating malaria burden and designing targeted interventions.

Overall, the prevalence of malaria among children aged 5 years or younger was 36.3%. At the district level, the prevalence had an average of 34.7%, a median of 37.3%, and ranged from a minimum of 10.5% to a maximum of 60.4%. The results from fitting an ordinary regression model to child malaria identified several factors significantly associated with childhood malaria risk, including child age in months, rural–urban residence, household wealth index, maternal age, and maternal education. These findings are consistent with previous studies conducted in sub-saharan Africa, which have reported similar associations among young children between socioeconomic status, maternal characteristics, and malaria risk [7, 31–33]. Older children often experience increased exposure to malaria vectors due to greater mobility. In contrast, children from poorer households or rural settings may face higher exposure due to environmental conditions and limited access to preventive measures.

Previous studies in sub-Saharan Africa have also demonstrated spatial variation in malaria predictors. For instance, studies conducted in Niger used random-effects models, while research in Zimbabwe employed geostatistical models to explore spatial variation in malaria risk [31, 34]. Although these studies confirmed spatial heterogeneity, they did not explicitly demonstrate how the effects of specific covariates varied across geographic regions. By contrast, the present study used GWR to quantify and map locally varying coefficients, thereby providing more detailed insight into the spatial dynamics of malaria predictors.

Our GWR analysis demonstrated spatial heterogeneity in key predictors of childhood malaria, including rural residence, use of mosquito nets, maternal age, maternal education, and household wealth. For example, the effect of rural residence on malaria risk was not uniform across districts. This variation may reflect differences in ecological and environmental conditions across rural areas. Some rural districts are located near water bodies, wetlands, or swampy environments that provide favorable breeding sites for malaria vectors, thereby increasing the risk of exposure among children. In other districts, improved malaria control interventions or environmental management may mitigate this risk, leading to weaker associations between rural residence and malaria prevalence. These findings align with those of Ssempiira et al. [35], who emphasised that the effectiveness of malaria interventions often varies across ecological settings and therefore requires context-specific implementation strategies. Similarly, Ejigu and Wencheko [36] demonstrated that non-stationary geostatistical models can provide a better representation of disease risk when spatial heterogeneity is present.

Mapping the spatially varying coefficients derived from the GWR model enabled the identification of location-specific relationships that may inform geographically targeted malaria interventions. Similar spatially informed approaches have been applied in Kenya and Zambia, where malaria control strategies were adapted according to localised risk profiles and environmental conditions [37–39]. Such approaches support more efficient allocation of limited public health resources by prioritising high-risk areas and tailoring interventions to local contexts.

In addition to the GWR analysis, this study also fitted a multilevel random-slope model to assess spatial heterogeneity in covariate effects formally. Both modeling approaches allow regression coefficients to vary across space; however, they differ in their statistical assumptions and estimation procedures. In the multilevel random-slope model, district-specific effects are assumed to follow a common global distribution, thereby introducing partial pooling and shrinkage toward the overall mean. In contrast, GWR estimates local coefficients using distance-based kernel weighting without imposing a global distributional structure.

The GWR model demonstrated substantially greater explanatory power (R2 = 0.81) than the global model, indicating that accounting for spatial heterogeneity in covariate associations improved model performance. However, GWR is typically designed for point-referenced data with dense spatial coordinates, such as enumeration areas. In this study, analysis was conducted at the individual level using aggregated areal units. Under such conditions, locally weighted estimation may produce unstable or inflated coefficients, particularly where the number of neighboring districts is small or unevenly distributed. By contrast, the multilevel random-slope model explicitly accounts for hierarchical data structures and allows district-level variation in selected covariate effects while borrowing strength across districts. This partial pooling reduces the risk of overfitting and produces statistically more stable and interpretable estimates. Although the random-slope model yielded a lower R2 compared with GWR, it provides a theoretically robust framework for modeling spatial heterogeneity when working with aggregated administrative data. These results suggest that while GWR is useful for exploratory detection of spatial non-stationarity, multilevel random-effects models may offer a more appropriate inferential framework when analysing district-level DHS data. Despite its advantages, GWR has several methodological limitations. These include potential multicollinearity among local coefficients, multiple hypothesis testing, and increased computational complexity [28, 40]. Nevertheless, the method provides a more realistic representation of spatially varying epidemiological processes and offers valuable insights for geographically targeted public health responses.

This study has several strengths. It utilises nationally representative DHS data, incorporates both hierarchical modelling techniques and spatial regression methods, and provides detailed insights into the spatial variability of malaria predictors that would not have been captured using conventional global regression models, thereby providing valuable evidence to inform geographically targeted public health interventions. Although the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) values from GWR are not directly comparable in absolute terms to those from global models, the substantially lower AIC and BIC observed in the GWR analysis suggest an improved model fit when spatial non-stationarity is accounted for.

Limitations

A major limitation of our study was the cross-sectional nature of DHS data, which limits causal inference, and reliance on self-reported fever, which may introduce recall or reporting bias. Unlike Malaria Indicator Surveys (MIS), which often include parasitological confirmation through rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) or microscopy, the DHS dataset used in this study did not contain laboratory-confirmed malaria test results for children. Consequently, childhood malaria was defined using reported fever episodes, an approach commonly adopted in DHS-based malaria studies [24–26]. However, fever is a non-specific symptom that may arise from a range of infectious and non-infectious conditions. As a result, some children classified as having malaria may have experienced fever due to causes unrelated to malaria, potentially leading to outcome misclassification and an overestimation of malaria burden. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the magnitude of the estimated associations. Nevertheless, in the absence of parasitologically confirmed malaria data, reported fever remains a useful proxy for assessing malaria-related morbidity in population-based surveys.

A further limitation concerns the use of district centroids as the spatial reference for the GWR analysis. Because precise geographic locations for individual children were not available, all observations within a district were assigned the same centroid coordinates. While this approach enabled estimation of spatially varying associations across Malawi, it inevitably simplified the data's underlying spatial structure. Districts may contain substantial heterogeneity in environmental, socioeconomic, and malaria-related conditions, and representing all observations by a single location may mask important within-district variation and introduce spatial uncertainty. Such simplification is consistent with the broader challenges associated with spatial aggregation and the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP), whereby statistical relationships may be influenced by the spatial scale and geographic representation of observations [41, 42]. Consequently, the estimated local coefficients should be interpreted as reflecting broad district-level spatial patterns in the associations between childhood malaria and the selected risk factors rather than precise local effects. Future studies using finer-scale geographic information, such as survey cluster coordinates, household locations, or community-level data, may provide a more detailed understanding of spatial heterogeneity in these associations.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated the value of Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR) for identifying spatial heterogeneity in the associations between childhood malaria and its predictors in Malawi. Unlike conventional global regression models, which assume spatially constant effects, GWR revealed substantial geographical variation in both the magnitude and direction of the estimated associations. These findings indicate that the influence of socioeconomic, household, and environmental factors on childhood malaria is not spatially uniform but varies across the country. The local coefficients generated by the model revealed distinct spatial patterns in the associations between these predictors and childhood malaria, suggesting that interventions effective in one area may not be equally effective elsewhere.

The findings highlight the importance of incorporating locally estimated associations into malaria control planning and decision-making. By accounting for spatial variation in the effects of key predictors, malaria programmes can move beyond one-size-fits-all approaches and develop more geographically targeted intervention strategies. In resource-constrained settings such as Malawi, this may contribute to more efficient allocation of prevention and treatment resources, ultimately improving the effectiveness of malaria control efforts.
