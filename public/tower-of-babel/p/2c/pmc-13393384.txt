Recognition Analysis of Ginseng Radix et Rhizoma and Confused Varieties according to Chemometrics and Machine Learning
Authors: Tingting Cao, Jiyu Zhao, Xuweisheng Ji, Hao Liang, Ning Zhou, Lin Kang, Feng Guo, Siyang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Ginseng radix et rhizoma (GRER) is widely used in China and around the world. Due to the shortages, high prices, and profitability, the market is also full of its confused varieties. Notoginseng radix et rhizoma, panacis quinquefolii radix, ginseng folium, and ginseng radix et rhizoma rubra are GRER’s common confused varieties, which affected the market order and drug safety in the case of incorrect use. Therefore, it is very important to realize the recognition analysis of GRER and its confused varieties. Methods: GRER and its confused varieties were studied using ultraperformance liquid chromatography–quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry to convert the data matrix. Then, the data matrix was used to conduct principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least-squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA). At the same time, the data matrix was also used to construct data identification models based on machine learning, and the best model was screened for external appraisal and verification analysis. Moreover, the differential chemical components were analyzed on the basis of feature screening. Results: the data matrices contained 1971 chemical components and PCA and PLS-DA results showed that GRER cannot be clearly distinguished from confused varieties. However, all the identification models based on K-nearest neighbor, artificial neural network, support vector machine, naive bayes, and random forest (RF) had an excellent identification effect with area under the curve (AUC) ≥0.980, accuracy ≥0.800, and precision ≥0.870 in which the RF model showed the best recognition effect with AUC = 1.000, accuracy = 1.000, and precision = 1.000. Twenty batches of test samples were accurately identified by external verification, and the correct rate was 100%. Pseudoginsenoside F11, ginsenoside Rb2, ginsenoside Ro, and so on were the important distinguishing chemical markers. Conclusion: compared with chemometrics, machine learning presented better identification results. Moreover, the RF model has the best recognition effect, which helps to identify the GRER and its confused varieties. In addition, the characteristic components based on feature screening also contributed to the discrimination of the GRER and its confused varieties.

Introduction

Ginseng radix et rhizoma (GRER), often referred to as the king of herbs, is widely used in China and around the world. GRER has effects of promoting fluid production, nourishing blood, calming the nerves, and improving intelligence. GRER has dual-purpose characteristics of medicine and food, and despite its high price, it is still in short supply. However, due to the shortages, high prices, and profitability, the market is also full of its confused varieties (substitution or adulteration). Notoginseng radix et rhizoma (NRER), panacis quinquefolii radix (PQER), ginseng folium (GSFM), and ginseng radix et rhizoma rubra (GRRR) are GRER’s common confused varieties. − NRER, PQER, and GRER are all medicinal roots and rhizomes belonging to the Araliaceae family. , However, their efficacies differs in focus. At the same time, GRRR is processed from GRER, but there were also instances in the market where GRER and other herbal materials were mistaken for GRRR. In addition, GSFM is the dried leaves of GRER and is added to GRER powder. Therefore, in the herbal markets, it affected the market order and drug safety in the case of incorrect use. It is very important to realize the recognition analysis of GRER and its confused varieties.

In order to identify GRER and its confused varieties, the researchers conducted extensive research. According to the experience of predecessors, the microscopic and morphological characteristics for distinguishing GRER, NRER, and PQER are summarized in Table . − Chan et al. established an liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS)-based mode to identify GRER and PQER. The result showed that ginsenoside Rf (C42H72O14) and pseudoginsenoside F11 (C42H72O14) were the important chemical markers. Based on near-infrared spectroscopy, the 2-6PC rule, and principal component analysis (PCA), Yap et al. developed a rapid and nondestructive method to distinguish GRER, NRER, and PQER. The results indicated that PQER exhibits a characteristic peak at approximately 1446 cm–1, which is absent in GRER. ,

Microscopic and Morphological Characteristics for GRER and Its Confused Varieties

Sandasi et al. integrated hyperspectral imaging, mid-infrared, and near-infrared technologies for data fusion, constructed efficient identification models using PCA and partial least-squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and achieved high-quality and accurate discrimination of GRER, NRER, and PQER with R 2 ≥0.90 and Q 2 ≥ 0.95. Li et al. conducted thermal analysis studies on GRER, NRER, PQER, and GRRR using thermogravimetry-derivative thermogravimetry and differential thermal analysis (DTA). The results showed that the DTA curve of GRER showed two distinct exothermic peaks at 323.03 and 524.14 °C; PQER exhibits two prominent exothermic peaks at 329.47 and 525.21 °C; NRER displays two clear exothermic peaks at 324.32 and 491.91 °C. whereas GRRR presents no obvious peaks in its DTA profile. Li et al. also developed an LC–MS method for the analysis of GRER and PQER. The results indicated that the ratio of ginsenoside Rf to 24­(R)-pseudoginsenoside F11 in GRER was greater than 700. Wang et al. established an online integrated ultraperformance liquid chromatography–quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UPLC–QTOF-MS) method for in-depth characterization and comprehensive comparison of 12 ginseng varieties. The results revealed that 1146 ginsenosides were identified, and 126 potential ginsenoside markers were screened for their authentication. Hong et al. applied ultrahigh-performance supercritical fluid chromatography ion mobility quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UHPSFC/IM-QTOF-MS) coupled with nontargeted metabolomics to analyze 12 Panax herbal medicines, and chemometric analysis revealed significant differences in the lipidome composition of GRER, GSFM, and GRRR. Based on LC–MS, Zhang et al. monitored the characteristic fragment ions at m/z 407.37 and m/z 423.36 derived from sapogenin cleavage, enabling the discrimination of GRER from its adulterants. Kim et al. established an analytical method using direct infusion-tandem mass spectrometry (DI-MS/MS) and the results demonstrated that DI-MS/MS coupled with chemometrics could effectively discriminate GRER, NRER, and PQER based on four target ions at m/z 783.5, 945.5, 1107.5, and 1149.2. The aforementioned studies are conducive to the identification of GRER and its confused varieties. However, there are inevitably some limitations. In terms of morphology and microscopy, the limitations are also quite evident and the work requires a solid professional foundation. The spectroscopy coupled with PCA, OPLS-DA and machine learning enables nondestructive online identification, while it is limited by low qualitative accuracy, weak anti-interference performance and inadequate detection of trace components. The identification of GRER and its confused varieties based on chemical compositions is relatively well-established but researchers still using traditional analytical methods rather than digital authentication technology, which is difficult to keep up with the digital development trend.

As can be seen from the previous analysis, the digital models based on PCA and PLS-DA algorithms have been widely explored in the analytical field of TCM, which facilitates the identification confused varieties and the extraction of proprietary chemical constituents. On the other hand, as the main force driving the development of artificial intelligence, machine learning such as random forest (RF), artificial neural networks (ANNs), support vector machine (SVM), naive bayes (NB), and K-nearest neighbor (KNN) also holds significant importance for achieving digital identification of traditional Chinese medicine. In addition, LC–MS combines an LC system with excellent separation performance and a high-precision MS system and has become a common analytical technique in TCM, capable of ensuring a large volume of chemical component data while maintaining accuracy. For example, Peng et al. adopted UPLC–QTOF-MS coupled with molecular networking to systematically characterize chemical constituents in Dayuanyin decoction, and preliminarily identified 216 compounds such as flavonoids and alkaloids. Based on UPLC–QTOF-MS analysis, Wu et al. identified and compared Lonicera japonica Thunb. and Lonicera hypoglauca Miq., and detected 72 chemical constituents including isochlorogenic acid A–C. Although LC–MS requires complex sample pretreatment, it has excellent structural elucidation capability, ultrahigh sensitivity and high resolution. Compared with spectroscopy and HPLC, this technique can obtain comprehensive mass spectral information and achieve favorable accuracy and reproducibility in quantitative detection. Therefore, considering the current research limitations as well as the advantages and practical applications of LC–MS, PCA, PLS-DA, and machine learning algorithms, analytical methods were established and validated in this study. UPLC–QTOF-MSE was used to characterize the chemical profiles of GRER, NRER, PQER, GSFM, and GRRR to acquire corresponding mass spectral data. Afterward, the spectral data were quantified for multivariate statistical analysis with PCA and PLS-DA. Meanwhile, predictive models were constructed based on RF, ANNs, SVM, NB, and KNN algorithms. Chemometric analysis results were compared with model outputs, and the optimal model was selected for external identification validation. Finally, differential chemical constituents were identified by feature screening.

Materials and Methods

Herbs and Reference Materials

Fifteen GSFM, 30 GRER, 20 NRER, and 20 PQER were sourced from various locations across China. All herbal materials were authenticated by team and met the requirements. Fifteen GRRR was bought from Huai’an Tongrentang Pharmacy and 5 GRRR was produced by the team. Table S11 presents the specific information on herbal materials. The ginsenoside Rf (lot no. 111719-202407, purity ≥95%), ginsenoside Rb1 (lot no. 110704-202432, purity ≥98%), ginsenoside Rb2 (lot no. 111715-202104, purity ≥95%), 20­(S)-ginsenoside Rg2 (lot no. 111779-200801, purity ≥95%), notoginsenoside R1 (lot no. 110745-202322, purity ≥95%), ginsenoside Rg3 (lot no. 110804-201504, purity ≥98%), ginsenoside Ro (lot no. 111903-202307, purity ≥92%), and pseudoginsenoside F11 (lot no. 110841-202410, purity ≥98%) were purchased from National Institutes for Food and Drug Control.

Analytical Reagent

Methanol, formic acid, and acetonitrile with lot numbers EF054-US, 250391, and 222372, respectively, were purchased from Honeywell Trading Co., Ltd. The water was produced by an ELGA Purelab chorus 1 LS ultrapure water system.

Sample Treatment and Liquid Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry Methods

20.00 mg of reference materials was accurately weighed, into which 200 mL of methanol was added. Subsequently, 1.00 mL of this solution was diluted to 200 mL to prepare a standard solution for the chemical identification.

All herbs were ground into fine powder and then screened through a no. 3 sieve. 1.00 g each of GRER and its confused varieties was precisely weighed individually, and 50 mL of methanol was added to each sample for 0.5 h of ultrasonic processing (power: 500 W, frequency: 40 kHz). Finally, each mixture was filtered through a 0.22 μm microfiltration membrane to obtain the corresponding test samples. ,, In addition, the quality control sample was a mixed sample of GRER, NRER, PQER, GSFM, and GRRR.

The UPLC–QTOF-MS were performed on a SCIEX Zeno UPLC-QTOF 7600 system. A Waters Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 1.7 μm) was employed for LC. Column temperature was regulated at 40 °C. Table shows the mobile phase conditions.

Data Processing and Analysis

The MS data of GRER, NRER, PQER, GSFM, and GRRR were processed by QI Tool 2.4 (Waters, USA) to obtain a three-dimensional quantitative data matrix consisting of retention time, mass-to-charge ratio, and ion abundance. During the data conversion process, the mixed quality control sample of the five medicinal materials served as the standard for data conversion. The specific parameters are as follows: retention time: 0 ∼ 34 min; MS peaks: automatic extraction; retention time deviation: 0.3 min. Based on the above parameters, we converted three-dimensional MS information into two-dimensional data points for analysis. Simca.P 14.1 (Umetrics, Umea, Sweden) was used for PCA and PLS-DA, in which R 2 and Q 2 were used to evaluate the model quality. In addition, Orange software 3.39 was used for data modeling, in which RF, SVM, ANNs, NB, and KNN were employed. , First, import the quantified two-dimensional data matrix into Orange software for reading. Second, set NRER, PQER, GSFM, GRRR, and GRER as classification targets, with Rt-m/z and ion intensity as feature variables and variable values, respectively. Then in the software’s model module, select RF, SVM, ANNs, NB, and KNN algorithms to construct data models. Finally, based on parameters such as accuracy, precision, and recall from the evaluate module, select the optimal model for external validation and identification analysis.

Precision Assessment

The mixed quality control samples of GRER, NRER, PQER, GSFM, and GRRR were tested in parallel five times to assess precision. Using retention times of 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 as divisions, one ion was selected from each segment to record its retention time, mass-to-charge ratio, and ion intensity, which were used to calculate the RSD value. Tables S1–S3 show precision assessment results. The RSDs were all less than 1.00%, which shows that the analytical method has good precision.

Repeatability Assessment

The five parallel batches of mixed quality control samples of GRER, NRER, PQER, GSFM, and GRRR were tested to assess the repeatability. Using retention times of 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 as divisions, one ion was selected from each segment to record its retention time, m/z, and ion intensity, which were used to calculate the RSD value. Tables S4–S6 show the results of repeatability assessment, and the RSDs were all less than 0.30%, which showed that the analytical method had good repeatability.

Stability Evaluation

The mixed quality control samples of GRER, NRER, PQER, GSFM, and GRRR were tested to assess stability at 0, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h. Using retention times of 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 as divisions, one ion was selected from each segment to record its retention time, m/z, and ion intensity, which were used to calculate the RSD value. Tables S7–S9 show the results of repeatability assessment, and the RSDs were all less than 0.70%, which showed that the samples had good stability.

Based on the above methodological validation, the analytical method developed in this study demonstrated good specificity, high accuracy and precision, excellent reproducibility, and stable sample performance within 48 h, thereby meeting the analytical requirements. On the other hand, as shown in Figure , GRER, NRER, PQER, GSFM, and GRRR showed different chromatograms under uniform conditions. As for small quantities of specimens, the identification can be achieved simply by comparing chromatograms. However, relying solely on chromatograms analysis to establish a correlations of the chemical compositions of GRER with its confused varieties presents considerable difficulty, let alone the impracticability of identifying GRER itself, its confusable counterparts, and conducting adulteration analysis by virtue of discrepancies in their chemical constituents. Nevertheless, multivariate analytical techniques can facilitate differential analysis of the chemical compositions of GRER, NRER, PQER, GSFM, and GRRR, thereby aiding in the screening of potential chemical markers. Consequently, in the following section, the data quantification on the base-peak chromatograms was performed based on the QI tool and subsequently was carried out chemometric analysis.

AUC represents the specific area enclosed jointly by the receiver operating characteristic curve and the axes of the coordinate system. Accuracy represents how well the model makes correct predictions. Precision is a measure of the proportion of predicted positive samples that are actually positive samples. Recall is a measure of the proportion of all data that are actually positive samples that are correctly predicted as positive samples. The F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. The parameters described above range from 0 to 1. The closer the value is to 1, the better the model performs. The MCC is a comprehensive evaluation parameter based on confusion matrix calculations. The MCC ranges from −1 to 1 and the closer the value is to 1, the better the model performs. Discussion

Prior to the commencement of the trial, an analytical method was developed. First, the ESI+, ESI– ion mode, methanol–water, and acetonitrile–water mobile phase systems were compared, and more component information could be detected in ESI– mode and acetonitrile–water mobile phase systems. Unlike quantitative analysis, qualitative analysis does not have explicit methodological requirements. However, to ensure the accuracy of MS data acquisition, this study primarily referenced the “Guidance on Analytical Method Validation” and “Guidance on Validation of Analytical Methods for Biological Samples from the Chinese Pharmacopoeia” to conduct the methodological research. Accuracy was evaluated using known chemical components-ginsenoside Rf, ginsenoside Rb1, ginsenoside Rb2, 20­(R)-ginsenoside Rg2, and calibrated leucine enkephalin. In addition, the RSD values for precision, repeatability, and stability were all less than 0.50%, which indicated that the analytical method is reliable and ensures the accurate acquisition of MS data.

As for PCA and PLS-DA, this study did not achieve the ideal results. The Q2 of PCA and PLS-DA models were less than 0.5, which to some extent indicated that the model was not very reliable due to various influencing factors such as data fluctuations and instrument status. Therefore, the different chemical components important for distinguishing GRER, NRER, PQER, GSFM, and GRRR were not screened on the basis of VIP values. The reason why chemometric analysis did not yield satisfactory results is that GRER, NRER, PQER, GSFM, and GRRR all belong to the Araliaceae family and have many of the same chemical components, such as saponins. Moreover, the MS analyses of GRER, NRER, PQER, GSFM, and GRRR were not collected at the same time, increasing systematic errors. Therefore, when they were included in the same PCA or PLS-DA model, their effects on the model is greatly reduced. Furthermore, compared with chemometrics, the machine learning presented a good identification result, especially the RF model. When screening for the optimal model, evaluations were conducted across three dimensions: 5-fold, 10-fold, and 20-fold. It consistently indicated that the RF model proved to be the top-performing model. During data modeling, an iterative parameter optimization strategy was employed to ultimately determine the optimal parameters for each model. Table S10 presents the optimal parameters for each model.

In the data modeling results, significant differences are observed in the identification performance of various algorithms. KNN presents the worst accuracy, while RF achieves optimal values across all evaluation indicators. Such discrepancies stem from distinctions in algorithmic principles, adaptability to complex features, and an anti-interference capacity against abnormal data. KNN conducts classification merely based on sample spatial distance, which tends to cause misjudgment of boundary samples when processing highly similar chromatographic profiles of the five medicinal materials. The SVM classifies samples via optimal hyperplanes, yet a single hyperplane fails to accurately characterize subtle compositional differences among medicinal herbs, resulting in limited classification potential. The NB algorithm is established on the premise of mutually independent features, whereas inherent correlations exist among ginsenoside components of medicinal materials. This inconsistent theoretical assumption restricts the classification accuracy to a certain extent. Although ANNs possess a strong nonlinear fitting capacity, they are prone to falling into local optimal solutions and cannot fully extract valid features during parameter tuning. As an ensemble learning method, RF realizes joint judgment through multiple decision trees, which can effectively avoid the inherent defects of the algorithms described above. In addition, abnormal samples deviating from the confidence interval were found in the experiment. RF was relatively insensitive to noisy data and abnormal samples. By integration of the judgments of multiple models, this algorithm could substantially reduce the probability of misclassification.

In terms of model establishment, over 100 batches of GRER, NRER, PQER, GSFM, and GRRR were collected by different personnel at different times and subjected to MS analysis in different laboratories to acquire the relevant MS data and construct data models. Therefore, the MS data employed by this institute comprehensively accounted for variations across laboratories and operators, thereby ensuring the robustness of both the data and the models. In addition, sample balance also affects model performance. The sample sizes in this study were 15 for GSFM, 30 for GRER, 20 for NRER, 20 for PQER, and 20 for GRRR. Despite numerical differences, no extreme imbalance exists, thus causing no obvious data bias. This finding is consistent with the confusion matrix and the model evaluation results. To avoid model overfitting, the data set was strictly divided into training and validation sets with no overlapping samples. Optimal parameters of each model were fixed, and 5-fold, 10-fold, and 20-fold cross-validations were performed, respectively. Moreover, internal cross-validation alone cannot guarantee the accuracy and reliability of the models. Therefore, external authentication and validation analyses are necessary. This study employed 20 batches of samples for external validation analysis, achieving a 100% accuracy rate. Notably, the classification probability of sample GRER004 belonging to GRER was 82%, meaning that 82% of the decision trees in RF categorized it as GRER, while 18% were classified as other varieties. This slightly lower value indicates that the sample lies near the classification boundary with partial characteristic similarity to the confused species. Nevertheless, the model achieved the correct identification. Such fluctuations are a normal prediction deviation without systematic error, which verifies the robust discrimination capability of the model. Therefore, the model did not exhibit overfitting. In addition, given that samples were collected in different laboratories across various institutions, ensuring data consistency is of paramount importance. In experimental analysis, to maximize consistency, a mixture of the five herbs was uniformly used as the quality control sample for data conversion. During data conversion, the data from each laboratory were inconsistent. Therefore, the deviation was initially confirmed by the retention times of the compounds with known chemical compositions. Then, the MS data were imported into the QI tool for automatic deconvolution, thereby obtaining data that maximally preserve consistency.

Compared with current research, − this study achieved significant advances in research design, methodological system and practical application value. It covered four frequently encountered confusable varieties in the market, namely NRER, PQER, GSFM, and GRRR. A systematic comparative framework integrating traditional chemometrics (PCA/PLS-DA) with five machine learning algorithms (RF, ANNs, SVM, NB, KNN) was innovatively constructed, which clarified the limitations of conventional methods and enables rigorous screening of the optimal model. A unified data transformation strategy using mixed quality control samples was proposed to effectively eliminate data deviations across multiple laboratories and batches. Ten specific differential markers were accurately screened from massive MS data based on the Gini coefficient, facilitating traceability of medicinal materials and adulteration identification. Furthermore, representative samples from multiple origins and batches were adopted, comprehensive methodological validation was completed, and multifold cross-validation as well as validation with 20 independent external samples was performed to ensure model robustness. The accuracy of external validation reached 100%.

In addition, as for the analysis results, the chemical constituents identified through feature screening based on the Gini index are largely consistent with current research on GRER and its confused varieties. Compared to PCA and PLS-DA, machine learning presented good identification result, especially the RF model. All in all, compared to chemometrics, machine learning presented better identification results. Moreover, the RF model had the best recognition effect, which helps to identify the GRER and its confused varieties. In addition, the characteristic components based on feature screening also contributed to the identification of GRER and its confused varieties, even adulterated samples. For example, if PQER is adulterated in GRRR samples, then identification and analysis can be achieved through testing for pseudoginsenoside F11. However, inevitably, this study also has some shortcomings: based on reference substances and literature comparison, 7 characteristic components were identified in the top 10 chemical markers. There were still three ions whose specific chemical compositions remained unclear, and further exploration is required. On the other hand, although the samples encompassed different years, origins, batches, and testing institutions, the data set remained limited. To establish a more precise and reliable data model, subsequent research should continue to expand the sample size. In addition to cases where PQER and other substitutes are sold as GRRR, the adulterations does indeed occur in the market. While adulteration detection is achievable based on screening for characteristic chemical components, the detection limits have not been determined. Therefore, in subsequent research, we will focus on methodological studies, such as establishing detection limits.

Conclusions

Based on UPLC–QTOF-MS analysis, chemometrics and machine learning were applied to identify GRER, NRER, PQER, GSFM, and GRRR. Compared with chemometrics, machine learning presented better identification results. Moreover, the RF model had the best recognition effect, which helped to identify the GRER and its confused varieties to a certain extent. In addition, the characteristic components-ginsenoside Rf, ginsenoside Rb1, ginsenoside Rb2, ginsenoside Ro, pseudoginsenoside F11, notoginsenoside R1, and ginsenoside Rg3 based on Gini feature screening also contributed to the identification of GRER and its confused varieties.
