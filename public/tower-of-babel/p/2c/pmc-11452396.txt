Meta-learning for real-world class incremental learning: a transformer-based approach
Authors: Sandeep Kumar, Amit Sharma, Vikrant Shokeen, Ahmad Taher Azar, Syed Umar Amin, Zafar Iqbal Khan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Modern natural language processing (NLP) state-of-the-art (SoTA) deep learning (DL) models have hundreds of millions of parameters, making them extremely complex. Large datasets are required for training these models, and while pretraining has reduced this requirement, human-labelled datasets are still necessary for fine-tuning. Few-shot learning (FSL) techniques, such as meta-learning, try to train models from smaller datasets to mitigate this cost. However, the tasks used to evaluate these meta-learners frequently diverge from the problems in the real world that they are meant to resolve. This work aims to apply meta-learning to a problem that is more pertinent to the real world: class incremental learning (IL). In this scenario, after completing its training, the model learns to classify newly introduced classes. One unique quality of meta-learners is that they can generalise from a small sample size to classes that have never been seen before, which makes them especially useful for class incremental learning (IL). The method describes how to emulate class IL using proxy new classes. This method allows a meta-learner to complete the task without the need for retraining. To generate predictions, the transformer-based aggregation function in a meta-learner that modifies data from examples across all classes has been proposed. The principal contributions of the model include concurrently considering the entire support and query sets, and prioritising attention to crucial samples, such as the question, to increase the significance of its impact during inference. The outcomes demonstrate that the model surpasses prevailing benchmarks in the industry. Notably, most meta-learners demonstrate significant generalisation in the context of class IL even without specific training for this task. This paper establishes a high-performing baseline for subsequent transformer-based aggregation techniques, thereby emphasising the practical significance of meta-learners in class IL.

Introduction

Natural language processing (NLP) has progressed from statistical models such as the Hidden Markov model (HMM) to deep learning (DL) techniques like transformers and long short-term memory (LSTM)1–7. These methods heavily depend on massive amounts of training data, like the 600 million relation pairs in FewRel, the 300 billion words in Generative Pre-Trained Transformers (GPT)-3, and the 2.5 billion words in Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT). Pretraining, as described in8, reduces the need for a significant amount of supervised data. Despite FewRel's 70,000 labelled samples, crowdsourcing was required due to volume, highlighting labelling challenges. This emphasises the necessity for few-shot learning (FSL) techniques, which minimise labelling efforts and alleviate the strain of learning from smaller datasets. Thus, this paper explores FSL techniques such as language model prompting in NLP and meta-learning. By using episodic learning and meta-learning with an emphasis on learning policies, models can effectively handle FSL scenarios by classifying new samples with a few examples. Despite displaying outstanding performance in the field of natural language processing (NLP), its evaluation via 5- or 10-way problems does not adequately capture the practical implications of intelligent classification across all classes. The widespread evaluation method shows that meta-learners are not being used properly for a more complete NLP classification, which makes it clear that more pragmatic implementations are needed in this area9,10.

In the domain of open-world learning, particularly in the context of Incremental Learning (IL), there is an ongoing debate about the appropriateness of employing meta-learning for extensive classification problems considering other techniques such as few-shot learning (FSL)11. This approach becomes significant in practical situations that involve increasing class sets, such as object detection or disease classification. Class incremental learning (IL) systems readily incorporate new classes without demanding extensive retraining, in contrast to closed-world supervised learning. A recent study investigates the integration of meta-learners, which are well known for their few-shot learning capabilities, into this situation. This approach eliminates the requirement for extensive retraining when introducing new classes12.

This work discusses the pragmatic issues and use cases that support the class-incremental learning (IL) scenario, enhancing its applicability to real-world problems. In many real-world situations, environments are dynamic, and new classes or categories constantly appear. Models must therefore integrate these new classes without requiring a significant amount of retraining. For example, when using object detection systems, new kind of items are constantly appearing in the environment. In medical diagnostics, the constant discovery of new diseases necessitates systems capable of quickly learning and identifying these new categories. Our goal in expanding on these examples is to demonstrate the usefulness of class IL. Traditional static learning models often fail in these dynamic environments due to the need for complete retraining to incorporate new information, a process that consumes significant computational resources and time.

Similarly, class IL encounters two primary obstacles: the requirement for partial retraining when incorporating new classes and the potential for neglecting older ones. Existing techniques such as bootstrapping require the development of unique classifiers for every newly introduced class, resulting in intricate learning processes13. Approaches that use examples from both familiar and unfamiliar classes strive to reduce confusion between different tasks but encounter difficulties in distinguishing between sets of classes that have been learned separately14. On the other hand, meta-learners only need a small number of class examples and use them directly for making inferences. This approach shows potential for resolving inter-task confusion without requiring extensive retraining, thereby potentially overcoming this challenge. Meta-learners provide viable solutions for the constraints in IL by utilising the capabilities of FSL. Current meta-learning methods face difficulties in effectively distinguishing support set examples that belong to overlapping classes.12 Suggest different representations for new categories; however, the process of training these new representations still poses a challenge. To take on this challenge, a new and innovative aggregation technique that utilises transformers has been presented. This technique significantly improves performance on Few-Shot Learning Natural Language Processing (FSL-NLP) datasets. Related works

This section presents a thorough examination of relevant literature and methodologies in this area. Its purpose is to offer a comprehensive overview of existing research and methodologies used in the objectives of the proposed paper.

Meta learning

The primary goal of meta-learning optimization is still to obtain a learning policy with parameters that are refined through gradient descent. At the outset, this materialized in several ways, most notably with the development of an optimizer trained on an LSTM network that produced immediate weight updates in response to gradients pertaining to the parameter loss of the model15. The incorporation of new parameters into the optimizer, which increased the method's potential susceptibility to overfitting, added complexity to an otherwise novel approach. The development of this discipline progressed by placing greater emphasis on the improvement of ideal initialization parameters for various tasks. This was exemplified by the Model Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML) framework16 and its subsequent modifications. This transition effectively removed the need to introduce new parameters and provided the clear benefit of utilizing established optimization algorithms, including ADAM. In addition, it exhibited a crucial attribute of model agnosticism, which is a unique algorithmic process that is crucial in determining the most effective initialization parameters for any given model. SNAIL17, a "Black-Box" meta-learning algorithm that procures weights via a support set, emerged concurrently during the same time. However, the enduring success and versatility of the MAML framework can be attributed to its wide-ranging implementation across various domains of natural language processing10. The ease with which it can be deployed in State-of-the-Art (SOTA) NLP architectures such as BERT is due to its model-agnostic characteristics, which have established it as a prominent entity in the NLP domain. MAML encounters considerable constraints because of the computational strain associated with recursive backpropagation to compute second-order derivatives for initialization parameters. In addition, the applicability of a MAML-optimized model in class IL is restricted to a fixed number of output classes, given that it primarily optimizes conventional supervised models. An alternative to MAML, LEOPARD10 introduced transfer learning with an adjustable quantity of output classes. They overcame this difficulty by generating parameters for the final classification layer using meta-learning like the initialization parameters. As demonstrated in18, their method has certain limitations, most notably the lack of normalization benefits associated with cosine similarity, as it employs weight dot products with query sample representations rather than cosine similarity.

Multi-instance multi-label (MIML)19 is an additional variant that has garnered significant attention. It employs class names as meta-information to reduce the impact of noise in the support set and accelerate learning. Although it exhibits performance comparable to SOTA on the FewRel dataset, its generalizability to other problems remains uncertain. By utilizing attention, the fast-adaptation technique restricts the capacity to learn from a support set, irrespective of its magnitude. In general, these models have demonstrated exceptional performance across a range of NLP tasks. In contrast to MAML, which is widely applicable to various model types, prototypical networks, and other metric learning (Table 1) methods can accommodate variable class numbers without requiring significant adjustments, as demonstrated in10. Additionally, they rely on a straightforward forward pass rather than gradient descent on the support set, which reduces the amount of computation required for both training and inference. These strategies implicitly discover how to optimize support sets by incorporating them into a more realistic contrast space, as opposed to explicitly learning a learning policy. The innovative algorithm for metric learning, Siamese networks19, generated feature vectors for individual images using Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). Prototypical networks

This section discusses prototypical networks, a technique in which metric-learning is responsible for the management of different class numbers. Prototypical networks are the subject of much attention among various metric-learning techniques because they maximize support set utilization through sophisticated aggregation techniques. The scoring module is still up for debate; it mostly involves Euclidean distance and other non-conventional methods, with a bias toward cosine similarity. The Euclidean distance was first introduced by21, but even with new methods that do not rely on averaging for prototype calculations, its use endures because of its origins. However,18 supports cosine similarity, demonstrating its efficacy in accommodating discrepancies in the magnitudes of prototype functions. In contrast to conventional supervised weights, the arbitrary nature of the support set causes magnitude variations in prototypes, which require a distance metric to counterbalance these effects. Other methods exist besides cosine similarity and Euclidean distance, including the utilization of dense layers and dot product, feature-level attention, and Mahalanobis distance. While the cosine similarity method serves as a robust foundation, it encounters a potentially unique challenge that may be addressed by exploring an alternative distance metric, as exemplified by the successful approach in24. Considering the number of effective approaches observed in prior research, the selection of the distance function may be model-dependent.

In the same way, the initial aggregation approach proposed by21 employs arithmetic mean, a metric that may encounter challenges when confronted with outliers; however, identifying viable alternatives is not a simple task. Investigating alternative methods such as the geometric mean may result in modifications to prototypes, thereby complicating comparisons. Prototypical networks prioritize query-aware methods to effectively compare data and accommodate outliers. Dynamic Memory Induction Networks (DMIN) accomplish SOTA results through aggregation utilizing capsule networks. Attention-based prototype calculations that concentrate on pertinent support set samples for the purpose of classification are additional effective methodologies. While DMIN exhibits potential, attention-based hybrid networks continue to be the preferred starting point. However, alternative approaches such as DMIN and other research that employ base class representations in their system development could provide significant contributions. Particularly for IL, researchers are developing aggregation techniques, which exemplifies the trend of adapting metric-learners to a variety of tasks. Hence, novel aggregation techniques are required in this context. Aggregation techniques are the subject of extensive discussion, but the encoding modules of many research papers such as25, which frequently employ BERT, a top-supervised model, demonstrate limited innovation. Nevertheless, research indicates that improving encodings, such as replacing BERT with LSTM, significantly improves the performance of older meta-learners26. In27, an innovative encoding technique for the classification of relations has been proposed. By utilizing the supposition that the head and tail entities in a relation class maintain a consistent mapping (H + R = T), they achieve their objective. The methodology they employ entails generating prototypes for these entities and amalgamating them with a sentence prototype to form a relation prototype. Research on ablation reveals that this technique yields significant improvements in performance. Particularly noteworthy are the substantial advancements achieved through the implementation of enhanced encoding techniques, as illustrated in23. Incremental learning

Incremental learning (IL) works by assuming that every class in a problem is in the dataset. However, new things continue to emerge. For example, while the FewRel dataset initially contains 100 relations, tens of thousands more have since surfaced. The models' tendency to conflate new and existing information due to this closed-world assumption poses a risk; particularly in domains such as medicine where incorrect diagnoses may result in inappropriate treatments, (the authors are investigating the use of meta-learning to manage this gradual learning process). In general, three primary categories of open world models exist. The first is Open Set Recognition (OSR), which is concerned with excluding elements from categories that are unknown. IL continues to teach a variety of new subjects on an ongoing basis, like online education. Class IL, on the other hand, teaches a model to recognize new categories. This is difficult to accomplish without retraining, however, because standard models cannot process new categories without modification. Open World Recognition (OWR) attempts to perform all three of the following: class detection, learning incrementally, and class rejection. However, automatic identification of new classes is a challenging task for which there are currently few viable solutions. This work, on the other hand, is not concerned with rejection when discussing IL. Although rejection can be convenient, models that rely on it frequently compromise the accuracy of existing knowledge, posing a significant challenge. Meta-learning appears auspicious for IL since it can seamlessly incorporate new classes and can acquire knowledge from a limited number of examples.

Certain earlier approaches, such as the one proposed by18, concentrated on classroom instruction without employing meta-learning. They attempted to generate representations for new classes by recalling information from previous classes; however, their inability to utilize episodic training hindered their capacity to learn from a limited number of examples. Others, such as28, developed a method for meta-learning without specifically designing it for IL. Since their method does not train specifically for the incremental task, this becomes a limitation. In language comprehension,12 implemented meta-learning on an IL task. They encoded base and new classes in two distinct ways and combined these encodings using attention. However, since the introduction of new classes caused complications, their method required retraining, and they had difficulty distinguishing between existing and new classes. By training directly for IL using simulated new classes, the method attempts to eliminate the need for retraining whenever new classes appear. This paper also talks about how to avoid new classes overlapping with existing ones by adding simulated new classes to the model to help build strong representations. IL encounters obstacles such as forgetting previously learned material and becoming confused between tasks. To avoid some of these issues, the meta-learning method is trained only once for a set of base classes and continues to add new classes without retraining. Confusion between tasks represents a notable obstacle for the model, as it encounters difficulties in distinguishing classes originating from distinct tasks due to its lack of explicit training in task separation. This issue has been addressed by simulated new classes, which is like the difficulty encountered in other research involving previously identified overlap between new and existing classes.

Model compression: knowledge distillation

The objective of Model Compression (MC) is to reduce the size of sizable models without sacrificing integrity, thereby enabling their utilization on hardware with lower power consumption. It entails instructing a miniature model to imitate a larger one. The approach, first proposed in reference29, involved training a neural network to replicate the predictions of intricate decision tree ensembles to simplify them. They accomplished this by minimizing the difference in prediction logits between the larger and smaller models, thereby effectively transferring knowledge from the larger model to the smaller model, particularly in situations where the initial training data was scarce. By employing knowledge distillation, a method popularized by30, the logits of the larger model serve as "soft" labels, providing the smaller model with more comprehensive information regarding similarities between classes. Enhancements in knowledge distillation facilitated the expansion of techniques through the reduction of disparities in hidden state activations between smaller and larger models31. The utilization of this phased methodology augmented the efficacy of information dissemination, specifically for models such as BERT, thereby elevating its applicability in compression techniques.

MC techniques are primarily associated with two fundamental methods: quantization and pruning. As a result of decreasing the precision of a model's activations and weights, quantization reduces the model's memory and storage requirements. In contrast, pruning involves assessing the significance of each weight in the model and eliminating those that are deemed irrelevant. Unstructured pruning entails the removal of weights on an individual basis; structured pruning, on the other hand, entails the elimination of weights in clusters or entire layers. Structured pruning is an exceptionally advantageous technique that not only facilitates improvements in speed but also results in significant reductions in memory consumption. Nevertheless, a significant aspect to keep in mind regarding structured pruning is that its effectiveness may cease once many irrelevant weights have been eliminated. This could have an adverse impact on the overall performance of the model due to the substantial size reduction. When memory is at a premium, as in the case of meta-learning utilizing large transformers like BERT, model compression techniques such as knowledge distillation are indispensable. In contrast to quantization, it effectively decreases the size of the model while sacrificing minimal accuracy, resulting in a substantial increase in speed without requiring specialized software. Distilling knowledge for large language models has been the subject of ongoing research, which has yielded innovations such as TinyBERT32, which significantly outperforms BERT-base and reduces its size by a factor of 10. In the face of alternative compression methods, empirical evidence supports knowledge distillation on account of its advantageous trade-off between memory usage and speedup.

Methodology

In this work, the shortcomings of meta-learning evaluation have been identified, and an IL-specific class meta-learner is developed here. To address the limitation of existing models that require retraining, a training methodology that utilizes surrogate new classes to simulate the class IL challenge has been developed. The strategy entails developing a transformer-based meta-learner that is proficient in an aggregation technique to take advantage of this specialized training regimen. Furthermore, the procedure for implementing model compression within this framework will be elaborated.

Surrogate novel class

To address the limitation of current class incremental learning, a machine learning method known as class incremental learning (CIL) introduces additional classes gradually after a model has been trained to identify a set of classes. The model must be able to adjust to new classes while remembering those it has already learnt. IL models, which require additional training to incorporate new classes, the introduction of a meta-learner is incorporated here. This learner requires exclusive training focused on base classes. However, a significant issue arises: in the absence of a training mechanism that integrates both base classes and new classes, how will the model effectively manage the ambiguity between different tasks? To address this concern, surrogate novel classes that are generated via self-supervised training methods have been utilized to simulate new classes. By employing these methods, a novel and previously unseen category for every meta-learning episode is created, emulating the IL task. This approach promotes the meta-learner's understanding of incorporating classes that it has not previously encountered. The conventional approach to episodic training in meta-learning involves the selection of N classes from a base class set B = {C1, C2, ..., Cb} to form the classes of episode E ⊆ B. For each of the selected classes, the authors proceed to randomly choose k examples to create distinct support and query sets (Algorithm 1). Subsequently, the testing phase involves the selection of query sets and support from a set of new classes, denoted as NC = {Cb + 1, Cb + 2, ..., Cb + n}, which are distinct from the ones used during training. This configuration replicates the objective of the typical supervised learning test set: assessing the model's ability to adapt to unfamiliar categories. During IL tests, the support and query sets include new and unique classes, E ←Uniform Sample (B ∪ NC, N), which creates an unfamiliar situation for the meta-learner. Due to a lack of training in distinguishing between base and novel classes, the model experiences inter-task confusion. To address it, the authors aim to make the training setup more closely resemble the class IL test setting by incorporating surrogate novel classes during the training process. At first, a substantial collection of substitute novel categories, SC = {SC1, SC2, ...SCs}, is created. During the training process, a predetermined number of NS << N of these classes is included in the support and query sets. This is done by using a sampling method like E ←Uniform Sample (B ∪ SC, N), which is reminiscent of class IL. The specific algorithm is described in Algorithm 2. The idea of emulating new classes is not revolutionary; others have used similar techniques to strengthen their models against challenges posed by new classes. However, in previous approaches, these novel categories were derived from a predetermined holdout set that was distinct from the original training set. This diminishes the quantity of training data accessible and presents a predicament: either the novel classes are excessively utilized in training, potentially leading to overfitting akin to other training classes, or they are sparingly employed in model updates, thereby diminishing their impact.

To generate substitute novel classes, a self-guided approach is required to create one class for each episode. With a training course consisting of 30,000 episodes, a challenge develops: generating a considerable number of classes, each having at least 10 examples. Forming support and query sets in the 5-shot task is essential. To accomplish this, the pre-training technique described in23 is utilized. Instances sharing identical head and tail entities are presumed to belong to the same class. Meta learning with transformer

Here a flexible aggregation model specifically designed to tackle the class-IL challenge has been proposed. In contrast to the manual construction of an aggregation method using attention mechanisms, as exemplified by12, this approach entails developing a transformer that is equipped with learned attention weights. These weights facilitate enhanced regulation of the utilization of attention mechanism outcomes. The Meta-learning Transformer (MetaFormer) model can acquire knowledge from and potentially outperform other models, such as prototypical networks. Prior studies have explored analogous aggregating models that can be learned. For example, matching networks utilize an attention-based LSTM to specifically assist with the selection of support set samples. Conversely, the model utilizes a transformer with enhanced attention mechanisms. Another approach that explores the utilization of a transformer to modify the support set is referred to as Few-shot Embedding Adaptation with Transformer (FEAT)28. However, their procedure involves initially generating prototypes for each category in the support set, followed by making modifications to these prototypes. This method limits the data aggregation in the transformer to the mean value of the K samples for each class. In contrast to this, the adaptation encompasses both the query set and all support samples, providing the transformer with a more comprehensive comprehension and consolidation of the support set.

Before proceeding with the adaptation process, it is essential to include queries (Q) in the support set (S) for query adaptation. However, it is challenging to determine which portions of the query set should be incorporated into the adapted set. This approach transforms the model into a form that bears similarity to a transductive algorithm. In situations where there is only one query available, which is often the case in real-life scenarios, the learning process may encounter difficulties. Despite learning from a specific set of examples to make predictions for a particular query set, it may struggle to gain insights from the query distribution. Alternatively, it is proposed to use an inductive approach to adaptation, where the adaptation function is provided with a single query. This approach allows the model to tailor its response to a particular query, as different queries may require unique adjustments. The new set-to-set transformation algorithm, as described in Algorithm 4, sequentially processes each query by incorporating it into the support set. This support set is subsequently adjusted and employed to compute the logits. It is important to note that the Meta-Former functions as a versatile learning and synthesis model. Transformer as set-to-set transformation

The development of Transformers was aimed at harnessing the potent capabilities of attention mechanisms. This led to the creation of a model that is exclusively structured around these mechanisms and feed-forward layers. These self-attention mechanisms enable transformers to understand sequence-to-sequence problems comprehensively, which makes them useful for addressing support set adaptation issues. The authors propose using a modified trans-former, referred to as tθ, to perform the set-to-set transformation. This design incorporates worldwide focus to enhance understanding of the distribution of the support set, utilizing this understanding to perform transformations, as depicted in Fig. 1. Transformers, originally designed as an encoder-er-decoder architecture for neural machine translation, are devices that encode and decode an input sequence (in the source language) into an output sequence (in the target language). The encoder utilizes neural networks and multiple layers of multi-head attention to process a sequence of word representations and generate an encoded vector. The decoder, a variant of the encoder, utilizes inputs from the encoder and previous decoder outputs to determine probable words. Following that,8 introduced BERT, an encoder-only architecture that transforms an input sequence into an output sequence of equivalent size. The encoder only method is well suited for set-to-set transformation, where the input of the support set is changed to generate the output. The transformer architecture is founded on the concept of attention, which quantifies the degree of similarity between two vectors. The advantage of attention lies in its ability to provide comprehensive sequential knowledge. Within the context of language modeling, attention scores (aij) are calculated between each query word (Qi) and key word (Kj) in the sequence. This calculation helps to identify related words that are spread out across the text. The use of remote but relevant words facilitates comprehension of the text—imagine losing character details after each page in a book. Similarly, the objective is to utilize attention mechanisms to provide meta-learners with a thorough understanding of the support set, using this understanding to carry out adaptation like what is shown in Fig. 1. Specifically, the focus on the query and the associated sample from the new class (as shown in Fig. 1a) would be significantly stronger, making it easier to adapt the query in a comparable manner. The proposal entails utilizing a transformer that adjusts by calculating attention relationships among the elements of the support set, as depicted in Fig. 2.Fig. 2How attention can be used to adapt a support set.

How attention can be used to adapt a support set.

The computational complexity of attention, which is O(n2) with respect to the length of the sequence, presents a substantial difficulty. The complexity arises from the need to compute n dot products for every element in the sequence, resulting in high memory requirements for modern pretrained models. The complexity of managing support sets for problems with many categories, such as the experiments involving 295 items, becomes evident. Nevertheless, aggregation methods exhibit reduced reliance on parameters, thereby mitigating this constraint. In addition, transformers enhance comprehension by incorporating positional embedding vectors into input elements, which are essential for discerning their sequential positions. The positional invariance of the attention mechanism, which treats items i and j equally regardless of their order in the sequence, is beneficial for set-to-set transformations. The consistency mentioned here corresponds to the argument made by28 that tθ(S) should yield the same outcomes regardless of the permutations applied to S, thus guaranteeing transformation stability.

Regulaization

Meta-learning regularization techniques facilitate the development of clustering properties in support sets. The objective of28 was to minimize the intra-class distance, or the Euclidean distance between prototypes and support/query set samples, prior to adaptation. By averaging support and query set samples from each episode, these prototypes were generated; however, this method was modified to utilize only the support set. One can argue that this regularization has both advantages and disadvantages. By relying on minimizing results in the mean of all samples, which is a well-established fact owing to the success of prototypical networks, it fails to motivate the improvement of aggregation techniques. To tackle this issue, the regularization proposed in reference27 was incorporated. This approach optimizes the inter-class distance between prototypes P while reducing the intra-class distance in the modified \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{S}$$\end{document}S^. The purpose of this is to maintain class separation during the adaptation process. The loss calculation utilizes cosine similarity for inter-class distance and Euclidean distance for intra-class distance, given that minimizing cosine similarity maximizes inter-class distance. By integrating these regularization methods with cross-entropy loss, where the regularization losses are assigned weights via the hyperparameters α and β, the overall loss is computed. When implementing various weight optimizers in the training process, the weight loss term is incorporated. Furthermore, this paper introduces a regularization method that is exclusive to architectures that enable learnable adaptation. Theoretically, the MetaFormer improves the aggregation of support set vectors from any encoder by adapting exclusively the support set. The encoder weights remain constant throughout the MetaFormer training process. Rather, the BERT encoder is initialized with weights obtained from another meta-learner. By utilizing this method, the risk of overfitting to training data due to encoder parameters is eliminated, allowing the emphasis to be placed on developing an enhanced adaptation process that is not influenced by the encoder employed.

Knowledge distillation

The encoder for sentences utilized information distillation to reduce memory requirements in comparison to BERT. It will facilitate more extensive training, such as 40-way training, which is expected to improve performance on the substantial 59-way task under investigation in this study. Even for smaller 10-way tasks,21 provided evidence that this strategy is effective at enhancing performance. Therefore, determining whether this effect extends to the architecture is the objective. Utilizing knowledge distillation to train a TinyBERT variant for meta-learning entails adherence to a straightforward procedure as described in reference32. In accordance with the training protocol for full-sized BERT, the literature on knowledge distillation for BERT emphasizes the importance of task-specific fine-tuning following general pretraining. The condensed version of the General TinyBERT is utilized in32. On the other hand, task-specific knowledge distillation pertains to the application of the knowledge distillation process to the dataset (such as FewRel) specifically designed for utilization with TinyBERT. This stage is critical as it refines the smaller model, like BERT, on specific datasets to ensure optimal performance. Knowledge distillation required initial training and optimization of a standard network utilizing BERT as the encoder on the FewRel dataset. The larger model utilized a BERT-based prototypical network, while the smaller model employed a TinyBERT-based prototypical network, to distill knowledge on FewRel. A typical network employs the TinyBERT sentence encoder, which has undergone 30,000 epochs of training utilizing the knowledge distillation loss. Before this, the encoder was initialized with the General TinyBERT parameters. Finally, the trained TinyBERT is retained as a pretrained language model that can be used as a sentence encoder by other meta-learners.

Experimental analysis

Evaluating the performance of a meta-learner becomes more complex because of selecting a support set at random. Additionally, determining the construction of the incremental learning task is critical. In this section, the methodologies and dataset employed to accurately evaluate the performance of a meta-learner in incremental and standard scenarios are elaborated.

Evaluation setup

Training models entail subjecting them to a variety of scenarios. The investigation commenced with smaller scenarios, including the 10-way 5-shot, and subsequently advanced to the 20-way 5-shot enticement. This methodology is characterized by testing in standard and gradual scenarios. The regular one consists of testing on new classes and training on established ones, whereas the gradual one consists of testing on both established and newly introduced classes. This enables us to assess the models' ability to adapt to new classes in isolation from their performance in familiar classes. The accuracy of both previous and subsequent classes, in addition to the accuracy of all classes, has been evaluated. To estimate these probabilities for a meta-learner, which is more difficult to do so than for a supervised model, episodic evaluation occurred. By sampling many episodes, episodic evaluation attempts to reduce the effect of the randomly selected support set S on accuracy. The influence of this effect is especially noteworthy in the 1-shot scenario, wherein the support set may contain an outlier sample distant from the query, which could cause the model to forecast a distinct class and introduce errors attributable to random effects. Although the impact of an outlier in the support set is less severe in the 5-shot scenario, it is still capable of sufficiently distorting class averages to influence predictions. Achieving an evaluation of the meta-learner's anticipated performance across diverse support set distributions through the sampling of multiple episodes, assuming the support set is devoid of infrequent outliers. Experiments are conducted after the evaluation has been established in accordance with 12. Furthermore, the results of the incremental setting have been presented in both 5-way and 10-way iterations. Random selection determines the classes in this scenario. This method eliminates the need to introduce a single additional class for every five-way problem, an action that would necessitate the addition of unique classes to 20% of every scenario. This methodology is more consistent with practical situations in which the availability of new courses may vary. These three experimental conditions are succinctly detailed in Table 4. The performance of meta-learners exhibits substantial variation because of their dependence on support sets that are selected at random33. It is difficult to reduce this variance because meta-learners rely on the support set for unseen tasks, but the small size of these sets results in a high variance. While this study does not provide a definitive solution to the problem at hand, augmenting the number of testing episodes to 10,000 aids in diminishing the variability associated with estimating mean performance. In addition, flawed methodologies employed to demonstrate metric learning improvements are highlighted in recommendations from34. Despite their emphasis on metric learning as opposed to meta-learning, their recommendations continue to be valuable. It is critical to maintain consistency in the backbone model across all techniques, perform multiple model tests, provide confidence intervals for accuracy, and refrain from receiving test set feedback during the training process. It was discovered that test set feedback was problematic, especially when early stopping was performed using the test set. BERT and TinyBERT are employed to tackle this issue; a distinct validation set is utilized to enable early stopping; each model executes three times; and the average accuracy across all executions is reported. Datasets

FewRel31, serves as the evaluation benchmark. The methods are assessed within both the incremental and episodic setups using this dataset. FewRel consists of 100 classes, with 700 examples in each class. These classes are split up into train, validation, and test sets with 64, 16, and 20 classes in each, respectively. However, only the standard episodic method of evaluation is possible due to restricted access to the test data. The model is trained on the given train and validation splits in the standard scenario, and it is then evaluated using private test data. The assessment involves measuring accuracy across all combinations of N∈ {5, 10} and K∈ {1, 5}. A distinct split, comprising 10 novel relations for validation, 16 for testing, and 54 base relations, was presented by12 for the incremental setup. Data from testing, validation, and training datasets are distributed among these base relations. To conduct testing, samples from five randomly chosen classes out of the sixteen test classes are combined with samples from all 54 base relations. The complete classification problem in incremental learning, which requires a model to classify across all base classes and a few new classes not encountered during training, is well-modeled by this methodology. To train and evaluate all incremental models, the dataset and methodology, reporting accuracy based on the 59-way task is used. To make a new set of fictional categories, the code is adjusted from an existing implementation. Here it is used to match up with the extensive Wikitext-103 dataset: a compilation of 103 million text tokens taken from Wikipedia35.The dataset's vastness allows us to generate over 32,000 classes, each with a minimum of 10 examples. However, to examine the effects of adding numerous new categories during training, even more than 32,000 classes are needed. As a result, the authors repeat the cycle whenever needed while going through each class in this made-up set. By ensuring that the class is repeated over thousands of iterations, this method lowers the possibility of overfitting.

Baselines

The employed approach is assessed in relation to a variety of robust comparisons. Initially, it is matched with the fundamental prototypical network, a model that laid the groundwork for many subsequent metric learners. Following this, two models known for their commendable performance on the FewRel benchmark are selected. One is Matching the Blanks (MTB)23, which not only introduced the relation encoding method adopted by several others but also achieved the most superior performance among all documented methods due to its self-supervised pre-training objective. Another model, Hybrid Prototypical Attention Networks (HATT)14, draws attention for its innovative aggregation technique that employs both instance and feature level attention in aggregating relations. Additionally, the method is evaluated against two models that are specifically utilized in incremental meta-learning. To minimize overlap between base and novel embeddings, the Incremental Prototypical Network (IncreProtoNet)12 integrates an attention mechanism to combine embeddings from a standard backbone and another backbone specifically trained to embed novel classes. Interestingly, this model requires novel classes to make any predictions, which limits direct comparison in non-incremental settings or in situations where the support set for the 5 or 10-way settings might not contain novel classes. Lastly, the FEAT model relies on a transformer for adapting class means.

Backbones

The core of a meta-learner relies on its fundamental model, which transforms the data into a vector. These embedded vectors from the support set serve as the basis for the meta-learner's decision-making. The authors employ the entity markers and entity start state technique, pioneered by23 and utilized by various other models. This method involves inserting tokens that signify the entity positions within the sentence, culminating in the extraction of a representation from BERT by combining the embedding of these marker tokens. In the typical N ≤ 10-way scenarios, BERT is used as the foundation, following the standard practice in NLP meta-learning. However in incremental settings, as Fig. 6 shows, the strategy ran into memory issues. This occurs because, toconclude a query set, BERT encodes the complete support set. For example, the technique used here handled a batch size of 59 * 5 = 295 samples for encoding when conducting inference on the 59-way 5-shot incremental task. This does not include the query set, which can have a variable size. This challenge is specific to NLP in meta-learning due to the substantial memory demands of predominant large pre-trained models. For instance, in their research using computer vision datasets,28 used a CNN with reduced memory requirements. Similarly, probably because of memory limitations,12 chose CNN as their main tool for experiments on the FewRel dataset. To get around CNNs' limitations in NLP, especially their inability to examine global data, the knowledge distillation is used. This means using TinyBERT instead of BERT, as described in Section III (C). Due to these memory constraints, training on large-scale way problems is not feasible with BERT in constrained memory because activations for every sample in an episode cannot be stored for gradient computations and descent. Hyperparameters

Every model in the tests uses the same hyperparameters whenever possible, adhering to a standard methodology. Each model is trained through 30,000 iterations, and the model with the best validation performance is chosen for additional testing through 10,000 iterations. Apart from MetaFormer, which benefits from a higher learning rate of 0.001 because of its frozen encoder, learning rates of 0.0002 and 0.5 are typically used. Additionally, optimization methods vary; Adam is used for baselines while MetaFormer and FEAT rely on standard gradient descent optimizers for learning adaptation parameters. Additionally, MetaFormer's regularization employs α = 0.01 and β = 10. However, IncreProtoNet, incorporating a CNN and complex attention mechanism, requires different hyperparameters specified by12. Meta-Former's encoder remains frozen during training, initialized with a TinyBERT from a previously trained prototypical network. In BERT experiments, initial training involves prototypical networks under the same settings, using their encoders to train Meta-Former. Hyperparameter tuning reveals the advantage of using a single optimizer over separate weight optimizers. Surrogate novel classes show minimal impact, as demonstrated in ablations. Consequently, all experiments employ a single optimizer and exclude surrogate novel classes.

Results

This section highlights the experiment outcomes across settings. It reveals that MetaFormer outperforms FEAT and matches prototypical networks. Following this, the impact of removing or altering substantial model components in the ablation study has been investigated. The authors offer analysis on some unexpected findings. Lastly, the diverse values for the attention weights learned by each variant of MetaFormer has been explored.

Incremental settings

The performance of the MetaFormer is initially assessed about the baseline models, as illustrated in Table 5. Interestingly, despite a six-fold increase in the number of classes, there is only about a 12-point accuracy decrease when moving from the 10-way 5-shot to the 59-way 5-shot setting. This might be due in part to the dataset's characteristics, where similar classes are scarce, resulting in new classes being distant from the query sample. It also suggests that even basic meta-learners like prototypical networks possess an embedding function complex enough to differentiate more classes than they were trained on. Prototypical networks, MetaFormer, and FEAT are the best performers overall; MetaFormer shows significantly higher accuracy in new classes. This is attributable to two factors: firstly, an overall enhancement in meta-learning performance on base classes due to learned adaptation, and secondly, this improvement is sustained despite robust regularization that bolsters generalization. It is worth noting that despite MTB achieving superior published performance, it lags in this evaluation due to its absence of pretraining and the shared encoding method across all models, rendering MTB's primary contributions ineffective. Discussions

This section discusses the trends and improvements found in the results, such as the random attention weight phenomenon and the big benefits of FEAT over MetaFormer. In addition to generating thought-provoking avenues for further investigation, a comprehensive evaluation of the study's merits and drawbacks has been taken. An overarching pattern that is discernible in every experiment is the compromise between performance in foundational and novel classes. As shown in Table 8, removing query adaptation affects overall performance but improves the performance of novel classes, possibly because of decreased variance but increased bias. Getting rid of attention weights has a similar effect: novel class performance improves for both BERT and TinyBERT by lowering variance. In many assertions, a consistent pattern is observed: one model outperforms others in novel-class performance while excelling in base-class performance. This phenomenon resembles the trade-off between bias and variance observed in conventional machine learning, wherein models exhibiting marginally higher variance outperform those with lower variance, but models with marginally greater bias may excel in testing. Highly variable models perform better than unknown instances of known base classes but worse than unknown novel classes in this situation. The distillation of knowledge is a significant trend that impacts the quality of experiments. While it aids in the substantial reduction of computational resources, its influence marginally hampers the study. For example, when knowledge distillation happens in TinyBERT-MetaFormer, freezing the encoder stops it from "forgetting" important weights while simulating BERT. Although knowledge distillation can be advantageous in terms of reducing computational pressure, its impact on accuracy is more significant than in other research, especially when ongoing distillation is not present during meta-learner training. Furthermore, knowledge distillation's support for larger-way training does not have much of an effect since the model can easily adapt to larger N-way problems even when training is limited.

Regarding the methodology, training with surrogate novel classes has minimal effect on the model due to the inherent challenge of creating these classes through a self-supervised method involving masking. Current procedures for generating these classes involve masking and replacing words with a common "[MASK]" token shared between two text pieces. However, this construction leads models to identify surrogate novel classes based solely on the presence of a mask token, rendering the learned knowledge irrelevant. In the future, it might be helpful to investigate other self-supervised tasks that do not use masking, like adding to existing relationships with data or using language models to make new class labels. Interestingly, it is observed that a MetaFormer with random weights tends to outperform those optimised using high learning rates, such as SGD or RMSProp, which consistently learn the same weights. This discrepancy might stem from the regularisation loss, potentially encouraging inaccurate weight values. Further research into designing specific regularisations for the model or exploring the impact of weights that tend towards negative values is warranted. Going back to experiments with learned attention weights using BERT and thinking of ways to make them more powerful, like adding biases, could also help us learn more about how attention mechanisms.

Conclusions and future works

Few-shot learning stands as a crucial technique in contemporary deep learning, especially with the emergence of increasingly larger models demanding even more extensive datasets. Constructing a model and dataset entirely from the ground up to address real-world problems incurs significant costs. Hence, in many scenarios, leveraging a few-shot learning technique, even at the expense of a marginal accuracy drop, is preferable to the arduous task of collecting and labelling vast datasets. Although few-shot learning has many practical applications, models trained and tested on simulated problems that do not accurately reflect real-world tasks are the focus of the meta-learning field. Throughout this paper, class-incremental learning has been advocated, emphasising its better alignment with real-world tasks. It has been demonstrated that meta-learners designed for this purpose exhibit favourable traits such as adept few-shot learning capabilities and robust generalisation to new classes.

The method trained meta-learners resistant to novel classes without requiring retraining by modelling the class-incremental learning problem. Then, a transformer-based meta-learner with a trainable aggregation function is shown. It uses weighted attention mechanisms that can be changed to work in a setting with class increases. The experimental results highlighted the superiority of this MetaFormer over an existing transformer-based method and its comparable or superior performance to the best baseline meta-learner. Furthermore, the findings indicated that meta-learners exhibit commendable generalisation capabilities, especially in addressing larger problems. Remarkably, training on a typical 10-way problem was adequate to achieve excellent results in incremental learning within the classroom.

Future work will focus on developing a self-supervised meta-learning technique that avoids mask tokens and overcomes the drawbacks of surrogate novel classes. Furthermore, to address the intricate optimisation problems that attention weights present, further research into the attention-weight learning process is essential. Finally, further research should be done on applying these methods to the more complex Open World Recognition setting, which more closely resembles real-world learning scenarios. In these situations, models might be asked questions about completely unidentified classes, which adds a more difficult but important dimension for use and assessment.
