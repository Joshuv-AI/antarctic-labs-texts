Deep learning for three-dimensional (3D) plant phenomics
Authors: Shichao Jin, Dawei Li, Ting Yun, Jianling Tang, Ke Wang, Shaochen Li, Hongyi Yang, Si Yang, Shan Xu, Lin Cao, Haifeng Xia, Qinghua Guo, Yu Zhang, Dong Jiang, Yanfeng Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlant phenomics, the comprehensive study of plant phenotypes, has gained prominence as a vital tool for understanding the intricate relationships between genotypes and the environment. Image-based plant phenomics has progressed rapidly, and three-dimensional (3D) phenotyping is a valuable extension of traditional 2D phenomics. However, the increased data dimensionality poses challenges to feature extraction and phenotyping. In recent decades, deep learning has led to remarkable progress in revolutionizing 3D phenotyping. Therefore, this review highlights the importance of using deep learning in 3D plant phenomics. It systematically overviews the capabilities of deep learning for 3D computer vision, covering 3D representation, classification, detection and tracking, semantic segmentation, instance segmentation, and generation. Additionally, deep learning techniques for 3D point preprocessing (e.g., annotation, downsampling, and dataset organization) and various plant phenotyping tasks are discussed. Finally, the challenges and perspectives associated with deep learning in 3D plant phenomics are summarized, including (1) benchmark dataset construction by using synthetic datasets and methods such as generative artificial intelligence and unsupervised or weakly supervised learning; (2) accurate and efficient 3D point cloud analysis by leveraging multitask learning, lightweight models, and self-supervised learning; and (3) deep learning for 3D plant phenomics by exploring interpretability, extensibility, and multimodal data utilization. The exploration of deep learning in 3D plant phenomics is poised to spur breakthroughs in a new dimension of plant science.Keywords: 3D phenomics, Deep learning, Dataset, Sampling, Annotation

Introduction

Plant phenomics is a critical and interdisciplinary research field that focuses on the measurement and analysis of plant traits codetermined by genomics and the environment [1,2]. In recent decades, studies have shown that plant phenomics enables the high-throughput identification, selection, and breeding of ideal varieties with desirable traits, such as high-yielding, stress-tolerant, and resource-efficient traits [[3], [4], [5]]. However, plant structure (e.g., shape, size, branching patterns, and spatial arrangement) and functional activities (e.g., light interception) occur mainly in three-dimensional (3D) space [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Therefore, 3D plant phenomics is becoming the frontier in the field of plant science.

3D plant phenomics requires high-quality 3D data and analysis tools. 3D data are now available in many ways, especially laser scanning and image reconstruction in field environments [11]. These data typically offer more detailed 3D structures and clearer object boundaries and allow objects to be analyzed from multiple angles without obstruction [12]. However, 3D data processing and interpretation are challenging because the 3D data structure is more complicated than the widely used 2D image data. In general, the point cloud is the basic representation type of 3D data, which are unordered, unstructured, and irregular, and contains numerous points with complex input features such as coordinates, color, intensity, and normals [13]. Hence, the development of precise, efficient, and robust tools for 3D phenotyping in data preprocessing, analysis, and applications is still ongoing.

Unlike traditional computer vision methods [14], deep learning has led to remarkable progress in data analysis in plant phenomics [15]. In recent decades, deep learning, especially convolutional neural networks, has improved 2D image-based phenotyping [16,17], such as phenology classification [18], spike counting [19], panicle segmentation [20,21], and yield regression [[22], [23], [24]]. By contrast, 3D deep learning has fewer applications in plant phenomics due to unique challenges. First, the inherent complex structure and diversity of plants make the processing and analysis of point cloud data more complicated. For example, the number of target organs is not fixed, the shapes are irregular, and mutual occlusion problems significantly increase the complexity of feature extraction and data segmentation. In addition, plant morphology changes dynamically during growth (e.g., leaf growth), thus requiring a model that can analyze temporal changes and capture dynamic characteristics. Moreover, the complexity of 3D plant structures makes data annotation difficult, and the available samples are very limited. These challenges limit the ability to fully harness the power of deep learning.

Although many research efforts have been made to develop deep learning algorithms for 3D plant phenotyping (e.g., segmentation) in the past decade [6,[25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30]], a comprehensive review of this interdisciplinary direction is lacking, except for some purely technical reviews [11,31,32]. Therefore, this study aims to provide an overview of deep learning for 3D plant phenomics from an interdisciplinary perspective. We aim to (1) provide an overview of the development of deep learning for 3D computer vision, which is the theoretical basis for interdisciplinary applications; (2) demonstrate the development of 3D deep learning in plant phenomics with respect to an applicable pipeline; and (3) summarize the challenges and perspectives of 3D deep learning in plant phenomics.

Deep learning for 3D computer vision

Deep learning has led to unprecedented progress in computer vision in the last half century. Unlike deep learning frameworks for processing 2D images with limited visual features and fixed perspective data in a compressed space, 3D deep learning faces more challenges and has made substantial progress in 3D representation, feature engineering, and 3D computer vision tasks, including 3D classification, 3D detection and tracking, 3D semantic segmentation, 3D instance segmentation, and 3D generation.

3D representation

3D representation is a pivotal component in the field of 3D vision, especially for feature engineering. 3D representation involves the efficient encoding and organization of 3D data for in-depth analysis [33]. 3D visualization facilitates the intuitive exploration and comprehension of intricate 3D structures and interconnections. Feature engineering encompasses the extraction of pertinent and distinguishing characteristics from 3D representations. Inspired by the human visual system, which balances detailed perception, structural understanding, and efficiency, machine vision was designed to replicate these remarkable visual abilities. Many methods have been developed to represent real 3D scenes with basic geometric forms, such as point clouds, meshes, voxels, graphs, and implicit functions, which are compared below (Fig. 1).

Comparison of different 3D primitive types for representing 3D data, evaluated across different criteria: shape details, structural complexity, computational efficiency, memory efficiency, and scalability. Specifically, computational efficiency encompasses the overall processing speed of a data representation, and a key aspect of this facet is spatial query efficiency, which refers to how effectively a representation supports the rapid retrieval of neighboring features. The color scale (orange to gray) indicates the relative performance level from very high to low for each criterion. This visual comparison highlights the trade-offs between different representation methods, such as point clouds, meshes, voxels, graphs, and implicit functions.

A point cloud is a collection of 3D points. Each of these points has a deterministic position along with other attributes, such as RGB color values. Point cloud representation discretely preserves more high-quality geometric information but lacks logical connections and arrangements between points (i.e., low structural complexity). Point cloud data are typically sparse, which contributes to their high computational and memory efficiency, especially for local operations and geometric calculations. They also demonstrate good scalability when dealing with large-scale datasets, leveraging distributed processing and parallel computation to enhance efficiency.

A mesh is a collection of vertices, edges, and faces that define the geometry of a 3D object, which is commonly seen in modeling and rendering works. Mesh data can be generated from points by methods such as Delaunay triangulation, which has high structural complexity. It typically results in reduced computational efficiency because of the processing demands imposed by a substantial number of faces and vertices, particularly in the case of intricate triangular meshes. Additionally, it tends to demand more memory, especially for high-resolution and intricate topologies, while providing the ability to represent intricate shapes and surfaces. Scalability also presents a challenge with mesh data, particularly when dealing with large-scale and high-resolution meshes that may introduce difficulties in terms of storage and computational resources.

Voxels represent 3D data in a regular grid format, which involves discretizing the 3D space occupied by the points into a grid of equally sized volume elements by using tools such as the Point Cloud Library (PCL), Open3D, and Visualization Toolkit (VTK). Voxel data typically support efficient parallel computation because of their regular grid structure. However, in high-resolution scenarios, voxel-based formats can become computationally expensive primarily because of their lower spatial efficiency, as representing large or sparse 3D spaces requires storing a large number of voxels—each potentially containing attribute information—which also leads to higher memory consumption. Although voxel data can effectively represent the volumetric shape of objects, they face scalability limitations because of their high computational cost.

Graphs are another organizational form with points as vertices and edges between each pair of vertices, which exhibit associations within spatial data and manifest the hierarchically clustered distribution of data. The computational efficiency and memory requirements of graph data depend on the scale of the graph and its adjacency relationships. Graph data can represent complex relationships and connectivity, and their scalability is determined by the size of the graph and the characteristics of the algorithms employed. For large-scale graph data, distributed graph processing frameworks may be required to achieve scalability.

Implicit representation models a scene using a mathematical function, such as a neural network, which can generate high-quality images from various viewpoints [34,35]. These models often produce more realistic and detailed visuals than traditional formats such as point clouds, meshes, or voxels do. However, since the model used in implicit representation varies depending on the scene, it is difficult to apply it directly to other data or tasks. Therefore, it is often necessary to convert the implicit format into more standard formats, such as point clouds or grids, with techniques such as the truncated signed distance function or Poisson surface reconstruction [36].

Although the different methods have their own strengths and weaknesses, they have all been widely used in different tasks [32], such as 3D classification, detection and tracking, semantic segmentation, instance segmentation, and generation.

3D feature engineering

Deep learning-based feature engineering methods can be classified into four main types: view-based methods, voxel-based methods, point-based methods, and graph-based methods.

View-based methods transform 3D objects into 2D images from different views and use 2D convolution filters to extract features, such as MVSG-DNN [37], HMVCM [38], and View-gcn [39]. View-based methods are not constrained by the excessively complex structure of the target but attempt to leverage view-wise 2D features from different perspectives and aggregate these features into a discriminative global descriptor. Even in the case of occlusion, views from different perspectives can complement each other to achieve good performance. The advantage of view-based methods is the ability to leverage multiview information to enhance the representation and understanding of 3D objects. However, the geometric relationships between 3D points could not be properly and accurately captured by multiview-based methods because the neighboring pixels in the 2D format could be geometrically distant from each other in the original 3D format.

In the voxel-based approach [40,41], a 3D model is divided into N∗M∗L voxel units. Each voxel represents partial information about the 3D model. Voxelization is inspired by the powerful feature abstraction and learning performance of deep neural networks for 2D imagery and originates from the application of local convolutions on uniform lattices such as pixels. Voxelization transforms a point cloud into standard 3D voxels, enabling rigid convolution measures similar to those from 2D imagery. However, the voxel-based method is largely limited by hardware because of its high computational cost, despite the attempts of some networks to operate on sparse voxel representations [42].

In recent years, point-based methods, such as PointNet [13] and PointNet++ [43], have attracted noticeable interest and development because they are easy to acquire and can retain the original 3D spatial information without any additional discretization that may destroy the original geometric representation of 3D data. PointNet ensures the invariance of the input point cloud and extracts point-level and global features of 3D objects. PointNet++ further improves its local feature representation ability by using multiscale and multiresolution grouping methods, which in turn increases its scalability to more complex geometric entities. To improve the speed of feature extraction, So-Net [44] preserves the topological properties of the input space by constructing a self-organizing map. To address the rotation problem in 3D convolution networks, PointCNN [45] leverages the spatial–local correlation of 3D data by employing X-transformation to weigh and arrange the inputs. Recently, the vision transformer (ViT) [46] has become a new “elixir” for solving vision tasks such as image classification, recognition, and text-to-image generation. Compared with CNNs, the ViT and self-attention models have been proven to be more effective for 2D pixels and have shown great potential in the point-level analysis of 3D data. A point cloud transformer (PCT) [47] is a deep network for segmenting/classifying point clouds. It contains an encoder whose main structure comprises transformers and a PointNet-like decoder. The point transformer (PT) network [48] integrates multiple transformers into a U-Net-like architecture for point cloud segmentation and semantic classification tasks. A dual-transformer architecture (one transformer is in the pointwise direction, and the other is in the channelwise direction) [49] was designed to carry out point cloud segmentation and classification tasks. The point pyramid transformer network (PPTN) [50] was designed to process either semantic segmentation or classification tasks for point clouds. Attention can also be combined with sampling in point clouds, which results in attention discriminant sampling (ADS) [51] for learning point clouds. Predictably, new attention/transformer-based models will continue to be developed in the field of 3D deep learning.

Graph-based methods [52,53] can be divided into spectral graph neural networks (GNNs) and spatial GNNs, which are widely used in local relationship modeling and shape analysis. Spectral GNNs leverage the spectral domain of graph signals to capture rich structural information. Their advantage lies in their ability to effectively model and learn from graph-structured data, enabling powerful representation learning and inference on complex networks. A representative method in this field is graph convolutional networks (GCNs) [52], which introduce a simplified spectral convolutional operator for efficient graph representation learning and achieve state-of-the-art performance in various graph-related tasks, such as node classification and graph classification. Compared with spectral GNNs, spatial GNNs have better flexibility and generalization ability. Graph construction and graph convolution are two key operations of GNNs. For example, the superpixel graph (SPG) [53] uses PointNet and graph convolution to encode vertex features and extract context information, respectively.

3D computer vision tasks

3D computer vision tasks include mainly 3D classification, 3D detection and tracking, 3D semantic segmentation, 3D instance segmentation, and 3D generation.

3D classification aims to classify 3D data into different categories or classes based on geometry, shape, and scale information. 3D classification tasks can be implemented with view-based methods, voxel-based methods, and point-based methods. For example, MVSG-DNN [37] is a view-based method that can classify 3D objects by applying basic convolution neural networks (CNNs) for the visual feature extraction of individual views and employs saliency LSTM to adaptively select the representative views based on a multiview context. HMVCM [38] not only uses viewwise discrimination and multiview association but also adaptively calculates the weight of group context features and fuses them into a compact 3D descriptor. NormalNet is a voxel-based CNN for 3D object classification and retrieval that uses the normal vectors calculated for all surface voxels as input [54]. PointNet and its variants have been widely used for 3D object classification because of their flexibility with respect to the direct input of points [13].

3D detection refers primarily to object/key-point detection, which is designed to automatically identify key points or areas of significance, uniqueness, or importance. Currently, 3D object detection methods based on point clouds can be categorized into two main types: single-stage detection and two-stage detection. Single-stage detection refers to a type of object detection algorithm that directly predicts object bounding boxes and class labels in a unified manner without using a region proposal stage. For example, VoxelNet [55] directly predicts the object frame with a 3D CNN. The SECOND network [56] is an upgraded version of VoxelNet [55], which introduces sparse 3D convolution to improve detection speed and memory usage. Another efficient approach is PIXOR [57], which projects all points onto a 2D feature map using 3D occupancy and point intensity information and therefore eliminates expensive 3D convolutions. PointPillars [58] proposed a new coding method that uses pillars to generate pseudo images suitable for 2D convolution. This technique achieves efficient 3D target detection with a good balance of speed and accuracy. In contrast, the two-stage detection models usually include a "region proposal" stage and an "object classification and precise localization" stage, making them relatively slower but more accurate. In the first stage, a series of candidate regions are generated in the image to locate potential regions that may contain targets. In the second stage, the candidate regions are fed into a classifier for object classification, and precise localization is performed to obtain accurate bounding box positions. For example, MV3D-Net [59] first generated 3D candidate boxes with a proposal network from a bird's-eye-view representation of a 3D point cloud, after which a region-based fusion network was designed to achieve precise box classification and regression. PointRCNN [60] directly operates on the original point cloud, predicting boxes for all points and removing background predictions, resulting in improved efficiency and accuracy.

3D semantic segmentation is a fundamental task of scene understanding, which involves dividing data into semantically interpretable regions according to a meaningful taxonomy in the real world for further target analysis and visual understanding. Deep learning-based 3D semantic segmentation can be implemented with view-based methods, point-based methods, and graph-based methods. For example, through mapping multiview 2D image semantic segmentation results into 3D objects, Boulch et al. [61] developed an efficient semantic segmentation network that uses multiple RGB views and depth composite views of 3D objects. Inspired by PointNet and PointNet++, several point-based methods have further improved semantic segmentation by improving the performance of local feature extraction, such as using efficient random point sampling [62], incorporating the spatial context into semantic 3D point cloud segmentation [63], and designing special modules such as graph embedding and pyramid attention [64]. By exploring the semantic relationship between each point and its neighbors through edge connections, Jiang et al. [65] designed a hierarchical graph for 3D semantic segmentation.

3D instance segmentation involves dividing objects of interest into a group of categories. It does not entail isolated object detection but rather a natural evolution from semantic segmentation to fine refinement. Generally, 3D instance segmentation techniques can be divided into two categories: proposal-based methods [66,67] and proposal-free methods [68,69]. Proposal-based methods generally include two steps: bounding box detection (e.g., region proposal) and segmentation. For example, 3D-Bonet [67] directly regresses the boundary boxes of each instance in the input 3D data and simultaneously predicts a point-level mask for each instance to efficiently segment all instances. Proposal-free methods cluster points into instances based on the similarity metrics. For example, the similarity group proposal network (SGPN) groups object instances by learning the similarity matrix [69]. However, the size of the similarity matrix increases in a quadratic fashion, making it impossible to handle very large scenarios given memory constraints. Similarly, multiscale affinity with sparse convolution (MASC) [68] is a method for learning the similarity between different points for instance segmentation. The network uses sparse convolution and clustering algorithms based on learned multiscale affinities to address 3D instance segmentation.

3D generation and reconstruction are used to infer the 3D geometry and structure of objects and scenes from one or more inputs. 3D object generation and reconstruction methods can be broadly divided into model-based methods and image-based methods. Model-based methods take 3D objects represented by voxels and points as inputs to neural networks. For example, 3D-ED-GAN is a framework that can fill the missing voxel data of 3D objects with low resolution by combining a 3D encoder–decoder and a generative adversarial network (GAN) [70]. Moreover, a long-term recurrent convolutional network (LRCN) is introduced to improve the resolution of 3D models and reduce the computational cost. In addition to overcoming the large storage requirements of voxel representation, OGN [71] converts voxels into OCtree, which consists of a block operating on a dense regular grid and a variable number of octree blocks based on hash tables. Image-based methods reconstruct 3D objects from single- or multiview images. For example, 3D-R2N2 [72] proposes a recurrent neural network architecture based on standard LSTM and a GRU. It unifies single-view and multiview 3D object reconstruction into a single framework, reconstructing a 3D object model from one or more images. PSGN [73] uses a complex two-dimensional generation network to encode the input image and introduces the Chamfer distance as a loss function to further improve the results. Recently, a neural radiance field (NeRF) [34] has been proposed for 3D scene representation and photorealistic view synthesis, which is a novel deep learning-based technique that involves optimizing an underlying continuous volumetric scene function with a sparse set of input views. To further achieve high accuracy and efficiency, Gaussian splatting [74] is proposed for 3D rendering and scene reconstruction, which represents each point in a point cloud as a 3D Gaussian blob.

Deep learning for 3D plant phenotyping

Deep learning has emerged as a promising approach for advancing 3D plant phenotyping. Data preprocessing is an essential prerequisite for utilizing deep learning for 3D plant phenotyping, including point cloud annotation, downsampling, and dataset organization. In addition, 3D phenotyping applications have been actively explored in various species with different data representations and network types.

Preprocessing for point-based plant phenotyping

Point cloud annotation

Manual labeling for 2D imagery data has been developed into several standard processes with various mature labeling tools. For example, LabelMe [75] was developed by the MIT Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Lab, which is a well-known 2D image labeling tool that provides the ability to circumscribe every single object in an image with a definable bounding box for object detection and segmentation tasks. LabelImg (https://github.com/HumanSignal/labelImg/releases), a Python-programmed lightweight labeling tool, focuses mainly on using rectangular boxes to annotate objects for detection tasks. The CVAT (Computer Vision Annotation Tool) (https://github.com/opencv/cvat), which was originally developed by Intel, can efficiently create pixelwise labels for mask segmentation tasks, including semantic segmentation and instance segmentation. However, 3D point clouds, as non-Euclidean data types, are typically unordered and unevenly distributed, complicating the development of annotation/labeling tools for point clouds.

Classic 3D labeling tools have usually been developed for vehicle-mounted LiDAR (light detection and ranging) point clouds of street views. Some tools can be directly transferred to the agricultural industry. Meshlab is perhaps the most well-known and prevalent point cloud visualization and processing tool because of its simple installation, neat software layout, and ease of use [76]. Meshlab is frequently used together with point cloud processing platforms such as the PCL to divide plant point clouds into different semantic sets [77]. The Semantic Segmentation Editor (SSE) (https://github.com/Hitachi-Automotive-And-Industry-Lab/semantic-segmentation-editor/) is a popular point cloud labeling tool for inputs in the PCD (point cloud data) file format. Although the SSE was originally designed for the annotation of LiDAR-captured data from self-driving cars, the tool was recently found to work conveniently on point clouds for agricultural engineering [26,78]. Established in 2012, LabelCloud [79] is a lightweight annotation tool originally designed for 3D object detection. This toolkit not only accepts multiple input formats, such as XYZ and PCD, but is also suitable for point clouds from different domains, including plant phenotyping. The annotation tool “Point_labeler” [80] was successfully applied to a large-scale scene dataset—SemanticKITTI. This tool is quite efficient for massive point clouds. Label3DMaize [81] is a MATLAB toolkit designed for labeling the point clouds of maize and other plants that have one main stem in their structure. Additionally, some commonly used point cloud visualization and processing software can be used for labeling, such as CloudCompare (http://www.cloudcompare.org/main.html) and LiDAR360 (the Green Valley International), which have been used to divide the leaf instances from point clouds of lettuce captured from Kinect Azure [82] and maize captured from LiDAR [25].

Downsampling

Modern 3D scanning systems (e.g., LiDAR and structured light systems) can acquire thousands or even millions of points of a crop sample. Owing to the differences in plant size and shape, as well as the disparity among scanning devices, obtaining a point cloud dataset with a unified point number is unlikely. On the other hand, deep learning networks usually accept a fixed number of points for each input sample because of batch training requirements. Furthermore, the input number of points is usually restricted to a small value below 10000 because of the limitation of GPU memory. Therefore, all the input point clouds should be downsampled to a fixed scale before training while minimizing the information loss.

Point-based sampling and voxel-based sampling offer diverse approaches for efficiently reducing the complexity of point clouds. Point-based sampling methods include random sampling (RS) and farthest point sampling (FPS). RS is perhaps the most classic sampling method and has been recently proven to still be effective for large-scale LiDAR point clouds [62]. RS has many strengths. One is the fast computing speed with O(n) complexity, and another is the ability to precisely control the number of points in the sample. RS has been integrated into the PCL and is easily accessible. FPS is an iterative downsampling strategy that can precisely control the number of sampled points [43]. Although FPS and its speed-up variants are efficient to implement [83], they may lose details of sparse and small areas in point clouds because FPS tends to sample sharp edges first and then interior areas. In addition to point-based sampling methods, voxel-based sampling (VBS) achieves downsampling by transforming the point cloud into a set of voxels (i.e., uniform 3D cubes) and then outputs a new point to represent each voxel. According to how each voxel is represented, there are two common variants of the VBS strategy—(i) the point set in the voxel is summarized by the center of gravity of all the points, and (ii) the voxel is summarized by the point that is closest to the center of gravity or the geometrical center of the voxel. VBS is fast (usually O(n)), but has difficulty controlling the number of voxels because the voxel cube is defined by the side length. In practice, hybrid sampling methods are designed to strictly control the number of generated voxels. For example, PSegNet [78] exploited a new voxelized farthest point sampling (VFPS) method that sequentially conducts a VBS stage and an FPS stage to downsample the point clouds of plants. VFPS inherited advantages from both FPS and VBS, including uniform density in sampling and the fixed number of points of the sampled cloud, despite requiring a longer computation time. Uniformly voxelized sampling (UVS) also adds FPS after a VBS-like step to control the number of voxel points [84]. The sampling of VFPS and UVS is shown in Fig. 3b.

Downsampling can also be performed indirectly by geometric partition measures such as meshing and facet oversegmentation. Greedy projection triangulation (GPT) meshing [85] performs triangulation on a 3D surface, which is an extension of Delaunay triangulation on 2D lattices. Meshing can be applied for both downsampling and upsampling purposes. Downsampling can be performed by controlling the edge length of the triangles, and upsampling is usually performed by interpolating virtual points from the triangles into the original point cloud. Like meshing, facet oversegmentation attempts to partition the point cloud into numerous small local point clusters that look like many “facets” in space [86]. Downsampling on facets can then be achieved by representing each facet with only one point (e.g., the facet center). Simple linear iterative clustering (SLIC) is a superpixel algorithm that can generate superpixels on 2D lattices and 3D facets for point clouds [87] and can strictly control the number of sampled facets. Similarly, the voxel cloud connectivity segmentation (VCCS) method [88] seeks to output smooth facets (also referred to as supervoxels) everywhere. Facet downsampling can also be performed using supervised learning. A superpixel sampling network (SSN) attempts to use a learning-based framework to segment point clouds into a set of boundary-aware facets [89], on which downsampling can then be performed conveniently. However, most meshing and facet oversegmentation methods cannot fix the number of partitions, and another round of FPS/RS may be needed after oversegmentation. The sampling of the meshing, SLIC, and SSN approaches is shown in Fig. 3c.

Recently, task-specific supervised downsampling methods have emerged. As a pioneering work, S-Net [90] is a point cloud simplification module designed for a downstream task network, such as classification, retrieval, or reconstruction. SampleNet is the subsequent work of S-Net, which has a soft projection module that is differentiable to enhance the generated sampled point distribution [91]. Despite the task-specific attribute, supervised downsampling methods usually require an unsupervised presampling step (such as FPS) to unify all point clouds in the dataset to ensure a fixed number of points (the restriction of network training), on which the supervised sampling and downstream tasks are then carried out. However, the training and inference processes of supervised sampling can be quite different, which complicates their implementation in practice.

Currently, there is no golden rule for selecting the best downsampling method for a specific type of plant [84]. Different methods have their strengths and weaknesses, and it is suggested that both quantitative and qualitative comparisons be carried out to determine the sampling strategy that best fits the data and the phenotyping tasks (Fig. 2). Additionally, some recent studies have demonstrated that the heuristic downsampling method can be designed based on conventional sampling methods by considering the difference between the topology of crops and other types of 3D objects. For example, inspired by sketching, a 3D edge preserving sampling (3DEPS) strategy was designed, which can intentionally preserve more boundary points during FPS and precisely control the ratio between the boundary points and interior points during sampling [26]. 3DEPS was proven to be more effective than standard FPS on a dual-function deep neural segmentation network for several types of plants. In addition, sampling based on self-organizing maps (SOMs) works in a purely unsupervised manner and precisely controls the number of points after downsampling [92]. SOMs perform shape-aware sampling on irregular plant point clouds by automatically encoding preliminary semantics to different organ types; e.g., stems are sampled as “lines”, and leaves are sampled as folded and curved “surfaces”. SOMs outperform several other popular sampling strategies in enhancing the performance of deep segmentation networks, especially for sparse crop point clouds. Dataset

3D plant datasets have quickly emerged in recent years. Notably, 3D plant datasets are typically in point cloud format, and these formats can be further converted into other formats (e.g., voxels, meshes, and graphs) for downstream phenotypic analysis. Deep learning on point clouds for plant phenotyping

Deep learning-based crop phenotyping methods have become a long-term hot topic in the field of agronomy. Since the introduction of deep learning techniques, 2D image deep learning methods (especially CNNs) have advanced the cutting-edge development of crop phenotyping. A detailed overview of the latest studies using deep CNNs in plant phenotyping applications can be found in Jiang and Li [16]. Owing to the increased difficulty of data acquisition and labeling and the irregularity and inhomogeneity of point cloud data, the overall development of 3D deep learning for crop data lags behind that of image-based deep phenotyping.

In recent decades, deep learning for 3D plant phenotyping has evolved into two major generations, depending on whether or not they are directly based on 3D data. By using an indirect projection framework that has three steps and is usually named 2D-3D reprojection, the first generation of studies on deep learning for crop point clouds tried to take a detour to solve the convolution problem. First, the point cloud is projected onto images from different angles. Mature 2D deep learning methods are subsequently applied to the images for segmentation/detection/classification. Finally, the results in image form are backmapped to the original point cloud to output the final 3D results. For example, Jin et al. [101] studied 2D-3D projection deep learning for the maize crop. Their study first projected the LiDAR points of a maize plot into images at 32 different views and then applied Faster RCNN to segment the maize stems from the images. The final back-mapping to 3D was achieved by a region-growing strategy. The segmentation method yielded accurate height predictions for maize shoots in three growing stages. Japes et al. [116] proposed a multiview semantic labeling method for multiple grape varieties. A total of 33 point clouds from four different grape varieties were captured by a structured light 3D scanner (Artec Space Spider). The semantic labeling task involves segmenting each point cloud into six semantic classes—berry, hook, twig, subtwig, rachis, and peduncle. A U-Net-like network is designed to carry out segmentation on 2D-projected point clouds, and the final segmentation is backmapped by the winner-take-all on scores from 2D images. Shi et al. [117] used multiview vision to segment plant parts from tomato seedlings with satisfactory accuracy. A multiview stereo (MVS) system (Basler acA1399-30 ​gm cameras) was constructed to generate point clouds for the potted tomato seedlings. The authors designed a fully convolutional network to segment the plant images into four different semantic classes, including leaf, stem, node, and background. Then, Mask-RCNN was applied to carry out leaf instance segmentation on the images. The backmapping from 2D to 3D was performed with a probability voting strategy. Gené-Mola et al. [118] used an MVS system to generate point clouds for apple trees, from which the apple fruits were located. Mask-RCNN was applied to the captured 2D images to segment the fruit masks, after which the 2D segmented results were combined and refined to generate 3D segmentation results. The research is believed to be able to serve robotic harvesting and agricultural management purposes. Windrim and Bryson [119] used 2D-3D reprojection deep learning to segment tree individuals from LiDAR point clouds. The authors designed a two-step roadmap: faster R-CNN was leveraged first to detect three individuals from the forest, and then another voxel segmentation architecture was used to segment each tree into the stem and lamina parts. This method could be used to estimate traits such as tree height and diameter.

The greatest advantage of 2D-3D reprojection is that it can convert complicated point cloud data into an image form that can be easily processed by mature 2D CNNs. However, its disadvantages are also obvious [120]. The first intrinsic problem of reprojection is the non-real-time process. The second problem is that the projection from the 3D point cloud to 2D images results in information loss; for instance, not all views are aligned with good 2D deep learning results, and projections must be made from multiple different views for crops with sophisticated spatial structures to ensure good performance. The third problem is the tedious flow of processing, which is highly species-dependent and has high technical requirements.

Direct deep learning on point clouds involves generalizing the 2D CNNs on the image data to the 3D data forms. This requirement leads to the second generation of studies for 3D deep learning that transform the point data into a set of uniform voxels and then apply 3D CNNs onto voxels to extract information from crops. Despite the promising results, the performance of 3D CNNs on voxelized data is highly dependent on the resolution of the voxelization, and the computational costs are usually high. This method needs to first transform the point data into a set of uniform voxels and then apply 3D CNNs to voxels to abstract information. Jin et al. [25] designed an encoder–decoder network called VCNN to achieve semantic and instance organ segmentation of maize point clouds. VCNN has a modified 3D CNN structure that can further facilitate maize phenotyping tasks, such as leaf counting, plant height estimation, point density estimation, and calculation of the corresponding ratio of crown diameter to plant height. Ao et al. [121] used PointCNN to segment the LiDAR data of maize fields into a leaf class and a stem class. The semantic segmentation results were later utilized to estimate the individual height, canopy width, and leaf area index. In addition to CNNs, the multilayer perceptron (MLP) can be used to construct a deep learning network for 3D plant phenotyping. Ziamtsov and Navlakha [12] constructed an MLP-based network that accepts the fast point feature histogram (FPFH) features of plants to carry out organ segmentation on tobacco and tomato crops. Their stem and lamina segmentation results were further used for phenotypic purposes such as leaf counting, leaf segmentation, and stem skeletonization.

3D deep learning has entered the third generation (an era of point-by-point calculation) since the emergence of PointNet [13] and PointNet++ [43]. The PointNet family not only provides a more direct and efficient computing framework but also achieves high performance through the introduction of rotation-invariant operations (e.g., input transformation and feature transformers) and the fusion of local features and global features. The main feature of PointNet++ is its encoder–decoder architecture, which achieves feature embedding with downsampling, upsampling, and skip connections. Exploring PointNet-like network architectures suitable for point clouds has become the main technical method for 3D plant phenotyping [122]. In terms of semantic segmentation, many semantic segmentation tasks have been enhanced by point-based networks from real data [77,123,124] or synthetic data [125]. In addition, Boogaard et al. [126] pioneered a comparison of training performance between using XYZRGB point cloud inputs and only XYZ inputs with a modified PointNet++ network. They found that more feature-rich inputs result in better performance. The authors also discussed the influence of sample imbalance on the performance of PointNet++ [127]. Similarly, point-based networks have shed new light on plant instance segmentation tasks. For example, Wang et al. [82] used PartNet, which incorporates a backbone of PointNet++ to conduct leaf instance segmentation for lettuce point clouds. Jayakumari et al. [128] proposed Crop-PointNet to segment tomato, eggplant, and cabbage plants from plot-level LiDAR data. Moreover, Li et al. (2022e) proposed a new network to perform both organ semantic segmentation and leaf instance segmentation based on PointNet with multiple loss functions.

Self-attention or transformer modules can be leveraged to further improve the performance of pointwise 3D deep learning on crops. For example, a model that integrates multihead attention and positional encoding was developed and demonstrated strong semantic segmentation performance on the ROSE_X dataset for plant organ segmentation [129]. Win-Former, which has a window-based transformer architecture, was developed for the semantic segmentation of maize point clouds and demonstrated generalizability to nonagricultural 3D data [130]. The plant segmentation transformer (PST) enables simultaneous semantic and instance segmentation of oilseed rape point clouds [6]. Similarly, the plant stratified transformer, featuring a U-Net-like structure with a stratified transformer backbone and fast downsampling layers, achieves organ segmentation in virtual and real Arabidopsis point clouds [131]. TPointNetPlus combines PointNet++ with transformer modules, achieving robust organ segmentation of cotton across various growth stages [132]. Moreover, PlantFormer is designed for crop point cloud completion and incorporates edge-convolution attention, coarse-to-fine upsampling, and a spatial context-aware transformer [133]. Overall, these models highlight the versatility and effectiveness of transformer-based approaches in advancing 3D plant phenotyping tasks.

Phenotyping tasks that involve deep learning on crop point clouds can be broadly classified into individual segmentation [101], organ semantic segmentation [29], and organ instance segmentation [[25], [26], [27],134], which further facilitate individual and organ-level phenotypic trait extraction [135]. Challenges and perspectives

Deep learning has accelerated the development of 3D plant phenomics, but it still faces numerous challenges (Fig. 4). First, 3D benchmark datasets are rare because of the challenges associated with high-quality 3D data collection and labeling. Second, 3D phenotyping requires high accuracy and efficiency. Finally, interpretability and extensibility are needed for advancing 3D phenotypic applications, such as discovering biologically meaningful new traits and enhancing digital twin applications of agriculture.

Challenges and perspectives of 3D deep learning in plant phenomics. (a) Benchmark dataset construction presents numerous challenges, including data preprocessing, data annotation, data reorganization, and data augmentation. (b) Accurate and efficient 3D point cloud analysis by leveraging multitask learning, lightweight models, and self-supervised learning. (c) Deep learning for 3D plant phenomics by focusing on model interpretability, extensibility, and multimodal data utilization.

Benchmark dataset construction

Datasets are the fundamental requirement for 3D deep learning and plant phenotyping. A benchmark dataset should be large, highly diversified, and well labeled. Creating a 3D point cloud deep learning dataset typically involves the following aspects after data collection: data preprocessing (e.g., registration, denoising, and completion), data annotation, data reorganization (sampling), and data augmentation. Here, we summarize key challenges and prospects in these steps [151].

Data registration: The registration of point cloud data refers to the process of aligning multiple 3D point cloud datasets into a common coordinate system or reference frame. This alignment is achieved by estimating the transformation (translation, rotation, and sometimes scaling) that best aligns the points in one point cloud with those in another [152]. However, numerous registration challenges arise when dealing with irregularly nonrigid plants [153]. First, different data sources or sensors may produce point clouds of different characteristics, including resolution and noise [154]. Second, when two point clouds do not substantially overlap, finding corresponding points or features for registration can be challenging [155,156]. Third, the plant objects or field scenes being registered undergo nonrigid deformations, making rigid transformations insufficient [153]. Finally, processing and registering large-scale point clouds can be computationally demanding. Although traditional ICP-based algorithms are widely known for registration [157,158], they rely on manual feature extraction and are sensitive to the initial pose value. Deep learning has introduced new opportunities for point cloud registration [159] and can be further explored for improving feature engineering [13,45], matching and registration accuracy, and robustness [160,161].

Data denoising: Point clouds often encompass not only the target but also extraneous objects from the surrounding environment [162]. Traditional denoising methods include mainly filter-based methods [163,164] and optimization-based methods [165], which are easy to understand but require handcrafted features. Deep learning techniques such as CNNs, GANs, and transformers are being increasingly integrated into 3D point denoising [[166], [167], [168]]. These methods have shown promise in terms of learning complex noise patterns and enhancing denoising performance. Further exploration of unsupervised/self-supervised learning methods for point cloud denoising is expected, which do not require pairs of clean and noisy data [169,170].

Data completion: This step involves filling in missing parts of a point cloud [171]. Incomplete point clouds can be caused by specular reflection, signal absorption, self-occlusion of objects, and occlusion of external objects. Numerous methods have been proposed for 3D data completion, such as point-based methods [[172], [173], [174], [175]], view-based methods [[176], [177], [178]], and graph-based methods [179,180]. However, point cloud completion of plants is especially challenging because of the diverse plant organs and their complex spatial relationships. The morphological and structural differences among different plant species also challenge the generalizability of completion methods [181]. Owing to the lack of complete real data, evaluating the accuracy and effectiveness of plant 3D point cloud completion methods is difficult. Deep learning has been more widely used in the accurate and robust completion of plant point clouds, such as in GAN-based networks [182]. In the future, self-supervised learning methods are expected to reduce the dependence on a large amount of annotated data and improve the generalization performance of the completion algorithms [174]. In addition, combining multimodal data generated by different sensors (such as LiDAR and cameras) is expected to improve the accuracy and robustness of plant 3D point cloud completion [126,176,178] because multimodal information can provide more comprehensive plant structural information.

Data annotation: This step provides the basis for training and testing deep learning models. The quality and accuracy of annotated data are critical to model performance and usability. Chaudhury et al. [183] noted that labeled point cloud data are all that is needed in 3D plant phenotyping. However, 3D labeling is much more time-consuming than 2D labeling is because it usually requires repeatedly zooming in and out from different views to accurately locate the label of each point. With the advancement of deep learning and computer vision, several methods have been developed to alleviate the problem of a lack of annotated data. First, transfer learning is used to pretrain the network based on synthetic data [184,185]. Recently, many studies have focused on crop growth models and functional structural plant models for 3D virtual data generation. For example, virtual wheat datasets simulated by the 3dCAP (3D canopy photosynthesis model) model with specified environments [186] can be used to generate labeled datasets for 3D deep learning [147]. The second method is to use unsupervised or weakly supervised learning frameworks to solve the annotation work involved in large datasets. For example, Luo et al. [111] developed a weakly supervised framework (Eff-3DPSeg) that used only partially (5 ​%) annotated data, greatly reducing the annotation effort of 3D data compared with fully supervised learning. Alternatively, these two directions can be combined. Additionally, recent advances in large language and large vision models have led to many innovations in the field of computer vision [187], such as the segment anything model (SAM) [188]. These innovations may empower not only image annotation but also point cloud annotation [189,190].

Data reorganization: Optimizing the sample size and data structure is beneficial for improving the model prediction result, even without modifying the hyperparameters or model architecture. First, data sampling is challenging because of the complex spatial and dynamic structure of plants. Generic downsampling algorithms may have difficulty adapting to this diversity, resulting in unbalanced sampling among different plants. Future downsampling methods need to be smarter and capable of understanding and retaining key information about a plant's 3D structure as well as maintaining sample balance, such as edge-enhanced sampling [26] and dynamic weighted sampling [120]. Second, future work should consider the complementary information of multisource data fusion and make full use of more features in addition to geometric information [126,136].

Data Augmentation: The limited quantity and diversity of datasets for 3D plant phenotyping may cause overfitting problems and further affect the generalization ability of the network. Data augmentation is one way to alleviate overfitting and increase performance. Traditional data augmentation methods include random rotations, translations, scaling, the addition of noise, etc. Although these methods have shown effectiveness in model training, existing data augmentation methods do not fully exploit the richer information of point clouds compared with their counterparts on 2D images. Here, we highlight some tricks and emerging strategies. First, simulated or synthetic crop 3D data can be used to provide an augmented dataset for training deep networks. Heiwolt et al. [125] constructed a synthetic 3D dataset containing 500 tomato plants to train and test PointNet++; the network pretrained on synthetic data performed normally on real tomato plant data. Liu et al. [147] used cropped real individual wheat samples to construct simulated plots to train a deep neural network for segmenting wheat heads. Second, point deformation of crop point clouds imitates the movement of crops in natural environments. Thus, deformation methods such as crop rotation, crop translation, scaling, and distortion are usually added to the original crop data to enrich the training data pool to improve task performance [25,134,145,146]. An improvement of approximately 1 ​% mIOU was achieved by rotating the plants along the x- and y-axes in every training iteration [137]. Third, 3D data augmentation can also be performed together with downsampling procedures [84,100]. For example, Peng et al. [148] combined VBS and FPS for 3D data processing of crops and conducted data augmentation by randomly selecting initial points in the FPS step. Guo et al. [142] used uniform and random downsampling as the primary data augmentation technique, which improved the model's robustness and generalization ability. A review of point cloud augmentation methods for general 3D deep learning can be found in Zhu et al. [191]. Future point cloud augmentation methods may focus on enhancing the physical meaning of plant structures by combining morphological knowledge with point cloud augmentation, which may adopt the strengths of different data augmentation methods [26].

Accurate and efficient 3D point cloud analysis: multitask learning, lightweight models, and self-supervised learning

(1)Improving accuracy with multitask learning

Improving accuracy with multitask learning

Accuracy is the basic demand of deep neural networks. Many efforts have been made to combine cutting-edge backbones and blocks from the computer vision and artificial intelligence communities to improve the accuracy of neural networks in 3D plant phenotyping. For example, PlantNet [26] incorporated EdgeConv [192], a convolutional block based on a dynamic GNN, into its main dual-function architecture to improve accuracy. The PSegNet model [78] incorporated both spatial attention and channel attention modules to concentrate point cloud features for better organ segmentation. The PST network [6] used transformer-like blocks to improve feature aggregation, which has also been verified in similar works, such as point cloud transformers (PCTs) [47], point transformers (PTs) [48], and dual point cloud transformers (DPCTs) [49]. These studies all suggest that the combination of transformers with point cloud networks is a reliable way to further improve learning performance.

Despite fusing cutting-edge backbones and blocks, multitask learning is important for improving network accuracy. By sharing the underlying representation, multitask learning allows the model to learn knowledge from one task and alleviate the labeling requirements of other tasks, thereby accelerating model training and practical application [193]. Since the development of SGPN [69], ASIS [194], and JSnet [193], dual-function networks for point cloud learning have attracted long-term interest. These networks attempt to simultaneously perform two or more tasks by applying a specially designed loss function to each subtask network. For example, VCNN [25] has an architecture that performs semantic segmentation of stems and leaves as well as instance segmentation of leaves from maize point clouds. Li et al. [27] added a new instance segmentation branch to PointNet with a new loss function and performed organ semantic segmentation and leaf instance segmentation for maize point clouds at the same time. Du et al. [6] designed a plant segmentation transformer (PST) to perform simultaneous organ semantic and instance segmentation for podding oilseed rape point clouds. Yu et al. [140] established the LFPNet architecture assisted by a 3D spatial transformer block for simultaneous classification and semantic segmentation of three types of fruit trees—apple, pear, and lemon canopies. PlantNet [26] and PSegNet [78] were recently designed to carry out simultaneous organ semantic segmentation and leaf instance segmentation on crop point clouds. Both networks used two different pathways with subloss functions to perform the two segmentation tasks.

(2)Optimizing efficiency by lightweight models

Optimizing efficiency by lightweight models

Efficiency is important for real applications. Lightweight 3D plant phenotyping models pose several challenges to reducing the model size and computational complexity while preserving performance and functionality [140]. Common lightweight methods include model pruning, quantization, knowledge distillation, depthwise separable convolution, and neural architecture search [40]. However, these techniques face specific challenges when applied to plants, including the complex geometry of plants, the dynamic nature of growth, and the complexity of the natural environment.

In the future, the development of 3D lightweight networks for plant phenotyping should focus on the following directions. First, the network structure should be optimized by adopting a more efficient feature extraction unit. For example, a nonparametric attention mechanism was introduced to enhance spatial representation learning and reduce computational overhead [195]. Second, the application of transfer learning and domain adaptation technologies can transfer models pretrained on rich datasets to new tasks through fine-tuning, which can significantly reduce training costs while maintaining good performance. For example, a two-view domain adaptation network named the deep-shallow domain adaptation network (DSDAN) was proposed to solve the challenge of cross-dataset point cloud recognition [196]. To explore local-level feature alignment between the two domains, a graph-based framework was proposed and effectively achieved feature alignment between different domains in point cloud semantic segmentation tasks [197]. Third, knowledge distillation is a technique that can create lightweight models with high accuracy and low computational demand by transferring knowledge from a large and complex teacher model to a smaller and more efficient student model. For example, Angarano et al. [198] proposed a novel method to address the problem of domain generalization for crop semantic segmentation in realistic scenarios using synthetic multidomain datasets. Fourth, efficient GNNs, transformer variants, and liquid time-constant networks (LTCNs) [199] can enable faster inference and reduce memory consumption in 3D tasks. For example, the point vision GNN (PointViG) is an efficient framework for point cloud analysis that incorporates a lightweight graph convolutional module to efficiently aggregate local features and mitigate oversmoothing. Experiments demonstrate that PointViG achieved 94.3 ​% accuracy with 1.5 ​M parameters on the ModelNet40 classification task. Finally, the introduction of self-supervised learning and weakly supervised learning enables the model to learn latent representations and intrinsic structures from a large amount of unlabeled data, making it possible to construct lightweight point cloud models that are cost effective and accurate. For example, the weakly supervised framework Eff-3DPSeg can achieve performance comparable to that of the fully supervised network even if only approximately 0.5 ​% of the data points are annotated.

(3)Leveraging self-supervised learning (SSL)

Leveraging self-supervised learning (SSL)

Another research trend is the exploration of other learning methodologies in addition to conventional supervised learning (SL) (Fig. 5a). Among the new learning methodologies, self-supervised learning (SSL) has attracted considerable attention in the 2D computer vision community because of its powerful performance when either the data or data annotations are insufficient. SSL addresses originally unlabeled data for which a supervised signal is manually activated by first hiding part of the available information and then trying to recover it [200]. The solution of the SSL task (which is also usually called the “pretext task”) captures high-level structured semantic knowledge from the data without a label so that the learned representation can be transferred to other downstream tasks as a powerful warm-up (pretraining) step. Luo and Sun [28] proposed a pioneering framework (briefly shown in Fig. 5b) that conducted self-supervised training with ConvUnet on a 3D crop dataset without labels and then carried out supervised semantic and instance organ segmentation with the same crop dataset. A new SSL branch shares a feature encoder with the supervised task branch. The result based on sparsely annotated crop training data is even comparable to the counterpart result based on full annotation under the same supervised network.

Overview of different deep learning methodologies applied to crop point cloud analysis. (a) The conventional supervised learning architecture, which relies on labeled data for training. (b) The self-supervised learning (SSL)-assisted deep learning framework proposed by Luo and Sun [28], which leverages unlabeled data to improve feature learning. (c) Domain adaptation (DA), where domain knowledge flows from the supervised branch to the SSL branch to improve generalization. (d) Possible DA assisted by SSL, where domain knowledge flows inversely, highlighting the emerging role of SSL in enhancing cross-domain adaptability.

It is also natural to connect SSL with domain adaptation (DA) applications. DA can be bidirectional. One direction is knowledge transfer from the source domain (labeled crop species) to the target domain (unlabeled crop species) (Fig. 5c). For example, a network is trained on labeled simulated data and is applied to real-world data that are only sparsely labeled. In the other direction—a possible inverse DA architecture is designed to further improve the recognition performance on the current well-labeled data domain by transferring knowledge self-learned from another unlabeled domain (Fig. 5d) [201]. Therefore, SSL is useful for inverse-directional DA because it usually involves self-learning.

Deep learning for 3D plant phenomics: interpretability, extensibility, and multimodal data utilization

(1)Enhancing model interpretability

Enhancing model interpretability

Interpretability is vital for trustworthy and scientifically grounded decision-making in agriculture and plant science. In the context of 3D deep learning, interpretability primarily refers to understanding why a model performs well—what features it relies on and how those features contribute to its outputs [202]. Explainable AI (XAI) methods—such as attention mechanisms, layerwise relevance propagation, and saliency maps—have shown promise in visualizing and interpreting decision pathways in deep models [[203], [204], [205]]. In practice, developing interactive visualization tools to explore model outputs in 3D space allows researchers and practitioners to better understand how models make decisions [137,206].

(2)Unlocking biological interpretability

Unlocking biological interpretability

Beyond understanding model internals, another form of interpretability concerns the biological or physiological relevance of model outputs. This concept refers to how well computational results—such as traits derived from 3D data—correlate with measurable plant functions and can support downstream biological interpretation and breeding decisions. For example, 3D-derived traits such as the average angle-to-height ratio (AHR) and average angle-to-stem length ratio (ALR) have been shown to outperform traditional traits in screening soybean plant architecture [207]. Similarly, LiDAR-derived 3D point cloud data have been shown to enable accurate and high-heritability estimates of grapevine total canopy volume and pruning wood volume, outperforming the exposed leaf area measured from images and destructive measurements of the pruning weight [208]. These examples underscore the importance of grounding computational insights in biological meaning.

Despite rapid advances, the application of 3D deep learning in plant biology remains in its early stages. A key challenge concerns translating technical advancements into meaningful biological applications. For example, real-time 3D reconstruction and spatiotemporal tracking of plant architecture could drive the development of digital twins for crop growth and management [209]. Time series 3D phenotyping data, when accurately registered [93,103,210], can support dynamic trait modeling and high-throughput phenotyping [211,212]. Reliable estimation of dynamic, heritable traits is essential for quantitative trait loci (QTL) discovery and precision breeding [213]. These biologically grounded applications will constitute the next frontier of scalable, interpretable, and impactful 3D phenotyping research.

(3)Boosting model extensibility

Boosting model extensibility

Extensibility refers to the ability of a model to adapt to different datasets, plant species, and conditions. Highly extensible models not only enhance technical robustness and reproducibility but also ensure the broader applicability and long-term value of phenotyping tools in real-world agricultural systems. To improve model extensibility, two key strategies are being actively developed: (i) synthetic dataset generation and hybrid modeling and (ii) leveraging foundation models.

First, synthetic datasets offer a scalable solution for diversifying training data. These datasets can be generated under varied growth conditions and structural complexities using procedural plant growth models, such as L-systems or functional-structural plant models (FSPMs) [214]. These virtual datasets can moreover reduce the cost of manual annotation, help models learn from a broader distribution of data structures, and improve model resilience to real-world variability. Additionally, hybrid modeling combines the advantages of data-driven learning and domain-specific physical models, which can enhance both interpretability and generalizability. For example, a novel knowledge-and-data-driven modeling approach was proposed for simulating tomato growth, where the GreenLab functional–structural model (a knowledge-driven submodel) is paired with a radial basis function network (a data-driven model), enhancing both biological fidelity and predictive performance [215]. Such methods also promote cross-platform interoperability and the integration of digital twin technologies [216], which link real and virtual crop systems for dynamic phenotype monitoring.

Second, the transition from artificial narrow intelligence (ANI) to artificial general intelligence (AGI) involves the catalysis of new levels of extensibility. Foundation models (FMs) such as CLIP and SAM are built upon large-scale, self-supervised pretraining across diverse modalities (text, image, 3D), enabling generalizable representation learning that transfers effectively to downstream tasks such as segmentation, classification, and visual question answering [[188], [189], [190],217]. SAM's concept of "promotable tasks" has inspired efforts to adapt its capabilities to 3D phenotyping—for instance, through 2D-to-3D mask projection and fusion in point cloud segmentation [188,190]. By distilling FMs into domain-specific 3D models, plant phenotyping can benefit from greater scalability, semantic consistency, and cross-scene generalization [189,217]. Recent developments such as PointCLIP adapt such models directly to 3D point cloud data [218]. A comprehensive review of foundation models for 3D point cloud analysis can be found in Thengane et al. [219].

(4)Unleashing the potential of multimodal data

Unleashing the potential of multimodal data

Multimodal data fusion is a powerful strategy for enhancing the accuracy, robustness, and physiological relevance of 3D plant phenotyping [220,221]. By integrating complementary data sources such as LiDAR, hyperspectral, thermal, multispectral, and RGB imagery, models can leverage both structural and biochemical information, enabling a more holistic understanding of plant traits.

Recent advances highlight the potential of multimodal fusion for precise 3D segmentation and species classification. For example, PMNet achieved accurate segmentation of aerial point clouds by integrating image features with 3D spatial information, demonstrating the advantage of combining visual and geometric cues [222]. Similarly, the hyperspectral point cloud projection model (HPProj), which is capable of self-supervised training using only hyperspectral imagery and enables zero-shot transfer to generate hyperspectral point clouds by fusing asynchronously acquired hyperspectral imagery and LiDAR points, was proposed based on deep contrastive learning [220]. This method enabled accurate mapping of 24 vegetation species in karst wetlands and revealed spatial variations in plant functional traits along a hydrological gradient.

UAV systems have further improved the capabilities of multimodal fusion in field phenotyping. Sankey [223] demonstrated the potential of UAV-based hyperspectral–thermal–LiDAR data fusion for phenotyping and classifying various populations of Fremont cottonwood within a common garden, with each data source contributing unique insights. Similarly, recent advances in UAV platforms have further expanded the applications of multimodal fusion in high-throughput plant phenotyping and trait analysis. Stumpe et al. [224] and Xie et al. [225] showed how registering multispectral and thermal data with 3D point clouds enables the tracking of physiological signals such as chlorophyll content and water status.

Conclusions

In conclusion, deep learning holds tremendous potential for revolutionizing 3D plant phenotyping by enabling accurate, efficient, and comprehensive scene understanding. By addressing challenges such as point cloud annotation, downsampling, and dataset organization, deep learning approaches can pave the way for innovative applications in plant phenomics and beyond. This review first presents the recent progress in deep learning in the computer vision community. Then, deep learning for 3D plant phenotyping is overviewed, highlighting the preprocessing steps for 3D deep learning and the progress made in plant phenotyping tasks. Current phenotyping applications are limited in terms of target segmentation and phenotypic trait extraction, considering the tremendous challenges associated with combining 3D deep learning with irregular plants. Finally, three perspectives are outlined, including benchmark dataset construction, 3D data interpretation by developing accurate and lightweight networks, and deep learning for 3D plant phenomics with high interpretability and extensibility. We believe that future efforts in these directions will contribute to artificial intelligence-based crop phenotyping research and future agriculture.
