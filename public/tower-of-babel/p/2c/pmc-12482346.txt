Enhancing cardiovascular risk prediction in Asian populations: A machine learning approach integrated with digital health platforms
Authors: Sazzli Kasim, Putri Nur Fatin Amir Rudin, Xue Ning Kiew, Nurulain Ibrahim, Nafiza Mat Nasir, Lim Bing Feng, Hanis Hamidi, Khairul Shafiq Ibrahim, Raja Ezman Raja Shariff, Suraya Abdul-Razak, Kazuaki Negishi, Sorayya Malek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to develop and validate a machine learning (ML)–based model for cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk prediction in a Malaysian cohort representative of the Southeast Asian population.MethodsData from the Responding to Increasing Cardiovascular Disease Prevalence (REDISCOVER) Study, including 10,044 participants, were analyzed, with 4,299 cases retained after exclusions. The dataset was split into training (70 %) and validation (30 %) subsets. Logistic Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), and Support Vector Machine (SVM) models were developed using feature selection techniques such as recursive feature elimination (RFE) and sequential backward elimination (SBE). Model performance was evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, calibration, and Net Reclassification Index (NRI).FindingsAmong the models evaluated, the SVM model with SBE-selected features performed best, achieving an AUC of 0.800. This was higher than the Framingham Risk Score (FRS; AUC = 0.693), Revised Pooled Cohort Equations (RPCE; AUC = 0.744), and WHO CVD charts (AUC = 0.741). NRI analysis showed significant improvements compared to FRS and RPCE (17.29 % and 14.23 %, respectively; p < 0.00001). Calibration analyses indicated initial overprediction by ML models, which was mitigated by Platt scaling.ConclusionML-based models incorporating regionally relevant variables demonstrated improved discrimination and reclassification compared with conventional risk scores in this Malaysian cohort. Further external validation is needed to establish their utility across broader Southeast Asian populations.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) remain the leading cause of mortality globally, accounting for 44 % of deaths from non-communicable diseases in 2021. According to WHO 2022, the Western Pacific region, including Asia, has seen a 47.4 % increase in CVD-related deaths, despite a reduction in age-standardised mortality rates. A diverse Asian population provides a unique opportunity to develop and validate CVD risk prediction models that could be applicable across Western Pacific and potentially beyond.

Traditional tools like the Framingham Risk Score (FRS), Revised Pooled Cohort Equations (RPCE), and SCORE2 (Systematic Coronary Risk Evaluation 2) were developed primarily with Western population data. As a result, they commonly misclassify risk in Asian populations, due to regional variations in genetics, lifestyle, and comorbidities [[1], [2], [3]]. For example, FRS has been demonstrated to overestimate risk in Chinese cohorts by 15–20 % [4], but SCORE2 underestimates stroke risk in Southeast Asian populations by approximately 12 percent [3]. These calibration issues can result in suboptimal therapy judgments, emphasizing the crucial need for more accurate and regionally adapted risk assessment systems.

There have been several CVD risk prediction methods created in Asia. Notably, Japan created the Japan Arteriosclerosis Longitudinal Study (JALS) score exclusively for the Japanese population [5]. China created the China-PAR model with Chinese cohort data [6]. A large Korean national cohort was used to generate the Korean Heart Study Score, which has been validated for predicting 10-year cardiovascular risk in East Asia [7]. In Southeast Asia, Thailand used data from the Electricity Generating Authority of Thailand (EGAT) cohort to develop the Rama-EGAT model (Thai CV Risk Score) [8]. Despite progress in other Asian regions, Malaysia still lacks a validated cardiovascular risk prediction tool based on its own population data. Malaysia's distinct ethnic variety, demographics, and culture may limit the usability of these Asian-developed technologies, highlighting the need for a locally customised model.

Evidence suggests that Asian populations, particularly Southeast Asians, demonstrate distinct cardiovascular risk profiles that are not fully represented by Western-derived models. A missense variant in the APOA5 gene has been significantly associated with coronary artery disease in multi-ethnic Southeast Asian cohorts [9]. A recent meta-analysis indicated that dietary patterns and their associations with cardiovascular disease risk differ among Asian regions, with Southeast Asian populations showing unique patterns in contrast to those in East and South Asia [10]. Furthermore, evidence shows that South and Southeast Asians develop type 2 diabetes and metabolic complications at lower BMI thresholds than Western populations, indicating a need for population-specific BMI cut-offs [11]. The observed differences highlight the necessity of developing and validating CVD risk prediction tools tailored to Southeast Asian populations.

Machine learning (ML) offers an alternative option by identifying complex interactions among risk factors and including region-specific variables. However, the clinical value of ML models in Asian populations remains underexplored, particularly with respect to improving calibration and risk reclassification indicators such as Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI). Furthermore, the implementation of ML in healthcare depends on the interpretability of model predictions. Recent innovations like SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) offer transparency by quantifying the contribution of various features to risk estimations [12]. Recent research in Asian populations shows that ML models identify important risk factors like age, blood pressure, and metabolic measurements when in combination with SHAP for interpretability. These models also provide more transparent and personalized cardiovascular risk predictions than traditional methods [13].

This study aims to develop and validate an interpretable ML-based CVD risk prediction model uniquely tailored to the Malaysian population. Given considerations of demographic and health concerns, Malaysia stands as a suitable setting for this study as it shares some similarities with other Southeast Asian countries. The approach addresses three major gaps: the calibration of risk estimates, the assessment of clinical utility through NRI, and the interpretability of SHAP to comprehend population-specific risk drivers. This research will aid in the endeavour to enhance clinical judgment and CVD risk assessment in multiethnic populations in Southeast Asia.

Methods

Study population

This study utilized data from the Responding to Increasing Cardiovascular Disease Prevalence (REDISCOVER) Study, an ongoing, longitudinal cohort study initiated in 2007 in Malaysia. Malaysia's diverse population, including individuals of Indo-China, South Asia, and East Asia origins, provides a robust foundation for investigating CVD across various ethnic contexts. Participants were recruited from eight states to ensure representation of major ethnic groups, including Malays, Chinese, Indians, and indigenous populations. A total of 12,617 participants aged 18–89 years were enrolled, with an average follow-up period of 12.05 years. Data were collected across 11 domains, including demographics, medical and family history, clinical measurements, and physical activity assessments. The primary outcomes were fatal and non-fatal CVD events within 10 years of baseline, recorded and adjudicated using hospital records and national death certificates.

The REDISCOVER study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board at Universiti Teknologi MARA (UiTM) (Ethics approval number: REC/UITM/2007 [10]). Data usage for the current analysis was authorized by the Project Leader on June 14, 2021.

Data preprocessing

For ML model development, 10,044 cases were selected based on criteria including age over 18 years, recruitment between 2007 and 2010, more than 10 years of follow-up, and no prior history of CVD. From the original 202 variables, 146 were excluded due to more than 30 % missing data, following recommended practices [14]. In addition, the variables recruitment_year, recruitment_location, spectacle, and hearing aid were removed as they were deemed clinically irrelevant, while race was excluded to enhance model robustness and ensure generalisability for the Malaysian population. The remaining 51 variables were then examined for zero or near-zero variance using the ‘nearZeroVar’ function in the R caret package, resulting in the removal of eleven variables including marital status, history of heart disease, stroke, hepatitis, cancer, COPD, asthma, tuberculosis, malaria, and use of asthma or stroke medications.

After these exclusions, the final complete dataset comprised 4,299 cases with 40 variables. Data were split into 70 % training (3,010 participants) and 30 % validation (1,289 participants) sets using stratified sampling to maintain the proportion of CVD events in both sets. Five-fold cross-validation was employed to prevent overfitting and validate model performance [15,16]. To address the significant class imbalance between CVD and non-CVD events, “both” option from the ROSE (Random Over-Sampling Examples) package in R was applied, which generates a balanced dataset by simultaneously performing random under-sampling of the majority class and over-sampling of the minority class through bootstrapping. This approach has been shown to effectively improve prediction performance in highly imbalanced datasets [17]. Continuous variables were normalized using z-score normalization and applied separately to training and validation datasets to prevent data leakage.

Feature selection

Feature selection was conducted using Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) and Sequential Backward Elimination (SBE). RFE systematically removes the least important features, refining the model iteratively until the most relevant subset is identified, while SBE starts with all features and sequentially removes the least significant ones. Both methods were applied to optimize the model's performance by retaining the most predictive variables.

Model development

ML models were developed using logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), and support vector machine (SVM) algorithms. The LR model was developed using L1 regularization to prevent overfitting. Hyperparameter optimization for the RF and SVM models was performed using a random search strategy combined with five-fold cross-validation. This process iteratively evaluated more than 30 parameter combinations to ensure stable parameter selection and minimize overfitting. For the RF model, the optimal parameters were mtry = 2 and ntree = 500. For the SVM model, the best-performing configuration employed a linear kernel with sigma = 0.01 and C = 0.1.

Model evaluation and calibration

Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) to assess discrimination, with AUROC >0.75 and a total sum of sensitivity and specificity greater than 1.5 is considered clinically useful [18,19]. To improve the reliability of predicted probabilities, Platt scaling was applied. Platt scaling is a post-processing calibration method that fits a logistic regression model to the classifier's decision scores, thereby transforming raw outputs into well-calibrated probability estimates [20,21]. This approach reduces systematic overprediction and ensures closer alignment with observed outcomes. . Calibration was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow Goodness-of-Fit Test, with calibration plots comparing predicted and observed 10-year CVD risks. The validation set was used for the final evaluation of the ML models and their calibration.

Comparison with conventional CVD risk scores

The 10-year CVD risk estimates for the validation set were calculated using conventional risk scores—FRS, RPCE, and WHO CVD—selected for their relevance to the Asian population [2]. Performance metrics, including accuracy, specificity, sensitivity, and AUROC, were calculated to compare the ML models against these traditional scores. Pairwise AUROC comparisons were conducted using the DeLong test, with significance set at p < 0.001. The NRI was calculated to evaluate the improvement in risk classification by the best-performing ML model compared to FRS and RPCE, with statistical significance assessed using a Z-statistics [22].

Model interpretation

Implementing ML models in clinical medicine can be challenging due to their complicated and complex decision-making processes. However, SHAP values provide a solution by improving model interpretability. SHAP sheds insight into the way various input features influence model outputs, allowing for the ranking of feature significance and understanding the fundamentals of predictions [23,24]. This technique enhances transparency in model decisions and is based in established scientific principles, which is essential for regulatory compliance and promoting public trust in medical AI. SHAP enables clinicians to identify essential risk factors and develop personalized treatment plans, thus enhancing patient outcomes [24].

Software and packages

All analyses were carried out using R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The caret package, which offered a unified framework for data partitioning, cross-validation, model training, hyperparameter tuning, and performance evaluation, was mainly used to implement data preparation, model building, and evaluation. For several tasks, such as addressing class imbalance, multiple imputation for missing values, algorithm-specific modelling, performance evaluation, and data import and manipulation, additional programs were utilized. Results

Patient characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the 4,299 individuals in the current study are shown in Supplementary Table 1. The distribution of each variable between the two groups (CVD event vs. no CVD event) and statistical significance are displayed in this table, which provides a univariate analysis of all variables.

Among these participants, 167 (3.88 %) experienced a CVD event within 10 years of follow-up. Participants who developed CVD events were older, with a mean age of 57.75 years compared to 51.33 years for those without an event. CVD events were more frequent among males (64.1 %), those currently unemployed at time of recruitment (65.9 %), rural residents (59.3 %), and non-smokers (58.7 %). Comorbidities were prevalent among those with CVD events: 28.1 % had diabetes mellitus, 12 % had hyperlipidemia, and 24.3 % had hypertension. Additionally, 41.47 % had a family history of hypertension. Of the 40 variables analyzed, 17 showed statistically significant differences between those who developed a CVD event and those who did not. Participants with CVD events had higher mean systolic and diastolic blood pressures (149.13 mmHg and 84.99 mmHg, respectively), a greater waist circumference (91.29 cm), with a waist-to-hip ratio of 0.91, and elevated levels of glucose (6.65 mmol/L), HDL-cholesterol (1.13 mmol/L), and triglycerides (2.03 mmol/L). The FVC and FEV1 of the individuals experiencing CVD events were significantly lower, which are 2.15 L and 1.89 L, respectively.

Feature selection

Thevariables used for CVD prediction in ML models using RFE and SBE with traditional risk scores, such as RPCE, FRS, and WHO CVD are compared in Table 2. Several features are consistently found in both traditional risk scores and ML models, emphasizing their significance in CVD prediction. The factors include gender, age, smoking status, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, and LDL cholesterol. ML models employing RFE and SBE identified supplementary features typically absent from conventional risk scores. Included are anthropometric measures such as BMI, waist circumference, and waist-to-hip ratio; functional markers including dominant and non-dominant handgrip strength; pulmonary function parameters like FEV1 and PEFR; and lifestyle factors such as education level. These characteristics indicate that ML techniques could capture larger factors influencing CVD risk. Conventional risk scores, such as RPCE, FRS, and WHO CVD, place an emphasis on conventional clinical variables such as blood pressure, lipid profiles, smoking status, diabetes baseline, and fasting blood glucose levels. These scores rely on well-established predictions, but ignore numerous novel variables discovered by ML methods.

Model performance

The AUC values for conventional CVD risk scores (FRS, RPCE, and WHO versions) and ML models that use SBE or RFE on a 30 % validation dataset are compared in Table 3. The SBE approach consistently produced superior ML models, with SBE-Logistic Regression having a highest AUC of 0.800 (95 % CI: 0.738–0.862), surpassing the FRS (AUC: 0.693, 95 % CI: 0.625–0.761). While RFE-based models outperformed the FRS, their overall performance remained lower than that of the SBE-selected feature sets. The RPCE (AUC: 0.744, 95 % CI: 0.682–0.806) and both WHO versions (Lab: 0.741, 95 % CI: 0.678–0.805; Non-lab: 0.726, 95 % CI: 0.660–0.792) outperformed the FRS but were less accurate than the top SBE-derived ML models, highlighting SBE's relative effectiveness over RFE and traditional approaches for enhanced CVD risk prediction.

SHAP analysis

The SHAP summary plot in Fig. 3demonstrates the influence of features identified through SBE LR-based feature selection on the model's predictions for CVD. The plot indicates that elevated feature values (red) correspond to features that enhance the SHAP value, thereby leading predictions towards the positive class (CVD presence). Low feature values (blue) frequently reduce the SHAP value, lowering the risk of CVD. The features are ranked according to their significance, with the most influential at the top.

SHAP Analysis of SBE LR-based feature selection.

The primary predictor is age with a higher age (shown in red) significantly increases the risk of CVD whereas lower age (in blue) substantially decreases it. Likewise, a higher risk of CVD is linked with reduced grip strength and higher BMI values. Other important variables include family history with hypertension and heart disease, and high cholesterol levels all of which play a substantial role in predicting CVD when present. Waist-to-hip ratio and right calf circumference both have a moderate relationship, with greater values generally increasing risk. Features such as cholesterol medication use, employment status, and fasting blood glucose levels had minor but significant effects on predictions.

Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) analysis

The NRI of LR (SBE-LR features) approaches to RPCE and FRS in predicting CVD occurrences are compared in Supplementary Table 4. For RPCE, ML approaches categorised individuals into more accurate risk categories, resulting in an overall NRI of 14.23. Improvements were reported for both individuals with and without CVD events. Specifically, the NRI was 6.00 for people who had CVD events and 8.23 for those who didn't. For FRS, the total NRI was slightly higher at 17.29, owing to a considerable improvement of 37.29 for people without CVD events, despite a negative NRI (−20.00) for those with CVD occurrences. These findings illustrate the potential of ML approaches to improve risk prediction over conventional tool such as RPCE and FRS.

Discussion

This study addressed a key gap in reliable CVD risk prediction for the Asian population in the Western Pacific region by developing and establishing a machine learning-based model tailored to their specific demographic, genetic, and lifestyle characteristics. The main discovery is that ML algorithms, namely LR with SBE feature selection, outperform the traditional FRS in this study.

The findings demonstrate that ML algorithms, particularly the LR model with SBE outperformed the FRS (AUC = 0.693) in terms of discrimination (AUC = 0.800) and reclassification capabilities (NRI). Furthermore, the LR model outperformed the FRS by 18.95 %, highlighting the potential of machine learning to improve risk stratification and clinical decision-making in Western Pacific populations by reducing diagnostic uncertainty and minimizing misclassification by conventional risk scores [25,26].

Despite the enhanced discrimination of the LR model, the initial study revealed calibration challenges, with the original model overestimating CVD risk. Calibration, an important feature for efficient clinical application, was performed by applying Platt scaling to correct overprediction and ensure the model's risk estimates were more clinically relevant and trustworthy. The use of calibration metrics in thisstudy, which was not prevalent in earlier studies, improves the practical application of this findings.

The addition of SHAP values emphasized the continued importance of the conventional predictors contained in well-known models such as the FRS, RPCE, and WHO CVD risk scores, such as age, gender, smoking status, blood pressure, cholesterol levels, and fasting blood glucose. Notably, the SBE-LR model includes novel factors that were absent in conventional models, such as BMI, waist-to-hip ratio, family history of heart disease, and dominant handgrip strength, which increased estimated accuracy by accounting for individual heterogeneity. SHAP-based ML models improve transparency by visually measuring the contributions of these attributes to individual predictions.

Although smoking is a well-established CVD risk factor, and the baseline analysis revealed a larger proportion of current smokers among participants who experienced CVD events, smoking status was not included in the final SBE-derived model. This is consistent with the framework of the feature selection technique, where SBE combined with ML algorithms prioritises predictors that optimize overall discriminative performance (AUC) rather than depending on single-variable significance. Consequently, the predictive value of smoking reduced once other significant variables, including age, blood pressure, waist-to-hip ratio, and metabolic indicators were added. This demonstrates how ML techniques capture complex interactions between risk factors and improve prediction by focussing on characteristics that have the greatest impact on model performance.

While the study did not include direct genetic testing or detailed dietary intake data, several selected parameters served as proxies for genetic and lifestyle factors. Inherited risk was determined by family history variables for cancer, diabetes, heart disease, and high blood pressure. The socioeconomic and behavioural aspects of cardiovascular risk were captured by lifestyle-related factors as handgrip strength, pulmonary function, waist-to-hip ratio, BMI, education level, and work status. Incorporating these variables allowed the model to better represent the diverse factors that influence CVD risk in the Malaysian population.

The ability of the model to maintain predictive accuracy when applied to unseen data from Malaysia's multiethnic population is referred to in this study as generalisation performance. In order to reduce the risk of subgroup bias, this was evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation on a nationally representative cohort that included Malays, Chinese, Indians, and indigenous populations. In addition to reducing model complexity and overfitting, the use of RFE and SBE for feature selection improved robustness. While these findings indicate their potential application in Malaysia's healthcare system, additional validation in Southeast Asian and Western Pacific populations is required.

The development of an ML model that significantly enhanced CVD risk prediction over traditional methods carries significant therapeutic significance. More specific risk classification helps healthcare providers to make better-informed judgments regarding resource allocation and focused preventative interventions, potentially minimizing both overtreatment and undertreatment with generalist models like the FRS [27]. By adding individual variability in addition to established risk indicators, the approach has the potential to reduce CVD-related morbidity and mortality in Malaysian and, more generally, Western Pacific populations.

Currently, regular CVD risk assessment in Malaysia does not include novel predictors like handgrip strength and calf circumference that were discovered by SHAP analysis. These are non-invasive, practical methods that can be used in primary care. In Southeast Asian adults, calf circumference has been validated as a screening tool for low skeletal muscle mass [28] and is linked to adverse metabolic profiles [29]. In Asian cohorts, lower handgrip strength has been linked to higher cardiovascular events and mortality [30]. These measures could improve early detection of individuals at risk and enhance integrated cardiovascular and functional health initiatives when incorporated into preventative initiatives.

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis was based on a single Malaysian cohort and despite being nationally representative and multiethnic, the REDISCOVER dataset's generalisability to other Southeast Asian and Western Pacific populations is limited by its lack of external validation. Second, proxies like family history, anthropometry, and education might not adequately reflect the effects of genetic markers and comprehensive nutrition data, which were not accessible. Third, the lack of standard clinical measurement of several of the novel variables found by SHAP, like calf circumference and handgrip strength, may restrict their use. Future studies should combine more genetic and lifestyle data to increase accuracy and applicability, as well as external validation in varied Asian cohorts. In fact, a novel comprehensive E(e)SEEDi-based scoring system from China for CVD risk prediction and prevention is very helpful in CVD health and worthy of conduction in the globe [31].

In conclusion, this study presents a unique ML model customized to predict CVD risk in the Asian population, displaying better performance over existing models. To facilitate broader clinical adoption and continuous validation, an online system for CVD risk prediction has been developed, accessible at https://myheartrisk.uitm.edu.my/, which integrates both ML-based models and the conventional FRS.The system is currently undergoing internal testing and refinement, and is not yet publicly accessible; access can be provided upon request. Interface screenshots are provided in the Supplementary Fig. 1. Future work will focus on potential validation within the Malaysian healthcare system and researching its applicability across varied populations in the Western Pacific.

Conclusion

This study developed and evaluated a ML-based CVD prediction model tailored to the Malaysian population, which demonstrated higher discrimination and reclassification compared to conventional scores. The lack of external validation is an important limitation that limits generalisability outside of Malaysia. To ensure robustness and scalability, future research will evaluate the model in external, multiethnic cohorts across Southeast Asia and the Western Pacific.
