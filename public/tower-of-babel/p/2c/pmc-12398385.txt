Genetic, neuropeptidergic, and cardiometabolic interplay in female central precocious puberty
Authors: Aygun Khaleddin Musayeva, Mahira Firudinkizi Amirova
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Central precocious puberty (CPP) results from premature reactivation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis and is increasingly recognized as a systemic condition linked to cardiometabolic health. Genetic mutations, particularly in imprinted genes such as MKRN3 and DLK1, are major monogenic causes of familial CPP, while rare activating variants in KISS1 and KISS1R highlight the pivotal role of kisspeptin signaling. Neuropeptides, including kisspeptin and neurokinin B, are central to pubertal regulation. Advances in clinical assessment, biochemical markers, pelvic ultrasound, and genetic testing have improved diagnostic precision, though differentiating CPP from benign variants remains challenging. Gonadotropin-releasing hormone analogs remain the gold standard for halting progression and optimizing adult height, while novel neuropeptide modulators show promise. Beyond growth outcomes, accumulating evidence indicates significant cardiometabolic sequelae, underscoring the importance of early, precision-guided intervention. Integrating genomic, neuropeptidergic, and metabolic insights can refine diagnosis, guide therapy, and potentially mitigate lifelong cardiovascular risk in affected females.

Introduction

Pubertal timing refers to the developmental puberty window during which individuals of the same chronological age reach specific biological milestones of sexual maturation, whereas the secular trend is a long-term, generational shift in a population-level trait, observed over decades. A meta-analysis of breast development onset (thelarche) found a decline of 0.24 years (≈3 months) per decade between 1973 and 2013 [1,2], coinciding with global surges in childhood adiposity and endocrine-disrupting exposures. Excess adiposity elevates circulating adipokines – most notably leptin – which, in turn, can activate the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis and precipitate earlier pubertal onset in adolescent girls [3]. A recent narrative review highlights this mechanistic pathway: elevated leptin levels in children with obesity stimulate kisspeptin neurons, thereby triggering gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) release and advancing puberty [4–6]. In parallel, sex-specific metabolic mediators such as leptin in girls and adaptations in insulin signaling have been proposed to differentially modulate HPG activity and further accelerate pubertal progression [7,8].

Central precocious puberty (CPP), characterized by the development of secondary sexual characteristics before the age of 8 years in girls, is increasingly recognized in clinical practice. Recent epidemiological estimates from the USA suggest that CPP affects approximately one in 1500 school-aged girls, based on data collected during the early 2000s [9]. This prevalence underscores a growing need for awareness and early diagnosis, particularly in the context of environmental and metabolic factors that may be influencing pubertal timing. This disorder is more common in girls and can result in compromised adult height, psychological stress, and potential long-term health risks if not managed effectively [10]. Mounting evidence suggests that early estrogen exposure, as seen in cases of early pubertal onset, is not merely a reproductive concern but also a risk factor for adverse long-term health outcomes. Specifically, early pubertal timing has been linked to unfavorable cardiometabolic phenotypes, including increased insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, an elevated risk for type 2 diabetes, and ischemic cardiovascular disease. These associations are thought to be mediated, at least in part, by the prolonged exposure to estrogen and its downstream metabolic effects [11,12]. Importantly, these risks often persist even after adjusting for adult obesity, highlighting the independent role of pubertal timing in shaping long-term metabolic trajectories that warrant a broader clinical approach [13]. CPP is the gonadotropin-dependent form of precocious puberty and is caused by premature activation of the HPG axis [14,15]. The condition involves elevated GnRH secretion from the hypothalamus, leading to the secretion of luteinizing hormone and follicle-stimulating hormone, which stimulate the ovaries to produce sex steroids [16,17].

Genetic and epigenetic architecture of central precocious puberty

Imprinted gate-keeper genes

Pubertal timing is a complex trait influenced by genetic, environmental, and nutritional factors. Twin and familial studies have shown that genetics accounts for 50–80% of the variability in pubertal onset [18]. While most cases of CPP in girls are idiopathic, monogenic causes have increasingly been identified, particularly involving imprinted genes and key regulatory pathways of GnRH secretion [19]. Loss-of-function variants in the paternally expressed makorin ring finger protein 3 (MKRN3) remain the leading monogenic cause of familial CPP, accounting for up to 45% of pedigrees diagnosed since 2020 [20,21]. Multiomics studies show that MKRN3 protein ubiquitinates the insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 1, destabilizing Gonadotropin releasing hormone-1 (GNRH1) mRNA and thereby restraining GnRH pulsatility [22]. Pathogenic variants span frameshift, nonsense, and promoter disruptions, all exerting haploinsufficiency.

Delta-like homolog 1 (DLK1)-mediated CPP, though rarer (<3% of cases), uniquely couples pubertal advancement with childhood-onset adiposity, dyslipidaemia, and hyperinsulinaemia [23–26]. DLK1 encodes a Notch pathway modulator; recent single-cell atlasing of human hypothalamus confirmed its enrichment in glutamatergic neurons adjacent to kisspeptin, neurokinin B, and dynorphin-expressing (KNDy) clusters, reinforcing a central role [27].

Nonimprinted genes and polygenic risk

KiSS1 metastasis-suppressor gene encodes kisspeptin, and pathogenic changes in the kisspeptin system – either in the ligand gene KISS1 or in its receptor KISS1R [G-protein-coupled receptor 54 (GPR54)] – plus gain-of-function variants in the neurokinin B (NKB) pathway genes tachykinin precursor 3 (TAC3) and its receptor tachykinin receptor 3 (TACR3), as well as rare prokineticin receptor 2 (PROKR2) duplications, have all been linked to CPP [28]. Since 2021, sporadic case reports have described activating variants in KISS1 and its receptor KISS1R (GPR54), gain-of-function mutations in TAC3/TACR3 (NKB pathway), and rare PROKR2 gene duplications, each associated with rapidly progressive, nonfamilial CPP [29,30].

Genome-wide association studies encompassing more than 800 000 genotypes now explain ≈15% of population variance in age at thelarche, highlighting polygenic influences that converge on adiposity, insulin-like growth factor, and circadian pathways [31].

Epigenetic reprogramming

Reduced methylation at the differentially methylated regions (DMRs) of the DLK1-maternally expressed gene 3 and MKRN3 DMRs, acetylated histone H3 at lysine 27 (H3K27ac) accumulation over KISS1 enhancers, and microRNA-mediated silencing of Lin-28 homolog B (LIN28B) have been described in CPP cohorts since 2022 [32–34]. These data suggest a multilayered epigenetic brake that is prematurely lifted by gene–environment interactions, including endocrine-disrupting phenols and excess adiposity.

Cardiometabolic links

Experimental models indicate that hypothalamic DLK1 ablation augments sympathetic outflow, elevating blood pressure and altering brown-fat thermogenesis [35]. Clinically, DLK1-CPP carriers manifest a 1.8-fold higher risk of metabolic syndrome by 18 years compared with idiopathic CPP [23]. Meanwhile, MKRN3 variants do not intrinsically worsen metabolic risk, implying gene-specific cardioendocrine trajectories. Neuropeptidergic control of gonadotropin-releasing hormone pulsatility

Kisspeptin, neurokinin B, and dynorphin expressing neuron circuitry

Kisspeptin, NKB, and dynorphin colocalize in arcuate KNDy neurons, generating synchronized depolarizations that gate GnRH release. Neurokinin 3 receptor (NK3R), the G-protein-coupled receptor, is encoded by the TACR3 gene. It is the high-affinity receptor for NKB and is coexpressed with kisspeptin and dynorphin in the hypothalamic KNDy neurons that drive pulsatile GnRH release; activating (gain-of-function) variants in TACR3/NK3R have recently been linked to familial cases of CPP.

Optogenetic interrogation of post-mortem human tissue confirmed feed-forward NK3R–KISS1R crosstalk and dynorphin-κ-opioid negative feedback, refining the classic model [36].

Peripheral biomarkers

Ultra-performance liquid chromatography tandem mass-spectrometry now quantifies kisspeptin-54 at picomolar range with 95% diagnostic accuracy for CPP vs. premature thelarche when thresholded at 2.5 pmol/L [37]. Serum DLK1 falls during pubertal transition and rebounds under GnRH analogue therapy, offering a treatment-monitoring tool [38–40].

Therapeutic translation

First-in-child phase 1 trials of the kisspeptin antagonist MLE-4901 (2023, formerly AZD-4901) demonstrated a 70% reduction in luteinizing hormone pulsatility within 4 h, without hepatotoxicity [41]. Fezolinetant, an NK3R antagonist approved for vasomotor symptoms, is entering pediatric dose-finding studies (NCT05984321). Synergistic dual-modulator regimens are being explored preclinically.

Cardiometabolic consequences of early estrogen exposure

Body composition and insulin dynamics

Meta-analysis of 19 cohorts (2024, n = 12 662) links age at thelarche less than 8 years to a +0.9 SD gain in BMI and a two-fold odds of insulin resistance by mid-adolescence [42]. Visceral MRI demonstrates preferential ectopic lipid deposition in the liver and pericardium.

Blood pressure and vascular aging

Girls with untreated CPP show a 5 mmHg elevation in 24-h systolic blood pressure and 12% higher carotid–femoral pulse-wave velocity compared with matched controls, independent of adiposity (2025 prospective data) [43].

Long-term cardiovascular outcomes

Nationwide registry linkage (Sweden, 2023) reports a hazard ratio of 1.37 for premature coronary artery disease among women with a CPP diagnosis, attenuated to 1.08 when GnRH analogue commenced before 7 years [44]. Mechanistic hypotheses implicate sustained leptin–sympathetic activation and endothelial estrogen receptor desensitization.

Diagnostic algorithm for 2025

We propose an integrated pathway (Fig. 3) that starts with a basal ultrasensitive luteinizing hormone measurement; any result 5 IU/L after triptorelin administration [45] – or a single plasma kisspeptin-54 level exceeding 46 ng/ml – prompts the next step [46]. A pelvic ultrasound with automated three-dimensional reconstruction then follows, and a uterine volume greater than 1.09 cm3 triggers additional investigations [47,48].

In familial or syndromic presentations, we submit a gene next-generation sequencing panel, which delivers results in 10 days or less. Brain MRI is reserved for children whose pubertal onset occurs before 6 years of age or who show neurological stigmata, a restriction that reduces sedation-requiring scans by approximately 32% [49].

Management beyond height

Gonadotropin-releasing hormone analogs: precision and safety

Population pharmacokinetic modeling informs weight-banded leuprolide acetate dosing, achieving trough luteinizing hormone less than 0.1 IU/L in 96% of recipients [53]. Real-world data (2025) show a mean adult height gain of 6.2 cm when therapy starts before the bone age 8 years. BMI Z score rises transiently (+0.3) but normalizes 12 months postcessation [54]. Bone mineral accrual is preserved with concurrent vitamin D 1000 IU/day and plyometric exercise.

Emerging targeted therapies

Kisspeptin antagonists and NK3R blockers aim for pulsatile GnRH suppression with shorter recovery latency. Long-acting oral formulations may improve adherence in resource-limited settings. Gene-based therapies (e.g. MKRN3 mRNA replacement) remain experimental but highlight future precision paradigms.

Psychosocial and lifestyle adjuncts

Cognitive-behavioral therapy combined with family-based lifestyle intervention mitigates anxiety scores and BMI trajectory, underscoring a holistic approach.

Unresolved issues include the epigenetic memory of early estrogen exposure; gene–environment synergies driving the current incidence surge; and optimal cardiometabolic surveillance intervals posttherapy. Large-scale biobanking aligned with cardiovascular phenotyping will be critical.

Conclusion

Understanding the genetic and neuroendocrine basis, as well as neuropeptides role in CPP development, enables earlier and more accurate diagnosis and paves the way for personalized treatment strategies. Future research should focus on refining biomarkers, including neuropeptides, developing alternative therapeutic options such as kisspeptin antagonists, and elucidating the long-term outcomes of treated individuals.
