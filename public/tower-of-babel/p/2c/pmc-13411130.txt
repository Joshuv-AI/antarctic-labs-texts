Visual and refractive pattern in patients with comitant large angle esotropia
Authors: Masoud Khorrami-Nejad, Mohammad Reza Akbari, Babak Masoomian, Karrar Karim Saleh, Elham Azizi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate visual acuity, refractive error, and angle of deviation in patients with large-angle esotropia and to investigate age related differences in the magnitude of deviation.MethodIn this retrospective study, medical records of 370 patients diagnosed with large-angle esotropia (horizontal deviation ≥ 50 prism diopters (Δ)) between 4 and 81 years were reviewed. Data collected included demographic information, corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA), cycloplegic refractive error, and angle of deviation in primary position. Amblyopia severity was categorized as mild, moderate, or severe, and its etiology classified based on clinical findings.ResultsThe mean age of patients was 28.9 ± 17.77 years, and 61.4% were male. The mean horizontal angle of deviation at distance was 72.2 ± 14.08Δ. The mean CDVA was 0.54 ± 0.55 logMAR in the strabismic eye and 0.14 ± 0.22 logMAR in the nonstrabismic eye (p < 0.001). The mean spherical equivalent (SE) was not significantly different between the strabismic (-0.31 ± 3.77 diopter (D)) and non-strabismic eye (-0.29 ± 3.46 D) (p = 0.9). Strabismic amblyopia was the most common etiology (62%), followed by mixed anisohyperopic + strabismic (17.6%), anisoastigmatic + strabismic (13.4%) and anisomyopic + strabismic amblyopia (7%). Finally, the magnitude of deviation was greater in the middle aged to old population (41 to 81 years) (79.72 ± 16.9 Δ) than in children (4–18 years) (67.72 ± 10.99 Δ) (p < 0.001).ConclusionLarge-angle esotropia is associated with significant visual impairments, including strabismic amblyopia, as the predominant factor, emphasizing the need for early diagnosis and individualized and timely intervention to prevent the long-term visual deficits.

Introduction

Large-angle esotropia is distinguished by its marked angle of deviation—typically exceeding 50 prism diopters (Δ)—and poses unique therapeutic, and functional challenges [1, 2]. Patients with large-angle esotropia often present with profound binocular vision impairments, reduced stereopsis, and frequently associated amblyopia, particularly in pediatric and untreated adult populations [3–5].

Large-angle esotropia may arise from diverse etiologies, including congenital, developmental, and acquired origins. Despite the etiologic variability, a consistent feature among patients with large-angle esotropia is the increased risk of amblyopia and challenges encountered in achieving optimal visual acuity, particularly in the deviating eye [6]. The prevalence and severity of amblyopia in esotropic patients closely correlates with the age of onset, duration of misalignment, and the degree of interocular visual imbalance [7]. In large-angle esotropia, suppression is often profound, and visual acuity in the non-dominant eye may be severely compromised unless early and aggressive interventions are undertaken [2, 3].

Refractive errors constitute another critical component in the evaluation of esotropia. Hyperopia is frequently observed among patients with esotropia, particularly in accommodative and partially accommodative forms, where uncorrected hyperopia leads to excessive accommodative convergence and consequent esodeviation [8]. However, the refractive profile in patients with large-angle esotropia—especially those not fitting neatly into the category of accommodative esotropia—has not been as extensively characterized in the literature.

In addition to visual acuity and refractive status, the angle of deviation—both horizontal and vertical—plays a central role in clinical decision-making [9]. The quantification of horizontal deviation informs the planning for strabismus surgery, especially in determining the type and extent of muscle recession or resection [10]. Vertical deviations, though typically less prominent in primary esotropia, may indicate underlying oblique muscle dysfunction or dissociated vertical deviation (DVD), which can complicate surgical planning and outcomes [11].

Despite the importance of these parameters, there remains a paucity of large-scale studies that comprehensively analyze the visual and refractive patterns in patients with large-angle esotropia. Most available data are derived from heterogeneous cohorts or are limited by small sample sizes and short follow-up durations. Moreover, few studies have systematically examined how refractive errors, amblyopia severity, and the magnitude of deviation interact in this unique population.

This retrospective study seeks to elucidate the clinical profile of patients with large-angle esotropia, including age distribution, visual acuity, refractive status, amblyopia severity and type and the magnitude of ocular deviation. These findings have the potential to enhance clinical understanding of large-angle esotropia and guide the development of tailored interventions to optimize visual outcomes.

Patients and methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational study was conducted at Farabi Eye Hospital, a tertiary-level ophthalmology center affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences in Tehran, Iran. The study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (ethics number: IR.TUMS.FNM.REC.1405.018) and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Data collection involved a systematic review of electronic medical records stored in the hospital’s centralized database. The study period spanned from January 2015 to December 2024. Given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de-identified data, the requirement for individual patient consent was waived by the IRB.

Participant selection

Medical records of patients with a confirmed diagnosis of large-angle esotropia were screened. Inclusion criteria required a documented horizontal angle of deviation equal to or greater than 50 Δ in the primary position [1, 2], as determined by standard orthoptic techniques. Only patients who had undergone a comprehensive ophthalmological evaluation, including visual acuity assessment, cycloplegic refraction, and ocular alignment measurement, were considered eligible. Patients with incomplete medical records, prior strabismus surgery, or associated neurological or systemic conditions affecting ocular motility were excluded from the study to ensure data integrity and minimize confounding.

A total of 370 patients met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled in the final analysis. Demographic data such as age and sex were recorded, alongside detailed clinical findings related to visual acuity, refractive error, and ocular deviation. All patients had been evaluated by fellowship-trained pediatric ophthalmologists or strabismus specialists.

Clinical assessments

All clinical evaluations were performed using standardized protocols. Cyclo-refraction was conducted by administrating cyclopentolate 1% eye drops and the results were checked after 40 min using an auto-refractometer (TOPCON KR-800) and Heine β-200 retinoscope. Subjective refraction was followed after objective evaluation, where required. Refractive error was documented as spherical power, cylindrical power, and spherical equivalent (SE), separately for each eye. For classification, myopia was defined as an SE of ≤ -0.50 diopters (D), hyperopia as an SE of ≥ + 0.50 D, and emmetropia as an SE falling between − 0.49 D and + 0.49 D. Astigmatism was considered present if the cylindrical power measured ≥ 0.75 D, and anisometropia was defined by a difference in SE between the two eyes of ≥ 1.00 D.

Corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA) was measured monocularly using a Snellen E chart at a distance of 4 m, and the results were converted to logMAR values for statistical analysis using this formula: VA logMAR= -log (VA E−Snellen). In cases where the patient was unable to discern optotypes on the chart, alternative clinical estimations were used and converted to logMAR equivalents. These equivalents were set as follows: counting fingers was assigned a value of 2.0 logMAR, hand motion was recorded as 2.3 logMAR, light perception as 2.6 logMAR, and no light perception (NLP) as 2.9 logMAR, in accordance with established conventions [12].

Measurement of the angle of deviation was conducted using the prism alternate cover test (PACT) at distance fixation (six meters), while fixating to the Snellen chart one line bigger than CDVA, and at near (33 cm), while fixating to a detailed accommodative target. The angle of horizontal and vertical deviation was recorded in prism diopter. Vertical deviations were assessed and documented, although they were not part of the inclusion criteria. Our data included patients with dominancy in one eye, even in cases with alternate esotropia. Therefore data reported as strabismic and non-strabismic eyes for CDVA and refractive error component.

Unilateral amblyopia was diagnosed primarily based on a two-line or greater interocular difference in CDVA and/or CDVA ≥ 0.2 logMAR in the worse eye, in the absence of identifiable organic pathology. Severity of amblyopia was categorized into three groups: mild (CDVA < 0.3 logMAR), moderate (CDVA between 0.30 and 0.6 logMAR), and severe (CDVA > 0.6 logMAR) [13]. The etiology of amblyopia was also determined based on clinical findings and categorized into strabismic amblyopia, and mixed amblyopia (strabismic + anisometropic) which contained anisohyperopic, anisoastigmatic and anisomyopic amblyopia. Each classification was made based on the predominant contributing factor in cases where multiple refractive anomalies were present.

Finally, slit-lamp biomicroscopy and dilated fundus examinations were assessed to confirm the absence of other ocular pathologies that could potentially account for reduced vision or influence ocular alignment. The ocular motility assessment, including ductions and versions, was also reviewed to verify the comitant nature of the esotropia and to exclude the presence of significant incomitance, restrictions, or paretic elements.

Patients with pure alternating esotropia and no ocular dominance were included in the total cohort for age, gender and deviation-angle analyses, but they were not included in eye-based comparisons of CDVA and refractive error, because classification into strabismic and non-strabismic eyes was not clinically applicable on them.

Statistical analysis

All data were entered into a secure, anonymized database and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables. Continuous variables, including age, CDVA, refractive errors, and deviation angles, were expressed as means with standard deviations. Categorical variables, such as sex and amblyopia type, were reported as frequencies and percentages.

Normality of continuous data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. For comparisons among the three amblyopia severity groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for normally distributed data. When data did not meet normality assumptions, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied. For categorical comparisons, the chi-square test was utilized. Figures were generated using MedCalc Statistical Software, version 20 (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium). A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 370 patients with large-angle esotropia were included in this retrospective study. Of these, 227 patients (61.4%) were male, and 143 patients (38.6%) were female. The mean age of the patients was 28.9 ± 17.77 years, ranging from 4 to 81 years.

Of the 370 patients, 220 had ocular dominance and were eligible for eye-based comparison of CDVA and refractive error, while 150 had pure alternating esotropia without ocular dominance and were therefore included only in the total cohort for age and gender and deviation-angle analyses. Among the 220 patients with ocular dominance, 3 patients did not cooperate sufficiently for reliable CDVA measurement; therefore, CDVA analysis was performed in 217 patients. The mean CDVA in the strabismic eye was 0.54 ± 0.55 logMAR, whereas in the non-strabismic eye was 0.14 ± 0.22 logMAR (p < 0.001).

Regarding refractive errors and among 220 patients, there was no statistical difference between strabismic and non-strabismic eye in sphere and spherical equivalent components (p > 0.05); however, in cylindrical component, strabismic eye showed a significant higher cylinder power (1.17 ± 1.21 diopter (D)) in comparison to the non-strabismic eye (0.84 ± 1.01 D) (p < 0.001).

The horizontal angle of deviation in primary position either in near or distance ranged from 52 to 105 Δ, with a mean of 72.2 ± 14.08 Δ at distance and 72.37 ± 14.57 Δ at near in all 370 patients (Fig. 1). The vertical angle of deviation in primary position ranged from 0 to 9 Δ, with a mean of 0.1 ± 0.9 Δ. Strabismic amblyopia was the most common cause, accounting for 88 cases (62%), followed by mixed anisohyperopic+strabismic amblyopia with 26 cases (17.6%), mixed anisoastigmatism+ strabismic amblyopia with 19 cases (13.4%), and mixed anisomyopic+ strabismic amblyopia with 10 cases (7%).

The distribution of the horizontal angle of deviation among all patients is depicted in Fig. 2. In parallel, we conducted a statistical analysis, considering 3 age groups, as children (4–18 years old), young adults (19–40 years old) and middle aged to older population (41–81 years old) and investigated the difference in magnitude of deviation. One way analysis of variance (ANOVA) showed a significant difference between age groups in their angle of deviation, with 67.72 ± 10.99 Δ in children, 71.32 ± 12.6 Δ in young adults and 79.72 ± 16.9 Δ in the middle aged to older population (p < 0.001). Further LSD post hoc analysis also confirmed significant difference between all three groups.

Discussion

The present study examined visual and refractive profiles of patients diagnosed with large-angle esotropia, drawing on data from a substantial cohort of 370 individuals referred to a tertiary eye care center. The mean angle of horizontal deviation at distance was 72.2 ± 14.08 Δ in primary gaze. CDVA, averaged around 0.54 ± 0.55 logMAR in the strabismic eye which was significantly worse than the non-strabismic eye (0.14 ± 0.22 logMAR) (P < 0.001). In addition, strabismic eye showed a significant higher astigmatism than the non-strabismic eye; however, from a clinical perspective, the difference was about 0.33 D which might be negligible. Strabismic amblyopia emerged as the leading cause of visual impairment, accounting for the majority of amblyopic cases, followed by mixed anisohyperopic and strabismic amblyopia. The magnitude of deviation also was significantly larger in middle aged to older population in comparison to the children group.

The mean horizontal angle of deviation reported in our cohort is consistent with previous studies that have characterized large-angle esotropia as deviations typically exceeding 50Δ [1, 2, 14]. Comparable studies have reported similar ranges of deviation in infantile and long-standing esotropia. For example, Archer et al. noted that congenital (infantile) esotropia often presents with horizontal deviations ranging from 40Δ to 80Δ or more [15]. Our findings of deviation angles exceeding 70Δ suggest a population with advanced or untreated esotropia, possibly due to late referral or limited access to early ophthalmic care.

As we observed in this study, 65.4% of our cases had amblyopia, with strabismic amblyopia being the most common form. In our study, most of the cases had severe (47.9%) and moderate (42.25%) amblyopia and less were mild (9.85%). There are contributing factors, influencing the severity of strabismic amblyopia including magnitude and constancy of ocular misalignment [16, 17]. Birch et al. found that the risk of amblyopia increases significantly when esotropia is constant and of large magnitude, particularly when onset occurs during the critical period of visual development [18]. Holmes and Clarke similarly emphasized that early-onset strabismus, especially when untreated, disrupts binocular interaction and leads to persistent amblyopia [19].

Our analysis of refractive status in patients with large-angle esotropia revealed a broad distribution of spherical equivalents, ranging from high myopia to high hyperopia. Notably, according to the classification, most cases were emmetropic. However this contrasts the established associations between hyperopia and esotropia, particularly in cases with a significant accommodative component [20]. Nevertheless, in cases of large-angle esotropia—especially those classified as non-accommodative—the degree of hyperopia tends to be less pronounced [21]. Our data corroborate these observations, thereby supporting a predominantly non-accommodative mechanism in large-angle esotropia. Astigmatism was also prevalent in our cohort, with higher cylinder values in the strabismic eye than the non-strabismic eye; although the difference was about 0.33 D, which might not be clinically meaningful. Previous studies have identified astigmatism as a contributing factor to amblyopia, particularly in cases of anisoastigmatism [22, 23]. However, in patients with large-angle esotropia, astigmatism may act more as a cofactor than a primary cause.

We also observed a significant larger angle of deviation in the middle aged to older population in compare to children. This might be due to receiving earlier treatment strategies in children in compare to the older population which have controlled the angle of deviation somewhat in that age group. Due to the retrospective nature of this study, we were unable to collect this data. Moreover, an age related decrease in the amplitude of accommodation in elderly contributes to the high accommodative convergence to accommodation ratio (AC/A), which can lead to increase in esotropia angle in elderly population and presence of relative divergence insufficiency [24].

The clinical implications of these findings are significant. First, the wide range of refractive errors underscores the need for individualized refractive correction, tailored to each patient’s profile. Second, the predominance of strabismic amblyopia emphasizes the importance of early and aggressive amblyopia treatment, including occlusion therapy and vision rehabilitation in addition to surgery plans. Third, the smaller angle of deviation in children in compare to the middle aged to elderly population, manifests the probability of earlier treatments in children, as well as, increase in AC/A ratio in the elderly due to decrease in accommodation amplitude in this age group.

Despite its strengths—including a large sample size and detailed stratification of amblyopia severity—our study has several limitations. The retrospective design introduces inherent biases, including selection and information bias. While all patients underwent standardized assessments, variations in examiner technique or inter-observer variability cannot be excluded. Additionally, as data were drawn from a single tertiary care center, the findings may not be generalizable to other populations or healthcare settings. In addition, because of the retrospective record-based design, the exact number of excluded cases and the specific reason for exclusion in each case could not be reliably retrieved.

Future research should prioritize prospective longitudinal studies that follow patients from the time of diagnosis through treatment and postoperative outcomes. Such studies should incorporate binocular function assessments, quality-of-life measures, and neurodevelopmental outcomes. Investigating the impact of refractive correction and early surgical timing on visual and sensory outcomes in large-angle esotropia would also be valuable. Moreover, the role of emerging therapies, such as digital vision therapy or neuro-enhancement techniques, warrants exploration in this challenging subgroup.

Conclusions

The present study highlights the distinct visual and refractive characteristics of patients with large-angle esotropia, demonstrating strabismic amblyopia as the predominant cause of visual impairment, with a wide range of refractive errors—particularly astigmatism—observed across the cohort. These findings underscore the critical importance of early detection, individualized refractive correction, and timely surgical intervention in mitigating amblyogenic risk and optimizing visual outcomes. The data presented offer valuable insight into the clinical profile of this specific strabismus subgroup and provide a foundation for future research aimed at improving management strategies and long-term visual prognosis.
