The effect of multimodal educational interventions on improving the lifestyle of the elderly: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Elnaz Ashrafi, Bahare Izadi, Omid Safari, Roghaye Farhadi Hassankiadeh, Morteza Mansourian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe health of the elderly represents a critical public health concern that can be positively influenced by the adoption of health-promoting lifestyle behaviors. This quasi-experimental study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of multimodal educational interventions in improving the lifestyle of older adults.Materials and methodsThis quasi-experimental intervention study was conducted with 118 eligible elderly participants recruited from health service centers in Qom, Iran. Data were collected using a two-part questionnaire comprising demographic information and the Health-Promoting Lifestyle Profile II (HPLP-II). The intervention group participated in eight 30-minute group sessions (organized into six groups), held twice weekly, covering topics such as nutrition, physical activity, stress management, and mental health. The control group did not receive any educational intervention during the study period. Two months following the completion of the educational program, the questionnaire was re-administered to both groups. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 21. Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize participant characteristics and study variables. Inferential analyses included Box’s M test to assess the homogeneity of variance-covariance matrices, as well as univariate and multivariate analyses of covariance (ANCOVA and MANCOVA), with the significance level set at 0.05.FindingsThe mean scores for lifestyle dimensions—including health responsibility, physical activity, nutrition, stress management, interpersonal relationships, spiritual growth, and self-actualization—did not differ significantly between the intervention and control groups prior to the educational program. However, following the intervention, the mean scores in the intervention group increased significantly across all dimensions (p < 0.0001).ConclusionThe findings indicate that the educational intervention had a positive effect on all six dimensions of lifestyle-related behaviors among the elderly.

Introduction

The global population is aging, and it is projected that by 2050, 16% of people worldwide will be aged 65 or older [1]. Concomitant with this demographic shift is an increased prevalence of chronic diseases, such as cardiovascular diseases and diabetes [2]. These conditions can significantly impair normal daily life activities [3]. Furthermore, advancing age is associated with a range of health-related changes, including physical frailty, cognitive decline, psychological disorders such as loneliness, chronic diseases, various cancers, impaired balance, diminished bone density, and polypharmacy. Consequently, older adults are at increased risk of injury and a diminished quality of life [4–6].

Adopting a healthy lifestyle is considered the most effective approach to mitigating age-related challenges and promoting successful aging [7]. A healthy lifestyle encompasses behaviors that promote, maintain, and enhance an individual’s health and well-being [8] while simultaneously reducing the risk of severe illness and premature mortality, thereby enabling a more fulfilling life [9]. A healthy lifestyle encompasses nutritious dietary choices, regular physical activity and recreation, avoidance of tobacco and excessive alcohol consumption, abstention from addictive or narcotic substances, effective stress management and relaxation techniques, and engagement in activities that mitigate exposure to health hazards [8]. Health-promoting lifestyle behaviors encompass six key components: health responsibility, physical activity, nutrition, spiritual growth, interpersonal relationships, and stress management [10]. Health responsibility involves recognizing and actively engaging in behaviors that enhance both personal and public well-being, thereby enabling individuals and communities to adopt and maintain healthy lifestyles [11]. Regular physical activity supports physical and mental well-being, promotes independence in activities of daily living, and contributes to improved psychological health [12]. In older adults, nutrition is vital for preventing and managing common age-related conditions such as cancer, cardiovascular disorders, and cognitive decline [13]. Spirituality forms a core element of a life philosophy. Finding purpose, maintaining hope through faith during hardships, and feeling connected to a higher power are strategies that can enhance coping mechanisms in the face of adversity [14]. Furthermore, maintaining interpersonal relationships is essential for a healthy lifestyle, as individuals constantly interact with others to express their emotions, thoughts, and needs [15]. Finally, stress management involves enhancing psychological resilience, employing effective coping techniques, and constructively managing stressful situations [12]. Therefore, implementing health-promoting lifestyle programs is an essential strategy for enhancing the well-being of older adults and preventing chronic conditions at the population level. Adopting a healthy lifestyle can transform aging, an inevitable stage of life, into an enjoyable opportunity to experience life. In the context of geriatric education, various teaching styles can be employed; however, it is important to recognize that older adults may not be receptive to all pedagogical approaches [16]. Multimodal education refers to the use of multiple modes of presentation, integrating elements such as printed materials, visual images, videos, text, and lectures [17]. A multimodal educational intervention may also incorporate educational posters, small-group teaching sessions, and the use of social media [18]. These modalities reinforce one another, enhancing the dynamism of learning and improving the learners’ overall educational experience [19]. The multimodal approach encompasses a combination of diverse educational methods to address different facets of health promotion, equipping older adults with practical knowledge and skills tailored to their specific needs. These programs not only mitigate the challenges and costs associated with age-related disease burden but also improve quality of life, enabling older adults to remain active and engaged within their communities. In light of these potential benefits, the present quasi-experimental study was conducted to determine the effect of multimodal educational interventions on improving the lifestyles of older adults.

Materials and methods

Study design

This quasi-experimental controlled interventional study was conducted in 2024 among elderly individuals attending comprehensive health service centers in Qom, Iran.

Study participants and sampling

Inclusion criteria were: age 60 years or older; absence of mental or psychological disorders and intact awareness of time and place (assessed via review of health records); ability to communicate verbally; absence of hearing impairments; and willingness to participate in the study. Exclusion criteria included acute physical illness, inability to cooperate with the research procedures, and missing more than one training session.

Sampling was conducted using a random cluster sampling method. Initially, a list of all comprehensive health service centers in Qom, Iran, was compiled, with each center considered a cluster. Four centers were then randomly selected from this list. Subsequently, using simple random assignment, two of the selected centers were allocated to the intervention group, and the remaining two to the control group. Finally, from the health records of older adults registered at the selected centers, 59 participants were randomly selected for each group (intervention and control).

Data collection tool

A

A demographic questionnaire, encompassing age, occupation, education level, marital status, and medical history, is fundamental for characterizing the older adult population. Numerous studies have underscored the importance of assessing these demographic variables, as they significantly influence lifestyle patterns among older adults. Consequently, understanding these factors is critical for designing effective and targeted interventions to improve lifestyle behaviors and health outcomes in this population [20, 21].

B

The Health Promoting Lifestyle Profile II (HPLP-II) questionnaire, developed by Walker based on Pender’s health promotion model in 1995 [22]. Provides a multi-dimensional assessment of health-promoting behaviors across six dimensions: health responsibility, physical activity, nutrition, spiritual growth and personal self-actualization, stress management, and interpersonal relations. This instrument comprises 52 items, each answered on a 4-point Likert scale: (1) Never, (2) Sometimes, (3) Usually, (4) Always. Individual item scores range from 1 to 4, yielding a total score range for health-promoting behaviors of 52 to 208. Separate scores are also calculated for each dimension, with the following ranges: spiritual growth and personal self-actualization, health responsibility, and interpersonal relations (9–36); stress management (8–32); physical activity (7–28); and nutrition (10–40). In both individual dimensions and the overall questionnaire score, higher values indicate more frequent engagement in healthy behaviors and a healthier lifestyle. The Cronbach’s alpha for the total questionnaire has been reported as 0.94, with subscale values ranging from 0.79 to 0.87 [23, 24].

Educational program

Prior to commencing the training program, participants received comprehensive information regarding the study’s process, purpose, and the assurance of data confidentiality. Subsequently, the baseline questionnaire was administered to all participants prior to the educational intervention. The training content was developed in accordance with the study objectives, and its validity was confirmed by a panel of five experts in the field of gerontology. The primary source of the training content was the three-volume “Education of Healthy Lifestyle” series published by the Ministry of Health, Treatment, and Medical Education. This material was presented to the intervention group by trained facilitators consisting of a mental health expert, a nutritionist, and a health care provider. The educational intervention comprised eight group training sessions, each lasting 30 min, conducted with six groups of older adults. Two training sessions were held each week. A detailed overview of the content, educational methods, and suggested activities for each session is presented in Table 1. The multimodal educational interventions incorporated the following elements: (1) for nutritional sessions: lectures combined with question-and-answer sessions that included recording daily food intake; (2) for physical activity sessions: lectures, question-and-answer sessions, and practical demonstrations involving group walks outdoors; (3) for stress management sessions: lectures, group discussions, brainstorming, and practical demonstrations of relaxation exercises to be performed at home; and (4) for mental health sessions: lectures, question-and-answer sessions, group discussions, and group artistic activities such as flower planting and gardening. During this period, the control group received no training. Two months following the completion of the training program, the questionnaire was re-administered to both groups. The data obtained were analyzed using SPSS version 21 software. Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize the data and provide an overview of the study variables. Inferential analyses included Box’s M test to assess the homogeneity of variance-covariance matrices, as well as univariate and multivariate analyses of covariance (ANCOVA and MANCOVA), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Research Ethics Committee of the Iran University of Medical Sciences (Approval ID: IR.IUMS.REC.1403.482). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. As this study employed a quasi-experimental design and did not constitute a clinical trial, trial registration was deemed not applicable. Clinical trial registration number: Not applicable.

Table 5 presents the results of four multivariate tests—Pillai’s Trace, Wilks’ Lambda, Hotelling’s Trace, and Roy’s Largest Root—all of which indicated a statistically significant difference between the intervention and control groups (p < 0.001). To further examine the individual effects on each outcome variable, a univariate analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was conducted, controlling for baseline differences (pre-test scores). As shown in Table 6, the results revealed a statistically significant difference in post-test mean scores for health-promoting lifestyle between the intervention and control groups (p < 0.001), indicating that the intervention was effective in improving participants’ health-promoting lifestyle behaviors.

Discussion

Ideally, the establishment of healthy behaviors and a sound lifestyle should begin in childhood and adolescence. This study investigated the effects of an educational intervention on six dimensions of lifestyle-related behaviors: health responsibility, physical activity, nutrition, stress management, interpersonal relations, spiritual growth, and self-actualization.

Education on a health-promoting lifestyle improved health responsibility behaviors among older adults in the intervention group, which aligns with findings from similar studies [25–27]. Increased awareness among older adults regarding their role in maintaining and improving their health status significantly enhances their health responsibility behaviors. Specifically, enhanced knowledge about their physical, psychological, and nutritional needs empowers older adults to adapt more effectively to the challenges of aging. This understanding facilitates the adoption of preventive and self-care behaviors, enabling them to take a more active role in managing their health, thereby promoting greater independence and an improved quality of life [28]. The findings of this study demonstrated that the training program had a positive and significant effect on mean scores for nutrition, physical activity, and stress management in the intervention group. Conversely, no significant changes were observed in the control group, which aligns with findings from similar studies [28–32]. Familiarity with daily nutritional needs, the main food groups, recommendations for limiting salt, fatty foods, and fried foods, and healthy weight management in older adulthood are key topics where training can significantly promote healthy eating behaviors. Given the physiological changes and potential physical limitations associated with aging, maintaining physical activity can be particularly challenging. Emphasizing the importance of physical activity, regular walking, and appropriate exercises is essential in educational interventions aimed at promoting physical activity among older adults, contributing to healthier aging. Furthermore, educational programs focusing on stress-coping methods and relaxation techniques play a crucial role in promoting mental well-being in older adults. In this study, the inclusion of these methods led to an increased mean score for stress management in the intervention group [33].

The mean score for interpersonal relations among older adults in the intervention group exhibited a significant increase following the implementation of the training program. The positive association between social support and a health-promoting lifestyle has been demonstrated in numerous studies [34–36]. Compared to other life stages, older adults often experience heightened social isolation, primarily as a consequence of retirement, diminished contact with colleagues and friends, and the loss of loved ones. Programs designed to enhance social participation among the elderly play a significant role in mitigating this isolation [37]. Group educational sessions appear to offer a valuable opportunity for older adults to articulate concerns related to grief, physical limitations, life challenges, and illnesses. Concurrently, these sessions facilitate the acquisition of communication skills, thereby promoting improved adaptation to their circumstances.

In this study, a significant increase was observed in the average scores of spiritual growth and self-actualization, both dimensions of a healthy lifestyle. This finding aligns with previous research. Specifically, it is consistent with studies reporting positive effects of educational interventions on health-related behavior and quality of life in diabetic patients [38], as well as those emphasizing the role of successful aging programs in promoting health behaviors among the elderly [39], Furthermore, research has consistently shown that structured educational interventions can enhance multiple aspects of a health-promoting lifestyle, including spiritual growth [40].

Moreover, spirituality has been highlighted as a key determinant of successful aging among older adults [41]. Given that spiritual inclination often intensifies with age—influenced by cultural and religious backgrounds—this dimension plays a significant role in elderly health. Other research has also confirmed that strengthening one’s spiritual and religious beliefs can yield beneficial effects on both physical and mental well-being [42].

Among the limitations of the present study, we can mention conducting the study in a limited sample of elderly individuals referring to health centers, and it is suggested that studies be conducted on a larger scale and in elderly centers or at homes. Another limitation was the follow-up of the elderly to implement the post-test, which was resolved by telephone follow-up to participate in the post-test.

Conclusion

The results of the present study showed that educational intervention can affect lifestyle dimensions including responsibility, nutrition, physical activity, stress management, interpersonal relationships, spiritual growth, and personal self-actualization. Therefore, the implementation of multimodal educational programs to promote health-related behaviors of the elderly should be a priority in the programs of government officials and government policies in order to reduce the economic burden of diseases on individuals, families, and communities.
