Evaluation of four insulin resistance surrogate indicators for predicting short-term mortality in critically ill patients with ischemic stroke: a nationwide retrospective cohort study
Authors: Qingrong Ouyang, Chenqi Zhang, Hui Zhang, Taoying Xiong, Yangmei Chen, Peng Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInsulin resistance (IR) is closely linked to the incidence and adverse outcomes of acute ischemic stroke (IS). However, the prognostic value of various surrogate IR indices remains inconsistent across studies. This study aimed to compare the associations and discriminative abilities of four surrogate IR indices—the triglyceride-glucose index (TyG), TyG-body mass index (TyG-BMI), atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), and metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR)—in relation to 28-day in-hospital mortality among critically ill patients with IS.Methods This study utilized data from the eICU database to investigate critically ill patients with IS. Associations between the four IR indices and short-term mortality were assessed using multivariate regression and Cox regression combined with restricted cubic spline modeling (RCS). Additionally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted to evaluate the discriminative performance of these surrogate IR indices. Stratified analyses were performed to identify potential interactions among demographic variables.ResultsA total of 1346 critically ill patients with IS (52.60% female) were enrolled in the study. The overall 28-day mortality rate was 8.77%, with no significant differences between genders. Multivariate regression analysis indicated that both the TyG and the METS.IR were independently associated with increased mortality, with Hazard Ratios (HR) of 1.44 and 1.41, respectively. Dose–response analyses revealed linear associations of TyG and AIP with short-term mortality risk, whereas TyG-BMI and METS-IR exhibited nonlinear relationships with mortality risk that plateaued at higher values. ROC analysis demonstrated that the TyG index had relatively superior discriminative performance among the four IR surrogate indicators in the overall population, male patients, and those over 60 years of age. In contrast, AIP (AUC = 0.68) showed stronger discriminative ability in patients under 60 years compared to the other indices. Subgroup analyses further confirmed significant associations of TyG with mortality in males (HR = 1.49, 95% CI 1.17–1.90; p = 0.0013) and of AIP in patients under 60 years (HR = 1.58, 95% CI 1.07–2.32; p = 0.0204). No significant interactions were observed across sex or age subgroups for the four IR surrogate indices.ConclusionsFour surrogate indices of IR were positively associated with short-term mortality risk in critically ill patients with IS. Among these indices, TyG and METS-IR demonstrated relatively stronger risk effects, while the AIP exhibited superior effect size and discriminative ability in patients under 60 years of age. In contrast, TyG-BMI displayed comparatively weaker performance both in the overall population and across subgroups. These findings address a critical gap in understanding the comparative utility of surrogate IR indices for assessing short-term mortality risk in severe IS. Routine evaluation of these indices may enhance clinical assessment and inform individualized patient management across different subgroups. Furthermore, incorporating robust indicators such as TyG into clinical risk assessment models could enhance the precision of mortality predictions and facilitate optimized patient management strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-025-02956-6.Keywords: Insulin resistance (IR), Acute ischemic stroke (IS), Triglyceride-glucose index (TyG), Atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), Metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS.IR), Short-term mortality

Introduction

Acute ischemic stroke (IS) is caused by sudden arterial occlusion, which disrupts blood flow to the brain, leading to oxygen and nutrient deprivation and subsequent brain tissue damage. As the predominant subtype of acute cerebrovascular disease, it accounts for approximately 70–80% of all stroke cases globally, with the proportion increasing to as high as 85% in low-income countries [1]. Moreover, the contribution of stroke to the global disease burden has exhibited a significant increasing trend [2]. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study, approximately 12.2 million new cases of stroke were reported in 2019, resulting in 6.6 million deaths [3]. Stroke has become the second leading cause of death and the third leading cause of disability worldwide [4]. Based on recent global incidence prediction reports, the standardized incidence rate of IS is projected to rise to 89.32 per 100,000 population by 2030. Notably, there is an emerging concern regarding the rising incidence of IS among younger adults, with an annual growth rate of 12.7% observed in individuals aged 45 to 60 years [4]. Additionally, the global death toll from IS increased from 2.04 million in 1990 to 3.29 million in 2019, and is projected to reach 4.9 million by 2030 [2]. This trend aligns with the ongoing demographic shift towards an aging population and the increasing prevalence of Metabolic Syndrome, which affects approximately 20% to 30% of adults worldwide [5]. Concurrently, the proportion of elderly patients with severe IS continues to rise, further contributing to the overall increase in mortality, with in-hospital mortality rates for severe cases reaching 20–30% [6]. In recent years, accumulating evidence has indicated the pivotal role of metabolic abnormalities—particularly IR, in the pathogenesis, prognosis, and mortality of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases [7–9]. As a central component of metabolic syndrome, IR is not only a precursor to type 2 diabetes but also an independent risk factor for atherosclerosis [10], hypertension [11], and other vascular disorders [12]. It is intimately associated with dysregulated glucose and lipid metabolism, as well as heightened inflammatory responses, collectively accelerating vascular damage and worsening clinical outcomes [13–15]. Therefore, understanding the role of IR in severe IS is crucial for identifying reliable surrogate markers that can improve early risk stratification and guide targeted therapeutic interventions.

IR is a key pathophysiological mechanism underlying metabolic syndrome, with multifactorial effects that facilitate the development and progression of IS [16]. Molecular study indicates that, under IR conditions, chronic hyperinsulinemia can activate the NADPH oxidase system, leading to endothelial dysfunction, characterized by a 40–60% reduction in endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) activity [17]. Additionally, hyperinsulinemia simultaneously upregulates plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1) expression, with plasma levels increasing 2 to threefold [18]. These alterations collectively create a pro-thrombotic environment, increasing the risk of vascular occlusion. Clinical cohort studies further validated the association between IR and cerebrovascular risk, showing that individuals with IR have an increased risk of stroke and adverse functional outcomes after stroke(mRS ≥ 3) [19, 20]. Currently, the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp (HIEC) is widely regarded as the gold standard for assessing insulin sensitivity and resistance. However, its widespread clinical application is limited by invasiveness, technical complexity, and high cost [21]. In this context, reliance on routine biochemical parameters for developing surrogate markers has received increasing attention. The TyG index, calculated from fasting triglycerides and fasting blood glucose, has demonstrated a strong correlation with HIEC results [22, 23]. A systematic review further reported that the TyG index has a sensitivity of 96% and a specificity of 99% in diagnosing IR, when compared to the HIEC [24]. To improve the clinical utility of such surrogate markers, combining parameters such as TyG with obesity indices (TyG-BMI) can reflect both insulin sensitivity and adiposity more comprehensively [25]. In addition to the TyG series, other alternative indicators—such as Lipid Accumulation Product (LAP) [25], METS-IR [26], and AIP have also been developed to evaluate IR status [27]. These easily obtainable indicators offer a practical, scientifically validated approach to evaluating IR.

Currently, multiple studies have established a significant association between IR surrogate indices and the incidence, prognosis, and mortality of IS. A retrospective cohort study involving patients with mild IS revealed that the TyG index was significantly correlated with higher National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) scores and the risk of neurological deterioration within 7 days [28]. Another systematic review and meta-analysis further confirmed that elevated TyG indices are associated with a higher risk of IS (OR = 1.37), as well as increased risks of stroke recurrence and mortality (OR = 1.50; and OR = 1.40; respectively) [29]. Moreover, a recent study utilizing the MIMIC-IV 3.0 database found that higher TyG levels were significantly associated with an increased mortality risk across all time points, whereas lower TyG-BMI levels were linked to an elevated risk of long-term all-cause mortality [30]. METS-IR has also been shown to be significantly associated with an increased risk of acute IS and early neurological deterioration [20, 31]. In addition, a longitudinal study based on Chinese CHARLS data revealed a significant positive correlation between the AIP and stroke risk [32]. Another study highlighted that AIP serves as a significant and independent predictor of poor functional outcomes at 3 months in patients with acute IS, particularly among those with large artery atherosclerosis subtype stroke [33].

However, existing research on short-term mortality risk in patients with severe IS remains limited. Most previous studies excluded critically ill patients with Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores ≤ 12, lacked comprehensive multivariate adjustment models, and underexplored the roles of METS-IR and AIP in predicting short-term mortality among critically ill IS populations. Furthermore, no studies have directly compared the association effects and discriminative ability of these indices with respect to short-term mortality. To address these research gaps, our study employed a retrospective cohort design involving 3,244 patients extracted from the eICU Collaborative Research Database. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the association effects and discriminative capacity of four IR surrogate indices for predicting 28-day all-cause mortality in critically ill IS patients. The findings will provide an evidence-based foundation for personalized metabolic management in critically ill IS and facilitate the development of more precise, targeted treatment strategies.

Methods

Source of data and research design

This retrospective multicenter cohort study employed the publicly available eICU Collaborative Research Database, which encompasses intensive care unit (ICU) data from over 200 hospitals across the United States, collected between 2014 and 2015 [34]. The dataset comprises a comprehensive array of information obtained from various intensive care units, including patient demographics, vital signs, nursing records, diagnostic details, treatment interventions, laboratory indicators, therapeutic regimens, and clinical outcomes. All procedures were conducted in strict adherence to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Patient confidentiality was ensured through the de-identification of all data, in compliance with HIPAA privacy regulations.

Study population

Following rigorous screening procedures, a total of 1346 eligible patients were included in this retrospective cohort study. The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) hospitalizations with a length of stay < 24 h; (2) multiple ICU admissions during the index hospitalization; (3) undocumented discharge disposition status; (4) the missing values of critical laboratory and anthropometric measurements, such as blood glucose, triglycerides, body mass index (BMI), and HDL-C. Patient screening flowchart

Data collection and processing

Variables were extracted from the eICU-CRD v2.0 database. All data were subsequently standardized. When the proportion of missing values for continuous covariates exceeded 1%, these values were treated as dummy variables, while missing values for categorical variables were analyzed as a separate group. In addition, multiple imputation by chained equations was performed to generate five imputed datasets to validate the results obtained using the missing-indicator method. The included variables were categorized as follows: Demographic characteristics and vital signs: age, gender, ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), respiratory rate, heart rate, mean arterial pressure (MAP), and the use of mechanical ventilation. Comorbidities: including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), congestive heart failure (CHF), acute myocardial infarction (AMI), diabetes mellitus (DM), and pneumonia. Laboratory indicators: glucose, serum creatinine, serum potassium, sodium, total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), mean platelet volume, and red blood cell (RBC) count. Scoring systems: including the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) IV score, and the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score. Primary endpoint: 28-day in-hospital mortality.

Calculation of the four IR substitution indices

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD. Skewed or non-normal variables were analyzed with the Kruskal–Wallis test and expressed as medians with IQR. Categorical variables are shown as counts and percentages, with comparisons using Fisher’s exact or chi-square tests. To assess the association between four surrogate IR indices and 28-day in-hospital mortality, three multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were developed. Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, and ethnicity; Model 3 further controlled for COPD, CHF, AMI, pneumonia, serum creatinine, total cholesterol, blood urea nitrogen, serum potassium, sodium levels, platelet count, and RBC count, SOFA score; GCS score; Before regression and subgroup analyses, all four surrogate IR indices were standardized using Z-score normalization. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) was used to explore the dose–response relationship between four surrogate IR indices and 28-day in-hospital mortality. Discrimination for short-term mortality was evaluated using ROC curve analysis, which calculated the AUC, optimal cut-off values, sensitivity, and specificity for each index. Stratified analyses tested the robustness of the findings, with results summarized in forest plots. All statistical analyses were conducted using EmpowerStats (http://www.empowerstats.com) and R software, with significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

A total of 1346 critically ill patients with IS were enrolled in the study, including 638 males (47.40%) and 708 females (52.60%). The mean age was significantly higher in males (69.82 ± 14.94 years) compared to females (67.01 ± 13.28 years) (p < 0.001). Several baseline characteristics exhibited significant gender differences, including age, SOFA score, APACHE IV score, GCS score, serum creatinine, sodium levels, Total cholesterol, and hematological parameters. Conversely, variables such as BMI, ethnicity, DM, and mechanical ventilation use showed no significant differences. Among the four surrogate indices of IR, TYG and TYG-BMI did not differ significantly between genders. However, the median AIP was significantly lower in males (0.36) compared to females (0.46) (p < 0.001). Additionally, males exhibited a lower mean METS-IR score (45.32 ± 14.66) compared to females (47.53 ± 17.82) (p < 0.001). The overall 28-day in-hospital mortality rate was 8.77%, with no significant difference observed between males and females (Table 1).

Univariate and multivariate analyses of the four surrogate IR indices for 28-day in-hospital mortality

The univariate analysis reveals significant associations between several baseline characteristics and 28-day hospital mortality, including respiratory rate, heart rate, SOFA score, APACHE IV score, use of mechanical ventilation, presence of pneumonia, DM, glucose levels, serum creatinine, HDL-C, TyG, TyG-BMI, AIP, and METS-IR. In contrast, variables such as gender, ethnicity, COPD, CHF, AMI, total cholesterol, triglycerides, and routine hematological parameters did not show significant associations with mortality (Supplementary Table 1). Each surrogate index was evaluated using both the raw values and the z-score standardized values. Additionally, regression coefficients were pooled after z-score standardization, combined with multiple imputation for missing covariates. Results from regression models using raw values are provided in Supplementary Table 2, while those based on z-score standardized and multiply imputed data are shown in Supplementary Table 3.

This section reports the results of multiple regression models performed on z-score standardized raw data (Table 2). Both the missing-indicator method and multiple imputation yielded consistent effect directions and comparable effect sizes. Across all models, TyG and METS-IR consistently demonstrated strong associations with elevated 28-day hospital mortality. In the fully adjusted model, TyG exhibited a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.44 (95% CI 1.14–1.81, p = 0.002), and METS-IR showed an HR of 1.41 (95% CI 1.07–1.86, p = 0.016). Both TyG and METS-IR exhibited stable correlations with 28-day hospital mortality, with only minor variations observed across models. In contrast, although TyG-BMI and AIP demonstrated significant hazardous effects in partially adjusted models (adjusted for age, gender, and ethnicity), these associations were no longer statistically significant in the fully adjusted model. Specifically, TyG-BMI yielded a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.27 (95% CI 1.00–1.62; p = 0.054), while AIP had an HR of 1.20 (95% CI 0.94–1.53; p = 0.140).

Dose–response relationships between IR surrogate indices and 28-day in-hospital mortality

Restricted cubic splines (RCS) combined with Cox proportional hazards models were employed to examine the dose–response relationship between four IR surrogate indexes and 28-day hospital mortality. Extreme outliers were excluded, defined as follows: AIP > 1.6 (n = 1), METS-IR > 200 (n = 1), TyG-BMI > 470 (n = 12), and TyG ≥ 12 (n = 1). Each indicator’s analysis was adjusted for confounding variables in the same manner as in Model 3. Dose–response patterns across the four indicators were observed using Z-score standardization (Fig. 2), with the relationships for the raw values presented in Supplementary Fig. 1. Notably, the overall trends identified by both methods were consistent. The TYG and the AIP exhibited linear relationships, with increasing levels associated with a corresponding rise in 28-day mortality risk among critically ill IS patients. Conversely, the TYG-BMI and METS-IR exhibited nonlinear associations. Specifically, when METS-IR.Z-score was < −0.143, the mortality risk increased significantly with higher METS-IR levels; beyond this threshold, the risk plateaued with no further significant increase. Similarly, when TYG-BMI.Z-score was < −0.146, higher levels were associated with increased mortality risk, but beyond this point, the risk remained stabilized.

ROC curve analysis of four IR surrogate indices in the overall cohort and subgroups by sex and age

ROC analysis of the four IR surrogate indices revealed variations in their predictive performance for 28-day hospital mortality across different sex and age subgroups (Table 3; Figs. 3, 4). In the total population, the TyG index demonstrated comparatively superior discriminative ability among the four indices, with an AUC of 0.61 (95% CI 0.55–0.66). This advantage was more pronounced in males, where the TyG index achieved an AUC of 0.65 (95% CI 0.57–0.73). In contrast, all indices exhibited lower predictive performance in females, with the TyG-BMI index showing the lowest AUC value (0.53). Among participants under 60 years of age, the AIP index yielded the highest AUC of 0.68 (95% CI 0.56–0.79) among the compared indices. In the older adult subgroup (≥ 60 years), the TyG index (AUC = 0.61, 95% CI 0.55–0.67) marginally outperformed both TYG-BMI (AUC = 0.59, 95% CI 0.53–0.65) and METS-IR (AUC = 0.59, 95% CI 0.53–0.65).

Subgroup analyses of the relationship between four IR surrogate indices and 28-day in-hospital mortality

Stratified analyses were performed to further explore the associations between the four standardized IR surrogate indices and 28-day hospital mortality across different sex and age subgroups (Fig. 5). In the male population, the TYG demonstrated the strongest association with the risk of 28-day in-hospital mortality (HR = 1.49; 95% CI 1.17–1.90; p = 0.0013), while the other three indices also showed statistically significant positive associations. Among females, the HRs for all four indices were lower compared to males, and none reached statistical significance (all p > 0.05). The performance of the indices varied across age groups. In participants younger than 60 years, the AIP exhibited the highest hazard ratio (HR = 1.58; 95% CI 1.07–2.32; p = 0.0204), indicating a stronger association than that of the TYG (HR = 1.33; 95% CI 0.97–1.82; p = 0.0741)in this subgroup. Among participants aged 60 years or older, both the METS-IR (HR = 1.34; 95% CI 1.06–1.69; p = 0.0156) and TYG (HR = 1.33; 95% CI 1.10–1.60; p = 0.0033) indices yielded significant and comparable risk estimates, which were slightly higher than that of TYG-BMI (HR = 1.29; 95% CI 1.05–1.58; p = 0.0163). In this age group, the AIP did not demonstrate statistical significance (HR = 1.14; 95% CI 0.92–1.40; p = 0.2209).

Subgroup analyses of IR surrogate indices and 28-day in-hospital mortality

Discussion

This study provides a novel and comprehensive evaluation of four surrogate indices of IR in relation to 28-day in-hospital mortality among critically ill patients with IS. Our findings indicate that the TyG and METS-IR are independently associated with an increased short-term mortality risk. RCS analyses revealed that TyG and AIP exhibited approximately linear relationships with mortality, suggesting that risk escalates steadily with higher index values. In contrast, TyG-BMI and METS-IR exhibited nonlinear associations with mortality, characterized by a threshold effect beyond which the mortality risk plateaued. Among these indices, TyG exhibited superior discriminative performance overall, as well as in males and individuals over 60 years of age. Although AIP was not significantly associated with mortality in the overall population, it demonstrated stronger effect sizes and improved discriminative ability in patients under 60 years, while TyG-BMI consistently showed weaker associations and discriminative performance across all subgroups. Notably, the AUC values for all four IR indices fell below 0.8, which, according to established academic standards [35], indicates limited accuracy for standalone clinical prediction. Nevertheless, their significant associations with mortality establish these indices as important risk factors, suggesting potential utility as valuable components within integrated risk prediction models. Incorporating a prioritized marker such as the TyG index into composite prognostic tools may enhance overall predictive performance and clinical applicability.

Currently, commonly used surrogate indices of IR can be categorized into the following types: 1. The TyG index and its derived indices such as TyG-BMI, triglyceride glucose-waist circumference (TyG-WC), and Triglyceride glucose-waist to height ratio (TyG-WHtR); 2. Lipid ratio-based indices, including AIP and TG/HDL-C; 3. Indices based on obesity parameters, such as LAP, Visceral Adiposity Index (VAI) [36], and Chinese Visceral Adiposity Index (CVAI) [37]; 4. Other emerging indices, such as the Estimated Glucose Disposal Rate (eGDR) [38], which estimates insulin sensitivity based on clinical parameters. Most of the above indices have been studied in the context of IS, primarily in relation to stroke risk and functional outcome assessment. The TyG index is widely recognized as the preferred surrogate marker for IR in clinical practice due to its simplicity and extensive validation [21, 39]. However, with advances in research and the development of new IR indices, some emerging markers have also demonstrated predictive value that is comparable to or even superior to the TyG index. For example, a longitudinal study based on CHARLS data showed that the TyG index combined with obesity metrics (such as WC, WHtR, BMI) is significantly associated with stroke risk and enhances predictive accuracy compared to the single TyG index. Notably, the TyG-WC combination exhibited the highest predictive ability for stroke (AUC = 0.696) [40]. Another study calculated four cumulative IR indices (Cum-TyG, Cum-MetS-IR, Cum-eGDR, and Cum-TG/HDL-C) by incorporating time variables, revealing that Cum eGDR (AUC = 0.670) markedly outperformed other common IR surrogate indices in predicting stroke risk among middle-aged and older populations [41]. Recently, a study explored six IR surrogate indices (eGDR, CVAI, TyG, TyG-BMI, METS-IR, and AIP) in a middle-aged and elderly cohort with metabolic abnormalities, finding that these indices were closely associated with increased stroke risk, with eGDR (AUC: 0.612) displaying particular potential in risk prediction [42]. Notably, all three aforementioned studies sourced their data from the CHARLS database. Additionally, a study based on a rural Chinese cohort investigated the associations between a series of visceral adiposity indices, including VAI, CVAI, LAP, TYG, TyG-BMI, TyG-WC and incident stroke risk, finding that CVAI achieved an AUC of 0.674, which was significantly higher than the other indices, indicating its superior predictive performance [43]. In terms of functional prognosis, a study identified the TyG-BMI, TyG index, TG/HDL-C ratio, and METS-IR as independent risk factors for early neurological deterioration (END) in both elderly and middle-aged patients with acute IS. Furthermore, the AUC values for the TyG-BMI, TyG index, TG/HDL-C ratio, and METS-IR were 0.736, 0.694, 0.684, and 0.722 (95% CI 0.697–0.747; P < 0.001), respectively, indicating the predictive ability of these four IR indices for END [44]. However, present research on various IR indices in IS primarily focuses on risk assessment, lacking exploration of their prognostic value for functional outcomes and mortality. Moreover, differences in study designs and the adjustment variables considered contribute to variability across studies.

Our study offers a comprehensive assessment of four IR surrogate indices and their association with 28-day in-hospital mortality in critically ill patients with IS. Due to the absence of waist circumference data in the eICU database, we could not compare the predictive efficacy of the VAI, LAP, and eGDR within this cohort. In our study, subgroup analysis revealed a significantly stronger association between the four IR indices and 28-day mortality in male than in female patients. This disparity may stem from sex-specific physiological differences. Males are more prone to visceral obesity [45], which promotes the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and free fatty acids [46], exacerbating hepatic IR and atherogenic dyslipidemia [47] and making IR indices more reflective of core metabolic dysfunction [48]. In contrast, estrogen in females confers cardiometabolic protection through improved endothelial function [49], insulin sensitivity [50], and lipid metabolism [51], potentially attenuating mortality risk associated with IR. Thus, while these indices directly capture mortality-related pathophysiology in males, female mortality is influenced by a wider range of mechanisms, such as fat distribution, hormonal effects, and immune responses, which weakens the association with IR alone. Among the four IR surrogate indices, the TyG index demonstrated relatively stronger risk effects and greater discriminative capability and stability in the overall population, as well as across various sex and age subgroups, as supported by the comprehensive analysis of the area under the curve (AUC), hazard ratios (HR), and their confidence intervals. This finding contrasts with a prior study that evaluated the predictive value of similar IR surrogates for stroke risk, in which, using fully adjusted multivariate regression models, TyG exhibited the highest odds ratio, while AUC comparisons indicated that TyG-BMI and METS-IR slightly outperformed TyG [42]. These discrepancies highlight that the same IR surrogate indices vary in their predictive performance across different populations and outcomes. In comparison, while the AIP showed no significant association with 28-day mortality in the overall population, it exhibited the strongest risk association (HR = 1.58, P = 0.0204) and discriminative ability (AUC = 0.68) among the four indices in patients under 60 years, indicating its age-dependent predictive value. This discrepancy may be attributed to the fact that younger patients generally have fewer comorbidities, greater physiological reserve, and fewer competing risk factors. As a result, metabolic abnormalities such as IR and atherogenic dyslipidemia are more likely to become primary drivers of adverse outcomes in this population. whereas in older adults, non-metabolic factors, including frailty, multimorbidity, immunosenescence, cognitive impairment, and reduced physiological reserve dominate mortality risk, thereby diminishing the prognostic utility of metabolically focused indices like AIP. Furthermore, dose–response analyses revealed significant associations between these IR indices and short-term mortality risk across their entire ranges. The TyG index and AIP exhibited strong linear correlations with severe IS, whereas TyG-BMI and METS-IR demonstrated nonlinear patterns characterized by a plateau in mortality risk at higher values, a phenomenon consistent with the “obesity paradox.” This paradox describes the counterintuitive finding that overweight or mildly obese patients with certain chronic or critical illnesses may exhibit lower mortality risk compared to those with normal weight [52, 53]. This pattern may be explained by the dual influence of metabolic dysfunction and nutritional reserve: at lower values, increasing levels of these indices reflect worsening glycolipid dysregulation, which elevates mortality risk; however, at higher values, the accompanying elevation in BMI may provide protective effects via enhanced energy and nutrient reserves, along with anti-inflammatory buffering. This protective mechanism may involve the utilization of circulating lipids as an alternative energy source and the attenuation of cytokine-induced damage. Consequently, this attenuates further risk escalation and results in a risk plateau. Although high metabolic risk remains clinically important, patients with similarly elevated metabolic risk but higher BMI, which likely suggests better nutritional status, tend to have more favorable outcomes compared to those with lower BMI, a condition often associated with malnutrition or sarcopenia. These findings highlight the complex interaction between metabolic dysfunction and body composition characteristics reflected by higher BMI in critically ill patients with IS, suggesting that the nutritional and energy reserve status represented by BMI may play an important role in the prognosis of critically ill patients with IS. However, the four IR surrogate markers exhibited a gradual, linear positive association with the risk of stroke [42], highlighting the importance of context-specific evaluation in their clinical interpretation [54, 55].

The major strength of this study lies in its rigorous methodology, which incorporated multivariate regression analysis, RCS, ROC analysis, and stratified analyses to systematically evaluate the association between four surrogate markers of IR and 28-day hospital mortality in critically ill IS patients. This comprehensive analytical strategy enhances the robustness and validity of our findings. Furthermore, the simultaneous use of four established IR surrogate markers helps reduce heterogeneity arising from variations across study populations and designs when assessing mortality in critically ill IS patients. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study is based on a retrospective design, and causal inferences warrant further validation through prospective research. Second, the eICU database lacks waist circumference data, which limits the availability of relevant information and prevents comprehensive comparisons with other established surrogate indices of IR, such as eGDR, VAI, and LAP. Third, as this multicenter study used a US cohort from 2014–2015, the generalizability of these findings may be limited to other geographic or ethnic populations, or to contemporary settings where mechanical thrombectomy is now the standard of care. Fourth, although multivariate adjustments were applied, several clinically relevant variables were not available in the eICU database, including NIHSS scores, withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment (WLST), detailed stroke subtypes, causes of death, and records of vascular interventions. The lack of these data impedes a complete assessment of their potential confounding effects. Fifth, eICU database lacks data on HbA1c, a key marker for distinguishing chronic IR. All variables used to calculate the surrogate indices of IR in this study were based on the first laboratory measurements taken at admission. As a result, the potential influence of acute stress on these indicators cannot be excluded. Therefore, future research should prioritize multinational, multicenter prospective studies to validate these IR indices in clinical practice and evaluate whether interventions targeting IR can effectively reduce mortality risk. Additionally, future work should focus on integrating multimodal biomarkers and clinical parameters, with particular attention to their temporal dynamics [56], and incorporate singular pooling [57] techniques into deep learning architectures. This approach would help extract highly discriminative embeddings from baseline data, ultimately improving the accuracy and personalization of risk prediction.

Conclusions

Our findings provide the first evidence that four surrogate markers of IR (TyG, AIP, METS.IR, and TyG-BMI) play a significant role in short-term mortality risk assessment among critically ill patients with IS. Notably, the TyG index demonstrated relatively superior discriminative ability and stronger risk effects in the overall population, while the AIP exhibited greater effect size and higher predictive performance in patients under 60 years of age. These findings address a critical gap in understanding the comparative utility of surrogate IR indices for assessing short-term mortality risk in severe IS. Routine evaluation of these indices may enhance clinical assessment and inform individualized patient management across different subgroups. Future prospective studies are needed to determine whether targeted management of these indices can improve clinical outcomes and reduce mortality. Furthermore, incorporating robust indicators such as TyG into clinical risk assessment models could enhance the precision of mortality predictions and facilitate personalized care.
