miR827 orchestrates the regulation of SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 with the assistance of lncRNA767 to enhance phosphate starvation tolerance and maize development
Authors: Lei Chen, Juan He, Xufeng Wang, Shiru Zhang, Jinkang Pan, Jianxiang Peng, Beixin Mo, Lin Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryMicroRNA827 (miR827) is functionally conserved among different plant species and displays species‐specific characteristics, but the mechanisms by which miR827 regulates phosphate (Pi) starvation tolerance and maize development remain elusive. We found that miR827 selectively targets the Pi transporter genes SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5. miR827 overexpression improved the Pi starvation tolerance, plant architecture and grain yield and quality, whereas miR827 suppression yielded a contrasting phenotype. In addition, we identified a specific long noncoding RNA (lncRNA767) that serves as a direct target and a facilitator of miR827 and can stabilize the SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 transcripts, leading to their translation inhibition. The orchestrated regulation of SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 modulates PHR1; 1 and PHR1; 2, which are critical transcription factors in Pi signalling, and thereby affects the expression of downstream Pi starvation‐induced genes. Together, these findings demonstrate that miR827, assisted by lncRNA767, enhances SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 suppression and thus exerts a significant impact on Pi homeostasis and several essential agronomic traits of maize.Keywords: Zea mays, microRNA827, long noncoding RNA, phosphate starvation, development

Introduction

Maize (Zea mays L.) is a staple crop that plays a crucial role in meeting the mounting global demand for food and renewable energy sources (Godfray et al., 2010). Phosphorus (P) is one of the most critical mineral nutrients for plant growth and crop yield (Oldroyd and Leyser, 2020). Approximately 70% of cultivated lands worldwide experience P deficiency (Xu et al., 2019). There is a significant increase in the demand for P during the reproductive growth stage of maize, which renders P deficiency a principal factor restricting the maize yield. To address these challenges, conventional practices rely heavily on chemical fertilizers. However, this strategy bears the potential burden of heightened operational inputs and consequential environmental contamination. Thus, the development of maize cultivars with enhanced tolerance to low‐phosphate (Pi) conditions that continue to exhibit high productivity is urgently needed. Achieving this objective requires the deployment of efficient strategies, such as molecular‐assisted breeding (Gilbert, 2009).

Long‐term crop improvement and persistent efforts of scientists have led to the identification of a series of genes to increase maize yields (Ma et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2022). Nevertheless, the emergence of a beneficial trait might be accompanied by the impairment of some other useful traits, and precisely modulating gene expression is an efficient way to combat this problem. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are endogenous small RNAs composed of 20–24 nucleotides (nt) that serve as crucial regulators of gene expression modulation. They function by pairing with target mRNAs through sequence complementarity, leading to their degradation or translational repression (Chen, 2004; Yu et al., 2019). Mounting evidence suggests that miRNAs play roles in distinct biological processes throughout the plant life cycle and have the potential for applications for crop trait improvement (Tang and Chu, 2017). MicroRNA827 (miR827) originated in the common ancestor of angiosperms and has been retained throughout evolution, except in legumes, where it was lost (Chávez Montes et al., 2014; Taylor et al., 2014). In plants, miR827 responds to multiple stresses, including Pi starvation, pathogen infection, drought and high temperature (Ferdous et al., 2016; He et al., 2019; Hewezi et al., 2016; Kant et al., 2011). In Arabidopsis, miR827 targets NITROGEN LIMITATION ADAPTATION (NLA), which enables the upregulation of PHOSPHATE TRANSPORTER 1 (PHT1) family members through ubiquitination‐mediated processes (Kant et al., 2011; Lin et al., 2013). This regulatory mechanism is crucial for the maintenance of Pi homeostasis. In rice, miR827 specifically cleaves the transcripts of two members of the SPX‐MAJOR FACILITATOR SUPERFAMILY (SPX‐MFS) subfamily, which encode proteins localized to the vacuolar membrane that regulate intravesicular Pi storage and transport (Lin et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2012). In tobacco, miR827 targets SPX‐MFS1/2, whose protein products are localized not only in the vacuolar membrane but also in the nucleus (Gu et al., 2016). Thus, although miR827 has changed its preferences for target genes during evolution, these target genes belong to the family of SPX domain‐containing proteins, and distinct members of this family participate in the Pi signalling pathway (Lin et al., 2017). However, the target genes of miR827 in maize have not been reported thus far. Moreover, previous studies that combined RNA‐seq with phenotypic analysis indicated that miR827 is highly abundant in the grains of high‐yielding maize lines (Jin et al., 2015; Wu et al., 2016), but the underlying mechanism remains unclear.

Long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) can regulate the expression of neighbouring or distant genes through either cis‐ or trans‐acting mechanisms (Gil and Ulitsky, 2019; Rinn and Chang, 2020). To date, only a limited number of plant lncRNAs, such as COOLAIR, LAIR, MAS, SVALAK, MISSEN, VAS and HIDDEN TREASURE 1, have undergone comprehensive functional characterization (Csorba et al., 2014; Kindgren et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2018, 2022; Xu et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2021). In maize, PILNCR1 and PILNCR2 interfere with the miR399‐mediated cleavage of PHO2 or PHT1s to affect low Pi tolerance (Du et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2023b). Arabidopsis PHOSPHATE STARVATION RESPONSE (PHR) functions as a central component of the Pi starvation response and triggers the expression of Pi starvation‐induced (PSI) genes, including MIR399 and MIR827, by directly binding to the PHR1‐binding sequence (P1BS) cis‐element motif (Park et al., 2022; Ried et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023a). However, in maize, whether any lncRNAs are involved in the miR827‐mediated response to Pi starvation remains unknown, and the mechanisms governing the accumulation of miR827 have not been elucidated.

Here, we revealed the regulatory mechanisms and roles of miR827 and its target genes, including SPX‐MFS1, SPX‐MFS 5 and lncRNA767, in modulating Pi homeostasis and development in maize. We elucidated that lncRNA767 functions in collaboration with miR827 to regulate the expression of both SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5. Furthermore, SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 interact with both PHR1;1 and PHR1;2, thereby modulating the Pi signalling pathway. We also demonstrated the significant impact of miR827 on the regulation of the plant architecture and grain yield and quality. Therefore, our findings support that miR827 positively regulates maize resistance to Pi starvation, which suggests that this miRNA has potential application in the breeding of high‐yield cultivars.

Results

The accumulation of miR827 is regulated by the developmental stage and Pi deficiency

We first examined the spatiotemporal expression of miR827 throughout maize development via northern blotting. The results showed that miR827 exhibited relatively low expression levels in the whole plants at the V1/V3 stages (the collar of the first/third leaf was visible at the vegetative stage) but accumulated significantly in the ears and silks at the R1 stage (silking stage), and this upregulation persisted into the R3 stage (milking stage) (Figure 1a). In addition, the expression levels of primary miR827 transcripts (pri‐miR827) were relatively higher in reproductive tissues than in vegetative tissues, showing a pattern similar to that found for mature miR827 (Figure 1b). This accumulation pattern indicates the necessity of miR827 during the reproductive growth stage.

In maize, the requirement for Pi gradually increases during the transition from the vegetative to the reproductive phase (Bender et al., 2013b), correlating with the miR827 expression pattern. To elucidate the function of miR827, we investigated the miR827 expression levels under Pi‐starvation conditions. We observed significantly increased accumulations of miR827 and pri‐miR827 in both the shoots and roots (Figure S1a, b). Interestingly, the expression of miR827*, the passenger strand of miR827, was also significantly induced by both low‐Pi and Pi‐deficiency stress treatments (Figure S1d). By examining our previously generated degradome data (He et al., 2022), we found that miR827 targets SPX‐MFS1, lncRNA767 and SPX‐MFS5 (Figure S2a, b). To further verify these candidate target genes, we performed a 5′ RACE assay, and the sequencing results showed that miR827 cleaves SPX‐MFS1 and lncRNA767 at the expected sites corresponding to miR827 nucleotides 10 and 11 (Figure S2c, d). However, the amount of cleaved transcripts of SPX‐MFS5 was too low to be detected. We further performed dual‐luciferase (dual‐LUC) reporter assay with maize protoplasts, and the results showed that SPX‐MFS1, lncRNA767 and SPX‐MFS5 exhibited significantly decreased expression levels in the presence of miR827, whereas miR827 treatment did not reduce the transcript levels of the miR827‐resistant versions of SPX‐MFS1, lncRNA767 or SPX‐MFS5 (denoted mSPX‐MFS1, mlncRNA767 and mSPX‐MFS5, respectively) (Figure 1c, d; Figure S3). These results provide evidence showing that SPX‐MFS1, lncRNA767 and SPX‐MFS5 are targets of miR827. Moreover, the expression levels of SPX‐MFS1, lncRNA767 and SPX‐MFS5 exhibited significant decreasing trends and were negatively correlated with miR827 following low Pi or Pi deprivation treatment (Figure 1e–h). Taken together, these results suggest that miR827 participates in the Pi signalling pathway by suppressing the expression of SPX‐MFS1, lncRNA767 and SPX‐MFS5.

Identification of lncRNA767

LncRNA767 was previously assigned the ID URS0000D75FAE_4577.3367 and was classified as a putative lncRNA in PLncDB (https://www.tobaccodb.org/plncdb/). The lncRNA767 gene has an overlapping region with the 5′UTR of the SPX‐MFS1 transcripts and aligns entirely with the SPX‐MFS1‐T004 transcript (Figure S2a). Through 3′ RACE detection, we confirmed the presence of a poly (A) tail in lncRNA767 (Figure S4a), indicating that the transcription of lncRNA767 is mediated by RNA polymerase II (RNAPII). We utilized the online ORF Finder tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/orffinder/) to assess the coding potential of lncRNA767. Only five open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted to potentially encode short peptides, the longest of which encoded 53 amino acids (Figure S4b). Additionally, these peptides exhibited no homologous matches to any known functional domains in the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). Therefore, we hypothesized that lncRNA767 does not possess any coding‐related function. We also analysed the expression pattern of lncRNA767 in various tissues at different developmental stages. The expression of lncRNA767 in roots and ears showed a rapid decline at stage V14 compared with stage V12 (Figure S4c). The V14 stage is characterized by rapid growth in preparation for flowering and kernel development (Bender et al., 2013a). To further characterize lncRNA767, we investigated its subcellular localization by separating the cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions. Our analysis revealed that lncRNA767 primarily accumulated in the cytoplasm, whereas the SPX‐MFS1‐T004 transcripts predominantly accumulated in the nucleus, as determined by RT‐qPCR (Figure 2a). 3′ RACE analysis also confirmed that lncRNA767 was abundant in the cytoplasm (Figure 2b). RT‐qPCR further confirmed that the SPX‐MFS1‐T004 transcript was localized in the nucleus (Figure 2b). Collectively, these results suggest that lncRNA767 is transcribed with a poly (A) tail and primarily localizes to the cytoplasmic compartment.

LncRNA767 stabilizes SPX‐MSF1/5‐derived transcripts and inhibits their translation

To explore the potential regulatory mechanism of lncRNA767, we investigated whether lncRNA767 functions in cis or trans to regulate SPX‐MFS1/5. Interestingly, the 3′UTRs of both the SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 transcripts were identified as targets of lncRNA767 via prediction using IntaRNA (http://rna.informatik.uni‐freiburg.de/IntaRNA/Input.jsp) and LncTar (http://www.cuilab.cn/lnctar) (Figure S5a–d). We designed firefly luciferase reporter constructs encompassing the entire 3′UTRs of SPX‐MFS1/5. These constructs were cotransfected with lncRNA767 into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves (Figure 2c). Interestingly, LUC activity decreased significantly after coexpression of lncRNA767 and the 3′UTR SPX‐MFS1/5 reporters (Figure 2d, e). However, LUC mRNA accumulation increased when 3′UTR SPX‐MFS1/5 reporters were coexpressed with lncRNA767 (Figure 2f). These results suggest that lncRNA767 stabilizes the transcripts of the SPX‐MFS1/5 mRNAs and represses their translation. Notably, we discovered multiple AU‐rich elements (AREs) in the 3′UTRs of SPX‐MFS1/5 (Figure S5e, f). It has been reported that the AREs located in the 3′UTR of mRNAs can serve as potent post‐transcriptional regulatory motifs capable of swiftly influencing alterations in both mRNA stability and translation (Vasudevan and Steitz, 2007). To investigate whether lncRNA767 regulates translational repression and mRNA accumulation through AREs, we performed a dual‐LUC reporter assay and observed that mutations in AREs in the 3′UTRs of SPX‐MFS1/5 attenuated the impacts of lncRNA767 on stabilizing and suppressing the translation of SPX‐MFS1/5 transcripts (Figure 2d, g, h). Together, our results suggest that lncRNA767 can potentially enhance the stability of SPX‐MFS1/5 mRNAs and repress their translation through interactions with their 3′UTRs.

miR827 is a positive regulator of maize resistance to Pi starvation

To assess the role of miR827 in response to Pi starvation, we generated miR827 overexpression (OX‐miR827) lines, miR827 short tandem target mimic suppression (STTM‐miR827) lines and CRISPR/Cas9‐edited (Crispr‐miR827) lines (Figure S6a, b). We selected three homozygous lines from independent events and determined the expression levels of miR827 via northern blotting. Compared with the wild‐type plants, the OX‐miR827 plants presented significantly higher miR827 expression levels, whereas the STTM‐miR827 and Crispr‐miR827 lines presented substantially lower miR827 expression (Figure 3a). Multiple phenotypic changes were observed in the OX‐miR827, STTM‐miR827 and Crispr‐miR827 plants under normal conditions. The whole‐plant biomass, root length, leaf width, stem width and plant height of the OX‐miR827 plants were higher than those of the wild‐type control plants (Figure 3b–e; Figure S7a–c). Conversely, the STTM‐miR827 and Crispr‐miR827 plants exhibited contrasting phenotypes, with a reduced plant height, a lower whole‐plant biomass and narrower leaf and stem widths (Figure 3b–e; Figure S7a–c). In addition, 31P nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis indicated that miR827 promoted the accumulation of P in roots, especially in the form of organophosphorus (Figure 3f, g).

To further assess the role of miR827 under Pi‐starvation conditions, we conducted Pi tolerance assays of miR827 transgenic plants. Under Pi‐starvation stress, the OX‐miR827 lines exhibited improved adaptation to Pi starvation, as indicated by increases in whole‐plant biomass, root length, leaf width, stem width and plant height compared with those of the wild‐type plants (Figure 3b–e; Figure S7a–c). In contrast, the STTM‐miR827 and Crispr‐miR827 lines exhibited significant reduction compared to the wild‐type plants (Figure 3b–e; Figure S7a–c). More importantly, the OX‐miR827 lines showed a higher accumulation of Pi compared to wild‐type plants, with the magnitude of increase were about 20% greater than that observed under normal conditions (Figure 3h). Conversely, the STTM‐miR827 and Crispr‐miR827 lines demonstrated decreased Pi content compared to wild‐type plants, with the magnitude of reduction being approximately 18% higher than that under normal conditions (Figure 3h). Additionally, the transcript levels of lncRNA767, SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 were markedly lower in the OX‐miR827 lines than in the control plants, whereas the STTM‐miR827 and Crispr‐miR827 lines exhibited considerably increased expression of these transcripts under both normal and Pi‐starvation conditions (Figure 3i–k). Moreover, we observed that the expression of SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 complemented the growth phenotype of the yeast mutant EY917, which is deficient in five Pi transporter proteins (Δpho84Δpho87Δpho89Δpho90Δpho91), indicating the potential role of SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 in Pi translocation (Figure 3l). Taken together, these findings suggest that miR827 acts as a positive regulator of the tolerance to Pi starvation, potentially by facilitating the accumulation of Pi through targeting SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5.

miR827 regulates the tolerance of maize to Pi starvation by suppressing SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5

To decipher the role of miR827‐mediated SPX‐MFS1/5 cleavages in regulating the tolerance of maize to Pi starvation, we generated miR827‐resistant SPX‐MFS1/5 overexpression plants, designated rSPX‐MFS1‐OE and rSPX‐MFS5‐OE, by mutating the miR827 recognition sites in SPX‐MFS1/5. The introduced mutations were identical to those used in the dual‐LUC reporter assay (Figure S3). RT‐qPCR analysis confirmed that the rSPX‐MFS1/5‐OE‐positive transgenic lines overexpressed the corresponding SPX‐MFS1/5 genes (Figure S8a, b). The plant height, root length and stem width of the rSPX‐MFS1/5‐OE lines under Pi‐starvation conditions were significantly lower than those of the wild‐type plants, which was analogous to the observations obtained for the STTM‐miR827 and Crispr‐miR827 plants (Figure S8c–j). We also utilized mutant lines (SPX‐MFS1/5 Mu ) with T‐DNA insertions in the 5′UTR of SPX‐MFS1/5 for further functional study. The RT‐qPCR results showed that the expression of the SPX‐MFS1/5 transcripts was markedly reduced in the SPX‐MFS1/5 Mu mutant lines (Figure S9a, b). The SPX‐MFS1/5 Mu mutant lines presented increased Pi starvation tolerance compared with the wild‐type plants (W22). The former displayed wider leaves and longer roots after exposure to Pi starvation (Figure S9c–e). Additionally, the Pi content was significantly lower in rSPX‐MFS1‐OE and rSPX‐MFS5‐OE plants compared with the wild type, whereas the Pi content in SPX‐MFS1/5 Mu mutant plants exceeded that of the wild type (Figure S10a, b). Taken together, these findings strongly indicate that miR827 regulates the tolerance of maize to Pi starvation by inhibiting the expression of SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5.

SPX‐MFS1/5 interact with PHR1;1/2 to participate in Pi signalling

The Pi transport activity assay conducted with mutant yeast suggested that SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 are responsible for Pi translocation. Subsequently, we investigated the reasons underlying the downregulation of SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 expression during Pi starvation. A plausible explanation is that the major facilitator superfamily (MFS) is classified as a “transceptors,” which can function as either a substrate sensor or transporter to participate in secondary active transport (Drew et al., 2021), and SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 are members of this superfamily. Due to the substrate concentration gradient dependency observed for the MFS family, their capacity to facilitate Pi uptake is compromised under Pi starvation conditions, which triggers increased primary active transporter movement (Drew et al., 2021). To investigate whether SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 act as transceptors, we conducted experiments to assess their subcellular localization and relationships with the central components of Pi signalling, PHR1;1 and PHR1;2.

To examine the subcellular localization, the SPX‐MFS1‐YFP and SPX‐MFS5‐YFP vectors were transiently expressed into maize mesophyll protoplasts, respectively. It was observed that the SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 were predominantly localized in the plasma membrane, and occasional faint fluorescent signals were detected in the cytoplasm, suggesting the possible presence of these proteins within intracellular compartments (Figure 4a; Figure S11a). We also performed immunofluorescence analysis to detect the localization of SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 using rSPX‐MFS1‐OE and rSPX‐MFS5‐OE transgenic plants. The results further confirmed that SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 were localized at the plasma membrane, with a lesser amount detected in the cytoplasm (Figure S12). Furthermore, we examined the localization of PHR1;1 and PHR1;2, and mainly detected the fluorescent signals of PHR1;1‐mCherry and PHR1;2‐mCherry in the cytoplasm and nucleus under normal Pi conditions (Figure 4a; Figure S11a). Interestingly, exposure to Pi starvation induced a shift in the localization of SPX‐MFS1‐YFP, SPX‐MFS5‐YFP, PHR1;1‐mCherry and PHR1;2‐mCherry to the nucleus (Figure 4a; Figure S11a). Additionally, the coexpression of SPX‐MFS1‐YFP with PHR1;1‐mCherry, SPX‐MFS5‐YFP with PHR1;1‐mCherry, SPX‐MFS1‐YFP with PHR1;2‐mCherry and SPX‐MFS5‐YFP with PHR1;2‐mCherry showed that YFP and mCherry fluorescence colocalized in the nucleus following Pi‐starvation treatment (Figure S13), indicating potential interactions between SPX‐MFS1/5 and PHR1;1/2. To further substantiate this hypothesis, we conducted bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays with maize mesophyll protoplasts. The results showed that the coexpression of SPX‐MFS1‐YFPC/PHR1;1‐YFPN, SPX‐MFS5‐YFPC/PHR1;1‐YFPN, SPX‐MFS1‐YFPC/PHR1;2‐YFPN or SPX‐MFS5‐YFPC/PHR1;2‐YFPN led to weak fluorescence signals in the cytoplasm under normal Pi conditions, whereas strong YFP signals were observed in the nucleus under Pi‐starvation conditions (Figure 4b; Figure S11b). Coimmunoprecipitation (co‐IP) assays with N. benthamiana leaves further confirmed the interactions between SPX‐MFS1/5 and PHR1;1/2 in vivo (Figure 4c). Similarly, yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) assays also validated these mutual interaction relationships (Figure 4d). Upon Pi deficiency, PHR1 contributes to Pi uptake by directly inducing PHT1 expression (Kumar et al., 2022). Significantly high expression of PHT1;1, PHT1;3, and PHT1;13 is induced in maize subjected to Pi deficiency (Wang et al., 2023b). We subsequently examined the expression of PHT1;1, PHT1;3, and PHT1;13 in the rSPX‐MFS1/5‐OE transgenic plants in response to Pi starvation. Compared with those in the wild‐type plants, the transcripts of PHT1;1 and PHT1;3 were downregulated in the rSPX‐MFS1‐OE plants, and the transcript levels of PHT1;1 and PHT1;13 were decreased significantly in the rSPX‐MFS5‐OE plants (Figure S14a–f). The rSPX‐MFS1/5‐OE plants exhibited a reduction in the expression of PHT1s, indicating that SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 affect PHR1;1/2 activity and thus the expression of downstream genes. Taken together, these findings indicate that SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 act as regulators of PHR1;1/2 in the Pi signalling pathway.

Additionally, the coexpression of SPX‐MFS1‐YFPC/SPX‐MFS1‐YFPN, SPX‐MFS5‐YFPC/SPX‐MFS5‐YFPN or SPX‐MFS1‐YFPC/SPX‐MFS5‐YFPN produced weak fluorescence signals in the plasma membrane under normal Pi conditions (Figure S15). However, in Pi‐starved protoplasts, strong YFP signals were observed predominantly within the nucleus (Figure S15). These results indicated that SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 can form homodimers, heterodimers or potentially even multimers to function.

LncRNA767 facilitates the miR827‐mediated regulation of SPX‐MFS1/5 expression and downstream Pi signalling

To further investigate the function and contribution of lncRNA767 to Pi starvation resistance, we generated lncRNA767‐overexpressing (OX‐lncRNA767) transgenic lines, which exhibited greater plant height, longer roots and wider stems than did the wild‐type plants (Figure S16a–e). In agreement with the transient expression results in N. benthamiana (Figure 2d–h), the expression of the SPX‐MFS1/5 proteins significantly decreased in the OX‐lncRNA767 transgenic lines, whereas the abundance of the SPX‐MFS1/5 mRNAs substantially increased (Figure S16f–h). Consequently, the reduction in the expression of the SPX‐MFS1/5 proteins in OX‐lncRNA767 plants contributes to the promotion of PHR1;1/2 to activate the downstream Pi starvation‐responsive pathway. As expected, the OX‐lncRNA767 plants exhibited enhanced tolerance to Pi starvation (Figure S16i–l).

We also employed a virus‐induced gene silencing (VIGS) strategy to suppress the expression of lncRNA767. Although the lncRNA767 and SPX‐MFS1‐T004 transcripts share overlapping regions, they exhibit differential subcellular localization. Specifically, lncRNA767 accumulates primarily in the cytoplasm, whereas SPX‐MFS1‐T004 localizes predominantly to the nucleus (Figure 2a, b). Because RNA‐mediated VIGS primarily functions in the cytoplasm (Rössner et al., 2022), it is hypothesized that lncRNA767 is selectively targeted with minimal effects on the SPX‐MFS1‐T004 transcripts. The lncRNA767‐VIGS plants displayed a dwarf phenotype, with a shorter root length and a lower total P content compared with the TRV:00 control plants (Figure S17a–e). The reduced tolerance of the lncRNA767‐VIGS plants to Pi starvation was probably attributed to the repression of lncRNA767, which hindered the decreased expression of the SPX‐MFS1/5 proteins and consequently repressed the activity of PHR1;1/2 (Figure S17f–k). These results imply that lncRNA767 assists miR827 in regulating SPX‐MFS1/5 expression and thereby influences the plant tolerance to Pi starvation.

miR827 enhances the maize field yield

To explore the potential use of miR827 in improving maize yield, we conducted field trials using OX‐miR827, STTM‐miR827 and Crispr‐miR827 transgenic plants. In comparison to the wild‐type plants at the R1 stage, the OX‐miR827 lines displayed a greater plant height, wider stems, increased spike and ear lengths, and a lower relative ear height (Figure 5a–e; Figure S18a, c, d) but similar counts of tassel primary branches and secondary branches (Figure S18b, c, e, f). Notably, compared with the corresponding wild‐type controls, OX‐miR827 plants consistently exhibited significant increases in mature ear length, kernel width, 100‐grain weight, grain yield per plant, kernel number per row and kernel number per plant (Figure 5f–m). In contrast, the growth inhibition and reduction in the grain yield observed in the STTM‐miR827 and Crispr‐miR827 lines were greater than those found for the wild‐type plants (Figure 5f–m). We were further exploring the potential impact of the target genes SPX‐MFS1/5 on maize yield. The expression profile analysis revealed a notable decrease in the expression levels of SPX‐MFS1/5 in maize ears at stage V14 compared with stage V12 (Figure S19). The V14 stage is characterized by rapid growth in preparation for kernel development (Bender et al., 2013a). This reduction suggests the essential roles of SPX‐MFS1/5 in kernel development. According to our observation, the SPX‐MFS1 Mu and SPX‐MFS5 Mu mutants exhibited comparable improvements in the plant architecture, ear length and kernel width, resembling those observed in miR827‐overexpressing transgenic lines (Figure S20a–n). Additionally, we observed increases in the growth and yield traits of OX‐lncRNA767 plants, suggesting that lncRNA767 is a booster of miR827 for improving yield (Figure S21a–h). These findings affirm the role of miR827 in positively regulating plant architecture and grain yield through its interactions with specific target genes in maize.

To investigate whether miR827 overexpression improves the yield performance under natural low Pi conditions, we planted the miR827 transgenic lines and wild‐type plants together in Pi‐deficient lateritic red soil (Shenzhen) and latosol conditions (Hainan). In lateritic red soil, the OX‐miR827 lines showed complete tassel emergence with strong stems, whereas the wild‐type plants only began to show tassel emergence (Figure S22). In contrast, the simultaneously planted STTM‐miR827 and Crispr‐miR827 plants exhibited retarded growth and lacked visible tassels (Figure S22). Notably, compared with those of the wild‐type plants, the OX‐miR827 plants demonstrated a remarkable improvement in the 100‐kernel weight under Pi‐deficient latosol conditions, resulting in a substantial increase in yield (Figure S23a–h). Therefore, our findings suggest that the overexpression of miR827 can improve both the development and grain yield under low‐Pi conditions, which makes it a valuable trait for promoting sustainable maize production.

miR827 improves the grain quality

We observed that the OX‐miR827 kernels exhibited a notably larger vitreous endosperm area than the wild‐type kernels did, whereas the STTM‐miR827 and Crispr‐miR827 kernels exhibited reduced endosperm areas, ranging from 61% to 65% (Figure 6a). Scanning electron microscopy analysis demonstrated that the vitreous endosperm of the STTM‐miR827 and Crispr‐miR827 kernels contained protein body (PB) granules, resulting in loosely and unevenly stacked starch formation, indicating a classical defect in grain filling (Figure 6b, c). Moreover, compared with the wild‐type kernels, the OX‐miR827 kernels exhibited an elevated starch content and reduced soluble sugar levels, whereas the STTM‐miR827 and Crispr‐miR827 kernels displayed a lower starch content and higher soluble sugar levels (Figure 6d, e). Our micro X‐ray fluorescence (μXRF) analysis indicated that the OX‐miR827 kernels had an increased total P content, whereas the STTM‐miR827 and Crispr‐miR827 kernels exhibited significantly lower P contents than did the wild‐type kernels (Figure 6f, g). Because P and zinc (Zn) are antagonistic to each other during uptake, we conducted an investigation to ascertain whether a heightened P content influences Zn accumulation. No change in the Zn content was observed in OX‐miR827 kernels (Figure S24a, c), implying that the miR827‐induced increase in the P content did not adversely affect Zn accumulation.

Notably, we observed a distinct orange–yellow colour in the kernels of OX‐miR827 plants compared with those of the wild‐type plants, whereas the STTM‐miR827 and Crispr‐miR827 exhibited a very pale‐yellow colour kernels (Figure 6h). Based on these observations, we hypothesized that miR827 might influence the carotenoid composition. By employing UPLC–MS, we analysed the carotenoid composition and found that the OX‐miR827 kernels exhibited greater accumulation of β‐carotene, α‐carotene, lycopene and zeaxanthin, confirming a strong correlation between an increased carotenoid content and the orange–yellow phenotype observed in the OX‐miR827 endosperm (Figure 6i, j; Figure S25a–g). In contrast, the STTM‐miR827 and Crispr‐miR827 kernels exhibited reduced levels of β‐carotene and lutein, corresponding to their pale‐yellow endosperm phenotypes (Figure 6h–j; Figure S25a–g).

To comprehensively investigate the impact of miR827 on starch and carotenoid metabolism, we determined the gene expression profiles in the ear tissues of OX‐miR827, STTM‐miR827 and the wild‐type plants at the R1 stage by RNA‐seq. Based on a Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis of the transcriptomic changes in OX‐miR827 and STTM‐miR827, we identified the involvement of miR827 in multiple regulatory pathways, with notable enrichment in starch and sucrose metabolism, carotenoid biosynthesis, and photosynthesis, among other pathways (Figure S26a, b; Data S1). Notably, RNA‐seq revealed increased expression of PHR1;1, PHT1;3 and PHT1;6 in OX‐miR827, elucidating the mechanistic basis for the improved grain yield observed in OX‐miR827 (Figure S26c) (Maharajan et al., 2019; Nagy et al., 2006; Wang et al., 2013). The genes GIF1, Y1, crtRB2, LCYE1 and PDS (vp5) were predominantly downregulated in STTM‐miR827, which corresponded to the dwarf phenotype, starch synthesis defects and carotenoid reduction observed in these plants (Figure S26d, e) (Buckner et al., 1990; Wang et al., 2008; Yan et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2018). Moreover, the expression levels of SPX‐MFS1, lncRNA767 and SPX‐MFS5 were lower in the OX‐miR827 kernels, but higher in the STTM‐miR827 kernels compared to the wild type. These changes were closely associated with the altered P content observed in the mature seeds of these plants (Figure 6f, g; Figure S26f–h). We also detected a higher sulfur (S) content in OX‐miR827 kernels, as indicated by KEGG analysis, which showed enrichment of S metabolism (Figures S24b, d and S26a). These results suggest that miR827 promotes the formation of the vitreous endosperm and the accumulation of carotenoids through multiple metabolic pathways.

Discussion

miR827 exhibits a high degree of functional conservation across diverse plant species but also displays certain species‐specific characteristics. Previous studies have shown that miR827 can target distinct genes to play a positive role in Pi starvation in Arabidopsis and rice (Kant et al., 2011; Lin et al., 2010, 2013). In this study, we revealed a novel aspect of the function of miR827 in maize. In addition to identifying SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 as direct targets of miR827, we found that lncRNA767, which is derived from the same locus as SPX‐MFS1, collaborates with miR827 to regulate the expression of SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 and thereby participates in the regulation of Pi signalling (Figure 7). Previous studies have suggested the potential involvement of miR827 in seed development, primarily due to its high expression levels during grain‐filling stages (Wu et al., 2016); however, the specific targets of miR827 and the underlying mechanisms remain elusive. Our findings revealed that the manipulation of miR827 expression in maize led to substantial alterations in agronomic traits. The OX‐miR827 transgenic plants displayed improved plant stature and grain yield compared with the wild‐type plants (Figure 5a–m; Figure S18a–g). Domestication and selection of teosinte to cultivated maize involves characteristics such as the plant architecture, ear size and grain type (Dong et al., 2019), which suggests that miR827 might have been selected during the domestication process. Our analysis demonstrated that miR827 tends to be selected in either the United States or China following chronological maize breeding and confers superior traits to the miR827‐overexpressing transgenic lines (Figure S27). Moreover, miR827 and the target SPX‐MFS1 have been reported to undergo selection during the domestication process from teosinte to maize landraces, and participate in the regulation of root system development (Ren et al., 2022), aligning with the longer root phenotype of OX‐miR827 plants (Figure 3b, d).

A proposed model for the role of miR827 in Pi starvation tolerance and development in maize. A simplified network of miR827‐mediated regulation of maize Pi starvation resistance and development. miR827 targets SPX‐MFS1/5 and lncRNA767 to improve Pi starvation tolerance by regulating PHR1;1/2 and the downstream Pi signalling pathway. miR827 can also enhance GIF1 expression to regulate plant growth and promote starch synthesis. Conversely, the suppression of miR827 expression leads to loose and irregular starch formation, manifested as the accumulation of protein body granules. miR827 can simultaneously promote Y1, crtRB2, LCYE1 and PDS expression to increase the carotenoid content and eventually improve the grain quality. An enhanced supply of Pi nutrients is also beneficial for enhancing maize development and increasing the grain yield. The solid lines indicate direct regulation; the dashed lines indicate unknown regulation; →, positive regulation or next step and ⊣, negative regulation.

Several recent studies have identified a family of SPX‐MFS proteins that serve as vacuolar Pi transporters; among these proteins, OsSPX‐MFS1 in rice and AtVPT1/PHT5 in Arabidopsis exhibit significant mutual homology and are responsible for facilitating Pi influx into the vacuole (Liu et al., 2015, 2016a). Recent research further revealed that OsSPX‐MFS1 is not solely confined to the vacuole but is also present in the endomembrane compartments and small vesicles in rice (Guo et al., 2023). Notably, our results showed that maize SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5, which share homology with OsSPX‐MFS1 (Figure S28a), are primarily localized in the plasma membrane and exhibit a minor presence in the cytoplasm (Figure 4a). The subcellular localization of SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 in maize differs from that of their homologues in rice, potentially due to the distinct transmembrane structural domains and 3D structures of these proteins (Figure S28b, c). In addition, this variance may be attributed to the presence of immature vacuoles in maize mesophyll protoplasts. Several MFS transporters have been recognized as transceptors that play dual roles by transporting their own substrates and serving as signalling components (Diallinas, 2017; Drew et al., 2021). For instance, the plasma membrane‐localized NRT1.1 functions as a nitrate cotransporter protein and a sensor in the nitrate signalling pathways in both Arabidopsis and rice (Hu et al., 2015, 2019). Therefore, we hypothesized that SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 might play a role in Pi signalling given their specific localization to the plasma membrane. Indeed, SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 could be translocated to the nucleus under Pi‐starvation conditions and were found to be capable of interacting with PHR1;1 and PHR1;2 (Figure 4a, b). Previous studies have revealed that SPX4 represses the translocation of PHR1;1 and PHR1;2 into the nucleus, resulting in the suppression of PSI gene expression in Arabidopsis and rice, respectively (Borges et al., 2019; Lv et al., 2014). Here, our results indicate that SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 play comparable roles in impairing the function of PHR1;1 and PHR1;2 in maize (Figure S14a–f). These findings unveil a critical phospho‐sensor signalling module and offer insight into the Pi signalling pathway (Figure 7).

LncRNAs are essential regulators of multiple biological processes and act through nonconserved mechanisms across diverse eukaryotic species (Liu et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2021). LncRNA767 was previously characterized as a putative lncRNA (Li et al., 2014) and subsequently identified as a potential participant in maize development (Zhu et al., 2017). Our study confirmed the existence of lncRNA767 and revealed that it possesses a 3′ poly (A) tail (Figure S4a), suggesting that RNAPII is responsible for the transcription of lncRNA767. In maize, previous studies have shown that two lncRNAs, PILNCR1 and PILNCR2, interact with miR399 to participate in the response to low‐Pi conditions (Du et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2023b). Our results also revealed the essential role of lncRNA767 in the Pi signalling pathway through its interactions with SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 (Figure 7). The regulatory mechanism of lncRNAs is highly diverse, and their subcellular localization is crucial for elucidating their molecular regulatory mechanisms (Statello et al., 2021). Considering the predominant cytoplasmic localization of lncRNA767 (Figure 2a, b), lncRNA767 may function in both cis‐ and trans‐manners. Consistent with this hypothesis, lncRNA767 stabilized the SPX‐MFS1/5 mRNAs and repressed their translation (Figure 2c–f; Figure S16f–h). Therefore, lncRNA767 functions as a facilitator of miR827 in response to Pi starvation.

Notably, recent studies have revealed that miR399‐overexpressing transgenic maize plants exhibit a classical phosphotoxic phenotype under Pi‐sufficient conditions, and this phenotype is attributed to the downregulation of PHO2, which results in the accumulation of PHO1 and PHT1 (Du et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2023a; Wang et al., 2023b). In conjunction with our findings, we hypothesize that Pi transport in maize involves both primary and secondary active transport systems, the latter of which depend on the high potential energy generated by the Pi concentration gradient across internal and external membranes. SPX‐MFS1/5 serve as secondary active transporters. Under low‐Pi conditions, SPX‐MFS1/5 are unable to absorb Pi from miR399‐overexpressing transgenic plants due to differences in concentration, which are coupled with the increase in Pi uptake facilitated by the activation of primary active transporters for stress resistance by miR399. Nevertheless, under Pi‐sufficient conditions, miR399‐activated PHO1 transports abundant Pi from roots to shoots, resulting in high Pi concentrations in the external environment that activate SPX‐MFS1/5 for Pi uptake and eventually cause a toxic phenotype in the shoots due to excessive primary and secondary active transport. However, our results revealed that the OX‐miR827 transgenic plants do not exhibit a Pi toxicity phenotype, regardless of whether they are subjected to Pi‐sufficient or Pi‐starvation conditions (Figure 3d). The rSPX‐MFS1/5‐OE plants decreased the expression of the PHR1;1/2 downstream gene PHT1s (Figure S14a–f). RNA‐seq analysis revealed increased PHT1;3 and PHT1;6 expression in OX‐miR827 plants (Figure S26c; Data S1), which was attributed to the presence of P1BS motifs in their promoter regions (Liu et al., 2016b). We speculated that in OX‐miR827 plants, although miR827 activates PHR1;1/2 to induce the expression of PSI genes, including PHT1s, it simultaneously decreases the expression of SPX‐MFS1/5, preventing the occurrence of a Pi toxicity phenotype (Figure 7). Additionally, the MIR827 promoter contains the P1BS element, which is commonly present in PSI genes, suggesting that MIR827 is a potential target of PHR1;1/2. Therefore, we hypothesize that miR827 is likely to act downstream of PHR1;1/2, whereas the target SPX‐MFS1/5 functions upstream of PHR1;1/2, forming a positive feedback loop within the Pi signalling pathway.

In summary, we characterized the essential role of miR827 in regulating Pi homeostasis and yield traits in maize. miR827 targets SPX‐MFS1/5 at the transcriptional level to repress their expression and thereby modulate the tolerance to low Pi conditions, plant architecture and yield traits in maize; additionally, lncRNA767 may assist miR827 in this process. The target genes of miR827, SPX‐MFS1/5, function as mediators of Pi signalling and as inhibitors of PHR1;1/2 (Figure 7). Furthermore, miR827 has the potential to increase the grain yield and improve grain quality, which makes it a potential genetic resource for the breeding of high‐yielding crops with improved Pi utilization in agriculture. Based on this model, future investigations should focus on elucidating the molecular mechanisms through which miR827 improves grain quality and explore the potential application of the miR827‐mediated regulation of PHR1;1/2 in modulating Pi signalling pathways.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

The OX‐miR827, STTM‐miR827 and Crispr‐miR827 transgenic lines were generated in the maize inbred line ZZC01 background by China National Seed Group Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The rSPX‐MFS1‐OE and rSPX‐MFS5‐OE transgenic lines were generated by Weimi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Changzhou, China), using the maize inbred line KN5585. The SPX‐MFS1 Mu and SPX‐MFS5 Mu plants in the W22 background were acquired from the MaizeGDB stock center (https://www.maizegdb.org/data_center/stock?id=9020818). All the maize materials were cultivated either in a greenhouse (25 ± 2 °C, 16‐h light/8‐h dark cycle, 65% humidity) or in trial fields across various locations in China, including Liaoning (41.8° N, 123.4° E), Sanya (18.4° N, 109.2° E) and Shenzhen (22.6° N, 114.0° E). The primers used for the transgenic genotyping analyses are listed in Table S1.

Vector construction and genetic transformation

To construct the OX‐miR827 vector, the mature miR827 sequence was initially introduced into the primary miRNA backbone and subsequently ligated into the pZZ00026‐PM vector under the control of the ubiquitin promoter. The STTM‐miR827 vector was constructed following established procedures as previously described (Tang and Tang, 2013; Wang et al., 2023a). The STTM‐miR827 fragment was first cloned and inserted into the pOT2‐poly‐cis‐UN vector and subsequently transformed into the pZZ00026‐PM vector under the control of the ubiquitin promoter. To generate CRISPR/Cas9‐edited mutants of miR827, we designed guide RNA sequences targeting the MIR827 gene using the CRISPR‐P platform (http://crispr.hzau.edu.cn/CRISPR2). Subsequently, these guide RNA sequences were cloned and inserted into the U6 promoter‐driven pZmCas9‐e35SPro vector. To construct miR827‐resistant SPX‐MFS1 (rSPX‐MFS1‐OE)‐ and SPX‐MFS5 (rSPX‐MFS5‐OE)‐overexpression vectors, the rSPX‐MFS1/5 sequences with mutations at the miR827‐binding sites were amplified through multiplex overlap PCR (Figure S3) and subsequently cloned into the pCAMBIA3301 vector driven by the native promoters of SPX‐MFS1/5. To construct the lncRNA767‐overexpression vector, the full‐length lncRNA767 sequence was inserted into the pCAMBIA3301 vector driven by the CaMV 35S promoter. All of the aforementioned constructs were subsequently transformed into the inbred line ZZC01 or KN5585 via Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation. The primers used for the vector construction are listed in Table S2.

Small RNA northern blot

Ten micrograms of total RNA from each sample was loaded onto a 15% denaturing urea–PAGE gel and transferred to a Hybond‐NX nylon membrane (GE Healthcare) using a semidry transfer apparatus, and this transfer was followed by chemical cross‐linking with 1‐ethyl‐3‐(3‐dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC) buffer (containing 200 mM EDC and 130 mM 1‐methylimidazole, pH 8.0) at 65 °C for 90 min. The membranes were subjected to overnight hybridization at 55 °C with biotin‐labelled probes in hybridization buffer (5 × SSC, 7% SDS, 2 × Denhardt's solution and 20 mM Na2HPO4, pH 7.2). A probe complementary to U6 served as an internal control. Following hybridization, the membranes were washed twice with washing buffer (2 × SSC and 0.1% SDS) at 50°C for 20 min. Subsequently, the membranes were processed using a Chemiluminescent Nucleic Acid Detection Module Kit (Thermo, 89880) following the manufacturer's instructions. The images were then analysed with a CheiScope 3300 Mini (CLINX, China). The probe sequences utilized in this study are listed in Table S3.

RNA extraction and RT‐qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus (Takara, 9109) following the manufacturer's instructions. Subsequently, cDNAs were synthesized using two distinct kits: the Mir‐X miRNA First‐Strand Synthesis Kit (Clontech, 638316) for miRNA detection and the HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, R323) for target gene quantification. qPCR was conducted with ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q711) on a CFX96 Real‐Time System integrated into a C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler (Bio‐Rad). The maize U6 and β‐actin genes served as internal controls for detecting miRNAs and target genes, respectively. Relative quantification was performed using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Detailed information on the primers used in this study can be found in Table S4.

Dual‐luciferase reporter assay

For dual‐LUC assays with maize protoplasts, the miR827 precursor was inserted into the pGreenII 62‐SK vector and utilized as the effector. The full‐length cDNA sequences of SPX‐MFS1, SPX‐MFS5, lncRNA767, miR827‐resistant mSPX‐MFS1, mSPX‐MFS5 and mlncRNA767 were cloned and inserted into the pGreenII 0800‐LUC vector to create the reporters. The mutated nucleotides are shown in Figure S3. The empty vector pGreenII 62‐SK was utilized as a control. Mesophyll protoplasts were isolated from the etiolated leaves of 2‐week‐old B73 plants. The protoplasts were cotransfected with 50 μg of per‐plasmid DNA and then cultured at 25 °C for 16 h. The firefly and Renilla luciferase (LUC and REN) activities were determined using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay system (Promega, E1960) and a GloMax 20/20 luminometer (Promega, E5311). The ratio of LUC to REN activity was used to compute the relative LUC activity. The primers used for plasmid construction are listed in Table S5.

For dual‐LUC assays with N. benthamiana, the full‐length cDNA sequence of lncRNA767 was cloned and inserted into the pGreenII 62‐SK vector as the effector. The 3′UTR sequences of SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 (270 and 284 bp) were amplified by PCR from leaf cDNA and inserted into the pGreenII 0800‐LUC vector as the effectors. The 3′UTR sequences of SPX‐MFS1 and SPX‐MFS5 with mutations in AREs were commercially synthesized (Tsingke Biological Technology, China) and constructed into pGreenII 0800‐LUC as controls. All the constructs were subsequently transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 (pSoup‐p19), which was subsequently resuspended in transformation buffer (10 mM MES, 27.8 mM glucose, 10 mM MgCl2 and 150 μM acetosyringone, pH 5.7). The different effectors were coinfiltrated with the reporter into N. benthamiana leaves. After 3 days, the inoculated leaves were removed, sprayed with luciferin working buffer (1 mM D‐luciferin sodium salt and 0.025% Triton X‐100) and maintained in the dark for 10 min. The luminescence signal was recorded using a plant living imaging system (Lumazone Pylon 2048B, Princeton, CT, USA) and quantified using ImageJ software. The LUC‐to‐REN luciferase ratio was also examined using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, E1960) following the manufacturer's instructions, and the RNA levels were analysed via RT‐qPCR.

31P NMR spectroscopy

P was extracted from 0.5 g of maize roots with 30 mL of buffer comprising 25 mM EDTA and 0.5 M NaOH. This extraction process was conducted on an end‐over‐end shaker at room temperature for 16 h. After centrifugation of the extracted solutions at 8000 g for 30 min, the supernatants were filtered using Whatman No. 42 paper. The filtered solutions were subsequently augmented with 25 mM methylene diphosphonate and subjected to freeze‐drying to obtain powdered samples. These powdered samples were subsequently redissolved in a 1 M NaOH–0.1 M EDTA solution with 30 min of vortexing. After centrifugation, 1 mL of supernatant containing 0.2 mL of D2O was carefully placed in a 10‐mm NMR tube. The 31P‐NMR data were obtained with an AVANCE III 500 MHz spectrometer (Bruker BioSpin GmbH, Rheinstetten, Germany), and spectral analysis was performed using MestReNova14.0 software.

μXRF element fluorescence imaging

The high‐resolution distributions of P, Zn and S in mature maize seeds were measured using an X‐ray fluorescence (XRF) spectrometer (M4 Tornado, Bruker, Germany). The scanning parameters adhered to the manufacturer's guidelines, with the vertical sampling steps set to 20 μm, a stage speed of 2.0 mm/s and a pixel exposure time of 10 ms. Excitation was achieved at 50 kV and 599 A, employing an X‐ray beam spot size of 20 μm. Cross‐sectional images of seeds from wild‐type, OX‐miR827, STTM‐miR827 and Crispr‐miR827 plants were captured, facilitating the subsequent quantification of the P, Zn and S contents via fluorescence intensity measurements.
