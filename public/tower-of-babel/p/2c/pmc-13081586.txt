Workplace violence and mental health conditions among domestic workers in Rwanda: a cross-sectional study using the propensity score matching analysis
Authors: Alain Favina, Dan Lutasingwa, Everest Turatsinze, Fred Mulisa, Deborah Kansiime, Ritah Mukashyaka, Nicholas Hobe, Octave Ngabo, Jean Marie Vianney Rukanikigitero, Moses Ochora, Louange Twahirwa Gutabarwa, Aflodis Kagaba, Mark Mohan Kaggwa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDomestic workers comprise a substantial workforce segment, especially in Sub-Saharan Africa, fulfilling crucial functions in sustaining households and economies. Nevertheless, they frequently encounter a multitude of obstacles and hazards within their work environment, including violence at the workplace. Despite pervasiveness, repercussions, and potential mental health consequences, studies with a focus on the mental health of domestic workers who experience workplace violence are still scarce. The purpose of this research was to examine the association between workplace violence and mental health conditions (depression, suicidal ideation, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder) among domestic workers in Rwanda.MethodA cross-sectional design was used. Data were collected in 2023, and a total of 862 Domestic Workers (DWs) participated in the study. The mental health conditions were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (depression), four questions to evaluate suicidal ideations from the General Health Questionnaire-28, Generalised Anxiety Disorder Assessment-7 (anxiety), and the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5. The association between workplace violence and mental health conditions was assessed using the propensity score matching analysis.ResultsA significant positive association was found between experiencing workplace violence and the severity of mental health conditions.ConclusionThe research emphasizes the concealed anguish endured by domestic workers and stresses the criticality of safeguarding their rights and welfare in order to establish an environment of dignity and esteem at work. This highlights the critical need for advocacy and intervention to prevent and mitigate workplace violence and mental health conditions among domestic workers in Rwanda.Keywords: Workplace violence, Depression, Suicidality, Anxiety, PTSD, Domestic workers, Rwanda

Introduction

Workplace violence (WPV) is increasingly recognised as a major occupational and public health problem, affecting workers across nearly all industries [1]. Domestic workers (DWs) are a particularly vulnerable occupational group, performing essential household tasks such as cleaning, cooking, and caring for children or elderly people [2, 3]. Globally, they face low wages, long working hours, social isolation, discrimination, and limited labour protections [4]. According to the International Labour Organisation (ILO), there were 8.7 million DWs in Sub-Saharan Africa in 2019, representing 7.3% of total employment in the region [5]. DWs may experience WPV such as verbal abuse, physical assault, sexual harassment, or rape from employers, co-workers, or other household members [6]. Evidence suggests that repeated exposure to violence undermines stress regulation and coping mechanisms, contributing to adverse emotional reactions such as sadness, anger, and fear, which in turn increase the risk of depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and suicidal behaviors [7, 8]. As per the World Health Organisation’s standardised framework [9], the WPV is defined and categorised into three forms: physical abuse (Physical violence is the use of physical force against people and causes physical or psychological harm). Instances of this type of violence are punching, kicking, pushing, pinching, and wounding with sharp objects; sexual violence (Every unwanted or harmful sexual action in which a person exerts his power over another one and causes threats, insults, or shame to him/her); and emotional abuse (sustained psychological mistreatment, such as intimidation, threats, humiliation, or verbal insults) [9].

Previous studies have reported high rates of mental health problems among DWs. For instance, in Hong Kong, 30.8% of migrant domestic workers (MDWs) experienced depressive symptoms, while 17.7% reported anxiety disorders [10]. In Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, 27.5% of housemaids reported depression and 32.3% reported anxiety symptoms [11]. Similarly, in Lebanon, about 25% of MDWs experienced at least one mental disorder during their lifetime [12]. These differences may be due in part to differences in how they are measured, but they may also be due to differences in socio-cultural factors, working conditions, and access to social support.

Prior research indicates that certain socio-demographic factors influence both the risk of experiencing WPV and its mental health consequences. Younger workers, women, and those with lower educational attainment are more susceptible to harassment, abuse, and exploitation [13–15]. Additionally, people starting work at a young age, unmarried, and less experienced are more susceptible to abuse and exploitation [13, 15]. Women have been reporting a high prevalence of gender-based making them more affected by violence in the workplace [14, 16]. Through the conservation of Resources (COR) theory, which highlights that exposure to stressors leads to a reduction of personal resources, such as coping capacity and social support [17]. Workplace violence may reduce resilience and increase susceptibility to depression, anxiety, PTSD, and suicidal ideation.

In Rwanda, mental health disorders are an increasing concern; a national survey estimates that 20.5% of the population has at least one mental disorder, with major depression (12.0%) and anxiety disorders (8.1%) being the most common [18]. DWs, who often operate in isolation, lack protections, and face high WPV risks, are particularly vulnerable to these mental health issues, including psychological distress, depression, and suicidal behaviours [19–26]. Their vulnerable situations, being isolated without access to support, make them a key population for research into the intersection of WPV and adverse mental health outcomes. Despite their essential role in supporting families and economies [5], DWs’ rights are frequently overlooked, exposing them further to WPV [2, 19, 27]. This exposure can exacerbate mental health problems.

Currently, little is known about the prevalence and types of WPV in Kigali, Rwanda, and how these relate to mental health conditions. This study aims to explore the relationship between WPV and mental health issues, such as depression, suicidal behaviours, anxiety, and PTSD, among domestic workers, providing insights to inform policymakers and employers in developing targeted prevention strategies.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted in Kigali, Rwanda’s capital, in 2023. The city comprises three districts, namely, Kicukiro, Gasabo, and Nyarugenge, with a population of about 1.29 million [28]. It is divided into 35 sectors, 161 cells, and 1,155 villages [28]. Each sector has a health centre providing accessible healthcare services to residents. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline [29] was used while reporting the findings of this study.

Study population and sample size

Our study population was DWs. The study included people aged 18 years and above with more than 3 months of working experience who agreed to participate and provided informed consent. A sample of 384 domestic workers was calculated. This was determined using a standard Kish and Leslie sample size calculation approach based on a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and maximum variability assumptions. The total sample size of 862 was pragmatically used to enhance statistical power and allow subgroup analyses.

Sampling technique and enrolment procedure

Participants were approached by Community health workers in their locality using a convenience sampling method. Willing participants were then invited to come to the nearby hospital, where data collectors conducted interviews. Trained research assistants (RAs) recorded participants’ responses after fully explaining the purpose of the study and the ethical considerations. The survey was anonymous, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time. Each interview session lasted approximately 30–40 min.

Data were collected through interviewer-administered questionnaires using the web-based and smart mobile Kobo Collect [30]. To ensure accuracy of translation, the questionnaire was translated into Kinyarwanda and then back-translated into English by independent bilingual experts. Discrepancies were reviewed and resolved by the research team, and the final version was pretested before use. In addition, RAs received training on standardised administration procedures. Participants who scored highly for depression, suicidality, anxiety, or PTSD were offered immediate counselling support.

Measurement

Socio-demographic variables

Basic socio-demographic characteristics, including age, sex, marital status, educational attainment, age at the start of employment, and years of work experience. We used these variables because prior research indicates they are associated with both exposure to WPV and mental health outcomes.

Workplace violence

WPV was assessed using a method adopted from a previous study conducted in Uganda that examined workplace violence within the last 12 months [31]. Workplace violence was defined as “self-reports of violent acts perpetrated by an employer or adult in a work-related position of authority or by peers at the workplace” [31]. Three forms of abuse at the workplace in the past year were assessed: physical, sexual, and emotional [31]. Respondents were asked to self-report any experience of abuse in the past 12 months (response options: yes/no). For analysis, we generated a binary variable for any WPV (yes to ≥ 1 type of violence) and also reported prevalence by type of abuse.

Depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) [32]. This scale consists of nine items that ask participants how often they have experienced specific symptoms of depression in the past two weeks, on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from “not at all” to “nearly every day.” Scores for each item range from 0 to 3, depending on the response. The total score for the scale ranges from 0 to 27, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. A cutoff of 10 and above was considered for people with major depression [32, 33]. The PHQ-9 has demonstrated good validity and reliability [33–35] across diverse populations, including in sub-Saharan Africa, and showed good internal consistency in our sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.90).

Anxiety symptoms

The generalized anxiety disorder-7 (GAD-7) [36] was used to measure anxiety symptoms in this study. This scale consists of seven items that ask participants to rate how often they have experienced specific symptoms of anxiety in the past two weeks, on a 4-point scale ranging from “not at all” to “nearly every day.“ The total score for the scale ranges from 0 to 21, with a cutoff score of 10 indicating the presence of anxiety [37]. When screening for an anxiety disorder, a recommended cutoff point for referral for further evaluation is 10 or greater [38]. The Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.90.

Post-traumatic stress symptoms

Post-traumatic stress symptoms were measured with the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5) [39]. This tool has 20 items scored from 0 (“not at all”) to 4 (“extremely”), producing a total score ranging from 0 to 80. A cutoff of ≥ 33 was used to indicate probable PTSD [39]. This tool has been widely used, including in Rwanda, among individuals who have experienced various forms of violence and trauma [40–43]. The Cronbach’s α in this study was 0.94.

Suicidal ideation

Suicidal ideation was assessed with four items from the General Health Questionnaire-28 (GHQ-28) that capture suicidal thoughts and behaviors. Items were scored according to the GHQ manual and summed to create a suicidality score, with higher scores indicating greater suicidal ideation [44]. The Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.86.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis

Quantitative data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The chi-square test and t-test were used to assess differences in basic sociodemographic variables between participants who experienced workplace violence and those who did not.

PSM analysis

We used propensity score matching (PSM) analysis in STATA 17 to test the relationship between WPV and the severity of mental health conditions (depression, suicidal ideations, PTSD, and anxiety). We followed the PSM guidelines for the analysis and reporting of the PSM results [45, 46]. We matched the participants who experienced WPV (experienced violence group) and those who did not (control group) for each type of abuse (verbal, physical, sexual, physical and emotional, emotional and sexual abuse, and all types of abuse). To control confounding covariates, we used the propensity score (PS) [47]. We balanced the experienced violence and control groups on the following covariates: age, sex, location/address, marital status, education level, age at the beginning of the work, and years of experience. We used the “tebalance” command in STATA to compare the distribution of the covariates between the experienced violence and control groups, and we assessed the balance using the standard mean difference (SMD). We considered SMD < 0.1 to indicate a good balance between the groups. We estimated the average treatment effect (ATE) of WPV on mental health conditions using the “teffects” command in STATA [48]. All the mental health conditions were added in the model as continuous variables. We used a p-value of 0.05 or less as the significance level for the 95% confidence interval of the ATE. In addition, we used the Bonferroni Correction to address multiple testing and potential alpha inflation. We adjusted the significance level by dividing it by the number of tests performed.

Slightly more than half had completed only primary education (56.1%). Prevalence of WPV

Nearly half of participants (46.6%) reported experiencing at least one form of WPV in the past 12 months. Emotional abuse was the most common form (34.9%), followed by physical abuse (23.3%) and sexual abuse (12.5%). Smaller proportions reported multiple forms of abuse: physical and emotional (15.6%), physical and sexual (8.9%), and emotional and sexual (7.2%). All three types of abuse were reported by 5.5% of participants. DWs who experienced WPV were more likely to have started work earlier and to have fewer years of work experience (Table 1).

Regarding mental health outcomes, the prevalence of depressive symptoms, suicidality, anxiety symptoms, and PTSD were 20%, 32.5%, 20.7%, and 23.5%, respectively.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, 862 participants were involved in the PSM analysis. Results showed a positive association between WPV and Mental health conditions (depression, anxiety, PTSD and suicidality) among Domestic Workers. The prevalence of WPV was 46.6% in the past 12 months, while the prevalence of mental health conditions was 20%, 20.7%, 23.5%, and 32.5% for depression, anxiety, PTSD, and suicidal ideation, respectively.

Several prior studies have reported varying rates of WPV among DWs. For instance, Ho et al. found that 37.5% of Migrant DWs in Asia faced physical abuse [12]. Similarly, Cheung et al. reported that 20.5% of Filipino Migrant DWs in Hong Kong experienced physical abuse, while 34.4% reported verbal abuse within the last year [49]. These findings emphasize the widespread occurrence of violence in domestic work environments. Although the prevalence rates differ across studies and are lower than the findings from the current study, the consistent evidence of high prevalences of abuse demonstrates the vulnerability of DWs to multiple forms of violence.

The results from this study revealed that WPV increased the likelihood of anxiety symptoms, depressive symptoms, and PTSD, which is consistent with previous studies among migrant workers [50–52]. Specifically, in a study among Migrant DWs conducted in Singapore, the exposure to verbal and physical abuse negatively affected mental health and overall well-being [50]. This association is likely because any violence causes stress and emotional strain, which can lead to anxiety, depression, and PTSD [49, 53, 54]. Additionally, many DWs lack social support and may feel isolated, making it harder to cope with abuse [19]. Job insecurity and fear of reporting violence can make the mental health conditions even worse, and repeated abuse can build up over time [12, 54].

The mental health conditions in this study were higher compared to a previous study conducted among the Rwandan [18]. However, similar to our study, this previous study found a high prevalence of mental health disorders among people with a history of trauma and violence.

Our findings can also be interpreted through the lens of the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, which posits that exposure to stressors such as workplace violence leads to a depletion of personal resources, including coping capacity and social support [17], For domestic workers in Rwanda, repeated exposure to verbal, physical, or psychological abuse may reduce resilience and increase susceptibility to depression, anxiety, PTSD, and suicidal ideation.

In this study, the prevalences of depression and anxiety were lower compared to the pooled estimates reported by Hasan et al. (2021) [55]. In contrast, PTSD and suicide were higher compared to previous studies. For example, among Filipino Migrant DWs in China, slightly more than 25% met the criteria for PTSD, while a study done in Singapore reported that 17% of deaths among Migrant DWs were due to suspected suicides [56]. These differences are likely due to multiple factors, including type of abuse, social isolation, long working hours, and precarious employment conditions, all of which increase vulnerability to mental health problems [57].

Limitations

This study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, as the data was collected using a cross-sectional design, it is difficult to establish causal relationships between variables. Future longitudinal studies should be conducted to establish the causality. Secondly, considering the multiple outcomes in this study, there may be a chance for alpha inflation. To control for it, we conducted the Bonferroni Correction and a p value of 0.016 was considered during analysis to evaluate the consistency of associations. Thirdly, our sample only includes participants from Kigali, and as such, the representativeness of the results may not extend to DWs throughout Rwanda. Finally, participant information was derived from self-reporting, especially for workplace violence, which may have introduced a recall and social desirability bias.

Implications and recommendations

This is the first study that evaluates the association between WPV and mental health conditions among DWs in Rwanda. Therefore, it has several implications. These results should be essential information for policymakers to enact and enforce laws that protect DWs from abuse and exploitation, including fair wages, regulated working hours, and safe work conditions. Secondly, the results show the need to expand access to mental health services among DWs. There is a need to implement awareness campaigns to educate DWs and employers about mental health, the legal framework, and available support. Employers should foster a supportive work environment and open communication for concerns or complaints. All DWs should join a peer support network to share experiences, offer mutual support, and develop coping strategies. Lastly, further research are needed including qualitative studies to explore the contextual factors and inform preventive and mitigation measures.

Conclusion

This study provides further evidence of WPV and mental health issues among DWs. The findings show a link between various types of workplace violence and mental health conditions such as anxiety, depression, suicidality, and PTSD. These results highlight the urgent need to implement preventative and mitigation measures to address both WPV and mental health concerns.
