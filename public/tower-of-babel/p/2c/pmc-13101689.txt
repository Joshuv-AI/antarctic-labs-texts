From N-of-1 to versatility in propionic acidemia: Antisense oligonucleotide-mediated skipping of a constitutive PCCA pseudoexon
Authors: Eriko Totsune, Yoichi Wada, Yasuko Mikami-Saito, Natsuko Arai-Ichinoi, Naoya Saijo, Jun Takayama, Yasuhiro Maeda, Yoko Nakajima, Osamu Ohara, Shigeo Kure, Atsuo Kikuchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPropionic acidemia is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in the PCCA or PCCB gene, resulting in deficient propionyl-CoA carboxylase activity. We identified a unique homozygous deep-intronic PCCA variant, NM_000282.4:c.1285-1358C>G, in an individual with neonate-onset propionic acidemia. Fibroblasts from this individual expressed only PCCA mRNA containing an 84-bp pseudoexon, which is present at low levels in healthy controls, leading to the loss of PCCA and PCCB proteins and severely reduced propionyl-CoA carboxylase activity. Transfection of fibroblasts with chemically synthesized antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) designed to skip the pseudoexon restored productive PCCA splicing, rescued PCCA protein expression, and markedly increased propionyl-CoA carboxylase activity above wild-type levels. The efficacy of the ASOs was further evaluated in fibroblasts from 7 additional individuals with propionic acidemia carrying mutations in PCCA or PCCB. ASO treatment successfully restored enzymatic activity, particularly in fibroblast lines, with residual activity exceeding 1% of normal. These findings suggest that ASO-mediated splicing correction targeting the 84-bp pseudoexon can restore mRNA, protein, and enzymatic function in individuals with deep intronic mutations, as well as in other individuals with propionic acidemia, indicating the feasibility of ASO therapy as a molecular treatment strategy for a subset of individuals with propionic acidemia.Keywords: MT: Oligonucleotides: Therapies and Applications, antisense oligonucleotide therapy, splice-switching antisense oligonucleotides, cryptic splicing, propionic acidemia, PCCA, PCCB, pseudoexon, propionyl-CoA carboxylase, PCC enzyme activity

Introduction

Propionic acidemia (PA, MIM #606054) is caused by a biallelic mutation in PCCA or PCCB, which encode the α and β subunits of propionyl-CoA carboxylase (PCC, EC 6.4.1.3), respectively.1 The loss of PCC activity results in the accumulation of propionyl-CoA-derived metabolites and secondary metabolic disturbances, including hyperammonemia, lactic acidosis, and high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis.2,3,4 The disorder severity largely correlates with the degree of residual PCC activity.5,6,7,8,9,10 Thus, a marked reduction in PCC activity results in neonatal-onset PA, the classic and most severe disease form. Neonates with this phenotype typically present with poor feeding, decreased arousal, lethargy, vomiting, and encephalopathy within the first few days of life.2,3,4,11 In contrast, individuals with partial PCC activity and late-onset PA may present with a milder but clinically significant course after infancy.4,12 In both forms, chronic complications include growth impairment, intellectual disability, epilepsy, pancreatitis, cardiomyopathy, chronic kidney disease, and optic nerve atrophy.2,4,11,12,13 In Japan and many other countries, PA is included in newborn screening (NBS) programs, enabling early diagnosis and treatment.3,9,11,14 However, current management strategies, such as dietary therapy, pharmacological interventions, and even liver transplantation, are limited to being primarily supportive.2,11,15,16,17,18,19,20,21,22,23 Therefore, the development of curative therapies for PA is highly anticipated.

Gene therapy, which encompasses gene addition, gene editing, mRNA replacement, and other strategies, offers the potential to mitigate or even cure genetic disorders by directly targeting the underlying disease mechanisms and pathophysiology.24,25,26,27,28,29 In particular, splicing abnormalities have recently emerged as attractive therapeutic targets, as exemplified by the success of nusinersen, an antisense oligonucleotide (ASO)-based therapy.30,31,32,33,34,35,36 ASOs modulate pre-mRNA splicing by binding to specific target sequences. This can promote canonical mRNA formation through pseudoexon skipping or generate truncated yet partially functional proteins by excluding exons with loss-of-function mutations.33,34,37,38,39,40 Furthermore, milasen, an ASO developed for a single child with Batten disease, highlights the remarkable progress in developing personalized ASO therapies for specific individuals.32,41 In the context of PA, an 84-bp pseudoexon (13:100305751-100305834, GRCh38) located in intron 14 of PCCA represents a compelling therapeutic target because it is constitutively expressed even in healthy individuals.33,42,43 This pseudoexon contains two premature termination codons, and the transcripts harboring it are largely degraded via nonsense-mediated decay.33,44,45 Given its substantial contribution to the loss of function, constitutive expression, and mutual stabilization of PCCA and PCCB proteins,46 therapeutic strategies aimed at skipping the pseudoexon are expected to substantially restore PCCA expression and PCC activity. Thus, ASO-mediated pseudoexon skipping is a promising approach.33,38,47 However, two critical questions remain unanswered: (1) whether ASO therapy can be effective even in the most severe forms of PA, such as neonatal-onset disease, and (2) which subsets of individuals with PA are most likely to benefit from ASO therapy.

In this study, we identified and functionally characterized a unique deep-intronic variant (NM_000282.4:c.1285-1358C>G) in an individual with neonatal-onset PA, showing complete inclusion of the 84-bp pseudoexon and near-absence of PCC activity. We designed ASOs targeting the pseudoexon, evaluated their ability to restore productive splicing, and assessed their effects on PCCA and PCCB protein expression and PCC enzymatic activity in fibroblasts derived from the individual. Finally, to explore the potential applicability of this approach to a wider broader population, we tested the ASOs in fibroblasts from additional individuals with PA carrying diverse PCCA or PCCB mutations.

Results

Identification of a rare homozygous deep-intronic variant, not previously implicated in PA, in an individual with neonatal-onset PA

The proband (designated A1) was born at term as the second child of non-consanguineous Japanese parents. On day 6, the individual developed poor feeding and decreased arousal. On the same day, NBS performed on day 4 revealed an elevated propionylcarnitine level of 34 μmol/L (cutoff >3.6 μmol/L) and propionylcarnitine acetylcarnitine ratio of 1.76 (cutoff >0.25), prompting an emergent visit to our hospital. Laboratory tests revealed hyperammonemia (plasma NH3 166 μmol/L), and urinary organic acid analysis showed elevated levels of 3-hydroxypropionate, 2-methylcitrate, and propionylglycine, with no increase in the level of methylmalonic acid. Based on these results, neonatal-onset PA was diagnosed. Targeted genetic panel testing did not reveal any mutations. Whole-genome sequencing identified a homozygous deep-intronic variant, NM_000282.4:c.1285-1358C>G, in intron 14 of PCCA (Figure 1A). Homozygosity was attributed to uniparental disomy, as determined by the polymorphic pattern across chromosome 13. The allele frequency of this variant was extremely low, with values of 0.000002235 in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD, v.4.1.0) and 0.000114 in the Tohoku Medical Megabank Organization (ToMMo, 61KJPN), a large-scale whole-genome sequence database of the Japanese population. Notably, this variant has not been previously reported in ClinVar or the Human Gene Mutation Database. In silico analysis using SpliceAI predicted an acceptor gain at −83 bp (Δscore 0.64) and a donor gain at the variant site (Δscore 0.63), indicating the insertion of an 84-bp pseudoexon.48 An identical pseudoexon insertion resulting from another homozygous deep-intronic mutation, NM_000282.4:c.1285-1416A>G, was reported in an individual with PA.38,47 Thus, the predicted inclusion of the pseudoexon in the NM_000282.4:c.1285-1358C>G variant supports a pathogenic classification.

NM_000282.4:c.1285-1358C>G variant caused aberrant splicing, leading to the insertion of a pseudoexon and resulting in reduced PCC activity

To functionally validate the pathogenicity of the NM_000282.4:c.1285-1358C>G variant, we analyzed skin fibroblasts derived from the affected individual (A1) for splicing patterns, RNA and protein expression, and PCC activity. Reverse-transcription PCR (RT-PCR) revealed two PCCA mRNA isoforms in fibroblasts from a healthy individual (wild-type [WT]), comprising a predominant and a minor transcript, whereas fibroblasts from individual A1 expressed only the minor transcript (Figure 1B). Sanger sequencing confirmed that the shorter transcript corresponded to the canonical splice product, while the longer transcript contained the predicted 84-bp pseudoexon insertion. A previous study using a minigene assay to assess the splicing effect of NM_000282.4:c.1285-1358C>G showed similar results.47 Western blot analysis revealed markedly reduced PCCA protein levels in A1 fibroblasts compared with WT fibroblasts (Figure 1C). PCC activity in A1 fibroblasts also profoundly decreased to 0.5% of that in WT fibroblasts (Figure 1D, Table S1). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the homozygous NM_000282.4:c.1285-1358C>G variant eliminates canonical transcript production and leads to near-complete loss of PCCA protein and PCC function, supporting its pathogenic classification according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines (PVS1+PM2+PM4+PP4).49

ASO-mediated pseudoexon skipping restored canonical splicing and elevated PCC activity in fibroblasts from individual A1

To correct the aberrant splicing caused by the NM_000282.4:c.1285-1358C>G variant, we focused on ASO-mediated exon skipping. ASOs targeting the 84-bp pseudoexon in intron 14 of PCCA have been previously developed for clinical application.38,47 However, according to consensus guidelines, the recommended ASO length is 18–22 nucleotides.32 Therefore, new ASOs were designed following this recommendation using eSkip-Finder,50 and potential off-target effects were evaluated using GGGenome (https://gggenome.dbcls.jp/). Five ASOs were selected, each predicted to achieve >75% exon-skipping efficacy with minimal off-target effects (Figure 2A, Tables S2 and S3). Their binding sites spanned the exon-splicing enhancer region.47

Next, the ASOs were transfected into A1- and WT-derived fibroblasts and splicing correction was assessed via RT-PCR and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). All 5 ASOs successfully shifted toward the correctly spliced isoform in both fibroblast types (Figure 2B). The proportion of pseudoexon-containing transcripts markedly decreased from 100% to 1.06% following ASO treatment (p < 0.0001; Figure S1, Table S4). Western blot analysis confirmed the re-expression of the PCCA protein (Figures 2C and 2D). PCC enzyme activity increased in all ASO-treated fibroblasts, increasing from 0.5% of WT activity to nearly 300% of WT activity (p < 0.0001; Figure 2E, Table S1).

The 84-bp pseudoexon-skipping ASO increased PCC activity in PA fibroblasts, particular those with residual PCC activity

To evaluate whether the effects of the 84-bp pseudoexon-skipping ASO are observed in other individuals in addition to A1, we examined the impact of ASO treatment on PCCA splicing, PCCA and PCCB protein expression, and PCC enzymatic activity in fibroblasts from individuals with PA carrying other PCCA or PCCB mutations (Table 1, Figures 3A and S2). Two 19-nucleotide ASOs were selected for validation: ASO AN_01, predicted to have the highest skipping efficiency by eSkip-Finder, and ASO AN_03, predicted to have the lowest off-target potential based on GGGenome analysis. These ASOs were transfected into 9 fibroblast lines, namely those from 7 individuals with PA, A1, and a WT control. Among them, 5 lines carried mutations in PCCA (A1, A2, A3, A4, and A5) and 3 carried mutations in PCCB (B1, B2, and B3).51,52,53

Overall, ASO treatment promoted skipping of the 84-bp pseudoexon in PCCA transcripts across all PA fibroblast lines. However, increases in protein expression and PCC enzymatic activity were primarily observed in fibroblasts with residual PCC function. At the mRNA level, ASO treatment decreased the pseudoexon-containing PCCA transcript in all PA fibroblast lines, whereas the expression of the correctly spliced transcript increased only in a subset of lines (Figure 3B, Table S5). At the protein levels, PCCA levels showed variable trends following ASO treatment, with increases observed in some fibroblast lines but not all, whereas PCCB protein levels were significantly increased in four fibroblast lines (A1, A5, B1, and WT) (Figure 3C, Table S5). Functionally, PCC activity increased after ASO treatment in 6 fibroblast lines (A2, A5, B1, B2, A1, and WT), whereas little or no change was observed in others (A3, A4, and B3) (Figures 2E and 3D, Table S5). Among the responsive lines, PCC activity increased by approximately 2- to 5-fold. Baseline PCC activity further distinguished responsiveness: lines with >1% of WT at baseline showed a greater increase than those with ≤1% of WT (3.4-fold vs. 1.1-fold change, p = 0.049). Fibroblast lines with biallelic null mutations (A4 and B3) showed little response to ASO treatment. No other relationship was found between PCC activity and genotype.

No off-target effects were observed for ASOs AN_01 and AN_03

To investigate potential off-target effects of the ASOs, RNA-seq was performed and revealed no significant transcriptomic changes in A1 or WT fibroblasts before or after ASO treatment, except for a marked increase in PCCA expression (Figures S3A–S3J, Table S6). Principal-component analysis (PCA), t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE), and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analyses confirmed clear separation between A1 and WT samples, even after ASO treatment (Figures S3K–S3M).

Discussion

In this study, we focused on a unique deep-intronic homozygous mutation in the PCCA gene, NM_000282.4:c.1285-1358C>G, identified in an individual with neonatal-onset PA. Functional analysis of fibroblasts derived from the individual revealed that PCCA mRNA contained an 84-bp pseudoexon derived from part of intron 14, resulting in the complete loss of both PCCA and PCCB proteins and a marked reduction in PCC activity. Our designed ASOs effectively restored PCC function in patient-derived fibroblasts by redirecting splicing from the 84-bp pseudoexon-containing transcript to the canonical isoform. Furthermore, ASO treatment enhanced PCC activity in a subset of fibroblasts obtained from individuals with PCCA or PCCB deficiency who did not carry the c.1285-1358C>G mutation in PCCA. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that splicing-switch ASOs can rescue PCC activity not only in fibroblasts harboring the deep-intronic mutation but also in a subset of fibroblasts from other individuals with PA.

ASO-mediated splicing correction represents a highly effective, molecularly targeted therapeutic strategy for individuals with neonatal-onset PA caused by homozygous NM_000282.4:c.1285-1358C>G. Given that the degree of PCC activity generally correlates with clinical severity,5,6,7,8,9,10 the near-complete absence of PCC activity in fibroblasts from the individual is consistent with the phenotype. Remarkably, ASO treatment restored PCC activity from almost undetectable levels to levels exceeding those in WT controls, indicating its potential to ameliorate the underlying metabolic disturbance in homozygous or even heterozygous carriers of the NM_000282.4:c.1285-1358C>G variant. Considering that liver transplantation, which confers partial systemic augmentation of PCC activity, improves survival, reduces metabolic decompensation, stabilizes neurological symptoms, ameliorates cardiomyopathy, and enhances quality of life,16,17,18,19,20 similar clinical benefits may be achieved through targeted ASO therapy, even if restricted to the liver, via GalNAc conjugation.37,54,55,56 Furthermore, administration of modified ASOs designed to target additional affected organs, including the central nervous system, could extend therapeutic benefits beyond those of liver transplantation. Taken together, the robust restoration of PCC activity observed in fibroblasts from individual A1 strongly suggests that this ASO could provide significant therapeutic benefit in this individual with neonatal-onset PA, as well as in other individuals carrying heterozygous or homozygous NM_000282.4:c.1285-1358C>G variants.

ASOs may provide therapeutic benefits not only for individuals with rare deep-intronic mutations but also for individuals with PA carrying other PCCA or PCCB mutations. Our analysis suggests that a residual PCC activity of 1% defines the threshold for determining the response to ASO treatment. In individuals harboring PCCA mutations, skipping of the ubiquitously expressed 84-bp pseudoexon is expected to increase full-length PCCA mRNA and functional PCCA protein levels, thereby increasing overall PCC abundance and enzymatic activity. Because PCC is a heteromultimeric enzyme composed of PCCA (α subunit) and PCCB (β subunit), proper assembly and mutual stabilization of both subunits are essential for maintaining PCC protein stability and enzymatic activity.46,57 Accordingly, even in individuals with PCCB mutations, increased availability of the normal PCCA subunit is expected to stabilize PCCB and facilitate holoenzyme assembly, potentially leading to secondary increases in total PCC abundance and enzymatic activity. Consistent with this interpretation, ASO treatment was more effective in fibroblasts harboring biallelic missense mutations (A5, B1, and B2) in which both PCCA and PCCB protein expression levels were elevated in parallel with PCC activity. In contrast, fibroblasts with homozygous null mutations (A4 and B3), in which baseline PCCA or PCCB protein expression was minimal, showed no increase in mRNA or protein expression and no corresponding increase in PCC activity following ASO treatment. Moreover, different outcomes were observed in fibroblasts carrying a combination of missense and nonsense mutations (A2 and A3). In A2 fibroblasts, no evident increase in mRNA and protein levels was observed, whereas PCC enzymatic activity increased, suggesting that subtle, undetectable expression changes contributed to relatively preserved residual PCC activity. In contrast, A3 fibroblasts exhibited increased PCCA mRNA levels without a corresponding increase in PCC activity, likely reflecting severely diminished baseline enzymatic function. Changes in protein abundance do not necessarily correlate with functional recovery of the PCC holoenzyme, quantification of PCCA and PCCB protein levels may be less informative than enzymatic activity as a measure of therapeutic efficacy. Taken together, these observations indicate that the therapeutic benefit of ASOs may depend on residual PCCA and PCCB protein stability and PCC enzymatic function, and is therefore strongly influenced by genotype, suggest that the ASO strategy is primarily effective for individuals harboring missense mutations that retain partial PCC activity.

The increases in PCC activity in A2, A5, B1, and B2 were modest compared with A1; nevertheless, even small gains in residual PCC activity may be clinically meaningful in PA. Prior reports suggest that individuals with milder forms generally retain higher PCC enzymatic activity than those with severe forms; however, the difference between mild and severe phenotypes may be only a few percentage points.5,8 In keeping with these findings, very mild forms of PA—often identified through newborn screening in Japan and frequently asymptomatic—retain PCC activity of approximately 3% of WT at the lower end of the range.1,9,10 Conversely, a late-onset PA case with PCC activity of 2.24% of WT, measured using the same assay, has been reported to develop severe cardiomyopathy.58 Together, these observations support the notion that even modest increase in residual PCC activity might shift disease severity toward a milder phenotype. Therefore, the modest PCC activity increases could plausibly translate into clinically relevant benefit for a subset of individuals with PA. These insights have crucial implications for stratification in clinical development, indicating that PCC activity-based selection may help maximize therapeutic benefits. Future studies should systematically define variants that are responsive to ASO-mediated augmentation to enable precision medicine approaches for PA.

Despite the promising findings, this study has some limitations. First, all analyses were performed in vitro using fibroblast models; thus, key in vivo properties of ASOs, such as pharmacokinetics, tissue distribution, immune responses, efficacy, safety, and durability, were not assessed. Evaluation of ASO biodistribution is particularly important because PA is a systemic metabolic disorder.1,37 Extrahepatic tissues implicated in PA include the heart, central nervous system, and kidneys. Although the liver is the primary site of PCC activity and a major therapeutic target, hepatocyte-restricted restoration of enzyme activity may be insufficient to fully correct systemic metabolic dysfunction, as suggested by clinical outcomes following liver transplantation.16,19,21,22,23 Metabolic instability has been reported when dietary protein restriction is not maintained even after liver transplantation, and post-transplant complications, such as metabolic encephalopathy, renal dysfunction, and QT prolongation, have been documented. These findings emphasize the need for ASO delivery strategies that can achieve effective distribution not only in the liver but also in extrahepatic tissues to enable comprehensive metabolic correction.37,54,55,56 Future studies using animal models will be required to evaluate the in vivo efficacy of the ASO-mediated strategy. Although Pcca knockout or hypomorphic mice are commonly used models of PA,59,60,61 the 84-bp pseudoexon inclusion is unlikely to occur in mice (Figure S4),62,63,64 suggesting that a humanized model that recapitulates the human splicing pattern will be necessary. Such a humanized mouse model would be suitable for assessing the systemic efficacy and safety of ASO treatment. Second, a larger panel of fibroblast lines derived from individuals with PA is required to better capture the genetic diversity of PA. Third, although restoration of PCC activity was demonstrated, its correlation with clinical outcomes, including metabolic stabilization and prevention of complications, remains to be established. Finally, individuals carrying biallelic null mutations or other loss-of-function mutations are unlikely to experience substantial recovery of PCC function. Therefore, genotype-specific variability in therapeutic responsiveness should be carefully considered, and alternative or complementary approaches, such as gene therapy, should be explored for not responding individuals with PA.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that ASO-mediated pseudoexon skipping can restore mRNA, protein, and enzyme activity in fibroblasts derived from an individual carrying a unique intronic mutation, as well as in fibroblasts from individuals with PA who retain residual PCC enzymatic activity. These findings suggest that ASO therapy is a promising molecular treatment strategy for individuals with PA and specific genetic backgrounds.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

WT skin fibroblasts from a healthy individual (NB1RGB) were provided by the RIKEN BRC through the National BioResource Project (Tsukuba, Japan). Eight fibroblast lines from individuals with PA were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (MEM) low-glucose (FUJIFILM Wako Chemicals, Osaka, Japan), and WT fibroblasts were cultured in MEMα (FUJIFILM Wako Chemicals). Both media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and the cells were maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

ASOs designed to skip the 84-bp pseudoexon in PCCA

A positive control ASO with the same sequence previously reported to induce skipping of the 84-bp pseudoexon was used (Table S7).38 A non-target ASO was used as a negative control.39 All ASOs were synthesized as phosphorothioate-modified, 2′MOE-modified ASOs and purchased from Nihon Gene Research Laboratories (Miyagi, Japan).

Genetic analysis

Whole-genome sequencing was performed using the DNBSEQ T7 platform (MGI Tech, Guangdong, China) in 150 bp paired-end mode with PCR-free libraries. The sequenced reads were aligned to the GRCh37 human reference genome using BWA-MEM (v.0.7.17-r1188), as previously described.65 Variant calling was performed using Genome Analysis Toolkit software v.4.2.6.1, and annotation was performed using SnpEff (v.5.1) after quality filtering. Intronic variants in PCCA were identified using Integrative Genomics Viewer (v.2.16.2).

RNA extraction

Cells were passaged in 24-well plates (2 cm2/well) at a density of 4 × 104 cells per well 1 day before transfection. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine LTX Reagent with PLUS Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and Opti-MEM I Reduced Serum Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a final ASO concentration of 20 nM. The ASO concentration (20 nM) was selected based on preliminary dose-response experiments in fibroblasts derived from individual A1. Comparable levels of pseudoexon skipping were observed at concentrations of 20 nM or higher (Figure S5). Accordingly, 20 nM was used for all subsequent experiments. At 24 h after ASO transfection, total RNA was extracted from fibroblasts using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) and QIAshredder (QIAGEN), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The RNA concentrations were measured using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

RT-PCR

Equal amounts of total RNA extracted from fibroblasts were reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa Bio, Shiga, Japan) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Primers for PCCA were designed to amplify the region spanning exons 13–17 and those for PCCB were designed to amplify the region spanning exons 10–15 (Table S8). RT-PCR was performed using the KOD One PCR Master Mix (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan) with the following cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 98°C for 1 min, 30 cycles at 98°C for 10 s, 57°C for 5 s, and 68°C for 1 s. The PCR products were analyzed on a 2% Agarose S gel (NIPPON GENE, Tokyo, Japan).

qPCR

cDNA was reverse transcribed from 200 ng of RNA in a 20 μL reaction. The probe for the canonical PCCA mRNA was designed between exons 14 and 15, whereas that for the PCCA mRNA containing the 84-bp pseudoexon was designed to have homology within the 84-bp pseudoexon sequence (Table S8). The multiplex real-time qPCR mixture contained 1 μL of cDNA and TaqMan Multiplex Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific), primer pairs, and two probes. The reaction was performed on a QuantStudio5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the following conditions: polymerase activation step at 95°C for 20 s, followed by 40 cycles of an amplification step at 95°C for 1 s and 60°C for 20 s.

ACTB was used as the housekeeping gene and quantified using PrimeTime Predesigned qPCR Assays (Hs.PT.39a.22214847; Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA, USA). The reaction mixture contained 1 μL of cDNA, Premix Ex Taq (TaKaRa Bio), ACTB qPCR Assays, and ROX Reference Dye II. The qPCR conditions were as follows: polymerase activation step at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of an amplification step at 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s. Data were analyzed using the ΔΔCt method with Design & Analysis 2 Software (Thermo Fisher Scientific), normalized to non-transfected controls. All reactions were performed in triplicate, and technical replicates with abnormal amplification curves were excluded.

RNA-seq

RNA-seq libraries were prepared from poly(A)+ RNA using the xGen Broad-Range RNA Library Prep Kit (Integrated DNA Technologies) and sequenced on a NovaSeq X Plus platform in 150-bp paired-end mode (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Raw reads were preprocessed with fastp (v.0.20.1) to trim adapter sequences and remove low-quality reads and subsequently mapped to the human reference genome (GRCh38/hg38) using STAR (v.2.7.10a). Gene-level read counts were obtained with featureCounts (v.2.1.1). All downstream analyses were conducted in R (v.4.3.2). Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package (v.1.42.1). Low-count genes were filtered prior to median-of-ratio normalization and dispersion estimates were calculated according to the DESeq2 standard pipeline. Pairwise comparisons were performed using the Wald test, and p values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate method. Log2 fold changes (LFCs) were shrunk using the apeglm method. MA and volcano plots were generated using ggplot2 (v.3.5.2), with adjusted p values and LFC cutoffs. PCA was performed on TPM values, and variance-stabilized counts were obtained using the DESeq2 vst() function. t-SNE and UMAP analyses were performed using the Rtsne (v.0.17) and umap (v.0.2.10.0) packages, respectively. The percentage of the 84-bp insertion was calculated based on the number of insertion events observed using IGV (v.2.16.2).41

Protein extraction

Cells were seeded in 24-well plates (2 cm2/well) at a density of 4 × 104 cells per well 1 day before transfection. Transfection was performed with a final ASO concentration of 20 nM as described previously. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, 60 μL of Cell Lysis Reagent (CelLytic M; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) containing protease inhibitor cocktail (P8340; Sigma-Aldrich) was added to each well, and the supernatant was collected following the manufacturer’s protocol. Protein concentration was measured using the Bradford method (Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit; Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a Model 680 Microplate Reader (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Western blot analysis

Equal amounts of each protein were mixed with 4× Laemmli Sample Buffer (Bio-Rad) containing 2-mercaptoethanol (Bio-Rad) and heated at 95°C for 5 min. The prepared samples and a protein marker were loaded onto 4%–15% Mini-PROTEAN TGX Precast Protein Gels (Bio-Rad), and electrophoresed. After electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred onto low fluorescence (LF) PVDF membranes (Trans-Blot Turbo RTA Mini 0.45 μm LF PVDF Transfer Kit; Bio-Rad) using the Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio-Rad). The membranes were then incubated with rabbit polyclonal antibody against PCCA (1:1,000; ab187686; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) or PCCB (1:1,000; 11139-1-AP; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA) as primary antibodies and a mouse monoclonal antibody against β-actin (1:1,000, 66009-1-Ig, Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA) as the loading control. Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (1:20,000; StarBright Blue 700 Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG; Bio-Rad) and Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (1:10,000; StarBright Blue 520 Goat Anti-Mouse IgG; Bio-Rad) were used as secondary antibodies, and sodium dodecyl sulfate (FUJIFILM Wako Chemicals) were added. Protein bands were visualized using a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad).

PCC activity assay

Cells were seeded in 100-mm dishes (55 cm2) at a density of 1 × 106 cells/dish 1 day before transfection. Transfection was performed with a final ASO concentration of 20 nM as described previously. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, the fibroblasts were detached using 0.25% trypsin/EDTA solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific), washed once with phosphate-buffered saline, centrifuged to obtain a cell pellet of 1 × 106 cells, and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen.

PCC enzyme activity was determined via ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) as previously reported.10 The cell pellet was resuspended in K2HPO4 buffer, sonicated, and homogenized. NaHCO3, ATP, and MgCl2 were added, propionyl CaA was added, and the mixture was incubated at 37°C for 30 min. The reaction was stopped with HClO4, and an IS solution was added. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected and analyzed via UHPLC-MS/MS.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and graph generation were performed using GraphPad prism software (v.10.5.0; San Diego, CA, USA). All experiments were performed independently at least thrice (n ≥ 3). Data are presented as the mean with standard error. Statistical significance was defined as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001. Comparisons between groups were evaluated using Welch’s t test or ordinary one-way analysis of variance, with post hoc multiple comparisons performed against the indicated control mean using Dunnett’s test.
