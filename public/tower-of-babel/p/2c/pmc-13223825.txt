Introduction

Germline BRCA1 mutations confer high risk of developing high-grade serous tubal-ovarian cancer (HGSTOC), a lethal cancer arising principally from the fallopian tube epithelium (FTE) [1,2]. Multiple studies have shown that BRCA1 deficiency results in increased proinflammatory NFκB signaling [[3], [4], [5], [6]], which has a well-recognized role in cancer initiation and progression (reviewed in [7]). This increase in proinflammatory signaling likely results from genomic instability [8,9] as BRCA1 acts to ensure high-fidelity DNA double-strand (ds) break repair, protect stalled replication forks, and facilitate proper mitotic spindle assembly and chromosomal segregation [[10], [11], [12]]. Consequently, BRCA1 deficiency can lead to DNA repair errors and mutagenesis, as well as genomic instability and generation of chromosomal fragments. These fragments can become incorporated into micronuclei [13], which are prone to rupture. The subsequent release of dsDNA into the cytosol can activate pattern recognition receptors (PRR) to drive downstream NFκB as part of the innate immune response. Indeed, elevated activation of cGAS-STING in BRCA1-deficient breast and ovarian cancer cells has been correlated with increased NFκB-mediated proinflammatory signaling [4,14,15].

In addition to NFκB, stimulation of cGAS-STING leads to activation of Interferon Regulatory Factor 3 (IRF3), a key transcription factor triggering expression of type-I interferons (IFN-I). While interferons are often recognized for anti-tumorigenic effects, unresolved, chronic, low level IFN-I signaling can promote malignant transformation and tumor progression [16,17]. Few studies have addressed IFN-I signaling in the fallopian tube epithelium in the context of predisposition to HGSTOC. However, increased levels of IFN-I-stimulated genes have been demonstrated in BRCA1-deficient HGSTOC, and more recently in serous tubal intraepithelial carcinoma (STIC) lesions, a carcinoma in situ precursor of HGSTOC [4,18,19]. Consistent with these reports, we have found increased levels of IFN-I-stimulated gene transcripts in primary FTE cells derived from BRCA1 mutation carriers as compared to control patients. These genes include MX1, OAS1, IRF3, IRF7, ISG15, and members of the ISGylation pathway including USP18 [3]. ISGylation is a ubiquitin-like posttranslational modification that alters the activity of its target proteins. Recent studies have indicated that ISG15 and ISGylation modulate innate immune signaling [[20], [21], [22], [23]], and we demonstrated that ISGylation enhances proinflammatory IFN-I signaling through the activation of dsRNA sensing-RIG-I or -MDA5 in non-malignant FTE cells [24]. Gene transcript levels of RIGI-like dsRNA sensing PRRs are also increased in BRCA1-mutated FTE cells and BRCA1-null HGSTOC cells compared to cells with functional BRCA1 [3,4]. Studies have established a link between BRCA1 and cytosolic dsRNA, where BRCA1 acts to inhibit RNA polymerase III, RNA loop formation and DNA:RNA hybrid formation [25,26]. Thus, BRCA1 deficiency may activate proinflammatory signaling via activation of additional PRRs.

Chronic low-grade proinflammatory signaling can lead to cancer initiation through multiple mechanisms. Activation of type-I IFN and NFκB signaling can promote stemness [7,27]. Moreover, the increased expression of downstream interferon-stimulated genes, including ISG15 and USP18, can confer resistance to DNA-damage induced cell death and give survival advantage to certain clones in a cell population [28,29]. This could highlight the underlying mechanism by which loss of BRCA1 and an eventual loss of TP53 function accelerates cancer initiation of HGSTOC. In this study, we determined the impact of disruption of BRCA1 using CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing on activation of IFN-I and NFκB signaling in an immortalized FTE cell line. We provide evidence that BRCA1 disruption preferentially enhances proinflammatory IFN-I signaling through increased PRR signaling that requires intact NFκB signaling.

Methods

Cell lines

Human immortalized non-malignant fallopian tube epithelial cell lines, FTE-194 and FTE-246 (both obtained from Dr. R. Drapkin; University of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia) and OE-E6/7 cells (obtained from Dr. W.S.B. Leung; University of Hong Kong, China) were grown in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10 % of heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, 100 U/ml of penicillin and 100 μg/ml of streptomycin (all from Life Technologies, Waltham, MA). Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 5 % CO2. Short tandem repeat analysis confirmed female origin of all 3 cell lines. Cells were routinely checked for mycoplasma and were used up to passage number 25.

Treatment reagents

NFκB inhibitor, 2-Amino-6-[2-(cyclopropylmethoxy)-6-hydroxyphenyl]−4-(4-piperidinyl)-3-pyridinecarbonitrile (ACHP; Tocris, Minneapolis, MN) was dissolved in DMSO to create a 20 mM stock solution. STING inhibitor, H-151 (Cayman chemicals, Cedarlane, Burlington, ON) and the Tank-Binding Kinase 1 (TBK1) inhibitor, GSK8612 (Millipore SIGMA, Oakville, ON), were dissolved in DMSO to a stock concentration of 10.74 mM and 10 mM respectively. Anifrolumab (AstraZeneca, Mississauga, ON), a type-I IFN receptor 1 (IFNAR1) blocking antibody, was dissolved in PBS at a stock concentration of 150 mg/ml. Recombinant human IFNβ (R&D, Minneapolis, MN) was dissolved in sterile water to a stock concentration of 56,000 IU/μl. Olaparib (AZD2281; Selleckchem, Cedarlane), a PARP inhibitor (PARPi), was dissolved in DMSO to a stock concentration of 5 mM. dsDN90 (4 μM) was generated by annealing 90 bp single-stranded sense and antisense DNA strands using sequences described by Abe and Barber [30]. Stock solutions were diluted in culture medium to achieve final concentrations indicated for each experiment.

CRISPR-Cas9 generated disruption of BRCA1

BRCA1 was disrupted in FTE-194 cells using a double-cut CRISPR-Cas9 approach that introduced two double-strand breaks within exon 11 to generate a deletion and frameshift resulting in an early stop codon upstream of key functional domains of the BRCA1 protein. The two guide RNA (gRNA) specific targeting sequences listed in Supplemental Table S1 were identified using CRISPOR (https://crispor.gi.ucsc.edu/) [31]. The primers selected had MIT scores of 90 and 94 (recommended >50). The MIT score is a measure of the uniqueness of the sequence in the genome. The selected primers also had high CFD (cutting frequency determination) scores of 94 and 95. This score also can range from 0 to 100 with high scores (80-100) being indicative of high specificity and efficiency primers that are less likely to cause off-target effects. The primers selected were not predicted to have off-targets within exons and all off-targets predicted had CFD scores of <0.2. The gRNAs were inserted into the CRISPR-Cas9 expression vector pSpCas9(BB)-2A-GFP (plasmid 48138; Addgene, Watertown, MA) and the constructs were sequence verified. FTE-194 cells were transfected with both constructs using Lipofectamine 2000TM (Life Technologies) and GFP-positive cells were isolated using flow cytometry with individual cells placed into 96-well plates for clonal expansion. Clones were screened for the deletion of 1,537 bp from exon 11 using PCR and gel electrophoresis (Supplemental Fig. S1A to E). Sequences incorporating the targeted region of exon 11 were amplified with Taq Polymerase (Life Technologies) using unique sets of nested and semi-nested primers (Supplemental Table S1) and the amplicons were sequenced. PCR cycling conditions, following an initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min, were 95°C for 30 sec followed by 63°C for 30 sec and 72°C for 60 sec for 40 cycles, and a final extension at 72°C for 15 min. Disruption of BRCA1 was also verified by western blot analysis (Supplemental Fig. S1F).

siRNA transfection

siRNA targeting BRCA1 was purchased from Dharmacon (Lafayette, CO). Cells were seeded into 6-well plates at 400,000 cells/well 24 h prior to transfection. Cells were transfected with siRNA targeting BRCA1 (20 nM; #J-003461-11) using Lipofectamine 2000TM. Control cells were transfected with an equivalent amount of non-targeting siRNA (On-TARGET plus control siRNA; #D-001810-02). siRNA amount and duration of treatment were previously optimized using western blot analysis [32].

Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total cellular RNA was extracted using RNeasy Plus Universal Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD) and reverse transcribed using a SuperScriptTM VILOTM cDNA synthesis Kit (Life Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The qPCR reaction was performed on a CFX OPUS 96 Real-Time PCR system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Bio-Rad) and primers listed in Supplemental Table S2. Cycling conditions, following an initial denaturation at 95°C for 10 sec, were 95°C for 15 sec followed by 60°C for 60 sec for 40 cycles. A dissociation curve was performed at the end of the reaction to confirm amplification of a single product. The relative transcript levels of the target gene were measured using the ΔCCT method that involves normalizing the CT value of the target gene to the geometric mean of housekeeping genes (GAPDH and YWHAZ) as described [33,34].

Western blotting

Cells were lysed using radio immunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA; 50 mM HEPES or 50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1 % Triton-X-100, 0.1 % SDS, 0.5-1 % sodium deoxycholate) supplemented with Complete® protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Roche Diagnostics, Laval, QC). After 30 min incubation on ice, cells were centrifuged at 9,000-17,750 g for 10-15 min at 4°C. Total protein content was quantified using a Pierce Protein BCA assay kit with BSA as standard (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) according to manufacturer’s guidelines. SDS-PAGE (6-12 %) was performed to separate extracted protein. Resolved proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes (BioRad) and the membranes were blocked and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibody (listed in Supplemental Table S3). The membranes were washed twice with either PBS-T (PBS with 0.1 % (v/v) Tween 20) or TBS-T (TBS with 0.1 % (v/v) Tween 20) at room temperature and then incubated for 1 h at room temperature with corresponding secondary antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (goat anti-mouse or goat anti-rabbit; both at 1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA). Immunoreactive bands were detected by enhanced chemiluminescence (Bio-Rad), and densitometric analysis was performed using Image lab (Bio-Rad).

Culture growth assay

Cell culture growth was assessed using the XTT dye reduction assay to indicate relative viable cell numbers. Cells were seeded at a concentration of 2,500 cells/well into 96-well plates and relative viable cell levels were assessed at 0 h and 72 h. XTT (Life Technologies) was added to a final concentration of 50 μg/ml and cells were incubated for 3 h at 37°C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nM in a Tecan model Infinite M PLEX plate reader.

Cell migration

Cell migration was determined by a scratch-wound assay. Cells were seeded at high density (180,000 cells/well) into 24-well plates. Once confluent, a scratch was made in the monolayer using a sterile 1 ml pipette tip. The wells were washed twice with PBS and fresh medium without FBS supplementation was added. Photographs at the time of wounding (T = 0 h) and 24 h later (T = 24 h) were taken using an inverted microscope. The gap between the wound edges at both time points was measured using Image J software. The percent closed wound distance was calculated as [(distance measured between wound edges at T = 0 h – distance at T = 24 h)/distance at T = 0 h] x 100.

Spheroid formation

Cells were seeded at a density of 2,500 cells/well in 96-well low-attachment tissue culture plates (Corning, Tewksbury, MA) in medium supplemented with 10 % FBS. Spheroids were photographed 24 h and 96 h later using an inverted microscope. The 2D-surface area (μm2) of the spheroids was measured using Image J software.

RAD51 foci formation

Immunofluorescence was performed as described in Sherker et al. [35]. Cells were seeded in 8-well chamber slides and 24 h later were treated with vehicle (DMSO) or 1 μM Olaparib for 19 h. Cells were incubated with 10 μM EdU (Click-iT EdU imaging kit; Life Technologies) 20 min before fixation. Medium was aspirated and cells were washed with ice-cold PBS. Nuclei were extracted using ice-cold nuclear pre-extraction Buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH7.4, 20 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.5 % NP-40, containing 1 mM DTT and protease inhibitors) for 10 min and then washed with PBS. Cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 10 min, washed in PBS (3 × 5 min) and blocked in 5 % BSA in PBS containing 0.2 % Tween-20 for 30 min at room temperature. The slides were then incubated for 2 h at room temperature with goat anti-rabbit RAD51 antibody (BioAcademia, catalog number 70-001; 1:10,000 dilution). The cells were then washed in PBS containing 0.2 % Tween-20 (3 × 5 min), incubated for 1 h at room temperature with anti-rabbit Alexa-Fluor 488 secondary antibody (Invitrogen) and washed twice with PBS containing 0.2 % Tween-20 (2 × 5 min) and once with PBS. The cells were then fixed again in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 5 min, washed in PBS (3 × 5 min), stained with EdU buffer containing Alexa Fluor 594-azide (Click-iT reaction cocktail) for 30 min and washed in PBS (3 × 5 min). The slides were mounted with Fluoroshield containing DAPI (SIGMA) and visualized by confocal microscopy. Images were captured using a Leica SP8 inverted confocal microscope with 40X and 63X objectives. The quantification of RAD51 foci containing cells is based on the number of EdU positive cells (40X objective) containing 5 or more RAD51 foci and was performed using Image J software. Approximately 100 EdU positive cells for each condition in each of 3 independent experiments were assessed.

In vitro clonogenic assay

Cells were seeded in 24- or 6-well plates at a density of 100 or 500 cells per well, respectively, and treated 24 h later with 1 μM Olaparib or DMSO vehicle. Culture medium was changed 19 h after treatment with Olaparib and cells were maintained in culture for 6 to 9 days. Cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 20 min and stained with 0.05 % crystal violet (SIGMA) for 30 min. Images were captured using a ChemiDoc Imaging system (Bio-Rad Laboratories) and the number of colonies with 50 or more cells were quantified using Image J software.

Statistical analysis

Data from at least three independent experiments are expressed as the mean ± SEM. One-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Holm-Sidak multiple comparison tests were used, as indicated in the figure legends, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant (GraphPad Prism, version 9.2.0). A Grubbs’ test was performed to identify outliers.

Results

We initially examined the levels of endogenous BRCA1 protein in three immortalized FTE cell lines, OE-E6/7, FTE-194, and FTE-246. Of these, FTE-194 cells were found to express robust levels of BRCA1 whereas both FTE-246 and OE-E6/7 cells were found to have 75 to 90 % less protein (Fig. 1A). Many pathological mutations of BRCA1 lie within the centrally located exon 11 and include frameshift mutations that result in the absence of the downstream serine cluster domain that is activated by DNA damage, and the BRCT domain involved in DNA repair, transcription regulation, and tumor suppressor function. Additionally, exon 11 encodes for the coiled-coil domain and nuclear location signals of BRCA1. Exon 11 of BRCA1 was therefore targeted using a double CRISPR-Cas9 approach for deletion of 1,537 bp resulting in a frameshift and early stop codon (Fig. 1B). Amplification of the genomic region using various PCR combinations followed by sequencing indicated generation of homozygous-null and heterozygous cells (Supplemental Fig. S1A-E). Three clones were selected for further study. FTE-194ΔBRc33 was determined to be homozygous null (BRCA1-/-): one allele possessed the expected deletion with a premature stop codon downstream of the 3′-gRNA cut site and the other allele possessed an insert at the 5′-gRNA cut site, resulting in a premature stop codon. FTE-194ΔBRc29 and FTE-194ΔBRc16 were determined to be heterozygous. FTE-194ΔBRc29 and FTE-194ΔBRc16 cells each contained one intact allele and one allele with the expected deletion and downstream stop codon. Western blotting indicated complete absence of BRCA1 protein in FTE-194ΔBRc33 (BRCA1-/-) cells whereas FTE-194ΔBRc16 (BRCA1+/-p) cells showed full-length protein but at a markedly reduced level compared to parental cells (p=protein; Fig. 1C and Supplemental Fig. S1F). BRCA1 protein was not detectable by western blotting in FTE-194ΔBRc29 cells (BRCA1+/-np; np = no protein).

BRCA1-/- and BRCA1+/-np cells exhibited decreased culture growth compared to parental and BRCA1+/-p cells as indicated by an XTT dye-reduction assay (Fig. 2A). All three of these BRCA1-deficient cell sublines showed decreased migration in a scratch-wound assay (Fig. 2B). Consistent with previous studies [24,36], FTE-194 parental cells readily form tight spheroids (Fig. 2C). The level of functional BRCA1 in the cells was assessed using a RAD51 foci formation assay [37]. Following BRCA1 localization to double-strand DNA breaks, RAD51 recombinase is recruited to the site and acts to procure template for homologous repair. In the absence of BRCA1, RAD51 recruitment is impaired. Cells were treated with 1 μM Olaparib or vehicle to induce double-strand DNA breaks and nuclei in S phase were examined for formation of RAD51 foci by immunofluorescence. In the absence of treatment, there was no difference in the percentage of cells in S-phase positive for RAD51 foci (Fig. 2D. Olaparib treatment increased foci formation, but only in BRCA1+/+ and BRCA1+/-p cells (Fig. 2D). RAD51 foci were not increased in BRCA1+/-np or BRCA1-/- cells after Olaparib treatment.

An in vitro colony formation assay was conducted to further examine potential residual BRCA1 protein function in these cells. BRCA1-deficient cells are sensitive to PARP inhibition and exhibit a decreased number of colonies. Cells were treated overnight with 1 μM Olaparib or vehicle and the number of colonies formed was quantified. In the absence of Olaparib treatment, a similar number of colonies were formed across the 4 cell sublines. Olaparib treatment of BRCA1+/+ and BRCA1+/-p cells resulted in a modest, approximately 20 %, decrease in the number of colonies formed as compared to vehicle-treated cells. In contrast, Olaparib treatment of BRCA1-/- and BRCA1+/-np cells decreased the number of colonies formed by >75 % (Fig. 2E), a finding consistent with loss of BRCA1 protein.

BRCA1-deficiency results in increased IFN-I signaling

Expression of IFN-I is induced by IRF3 that is activated downstream of PPRs. All three BRCA1-deficient cell sublines exhibited phosphorylation of IRF3 (p-IRF3) with little to no p-IRF3 detected in parental cells (Fig. 3A). Levels of phosphorylated STAT2 (p-STAT2) were also increased in BRCA1-deficient cell sublines (Fig. 3B) relative to parental cells, indicating the presence of elevated levels of type-I IFNs. PRRs also act to increase NFκB signaling. BRCA1+/-np cells exhibited higher levels of phosphorylated RELA (p-RELA), reflecting activation of canonical NFκB signaling; however, neither BRCA1-/- nor BRCA1+/-p cells consistently exhibited increased p-RELA levels (Fig. 3C). None of the three BRCA1-deficient cell sublines displayed increased activation of the non-canonical NFκB pathway as evidenced by levels of p52. Consistent with increased p-IRF3 levels, transcript levels of selected interferon-stimulated genes ISG15, IRF7, and OAS2 as well as ISG15 and USP18 protein levels were elevated in all three BRCA1-deficient cell sublines (Fig. 4A and B). Discussion

Increased NFκB signaling in BRCA1-deficient breast and ovarian cancer cells has been previously demonstrated, with some studies linking this increase in proinflammatory signaling to activation of the dsDNA-sensing cGAS-STING pathway [4,5,15,44]. Additionally, Kader et al. [18] recently employed spatial RNA sequencing to characterize molecular changes during transition from FTE to HGSTOC and found evidence of increased cGAS activation and IFN-I-stimulated gene expression in STIC lesions. This evidence highlights the potential involvement of this signaling pathway, and IFN signaling in particular, as an early event in HGSTOC progression.

STIC lesions are thought to form from persistent tracts of secretory FTE cells bearing p53 mutations (p53 signatures), and eventually progress to an invasive phenotype that simultaneously seed the ovary and nearby pelvic structures. STIC lesions from patients with HGSTOC have been shown to harbor somatic missense and nonsense mutations in TP53 identical to that found in ovarian tumors [19]. While an increased number of STIC lesions have been described in fallopian tubes from BRCA1 mutation carriers as compared to the general population [45,46], no differences appear to exist in p53 signatures [45], indicating a role for BRCA1 in mitigating the transition from p53 signatures to STIC lesions. The FTE-194 cells used in this study were derived from non-malignant fallopian tube epithelium and were immortalized using stable expression of SV40 large T antigen and hTERT. Inactivation of p53 and pRB is an integral consequence of SV40 large T antigen expression, as originally described by Drapkin and colleagues, who established this cell line [47]. In this regard, FTE-194 likely reflect cells within p53 signatures or STIC lesions.

In previous work, we found that primary non-malignant FTE cells derived from BRCA1 mutation carriers exposed to peri‑ovulatory follicular fluid exhibited upregulation of multiple IFN-I-stimulated genes as compared to cells derived from non-mutation carriers, suggestive of an increased IFN response [24]. However, many of these genes, including ISG15 and USP18, were also elevated in cells prior to follicular fluid exposure. Moreover, overexpression of BRCA1 in OE-E6/7 cells decreased ISG15 and USP18 protein levels [24]. In the current study, we extend these findings by demonstrating that loss of BRCA1 in FTE-194 cells leads to increased levels of activated IRF3 and STAT2, and increased levels of several IFN-I-stimulated gene transcripts and proteins. These include ISG15 and USP18, and an increase in ISGylation. This upregulation is consistent with chronic autocrine IFN-I signaling and is blocked by inhibition of IFNAR. Additionally, the upregulation is dependent upon TBK1, which mediates activation of IRF3 by STING as well as other PRRs.

We have previously reported increased ISGylation despite elevated levels of USP18 [3,24,48]. USP18 serves additional roles in IFN-I signaling beyond its canonical deconjugase activity and its role as a negative regulator of IFN-I signaling. USP18 has been shown to enhance STING-mediated IFN-I response by recruiting USP20 that acts to stabilize STING [49]. Similarly, USP18 has also been shown to facilitate dsRNA-sensing RIG-I-like receptor IFN-I responses by mediating the polyubiquitination and aggregation of MAVS, a key downstream adaptor of RIG-I and MDA5 [50]. These studies suggest that USP18 could increase IFN-I production and signaling by enhancing dsDNA-or dsRNA-sensing, while also acting to limit excessive IFN-I activation. USP18 knockout studies in BRCA1-deficient FTE cells are warranted to clarify its function in IFN-I signaling.

An interesting finding in this study was that ISG15 and USP18 levels were not co-regulated as a consequence of BRCA1 deficiency. While either IFNAR or TBK1 inhibition markedly decreased both interferon-stimulated gene proteins, STING inhibition by H-151 reduced USP18 but not ISG15 protein levels. This uncoupling may reflect differences in canonical and non-canonical activation of STING that impact its downstream signaling. Canonical activation of STING by cGAS detection of cytoplasmic dsDNA results in STING palmitoylation, aggregation, and migration from the endoplasmic reticulum to the Golgi apparatus, where it recruits both TBK1 and IRF3. TBK1 then phosphorylates both IRF3 and STING. STING activated through this pathway is also able to activate non-canonical NFκB signaling [51]. In contrast, non-canonical activation of STING is triggered by DNA damage-induced formation of a complex consisting of ATM, PARP1, TRAF6, IFI16, and p53. As part of this complex, the E3-ligase TRAF6 ubiquitinates STING in a K63 linkage, which preferentially activates canonical NFκB signaling and acts independent of TBK1 and IRF3 activation (Fig. 8) [52]. H-151 exerts its inhibitory actions by covalently binding to STING and obstructing cysteine residue C91 palmitoylation, thereby preventing the interaction of STING with TBK1 [53]. Thus, non-canonical activation of STING and downstream activation of NFκB signaling may remain intact in H-151-treated cells. Alternatively, additional PRRs may contribute to activation of the IFN signaling via the TBK1-IRF3 pathway. The promoter of ISG15 contains an interferon-stimulated response element (ISRE), indicating the gene may be a direct target of IRF3 [54]. Thus, IRF3 activation by the other PRRs that come into play when STING is inhibited may be sufficient to maintain elevated ISG15 levels. In addition to the dsDNA-sensing cGAS-STING pathway, cytosolic dsRNA-sensing MDA5 and RIG-I pathways converge on TBK1 to activate downstream IFN-I signaling. We found that protein levels of interferon-stimulated genes MDA5 and RIG-I were increased in BRCA1-deficient cells. Formation of micronuclei, that can give rise to cytosolic dsRNA structures, have been demonstrated in BRCA1-null UWB1-289 HGSTOC cells [4]. Moreover, these cells have increased transcript levels of RIG-I-like PRRs as compared to cells transfected with wild-type BRCA1 [4].

Inhibition of STING, TBK1, NFκB or IFNAR by H-151, GSK8612, ACHP, and Anifrolumab respectively, abolished p-STAT2 levels (Supplementary Fig. S4). This provides further evidence of the role of PRRs and NFκB in stimulating low-grade IFN-I signaling in BRCA1-deficient cells. Despite the clear evidence of increased IFN-I signaling (increased p-STAT2 and transcript or protein levels of multiple IFN-stimulated genes), we were unable to detect increased levels of IFNβ in BRCA1-deficient cells by qPCR. IFN-I consists of 16 members in addition to IFNβ. These include 13 IFNα members, IFNε, IFNκ, and IFNω, all of which signal through a common IFNAR [56]. IFNε, which is not increased by PRR activation, has been reported to be constitutively expressed in FTE cells and down-regulated in STIC lesions and HGSTOC [18,57]. However, we detected little or no IFNε transcripts by qPCR (Ct values >35; data not shown) in FTE-194 parental and BRCA1-deficient cells in preliminary experiments. Thus, a comprehensive assessment of IFN-I levels is needed to reveal which IFN-Is drive the increase in signaling.

Another possibility is the activation of alternative dsDNA sensors, such as IFI205, in addition to cGAS-STING. A recent study by Wang et al. [17] has shown that IFI205 is associated with DNA damage response (DDR)-mediated stress, including replication fork instability or disrupted DNA replication following oncogene inactivation in mammary epithelial cells. Stalled replication forks and defects in DNA replication can also result from BRCA1 inactivation. Moreover, the cGAS/STING pathway or IFI205 may act to prime the MAVS-dependent dsRNA-sensors MDA5 and RIG-I, which upon reaching an optimal stability in a cell, drive the low chronic IFN response, as demonstrated in immune and other cells [58]. Studies have found that STING most likely interacts with RIG-I and MAVS, and transmits RIG-I-mediated downstream signals to drive IFN responses [38].

Surprisingly, our experiments did not demonstrate consistent increased activation of canonical or non-canonical NFκB signaling in BRCA1-deficient FTE cells. The weak and transient nature of NFκB activation may account for both the difficulty in detecting constitutive activation and the variability we observed in BRCA1-deficient FTE cells. This variability could stem from the negative regulation of NFκB by IRF3, IRF7, and USP18, that restrain excessive or undue activation of NFκB signaling [[59], [60], [61]]. This basal signaling might have masked the extent of further NFκB activation induced upon BRCA1 deficiency.

We found that inhibition of NFκB signaling using ACHP effectively inhibited the increase in ISG15 and USP18 as well as suppressed p-STAT2, indicating a requirement for intact NFκB signaling. These findings suggest that NFκB may play more of a permissive rather than primary role, with IRF3/IRF7/STAT2 serving as main drivers of the increased IFN-I response. This is consistent with previous studies that showed NFκB helps maintain the IFNβ promotor in an open chromatin configuration, which facilitates recruitment of important activators such as IRF3/IRF7 that are induced upon PRR activation, to elicit a robust IFN-I response (reviewed in [62]). This interpretation is also supported by our findings that ACHP treatment decreased p-STAT2 levels induced upon BRCA1 deficiency in FTE cells, but had no impact on p-STAT2 levels when parental FTE cells were exogenously treated with IFNβ. This further supports that NFκB acts upstream of IFNAR-JAK-STAT signaling axis. Overall, these findings suggest that NFκB is necessary, but it is not the main driver of proinflammatory IFN-I signaling in BRCA1-deficient FTE cells.

Our data demonstrate that BRCA1 disruption leads to preferential activation of IFN-I signaling in non-malignant FTE cells. This is in part reflected by increased IRF3 and STAT2 phosphorylation and upregulation of downstream ISGs, including ISG15, USP18, OAS2, IRF7, RIG-I, and MDA5. Phosphorylation of IRF3 is a crucial immediate downstream event following the activation of PRRs, serving as a key activator of IFN-I signaling [63]. Upon activation, IRF3 together with NFκB induce small amounts of IFN-I, a phase often referred to as the priming or first stage of IFN signaling [64]. Upon secretion, IFN-I binds to IFNAR receptors, where IRF7 plays a pivotal role in inducing the transcription of downstream IFN-I-stimulated genes including ISG15, USP18, and OAS2, also referred to as late-phase or second stage of IFN signaling [65]. The binding of IFN-I to IFNAR results in activation of STAT1 and STAT2 by JAK1, that combine with IRF9 to induce synthesis of IRF7 [66,67]. Therefore, the increased transcript levels of IRF7 and the induction of interferon-stimulated genes provide additional evidence that IFN-I signaling is preferentially activated upon BRCA1 disruption in FTE cells. This is supported by our previous work as well as studies using HGSTOC cells. We previously found that these late-phase interferon-stimulated genes and IRF7 transcript levels were upregulated in primary FTE cells from BRCA1 mutation carriers following follicular fluid exposure [3]. These genes were also found to be upregulated in unstimulated BRCA1-mutated HGSTOC cells, and further enhanced in dsDNA-stimulated cells [4]. The amplified IFN response in dsDNA-stimulated BRCA1-mutated HGSTOC cells was linked to NFκB signaling. However, the investigators did not address which signaling pathway/s mediate autocrine IFN-I responses in unstimulated cells, nor did they use specific inhibitors to confirm the involvement of NFκB in dsDNA-stimulated BRCA1-mutated HGSTOC cells.

Type-I IFNs have been shown to have both tumor inhibiting and promoting actions that are dependent upon the duration and strength of IFN-I signaling [28]. Chronic low-level IFN signaling has been shown to promote cell survival and resistance towards external environmental stressors by maintaining a steady-state of upregulated key interferon-stimulated genes [68,69]. Among these ISGs, ISG15 and USP18 establish a feedforward loop through modulation of PRRs or their downstream effectors. We have previously demonstrated that ISGylation amplifies dsRNA-sensing RIG-I/MDA5-induced proinflammatory signaling in FTE cells [24]. Consistent with this, USP18 has been shown to interact with MAVS, an essential downstream adaptor of RIG-I/MDA5 pathway, and upregulates IFN-I response [50]. This ISG-driven positive feedback loop, in turn, helps maintain their steady state while sustaining low levels of IFN-I signaling. Additionally, ISG15 has been demonstrated to rescue BRCA1-deficient cells from deleterious effects of acute exposure to IFNβ [70], and other IFN-I-stimulated genes, such as OAS family members, have been demonstrated to promote cell survival upon DNA damaging conditions [71]. This DNA damage-mediated upregulation of ISG15 and USP18 is part of an interferon-related DNA damage resistance signature that might enable these cells to endure stress. While majority of cells likely succumb to the DNA damage, a subset may survive, undergo transformation and eventually give rise to early STIC. Because this transformation unfolds gradually over many years, replicating this process in vitro and in vivo remains an immense challenge.

Our findings on the inhibitory effect of BRCA1 disruption on cell migration, spheroid forming capacity, and cell culture growth in non-malignant FTE cells align with several studies on HGSTOC cancer cells and breast epithelial cells. In human immortalized mammary epithelial cells, BRCA1 deletion inhibited cell cycle progression, which was restored by overexpression of BRCA1 [72]. Similarly, the absence of functional BRCA1 in UWB1.289 cells reduced cell motility compared to UWB1.289 cells with stable transfection of BRCA1cDNA [73]. BRCA1 knockdown also inhibited cell-cell adhesion or clonogenic capacity in non-malignant mammary epithelial cells, likely through its role in spindle positioning, which maintains the cell division axis and prevents arbitrary orientation of division [74]. However, increased cell proliferation, motility, and invasion of BRCA1-deficient mammary epithelial cells has also been reported [72,75], suggesting that these effects may be cell-context dependent [76].

Study limitations

The results presented in this study were limited to in vitro experiments with an immortalized human FTE cell line. Few such euploid cell lines are available and we selected FTE-194 cells to manipulate BRCA1 levels as these cells exhibited the greatest levels of endogenous BRCA1. Three independent edited clones were used, with two clones heterozygous for an edited BRCA1 allele. In one of these clones, we were able to detect a low level of BRCA1 protein; however, no protein was detected in the other heterozygous clone, raising the possibility of epigenetic silencing of the unedited allele. Similar to BRCA1-/- cells, induction of double-stranded DNA breaks by PARPi treatment failed to increase RAD51 foci formation in BRCA1+/-np cells, a marker for homologous recombination repair. Likewise, the results of our clonogenicity assay are consistent with functional BRCA1 activity in BRCA1+/-p cells but not in BRCA1+/-np or BRCA1-/- cells. Based on these results, we postulate that the unedited allele in BRCA1+/-np cells may have been epigenetically silenced. Further characterization of these cells would be required to confirm this interpretation. Nonetheless, the data clearly demonstrate a consistently increased level of type-I IFN signaling upon loss of BRCA1 that could contribute to the predilection for malignant transformation. Although our experiments clearly indicate increased IFN-I activity (increased IRF3 phosphorylation (activation), which directly stimulates IFN-I production to result in STAT2 activation, a hallmark of IFN-I signaling), we did not detect increased transcript levels of IFNβ upon loss of BRCA1. Future studies are thus required to characterize which type-I IFNs are upregulated. We were also unable to directly test the possible contribution of RIG-I-like PRRs to increased proinflammatory signaling induced by BRCA1-induced deficiency. Transfection of siRNAs alone can activate a low level PRR response, which interfered with our ability to interpret experiments targeting RIGI and MDA5 transcripts using siRNA (not shown). While inhibitors of these PRRs are not currently available, a gene editing approach could be used to disrupt RIGI and MDA5 in BRCA1-null cells. Lastly, while steps were taken to minimize off-target CRISPR gene editing, we cannot completely rule out the possibility that off-target editing may have occurred. The use of three independent clones in this study and confirmation of increased ISG15 and USP18 levels upon knockdown of BRCA1 with targeting siRNA further minimizes the likelihood that the main results observed are due to off-target gene editing.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that loss of BRCA1 in non-malignant FTE cells results in autonomous low-level IFN-I signaling that could contribute to early events associated with malignant transformation and development of HGSTOC. These findings support further investigation of targeting PRRs or IFN-I signaling in FTE cells as a strategy to mitigate the risk of HGSTOC in BRCA1 mutation carriers, particularly those electing to delay risk-reducing bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy.
