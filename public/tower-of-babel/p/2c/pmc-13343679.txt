Efficacy and safety of albumin-bound paclitaxel combined with simvastatin in the second-line treatment of small cell lung cancer: a phase II randomized controlled trial
Authors: Maoying Guan, Wencheng Zhao, Wengang Zhang, Xuyang Chen, Runze Huang, Xinyue Liu, Yujie Li, Hao Wang, Lishu Zhao, Kandi Xu, Li Ye, Zhimin Chen, Yujin Liu, Qianqian Zhang, Hongbin Ji, Yayi He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSmall-cell lung cancer (SCLC) responds to first-line therapy, but most patients rapidly develop resistance. This study aimed to investigate the efficacy and safety of nab-paclitaxel combined with simvastatin as second-line treatment for SCLC patients.MethodsIn this phase II randomized trial, patients received nab-paclitaxel alone or in combination with simvastatin. The primary endpoint was disease control rate (DCR); secondary endpoints included objective response rate (ORR), progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), and safety.ResultsDCR was higher for the nab-paclitaxel plus simvastatin group than in the nab-paclitaxel group (92.9% vs. 44.4%, P = 0.005). ORR (50.0% vs. 11.1%, P = 0.017) and median PFS (113 vs. 62 days; HR = 0.42, 95% CI = 0.19–0.92; P = 0.029) were also improved. No significant difference in OS was observed. Treatment-related toxicities were manageable in both groups.ConclusionsIn this phase II randomized study, nab-paclitaxel plus simvastatin was associated with improved DCR, ORR, and PFS, with manageable toxicity, as second-line treatment for patients with small-cell lung cancer.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT04698941.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04835-7.

Background

Lung cancer is the most common malignancy worldwide, and small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) accounts for approximately 15% of all newly diagnosed lung cancer cases, with a 5-year survival rate of less than 7% [1, 2]. SCLC is estimated to cause 250,000 new cases worldwide and results in at least 200,000 deaths [3]. It is characterized by high invasiveness, early metastasis, and extremely poor prognosis [4, 5]. Approximately two-thirds of patients with SCLC develop systemic metastases immediately at diagnosis, often involving lymph nodes, contralateral lung, liver, brain, and bone [6]. The median overall survival (OS) of patients with localized SCLC was 12 to 14 months, and the OS was 8 to 12 months for patients with SCLC [7], even with aggressive treatment.

Platinum-based chemotherapy remains the standard first-line treatment for extensive-stage SCLC, yielding a high objective response rate (ORR) of 50–60% [8]. However, most patients relapse within 6 months of treatment completion, and the efficacy of second-line therapies is significantly reduced [9, 10]. Treatment options for relapsed SCLC are limited, and while paclitaxel-based regimens are commonly used in second-line therapy, their effectiveness is hindered by the rapid emergence of drug resistance. This results in short-lived responses, contributing to the dismal survival rates associated with SCLC [11]. Therefore, identifying novel therapeutic combinations that can overcome or delay resistance to second-line treatment is critical in the management of SCLC.

Emerging research has revealed that abnormal metabolism plays a key role in both tumour development and chemotherapy resistance [12]. In particular, altered cholesterol synthesis pathways in cancer cells contribute to drug resistance, a major challenge in treating SCLC. Our previous studies demonstrated that statins can delay the onset of chemotherapy resistance in SCLC [12]. By inhibiting the mevalonate-geranylgeranyl diphosphate pathway, statins block the prenylation of oncogenic proteins essential for cell survival, proliferation, and drug resistance. This mechanism offers a promising strategy to enhance the effectiveness of chemotherapy and overcome resistance in SCLC [12].

Albumin-bound paclitaxel (nab-paclitaxel) offers several advantages over traditional solvent-based paclitaxel, including improved tumour penetration, enhanced efficacy, and a more favourable safety profile [13, 14]. Our previous research suggested that the combination of statins with nab-paclitaxel may exert synergistic anti-tumour effects, potentially helping to overcome chemotherapy resistance in SCLC [12]. In one case from our study (SP651), treatment with simvastatin and nab-paclitaxel led to a 40.74% reduction in pulmonary tumour size, from 2.7 cm to 1.6 cm, achieving a partial response (PR) [12]. Building on this preliminary evidence, the present study aims to assess whether the combination of nab-paclitaxel and statins can further improve treatment outcomes and delay the onset of resistance compared to nab-paclitaxel monotherapy in patients undergoing second-line treatment for SCLC.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a prospective, randomized, controlled, open-label, phase II, parallel-group superiority trial with a 1:1 allocation ratio, comparing nab-paclitaxel plus simvastatin versus nab-paclitaxel alone in patients with small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) who developed resistance to first-line chemotherapy. The trial was conducted at Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital. Study treatments were administered by qualified oncology clinicians according to institutional standards. The random allocation sequence was generated by an independent statistician using a computer-based random number generator. Permuted block randomization was used (block size = 10) with no stratification. Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared by research staff not involved in patient recruitment; envelopes were opened sequentially at the time of randomization, and investigators enrolling participants had no prior access to the allocation sequence.

Eligible patients voluntarily participated with full understanding of the study, provided written informed consent, and were willing and able to complete all trial procedures. Participants were aged 18–75 years and had histopathologically confirmed SCLC diagnosed by fiberoptic bronchoscopy or percutaneous lung biopsy. Patients were required to be ineligible for surgical resection or radical radiotherapy and to have documented resistance to at least one line of chemotherapy, with at least one measurable lesion according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1 assessed within 4 weeks prior to randomization. Adequate organ function and tolerance for chemotherapy were required based on laboratory testing. Key exclusion criteria included any active malignancy within the past 5 years or concurrent active malignancy, known human immunodeficiency virus infection, active pulmonary tuberculosis, receipt of a live vaccine within 28 days prior to the first dose, current or prior statin therapy at the time of enrollment, pregnancy or breastfeeding, a history of substance abuse or drug addiction, or any other condition that, in the investigator’s judgment, could lead to early termination of study participation.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice guidelines and was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04698941) on 7 January 2021. The protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital (Institutional Review Board (IRB) No. L20-442–2), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before any study-related procedures. No important changes were made to the trial after it commenced; all prespecified outcomes and analyses were conducted as planned. Patients were recruited between 26 July 2021 and 22 November 2024 and were followed until radiographic disease progression, death, or the last clinical contact; the data cutoff date was 30 June 2025.

Procedures

Nab-paclitaxel (nab-P) was administered intravenously at a dose of 260 mg/m2 on day 1 of each 21-day cycle, for 4 to 6 cycles, or until progressive disease or unacceptable toxicity occurred. Simvastatin was administered orally at a fixed dose of 20 mg daily in the combination therapy group. Dose adjustments for nab-paclitaxel plus simvastatin (nab-P + SIM) were allowed based on predefined protocol criteria to manage toxicity. Tumour assessments, including CT scans of the thorax and abdomen, were conducted every 6 weeks (± 7 days) according to RECIST v1.1 criteria. Concomitant medications and supportive care were permitted throughout the trial and were not protocol-restricted.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was disease control rate (DCR), defined as the proportion of patients achieving complete response (CR), partial response (PR), or stable disease (SD). Secondary endpoints included objective response rate (ORR), progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), safety, and quality of life. PFS was defined as the time from randomization to the first documented disease progression or death from any cause, and OS as the time from randomization to death from any cause. Safety was evaluated based on the incidence and severity of adverse events (AEs), which were actively solicited at each treatment cycle, recorded in case report forms, and graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 4.03. AEs were collected from the first dose until 12 weeks after the last dose or the last clinical contact, whichever occurred first.

Statistical design

The aim of this study was to evaluate whether the addition of simvastatin to nab-paclitaxel could achieve a level of disease control sufficient to support further prospective evaluation of this combination in the second-line treatment of small-cell lung cancer. Based on published data, the disease control rate (DCR) of nab-paclitaxel monotherapy in relapsed or refractory SCLC generally ranges from approximately 30.8% to 48.5% [11, 15]. A DCR of 30% was therefore used as a conservative reference for the control group, and a DCR of 60% was prespecified for the experimental group as a clinically meaningful target. Using a superiority design informed by Fleming-type phase II principles, with a two-sided significance level of 0.05, 80% power, and a 1:1 allocation ratio, 36 evaluable patients were required. Allowing for a 10% dropout rate, the planned sample size was increased to 40 patients.

Efficacy analyses were conducted in the modified intention-to-treat population (all randomised participants who received at least one dose of study treatment) and analysed according to assigned group. Safety analyses included all participants who received at least one dose of study treatment. No formal interim analyses or statistical stopping rules were planned or performed. ORR, DCR, and adverse events (AEs) were summarised descriptively. Between-group comparisons for DCR, ORR, and AEs were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, with effect sizes reported along with 95% confidence interval (CI). Progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared between groups using the log-rank test; hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CI were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. Analyses were performed using complete-case data, and missing outcome data were not imputed. Prespecified exploratory biomarker analyses assessed associations between baseline geranylgeranyl diphosphate synthase 1 (GGPS1) expression (high vs low; H-score threshold of 200) and clinical outcomes within each treatment group; these analyses were exploratory and not powered for definitive subgroup inference.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining for GGPS1 expression was performed on pre-treatment tumour samples from patients receiving either nab-P or nab-P + SIM treatment. The samples were processed using standard IHC protocols with an anti-GGPS1 antibody (Abcam, ab48508, Abcam, Cambridge, UK; 1:500 dilution). Tumour tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. Detection was carried out using a 3,3′-diaminobenzidine substrate, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Images were captured at 63 × magnification. Staining intensity and distribution were assessed by Image J (V 1.51), and the H-score was calculated based on both the percentage of positively stained cells and staining intensity. H-score was quantified on a scale of 0–300, taking into consideration the percentage of positive cells and staining intensity as previously described [16, 17], where H-score represents the percentage of positive cells multiplied by an intensity score of 0–3. The IHC H-score of 200 was used as the threshold to define the high GGPS1 group and the low GGPS1 group [12, 17], GGPS1 expression could also be treated as a continuous variable for exploratory analysis.

Results

Patient and treatment characteristics

A total of 40 patients with progression after first-line chemotherapy were enrolled; 8 were excluded from the efficacy analysis. In the nab-P group, 2 participants transferred care to local hospitals and were subsequently lost to follow-up, with no post-baseline assessments available. In the nab-P + SIM group, two participants discontinued simvastatin after one cycle at their request, and four participants transferred care to local hospitals and were lost to follow-up. Tumour response

The best overall response results are presented in Table 2. For the nab-P group, 11.1% of patients achieved a PR, whereas the nab-P plus simvastatin group showed a significantly higher PR rate of 50% (P = 0.017). SD was observed in 42.9% of patients in the nab-P + SIM group and 33.3% in the nab-P group (P = 0.587). While 50% of patients in the nab-P group experienced PD, only 7.1% of patients in the combination group showed PD (P = 0.011). Importantly, the DCR, which encompasses CR, PR, and SD, was significantly higher in the nab-P + SIM group, reaching 92.9%, compared to only 44.4% in the nab-P group (P = 0.005). The ORR, which includes CR and PR, was significantly higher in the nab-P + SIM group, reaching 50.0%, compared to only 11.1% in the nab-P group (P = 0.017). We observed the best tumour size change rate for each participant. The waterfall plot (Fig. 2) clearly shows that the nab-P + SIM group achieved significantly greater tumour reduction compared to the nab-P group (P = 0.01), with a mean change of − 0.25 ± 0.36 for nab-P + SIM group and 0.24 ± 0.42 for nab-P group, as also indicated by the t-test (t = − 3.52, P < 0.01). This analysis was performed descriptively to compare the distribution of maximum tumour size change between groups. The swimmer’s plot (Fig. 3) illustrates marked inter-patient heterogeneity in progression-free survival (PFS) across both treatment groups. Patients in the nab-P + SIM group more frequently exhibited longer PFS durations, with a subset of individuals achieving relatively prolonged disease control. In contrast, patients treated with nab-P alone more commonly experienced earlier disease progression. Consistent with these patterns, a higher proportion of patients in the combination group achieved stable disease or partial responses as their best observed response, whereas progressive disease occurred earlier and more frequently in the nab-P group. These descriptive findings are concordant with the PFS differences observed in the Kaplan–Meier analysis. Survival analysis

We analysed the PFS and OS between the two patient groups, in Fig. 4. In the nab-P group, the median PFS was 62 days (95% CI: 6.563–117.437), while in the nab-P + SIM group, the median PFS was significantly longer at 113 days (95% CI: 59.831–166.169). The hazard ratio (HR) for PFS between the two groups was 0.42 (95% CI: 0.19–0.92; P = 0.029), indicating a statistically significant improvement in PFS with the combination therapy. Regarding OS, the median OS in the nab-P group was 204 days (95% CI: 164.501–243.499), compared to 208 days (95% CI: 92.495–323.505) in the nab-P + SIM group. The HR for OS was 0.76 (95% CI: 0.34–1.70; P = 0.504), suggesting no significant difference in OS between the two treatment groups. Safety

The toxicity profiles of two groups are summarized in Table 3. Toxicities are reported by incidence, both for any grade and for severe cases (Grade ≥ 3). The incidence of treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) was similar between the two treatment groups, with some differences in severity. In terms of bone marrow suppression, leukopenia occurred in 7.1% (1/14) of patients in the nab-P + SIM group and 16.7% (3/18) in the nab-P group. Severe leukopenia (Grade ≥ 3) was observed in 7.1% (1/14) of patients in the nab-P + SIM group, compared to none in the nab-P group. Anaemia was more common in the nab-P + SIM group, affecting 28.6% (4/14) of patients, with 7.1% (1/14) having severe anaemia. In the nab-P group, anaemia was seen in 16.7% (3/18) of patients, with no severe cases. Neutropenia was reported in 14.3% (2/14) of patients in the nab-P + SIM group and 27.8% (5/18) in the nab-P group, with severe neutropenia (Grade ≥ 3) occurring in 7.1% (1/14) of patients in the nab-P + SIM group and 22.2% (4/18) in the nab-P group. Thrombocytopenia was observed in 7.1% (1/14) of patients in both groups, with no cases of severe thrombocytopenia in either group.

For liver toxicity, elevated alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was reported in 28.6% (4/14) of patients in the nab-P + SIM group and 33.4% (6/18) in the nab-P group. Severe liver toxicity (Grade ≥ 3) occurred in 7.1% (1/14) of patients in the nab-P + SIM group and 11.1% (2/18) in the nab-P group. Elevated aspartate aminotransferase (AST) was more common, occurring in 64.3% (9/14) of patients in the nab-P + SIM group and 72.2% (13/18) in the nab-P group, with severe cases in 21.4% (3/14) and 16.7% (3/18), respectively. Elevated total bilirubin (TBil) was reported in 7.1% (1/14) of patients in the nab-P + SIM group, but none in the nab-P group. Renal toxicity was uncommon, with 7.1% (1/14) of patients in the nab-P + SIM group and 11.1% (2/18) in the nab-P group affected. No cases of severe renal toxicity (Grade ≥ 3) were observed in either group. Peripheral neuropathy was observed in 14.3% (2/14) of patients in the nab-P + SIM group and 11.1% (2/18) in the nab-P group, with severe cases occurring in 7.1% (1/14) of the nab-P + SIM group and none in the nab-P group. Overall, the addition of simvastatin to nab-paclitaxel did not significantly increase the incidence or severity of any TRAEs compared to nab-paclitaxel alone. No clinically meaningful differences in the incidence or severity of TRAEs were observed between groups, indicating a comparable safety profile for both treatments. No creatine kinase (CK) elevation or statin-related myopathy was observed during the study, and no treatment modifications were required for muscle-related adverse events. Toxicity profile

Immunohistochemical analysis of GGPS1 expression

We collected histological specimens from 8 patients before treatment and conducted IHC analysis of GGPS1 expression. The H-scores for the nab-P + SIM and nab-P groups were compared to assess the correlation between GGPS1 expression and treatment response. Figure 5A illustrates a typical case from each treatment group, highlighting GGPS1 expression levels and corresponding treatment responses. Discussion

SCLC remains one of the most aggressive malignancies, characterized by rapid progression, early metastasis, and high relapse rates [4]. Despite initial sensitivity to chemotherapy, most patients inevitably experience recurrence and resistance, highlighting an urgent need for more effective second-line treatments [4]. Current second-line options, including topotecan, irinotecan, gemcitabine, paclitaxel, and vinorelbine, offer limited clinical benefit, with ORR typically below 25% and median OS ranging from 6 to 9 months [9, 18–21]. Albumin-bound paclitaxel (nab-paclitaxel), a solvent-free taxane formulation, improves drug delivery and reduces toxicity, and has shown promise as a second-line agent by enhancing tolerability and anti-tumour efficacy [17]. Our prior studies suggest that co-administration of statins may potentiate the anti-tumour effects of nab-paclitaxel in SCLC [12]. Based on this rationale, the present study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of nab-paclitaxel combined with simvastatin compared to nab-paclitaxel monotherapy in SCLC patients who progressed after first-line chemotherapy. This study represents the first clinical investigation evaluating statins as chemosensitizers in the second-line setting for SCLC.

In this study, the addition of simvastatin to nab-paclitaxel was associated with a marked improvement in tumour response. The ORR increased from 11.1% with nab-paclitaxel alone to 50.0% in the combination group, and the DCR was likewise higher in the combination group (92.9% vs. 44.4%). These findings suggest that simvastatin may enhance the short-term anti-tumour activity of chemotherapy in the second-line setting. While immunotherapy-based combinations have demonstrated activity in relapsed SCLC, including encouraging activity reported with immunotherapy-based combinations in the 2SMALL study with lubritidine plus immune checkpoint inhibition [22], access to such regimens remains limited in real-world practice. In this context, the improvement in median PFS observed with nab-paclitaxel plus simvastatin (113 vs. 62 days; HR = 0.42) indicates that continued optimization of chemotherapy-based combinations may remain clinically relevant, particularly for patients who are ineligible for or lack access to immunotherapy [23]. The efficacy outcomes in the nab-paclitaxel monotherapy group in the present study are consistent with prior retrospective reports, which have described ORRs of 11–33%, DCRs around 44%, and median PFS ranging from approximately 48 to 66 days) [16, 24–26], supporting the validity of the observed between-group differences. Despite the improvement in PFS, no significant difference in OS was observed (208 vs. 204 days). This finding is not unexpected, as OS in relapsed SCLC is strongly influenced by subsequent therapies, inter-patient heterogeneity, and limited sample size, factors that may attenuate the survival impact of earlier disease control [25]. (Details of post-progression therapies are summarized in Additional file 1: Table S1.) Taken together, these data indicate that the combination of nab-paclitaxel and simvastatin can improve disease control and delay progression without increasing toxicity, while the effect on long-term survival requires further evaluation in larger studies.

Grade ≥ 3 adverse events were primarily hematologic, with bone marrow suppression being the most common severe toxicity in the nab-P group, consistent with previous reports [13, 26]. The incidence of grade ≥ 3 hematologic toxicities, particularly neutropenia, was numerically lower in the nab-P + SIM group than in the nab-P group (7.1% vs. 22.2%). Overall, the addition of simvastatin did not increase the incidence or severity of treatment-related adverse events. Mild to moderate elevations in liver enzymes were observed in both groups, without evidence of exacerbated hepatotoxicity or nephrotoxicity. Notably, no creatine kinase elevation or statin-related myopathy was observed during treatment. These findings indicate that nab-paclitaxel plus simvastatin has a manageable safety profile and does not compromise treatment tolerability in the second-line SCLC setting.

Drug resistance in SCLC is primarily associated with factors such as transcriptional subtypes and transdifferentiation, alterations in DNA damage repair mechanisms, changes in differentiation states, upregulation of drug resistance proteins, modifications in tumour metabolism, and the tumour microenvironment [27]. In tumour metabolism, GGPS1 catalyzes the conversion of farnesyl diphosphate to geranylgeranyl diphosphate, a crucial step in the isoprenoid biosynthesis pathway [12]. Previous studies have suggested that GGPS1 may be involved in tumour initiation and progression, with higher GGPS1 expression being significantly associated with poorer prognosis and OS across multiple cancer types [28]. In the present study, we performed exploratory analyses to examine the relationship between baseline GGPS1 expression and treatment response in patients with SCLC receiving nab-paclitaxel with or without simvastatin. Although limited by the very small number of available tumour samples, our findings suggest that the addition of simvastatin may alter the unfavourable pattern typically associated with higher GGPS1 expression. In the nab-paclitaxel alone group, treatment response remained poor overall, whereas in the nab-paclitaxel plus simvastatin group, some patients with relatively high GGPS1 expression still achieved tumour shrinkage and sustained disease control. These observations raise the possibility that simvastatin may attenuate the adverse association between elevated GGPS1 expression and poor treatment outcome. Modulation of GGPS1-related metabolic pathways may enhance chemotherapy sensitivity in SCLC. However, given the exploratory nature of these analyses and the very limited sample size, no definitive conclusions regarding predictive biomarkers or resistance mechanisms can be drawn. Larger, biomarker-driven studies are required to further clarify the role of GGPS1 and the potential interaction between statins and chemotherapy in SCLC.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First of all, the relatively small sample size inherent to this phase II randomized study may have limited the statistical power to detect differences in certain endpoints, particularly overall survival. Nevertheless, the sample size was adequate for evaluation of the primary endpoint of disease control. In this setting, the observed improvements in disease control rate, objective response rate, and progression-free survival provide a rationale for further investigation of this combination regimen in larger, confirmatory studies. Secondly, this was a single-center study, which may limit generalizability; however, uniform treatment administration and response assessment ensured internal consistency of efficacy and safety evaluations. Multicenter studies will be needed to further confirm these findings across broader clinical settings. Finally, analyses involving GGPS1 expression were exploratory due to the limited availability of tumour specimens. The study was not powered enough to assess biomarker–treatment interactions, and paired pre- and post-treatment samples were largely unavailable. Accordingly, all biomarker-related findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and need further investigation.

Conclusion

In this prospective randomized phase II study, the addition of simvastatin to nab-paclitaxel was associated with significantly improved disease control rate, objective response rate, and progression-free survival compared with nab-paclitaxel alone in patients with relapsed small-cell lung cancer after first-line chemotherapy. The combination regimen was generally well tolerated and did not result in clinically meaningful increases in treatment-related toxicity. Although no significant overall survival benefit was observed, the favourable anti-tumour activity and manageable safety profile support further evaluation of this regimen in larger, multicenter studies. Exploratory biomarker findings also suggest that GGPS1-related metabolic pathways might be relevant to treatment sensitivity and warrant further investigation.
