Geometry-programmed self-wrinkling in organo-hydrogels for anisotropic mechanics and adaptive sensing
Authors: Haobo Qi, Hang Yang, Tian Li, Min Li, Wei Zhou, Xinyu Dong, Lailai Zhu, Wei Zhai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gel-based soft materials are attractive for flexible electronics and biointerfaces but are often limited by insufficient mechanical robustness and constrained functional integration. Here, we introduce a geometry-programmed self-wrinkling strategy that enables the spontaneous formation of aligned wrinkle architectures during thermal–evaporative gelation of poly(vinyl alcohol)–based organo-hydrogels. Without external patterning or post-processing, this process produces materials with enhanced mechanical robustness and pronounced anisotropy in deformation, fracture, and ionic transport. By leveraging these intrinsic properties, we demonstrate multiple sensing and actuation functions, including directional strain sensing, multidirectional sliding detection, deformation-driven rolling sensors, and temperature-triggered alarms. These results highlight geometry-programmed self-wrinkling as a scalable route to integrate structural reinforcement and directional functionality into soft materials through a physically driven formation process.

Introduction

Soft materials play a central role in emerging technologies ranging from biomedical devices and biointerfaces to flexible robotics and wearable sensing platforms1–3. Among them, gel-based soft materials, including hydrogels and organo-hydrogels, are particularly attractive owing to their high ionic conductivity, intrinsic compliance, and tissue-like mechanical properties, positioning them as promising candidates for biointerfaces and flexible electronic systems4,5. However, the highly solvated and weakly crosslinked nature of gel networks often leads to limited durability under repeated deformation, insufficient toughness in dynamic environments, and restricted sensing performance, which together constrain their long-term reliability and multifunctional deployment6,7.

Bioinspired structural design has emerged as an effective approach to addressing the mechanical limitations of gel-based soft materials by introducing architectures that redistribute stress and enhance energy dissipation efficiency8–10. A representative example is honeycomb-like structured hydrogels, in which directional freeze casting establishes aligned cellular frameworks while salting-out–induced polymer aggregation concurrently reinforces the pore walls at the molecular level, together producing hierarchical networks with exceptional strength, toughness, and fatigue resistance11. Similarly, bamboo-inspired composite hydrogels exploit evaporation-induced structuring during gelation to generate aligned, lamellar internal architectures, while complementary molecular modifications stabilize interfacial interactions within the evolving network and enhance load transfer across length scales12. Accumulating studies suggest that bioinspired structural design, when coupled with molecular-level engineering during gel formation, offers an effective route to improving the mechanical performance of gel-based soft materials.

Wrinkle architectures represent another widely explored bioinspired motif, inspired by textured natural interfaces such as fingerprints and skin folds13,14. In engineered gel-based systems, surface wrinkling has been extensively employed to enhance sensing performance; for example, wrinkled iontronic interfaces substantially improve pressure sensitivity and resolution by promoting localized deformation and increasing interfacial contact area, while wrinkled nanocomposite hydrogels exploit surface strain concentration to achieve highly sensitive and robust electrical responses4,15,16. In most existing implementations, however, such surface wrinkles are introduced through external or post-forming processes, including stretch–release cycles applied to fully crosslinked gels17–19, swelling-induced instabilities that develop post-gelation20,21, and externally imposed patterns using photolithographic or molding techniques on chemically crosslinked networks22–24. As a result, functional enhancement is primarily achieved through geometric amplification at the surface, while the underlying molecular network that governs mechanical integrity and durability remains largely unchanged.

In contrast, wrinkles commonly emerge in situ in natural systems and in soft materials as they form or evolve, a phenomenon commonly referred to as self-wrinkling25–28. Representative examples include the spontaneous wrinkling of drying biological tissues, polymer films, and soft coatings, where surface patterns develop concurrently with solvent loss or phase transitions29–31. Such wrinkling behavior is commonly attributed to the accumulation of compressive stress during formation, which is relieved through surface undulation as the system seeks a lower-energy configuration32,33. Consistently, in exploratory experiments we observed that thermally assisted evaporation during gelation readily led to the spontaneous formation of surface undulations, indicating that temperature gradients and solvent loss can promote wrinkle formation in hydrated gel systems.

In this work, we develop a geometry-programmed self-wrinkling strategy in which thermal–evaporative gelation drives the spontaneous formation of surface wrinkles, while the planform boundary of the system governs their macroscopic orientation. Using poly(vinyl alcohol)–based organo-hydrogels as a model platform, we show that this in situ, physically driven process embeds aligned wrinkle architectures directly into the evolving gel network, rather than imposing them through external patterning or post-processing. As a result, the resulting materials exhibit enhanced mechanical robustness together with pronounced anisotropy in deformation, fracture behavior, and ionic transport. By leveraging this intrinsic structural anisotropy, we further demonstrate a series of directional and adaptive functions, including motion-responsive sliding sensors, deformation-driven rolling sensors, and thermally actuated temperature alarms (Fig. 1a). Results

Onset and geometry-guided organization of wrinkles

In this work, we developed a self-wrinkling strategy to fabricate well-aligned wrinkle architectures in a poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA)–based organo-hydrogel system, referred to as the wrinkled structured organo-hydrogel (WSO). As illustrated in Fig. 1b, a PVA solution prepared in a water/glycerol mixture is cast into a rectangular mold consisting of a thermally conductive copper base and thermally insulating polylactic acid (PLA) sidewalls. The mold is placed on a temperature-controlled heating platform (typically 40-90 °C) with the top surface exposed to ambient air, allowing evaporation to proceed under controlled laboratory conditions (23 ± 0.5 °C, 55 ± 2.5% relative humidity (RH), no forced airflow). During heating, the PVA solution receives heat predominantly from the copper base, while the free surface undergoes evaporative cooling, establishing a pronounced vertical temperature gradient across the liquid layer (Fig. 1b, left). Owing to such vertical temperature gradient, convective flow consistent with Rayleigh–Bénard convection34 is observed, as supported by numerical simulations and thermal imaging (Supplementary Fig. 1), promoting heat and solvent redistribution prior to surface solidification.

As evaporation proceeds, continuous solvent loss at the free surface increases the local polymer concentration, triggering a sol–gel transition that leads to the formation of a thin, mechanically coherent surface skin layer, while the underlying bulk remains fluid (Fig. 1b, middle). Thermal imaging reveals lower surface temperatures near the boundaries of the skin-covered area (Supplementary Fig. 2; schematized in Fig. 1b), indicating locally enhanced evaporative cooling and, consequently, spatially non-uniform evaporation across the interface. With further evaporation, the initially smooth surface skin first exhibits subtle distortions, which evolve into localized undulations and progressively propagate across the interface, ultimately organizing into continuous, highly aligned wrinkle bands, as shown in Supplementary Movies 1 and 2, and schematized in Fig. 1b (right).

To identify the conditions required for wrinkle initiation, we varied the heating temperature while keeping the mold geometry and initial solution composition unchanged. Adjusting the heating temperature modulates the thermal contrast between the bottom-heated substrate and the evaporatively cooled free surface. We quantify this contrast using the average maximum temperature difference, ΔTm, defined as the temperature difference between the heating substrate and the free surface, averaged from the early heating stage to the onset of surface wrinkling (Supplementary Fig. 3). Experiments conducted over a heating range of 40–90 °C yield distinct ΔTm values, and the resulting wrinkling behavior is summarized in Fig. 1c by plotting the measured outcomes against ΔTm and the surface polymer concentration at the moment when wrinkles first appear. At a heating temperature of 40 °C, ΔTm remains small, evaporation is weak, and no surface wrinkling is observed within the experimental window (Region \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textcircled{2}$$\end{document}V2). In contrast, for heating temperatures of 50 °C and above, which produce ΔTm values on the order of 3–4 °C or higher, surface wrinkling becomes accessible and emerges once evaporation has sufficiently densified the surface layer to sustain compressive deformation (Region \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textcircled{1}$$\end{document}V1). Within this regime, the surface polymer concentration measured at wrinkle onset increases modestly with ΔTm, reflecting enhanced surface enrichment under stronger evaporation conditions.

These experimental trends indicate that wrinkle initiation arises from the coupled effects of thermal driving and surface skin development. A sufficiently large thermal contrast, imposed by bottom heating and reflected in ΔTm, regulates the evaporation rate at the free surface and thereby sets the magnitude of stress that can develop within the skin. As evaporation progresses, solvent loss densifies the surface region into a solid-like layer capable of sustaining compressive deformation. The spatially non-uniform surface temperature distributions captured by thermal imaging reflect this evaporation process and support the presence of heterogeneous stress development across the interface. From this perspective, Region ③ corresponds to conditions in which the thermal driving force is already substantial, but the surface skin has not yet fully developed at the time of observation, resulting in a transient smooth state.

Once surface wrinkling is initiated, we find that the macroscopic wrinkle orientation and domain organization are governed primarily by the planform geometry of the mold. This effect is most clearly demonstrated for rectangular molds with varying aspect ratios (Fig. 1b, middle), where wrinkles consistently align parallel to the short edges, with domain transitions localized near the corners. Changing the mold size does not alter the global orientation pattern, indicating that wrinkle direction is dictated by geometry rather than by local material parameters. Side-view imaging further confirms that the evaporatively formed surface skin exhibits positive Gaussian curvature (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa \, > \,0$$\end{document}κ>0) throughout the wrinkling process (Supplementary Fig. 4). During wrinkle formation, this curved skin layer is supported by the underlying fluid bulk and laterally constrained by the mold boundary. Within this setting, recent theoretical work on confined elastic shells predicts that wrinkle orientation and domain layout are controlled by the planform boundary through geometric selection rules35. Together, these results show that thermal–evaporative conditions determine when wrinkles form, while boundary geometry robustly programs how they are oriented.

Composition-dependent wrinkle size evolution

The wrinkling process in our system can be viewed as comprising two coupled but distinct regimes. In the initial onset–organization regime, evaporation induces a mechanical instability in the surface skin, while geometric confinement associated with the planform boundary of the curved surface establishes the macroscopic wrinkle organization. Following this initial pattern formation, the system enters a growth–maturation regime in which continued evaporation, gelation, and solvent redistribution progressively evolve the wrinkle morphology, giving rise to the final wrinkle wavelength and amplitude. A key implication of this separation is that wrinkle formation persists across a broad range of material formulations, whereas the characteristic wrinkle dimensions remain sensitive to material composition and processing parameters that influence the later stages of evolution.

To examine how composition and processing affect wrinkle development during this growth-maturation regime, we varied the secondary solvent (glycerol) content, incorporated different functional additives, and adjusted the precursor mass, while keeping the mold geometry and heating conditions unchanged. Across all formulations investigated, surface wrinkles reproducibly emerge under identical thermal–geometric conditions, indicating that wrinkle formation reflects a general response of the PVA-based organo-hydrogel system rather than a behavior restricted to a specific composition. As shown in Fig. 2a–c, well-developed wrinkle patterns are consistently observed in additive-free samples (NA–2%GLY), carbon-nanotube–doped samples (CNT–2%GLY), and MgB2-nanosheet–doped samples (MN–2%GLY), as confirmed by optical imaging and three-dimensional surface reconstructions. While the overall wrinkle organization remains comparable under the same mold geometry, the characteristic wrinkle dimensions vary with composition and processing history. Systematic variations in wrinkle dimensions are observed upon changing composition and precursor mass, accompanied by changes in skin-layer and precursor properties (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). Increasing the glycerol concentration leads to a monotonic increase in wrinkle amplitude A accompanied by a decrease in wavelength w (Fig. 2f). For example, increasing the glycerol concentration from 0% to 2% in additive-free samples (NA–0%GLY to NA–2%GLY) results in an increase in A from 0.06 ± 0.02 mm to 0.22 ± 0.06 mm (Fig. 2d). The incorporation of functional additives further modifies wrinkle development at fixed glycerol content. At 2%GLY (Fig. 2g), samples containing different additives exhibit distinct wrinkle dimensions, with MN–2%GLY displaying narrower wavelength distributions and more uniform wrinkle morphology compared with NA–2%GLY and CNT–2%GLY (Fig. 2c, e). In addition, the precursor mass influences wrinkle development. For MN–2%GLY–x g samples, increasing the precursor mass leads to larger wrinkle amplitudes accompanied by a reduction in wavelength (Fig. 2h, i). At higher masses, the distribution of wrinkle sizes becomes broader, indicating increased spatial variability in wrinkle growth. Moreover, the mold geometry also modulates wrinkle dimensions: compared with the rectangular mold, the triangular mold produces a smaller wavelength but a larger amplitude (Fig. 2j). Together, glycerol content, additive incorporation, and precursor mass provide experimentally accessible parameters for modulating wrinkle dimensions within a common formation framework.

Finally, we assessed the post-formation stability of the wrinkle morphology using representative WSO samples (MN–2%GLY, rectangular mold) under relevant environmental conditions (Supplementary Fig. 9). Under ambient storage (25 °C), the wrinkle profiles remain essentially unchanged over 28 days, with both w and A staying within experimental uncertainty (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). Exposure to elevated temperature (80 °C) leads to a gradual reduction in amplitude during the first week, accompanied by a smaller decrease in w, after which the morphology stabilizes (Supplementary Fig. 9c, d), indicating partial relaxation followed by a new steady state. Stepwise relative-humidity cycling induces reversible changes in both w and A, with swelling at high humidity and contraction at low humidity and minimal hysteresis upon cycling (Supplementary Fig. 9e, f). These observations indicate that wrinkle dimensions are responsive to solvent content and environmental history, while the patterned surface remains structurally intact after formation.

Wrinkle-induced reinforcement and mechanical anisotropy

To examine how wrinkle formation and alignment influence mechanical performance, we focus on PVA organo-hydrogels reinforced with MgB2 nanosheets and containing 2 wt% glycerol, which reproducibly form well-defined wrinkle architectures (denoted WSO–MN). Wrinkle-free counterparts with identical composition (WFO–MN) were prepared by suppressing the surface temperature gradient during processing. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) confirms that WFO–MN and WSO–MN contain comparable residual solvent contents (Supplementary Fig. 10), ensuring that differences in mechanical behavior primarily reflect structural differences introduced during wrinkle formation rather than variations in solvent content.

Figure 3a compares the tensile responses of wrinkle-free samples (WFO–MN) and specimens loaded along an individual wrinkle ridge of the wrinkled organo-hydrogel (WSO–MN–SW). The stress–strain curve of WSO–MN–SW extends to substantially higher strain while maintaining a continuously increasing stress level, whereas WFO–MN fails at lower stress and strain. Quantitatively, the fracture strain increases from 606.07 ± 59.35% for WFO–MN to 997.90 ± 130.14% for WSO–MN–SW, accompanied by increases in fracture strength from 17.12 ± 3.53 MPa to 51.72 ± 3.43 MPa and fracture toughness from 66.05 ± 7.81 to 315.93 ± 63.94 MJ m-3 (Fig. 3b, c). These differences in mechanical response are accompanied by systematic structural distinctions between wrinkled and wrinkle-free regions. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) shows that WFO–MN retains a relatively open porous network, whereas the wrinkled regions in WSO–MN exhibit a denser and more layered morphology (Fig. 3e). Consistent with this densification, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) indicates a higher crystalline fraction within the wrinkled regions, with the melting peak area of WSO–MN–SW being approximately 1.2 times that of WFO–MN and also exceeding that of the non-wrinkled regions within WSO–MN (Fig. 3f). At the molecular level, Fourier-transform infrared spectra of WSO–MN display more pronounced borate-associated bonding features at ~680 cm−1 (O-B-O)36,37, ~1110 cm−1 (B-O-C), and ~1415 cm−1 (B-O stretching)37,38, together with a broadened O-H stretching band near 3417 cm−1, compared with WFO–MN (Fig. 3g). At the macroscopic scale, the presence and alignment of these reinforced wrinkle regions lead to a pronounced dependence of mechanical response on loading orientation. When WSO–MN samples are stretched parallel to the wrinkle direction (WSO–MN–WD), they sustain exceptionally large deformation while maintaining high stress, reaching fracture strains above 1000% together with elevated fracture strength and toughness (Fig. 3b, c). Notably, these metrics place WSO–MN among the strongest and toughest hydrogel-based materials reported to date (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Table 1). In addition to these enhanced fracture metrics, the stress–strain response in this configuration exhibits a non-monotonic, stepwise evolution at large strains, characterized by successive stress plateaus and drops prior to final failure (Fig. 3a, inset and Supplementary Movie 3). This response reflects progressive damage events occurring at different stages of deformation rather than a single abrupt fracture. In contrast, when the same material is loaded perpendicular to the wrinkle direction (WSO–MN–PWD), the tensile response is markedly less robust. Fracture strain and strength are comparable to, or lower than, those of the wrinkle-free sample, and the stress–strain curves show early stress saturation with limited extensibility (Fig. 3a). This behavior indicates that under perpendicular loading, deformation is concentrated within regions between wrinkle ridges, reducing the effective participation of the reinforced wrinkle structure in load bearing. Consistent with this behavior, finite-element simulations qualitatively illustrate a crack-arresting role of the wrinkled architecture. Under uniaxial stretching, loading along the perpendicular to the wrinkle direction (PWD) leads to pronounced stress localization within a narrow necking band, which accelerates damage accumulation and promotes premature failure (Supplementary Fig. 11a). In contrast, loading along the wrinkle direction (WD) redistributes stress across multiple wrinkle segments, suppressing the formation of a single dominant localization zone and thereby postponing catastrophic fracture to higher strain levels (Supplementary Fig. 11b).

Beyond monotonic tensile loading, wrinkle alignment also influences fracture resistance and damage evolution under deformation modes relevant to practical use. Figure 3i compares the fracture energy measured under pure-shear loading for WFO–MN, WSO–MN–PWD, and WSO–MN–WD (Fig. 3i). The WD configuration exhibits a higher fracture energy than both the PWD configuration and the wrinkle-free counterpart, indicating increased resistance to crack extension when deformation is applied along the wrinkle alignment. Cyclic tensile tests on pre-notched specimens subjected to repeated loading to 20% strain further reveal a pronounced orientation dependence (Fig. 3j). For WSO–MN–PWD, the crack tip advances progressively from the 1st to the 1000th and 5000th cycles, accompanied by a reduction in the force–strain hysteresis loops, reflecting cumulative damage and stiffness loss. In contrast, WSO–MN–WD shows minimal crack extension over the same number of cycles, with a comparatively stable hysteresis response. These results further support that aligned wrinkle ridges act as stable load-bearing pathways, limiting stress localization and crack advancement across different loading modes.

As shown in Fig. 4a, the electrical conductivity measured along WD is consistently higher than that measured along PWD across all tested temperatures (85–95 °C) and glycerol concentrations (1–3 wt%). For example, WSO-MN fabricated at 90 °C with 2% glycerol exhibits a conductivity of 0.068 S m⁻¹ along WD, compared with 0.020 S m−¹ along PWD. While increasing glycerol content leads to a modest increase in absolute conductivity (e.g., at 90 °C, from 0.062 sS m⁻¹ at 1% glycerol to 0.076 S m⁻¹ at 3% glycerol), the conductivity contrast between WD and PWD remains the dominant and persistent feature. Increasing evaporation temperature reduces the overall conductivity, consistent with partial solvent loss during drying (Supplementary Fig. 12); importantly, the directional separation between WD and PWD is preserved, indicating that the anisotropy is not tied to a specific processing point but reflects the wrinkle-organized internal structure.

This directional architecture also leads to anisotropic electromechanical sensitivity under deformation. Figure 4b compares the strain-dependent resistance change for samples stretched along different orientations. The gauge factor (GF) is highest for WSO–MN–PWD (GF = 1.404), intermediate for WSO–MN–WD (GF = 0.671), and lowest for specimens loaded along a single wrinkle ridge (WSO–MN–SW, GF = 0.446). All configurations exhibit highly linear responses up to 500% strain, with correlation coefficients of 0.998. The frequency-dependent measurements in Fig. 4c show that this directional sensitivity is maintained over a range of deformation rates, indicating a stable electromechanical coupling. In addition to tensile deformation, WSO–MN also exhibits pressure sensitivity under compression, with two approximately linear response regimes observed below 20 kPa (Fig. 4d), further reflecting the mechanically compliant.

To evaluate environmental robustness, time-dependent conductivity measurements were conducted under storage and humidity variations while monitoring both WD and PWD responses. Under ambient storage for 28 days, the conductivity in both directions remains essentially unchanged, and the anisotropic contrast is preserved (Supplementary Fig. 13a). Accelerated drying at 40 °C leads to a gradual decrease in absolute conductivity due to solvent loss; however, the WD–PWD separation remains clearly observable throughout the test (Supplementary Fig. 13b). Similarly, under stepwise relative-humidity cycling between 30% and 90% RH, both resistance and calculated conductivity show step-like changes while maintaining directional contrast (Supplementary Fig. 14). These results indicate that although ionic conductivity depends on solvent content, the wrinkle-induced anisotropy is structurally encoded and remains stable under long-term storage and dynamic environmental conditions.

The observed anisotropy in electrical properties originates from the enhanced ionic transport ability along the wrinkle structures. Nyquist plots (Fig. 4e) show that WSO–MN–WD exhibits a smaller overall impedance than WSO–MN–PWD, a trend that persists across the measured frequency range (Fig. 4f) and is also observed for WSO–MN–SW and WSO–MN–NW (Supplementary Fig. 15). Equivalent-circuit fitting using a Randles-type model39,40 (Supplementary Fig. 16) reveals that the Warburg impedance associated with ion diffusion along WD is approximately an order of magnitude smaller than that along PWD (Supplementary Table 2), indicating reduced diffusion resistance in the wrinkle-aligned direction. Complementary two-dimensional small-angle X-ray scattering (2D SAXS) measurements further reveal anisotropic internal organization in WD samples, characterized by directionally elongated scattering patterns and a Hermans orientation12 factor of fc = 0.51 (Fig. 4g–j). This intermediate degree of alignment suggests reduced transport tortuosity and enhanced directional connectivity of ion-conductive domains along WD41–43. Together, these electrical and structural measurements indicate that the wrinkled architecture not only concentrates conductive pathways locally but also organizes them directionally, enabling efficient ionic transport along WD and giving rise to the observed electrical anisotropy.

Wrinkle-induced multidirectional sliding sensing

The combination of wrinkle-induced mechanical and electrical anisotropy enables WSO–MN to function as a platform for detecting complex, direction-dependent mechanical stimuli. To demonstrate this capability, we constructed a sliding sensor using a WSO–MN film featuring a curved wrinkle arrangement, with the concave vertex oriented downward (Fig. 5a). During finger sliding, the local contact pressure and shear direction relative to the wrinkle orientation modulate the conductive pathways, producing characteristic resistance responses that depend on the direction of motion. Resistance changes were recorded for four sliding directions (up, down, left, and right) using three electrode configurations: PWD, WD, and a combined WD–PWD configuration (Fig. 5c–e). In the PWD configuration, sliding across the wrinkle ridges generates pronounced resistance variations, whereas sliding parallel to the ridges produces weaker responses. In contrast, the WD configuration exhibits stronger signals for sliding along the WD and reduced sensitivity to perpendicular motion. The directional nature of these signals is further illustrated by the time-resolved resistance change rates (Fig. 5f–i), which show stable and repeatable waveforms for repeated sliding in the same direction, alongside clear differences between different directions. To evaluate the practical usability of these anisotropic signals, the sensor was mounted on the index finger, and resistance signals generated by thumb sliding were collected in real time (Supplementary Movie 4 and Fig. 5j). A pre-trained artificial neural network (ANN) was used to classify the sliding directions and convert the recognized signals into control commands for a robotic arm (Fig. 5b). The system achieved a recognition accuracy exceeding 92% across four sliding directions (Supplementary Fig. 17), demonstrating that the wrinkle-induced signal anisotropy is sufficiently robust and separable for real-time motion decoding. Independent validation using sensors fabricated from different wrinkle batches, analyzed via t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE), further confirms consistent clustering of sliding signals by direction (Supplementary Fig. 18), indicating good reproducibility across devices.

To assess reliability under conditions relevant to wearable use, we examined the sliding sensor response after exposure to sweat, frictional wear, and repeated bending. Following immersion in artificial sweat for up to 60 min, the wrinkle topography remains intact apart from moderate swelling-induced dimensional changes, and the direction-dependent sliding signals remain clearly distinguishable (Supplementary Figs. 19–22). After 5000 friction cycles along WD and 5000 cycles along PWD, the wrinkle morphology and characteristic signal waveforms are largely preserved, with only a modest reduction in decoding accuracy (Supplementary Figs. 23–26). Similar stability is observed after cyclic bending along both directions (Supplementary Figs. 27–30). Together, these results show that the anisotropic sliding-sensing functionality arises from the intrinsic wrinkle-organized architecture and is retained under mechanical and environmental perturbations relevant to long-term, on-body operation.

Mechanical deformation enables rolling strain sensors and temperature alarms

In addition to the anisotropic mechanical and electrical properties of WSO-MN, the wrinkle architecture introduces spatial heterogeneity in stiffness and stress distribution across the film thickness. This leads to a mechanical instability of the surface layer, especially under thermal stimulation, this heterogeneity gives rise to pronounced out-of-plane deformation, enabling spontaneous and programmable rolling behavior. As shown in Fig. 6d, when WSO–MN is heated to elevated temperature (80 °C), the wrinkled film undergoes rapid inward rolling, forming a cylindrical geometry with its rolling axis aligned parallel to the wrinkle direction. Quantitative curvature analysis shows that the initial curvature increases from 0.176 ± 0.014 mm⁻¹ to 0.371 ± 0.017 mm⁻¹ within 5 min of heating (Supplementary Fig. 31). This rolling behavior originates from the modulus mismatch between the reinforced wrinkle ridges and the comparatively softer matrix, which generates internal stress upon thermal activation. As solvent redistribution and thermal softening proceed, this stress imbalance drives bending and self-rolling, with the wrinkle alignment dictating the rolling direction. Leveraging this rolling behavior, we designed a rolling strain sensor by assembling two parallel wrinkled WSO–MN strips joined at both ends with a pure PVA solution and thermally activating the structure at 80 °C (Supplementary Movie 5 and Fig. 6a, b). Upon rolling, the wrinkle ridges of the inner layer come into contact with the smooth surface of the outer layer, forming multiple parallel conductive pathways and establishing a low-resistance initial state (Fig. 6c). When tensile strain is applied, progressive separation of the wrinkle contacts occurs, leading to stepwise increases in resistance (Fig. 6e). This contact-mediated sensing mechanism is reversible; upon release, the sensor returns to its rolled configuration with minimal hysteresis (3.86%). The rolling sensor maintains stable performance over 2,500 loading cycles at 300% strain (Fig. 6f), outperforming most reported hydrogel-based and structural strain sensors in terms of durability and signal stability (Fig. 6g, h and Supplementary Table 4). Owing to its low activation force and large deformation range, the rolling sensor is particularly suited for detecting localized and subtle motions. When mounted across finger joints, bending-induced elongation of the sensor produces clear resistance changes that enable recognition of distinct hand gestures and real-time control of a robotic hand (Supplementary Movie 6, Fig. 6i and Supplementary Fig. 32).

The same thermally driven rolling behavior also enables simple temperature-triggered actuation. To demonstrate this capability, we constructed a temperature alarm in which a metal rod suspended from a rolled WSO–MN film functions as a mechanical switch within an LED circuit (Fig. 6j, k). The rod, with a mass 5.7 times that of the WSO–MN film, initially remains separated from a copper contact, keeping the circuit open. As the ambient temperature increases from 33 °C to 58 °C, the WSO–MN rolls further, lifting the rod and closing the circuit within approximately 2 min (Fig. 6l). This design illustrates how wrinkle-enabled thermal deformation can be directly translated into functional actuation without additional power sources or complex circuitry.

Discussion

In conclusion, we demonstrate a geometry-programmed self-wrinkling strategy that enables the spontaneous formation of aligned wrinkle architectures in PVA-based organo-hydrogels through thermally driven evaporation and surface gelation. By decoupling wrinkle orientation from wrinkle size, this process allows macroscopic wrinkle organization to be robustly dictated by planform geometry, while subsequent growth and maturation govern wrinkle dimensions across diverse material compositions. The resulting wrinkle-induced structural heterogeneity introduces localized densification and alignment within the surface layer, leading to pronounced mechanical reinforcement, strong anisotropy in deformation and fracture behavior, and directionally enhanced ionic transport. These intrinsic structural features are directly translated into practical functions, including anisotropic electromechanical sensing, multidirectional sliding detection, deformation-driven rolling strain sensors, and temperature-triggered actuation, without external patterning or complex device architectures. Collectively, this work establishes geometry-programmed self-wrinkling as a general and scalable route to encode aligned, multifunctional architectures into soft materials through a spontaneous physical process, providing a versatile platform for robust and adaptive soft electronic and biointerfacing systems.

Methods

Materials

PVA (Mw 146,000–186,000, 99+% hydrolyzed), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, pellets), and glycerol were purchased from Sigma. Multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs, OD: 10–20 nm, Length: 10–30 μm, Purity: >95%) were purchased from XFNANO Inc., China. PLA filaments were purchased from 3D AURA Pte Ltd, Singapore. MgB2 powder was brought from Xiaohuang nanotech, China and MN were prepared following previous work40. In short, EDTA (12.89 g) and NaOH (3.95 g) were dissolved in 90 mL of H2O, and the pH was adjusted to 10. MgB2 powders (2 g) were then added to this solution at 25 °C. After 3 days of exfoliation, the suspension was washed with deionized water and centrifuged multiple times to separate the powders. The precipitate was dispersed in water and delaminated using probe sonication for 30 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. Finally, after freeze-drying and annealing at 750 °C, MgB2 nanosheets were obtained.

Fabrication of PVA solution (2% glycerol concentration)

First, 1 g glycerol was added into 48 g DIW. After that, 1 g of PVA was added to the glycerol-DIW solution at 95 °C and stirred at 450 rpm for 1 h. The glycerol concentration of the final solution should be 2 wt%. For glycerol-dependent experiments, the glycerol concentration was varied as specified in the corresponding sections.

Fabrication of PVA-MgB2 solution (2% glycerol concentration)

First, 0.1 g NaOH was dissolved in 48 g deionized water (DIW). Then 1 g glycerol and 0.04 g MN were added into alkaline DIW and ultrasonicated with 200 W power for 20 min to obtain a well-dispersed solution. After that, 1 g of PVA was added to the glycerol-MgB2 solution at 95 °C and stirred at 450 rpm for 1 h. The glycerol concentration of the final solution should be 2 wt%.

Fabrication of PVA-CNT solution (2% glycerol concentration)

First, 1 g glycerol and 0.04 g MWCNT were added into 48 g DIW and ultrasonicated with 200 W power for 20 min to obtain a well-dispersed solution. After that, 1 g of PVA was added to the glycerol-DIW solution at 95 °C and stirred at 450 rpm for 1 h. The glycerol concentration of the final solution should be 2 wt%.

Fabrication of 3D printed molds

All samples used for mechanical and electrical tests were prepared with rectangular molds (40 mm wide and 80 mm long). To investigate the effect of mold shape on wrinkle patterns, we also employed other rectangular molds (20–80 mm wide and 80 mm long), a circular mold (64 mm in diameter), and an equilateral triangular mold (86 mm side length). All models were designed in SolidWorks and exported as STL files for 3D printing. The molds were fabricated using an Adventure 3 (Flashforge, China), a fused deposition modeling 3D printer with PLA filaments.

Fabrication of WSO

Unless otherwise specified, all samples characterized and utilized in this study were prepared using a heating temperature of 90 °C. Specifically, a PLA mold (area: 3200 mm²) was securely attached to a copper plate and placed onto a uniformly heated platform maintained at 90 °C. Subsequently, 12 g of the prepared PVA composite solution was poured into the mold, and WSO was obtained after a 40 min heating process. The heating duration was determined based on the evaporation mass-loss curve (Supplementary Fig. 33). For mass-dependent experiments, the precursor mass was varied as specified in the corresponding sections.

Fabrication of WFO

For comparison, WFO were fabricated by eliminating the temperature gradient during evaporation. Specifically, 12 g of the PVA composite solution was poured into a plastic petri dish (area: 3200 mm²) and heated at 90 °C for 6 h in an oven to obtain WFO.

Characterization of the emergence time of wrinkles, temperature differences

The emergence time of wrinkles was determined through visual observation. The moment when distinct wrinkle patterns first appeared on the surface was recorded as the emergence time of wrinkles. Surface temperature measurements were obtained using an infrared thermal camera (iRay, USA). For samples with wrinkle formation (heated above 50 °C), the average temperature difference was calculated by subtracting the minimum surface temperature from the maximum surface temperature over a 1 min interval immediately before wrinkle emergence. For samples without wrinkle formation (heated at 40 °C), the average temperature difference was determined by averaging the difference between the highest and lowest surface temperatures recorded over the 30 min interval immediately before complete evaporation of the polymer solution.

Characterization of PVA concentration in the skin layer at the time when wrinkle emerge

The PVA concentration at the time when wrinkles emerge was assessed by isolating the skin layer from the PVA solution. The wet mass of the skin layer (mwet) was first recorded. The sample was then dried in an oven at 80 °C for 48 h to ensure complete solvent removal, after which the dry mass (mdry) was measured. Thermal analysis

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA Q500, TA Instruments) was used to quantify the solvent content of the samples. Characterization of skin layers’ modulus and relaxation

A universal tensile machine (Instron 5500, USA) was used to test the modulus of the peeled skin layer.

Fabrication of the sliding sensor

To fabricate a sliding sensor, strips of silver conductive tapes (BJS tapes, China) were affixed to a 3 M VHB tape (3 M, USA) measuring 12 mm × 12 mm, with each silver tape having a width of 5 mm. Except for the silver portion, the rest of the region was coated with super glue. A 12 mm × 12 mm WSO-MN organo-hydrogel slice was then placed on a VHB tape to finish the fabrication.

Deep learning for sliding-gesture recognition

To evaluate the feasibility of recognizing and classifying sliding gestures from the collected sensor signals, we developed a five-layer feedforward ANN. The primary objective of the ANN was to recognize the type of slide gesture represented by a continuous input data stream. Each layer extracted features via fully connected nodes with rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation functions. The network was trained using a dataset comprising four distinct sliding signal classes, consistently collected with a bench multimeter (DMM6500, Keithley, USA). Each input sample contained 51 data points. The complete dataset was randomly divided into training (80%), validation (10%), and testing (10%) subsets. The ANN was trained for 20 epochs, and classification performance was assessed using accuracy, classification rate, and loss function metrics. Following training, the ANN was deployed on a laptop connected to the DMM6500 to perform real-time recognition of sliding gestures.

Fabrication of the rolling sensor

The rolling sensor was made using two slices of WSO-MN, with each slice measuring 5 mm × 12 mm. A single droplet of PVA-MgB2 solution with a glycerol content of 2% was utilized as an adhesive to join the terminals of two slices. The rolling sensor was finally acquired after being placed in an oven at a temperature of 80 °C for a duration of 5 min.

Fabrication of the WSO temperature alarm

The temperature alarm was fabricated using a 4.5 mm × 24 mm WSO as the switching module. One end of the WSO was attached to the top of an acrylic rectangular container (100 mm × 100 mm) using VHB tapes, and a convex metal ring was positioned at the opposite end. The entire circuit was connected with silver tape. The assembled acrylic container was then placed in a variable-temperature oven (Lichen, China) set to heat to 60 °C. A thermometer (Omega, USA) was used to record the temperature variation during the heating process.
