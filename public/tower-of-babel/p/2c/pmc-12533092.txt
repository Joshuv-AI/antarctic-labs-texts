A music source separation method integrating time–frequency decoupling and mamba-based state space modeling
Authors: Chongbin Zhang, Jiaxiang Zheng, Moxi Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Music source separation, as a fundamental task in intelligent audio processing, plays a critical role in enhancing the performance of music generation, editing, and understanding systems. However, existing separation models often suffer from structural limitations such as reliance on a single modeling path, entangled time-frequency representations, and difficulty in adapting to heterogeneous sound sources. Furthermore, they struggle to maintain an effective balance between long-range dependency modeling and inference efficiency. To address these challenges, this paper proposes a novel dual-path state space modeling architecture, MSNet. By introducing decoupled modeling mechanisms for temporal and frequency pathways, and incorporating Mamba-based state space units for multidimensional structural parsing of audio signals, MSNet enhances selective control and structural representation in time-frequency modeling. Experimental results demonstrate that MSNet achieves state-of-the-art performance on the MUSDB18 dataset across multiple evaluation metrics. In particular, it shows superior robustness and stability when dealing with dynamically complex sources such as vocals and drums. Additionally, the model achieves a real-time factor (RTF) below 0.1 while maintaining superior separation quality, making it suitable for deployment in practical applications. This study not only demonstrates the feasibility of state space models for complex audio modeling but also introduces a new architectural paradigm for music source separation that balances accuracy and efficiency. The implementation is publicly available at: https://github.com/NMLAB8/Mamba-S-Net.

Introduction

Music Source Separation (MSS), as a fundamental task in the field of audio signal processing, aims to recover individual sound sources, such as vocals, drums, bass, guitar, and keyboard, from a mixture of musical audio signals. This task not only holds significant theoretical value, but also plays an increasingly vital role in practical applications. Its core capability, extracting meaningful and structured source information, directly supports a wide range of applications including music production, audio restoration, augmented reality audio rendering, intelligent musical accompaniment, automatic lyric generation, collaborative online mixing, smart audio editing, and virtual instrument control. In particular, with the rise of Artificial Intelligence Generated Content (AIGC), human–computer interaction, and multimodal modeling, high-quality source separation has become a key bridge connecting perception, understanding, and generation. Consequently, the construction of high-fidelity, robust, and low-latency separation systems has become a central challenge in building intelligent audio understanding systems.

In recent years, deep learning methods have driven rapid progress in music source separation, with mainstream research following two primary directions: (1) mask estimation based on frequency-domain representations and (2) end-to-end modeling in the time domain directly on waveforms. The former typically leverages Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) to map audio waveforms into complex spectrograms and then applies network architectures such as U-Net, DenseNet, or Transformer to predict spectral masks corresponding to each source. Representative systems include Spleeter and Open-Unmix. These approaches offer relatively simple architectures, ease of training and deployment, and high accuracy, particularly in separating structured musical elements such as melody lines. Despite their effectiveness, these methods also face notable limitations: first, spectrograms, as static snapshots, struggle to capture long-term contextual information; second, their representational capacity for non-structured percussive instruments and rapid transient events is limited, often resulting in separation artifacts.

In contrast, time-domain modeling approaches, such as Demucs and Conv-TasNet, operate directly on waveform signals. These methods utilize convolutional, residual, or recurrent network modules to capture the temporal evolution of audio signals. By retaining phase information more effectively, they enhance the naturalness and perceptual quality of the reconstructed signals. Time-domain models often outperform frequency-domain methods in terms of reconstruction accuracy and perceptual evaluation, especially for non-stationary signals like drums and bass. However, their computational cost is significantly higher. The complexity of the model architecture and the large number of parameters impose greater demands on hardware resources and latency control. In practical deployments, such as mobile devices, online editing tools, and real-time performance systems, these models often struggle to meet both real-time and computational efficiency requirements.

Moreover, whether operating in the time or frequency domain, most existing models adopt a unified modeling path that overlooks structural differences among various sound sources in temporal and spectral dimensions. This lack of structural adaptability and modeling flexibility limits their performance in heterogeneous multi-source scenarios and impairs the robustness of the separation system.

Most current models still rely on conventional architectures such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), Long-Short-Term Memory Networks (LSTMs), or Transformers. Each of these has strengths in capturing either local dependencies or global contextual information, but also exhibits specific limitations. CNNs are effective in modeling local patterns, but struggle with long-range dependencies. LSTMs can capture temporal sequences but suffer from limited parallelizability, time-consuming training, and gradient vanishing issues. Although Transformers have achieved remarkable success in speech modeling, their fully connected attention mechanism incurs high computational costs, particularly when processing high-resolution audio spectrograms.

Given these challenges, the development of more efficient and selectively controllable temporal modeling frameworks has become a key direction in architectural optimization.

Against this backdrop, the Mamba model1 has emerged as a novel framework for Selective State Space Modeling, demonstrating strong capabilities in time series modeling with structural efficiency. Unlike traditional sequence modeling methods, Mamba is based on a state-space formulation using ordinary differential equations (ODE), which enables it to process long sequences with linear-time complexity. In addition, it incorporates mechanisms for selective filtering and dynamic control of state information, thereby avoiding the excessive computational overhead associated with global attention in long-sequence processing.

Recent studies have successfully applied Mamba to tasks such as speech recognition, language modeling, and time series forecasting, revealing its strengths in precise long-term dependency modeling, low-latency inference, and flexible expressive capacity. These characteristics suggest that Mamba has substantial theoretical and practical value for high-dimensional dynamic sequence modeling. Introducing it into the task of separation of music sources offers the potential to achieve an optimized balance between modeling depth, separation accuracy, and inference efficiency.

Based on the aforementioned considerations, this study proposes a music source separation network based on dual-path state space modeling, MSNet (Mamba Separation Network). The model introduces two independent yet synergistic pathways: a temporal path and a spectral path, each employing Mamba-based state-space modules to model the temporal dynamics and spectral structure of audio signals, respectively. Furthermore, MSNet incorporates a multiband division mechanism that partitions the audio spectrogram into low-, mid-, and high-frequency subbands. Modeling is performed independently within each subband to enhance the network’s capacity to analyze local spectral textures and pitch distributions.

The overall architecture of MSNet achieves multidimensional decoupled modeling from a macro level (time–frequency) to a micro level (multiband). Through a unified design across the encoding, fusion, and reconstruction stages, the model ensures smooth information flow and consistent representation among modules. In addition, MSNet emphasizes computational efficiency and deployability by employing parallel pathway designs, skip connections to reduce path length, input normalization, and residual connections to enhance training stability. During inference, the model achieves a real-time factor (RTF) below 1, satisfying the performance requirements of real-time music editing and integration of the audio system.

Related work

Music source separation

Separation of musical sources has long been a significant research topic in the field of audio signal processing, with its technological evolution reflecting a fundamental shift from traditional signal processing techniques to deep learning-based paradigms. Early methods, such as non-negative matrix factorization (NMF), aimed to decompose an audio spectrogram into a product of a dictionary matrix and an activation matrix, allowing for preliminary unsupervised source separation 2. However, the performance of NMF was constrained by the representational limitations of hand-crafted features. To overcome this bottleneck, sparse coding techniques 3, incorporating an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell _1$$\end{document} norm regularization term, were introduced to enhance separation accuracy. In parallel, Independent Component Analysis (ICA) 4, based on the assumption of statistical independence among signals, showed promising results in single-channel separation tasks.

With the advancement of deep learning, neural network-based methods have become dominant. The introduction of the U-Net architecture marked a milestone in the application of deep learning to vocal separation tasks. This model used an encoder-decoder structure with skip connections in the frequency domain and achieved an SDR of 11.7 dB on the MIR-1K data set 5,6. Subsequently, extensive research has been conducted on the MUSDB18 dataset 7. In particular, the Open-Unmix model 8 utilized LSTM layers and fully connected layers, offering a fully open source implementation and achieving an SDR of 5.3 dB in MUSDB18.

To improve the efficiency of extracting time-frequency characteristics, the MMDenseLSTM model integrated the structural advantages of CNN and LSTM, producing an SDR of 6.0 dB in the SiSEC 2018 evaluation 9,10. D3Net further improved performance by introducing multiscale dilated convolutions and dense connections to significantly enlarge the receptive field, achieving an SDR of 6.8 dB in the MUSDB18-HQ data set 11.

To avoid potential information loss caused by spectral transformations, waveform-based modeling in the time domain has attracted increasing attention in recent years. The Wave Domain Network was the first to demonstrate the feasibility of end-to-end music source separation in the waveform domain 12. Although its performance was slightly inferior to that of frequency-domain methods, its technological significance is noteworthy. This waveform-based modeling strategy was initially explored in speech separation and was later extended to music signal processing. Wave-U-Net adopted a multiscale structure to achieve complete end-to-end modeling in the time domain, achieving an SDR of 4.6 dB in temporal separation tasks 13. Building on this foundation, the Demucs model 14 introduced a deep encoder–decoder architecture in the waveform domain, incorporating bidirectional LSTM layers and attaining an SDR of 6.3 dB on the MUSDB18 test set, with particularly strong performance in separating vocals, drums, and bass.

Inspired by Demucs, Conv-TasNet 15, originally designed for speech separation, was adapted for music source separation. Replaced the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) with a learnable encoder and applied a temporal convolutional network, achieving an SDR of 7.0 dB. This established a new paradigm for end-to-end modeling in music separation.

With further advances in the field, hybrid domain methods marked another breakthrough in music source separation. The HDemucs model 16 effectively integrated spectral and waveform information to overcome the limitations of single-representation modeling, achieving an SDR of 8.4 dB in the MUSDB18 dataset. The LaSAFT method 17 introduced attention mechanisms in the frequency domain and performed source separation in latent space, using targeted weight modulation to significantly enhance the model’s ability to distinguish instrument timbres, achieving an SDR of 8.9 dB for vocal separation. KUIELab-MDX-Net 18 adopted a dual-stream architecture to process the features of the time and frequency domain separately and utilized a carefully designed fusion mechanism to address inconsistencies between domains. It ultimately won first place in the MDX 2021 international challenge 19.

With the success of Transformers across various domains, Transformer-based architectures have also gained traction in music source separation. Although SepFormer 20 was originally designed for the single-channel speech separation task in the WSJ0-2mix dataset 21, it was the first to introduce a pure attention-based mechanism into source separation. By alternating short- and long-range attention layers, it modeled temporal–spectral dependencies and achieved an SDR of 20.4 dB in speech tasks. Although not directly developed for music separation, SepFormer provided important insights for the subsequent application of attention structures in this field. The Band-Split RoFormer model 22 addressed the variability of spectral characteristics between frequency bands by introducing band-wise processing and rotary positional encoding, reaching an SDR of 9.2 dB in the MUSDB18-HQ dataset. The Sound of Pixels method 23 pioneered the integration of visual information into music separation. Through joint audiovisual learning, it significantly improved the separation accuracy in complex scenes, increasing the separation accuracy in the MUSIC dataset by 15%.

In terms of practicality and flexibility of the model, researchers have proposed a series of innovative solutions. The Query-based Source Separation method 24 introduced a query mechanism that enables the system to handle previously unseen instrument types, thus extending the applicability beyond the limitations of traditional models. Conditioned U-Net 25 incorporated conditioning mechanisms into the U-Net architecture, allowing a single model to perform multiple source separation tasks, significantly reducing deployment costs. By introducing data augmentation and model ensemble strategies, the approach in 26 achieved an improvement in SDR of 1.5 dB on the MUSDB18 test set. The mix invariant training method 27 was the first to implement fully unsupervised training for music source separation. Using a mixture-invariance constraint mechanism, it reduced the dependency on labeled data by 80%.

The integration of multitask learning has further advanced the field. The Separation-Transcription Joint Network (Cerberus) 28 demonstrated the potential for simultaneous modeling of separation and transcription in polyphonic mixtures involving up to five instruments. Although originally designed to perform source separation and note transcription, the model also achieved an SDR of 10.0 dB in the pure separation task on the synthetic Slakh2100 dataset 29. The Activation-Aware Separation model 30 incorporated an auxiliary instrument activity detection task to enhance the model’s ability to capture the temporal structure of the instruments, leading to an SDR of 7.8 dB on the MUSDB18 dataset. The Multisource Diffusion Model 31 pioneered the application of diffusion models to the separation of music sources, using generative modeling to achieve high quality separation. It achieved a mean opinion score (MOS) of 4.2 out of 5 in subjective listening evaluations, demonstrating strong generative capabilities.

Evaluation metrics and benchmark datasets have also played a critical role in advancing the field. The first evaluation campaign on stereo music source separation 32 established core metrics such as Signal-to-Distortion Ratio (SDR), Signal-to-Interference Ratio (SIR) and Signal-to-Artifacts Ratio (SAR), providing a unified framework for subsequent performance comparisons. The MUSDB18 data set 7, which includes 150 full-length multitrack music recordings, has become the most widely adopted benchmark in this domain. Regarding the impact of loss functions on separation performance, a systematic study 33 showed that combining loss in the time and frequency domains can produce an SDR gain of up to 2.5 dB. Furthermore, the use of deep feature loss 34 significantly improved the perceptual quality of the separation results, receiving higher scores on subjective listening tests.

The development of open-source tools has significantly accelerated the practical deployment of music source separation technologies. The Spleeter model 35 optimized both the network architecture and the training strategy, achieving a five-fold increase in processing speed while maintaining high separation quality, allowing real-time music source separation. Open-source frameworks based on waveform and spectrogram domains were introduced, respectively, by 36 and 37, substantially lowering experimental barriers and development costs and facilitating deployment in academic and industrial settings. In recent years, related studies have continued to expand the applications of music source separation scenarios. For example, 38 introduced a crosstalk modeling mechanism in hearing aid applications, significantly improving separation performance in real-world acoustic environments. On the other hand, unsupervised separation methods based on generative models 39 offer promising solutions for low-resource settings, achieving performance comparable to fully supervised models even with limited training data.

Despite the considerable progress achieved in recent years, mainstream separation models still face several key challenges. Typical architectures such as CNNs, RNNs, and transformers exhibit notable limitations in modeling long-range dependencies, handling multiscale structures, and supporting low-latency inference. RNNs are susceptible to gradient vanishing, making them ineffective in capturing long-term temporal context. Although Transformers offer global receptive fields, their attention mechanisms result in high computational complexity and memory usage, limiting their applicability to real-time or resource-constrained audio processing tasks. In addition, music signals often involve complex superpositions of multiple sources and exhibit prominent rhythmic periodicity, melodic structures, and hierarchical harmonics. These properties place higher demands on the models’ ability to achieve precise time–frequency separation.

To address these issues, this study introduces the recently proposed Mamba architecture, which is based on a Selective State Space Model (SSM). Mamba has linear computational complexity, robust modeling capabilities for long sequences, and high hardware compatibility. Through its structured recurrence mechanism and input-dependent selection strategy, Mamba effectively enhances the model’s capacity to capture salient audio features, particularly excelling in tasks that require efficient modeling of long-range context and cross-band information. Therefore, This study constructs a source separation model centered on the Mamba framework, aiming to improve separation accuracy and offer a more adaptive solution for multi-source separation in complex audio environments while maintaining real-time processing capability.

The Mamba architecture

Mamba is a recently proposed framework for long-sequence modeling, based on Selective State Space Models (Selective SSMs), initially introduced by Gu, Dao, and colleagues 1. The core idea of Mamba is to leverage the structured recurrence property of state space models to enable efficient long-sequence representation learning with linear computational complexity. Furthermore, Mamba incorporates a selection mechanism into the traditional state-space framework, allowing the model to dynamically focus on salient segments of the input sequence. Compared to fixed-parameter linear time-invariant models such as S4, this mechanism achieves a notable advantage in balancing inference efficiency and contextual modeling capability.

Unlike transformer architectures, which rely on global attention mechanisms and incur computational complexity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(n^2)$$\end{document}, Mamba adopts a hardware-efficient Selective Scan algorithm that reduces the sequence processing complexity to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(n)$$\end{document}. Its implementation integrates recomputation, prefix-sum parallel scans, and memory-aware scheduling strategies, enabling higher execution efficiency on modern GPU platforms. These optimizations allow Mamba to maintain low resource consumption while achieving competitive performance in various tasks. Experimental results demonstrate that Mamba performs on par with, or even exceeds, Transformer-based architectures in language modeling, audio modeling, and video understanding tasks 1,40.

The generalizability of the Mamba architecture has been extensively validated across multiple domains. In image processing, visual variants such as Vision Mamba (ViM) 41 and LocalMamba 42 have successfully introduced techniques including bidirectional scanning, local window modeling, and cross-channel spatial fusion. These adaptations have enabled effective application to tasks such as image classification, semantic segmentation, and super-resolution, demonstrating strong feature modeling capacity and scalability.

In the field of speech processing, Mamba has been applied to speech enhancement, source separation, and automatic speech recognition tasks. Studies show that Mamba is particularly effective in reconstruction-oriented tasks, while classification-oriented tasks often benefit from the integration of additional decoders or post-processing modules to achieve optimal performance 43.

Although Mamba, as a new generation of Selective State Space Models (Selective SSMs), remains in the early stages of research, its structural advantages and task adaptability have already begun to emerge. On the one hand, Mamba enhances the extraction of salient information in long sequences through its input-dependent selection mechanism while maintaining linear inference complexity, thus overcoming the computational bottleneck \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(n^2)$$\end{document} inherent in traditional Transformer architectures with global attention. However, its unified and modular architectural design provides a solid foundation for cross-modal applications in the language, vision, and audio domains.

In recent years, the adaptability of the Mamba architecture has continued to expand. For example, the Cobra model 44 integrates Mamba into the backbone of multimodal large language models (MLLM), showing notable advantages in cross-modal alignment and spatial reasoning. The Mamba variants have also demonstrated high flexibility and computational efficiency in graph neural networks 45, remote sensing image processing 46, and long-video understanding 47.

Compared to conventional sequence-modeling architectures, such as CNNs, RNNs, and Transformers, Mamba exhibits distinctive strengths in long-sequence processing. It offers notable improvements in modeling long-range dependencies, compressing redundant information, and reducing memory consumption. By combining structured recurrence with an input-based selection mechanism, Mamba captures critical structural features in sequential signals while maintaining linear computational complexity. These characteristics make it particularly effective for audio modeling tasks, especially when dealing with musical audio data that exhibit strong temporal continuity and hierarchical structure.

In music source separation tasks, audio signals often span long durations and involve complex overlapping sources with non-stationary dynamics. Traditional RNNs struggle with vanishing gradients and limited memory capacity when modeling long-term dependencies, whereas Transformers, though powerful, suffer from high computational overhead and inference latency due to their global attention mechanism. This makes them less suitable for real-time or resource-constrained environments. Mamba, by maintaining a balance between modeling depth and computational efficiency, enables more effective separation of multiple musical sources, such as vocals, drums, bass, and accompaniment, particularly in complex compositions with sustained structures and wide dynamic ranges.

Therefore, incorporating Mamba into the separation of music sources enhances the model’s capacity for long-range and multiscale structural modeling while maintaining practical computational complexity for real-time applications. This provides a new direction for the building of end-to-end source separation systems that achieve high accuracy and real-time performance.

Method

This section systematically elaborates on the overall architecture and key technical pathways of the proposed MSNet model for multi-source audio separation. The discussion encompasses the complete pipeline, including audio pre-processing, multiband encoding, Mamba-based state modeling, decoding and reconstruction, and loss function optimization.

Architecture overview

MSNet is a modular deep neural network designed for multi-source music source separation tasks. It performs joint modeling in both the frequency and temporal dimensions. The system takes dual-channel time-domain audio signals as input and generates reconstructed signals for each individual source through a multistage process comprising feature transformation, frequency-band encoding, Mamba-based state modeling, decoding, and inverse transformations. Considering the non-linear characteristics of human auditory perception, the spectrogram is divided into three non-overlapping bands: low frequency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[0, F_1)$$\end{document}, mid frequency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[F_1, F_2)$$\end{document}, and high frequency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[F_2, F)$$\end{document}.

Each frequency band is processed by an independently configured convolutional encoder with distinct stride and kernel size settings for sparse downsampling. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\bf X}_i$$\end{document} denote the input for the i-th frequency band, the downsampled output is given by:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\bf Y}_i = \textbf{C}_i(\textbf{P}({\bf X}_i)) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{P}(\cdot )$$\end{document} denotes symmetric padding along the frequency dimension, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{C}_i$$\end{document} is a 2D convolutional operation with customized configurations. The kernel size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_i \in \{3, 4, 16\}$$\end{document} and stride \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_i \in \{1, 4, 16\}$$\end{document} correspond to low-, mid-, and high-frequency compression strategies, respectively. The output channel number of all encoders is kept consistent to ensure compatibility for subsequent channel-wise fusion.

Following downsampling, depthwise separable convolution modules \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{M}_i$$\end{document} are used to enhance the representational capacity of frequency-specific features. Each module comprises normalization, pointwise convolution, gated activation, depthwise convolution, non-linear activation, and residual connection. Specifically, the input is first normalized using Group Normalization, then passed through a 1D convolution for channel feature extraction. A Gated Linear Unit (GLU) suppresses redundant dimensions, followed by a depth-wise convolution for local context extraction. The result is normalized again and activated using the Swish function, and finally assigned to the target output dimension via a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 \times 1$$\end{document} convolution. A residual connection adds the original input to the output. The depth of convolution varies across frequency bands: three layers for the low-frequency band, two for mid-frequency, and one for high-frequency.

The outputs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{Z}_i$$\end{document} from all frequency bands are concatenated along the channel dimension and fed into a unified fusion module. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{G}$$\end{document} denote a 2D convolution operator; then the fused output tensor is:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\bf H} = \textbf{G}(\textrm{Concat}(\textbf{Z}_1, \textbf{Z}_2, \textbf{Z}_3)) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The resulting tensor has the shape \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\bf H} \in \mathbb {R}^{B \times C \times F' \times T}$$\end{document}, where C is the number of channels and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F'$$\end{document} is the compressed frequency dimension. The encoder as a whole is constructed as a three-layer stack, with output channels of 32, 64, and 128, respectively, to provide stable and multi-scale feature representations for the subsequent time-frequency state modeling module.

Mamba-based time–frequency decoupled network

The core separation module employs the Mamba architecture based on the Selective State Space Model (SSM) to model sequential dependencies in the audio spectrograms. This module adopts an alternating modeling strategy between frequency and temporal paths, combined with Fourier transforms and inverse Fourier transforms, enabling effective time–frequency interaction while maintaining linear computational complexity. The structure is illustrated in Fig. 2.

The Mamba model builds upon the framework of continuous linear time-invariant state-space systems, defined as:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d\textbf{h}(t)}{dt} = \textbf{A} \textbf{h}(t) + \textbf{B} \textbf{u}(t), \quad \textbf{y}(t) = \textbf{C} \textbf{h}(t) + \textbf{D} \textbf{u}(t) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{u}(t)$$\end{document} denotes the input, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{h}(t)$$\end{document} the hidden state, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{y}(t)$$\end{document} the output. The matrices \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{A}, \textbf{B}, \textbf{C}, \textbf{D}$$\end{document} represent the state transition, input, output, and input transformations, respectively.

To accommodate discretized audio sequences, the system is discretized using a Zero-Order Hold (ZOH) method. The state update at each time step is expressed as:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{h}_t= & \exp (\Delta _t \odot \textbf{A}) \odot \textbf{h}_{t-1} + (\Delta _t \odot \textbf{B}_t) \odot \textbf{u}_t \end{aligned}$$\end{document}10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{y}_t= & \sum _k \left( \textbf{h}_{t,k} \cdot \textbf{C}_{t,k} \right) + \textbf{D} \odot \textbf{u}_t \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\exp (\cdot )$$\end{document} represents the element-wise exponential, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta _t$$\end{document} is a learnable The model parameters are generated dynamically as a function of the input. Given an input sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} To effectively model the two-dimensional structure of the audio tensors, a dual path Mamba encoding mechanism is proposed, where the modeling is performed separately along the frequency and temporal dimensions. Let the input tensor be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\bf X} \in \mathbb {R}^{B \times C \times F \times T}$$\end{document}, where F and T represent the number of frequency bins and time frames, respectively, C is the number of channels and B is the batch size.

First, the input tensor is reshaped along the frequency path as follows:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\bf X}_f = \text {reshape}_f({\bf X}) \in \mathbb {R}^{(B \cdot T) \times F \times C} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {reshape}_f(\cdot )$$\end{document} denotes the operation of merging the time frames into the batch dimension, treating the frequency axis as the sequence dimension. \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {reshape}_t(\cdot )$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {reshape}_o(\cdot )$$\end{document} refer to the transformations from frequency-path output to time-path input, and from time-path output back to the original tensor shape, respectively.

To enhance the interaction between frequency and temporal features, a frequency-domain transformation is inserted after each Mamba layer. Even-numbered layers apply the real-valued Fast Fourier Transform (RFFT), while odd-numbered layers apply the inverse RFFT (IRFFT), forming an alternating iterative modeling mechanism:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\bf H}^{(i+1)} = {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \text {RFFT}(\text {Mamba}({\bf H}^{(i)})), & i \bmod 2 = 0 \\ \text {IRFFT}(\text {Mamba}({\bf H}^{(i)})), & i \bmod 2 = 1 \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The entire separation network stacks six dual-path Mamba modules, each followed by a transformation operator \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {T}_i$$\end{document} and a residual skip connection. This design enhances the representational capacity of the network and preserves the stability of the gradient. The iterative modeling process can be described as follows:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\bf H}^{(0)} = {\bf X}_{\text {enc}}, \quad {\bf H}^{(i+1)} = \text {T}_i(\text {M}_i({\bf H}^{(i)})), \quad i = 0,1,\dots ,5 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\bf X}_{\text {enc}}$$\end{document} is the encoder output, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {M}_i$$\end{document} denotes the i-th Mamba module, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {T}_i$$\end{document} represents the corresponding frequency domain transformation.

Decoder and audio reconstruction

To achieve a complete mapping from abstract separation features to perceptible audio signals, a decoder and reconstruction module is constructed, designed symmetrically with respect to the encoder in terms of structure and multi-scale feature processing strategies. This module not only performs upsampling and channel restoration, but also retains key local structural information through skip connections, ultimately producing the separated multi-channel audio signals.

The decoder first receives the output tensor from the Mamba separation module, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\bf H}_{\text {mamba}} \in \mathbb {R}^{B \times C \times F \times T}$$\end{document}, and integrates it with the encoder skip features using gated skip connections. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\bf H}_d$$\end{document} denote the current decoder features, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\bf S}_s$$\end{document} the skip features from the encoder side, the fusion process is defined as:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{F} = \text {G}(\text {R}({\bf H}_d + {\bf S}_s)) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {R}(\cdot )$$\end{document} denotes channel expansion and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {G}(\cdot )$$\end{document} is a gating activation function. This mechanism enables the effective integration of semantic features from multiple levels while preserving structural consistency across the decoding pathway.

Next, learnable transposed convolutions are applied along the frequency dimension for sparse upsampling. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\bf X}_i$$\end{document} be the input in layer i, with kernel size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_i$$\end{document} and stride \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_i$$\end{document}, the upsampling operation is given by:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\bf Y}_i = \text {U}_{s_i,k_i}({\bf X}_i) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {U}_{s_i,k_i}(\cdot )$$\end{document} denotes a 2D transposed convolution with kernel size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_i$$\end{document} and stride \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_i$$\end{document}. This upsampling method supports non-uniform reconstruction across frequency bands and mirrors the downsampling configurations of the encoder.

As convolution operations may introduce mismatches in length, symmetric cropping is applied at each layer to align the frequency dimension with the original spectrogram. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell _c$$\end{document} be the current output length and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell _o$$\end{document} the target length, the valid cropped region is:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\bf Y}_{\text {trim}} = {\bf Y}[:, :, d : d + \ell _o, :], \quad d = \frac{|\ell _c - \ell _o|}{2} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The final output tensor of the decoder is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\bf H} \in \mathbb {R}^{B \times 16 \times F \times T}$$\end{document}, corresponding to the 4 sources and the 2 stereo channels, each comprising real and imaginary parts, i.e., the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$4 \times 4 = 16$$\end{document} channels.

To reconstruct the audio signals, this tensor is first reshaped into a complex-valued spectrogram format. Given the number of sources \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 4$$\end{document}, the reshaped tensor has the form:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\bf S} \in \mathbb {R}^{B \times n \times 4 \times F \times T} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, each sub-tensor of size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$4 = 2 \times 2$$\end{document} represents two stereo channels per source, each containing real and imaginary components.

Subsequently, inverse normalization is applied to restore the spectral magnitude. Given the normalization statistics–mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu$$\end{document} and standard deviation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma$$\end{document}—the restoration is defined as:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{{\bf S}} = {\bf S} \cdot \sigma + \mu \end{aligned}$$\end{document}After the real-valued representations are converted into complex spectrograms, an inverse short-time Fourier transform (ISTFT) is performed to return to the time domain. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\bf S}_c$$\end{document} denote the complex spectrogram for each source, and the reconstruction step is:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\bf S}_{\text {time}} = \text {T}^{-1}({\bf S}_c) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {T}^{-1}(\cdot )$$\end{document} denotes the ISTFT operation, configured with the same window parameters as the STFT forward.

Finally, the padded region is removed by cropping the output time-domain tensor:25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\bf S}_{\text {final}} = {\bf S}_{\text {time}}[:, :, :, :-p] \in \mathbb {R}^{B \times 4 \times 2 \times L} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where p is the original padding length and L the initial sampling length. The dimension 4 represents the number of sources (vocals, drums, bass, others), and 2 indicates the stereo channels. This output constitutes the final result of multi-source audio separation.

Model optimization

To improve the spectral reconstruction accuracy of multi-source audio signals, this study adopts a frequency-domain Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) as the sole optimization objective during training. This loss function measures the difference in point amplitude between predicted and reference complex spectrograms and aggregates the squared errors across frequency, time, and channel dimensions. It achieves global modeling while ensuring perceptual consistency and numerical stability.

Let the reconstructed waveform tensor output of the model be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{{\bf S}} \in \mathbb {R}^{B \times N \times M \times L}$$\end{document}, and the corresponding ground truth be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\bf S} \in \mathbb {R}^{B \times N \times M \times L}$$\end{document}, where B is the batch size, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N = 4$$\end{document} denotes the number of sources, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M = 2$$\end{document} is the number of channels, and L is the sampling length. Each audio sequence is flattened into \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(B \cdot N \cdot M)$$\end{document} one-dimensional signals, and a Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) is applied to obtain the complex-valued spectrograms:26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{{\bf X}} = \text {STFT}(\hat{{\bf S}}), \quad {\bf X} = \text {STFT}({\bf S}) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}To define the loss in real-valued space, the complex spectrograms are separated into real and imaginary parts, resulting in the tensors:27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{{\bf X}}_r, {\bf X}_r \in \mathbb {R}^{B \times N \times M \times F \times T \times C} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where F is the number of frequency bins, T is the number of time frames, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C = 2$$\end{document} denotes the real and imaginary channels. For each sample b and source n, the spectral loss is defined as:28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {L}_{\text {spec}}(b, n) = \frac{1}{F T C} \sum _{f=1}^{F} \sum _{t=1}^{T} \sum _{c=1}^{C} \left( \hat{{\bf X}}_{b,n,:,f,t,c} - {\bf X}_{b,n,:,f,t,c} \right) ^2 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The final training objective is computed as the root mean square across the batch and source dimensions:29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {L}_{\text {total}} = \frac{1}{B N} \sum _{b=1}^{B} \sum _{n=1}^{N} \sqrt{ \mathscr {L}_{\text {spec}}(b,n) } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This loss function emphasizes the magnitude difference in the spectrogram domain, effectively avoiding the sensitivity to phase errors commonly encountered in time-domain modeling. It also aligns more closely with the perceptual characteristics of the human auditory system, particularly in relation to energy distribution.

Throughout the training process, the model is optimized exclusively using this frequency-domain loss. No time-domain reconstruction terms or adversarial regularization objectives are introduced, thereby preserving the simplicity and interpretability of the training objective, reducing hyperparameter dependencies, and enhancing convergence stability.

At this point, all components of the proposed MSNet architecture have been detailed, including audio preprocessing, multi-band encoding, dual-path Mamba-based time–frequency modeling, symmetric decoding, and reconstruction strategies. The complete computational procedure of MSNet is summarized in Algorithm 1, which provides a structured overview of the forward pass of the model for multi-source audio separation. In the following section, we proceed to describe the experimental setup and evaluation results to assess the effectiveness and generalization performance of the proposed framework.

MSNet: time–frequency decoupled Mamba-based source separation

Experiments

To comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed MSNet model in the task of separating music sources, this section presents a systematic experimental analysis from three perspectives. First, we introduce the training configuration and inference procedures to ensure reproducibility and consistency. Second, we compare MSNet’s performance against representative baseline models to assess its overall source separation capability. Finally, we conduct ablation studies to investigate the individual contributions and modeling effects of key components in the architecture. Each of these aspects is discussed in detail below.

Training and inference settings

In this study, the MSNet model is trained and evaluated on the publicly available MUSDB18 dataset. This dataset consists of 150 stereo music tracks, each comprising four isolated sources: vocals, drums, bass, and other accompaniment. As a widely adopted benchmark in the music source separation community, MUSDB18 provides a standardized basis for model evaluation.

During the training phase, all audio samples are resampled to 44.1 kHz. Each stereo signal is segmented using an 8-second sliding window with a stride of 1 second to augment sample diversity. The segmented audio is then transformed into complex-valued spectrograms via the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT), using a window size of 4096, a hop length of 1024, and the Hann window function. The resulting complex spectrogram is further decomposed into its real and imaginary components along the channel dimension. Before input of the model, all spectrograms undergo Z-score normalization to enhance training stability and accelerate feature convergence.

Model training is conducted for a total of 100 epochs using the Adam optimizer, with an initial learning rate set to 0.0005. An exponential decay strategy is applied every 10 epochs with a decay rate of 0.98. The batch size is set to 32, the weight decay factor is zero, and the momentum terms are configured as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _1 = 0.9$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _2 = 0.999$$\end{document}. All experiments are carried out with a fixed random seed of 42 to ensure reproducibility.

To enhance model robustness, several data augmentation strategies are employed during training, including random amplitude scaling (scaling ratio between 0.25 and 1.25), channel flipping, remixing across sources, and random segment shifting. However, no homogeneous time-shifting augmentation is applied.

In terms of implementation, both model training and inference are performed on four parallel NVIDIA A800 GPUs using the PyTorch framework (version 2.2). To improve I/O efficiency, eight worker threads are allocated for data loading.

Experimental design

Objective evaluation

To systematically evaluate the performance and structural effectiveness of MSNet in multi-source audio separation tasks, two categories of experiments are conducted: (1) comparative evaluations with representative baseline models and (2) ablation analyses targeting key components of the model architecture. All experiments are carried out on the MUSDB18 dataset, using a consistent data partitioning, preprocessing pipeline, and training configuration in the training and evaluation stages. To ensure the reliability and statistical significance of the results, each model is trained five times independently under identical settings, and the average performance metrics are reported.

For baseline comparisons, three widely adopted music source separation models are selected: Spleeter35, HDemucs v316 and Demucs14. Spleeter is a typical spectrogram-masking regression model that employs a U-Net architecture to estimate masks in the frequency domain. It is known for its high separation efficiency and lightweight design. HDemucs v3 represents a hybrid approach that combines both frequency-domain and time-domain processing, utilizing a transformer-based architecture with multi-scale feature extraction to leverage the advantages of both spectral and temporal representations. In contrast, Demucs adopts a full-time-domain modeling approach using convolutional layers and bidirectional LSTMs, allowing direct waveform-level reconstruction of source signals and demonstrating strong performance in terms of fidelity and transient preservation. These three models, respectively, represent the dominant paradigms in frequency-domain, hybrid-domain, and time-domain approaches, serving as a comprehensive comparative framework for validating the performance of MSNet. All models are trained with stereo 44.1 kHz audio inputs and produce four-stem output (vocals, bass, drums, and other), under identical training epochs, optimizer settings, batch sizes, and learning rate schedules.

Evaluation metrics are computed using the BSS Eval v4 toolkit, including Signal-to-Distortion Ratio (SDR), Signal-to-Interference Ratio (SIR), Signal-to-Artifacts Ratio (SAR), and Image-to-Spatial Ratio (ISR), providing a comprehensive assessment of model performance in fidelity, interference suppression, and structural reconstruction. In addition, the Real-Time Factor (RTF), defined as the processing time per unit length of audio during inference, is reported to evaluate the feasibility of the model’s deployment and runtime efficiency.

In the ablation study, three model variants with specific architectural modifications are designed to analyze the contributions of key modules in MSNet. First, the No-BandSplit variant removes the frequency band split mechanism in the original model, employing a unified convolutional encoder for the entire spectrum. This design assesses the role of multi-band structures in frequency-specific separation and local modeling. Second, to evaluate the dual-path Mamba modeling strategy proposed in MSNet, two comparative variants are constructed. The first, No-TimeMamba, places all Mamba layers along the frequency path and removes the state-space evolution in the temporal path, eliminating time–frequency transformations. The second, No-FreqMamba, retains only the temporal path while disabling all Mamba layers of the frequency path, thus limiting the model’s ability to capture temporal dynamics while neglecting global modeling across the frequency dimension. These two variants represent single-path frequency modeling and single-path temporal modeling, enabling an investigation into the necessity of dual-dimensional modeling for complex source separation tasks. All ablation models maintain the same parameter count, data augmentation strategies, and training procedures, with only the modeling paths selectively activated or deactivated, ensuring a controlled structural performance analysis.

Through the above comparative and ablation experiments, this study provides a systematic and multidimensional evaluation of the design merits of MSNet, including its modeling strategy, frequency decomposition scheme, and state-space modeling mechanisms.

Subjective evaluation

To comprehensively assess the separation quality of the models, we organized an evaluation panel consisting of 10 experienced listeners for comprehensive subjective evaluation, including audio engineers, music producers, and musicians with extensive audio processing backgrounds. We randomly selected three 10-s audio clips from the MUSDB18 test set, covering all four source categories (vocals, bass, drums, and others). Each evaluator was required to assess the separation results of these three clips in four models (MSNet, Spleeter, HDemucs v3, and Demucs), totaling 12 audio samples for evaluation.

The evaluation focused on two key aspects: (1) overall separation quality, measuring the perceptual fidelity and naturalness of separated sources; (2) cross-contamination, assessing the presence of unwanted artifacts or leakage from other sources. Both evaluation criteria used a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very poor, 5 = very good).

For each test clip, the assessors listened to the separation results of the four models in random order without knowing the identity of the model. The evaluation interface presented each separated source individually, enabling evaluators to focus on specific quality aspects and avoid bias from source mixing effects.

The subjective evaluation was conducted in a controlled acoustic environment using high-quality studio monitoring speakers. Each evaluator completed the entire assessment in approximately 30 minutes. Before formal evaluation, all participants underwent brief training using sample audio clips to familiarize themselves with the evaluation criteria and interface operations. During the evaluation process, participants could replay audio clips as needed and were encouraged to use the entire scoring range to ensure adequate discrimination between models.

The evaluation results are reported as mean opinion scores and standard deviations between all evaluators and test samples.

Baseline comparison results

Objective evaluation

Table 1 and Fig. 3 summarize the source separation performance of the MSNet, Demucs,HDemucs v3 and Spleeter instruments in the class of instruments. Overall, MSNet consistently outperforms the other three models across all evaluation metrics, demonstrating the superiority of its architecture in multi-source modeling tasks. To provide context for the quantitative results, it is important to establish typical performance ranges for the evaluation metrics used. In music source separation, SDR (Signal-to-Distortion Ratio) values above 6 dB are generally considered good performance, with values exceeding 8 dB indicating excellent separation quality. SIR (Signal-to-Interference Ratio) measures interference suppression effectiveness, where values above 10 dB demonstrate satisfactory source isolation and values above 15 dB represent superior performance. SAR (Signal-to-Artifacts Ratio) assesses reconstruction fidelity, with values above 6 dB indicating acceptable artifact levels and higher values reflecting cleaner separation outputs. ISR (Image-to-Spatial-distortion ratio) evaluates spatial consistency, where values above 10 dB suggest good preservation of source characteristics. These benchmarks provide a framework for interpreting the absolute performance levels achieved by each model. A closer examination of the interaction between source types and metric dimensions reveals further insights into the strengths and limitations of each modeling approach.

In the vocal separation task, MSNet achieves an SDR of 8.36 dB, surpassing Demucs by 0.51 dB and HDemucs v3 by 0.44 dB. In particular, it also has the lowest standard deviation (±0.17), significantly lower than Demucs (±0.32), HDemucs v3 (±0.29), and Spleeter (±0.45), indicating both superior performance and improved stability. MSNet’s high SIR (17.37 dB) and ISR (13.54 dB) further reflect its excellent ability to reconstruct melodic content and suppress vocal interference, making it particularly suitable for voice-dominant applications such as karaoke source separation and automatic subtitle generation.

For bass separation, while HDemucs v3 achieves the best performance with an SDR of 7.31 dB, outperforming MSNet (7.26 dB) by a small margin, MSNet still retains competitive performance and a slight advantage over Demucs in SDR (7.26 vs 6.85 dB) and SIR (13.01 vs 12.25 dB), with a more pronounced improvement in SAR (6.75 vs 6.49). However, MSNet’s SAR performance is notably inferior to HDemucs v3’s 6.79 dB. This suggests that MSNet’s frequency-path modeling excels in extracting low-frequency harmonic content, which is essential for isolating sustained, non-transient accompaniment parts.

In the drum source category, HDemucs v3 demonstrates the strongest performance with an SDR of 8.42 dB and SIR of 14.52 dB, slightly outperforming MSNet’s SDR of 8.35 dB and SIR of 14.42 dB. While MSNet ranks second among all models, drum signals exhibit strong transients and complex rhythmic structures, placing high demands on the temporal continuity modeling of a model and the awareness of the local context. Although Demucs utilizes a time-domain approach with BiLSTM layers, it may suffer from long-term dependency attenuation. HDemucs v3’s hybrid approach combining spectrogram and waveform processing shows particular effectiveness for percussive content, while MSNet’s dual-path Mamba-based state-space design yields competitive modeling and good context consistency.

For the other source category, MSNet leads with an SDR of 6.49 dB, outperforming HDemucs v3 (6.18 dB), Demucs (6.13 dB), and Spleeter (4.22 dB). This category comprises heterogeneous, unstructured accompaniment signals such as percussion, guitar, and piano, posing significant challenges to generalization. MSNet’s design, combining frequency-band decomposition with dual-path coupling, enables flexible perception of signal patterns in both local frequency and global time domains, resulting in superior separation of nonstructured audio content.

Furthermore, the differences in standard deviations reflect notable disparities in model stability. MSNet consistently produces the smallest performance fluctuations, such as the ±0.17 standard deviation in vocal SDR, compared to ±0.29 for HDemucs v3 and ±0.45 for Spleeter. This consistency is particularly valuable for real-world applications that involve dynamically varying audio inputs.

In terms of overall performance, MSNet achieves an average SDR of 7.61 dB, outperforming HDemucs v3 by 0.15 dB, Demucs by 0.41 dB and substantially surpassing Spleeter’s 5.45 dB. MSNet also scores the highest in SIR and ISR. Although SAR improvements are more moderate, they suggest a balanced performance that maintains timbral naturalness while effectively separating signals and reconstructing structure.

Ablation experiments

To further verify the contributions of each key component of MSNet to overall performance, three structurally ablated variants, No-BandSplit, No-TimeMamba and No-FreqMamba, were designed and evaluated in the MUSDB18 dataset. Each variant was altered only at the specified structural layer, while training hyperparameters, optimizer configuration, data augmentation strategies, and random seeds were identical to those of the full model. This ensures that the results are directly comparable and suitable for causal analysis. Detailed performance metrics are reported in Tables 2, 3, 4 and 5.

In the No-BandSplit variant, the original band-splitting mechanism was removed and replaced with a unified convolutional encoder that models the entire frequency spectrum. Although this variant still achieves a high overall separation performance (overall SDR of 7.07 dB), it exhibits a drop of 0.54 dB compared to the full model. This indicates that while band splitting is not the core modeling framework, it plays a vital role in optimizing local spectral modeling capabilities and improving the model’s perception of complex source structures. Specifically, the Bass SDR decreases to 6.32 dB and the Other SDR to 6.06 dB, both significantly lower than those of the complete model. These source categories often contain extensive low-frequency extensions or unstructured components, with ambiguous spectral boundaries and dispersed energy distributions. Bandwise modeling helps fit and extract stable features within distinct frequency partitions, preventing contextual entanglement and reduced modeling efficiency that may arise from a unified encoder. Furthermore, this variant shows slight degradations in ISR and SAR, reflecting weakened timbral restoration and structural reconstruction capabilities, which underscores the importance of frequency-band awareness for preserving the naturalness and fidelity of separation results.

In contrast, the No-TimeMamba variant exhibits a more pronounced performance degradation, with its overall SDR dropping to 6.22 dB—a decrease 1.39 dB relative to the complete model -demonstrating that temporal path modeling is more central to the separation mechanism. This variant removes all state-space evolution modules along the time dimension, restricting sequence modeling solely to the frequency axis, and thus losing the ability to capture temporal structures and dynamic evolutions. The degradation is especially pronounced for vocals and drums, with the vocal SDR falling to 7.10 dB and the drum SDR to only 6.41 dB. This result indicates that for sources characterized by rhythmic patterns, phrasing, and legato transitions, the absence of temporal modeling hinders the model’s ability to effectively learn intrinsic dynamic organizational rules, resulting in discontinuous separation output and rhythmic misalignments. In the SIR and ISR metrics, which measure interference suppression and structural reconstruction, respectively, the No-TimeMamba variant also underperforms the full model, further confirming the indispensable role of the temporal path Mamba in processing rhythmically strong and rapidly varying audio components.

The No-FreqMamba variant exhibits the poorest performance among the three, as it completely removes the Mamba layers along the frequency path, retaining only temporal-path modeling. Its overall SDR drops to 5.58 dB, representing a decrease of more than 2 dB compared to the full model. All four sources show notable degradation, with the Bass and Other categories performing worst: the Bass SDR decreases to 4.82 dB, and the Other SDR drops further to 4.54 dB. These types of signal typically exhibit strong frequency continuity or broadband extension, where the distribution of energy and textural relationships across frequency bands plays a critical role in the quality of the separation. The absence of frequency-path modeling prevents the model from capturing global spectral features such as pitch variation, harmonic resonance, and spectral density distribution, severely impairing the modeling precision. Even for the Vocals category, the SDR drops to 6.86 dB, substantially lower than the 8.36 dB achieved by the full model. This further demonstrates that temporal modeling alone cannot compensate for the lack of spectral information, and frequency-axis modeling is equally essential for distinguishing vocal melodies from background accompaniment. Furthermore, the SAR and ISR metrics show significant reductions, falling to 4.99 dB and 9.32 dB, respectively, indicating that the model exhibits structural deficiencies in timbral fidelity and spectral shape reconstruction.

Taken together, the results of the three ablation experiments indicate that each structural component of MSNet plays a distinct, yet complementary, role in the separation task of the music source. The band-splitting mechanism enhances localized spectral resolution; the temporal-path Mamba module improves the modeling of rhythmic and structural evolutions; and the frequency-path Mamba module is crucial for capturing global pitch distributions and inter-spectral relationships. The synergy among these components ensures that MSNet achieves high fidelity, strong robustness, and low variance across multiple source types. Among the three, removal of the frequency path results in the most severe performance degradation, underscoring its fundamental role in accurate spectral modeling. The absence of a temporal path also significantly weakens the ability of the model to process dynamically transient signals. Therefore, the coordinated design of dual-path modeling not only enables complementary time-frequency representation, but also provides a more refined and multidimensional foundation for characterizing diverse audio sources, which is a key innovation of the architectural strategy of MSNet.

Summary

Based on the results of the baseline comparisons and ablation experiments, MSNet demonstrates competitive performance in multi-source separation tasks on the MUSDB18 dataset. It achieves notable performance on vocally and rhythmically complex sources, such as vocals and drums, obtaining higher scores across multiple evaluation metrics including SDR, SIR, SAR, and ISR compared to existing mainstream models. While HDemucs v3 shows superior performance in bass and drum separation, demonstrating the effectiveness of hybrid spectrogram-waveform processing, MSNet excels particularly in vocal and other source separation. Although Demucs and Spleeter exhibit certain separation capabilities, they show limitations in aspects such as model stability, spectral fidelity, and rhythmic consistency compared to both MSNet and HDemucs v3.

Ablation studies validate the contributions of each architectural component within MSNet. The band-splitting mechanism improves the resolution of local spectral features, the Mamba-based temporal path effectively models signal evolution over time, and the frequency path maintains fidelity in reconstruction of spectral distributions and timbral characteristics. These components collectively establish a multiscale, multibranch, and time-frequency coupled modeling framework that provides structured support for the separation of complex mixed audio signals.

Furthermore, subjective evaluation results provide additional evidence, showing that MSNet maintains a slight advantage over other models, though the differences are marginal. Statistical analysis indicates that the perceptual differences between MSNet, HDemucs v3, and Demucs approach the threshold of human auditory discrimination, suggesting that the main audio separation technologies currently have reached a mature level in terms of perceptual quality.

In general, MSNet demonstrates the effectiveness of its architectural design through consistent empirical performance across multiple evaluation approaches, achieving competitive results alongside HDemucs v3 as one of the leading models in the field, indicating its potential applicability for future deployment and extension in real-world application scenarios.

Discussion

Theoretical value and generalization significance of time–frequency modeling strategies

This study proposes the MSNet model, which employs a dual-path architecture to separately model the temporal and frequency structures of audio signals. The model integrates Mamba-based state space units along both the time and frequency paths to jointly capture the sequential evolution and spectral distribution of the signals. Ablation experiments demonstrate that removal of either the temporal or frequency Mamba module leads to a substantial decrease in overall performance, particularly in the separation of sources with structurally complex or rapidly changing characteristics. These empirical results confirm the necessity of decoupled time–frequency modeling in music source separation tasks. The benefits are not limited to improvements in separation accuracy, but also include greater consistency and reduced variance in the output, indicating improved robustness of the modeling strategy.

In the existing literature, most models tend to focus on either spectral-domain or time-domain modeling. For example, U-Net-based models that employ spectral masking49 and end-to-end time-domain architectures50, each excel within their respective domains, but often lack the capacity to fully represent time-frequency interactions. Recent work has begun to highlight the importance of incorporating both temporal and spectral structures. Models such as TFiLM51 and Dual-Path RNN52 introduce cross-dimensional fusion modules at various levels to address this issue.

Unlike conventional cascaded designs, the dual-path Mamba mechanism proposed in this study features a structurally symmetric and functionally decoupled architecture. This configuration allows the model to independently capture pitch-related spectral features and temporal dynamics. Moreover, the use of state-space modeling facilitates the representation of long-range dependencies, enabling a more effective trade-off between modeling coverage and convergence speed.

Furthermore, the proposed strategy demonstrates strong potential for generalization between tasks. In domains such as speaker recognition, emotion recognition, and pitch estimation, both spectral features, such as voiceprints, and temporal characteristics, such as emotional dynamics and acoustic continuity, constitute critical modeling dimensions. Previous studies have shown that spectral distribution patterns are highly correlated with speaker identity, while temporal amplitude envelopes significantly influence emotional perception and prosodic reconstruction53. Therefore, the dual-path state-space modeling framework adopted in MSNet is not limited to the task of separating music sources. Rather, its underlying modeling principles are applicable to a broader range of multimodal and multi-scale audio understanding tasks, offering a structural foundation for representing more complex audio events.

The performance of MSNet in this study substantiates the effectiveness of combining decoupled time-frequency modeling with state-space representations. This strategy improves the ability of the model to analyze structural features in various types of sources and exhibits notable flexibility and scalability. As such, it provides both theoretical support and practical direction for future research in cross-task modeling and multidimensional information integration.

Boundary effectiveness and task dependency of the frequency band splitting mechanism

In the proposed MSNet model, the frequency band splitting mechanism divides the complex spectrogram into three subbands—low, mid, and high frequencies—and feeds them into separate encoders for feature extraction. This design enhances the modeling of localized spectral structures. Ablation experiments show that, compared to the full model, the No-BandSplit variant exhibits performance degradation across multiple sources, with particularly noticeable drops in the Bass and Other categories. These results suggest that, although frequency band splitting may not be as critical as the dual-path Mamba mechanism, it plays a significant auxiliary role in improving spectral resolution and mitigating structural interference.

Audio signals typically exhibit pronounced structural heterogeneity in frequency dimension, with different frequency bands that encode distinct acoustic semantics and expressive features. For example, the low-frequency band generally conveys fundamental pitch and rhythmic contours, the mid-frequency band covers melodic content and core vocal harmonics, while the high-frequency band contains rich overtones, spatial cues, and percussive noise components54. In music source separation tasks, due to the non-uniform spectral distributions of different sources, dividing the spectrum into frequency bands can reduce mutual interference and enhance modeling focus. However, the commonly used three-band fixed splitting remains a heuristic strategy. Its boundary settings lack adaptability to the characteristics of the data, limiting the full use of spectral distributions, particularly under conditions that involve pitch shifts, timbre variations, or stylistic changes.

Recent studies have attempted to move beyond fixed-band strategies by exploring more generalized frequency modeling approaches. One direction involves introducing learnable band-splitting mechanisms, where boundary positions are parameterized and dynamically adjusted during training to better match the spectral characteristics of the input. For example, FSI-Net55 employs a Gaussian attention mechanism to automatically attend to the relevant frequency regions, outperforming traditional equal-division methods. Another direction leverages data-driven spectral clustering, either through spectral similarity-based grouping or unsupervised latent-space clustering using autoencoders. These methods avoid manual specification of frequency boundaries and instead learn optimized sub-band structures directly from data, thereby enhancing the representational capacity for complex source modeling.

Moreover, the effectiveness of frequency band splitting is inherently task-dependent. In the present study, the task involves four-source separation (vocals, bass, drums, others), with each source type exhibiting relatively distinct spectral coverage. Under such conditions, the three-band division is reasonably aligned with the task requirements. However, in more complex scenarios—such as five- or six-stem separation tasks involving instruments like violin, piano, or guitar, which exhibit substantial spectral overlap—the fixed three-band strategy may become inadequate or even lead to modeling conflicts between bands. To address this, future systems may consider implementing task-adaptive band structures, potentially leveraging meta-learning or multi-task frameworks to dynamically adjust the spectral modeling paths according to source types or data domains, thereby enhancing system generalizability and adaptability.

In summary, frequency band splitting, as a form of structural enhancement, demonstrates measurable performance gains in the current task setting. However, its static nature imposes limitations in terms of representational flexibility and task transferability. Future modeling efforts should aim to improve the adaptability of frequency modeling by introducing learnable architectures, cross-sample optimization mechanisms, or multi-scale spectral strategies. Such advancements would equip audio separation systems with greater robustness and versatility in complex environments, expanding their applicability in music production, audio restoration, and multichannel signal understanding.

Trade-off between modeling capacity and real-time efficiency

In the task of musical source separation, model performance is not measured solely by separation accuracy; practical considerations such as response speed and deployment feasibility are equally important, particularly in scenarios involving real-time processing, mobile deployment, or on-line platforms. MSNet incorporates a dual-path state-space modeling mechanism and a multi-band parallel encoding structure. Although this increases the complexity of the model compared to simpler approaches, empirical results show that the model still maintains real-time processing capability Its measured real-time factor (RTF) remains consistently below 1, satisfying the requirements for near-real-time audio processing and demonstrating strong system engineering capabilities.

Conventional audio separation models often face a clear trade-off between modeling capacity and computational efficiency. For example, the Demucs family achieves high fidelity through full time-domain modeling, but its architecture, consisting of multiple convolutional layers, LSTM units, and long sequence processing components, significantly increases inference time. This presents a deployment challenge, particularly on low-power or mobile devices. In contrast, MSNet replaces traditional recurrent structures with Mamba-based state-space modules, which retain long-range modeling capabilities while significantly reducing the costs associated with computational graph expansion and memory usage. This enables a favorable balance between separation quality and practical deployment requirements. In particular, the parallel band structure of MSNet shortens the length of the inference path, and under multithreaded execution, it further compresses the overall computational latency.

From an architectural perspective, MSNet does not rely on excessive stacking or high-dimensional attention mechanisms. Instead, it achieves clarity in computational pathways and resource scheduling through the decoupling between structural modules and functional layering. Some recent studies have explored model distillation to produce lightweight separation models. The structural design of MSNet already exhibits a high degree of parameter compression potential, making it well suited for integration with distillation, pruning, and other model optimization techniques.

It is important to emphasize that as source separation systems are increasingly deployed in applications such as real-time accompaniment reconstruction, enhanced karaoke systems, and interactive mixing platforms, real-time capability is no longer an auxiliary metric, but a critical determinant of practical usability. Existing research has indicated that even slight degradations in reconstruction quality may be acceptable, provided that the system responds within the acceptable range for human-computer interaction; otherwise, the user experience may be significantly compromised. The ability of MSNet to strike a balance between high performance and low latency gives it strong potential for cross-platform applications, particularly in time-sensitive scenarios such as online audio services, VR / AR acoustic scene reconstruction and embedded music processing.

Therefore, MSNet’s architectural design reflects a strong awareness of both modeling depth and operational efficiency. Through effective path parallelism, state-space compression, and inference optimization strategies, the model achieves a dual advantage of theoretical modeling capacity and practical processing efficiency, indicating its feasibility and promise for real-world deployment.

Challenges and insights from source-type-specific modeling sensitivities

The experimental results of this study reveal distinct sensitivities of different source types to structural modeling mechanisms. For instance, vocal sources exhibit a substantial performance drop when the temporal Mamba module is removed, whereas bass and drum signals show stronger dependence on the integrity of frequency modeling components. This performance disparity indicates a strong coupling between source-type attributes and modeling structures, suggesting that a uniform modeling strategy may present representational limitations in multisource tasks. Consequently, designing architectures with source-type adaptivity emerges as a critical direction for future research.

Vocal signals typically exhibit clear prosodic patterns, alternating vowel–consonant structures, and pronounced temporal dependencies. Acoustically, they are characterized by alternating periodicity and energy bursts, making them particularly sensitive to temporal modeling quality. In ablation studies, the No-TimeMamba model experienced the greatest performance degradation in vocal separation, underscoring the importance of temporal modeling in the capture of human vocal expressions. In contrast, bass and drum signals tend to have more stable spectral structures and rhythmic regularity, displaying higher spectral consistency. Their modeling performance relies more heavily on frequency resolution. When the Mamba frequency path module is removed (No-FreqMamba), the separation performance of these sources decreases significantly, suggesting that the absence of frequency modeling directly impairs the integrity of low-frequency structures and rhythmic patterns. This asymmetric dependency between source types and modeling paths highlights the risk of a’mean performance trap’ in systems that rely on unified modeling strategies.

To address this challenge, future systems may incorporate dynamic modeling mechanisms that are aware of source-type characteristics and can adapt modeling paths based on the acoustic properties of the input. For example, in speech synthesis and speaker recognition, prior studies have introduced gating controllers or attention-routing mechanisms that direct different semantic sub-tasks through distinct architectural pathways, thereby enhancing specialization. Similar ideas may be applied to the separation of music sources by designing differentiated paths for signals with varying frequency energy distributions or temporal complexities. For instance, classifiers based on the spectral centroid or zero-crossing rate could be used to route rhythmically rich signals toward the temporal modeling path, while pitch-dominated sustained signals could be routed toward the frequency modeling path.

Another promising direction involves source-aware conditioning, where source-type information (e.g., embeddings or label prompts) is explicitly injected to guide the model in dynamically adjusting attention distributions or configuring state space parameters during training. Related techniques have shown promise in multi-speaker speech recognition and sound event detection. For example, Adaptive Computation Blocks (ACB) enable dynamic selection of network depth and computational paths based on the task, thereby improving modeling efficiency and generalization in complex input scenarios. Introducing such mechanisms into music source separation may help overcome the limitations of uniform-path designs in handling diverse signal types.

In summary, the observed heterogeneous dependency of different source types on modeling components provides valuable insight for the development of adaptive modeling mechanisms. Future systems should aim to retain the generalizability of the overall architecture while enhancing the responsiveness to source-type variability. Through mechanisms such as dynamic path selection, guided attention, or conditional control, models can be made more flexible and source-aware, ultimately achieving more robust performance across multi-source, multi-style, and high-complexity separation tasks.

Limitations and future work

Although the proposed MSNet model demonstrates significant advantages in architectural design and separation performance, particularly in dual path time-frequency modeling, band-splitting enhancement, and inference efficiency optimization, there are still several limitations in the scope and experimental settings of the current study that warrant further extension and refinement in future work.

The model was trained and evaluated exclusively on the MUSDB18 dataset. Although this data set has become a standard benchmark for music source separation, its coverage of musical genres, language diversity, and recording conditions is relatively limited and primarily includes four types of source (vocals, bass, drums, and others). This constraint limits the generalizability of the model to real-world scenarios, where source components are far more diverse. These may include traditional instruments, electronic effects, synthesizers, and background noise, whose spectral and temporal characteristics can significantly deviate from the training data. Future work could explore transferring the model to more realistic music environments with greater variation in language, style, and scene, or conducting generalization tests on more challenging open-domain audio datasets, such as Slakh2100, FUSS, or OpenMIC.

Although the effectiveness of structural modules was systematically verified through ablation studies, the current work lacks a fine-grained quantitative analysis of model complexity and inference resource overhead. MSNet achieves favorable inference speed on an A800 GPU, but its performance on embedded systems, mobile devices, or edge computing platforms remains to be evaluated. Future research may incorporate model compression, knowledge distillation, parameter sharing, and hardware-aware optimization strategies to meet the demands of low-power environments. Additionally, the stability of the dual-path Mamba modules under long-duration or ultra-wideband inputs, along with issues such as gradient propagation efficiency and potential state explosion, should be further investigated and optimized in large-scale training scenarios.

Furthermore, the current MSNet model employs a unified modeling path and optimization objective, without fully leveraging prior labels or source-type knowledge. For example, the model does not explicitly incorporate the ’target source category’ as a control signal during training, which limits its ability to perform conditional separation based on task directives. This poses practical challenges in applications such as interactive music editing or targeted stem extraction. Future work could explore conditional generation mechanisms or controlled attention to enable explicit modeling of target sources and selective enhancement, thus improving the model’s responsiveness to human-computer interaction and targeted processing tasks.

From a theoretical perspective, although state-space models such as Mamba have been increasingly recognized for their efficiency and expressive power in sequence modeling, their propagation dynamics and internal mechanisms over multidimensional spectrogram structures still lack a unified mathematical explanation. The current model demonstrates empirical effectiveness, but the state-space behavior along the frequency dimension remains largely unexplored in terms of interpretability. Future research could investigate this from the perspectives of graph-based modeling, multi-resolution transforms, or spectral attention flows, aiming to establish a more theoretically grounded architecture. Ultimately, a unified modeling framework for the time-frequency interaction can drive further improvements in model performance and structural innovation.

Conclusion

This study addresses the task of multisource music source separation by proposing MSNet, a structured separation model based on dual-path state-space modeling. The model is designed to overcome the limitations of single-domain modeling, insufficient separation stability, and deployment inefficiencies observed in existing approaches. The core innovation of MSNet lies in its decoupled time and frequency modeling paths, wherein Mamba state-space units are employed to capture the temporal dynamics and spectral structures of audio signals. Additionally, a multi-band splitting strategy is introduced to enhance localized frequency perception. The architecture strikes a balance between modeling depth and inference efficiency, offering both scalability and deployment feasibility.

Experimental results in the standard MUSDB18 dataset demonstrate that MSNet achieves superior performance across all four source categories, vocals, bass, drums, and others, compared to the main models. Specifically, MSNet achieves an average SDR of 7.61 dB, outperforming Demucs by 0.41 dB and Spleeter by 2.16 dB. In the vocal separation task, MSNet reaches an SDR of 8.36 dB and an SIR of 17.37 dB, with a standard deviation of only ±0.17, indicating higher accuracy and greater stability. In terms of practical deployment, MSNet maintains a real-time factor (RTF) below 1, achieving real-time processing capability while delivering superior separation quality, validating its deployment potential in practical applications.

This study also highlights the heterogeneous sensitivities of different types of sources to architectural components: for example, vocals are highly dependent on temporal modeling, while bass and percussion rely more on spectral modeling. These findings offer important insights for the design of source-aware structures. In addition, by evaluating the trade-off between inference efficiency and structural complexity, the study explores future directions including learnable band splitting, conditional modeling control, and adaptive computational paths.

In summary, MSNet not only achieves state-of-the-art separation accuracy but also demonstrates high system efficiency and structural extensibility, providing a new avenue for developing more powerful and flexible audio separation frameworks. Future research may further extend MSNet to complex scenarios involving diverse musical styles, multiple languages, and multi-channel environments, while also exploring its broader potential in controllable modeling and cross-task generalization.
