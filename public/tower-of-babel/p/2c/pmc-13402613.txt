Research on optimization of power grid load forecasting models based on deep learning
Authors: Junhua Hu, Hao Huang, Wenjin Chen, Si Zhang, Daijun Xia, Silei Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Precise forecasting of power grid load is essential for maintaining the stability and efficiency of contemporary energy systems. Traditional statistical and machine learning methods often struggle to capture the nonlinear temporal dependencies and dynamic variations in power loads, limiting their predictive accuracy. To overcome these difficulties, this study proposes an Adaptive Deep Load Predictor (ADLP) that integrates hybrid temporal encoding, a dynamic attention mechanism, and an online adaptive updating strategy. By integrating bidirectional long short-term memory networks for temporal sequence analysis with convolutional neural networks for extracting spatial features, ADLP effectively captures both short-term fluctuations and long-term dependencies in load patterns. The dynamic attention mechanism further enhances predictive performance by assigning adaptive importance to historical data, while the online adaptive updating module ensures real-time adaptability to evolving load conditions. This study introduces a Dynamic Grid-Aware Load Optimization (DGLO) strategy to enhance power grid operations based on real-time predictive insights. It integrates predictive adaptive control, stochastic robust optimization, and energy storage integration to dynamically adjust grid operations. The predictive adaptive control mechanism refines real-time generation adjustments using deep learning-based forecasting and proportional-integral-derivative control. The stochastic robust optimization component compensates for uncertainties in load variations, improving system resilience. The energy storage integration module optimizes charge-discharge cycles to stabilize energy utilization. Extensive experiments on real-world datasets demonstrate the superiority of the proposed framework over traditional and existing deep learning methods. The results confirm its enhanced forecasting precision, adaptability, and robustness in smart grid applications. Future work will focus on improving computational efficiency, integrating reinforcement learning, and expanding generalization across different grid environments.

Introduction

Reliable forecasting of power grid load is essential for maintaining the stable and efficient functioning of modern energy systems. With the increasing complexity of power networks due to renewable energy integration, electric vehicle adoption1, and fluctuating consumption patterns, traditional load forecasting methods struggle to maintain high accuracy. Not only does effective forecasting help balance supply and demand, but it also enhances economic efficiency and reduces environmental impact2. Precise load predictions can support grid reliability by mitigating risks associated with power shortages and system failures. However, existing methods face challenges in handling nonlinearity, high-dimensional dependencies, and the influence of external factors such as weather conditions and socioeconomic trends3. Therefore, developing optimized power grid load forecasting models, particularly those leveraging deep learning, is essential to improve prediction accuracy and adaptability under diverse conditions4.

Early approaches to power grid load forecasting relied on symbolic AI and knowledge representation techniques. These methods were primarily based on expert systems, rule-based models, along with statistical methods like exponential smoothing and autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA)5. By leveraging domain knowledge and predefined rules, these models offered interpretability and reliability in structured environments. However, their dependence on manually crafted rules and assumptions limited their ability to adapt to dynamic and complex grid conditions6. These methods struggled with capturing nonlinear relationships in large-scale datasets, making them less effective in modern power systems characterized by high variability and external influencing factors. As a result, researchers sought alternative approaches that could model intricate dependencies more effectively7.

To address the limitations of rule-based and statistical methods, data-driven machine learning techniques emerged as a promising alternative. Models such as support vector machines (SVM), random forests, and gradient boosting trees demonstrated improved forecasting accuracy by learning patterns from historical data8. Unlike traditional methods, these approaches did not rely on predefined rules but instead leveraged feature engineering and data-driven optimization to enhance predictive performance9. However, these models still required significant effort in feature selection and manual tuning, which limited their scalability and adaptability. They struggled with long-term forecasting due to their reliance on handcrafted features10, which might not fully capture the intricate temporal dependencies present in power load data. As machine learning methods gained traction, researchers turned to deep learning techniques to further enhance forecasting capabilities11.

Deep learning approaches, especially transformer-based frameworks, long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, and recurrent neural networks (RNNs)12, have revolutionized power grid load forecasting by automatically capturing temporal dependencies and nonlinear relationships. These models excel in handling high-dimensional data, incorporating multiple influencing factors such as weather, economic indicators13, and grid topology. Pretrained transformer models, such as those inspired by BERT and GPT architectures, have further improved forecasting performance by leveraging large-scale learning on diverse datasets. However, despite their advantages, deep learning models often require substantial computational resources14, large datasets, and careful hyperparameter tuning. Moreover, their black-box nature poses interpretability challenges, making it difficult to diagnose errors and build trust in real-world applications15.

Based on the limitations of existing methods, we propose an optimized deep learning framework for power grid load forecasting that enhances predictive accuracy, computational efficiency, and interpretability. By integrating attention mechanisms, hybrid architectures combining convolutional neural networks (CNNs) with LSTMs, and explainable AI techniques, our approach addresses the shortcomings of traditional deep learning models. Transfer learning strategies enable better generalization across different power grid environments, reducing the need for extensive retraining. Our framework not only improves short-term and long-term load forecasting accuracy but also enhances robustness against external disruptions, ultimately contributing to a more reliable and adaptive power grid.

In this context, the present work is guided by the following research questions:How can multi-scale temporal representation learning be structured to simultaneously capture short-term volatility and long-term periodicity in heterogeneous electricity markets?How can adaptive weighting and online updating mechanisms mitigate temporal redundancy and distributional drift in real-world load sequences?How can forecasting outputs and prediction error dynamics be explicitly integrated into operational adjustment mechanisms, thereby forming a forecast-driven decision-support architecture rather than a loosely coupled forecasting–control pipeline?The theoretical motivation underlying these questions is that forecasting and grid optimization should not be regarded as independent modules, but as interdependent layers within a hierarchical dynamic system. From a systems perspective, load prediction constitutes an information layer, while grid control and scheduling represent a decision layer. Misalignment between these layers may lead to reserve over-allocation, renewable curtailment, and unstable storage cycling. A structured coupling between representation learning and operational adjustment therefore provides a conceptual framework for embedding data-driven temporal modeling within cyber-physical energy systems. The contribution of this study lies not merely in architectural assembly, but in articulating a coordinated cross-layer design principle for forecast-driven grid adaptation, thereby enriching the broader methodological understanding of how predictive learning can inform operational stability in modern power systems.

Related work

Deep learning in load forecasting

Deep learning has become a pivotal approach in power grid load forecasting due to its ability to capture complex nonlinear relationships in energy consumption patterns16. In contrast to conventional statistical approaches like exponential smoothing and autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA), deep learning models enhance forecasting accuracy through hierarchical feature extraction. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs), especially long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, have shown exceptional effectiveness in capturing temporal dependencies for load forecasting17. LSTMs mitigate the vanishing gradient problem by using gating mechanisms, allowing them to retain long-term dependencies, which are crucial for predicting power demand fluctuations. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have also been explored in load forecasting by treating time-series data as spatial representations18. CNN-based models can extract local temporal dependencies and patterns, often outperforming traditional methods when combined with other deep learning architectures. Hybrid models integrating CNNs with LSTMs have been proposed to enhance forecasting precision by leveraging the strengths of both architectures19. Transformer models have emerged as another effective deep learning approach in load forecasting. By utilizing self-attention mechanisms, transformers can capture long-range dependencies in time-series data more efficiently than LSTMs. Studies have shown that transformer-based models outperform traditional deep learning approaches in large-scale power grid forecasting tasks, especially when handling multi-step predictions20. Despite these advancements, deep learning-based load forecasting faces challenges such as data sparsity, interpretability, and computational complexity. Various techniques, including attention mechanisms, data augmentation, and ensemble learning, have been introduced to address these limitations. Recent studies emphasize explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) techniques to enhance the interpretability of deep learning models, fostering greater trust and wider adoption in power grid management.

Optimization techniques for model improvement

The optimization of power grid load forecasting models involves refining deep learning architectures, enhancing training methodologies, and incorporating external influencing factors. Hyperparameter tuning plays a critical role in improving forecasting accuracy, with techniques such as Bayesian optimization, genetic algorithms, and particle swarm optimization being commonly employed to optimize neural network parameters21. Loss function engineering is another crucial optimization strategy. Traditional loss functions such as mean squared error (MSE) often fail to capture asymmetric forecasting errors, leading to the adoption of custom loss functions like quantile loss, Huber loss, and pinball loss to enhance model robustness22. Attention mechanisms have also been integrated into deep learning models to dynamically weigh important temporal features, leading to improved predictive performance. Transfer learning and domain adaptation techniques have been increasingly utilized to enhance forecasting models by leveraging knowledge from related tasks23. Pretrained deep learning models can be fine-tuned using a smaller dataset, reducing the need for extensive labeled data24. Federated learning has also emerged as a novel optimization approach, enabling multiple stakeholders to collaboratively improve forecasting models while preserving data privacy25. Computational efficiency remains a challenge in deep learning-based load forecasting due to the high dimensionality and volume of data. Model compression techniques such as knowledge distillation, pruning, and quantization have been applied to reduce the complexity of deep learning models while maintaining predictive performance26. Cloud-based distributed training frameworks further improve scalability, allowing real-time forecasting in large-scale power grids.

External factors in load forecasting

Accurate power grid load forecasting requires consideration of various external factors beyond historical load data. Weather conditions, including temperature, humidity, wind speed, and solar radiation, significantly influence electricity consumption patterns27. Deep learning models incorporating weather forecasts have demonstrated enhanced prediction accuracy, with multi-modal data fusion techniques improving the integration of heterogeneous information sources. Economic and demographic variables such as industrial activity, population growth, and urbanization trends also impact power demand. Socioeconomic-aware load forecasting models leverage macroeconomic indicators and demographic data to refine forecasting accuracy28. The inclusion of real-time electricity price signals further enhances demand-side management strategies by capturing the effects of price elasticity on consumption patterns. Renewable energy integration has introduced additional complexities to load forecasting due to the intermittent nature of solar and wind power generation29. Hybrid models combining load and renewable generation forecasting have been proposed to address these uncertainties. Reinforcement learning-based approaches have been explored to optimize demand response strategies by dynamically adjusting power consumption based on renewable availability30. The growing deployment of smart grids and Internet of Things (IoT) technologies has led to an influx of high-resolution energy consumption data. Advanced metering infrastructure (AMI) enables granular load forecasting, allowing utilities to develop more precise demand-side management strategies31. Edge computing and decentralized AI techniques have been introduced to process data closer to the source, reducing latency and improving real-time decision-making in power grid operations32.

Recent developments in deep learning-based power load forecasting have extended beyond deterministic prediction toward probabilistic modeling with stronger regularization and uncertainty control. For example, Lu et al.33 proposed a probabilistic load forecasting framework based on a non-crossing sparse-group Lasso-quantile regression deep neural network, which improves prediction accuracy and maintains quantile consistency. This approach highlights the importance of structure-aware regularization in deep load predictors. In addition, optimization and training strategies for deep models are increasingly being enhanced to handle distributed and heterogeneous data sources. Gao et al.34 introduced an optimized updating adaptive federated learning approach for collaborative diagnosis under label heterogeneity and communication redundancy. While their work targets industrial equipment, the adaptive updating mechanism and efficient federated training design are highly relevant for distributed energy forecasting in power grid contexts. These studies underscore the importance of combining forecasting accuracy with model adaptability and communication efficiency, aligning with the goals of our ADLP framework.

Although hybrid CNN-LSTM encoders, attention mechanisms, PID-based control, stochastic scheduling, and energy storage coordination have been extensively investigated in prior studies, most existing approaches address forecasting and grid operation as loosely coupled or sequentially separated modules. Forecasting models are typically optimized for predictive accuracy alone, while operational control strategies are designed independently based on externally provided or assumed load estimates. Experiments

Task definition

This study investigates short-term electricity load forecasting as a supervised multivariate time series regression problem. The input variable X consists of historical system-level load sequences aggregated across users, formally defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L(t)=\sum _{i=1}^{N} P_i(t)$$\end{document}, together with structured temporal features including hour of day, day of week, and month of year, and exogenous meteorological variables including ambient temperature. The load sequence is further represented through decomposition into base, periodic, and stochastic components, and encoded via hybrid temporal representations that capture long-term dependencies and local patterns. The output variable Y is the future electricity load within a fixed prediction horizon, denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{L}(t)=f(L(t_1),\dots ,L(t_T),T_{\text {env}}(t);\theta ),$$\end{document} corresponding to multi-step ahead numerical load values. The learning paradigm is supervised regression, where the objective is to minimize the discrepancy between predicted and ground-truth future load values over the forecasting window.

The supervision signal is derived from officially published system-level electricity demand records provided by the AEMO Electricity Demand Dataset and the OPSD platform. Ground-truth labels correspond to actual realized load measurements recorded by national power market operators through standardized metering and statistical aggregation procedures. These measurements are system-generated operational statistics rather than manually annotated data, ensuring objective and high-precision supervision. Each training sample is constructed by pairing a historical observation window with its corresponding future load trajectory, forming explicit input–output mappings for parameter learning under the defined regression objective.

Dataset and data preprocessing

Datasets

Experiments are conducted on two publicly available electricity demand datasets: the AEMO Electricity Demand Dataset released by the Australian Energy Market Operator and the Open Power System Data platform. The AEMO dataset provides half-hourly aggregated system-level electricity demand of the Australian National Electricity Market over five consecutive years, yielding approximately 175,200 chronological load observations. The OPSD dataset contains hourly national electricity demand records for European countries over eight consecutive years, resulting in more than 70,000 ordered load entries per country. Both datasets consist of continuous numerical load values with associated calendar attributes and meteorological temperature records. Ground-truth labels correspond to the actual realized future load values recorded by official market operators through standardized metering and statistical aggregation procedures. The supervision signal is therefore derived from system-generated operational statistics rather than manual annotation, ensuring objective and high-precision regression targets.

Data preprocessing follows consistent rules across datasets. Records containing negative load values are removed, and periods with consecutive missing observations longer than 24 hours are discarded. Isolated missing values are imputed using linear interpolation. Extremely low-demand outliers below the 1st percentile of the training distribution are filtered to reduce abnormal disturbance. Calendar variables including hour, weekday, and month are numerically encoded, and all load and temperature variables are normalized using min–max scaling computed exclusively on the training portion. Chronological time-based splitting is adopted to preserve temporal causality, with the earliest 70% of observations used for training, the subsequent 10% for validation, and the final 20% for testing. No random shuffling or cross-entity mixing is performed, ensuring strict temporal consistency for short-term load forecasting.

Data preprocessing

Data preprocessing is conducted consistently on the AEMO Electricity Demand Dataset and the Open Power System Data platform. Duplicate timestamps are removed by retaining the first occurrence of each time index to ensure unique chronological ordering. Missing values are handled according to strict rules: isolated missing entries shorter than 6 consecutive time steps are filled using linear interpolation, while segments with more than 24 consecutive missing hours are discarded entirely. Abnormal observations are identified using statistical thresholds computed from the training split; load values outside the range defined by the 1st and 99th percentiles are treated as outliers and removed. Negative demand values are eliminated as invalid system records. All timestamps are aligned to uniform temporal grids, half-hourly resolution for AEMO and hourly resolution for OPSD, guaranteeing consistent time alignment.

Standardization is performed using min–max normalization applied to load and temperature variables based solely on training-set statistics to prevent data leakage. Calendar attributes including hour of day, day of week, and month of year are encoded as normalized numerical indices to achieve temporal alignment. For supervised sequence construction, a sliding window strategy is adopted with a fixed historical length of 168 time steps and a forecasting horizon of 24 time steps. The stride is set to 1 to maximize sample utilization while preserving temporal continuity. No data augmentation designed for visual or audio modalities is applied, since the task involves structured numerical time series. External meteorological features are directly incorporated without auxiliary feature extraction models, forming unified multivariate input tensors for regression learning.

To enhance experimental transparency and real-world plausibility, additional clarification regarding cross-market heterogeneity and dataset characteristics is provided. The two datasets used in this study represent distinct electricity market environments with differences in temporal resolution, climatic conditions, and demand variability patterns, thereby introducing domain heterogeneity challenges for forecasting models. The AEMO dataset reflects the Australian National Electricity Market, characterized by half-hourly resolution, pronounced daily and seasonal temperature sensitivity, and relatively high renewable penetration. In contrast, the OPSD dataset represents aggregated European national-level demand data with hourly resolution, multi-regional climatic diversity, and differing regulatory and market structures. These differences result in variations in load volatility, periodic intensity, and weather–load coupling strength. By evaluating the forecasting framework independently on both datasets without cross-market training transfer, the study assesses generalization capacity under heterogeneous temporal structures and demand dynamics rather than relying on a single-market validation. Evaluation metrics and baseline

Metrics definition

Performance is evaluated using four primary accuracy metrics that are standard in electricity load forecasting: Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE), and the coefficient of determination \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document}. MAE measures the average absolute deviation between predicted and ground-truth load values, reflecting overall prediction bias. RMSE emphasizes large deviations by penalizing squared errors, providing sensitivity to peak-load forecasting accuracy. MAPE evaluates relative percentage deviation, enabling scale-invariant comparison across datasets. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} quantifies the proportion of variance explained by the model, measuring goodness of fit. In addition to predictive accuracy, two efficiency-oriented auxiliary metrics are reported: the total number of trainable parameters (Params), which reflects model capacity and memory footprint, and floating-point operations (FLOPs), which quantify computational complexity. These metrics provide a comprehensive assessment covering accuracy, robustness, and computational efficiency.

Evaluation protocol

Evaluation follows a strict chronological time-based protocol to preserve causality in short-term load forecasting. The datasets are split into training, validation, and testing sets according to temporal order, and all hyperparameters are selected using validation performance. Testing is conducted exclusively on future unseen time periods, ensuring that no information from the test horizon is accessible during training. Forecasting performance is computed over a fixed multi-step prediction horizon of 24 time steps using a sliding-window evaluation strategy with stride equal to 1. All reported metrics are averaged across the entire test period. The evaluation setting is fully offline, where models are trained on historical data and evaluated on held-out future sequences without real-time parameter updates. No cross-entity mixing or random reshuffling is applied, guaranteeing strict temporal consistency and preventing data leakage.

Statistical settings

To ensure statistical reliability, each model is independently trained and evaluated over \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N=10$$\end{document} runs with different random initialization seeds. For each metric, results are reported in the form of mean ± standard deviation computed across the 10 runs. Statistical significance between the proposed method and each baseline is assessed using a two-sided paired t-test based on per-run results. A significance threshold of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p<0.05$$\end{document} is adopted to determine whether performance differences are statistically meaningful. All models are implemented under identical training conditions, including identical data splits, optimization schedules, and evaluation procedures, ensuring fair comparison. Reporting both central tendency and dispersion provides a robust estimate of model stability and generalization consistency.

Baseline

Six representative baselines are adopted to cover traditional statistical approaches, conventional machine learning models, mainstream deep learning architectures, state-of-the-art sequence models, and lightweight forecasting frameworks. ARIMA is included as a classical statistical time series model that captures autoregressive and moving-average dynamics. Support Vector Regression (SVR) represents traditional machine learning regression methods with kernel-based nonlinear modeling. Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) serves as a standard recurrent neural network for sequence modeling. CNN-LSTM integrates convolutional feature extraction with recurrent temporal modeling to capture both local and long-range dependencies. Informer is employed as a state-of-the-art transformer-based time series forecasting architecture designed for long-sequence efficiency. LightTS is adopted as a lightweight forecasting model emphasizing parameter efficiency and computational reduction. These baselines provide comprehensive coverage across methodological categories.

Conclusions and future work

This study introduced ADLP, an adaptive deep learning framework for power grid load forecasting, and DGLO, a dynamic optimization strategy for grid-aware load adjustment. Through extensive experiments on four benchmark datasets, AEMO, OPSD, Pecan Street, and RE-Europe, the ADLP model demonstrated consistent improvements over traditional machine learning and state-of-the-art deep learning models across multiple evaluation metrics, including MAE, RMSE, and MAPE. Ablation studies confirmed the complementary value of each architectural component, particularly the hybrid temporal encoding and online adaptive updating mechanisms. Additional experiments also validated the effectiveness of the DGLO strategy in improving prediction stability and grid responsiveness under dynamic conditions.

Despite these promising results, several limitations remain. The model relies on the availability of high-quality, temporally aligned input data, including weather and pricing signals. Performance may degrade in data-sparse or noisy environments. The computational demands of real-time adaptation also present a challenge for low-resource deployment settings.

Future work will explore model compression techniques, including pruning and quantization, to reduce runtime cost. Another promising direction is the integration of reinforcement learning-based control agents within the DGLO framework to improve autonomous grid interaction. Further, expanding evaluation to include multi-region datasets and real-time edge device deployment will help assess the generalization and scalability of the proposed system in large-scale smart grid scenarios.

Method

Overview

With the rising electricity demand and the integration of renewable energy sources, predicting and managing power grid loads has become increasingly vital. This section introduces the methodology developed in this work to model and analyze power grid load, focusing on predictive modeling and optimization strategies.

Section “Preliminaries” introduces the fundamental concepts and mathematical formulations of power grid load dynamics. It provides the necessary preliminaries, including definitions, notations, and the mathematical representation of load variations over time. The proposed predictive model is presented in “Adaptive deep load predictor (ADLP)”. Unlike traditional load forecasting approaches, which rely solely on statistical methods or conventional machine learning, our model integrates advanced deep learning techniques. The methodology section describes the architecture, training process, and input features considered in the predictive framework. A novel optimization strategy in “Dynamic grid-aware load optimization (DGLO)” is introduced to enhance the stability and efficiency of power grid operation. This strategy leverages domain-specific constraints and real-time data to improve decision-making in load distribution and grid management. By structuring the methodology into these interconnected components, this paper aims to provide a comprehensive solution for accurate load forecasting and efficient power grid management.

Although both ADLP and DGLO are described within a unified methodological framework, the primary experimentally validated contribution of this study lies in short-term load forecasting. ADLP constitutes the core methodological component and the main subject of empirical evaluation. DGLO is formulated as a forecast-driven operational enhancement layer that illustrates how predictive outputs can support grid-aware decision-making, rather than as a standalone, fully benchmarked control or optimization system. This distinction clarifies the hierarchical structure of the proposed framework.

Preliminaries

The power grid load can be defined as the total electrical demand imposed on a power system at a given time. Let the load function be represented as:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L(t) = \sum _{i=1}^{N} P_i(t), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where L(t) is the total load at time t, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_i(t)$$\end{document} is the power consumption of the i-th consumer, and N represents the total number of consumers connected to the grid.

To model the temporal behavior of power demand, we consider the load as a time series:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L = \{ L(t_1), L(t_2), \dots , L(t_T) \}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where T is the total number of time steps in the observation window.

The load signal can be decomposed into three main components:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L(t) = L_{\text {base}}(t) + L_{\text {var}}(t) + L_{\text {rand}}(t), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{\text {base}}(t)$$\end{document} represents the base load (long-term average demand), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{\text {var}}(t)$$\end{document} captures systematic variations due to daily and seasonal trends, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{\text {rand}}(t)$$\end{document} denotes random fluctuations.

To model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{\text {var}}(t)$$\end{document}, we introduce a periodic function:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L_{\text {var}}(t) = \sum _{k=1}^{K} A_k \cos \left( \frac{2\pi k t}{T} + \phi _k \right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_k$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi _k$$\end{document} are the amplitude and phase shift of the k-th harmonic component, respectively.

The power load exhibits stochastic behavior due to consumer activities and external factors. Hybrid Temporal Encoding

To effectively model power load variations, the Adaptive Deep Learning Predictor (ADLP) integrates both short-term and long-term dependencies through a hybrid encoding approach that combines bidirectional long short-term memory (Bi-LSTM) networks and convolutional neural networks (CNNs). This design allows ADLP to capture global sequential relationships while extracting essential local patterns, thereby enhancing predictive accuracy. Online Adaptive Updating

To ensure adaptability to real-time variations in load patterns, Adaptive Deep Learning Predictor (ADLP) integrates an online updating mechanism based on gradient-based optimization. This mechanism enables continuous refinement of the prediction model parameters, allowing it to dynamically adjust to changing data distributions. The parameter update is performed iteratively using the following gradient descent update rule:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \theta ^{(n+1)} = \theta ^{(n)} - \eta \nabla _\theta \mathcal {L}(\theta ^{(n)}) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {L}(\theta )$$\end{document} represents the forecasting loss function, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta$$\end{document} is the learning rate, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document} is the set of model parameters being optimized. To further improve model stability and convergence speed, an adaptive learning rate strategy can be incorporated, expressed as:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \eta ^{(n+1)} = \eta ^{(n)} \cdot \frac{1}{1 + \lambda n} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document} is a decay factor that gradually reduces the learning rate as iterations progress, preventing excessive oscillations during optimization. To mitigate the impact of abrupt changes in the load pattern, momentum-based optimization can be employed:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} v^{(n+1)} = \beta v^{(n)} + (1 - \beta ) \nabla _\theta \mathcal {L}(\theta ^{(n)}) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \theta ^{(n+1)} = \theta ^{(n)} - \eta v^{(n+1)} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where v represents the velocity term that accumulates past gradients, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} is the momentum coefficient that controls the influence of previous updates. By integrating these mechanisms, ADLP enhances its adaptability to real-time variations, ensuring more accurate and robust load forecasting under dynamically changing conditions.

In terms of practical deployment, the proposed ADLP model is designed with real-time applicability in mind. To ensure efficient operation in streaming environments, the online adaptive updating module leverages lightweight gradient-based updates on mini-batches of new data without requiring full retraining. The model parameters are fine-tuned using stochastic gradient descent based on a short sliding window of the most recent observations, enabling it to adapt to evolving load patterns with minimal computational overhead. We conducted performance profiling to assess the model’s suitability for real-time applications. On an NVIDIA A100 GPU, the average inference latency per sample is approximately 6.9 milliseconds, as shown in Table 6, which is well within the acceptable range for most real-time smart grid control systems. The online update step, when triggered periodically or based on drift detection, requires less than 15 milliseconds per iteration due to the selective update of only a subset of network layers (like attention weights or final linear projections), rather than the entire model. This efficient updating mechanism allows ADLP to continuously refine its predictions in response to new input conditions, such as sudden demand spikes or renewable output variations. The architecture is compatible with edge or cloud-based deployment frameworks, and its real-time performance makes it suitable for integration into adaptive grid control systems that require both fast inference and ongoing learning.

Dynamic grid-aware load optimization (DGLO)

Accurate load forecasting is only one part of an effective power grid management system. To ensure stability and efficiency, it is necessary to dynamically optimize load distribution based on real-time predictions. In this section, we introduce three key innovations of the Dynamic Grid-Aware Load Optimization (DGLO) strategy (as shown in Fig. 3).Figure 3Dynamic grid-aware load optimization (DGLO) framework. The DGLO framework enhances grid reliability and energy efficiency by integrating predictive adaptive control, stochastic robust optimization, and energy storage integration. Predictive adaptive control

To effectively model power load variations and improve real-time load balancing, the Adaptive Deep Learning Predictor (ADLP) and the Dynamic Grid Load Optimizer (DGLO) integrate advanced control mechanisms that dynamically adapt to changing conditions. The DGLO employs an adaptive control mechanism that continuously updates the generation plan based on forecast error dynamics. This is achieved through a proportional-integral-derivative (PID) control approach, formulated as:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Delta G_i(t) = K_p e(t) + K_i \int e(t) dt + K_d \frac{d}{dt} e(t), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e(t) = L(t) - \hat{L}(t)$$\end{document} represents the prediction error between actual and estimated load, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(K_p, K_i, K_d)$$\end{document} are the PID control gains that regulate generation adjustments. This predictive control mechanism ensures grid stability under varying demand conditions by dynamically responding to load fluctuations.

The proportional term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_p e(t)$$\end{document} reacts to the immediate error, making quick adjustments to compensate for rapid changes in demand. The integral term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_i \int e(t) dt$$\end{document} accumulates past errors, addressing steady-state deviations by fine-tuning generation to avoid sustained discrepancies. The derivative term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_d \frac{d}{dt} e(t)$$\end{document} anticipates future errors based on the rate of change, helping to minimize overshooting and improve system responsiveness.

To enhance the accuracy of load predictions, a Bi-LSTM-based prediction model is employed, which captures both long-term dependencies and short-term variations in power load data. This combination enhances the robustness of load forecasting by integrating both historical trends and short-term fluctuations.

By dynamically adjusting the charging and discharging rates based on real-time grid demand, this system enables efficient energy utilization.
