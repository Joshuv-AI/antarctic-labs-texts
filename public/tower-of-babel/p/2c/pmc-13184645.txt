Deep learning Algorithm for Wound assessment after total kNee (DAWN) arthroplasty
Authors: Sai Pendyala, Aditya Vijay, Nimra Akram, Andrew Coppola, Samantha Jones, Nick D. Clement, Gustavo Carneiro, Deiary F. Kader
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsThis study aims to develop and internally validate an AI approach based on a deep learning (DL) algorithm to classify wound photographs as either ‘healing well’ or ‘requiring review’ after a total knee arthroplasty (TKA).MethodsA prospective cohort study will be conducted at a single, high-volume Elective Orthopaedic Centre. Adult patients who have undergone primary TKAs will be recruited either upon re-attendance at a wound review clinic or if they have a wound concern. Within the first two weeks postoperatively, an orthopaedic research fellow will obtain two standardized photographs of the wound, and the participant will complete a six-item symptom survey. Two blinded consultant orthopaedic surgeons will independently label each case (with the photographic and survey knowledge only) as ‘healing well’ or ‘requires review’. The dataset of wound images will be split into an 80:20 ratio and a pre-trained DL algorithm will be fine-tuned using 80% of the data with five-fold cross-validation being employed. The folds will be generated using stratification not only by outcome labels but also by demographic variables in order to maintain similar distributions across folds. The remaining 20% will be the test set allowing for internal validation and assessment of the efficacy of the developed algorithm. Ethical approval has been granted by the NHS REC/HRA (IRAS 340642).ConclusionThis study will generate one of the first prospectively validated DL tools for orthopaedic wound triage. Embedding objective imaging and symptom data into a DL algorithm will allow for early detection of complications, timely intervention, streamlined follow-up, and support NHS digital-first pathways. This study’s design directly mirrors NHS post-TKA pathways, supporting translatability into the current postoperative workflow for patients. The development of an early-detection system further enables patients to communicate concerns and receive timely assessment and treatment of any postoperative wound issues. The findings of this study will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and presentations at national and international conferences.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2026;7(5):659–666.

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is a well-established and cost-effective intervention for end-stage osteoarthritis, offering substantial pain relief and functional improvement.1,2 In the UK alone, over 198,000 primary TKAs were performed between January 2020 and December 2022, with projections indicating a 36.6% increase in volume by 2060.2 With the growing number of procedures, there is a commensurate need to streamline postoperative care, particularly wound assessment.2

Wound assessment typically takes place during the first two weeks following surgery, either in primary care or outpatient settings.3,4 Despite structured discharge advice, many patients experience significant anxiety about their surgical wounds. This psychological distress is well-recognized in the literature and is associated with impaired wound healing, creating a feedback loop that increases the likelihood of unnecessary clinical consultations.4,5 These concerns generate a substantial burden on healthcare resources, particularly for orthopaedic and general practice teams.3

The cost of managing a straightforward surgical wound over 12 months is estimated to exceed £6,000 and may be more than double for wounds that do not heal appropriately.3 Most costs for uncomplicated wounds stem from in-person outpatient assessments (approximately 69%).3 The AI tool could substantially reduce reliance on these assessments and therefore costs by enabling remote monitoring and early identification of wound concerns.

Current tools used to assess surgical wound healing, such as the Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) criteria,6 ASEPSIS score (Additional treatment, the presence of Serous discharge, Erythema, Purulent exudate, and Separation of the deep tissues, the Isolation of bacteria, and the duration of inpatient Stay),6 and the Southampton scoring system,6 are primarily designed to detect surgical site infections (SSIs).7 These scoring systems are not validated for routine orthopaedic wound healing and remain limited by poor reproducibility, interobserver variability, and a focus on overt infection rather than early or subtle signs of impaired healing.3,7 Consequently, wound assessments are often based on subjective clinical judgement, ideally performed by orthopaedic surgeons due to their specialist insight, but this model is labour-intensive and inconsistent.4 In contrast to traditional SSI surveillance tools that rely primarily on subjective patient or surgeon self-report, our approach is novel in that it integrates both objective photographic data and patient-reported symptom data, thereby enhancing diagnostic accuracy.

Theoretical and empirical insights from wound healing science, patient-centred care, and AI suggest an opportunity to modernize this aspect of surgical recovery.8 Psychological stress, common in the postoperative period, is known to negatively impact biological healing processes.5 A system that offers patients objective, timely feedback on wound progression could therefore improve both physical and emotional recovery trajectories. This aligns closely with principles of patient-centred care, which prioritize empowering patients through accessible, accurate information.

AI approaches, particularly those based on deep learning (DL) and computer vision (CV) algorithms, have demonstrated utility in other areas of medical image classification.8-10 While there is emerging evidence for AI-assisted wound assessment, most existing studies focus on general or plastic surgical settings, rarely include orthopaedic wounds, and often lack rigorous external validation.10-14 Furthermore, prior research frequently fails to comply with Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) or Prediction model Risk Of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST) guidelines,15 limiting reproducibility and clinical relevance. Among the few studies that employed mobile-based image analysis, none addressed orthopaedic-specific wounds or stratified performance across different wound types or skin tones.13

This study, Deep learning Algorithm for Wound assessment after kNee Arthroplasty (DAWN), seeks to address these gaps by developing and internally validating a DL model capable of classifying post-TKA surgical wounds as either ‘healing well’ or ‘requires review’. The model will be trained on standardized, prospectively collected clinical images paired with two expert-labelled outcomes. By embedding this tool within a future smartphone-based interface, we aim to support patients through their recovery while reducing unnecessary reviews and promoting optimal resource allocation.

By integrating core concepts from wound healing, patient psychology, and health service optimization, this study proposes a novel, scalable approach to improving postoperative wound care. It adheres to TRIPOD-AI and PROBAST guidelines15 and represents a foundational step toward the implementation of a clinically robust, patient-facing AI tool in orthopaedic practice.

Study objectives

The study aims to develop and validate a DL-based model that can assess a post-TKA surgical wound to indicate whether it requires a clinical review, utilizing a binary classification of ‘healing well’ or ‘requires review’ and addresses the following questions:

Primary question: Can the assessment of post-TKA surgical wounds be automated to identify wounds with a clinical concern, utilizing a DL model to ensure a high level of congruency with expert opinion?

Secondary question: Can the DL model provide further information with regard to the wound, including the dimensions of the wound, closure method, and eventually detect prognosticators of wound complications?

Methods

Study design and setting

This study is a prospective, single-centre cohort investigation conducted at a high-volume regional arthroplasty unit. Adult patients undergoing primary TKA will be consecutively enrolled within one month postoperatively. The primary objective is to develop and internally validate a DL model that categorizes postoperative wound images as either 'healing well' or 'requires review'. Participants, recruitment, and sample size

Eligible patients (Table I) will be identified from surgical theatre lists and initially provided with study information packs before hospital discharge. They will be subsequently approached by research fellows during routine follow-up appointments, such as the day-ten physiotherapy/nurse-led wound clinics or those who attend with concerning wounds within the first two weeks of their procedure. This approach minimizes the preoperative cognitive burden placed on patients on the day of their procedure. This enables patients to have adequate time to understand the study and retain the information, which forms part of a sound informed consent process. Wounds requiring interim dressing changes will not be excluded, providing assessment occurs within the defined postoperative window.

Sample size was calculated using the Riley et al framework,16 determining that a cohort of 300 patients is required. With an anticipated 10% prevalence of wounds needing clinical review (approximately 30 cases), this size ensures at least 20 events per candidate hyper-parameter, a shrinkage factor greater than 0.90, and an optimism-corrected area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) CI narrower than ± 0.05. These assumptions were confirmed using the ‘pmsampsize’ simulation on the statistical package R v. 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria), demonstrating sufficiency for a robust five-fold cross-validation.

Data collection and preprocessing

Following aseptic dressing removal, standardized digital photographs of each patient’s wound will be captured using a dedicated study smartphone. All images will be taken in accordance with a predefined standardized protocol using smartphone-based imaging, by a trained orthopaedic research fellow who will also record a binary subjective assessment (‘healing well’ or ‘requires review’). Wound photographs and symptom surveys will be collected routinely between ten and 14 days postoperatively.

Images will be directly uploaded to a secure NHS OneDrive account, anonymized, and subsequently transferred to an encrypted, password-protected server. Additionally, each patient will complete a brief six-item symptom survey, incorporating clinically relevant information to rule out surgical-site infections and surgical wound dehiscence.17 This survey records the presence of features not visually detectable, such as: fluid leaking from the wound (with colour specification); redness around the wound; swelling of the wound area; foul smell from the wound; hot skin around the wound; and temperature above 38°C. The response option for these features are; yes, no, or not sure (Figure 3). The method of wound closure will be recorded and included as a structured variable in the dataset. Where tissue adhesives are used as part of routine care, photographs will be assessed for features of type-IV hypersensitivity reactions (e.g. swelling, erythema beyond the wound margins, blistering, signs of wound dehiscence).18 Additionally, requirement of face-to-face review for non-wound-related reasons will be recorded and included as a candidate variable.

Six-item patient self-reported symptom survey: Patient questionnaire assessing postoperative symptoms not visible in photographs. Survey captures six key indicators of potential wound complications including discharge characteristics, inflammatory signs, and systemic symptoms. Completed simultaneously with wound photography to provide comprehensive wound assessment data for deep learning algorithm training.

Two consultant orthopaedic surgeons will independently assess and label each wound, blinded to each other’s evaluations. Disagreements will be resolved by a third consultant surgeon. Inter-rater reliability will be formally assessed using the Cohen’s κ statistic. Orthopaedic research fellows will inspect photographs for image quality, convert files to lossless.png format, and annotate visible pathological features, including erythema, wound dehiscence, discharge, necrosis, and swelling. These annotated images and survey responses will constitute the predictor variables. Missing data are anticipated to be minimal; any incomplete photo-survey pairs will be excluded from analysis. In cases of class imbalance, techniques such as synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE), focal loss, or class weighting will be applied solely within training datasets to avoid the overfitting phenomenon.

Model development and validation

All model development and statistical analyses will be conducted using Python v. 3.10 (Python, USA) and the PyTorch library (PyTorch, USA). Pretrained DL models based either on convolutional neural networks or vision transformers,19,20 will be trained on 80% of our collected data. The ‘pandas’ and ‘numpy’ libraries will be employed for data manipulation alongside five-fold stratified cross-validation. The remaining 20% of the data (which will remain untouched until final validation) will be the test-set used to assess discrimination (AUROC, sensitivity, specificity), calibration (calibration curves, expected calibration error), classification accuracy (F1 score), and localization performance (intersection-over-union for annotated regions). CIs will be calculated using the cross-validation method. Following cross-validation, model weights will be retrained on the complete dataset. Secondary analyses will explore associations between wound characteristics, closure method, survey results, the frontline binary subjective assessment performed at the time of image acquisition, and the model’s confidence scores.

A future external validation study using a separate, prospectively collected cohort from a collaborating centre is planned to assess generalizability, applying the same performance metrics. Skin tone will also be analyzed during external validation. This will be documented using a validated skin tone scale and model performance will be stratified accordingly. All statistical analyses will be performed using the software package Python v. 3.10. Data visualization will be carried out using Matplotlib, and model calibration using calibration methods from scikit-learn.

Data governance, ethics, and oversight

All hard-copy documentation, including signed consent forms, will be stored securely. Digital wound photographs will undergo manual cropping to isolate the region of interest, metadata removal, and pseudonymisation using a study-specific identification code before secure uploading to NHS OneDrive. Each day, image files and associated survey data will be downloaded to an encrypted, Trust-approved network drive accessible only to the password-protected accounts held by the research team. After secure download, the original OneDrive files will be permanently deleted. This rigorous workflow, involving region-of-interest cropping, metadata elimination, pseudonymization, and secure tiered storage, minimizes any risk of participant re-identification and complies fully with UK General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)21 and NHS information governance standards. The study carries minimal risk to participants, as image capture is noninvasive and dressing changes employ standard aseptic techniques.

Ethical approval for this study has been granted by the NHS Research Ethics Committee and the Health Research Authority (IRAS 340642) and this study is registered on the ISRCTN study registry (pending ID). Any significant amendments to the protocol will be submitted for review and approval by the study sponsor, Research Ethics Committee, and the local Research and Development office before implementation. Investigators will comply with any audits or inspections by the study sponsor, providing direct access to all relevant source data and documentation as required. This study is conducted without external commercial funding. Institutional support will be provided by the study sponsor (the host NHS Trust).

Discussion

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this protocol describes the methodology for the first prospective cohort study aimed at developing and validating a DL model to assess postoperative wounds following TKA. Despite the increasing volume of TKAs22,23 and the associated burden of postoperative wound care, there remains a lack of validated, standardized tools to objectively assess wound healing in orthopaedic surgery and current clinical practice relies heavily on subjective assessment and variable expertise, often resulting in patient anxiety, inconsistent follow-up, and increased healthcare use.3,11 DL models based on convolutional neural nets or vision transformers provide a pragmatic framework to combat this issue by standardizing and automating wound analysis.8,9,24 By combining rigorously standardized clinical images, a brief symptom questionnaire and blinded expert labels, DAWN offers an objective alternative, which can be compared to frontline wound evaluation.

Strengths of this study include its real-world data collection, rigorous methodological design, adherence to the PROBAST-AI and TRIPOD-AI guidelines,15 and commitment to patient-centred care. The trial methodology and protocol adhere to the CONSORT and Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines for a pilot study. Furthermore, study materials and survey questions were reviewed and updated following consultation with patients and the public in the centre’s monthly patient forum.

The single-centre setting inevitably limits demographic and environmental diversity. Highly controlled imaging conditions may not mirror real-world variation once wounds are photographed in patients’ homes. These constraints are considered acceptable at the proof-of-concept stage to minimize noise and demonstrate technical feasibility, but they underscore the need for a multicentre external validation to recruit patients from hospitals with broader ethnic representation and different device environments.9,13,14

The next stage will provide external validation and a definitive appraisal of generalizability. The current binary outcome reflecting current triage pathways, while clinically intuitive, cannot convey gradations of risk. Therefore, future iterations of the model will explore richer probabilistic outputs and multi-tier risk stratification once larger datasets become available. Evidence from other wound care domains supports the feasibility of this trajectory. For example, a recent study developed an AI wound assessment tool using more than 4,000 images across acute and chronic wound types, demonstrating accurate and generalizable results across varied anatomical sites, healing stages, and skin tones.20 These findings indicate that large-scale, diverse datasets can underpin robust digital wound triage, and DAWN represents an early application of such methods specifically to orthopaedic wounds. Additionally, DAWN will address key issues in previous wound detection models such as standardization of photographs taken to improve accuracy of model training, direct correlation with clinical signs and symptoms by using a survey and evaluation by two expert orthopaedic surgeons.

The DAWN algorithm will be developed within an academic NHS environment and is intended solely for research and clinical evaluation, with any future deployment contingent on external validation, regulatory and NHS governance approval.

Recent commentary on the deployment of AI models in orthopaedic imaging has shown a persistent gap between algorithm development and successful clinical integration, even when model performance has been favourable.25 The challenges highlighted have been sociotechnical rather than purely model-related encompassing workflow alignment, human factors, accountability and largely organizational readiness. As such, implementation frameworks have been proposed to guide the transition from model innovation to clinical practice, establishing the need for a robust development strategy.

The development strategy adopted for DAWN is consistent with emerging guidance advocating structured, staged evaluation of artificial intelligence before consideration for routine use in elective orthopaedics.26 The Clinical Practice Integration of Artificial Intelligence (CPI-AI) framework extends the established principles for surgical innovation to AI systems, providing a structured pathway from early concept development through to post-deployment surveillance.26

Within this framework, DAWN aligns most closely with CPI-AI stages 1 and 2a, focusing on algorithm development using prospectively collected data and internal validation, with planned external validation in independent cohorts.26 This protocol does not seek to imply readiness for autonomous clinical use. Rather, it aims to generate the foundational methodological evidence required before progression to later feasibility and implementation studies, where workflow integration, human factors, and clinical impact can be more appropriately assessed.

Beyond feasibility, economic considerations also strengthen the case for digital wound assessment. A quasi-experimental evaluation of a large home health programme in the USA showed that adopting a digital wound care solution reduced average healing times by over four days, lowered skilled nursing visits, and saved 309 staff days. Such findings are highly relevant to the NHS, where chronic wounds already cost an estimated £8.3 billion annually, highlighting the potential system-level benefits of orthopaedic-specific tools such as DAWN.22 Building on this economic rationale, a validated DAWN model could be embedded into a smartphone application, reducing reliance on in-person reviews, and enabling early identification of concerning wounds. While this protocol utilizes clinician-acquired images to ensure methodological consistency, subsequent phases will evaluate patient- or caregiver-acquired images using standardized guidance, allowing for scalability and community deployment. This has the potential to detect wound complications in a timely manner, reduce wound-related anxiety, and promote self-monitoring, particularly relevant in remote care settings or during recovery in the community.

This study aims to show that AI-based wound assessment is both technically feasible and potentially cost-saving. Within orthopaedics, DAWN represents an early step towards adapting such approaches to the specific challenges of postoperative wound monitoring. Future multicentre studies will be essential to validate performance, confirm generalizability, and establish the clinical and economic value of implementing DAWN at scale.

In conclusion, this proposed framework synthesizes concepts from wound healing, patient-centred care, and AI-driven healthcare to create a DL-based solution for post-TKA wound assessment. This approach is well-suited to address the current gaps in wound assessment practices, offering a standardized, scalable, and patient-centred tool that has the potential to improve both patient outcomes and healthcare resource use. By adhering to robust methodological standards and leveraging the latest advancements in AI, this study aims to contribute a clinically valuable tool to the field of orthopaedics.

Take home message

- The study results will demonstrate the ability of an AI algorithm to effectively identify wounds that require review by a healthcare professional post-total knee arthroplasty.

- Future work will be undertaken to externally validate the aforementioned algorithm in order to allow for the production of a smartphone application trained on the expertise of consultant orthopaedic surgeons, the aim of which is to provide patients with real-time feedback on wound concerns.
