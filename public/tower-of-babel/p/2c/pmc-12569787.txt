Thalamus not basal forebrain is atrophied in non‐demented PSEN1 E280A carriers
Authors: Stefan Teipel, Ana Baena, Bing He, Lusiana Martinez, David Aguillon, Yakeel T Quiroz, Alice Grazia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONA previous study of non‐demented presenilin‐1 (PSEN1) E280A carriers found basal forebrain and hippocampus, but not the thalamus, to be preserved. This study tested the hypothesis of preservation in an independent PSEN1 E280A sample and explored associations with amyloid and tau pathology.METHODSWe analyzed multimodal neuroimaging data from 57 individuals in the Colombia–Boston (COLBOS) cohort (non‐carriers: 30 and carriers: 27). We used Bayesian multiple regression with priors to test our hypothesis.RESULTSCarrier status had no effect on basal forebrain (Bayes factor [BF10] = 0.54) and hippocampal volume (BF10 = 1.05). However, smaller volumes were found in the thalamus of mutation carriers (BF10 = 8.74). We found evidence against an effect of amyloid and tau pathology on basal forebrain, but evidence for an effect on the thalamus.DISCUSSIONOur results support the preservation of the cholinergic basal forebrain and hippocampus, while highlighting early thalamic involvement in PSEN1 E280A carriers. INTRODUCTION

The cholinergic basal forebrain has long been recognized as central to Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathogenesis, culminating in the cholinergic hypothesis, which links the selective degeneration of basal forebrain cholinergic neurons to memory loss and led to the use of acetylcholinesterase inhibitors for symptomatic treatment. 1 , 2 In neuroimaging studies, atrophy of the cholinergic basal forebrain has been shown as an early event in sporadic AD, 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 parallel to or even preceding atrophy of the hippocampus in prodromal cases. 7 In contrast, two previous studies have shown lack of evidence for atrophy and functional disconnection of the basal forebrain and the hippocampus in cognitively unimpaired carriers of the presenilin‐1 (PSEN1) gene with mutation at codon 280 (E280A) for autosomal dominant AD. 8 , 9 Nonetheless, these carriers already showed significant atrophy and functional disconnection of bilateral thalamus. 8 , 9

Contemporary models propose that tau pathology propagates trans‐synaptically along connected networks. 2 In this framework, the basal forebrain cholinergic system, with its extensive projections to neocortical and allocortical regions, has been hypothesized as an early site of tau aggregation but also as a potential seed region driving pathology. 2 Initial human studies employing structural and functional covariance analyses indicated that tau burden in the basal forebrain was associated with altered connectivity and atrophy patterns across widespread cortical regions, such as amygdala and medial temporal lobe in sporadic AD. 10 , 11 This evidence, together with our previous findings on the Colombia kindred, 8 , 9 led us to generate the new hypothesis that basal forebrain structure and function is preserved in the initial stages. This is possibly due to a form of early cholinergic plasticity as opposed to sporadic AD forms, 9 potentially due to different temporal progression of neuropathological changes. 2

To test these assumptions, we first conducted a confirmatory analysis in an independent sample from the Colombia kindred with carriers of the PSEN1 E280A mutation and matched non‐carrier family members on atrophy of the basal forebrain, hippocampus, and thalamus. We investigated the hypothesis that there is no atrophy of the cholinergic basal forebrain and the hippocampus, but evidence of atrophy of the thalamus already in preclinical and prodromal mutation carriers. As a secondary adjunct analysis, we analyzed associations of amyloid status as determined by amyloid sensitive positron emission tomography (PET) with brain volumes. Based on our previous evidence we expected to find an association of amyloid positivity with reduced volumes on thalamus, but not with volumes of the basal forebrain or hippocampus. 8 In addition, extending previous evidence, 12 we studied associations of basal forebrain volumes with regional cerebral tau pathology determined from tau sensitive PET.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data source

Data were made available from the Colombia–Boston (COLBOS) biomarker study. COLBOS is a collaborative longitudinal biomarker study between the Universidad de Antioquia, Colombia, and the Massachusetts General Hospital, Boston. Participants underwent clinical, biomarker, and imaging examinations every 2 years. All neuroimaging data were collected in Boston during the same week. Here, we have accessed cross‐sectional data from a subset of 58 cases. All participants provided written informed consent and received compensation. 13 The study was approved by the University of Antioquia and the Massachusetts General Hospital local institutional review boards. 13 The research was performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Helsinki declaration of 1975 and its later amendments.

Participants

The COLBOS cohort includes participants who carry the PSEN1 E280A autosomal dominant mutation, and did not fulfil criteria for dementia at baseline. The typical age for experiencing cognitive impairment in the mutation carriers is age 44 years, for dementia age 49 years. 14 In addition, age‐ and sex‐matched members of the same kindred without autosomal mutations were included. Exclusion criteria included a significant medical, psychiatric, or neurological disorder, or a history of stroke, seizures, substance abuse, or other disorders affecting motor, visuospatial, or cognitive abilities. 13

Cognitive measures

For this analysis, we used clinical staging by the Functional Assessment Staging Tool (FAST), 15 global cognition assessment by the Mini‐Mental Status Examination (MMSE) score, 16 and the Colombian version of the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer's Disease (CERAD) 17 word list recall as well as construction praxis. Details of the cognitive measures are described elsewhere. 13

MRI

All participants underwent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) on a 3 Tesla Trio Siemens scanner at the Pablo Tobón Uribe Hospital in Medellín, Colombia, including a 3D T1‐weighted MPRAGE MRI scan (T1‐FFE; repetition time [TR] = 2400 ms, echo time [TE] = 3.61 ms, flip angle = 8°, field of view [FOV], 240 × 240 mm, 1.3 × 1.3 × 1.2 mm, and 160 slices).

Amyloid and tau PET scans

PET images were acquired using a Siemens/CTI ECAT HR scanner (3D mode; 63 planes; 15.2‐cm axial FOV; 5.6‐mm resolution; 2.4‐mm slice interval). Pittsburgh Compound B (PiB) PET for amyloid imaging followed an 8.5–15.0 mCi bolus and a 60‐min dynamic scan (69 frames), with standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) calculated from 10 frames between 40 and 60 min. 12 [18F]Flortaucipir PET was synthesized at Massachusetts General Hospital (mean yield 14%, specific activity 216 GBq/µmol) and acquired 80–100 min post‐injection (9.0–11.0 mCi; 4 × 5‐min frames). 18 All images were reconstructed, attenuation‐corrected, and reviewed for motion and count quality.

Image data processing

MRI data

For volumetric analysis, MRI data were processed by using statistical parametric mapping (SPM12, Wellcome Trust Center for Neuroimaging) and the CAT12.3 toolbox (http://dbm.neuro.uni‐jena.de/cat), implemented in MATLAB R2019 (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). First, MRI scans were automatically segmented into gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) partitions of 1.5 mm isotropic voxel‐size using the prior free Adaptive Maximum A Posterior (AMAP) segmentation routine of the CAT12 toolbox. The resulting GM and WM partitions of each subject, in native space, were then high‐dimensionally registered to the MNI reference template using the DARTEL algorithm. 19 Individual flow fields resulting from the DARTEL registration to the reference template were used to warp the GM segments and voxel values were modulated for volumetric changes introduced by the high‐dimensional normalization, such that the total amount of GM volume present before warping was preserved. For extraction of basal forebrain volume, we used a cytoarchitectonic map of basal forebrain cholinergic nuclei in MNI space, derived from combined histology and MRI of a post‐mortem brain, as described previously. 20 Hippocampus and thalamic volumes were derived using the Hammers brain atlas regions of interest. 21 We calculated the ratios of volumes to total intracranial volume (TIV) to correct for head size in subsequent analyses. For application to the spatially co‐registered PET scans (see below), we used FreeSurfer image analysis suite (version 5.0) to identify GM, WM, pial surfaces to permit region of interest (ROI) parcellation for cerebellar GM, hippocampus, and the following Braak stage‐related cortices: entorhinal, hippocampus, parahippocampal, inferior temporal, fusiform, and posterior cingulate, as described previously. 22

PET data

Each individual PET dataset was rigidly co‐registered to the individual's MPRAGE MRI scan using statistical parametric mapping (SPM8; Wellcome Trust Centre for Neuroimaging). The cortical ribbon and subcortical ROIs defined by magnetic resonance imaging as described above were transformed into the PET native space; PET data were sampled within each right/left ROI pair.

For [11C]PiB PET data were expressed as the distribution volume ratio (DVR) with cerebellar GM as the reference tissue; regional time‐activity curves were used to compute regional DVRs for each ROI using the Logan graphical method applied to data from 40 to 60 min after injection. 22 Global [11C]PiB retention was assessed using a large cortical ROI aggregate that included frontal, lateral temporal, and retrosplenial cortices, as described previously. 23 The cortical PIB‐DVR was binarized according to a positivity threshold of > 1.2, as previously described, 18 to distinguish between amyloid‐positive and amyloid‐negative scans.

For [18F]Flortaucipir PET—specific binding was expressed in FreeSurfer ROIs as the SUVR to cerebellum, similar to a previous report, using the FreeSurfer cerebellar GM ROI as the reference. 12

Statistical analysis

We used Bayesian multiple regression with Bayes factor (BF) hypothesis testing and parameter estimation with volumes as dependent variables, mutation carrier status as independent variable, and age and sex as confounders, to compare the alternative hypothesis against the null hypothesis (i.e., the assumption that there is an effect of carrier status, H1). 24 , 25 Since the BF10 is sensitive to the choice of the priors, 26 we used flat uninformative priors and, in a sensitivity analysis, we determined the BF10 when using priors that were informed by our previous study. 8 Informed prior specifications are given in Supplementary material A and Figures S1 and S2. In addition, we determined parameter estimates for the effects of interest and their 95% credible intervals. Model convergence was assessed using the R‐hat metric, with values close to 1 indicating good convergence. 27 Model fit was evaluated using posterior predictive p‐values (PPP), 28 calculated by comparing test statistics from replicated datasets drawn from the posterior distribution to the observed statistic.

A PPP near 0.5 indicates good fit, while values farther from 0.5 suggest poorer fit.

In an analogous analysis, we used amyloid‐positivity versus negativity in PIB‐PET as independent predictor, controlling for age and sex. In a further model, we determined correlations between brain volume and cognitive scores. We calculated multivariate regression models with pairs of brain volume and cognitive scores as dependent variables, regressed on age, gender, education, and carrier status. More detailed information on the model definition with an example code can be found in the Supplementary Material B. All Analyses were conducted using the “brms” package in R, accessed through RStudio (version 2024.12.1).

RESULTS

From the 58 cases, one case was excluded due to a missing volumetric MRI scan, leaving 27 mutation carriers, and 30 non‐mutation carrier family members. We found evidence for no difference in age, sex distribution, and education between groups, and strong to very strong evidence for a difference in MMSE scores and FAST stages (Table 1). According to FAST stages, 29 non‐carriers were classified as cognitively normal with no objective or subjective functional decrement (FAST stage 1), one was classified as normal aged adult with subjective deficits but no objective functional deficit (FAST stage 2). 29 Of the carriers, 16 were classified as cognitively normal (FAST stage 1), six as subjective cognitive deficits (FAST stage 2), four as mild cognitive impairment (FAST stage 3), and one as mild dementia (FAST stage 4).

Association of carrier status with brain volumes

We first report the results of Bayesian multiple regression using default flat priors in the package “brms,” followed by results using informative priors based on the previous study results 8 (Table 2). Volumes by carrier status are shown in Figure 1. For default flat priors, we found strong evidence for no effect of mutation carrier status on basal forebrain volume and moderate evidence for no effect on hippocampus volume. In contrast, we found moderate evidence for an effect of mutation carrier status on thalamic volume, with smaller volumes in mutation carriers. When using the informed priors, evidence remained in favor of no effect of mutation carrier status on basal forebrain and hippocampus volumes, with reduced level of evidence, and unaltered parameter estimates. For thalamus, the use of an informative prior resulted in an increased level of evidence for an effect with slightly shrunken estimates of the mutation carrier parameter towards the prior mean (Figure 2). In summary; however, effects remained consistent between default flat and informed priors. When we excluded the one carrier with mild dementia (FAST stage 4) from the models for thalamic volume, leaving only preclinical and prodromal cases, the results remained almost identical (Table 2). PPP values of about 0.50 for all models indicate a good fit (Table 2). R‐hat values were 1.0 for all parameter estimates in the models indicating good chain convergence.

Association of amyloid status with brain volumes

The evidence for effects or lack of effects was very similar to mutation carrier status due to high collinearity between both variables, with all non‐carriers being amyloid‐negative and all but one mutation carrier being amyloid‐positive. Results showed strong evidence against an effect of amyloid status on basal forebrain and hippocampus volumes, and moderate evidence for an effect of amyloid status on thalamic volume with larger volume in amyloid‐negative cases (see Table 3 and Figure 3).

Associations between brain volumes and cognitive performance

For the basal forebrain, we found moderate evidence of no association with any of the cognitive scores after controlling for age, sex, education, and carrier status (Table S1). Consistently, the 95% credible intervals of the parameter estimates included zero for all comparisons (Figure S3). For the hippocampus, we found moderate to anecdotal evidence of no associations with cognitive scores, except for word list recall with anecdotal evidence of an effect (Table S1). Consistently, only the 95% credible interval of the estimate for word list recall excluded zero (Figure S3). For the thalamus, we found anecdotal to moderate evidence for an association with MMSE score, FAST, and recall, but not for praxis (Table S1), consistent with the distribution of parameter estimates (Figure S3) suggesting better cognitive performance with higher volume.

Associations between brain volumes and regional tau uptake

For the basal forebrain, we found moderate to inconclusive evidence of no association with tau load in any of the brain regions after controlling for age, sex, education, and carrier status (Table S2). Consistently, the 95% credible intervals of the parameter estimates included zero for all comparisons (Figure S4). For the hippocampus, we found anecdotal to strong evidence for an association with tau load in entorhinal cortex, hippocampus, and parahippocampal gyrus (Table S2), with higher volumes associated with lower tau load (Figure S4). For the other tau regions, we found evidence against an effect (Table S2). For thalamus, we found anecdotal to moderate evidence for an association with tau load in the parahippocampal gyrus and the posterior cingulate gyrus (Table S2), with higher volumes associated with lower tau load (Figure S4). For the other tau regions, we found evidence against an effect (Table S2).

DISCUSSION

We confirmed evidence for the absence of atrophy in the cholinergic basal forebrain and the hippocampus, alongside clear evidence of thalamic atrophy in preclinical and prodromal stages of PSEN1 E280A mutation carriers with autosomal dominant AD. These findings replicate previous results in an independent sample with the same Colombian mutation. 8 , 9 This supports the hypothesis that in contrast to sporadic AD, early neurodegeneration in PSEN1 E280A carriers spares the basal forebrain and hippocampus, while affecting the thalamus. Further supporting this interpretation, we found no effect of amyloid status on hippocampal or basal forebrain volumes, as opposed to a moderate effect on thalamic volume. This is in contrast to the associations of amyloid pathology with basal forebrain atrophy found in prodromal sporadic AD. 6 , 30 , 31 Consistent with previous evidence, 8 we found absence of associations between hippocampus and basal forebrain volumes and cognitive performance, but an association of thalamic volume with cognitive performance. Indeed, hippocampal volume was associated with tau pathology in regions that are typically involved in Braak stages I to III 32 and thalamic volume with brain regions that are typically involved in Braak stages IV to V. 32 In contrast, basal forebrain volume was not associated with tau load in any of the selected regions.

Recent studies looking at the PSEN1 E280A mutation have highlighted the thalamus as a structurally and functionally vulnerable region that may play a key role in the preclinical phase of the disease. A structural MRI study indicated that cognitively unimpaired PSEN1 E280A mutation carriers exhibited reduced medial thalamic volume, which was significantly associated with greater amyloid and tau burden. 33 This volumetric reduction aligns with earlier PET imaging findings of hypometabolism in the thalamus of asymptomatic PSEN1 E280A mutation carriers, both at baseline and after 2 years of follow‐up. 34 Notably, in this previous PET study the thalamus contributed most to differentiating mutation carriers from non‐carriers, indicating its particular sensitivity to the pathological cascade in preclinical autosomal dominant AD. 34 Further support for early thalamic involvement comes from studies on connectivity and microstructural integrity. PSEN1 mutation carriers showed thalamic volume reduction and altered diffusivity metrics prior to clinical onset, 35 suggesting that microstructural degeneration and WM tract disruption may precede hippocampal atrophy, commonly observed later in the disease course. Consistent with these imaging findings, neuropathological observations in amyloid precursor protein (APP)/PSEN1 mice demonstrated frequent thalamic lesions, the presence and severity of which were linked to amyloid plaques. 36 A study extended these results showing longitudinal increases in amyloid‐associated PET signal in thalamus, neocortex, and hippocampus, reinforcing the idea that thalamic pathology in this model primarily reflects Aβ deposition. 37

Despite our data cannot provide causal evidence, one possible explanation for the lack of atrophy in the basal forebrain and hippocampus is an increase in volume during the clinical and prodromal stages of the disease due to developmental and early pathological changes. Indeed, children and adolescents with the Colombian PSEN1 E280A mutation at age 9 to 17 years showed increased hippocampus, parahippocampus, and parietal and temporal lobe volumes compared with non‐carriers 38 which might reflect neuroinflammation with glia activation and neuronal hypertrophy that only later is followed by volume reduction through neuronal shrinkage and neuronal loss. Moreover, we hypothesize that aberrant neuronal sprouting and altered myelination may also contribute to these volumetric enlargements, and this is currently being tested in children of the same cohort using advanced myelin imaging techniques.

When considering tau PET, we found associations of hippocampus volume with tau signal in the entorhinal cortex, hippocampus, and parahippocampal gyrus, and corresponding to Braak stages I to III. 32 Thalamic volume was associated with tau signal in the parahippocampal and posterior cingulate gyrus, corresponding with Braak stages III and IV. 32 Previous studies have also shown that tau aggregation in the medial temporal and neocortical areas of amyloid‐positive PSEN1 E280A carriers begins several years before symptom onset and is related to cognitive decline. 39 However, direct evidence linking tau accumulation to thalamic degeneration remains limited. A PET study in PSEN1 E280A mutation carriers found network‐level disruptions associated with tau, particularly within the default mode and salience networks, although the thalamus itself was not identified as a primary site of tau deposition. 40 Moreover, findings from the DIAN cohort suggest that while amyloid plaques deposition precedes tau pathology, the latter becomes more prominent and spatially widespread as individuals transition into symptomatic stages. 41 This accelerated tau progression, particularly in younger autosomal dominant AD populations, may limit the temporal window in which isolated tau accumulation could be detected in relation to preserved cognitive function.

Lastly, evidence was against an association of basal forebrain volume with tau load in any of the selected brain regions. One study comparing antemortem basal forebrain volume with postmortem neuropathological scores found moderate evidence for an association of basal forebrain volume with Braak stages in sporadic AD cases, but with a large proportion of advanced stages of dementia. 42 Additionally, another study found an association of basal forebrain volume with tau load in brain regions mainly representing Braak stages IV and V. 43 Together these findings suggest that the projection sites of the cholinergic basal forebrain are affected by tau pathology in more advanced stages of AD.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is the Bayesian framework, which leverages knowledge from previous studies and integrates it directly into the calculation of evidence from new data. In addition, it allowed us to calculate the stability of the effects under different a priori assumptions. Another major strength of this study is that the Colombian cohort constitutes a uniquely homogeneous dataset of individuals who are certain to develop AD in early adulthood, allowing for the prospective study of preclinical pathophysiological changes without the confounding influence of risk factors. However, the study is not without limitations. The relatively small sample size may reduce generalizability, and the imaging markers used cannot distinguish between different causes of volume changes, such as neuronal loss, shrinkage or hypertrophy. Moreover, we did not have access to the longitudinal data, as well as to the tau PET levels in the basal forebrain region, so we could not test for direct affection of the basal forebrain by tau pathology and compare this with the observation that the cholinergic basal forebrain was affected early by tau pathology in sporadic AD. 2 , 44

CONCLUSION

Our data confirm the absence of an early involvement of the cholinergic basal forebrain and hippocampus, but early involvement of the thalamus in autosomal dominant AD due to the PSEN1 E280A mutation. This has implications for the selection of treatment targets in this population. It may suggest that early cholinergic treatment is not as beneficial in these cases as in sporadic AD. Our findings warrant extension to other autosomal dominant AD mutations to determine whether lack of basal forebrain cholinergic involvement is specific to the PSEN1 E280A mutation or can be found in other autosomal dominant mutations.
