A deep learning–based study on automated CT diagnosis of kidney stones, hydronephrosis and pyonephrosis
Authors: Hanlin Gao, Hongyao Chen, Yihe Wang, Weiping Wang, Chao Yao, Xiaobin Zhu, Chenjing Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutomated identification of urinary system diseases on non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT) can facilitate early diagnosis and inform treatment decisions. However, kidney stones, hydronephrosis, and pyonephrosis share overlapping NCCT appearances and require diagnostic cues at different spatial scales, making accurate identification within a unified framework challenging. To address this, we constructed an integrated CT-based framework for automated identification of urinary system diseases. First, the collected CT data were split into training, validation, and test sets and processed using a unified preprocessing pipeline for resolution standardization and normalization. Subsequently, we evaluated multiple image enhancement strategies, including histogram equalization, CLAHE, Laplacian sharpening, and brightness enhancement. We conducted a systematic comparison of these enhancement methods to quantify their effects on disease identification performance. To support unified automated diagnosis of kidney stones, hydronephrosis, and pyonephrosis, we developed a multi-disease renal diagnostic network, MSF-TEA Net. Through multi-scale feature fusion and multi-evidence collaborative modeling, it effectively represents the image features of different pathological states. This model introduces Tri-Evidence Attention (TEA), which models the high-density features of small calculi targets, the global morphological changes of fluid accumulation, and the inflammatory texture features of pus accumulation. Through adaptive weighted fusion, the network enhances discrimination and diagnostic performance when complex lesions coexist. Experimental results show that, within the unified MSF-TEA Net framework, Laplacian sharpening achieves the best performance among the tested enhancement methods, with a test accuracy of 94.40% ± 1.13% and an AUC of 99.30%, outperforming the other enhancement strategies. Ablation studies further confirm the contributions of PPM, ASPP, and TEA. Overall, the proposed unified framework enables reliable identification of kidney stones, hydronephrosis, and pyonephrosis, supporting clinical decision-making.Keywords: Non-contrast CT, Kidney stones, Hydronephrosis, Pyonephrosis, Image enhancement, Multi-scale feature fusion, Tri-Evidence attention

Introduction

Kidney stones, hydronephrosis, and pyonephrosis are among the most common and clinically important renal diseases in urology, characterized by high incidence, rapid progression, and a high risk of complications [1–3]. Kidney stones usually result from crystal deposition due to an imbalance in urine composition. When they obstruct the ureter, they can cause obstructive hydronephrosis, leading to increased renal pelvic pressure, compression of the renal parenchyma, and impaired renal function [4]. When obstruction is complicated by bacterial infection, hydronephrosis can rapidly progress to pyonephrosis, accompanied by severe pathological changes such as thickening of the calyceal walls, purulent fluid accumulation, and inflammatory changes (e.g., pyelonephritis). If not treated promptly, this condition may lead to urosepsis or acute kidney injury and can be life-threatening [5]. Therefore, kidney stones, hydronephrosis, and pyonephrosis constitute a typical pathological continuum of “stone obstruction—urinary tract obstruction—infection aggravation.” Early, accurate, and imaging-based severity grading of these conditions is crucial for optimizing clinical decision-making and reducing disease risk.

Currently, the diagnosis of kidney stones, hydronephrosis, and pyonephrosis primarily relies on radiologists’ manual interpretation of non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT) images. Clinical judgment is typically based on imaging features such as high-density foci indicative of stones, dilation of the collecting system due to hydronephrosis, and wall thickening and changes in fluid density associated with pyonephrosis [6, 7]. However, these lesions span multiple scales in terms of texture, structure, and morphology, and the disease progression is continuous—it is often difficult to clearly distinguish between mild hydronephrosis and a normal renal pelvis, or between severe hydronephrosis and early pyonephrosis, leading to manual diagnoses that are highly dependent on experience and subjective [7–9]. Furthermore, in high-pressure clinical environments, doctors need to screen for lesions layer by layer in a large number of NCCT slices, a process that is time-consuming and prone to fatigue-related errors. Differences in equipment and imaging conditions can further affect diagnostic consistency.

With the rapid development of deep learning in medical imaging, artificial intelligence-based automated analysis methods have been increasingly applied to urinary system disease diagnosis. Existing studies have investigated related renal diseases from different perspectives, including CT-based kidney stone detection, KUB radiograph-based stone detection and segmentation, ultrasound-based hydronephrosis detection, CT/radiomics-based hydronephrosis–pyonephrosis differentiation, and multi-disease renal abnormality classification. In addition, recent advances in medical image analysis have highlighted the value of multi-scale feature extraction, attention-based refinement, generative artificial intelligence, and foundation-model adaptation for improving feature representation. For example, InFeNet used a multiscale strategy to reduce fragmented predictions and incomplete boundaries in kidney pathological image analysis [8]. RaNet introduced residual attention mechanisms to enhance feature refinement in T2-weighted MRI analysis [9]. Recent reviews have also summarized the application of generative AI across different medical imaging modalities for disease diagnosis [10]. Moreover, V-SAM leveraged advanced feature extraction and foundation-model-based strategies for kidney biopsy image analysis [11]. These studies provide useful methodological insights for medical image representation learning, but their imaging modalities, diagnostic objectives, disease categories, and evaluation settings differ from the present NCCT-based four-class classification task.

In kidney stone-related studies, Ramesh et al. developed a convolutional neural network (CNN)-based detection model for automated kidney stone identification, achieving performance comparable to that of clinicians across diverse samples [12]. Huang et al. designed a hybrid diagnostic system using InceptionResNetV2 and U-Net for renal stone detection and segmentation on KUB radiographs [13]. Building on CT-based research, Patro et al. proposed a CNN model based on Kronecker convolution for automated kidney stone detection on coronal CT images, reporting an accuracy of 98.56% [14]. More recently, Alzughaibi et al. integrated a Swin Transformer feature extractor with GCN, TCN, and 3D-CAE modules for CT-based kidney stone detection, achieving high diagnostic performance [15]. These studies demonstrate the potential of deep learning for stone identification. Nevertheless, they mainly focus on stone detection or segmentation, and some of them are based on KUB radiographs rather than NCCT images. Therefore, they are not directly comparable to the present NCCT-based four-class classification task.

For hydronephrosis and other renal abnormality detection, Bhandari et al. proposed a lightweight CNN combined with explainable algorithms to identify multiple renal abnormalities, including hydronephrosis, cysts, stones, and tumors, in CT images [16]. Lien et al. constructed and compared U-Net, UNet++, and Res-UNet for automatic hydronephrosis detection from renal ultrasound images [17]. Islam et al. further developed a deep convolutional network for ultrasound-based hydronephrosis classification [18]. These studies suggest that deep learning can effectively capture renal structural abnormalities. However, ultrasound and CT differ substantially in image appearance, diagnostic cues, spatial resolution, and clinical interpretation. Moreover, several of these studies focused on binary classification or specific abnormality detection rather than unified differentiation among normal kidneys, kidney stones, hydronephrosis, and pyonephrosis.

For the differentiation between hydronephrosis and pyonephrosis, CT-based radiomics and machine-learning methods have also been explored. Yuan et al. constructed a combined model using CT radiomic features and a three-dimensional convolutional neural network to distinguish calculous hydronephrosis from calculous pyonephrosis, achieving an AUC of 96.70% in the test set [19]. Yang et al. compared several machine-learning algorithms, including RF, XGBoost, SVM, and LR, for the differential diagnosis of hydronephrosis and pyonephrosis, and reported that the Gradient Boosting Decision Tree model achieved the best performance [20]. These studies are highly relevant to the clinical challenge of distinguishing infection-related obstruction from noninfected hydronephrosis. However, they are mainly binary differentiation tasks and do not include normal kidneys or isolated kidney stones as competing diagnostic categories.

Although deep learning has made substantial progress in AI-assisted renal imaging, an important unresolved problem remains: whether a single NCCT-based model can distinguish normal kidneys, kidney stones, hydronephrosis, and pyonephrosis under the same preprocessing pipeline, patient-level data split, and evaluation protocol. This task is clinically important because these conditions may represent a continuous pathological spectrum from stone formation to urinary obstruction and infection aggravation. Meanwhile, their NCCT findings involve different spatial scales and diagnostic cues, including small high-density calculi, collecting-system dilatation, and infection-related inflammatory texture changes. Existing models are often designed for a single disease, a specific imaging modality, or a binary diagnostic task, making it difficult to directly address this unified four-class NCCT classification problem. Therefore, there is a need to develop a unified intelligent diagnostic model that integrates multi-scale features and multi-source pathological evidence for automated identification of normal kidneys, kidney stones, hydronephrosis, and pyonephrosis.

To address the aforementioned problems, this paper proposes a unified automatic diagnostic framework for multiple kidney diseases. Its core components include multi-scale feature fusion, multi-evidence modeling, and multi-task supervised learning. Centered around the continuous pathological spectrum of “stones-hydronephrosis-pyonephrosis,” this framework achieves joint identification and differential diagnosis within a single model. Materials and methods

The overall methodological process of this study is shown in Fig. 1. First, the collected NCCT kidney image data were uniformly organized and divided into training, validation, and test sets. All images were preprocessed through standardization, their resolution was uniformly adjusted, and they were then normalized. Based on this, data augmentation strategies such as random cropping, horizontal flipping, rotation, and color perturbation were introduced to improve the model’s robustness to different scanning conditions and individual variations. Subsequently, the system compared several common image enhancement methods (including histogram equalization, CLAHE, Laplacian sharpening, and brightness enhancement) and analyzed their impact on the model training process and classification performance. During the model building and evaluation phase, ablation experiments were conducted to verify the effectiveness of each functional module, and comparative experiments were performed with several mainstream deep learning models. Finally, five evaluation metrics—Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score, and AUC—were used to comprehensively assess the model’s automatic recognition performance in the four-class classification task of normal kidneys, kidney stones, hydronephrosis, and pyonephrosis.

Datasets

This study was conducted after obtaining approval from the Ethics Committee of Shiyan People’s Hospital, Bao’an District, Shenzhen (Ethics Approval Number: 2025SYLL14). All research procedures strictly followed the ethical guidelines for medical research outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The imaging data used in this study were obtained from routine clinical chest and abdominal examinations at the hospital, using non-enhanced computed tomography (NCCT) coronal DICOM images. Inclusion criteria included: (1) age 18–80 years; (2) good image quality without significant motion artifacts or severe artifacts caused by metal implants; and (3) a confirmed diagnosis of normal kidney, kidney stone, hydronephrosis, or pyonephrosis based on NCCT findings and corresponding clinical records. Exclusion criteria included cases with co-existing severe extrarenal diseases, inconsistent imaging and clinical information, unresolved diagnostic disagreement, or incomplete imaging data. To protect patient privacy, all identifiable information was strictly anonymized before data collection.

This study constructed a single-center retrospective NCCT dataset containing 1,991 coronal DICOM images from 632 independent patients/cases. The unit of analysis was defined as one DICOM image sample, while data partitioning was performed at the patient/case level to avoid data leakage. Therefore, all DICOM images derived from the same patient/case were assigned to the same subset.

The final dataset included four categories: 356 normal kidney images from 98 patients/cases, 713 kidney stone images from 272 patients/cases, 591 hydronephrosis images from 155 patients/cases, and 331 pyonephrosis images from 107 patients/cases, totaling 1,991 coronal DICOM images from 632 patients/cases. On average, each patient/case contributed approximately 3.15 DICOM images. To reduce the risk of data leakage, dataset splitting was conducted at the patient/case level rather than by random image-level splitting.

Ground-truth labels and representative image samples were determined through a structured annotation procedure. The eligible NCCT examinations and corresponding clinical records were independently reviewed by two experienced physicians with expertise in urology and radiological interpretation. Each reviewer assigned one of four diagnostic labels: normal kidney, kidney stone, hydronephrosis, or pyonephrosis, and selected coronal DICOM images that clearly displayed the kidney and the main diagnostic findings of the corresponding category. The reviewers were blinded to the model outputs and experimental results, but they were not blinded to relevant clinical information, because clinical and laboratory evidence was required as part of the reference standard, especially for pyonephrosis.

If disagreement occurred, the two reviewers jointly re-examined the NCCT images and the corresponding clinical records, and the final label and selected images were determined by consensus. Cases with unresolved disagreement or inconsistent imaging and clinical information were excluded from the final dataset. For normal kidney cases, the reference standard was the absence of visible urinary tract abnormalities on NCCT and no corresponding clinical diagnosis of kidney stone, hydronephrosis, or pyonephrosis. For kidney stone cases, the label was based on the presence of high-density calculi on NCCT, supported by the clinical imaging diagnosis. For hydronephrosis cases, the label was based on collecting-system dilatation on NCCT without clinical or laboratory evidence supporting infection-related pyonephrosis. For pyonephrosis cases, the diagnosis was not based on NCCT appearance alone, but was supported by corresponding clinical, laboratory, operative, drainage, culture, or follow-up information, including one or more of the following findings: fever, leukocytosis, urinary tract infection evidence, positive urine culture, pus drainage, operative findings, or clinical follow-up. These reference criteria were used to improve the reliability of the ground-truth labels, particularly for distinguishing pyonephrosis from noninfected hydronephrosis.

Data preprocessing and augmentation

The imaging data used in this study were all coronal NCCT scans. Due to substantial variations in scanning conditions, scanner models, and image noise across patients, the raw images required rigorous preprocessing before being fed into the model to improve consistency and training stability. First, each original image was read and its pixel matrix was extracted. After converting the pixel values to floating-point format, min–max normalization was applied to linearly rescale the intensity to the range of 0–255, which helps reduce intensity shifts caused by scanner-related differences. Subsequently, the processed images were converted to uint8 format, and the single-channel CT images were replicated to three channels to be compatible with ImageNet-pretrained deep convolutional networks. To ensure consistent input dimensions, all images were resampled to 224 × 224 pixels and then normalized using the ImageNet mean (0.485, 0.456, 0.406) and standard deviation (0.229, 0.224, 0.225), yielding a stable distribution of the final input features.

For data partitioning, a stratified random sampling strategy was adopted to split the 1991 NCCT images into training, validation, and test sets at a ratio of 7:1.5:1.5, resulting in 1393, 299, and 299 images, respectively. To prevent data leakage caused by images from the same patient appearing in different subsets, splitting was performed at the patient level, such that all images from each patient were assigned to only one subset. The training set was used to optimize model parameters, the validation set was used for hyperparameter tuning and model selection, and the test set was held out for evaluating generalization on unseen samples. All categories (normal, kidney stones, hydronephrosis, and pyonephrosis) maintained consistent class proportions across the three subsets, ensuring fairness, statistical reliability, and reproducibility.

To enhance generalization, two types of data augmentation were applied during training. Conventional random augmentations included random horizontal flipping (p = 0.5), random rotation (±10°), and random perturbation of brightness and contrast (brightness = 0.2, contrast = 0.2), simulating variations due to patient positioning, scanners, and acquisition conditions. In addition, to systematically assess the impact of contrast enhancement on performance, separate experiments were conducted using histogram equalization [21], CLAHE [22], Laplacian sharpening [23], and brightness enhancement [24]. The resulting performance was compared across enhancement schemes in the four-class classification task to identify the optimal strategy for automated identification of kidney stones, hydronephrosis, and pyonephrosis.

To ensure a controlled comparison of image enhancement methods, all enhancement strategies were applied after patient/case-level data splitting and before network input transformation. Within each enhancement group, the same deterministic enhancement procedure was applied consistently to the training, validation, and test sets. The enhancement step was performed after DICOM intensity normalization and before resizing, tensor conversion, and ImageNet normalization.

The evaluated enhancement strategies included original images, histogram equalization, CLAHE, Laplacian sharpening, and brightness enhancement. All enhancement methods were implemented using fixed, predefined settings before model training, and no enhancement parameter was tuned using the test set.

Brightness enhancement used as a preprocessing strategy was different from the stochastic brightness and contrast perturbation used for data augmentation. Specifically, preprocessing-based brightness enhancement was deterministic and was applied identically to the training, validation, and test images within the corresponding enhancement group. In contrast, brightness and contrast perturbation was used only as a training-time random augmentation and was not applied to the validation or test sets.

The optimal enhancement method was selected based only on validation-set performance. The independent test set was held out and used only for final evaluation after the enhancement method and model checkpoint had been determined.

Model

This study proposes a diagnostic model for kidney stones, hydronephrosis, and pyonephrosis called the Multi-Scale Fusion and Tri-Evidence Attention Network (MSF-TEA Net). As shown in Fig. 2, the overall framework consists of four core components: a hierarchical feature extraction network based on ResNet-18; a context-aware multi-scale enhancement module operating on mid- and high-level features; a top-down feature pyramid fusion module; and a three-evidence attention module integrating structural, morphological, and textural pathological evidence. Through the synergistic interaction of the aforementioned modules, the model can simultaneously perceive local texture details, organ structural relationships, and global morphological patterns within a unified framework, providing rich and highly discriminative feature representations for subsequent kidney state classification.

In the ablation study, the no_se variant was constructed by removing this SE channel attention module while keeping the remaining network structure unchanged.

Tri-Evidence attention

To explicitly model the complementary relationships between different types of pathological clues in multi-disease diagnosis of the kidney, this paper proposes Tri-Evidence Attention (TEA) on the mesoscale fusion layer , as shown in Fig. 3. This module decouples and models image features from three complementary dimensions—structure, morphology, and texture—and achieves adaptive fusion through a learnable attention mechanism. It is a key component of the unified diagnostic framework presented in this paper.

Training protocol and reproducibility

To improve reproducibility, all experiments were conducted under a unified training protocol. The key experimental settings are summarized in Table 2. Briefly, all models were trained using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001 and a weight decay of 1e-4. The learning rate was adjusted using ReduceLROnPlateau based on validation loss. No early stopping was applied, and the checkpoint with the highest validation accuracy was selected as the final model for test-set evaluation.

Results

Image enhancement strategy analysis

To systematically evaluate the impact of different image enhancement strategies on model convergence characteristics and diagnostic performance, as shown in Fig. 4, this study introduced four typical image enhancement methods—histogram equalization, CLAHE, Laplacian sharpening, and brightness enhancement—based on the original NCCT images. Comparative experiments were then conducted to analyze the effects of these different enhancement strategies on the model training process and final classification performance.

Comparative examples of different image enhancement algorithms applied to renal NCCT images

This study evaluated various enhancement strategies under the unified MSF-TEA Net framework and the controlled enhancement protocol described above. All enhancement methods were compared using the same patient/case-level data split, preprocessing pipeline, augmentation strategy, and training protocol. The optimal enhancement method was selected according to validation-set performance only, and the independent test set was used only for final evaluation. Each enhancement method was trained independently three times, and the reported metrics are presented as the mean ± standard deviation.

As shown in Fig. 5, under different image enhancement strategies, the model’s training loss continuously decreases with the number of iterations, and the validation loss generally shows a convergence trend that is initially fast and then slows down. The validation accuracy stabilizes after approximately 20–30 epochs, indicating that the network training process is generally stable and does not exhibit significant overfitting. By comparing the curves of each method, it can be observed that Laplacian sharpening achieves a lower validation loss early in training and maintains the smoothest, lowest loss curve and highest validation accuracy curve throughout the process; the original image and brightness enhancement methods have slightly slower convergence speeds and lower final validation accuracy than Laplacian sharpening, but are still superior to histogram equalization and CLAHE. While histogram equalization and CLAHE show greater fluctuations in validation loss and more pronounced oscillations in the validation accuracy curve, suggesting that they are more sensitive to noise and case variations, and thus slightly less stable.

The impact of different image enhancement methods on model convergence behavior

The original and brightness enhancement methods also showed relatively stable performance. The original method achieved a validation accuracy of 94.47% ± 1.23%, a test accuracy of 92.48% ± 0.54%, precision, recall, and F1-score of approximately 92.54% ± 0.57%, 92.48% ± 0.54%, and 92.38% ± 0.57%, respectively. The brightness enhancement method achieved a test accuracy of 92.73% ± 0.61%, with precision, recall, and F1-score of approximately 92.91% ± 0.52%, 92.73% ± 0.61%, and 92.67% ± 0.65%, respectively. Although histogram equalization and CLAHE also achieved test accuracies of approximately 91–92%, they resulted in higher losses and showed slightly inferior convergence stability compared with Laplacian sharpening and the original images.

Compared with the original unenhanced images, Laplacian sharpening showed consistent advantages across the main evaluation metrics. Its validation accuracy increased by 2.51% points, test accuracy increased by 1.92% points, and precision, recall, and F1-score improved by approximately 1.9–2.0% points. Furthermore, the test loss decreased from 37.67% to 33.54%, indicating a lower prediction loss on the held-out test set. Overall, Laplacian sharpening provided an approximate 2-percentage-point improvement in this task and was selected as the most suitable image enhancement strategy for the identification of kidney stones, hydronephrosis, and pyonephrosis.

To further evaluate the computational efficiency of the proposed MSF-TEA Net, inference time and FLOPs were measured under the Laplacian sharpening setting. Using an input size of 224 × 224 on the RTX 5090 GPU, the model required 5.811 GFLOPs for a single forward pass and achieved an average inference time of 0.453 ms per image. These results indicate that MSF-TEA Net can complete image-level prediction rapidly under the tested hardware condition.

AUC analysis was performed using a one-vs-rest strategy for the four-class classification task. For each class, the predicted probability of that class was treated as the positive score, while the remaining three classes were treated as the negative group. Class-specific ROC curves were first calculated for normal kidney, kidney stone, hydronephrosis, and pyonephrosis. Macro-AUC was calculated as the unweighted average of the four class-specific AUC values, whereas Micro-AUC was calculated by pooling all one-vs-rest predictions across the four classes. Each experiment was repeated three times, and the reported AUC values represent the average performance across the three independent runs.

To further evaluate the discriminative power of different image enhancement strategies in multi-class diagnostic tasks, this study calculated the Macro-average and Micro-average ROC curves under different enhancement algorithms. As shown in Fig. 6, among the evaluated enhancement methods, Laplacian sharpening consistently achieved the highest AUC, with both Macro-AUC and Micro-AUC reaching 99.30%. Brightness enhancement and the original image method followed. This indicates that the proposed MSF-TEA Net is generally robust to different image enhancement strategies, while edge and structure enhancement may further improve its ability to identify lesions such as kidney stones, hydronephrosis, and pyonephrosis.

As shown in Fig. 7, the class-wise results further demonstrated the different effects of image enhancement methods on the four categories of normal kidney, kidney stone, hydronephrosis, and pyonephrosis. Laplacian sharpening achieved strong performance across most classes, but the performance was not completely balanced. For normal kidneys, it obtained a recall of 98.59% and a precision of 92.11%. For kidney stones, the precision and recall reached 93.38% and 98.60%, respectively, showing strong recognition ability for small high-density calculi. For hydronephrosis, Laplacian sharpening achieved a precision of 95.50% and a recall of 89.08%, indicating a favorable trade-off between precision and recall.

Analysis of the impact of various image enhancement strategies on the recognition performance of different categories

However, for infection-related pyonephrosis, although the precision reached 98.21%, the recall was only 83.33%, indicating that some pyonephrosis cases were not correctly identified by the model. This finding is clinically important because pyonephrosis is an urgent infection-related condition, and false-negative predictions may delay timely intervention. The lower recall may be associated with the overlapping NCCT appearances between severe hydronephrosis and early pyonephrosis, especially when infection-related wall thickening, fluid-density changes, or inflammatory texture features are subtle. Therefore, although Laplacian sharpening improved overall class-wise recognition by enhancing lesion boundaries and structural cues, the class-level sensitivity of pyonephrosis should be interpreted cautiously. Future work should further optimize the infection/pyonephrosis branch and investigate threshold-adjusted strategies to improve sensitivity in screening-oriented clinical scenarios.

Ablation experiments

To evaluate the contribution of each key component of MSF-TEA Net, this study constructed several ablation variants by removing individual modules from the complete MSF-TEA Net framework. The complete model consisted of the ResNet-18 backbone, PPM, ASPP, SE, TEA, and the multi-task structure. The evaluated variants included baseline_resnet18, no_ppm, no_aspp, no_se, and no_tea.

In all ablation experiments, precision, recall, and F1-score were calculated using the weighted-average strategy to account for class imbalance. Therefore, these metrics may not always be numerically identical to accuracy, especially when class-wise performance differs across imbalanced categories. All ablation variants were trained and evaluated under the same fixed training protocol to ensure methodological consistency.

As shown in Table 4, baseline_resnet18 achieved an average accuracy of 88.47% ± 5.73%, with relatively unstable performance across repeated runs. This suggests that using the backbone alone was insufficient to consistently capture the multi-scale and multi-morphological imaging patterns of kidney stones, hydronephrosis, and pyonephrosis.

When either PPM or ASPP was removed from the MSF-TEA Net framework, the accuracy decreased to 92.31% ± 1.76% and 92.23% ± 0.54%, respectively. After removing the TEA module, the accuracy decreased to 91.73% ± 1.06%, and the F1-score also decreased. This suggests that explicitly modeling the three types of evidence—stone structure, hydronephrosis morphology, and infection-related texture—helped improve the model’s discriminative ability in complex cases with overlapping pathological features.

Among the single-module removal variants, no_se achieved relatively better performance, with an accuracy of 93.57% ± 0.63%. Its performance was higher than those of no_ppm, no_aspp, and no_tea, but still lower than that of the complete MSF-TEA Net. This suggests that SE channel attention provided a moderate additional benefit, whereas PPM, ASPP, and TEA played more central roles in improving model performance.

Overall, the ablation results suggest that PPM, ASPP, and TEA are important components supporting the performance of MSF-TEA Net, while SE provides a relatively smaller but positive contribution. Because the dataset is imbalanced and each experiment was repeated three times, the differences among ablation variants should be interpreted cautiously.

Performance comparison with mainstream deep learning models

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of the proposed MSF-TEA Net in the four-class classification task of normal kidney, kidney stones, hydronephrosis, and pyonephrosis, this study compared it with several mainstream CNN-based and hybrid architectures, including ResNet50 [18, 26], DenseNet201 [27], Xception [28], InceptionResNetV2 [29], and the classic VGG16 [30, 31]. In addition, to address recent advances in Transformer-based vision models, Swin-Tiny [32] was included as a representative vision Transformer baseline. All baseline models were trained and evaluated under the same fixed training protocol to ensure a controlled comparison.

The experimental results are shown in Table 5. Under the same fixed training protocol, MSF-TEA Net achieved the highest average performance among the evaluated CNN-based, hybrid, and Transformer-based models, with a test accuracy of 94.40% ± 1.13%. Compared with InceptionResNetV2, Xception, DenseNet201, ResNet50, and Swin-Tiny, MSF-TEA Net showed higher average accuracy under the present experimental setting. The weighted-average precision, recall, and F1-score were 94.46% ± 1.10%, 94.40% ± 1.13%, and 94.36% ± 1.15%, respectively. These results suggest that the integration of multi-scale feature fusion and Tri-Evidence attention may be beneficial for representing heterogeneous renal lesion features in this NCCT four-class classification task. However, the comparison should be interpreted as a controlled comparison under a fixed training protocol rather than as individually optimized benchmarks for each baseline model.

Discussion

This study addresses the continuous pathological spectrum of “kidney stones-hydronephrosis-pyonephrosis” and constructs a unified automatic identification framework using NCCT. Under a unified preprocessing and data partitioning strategy, the system compares the impact of various image enhancement schemes on training convergence and diagnostic performance. Based on this, the MSF-TEA Net is proposed as the core diagnostic model, achieving joint four-category identification of normal kidneys, kidney stones, hydronephrosis, and pyonephrosis. The overall results indicate that this framework can simultaneously account for differentiated imaging clues such as small, high-density calculi, macroscopic deformation of the collecting system, and infection-related textural abnormalities within the same model. Under the optimal enhancement strategy, the model achieved an overall performance of 94.40% ± 1.13% test accuracy and an AUC of 99.30%, validating the effectiveness and feasibility of the unified process design and evidence-based collaborative modeling for complex multi-disease identification tasks in the kidneys.

Image enhancement experiments further demonstrate that the strategy of “enhancing edges and high-frequency structural information” is more effective in consistently improving model generalization in the NCCT four-category classification task than the strategy of “simply increasing local contrast.” Compared to the unenhanced original images, Laplacian sharpening achieved consistent gains at both the validation and testing levels: a 2.51 point increase in validation accuracy and a 1.92 point increase in test accuracy. This indicates that the enhancement not only improves discriminability but also leads to a more thorough optimization process and more robust generalization. In contrast, while histogram equalization and CLAHE can enhance contrast, they are more prone to introducing noise sensitivity and convergence fluctuations, resulting in overall less benefit compared to Laplacian sharpening. This phenomenon is explainable at the task level: signs related to kidney stones and pyonephrosis often manifest as boundary thickening and subtle textural changes, and emphasizing edge/structure enhancement is more suitable for these diagnostic clues.

Ablation experiments mechanistically explain the source of the performance gains of the MSF-TEA Net. The accuracy of baseline_resnet18 was only 88.47%±5.73%, suggesting that relying solely on conventional backbone features is insufficient to cover multi-scale and multi-morphological patterns of kidney lesions; removing PPM or ASPP reduced the accuracy to 92.31%±1.76% and 92.23%±0.54%, respectively, indicating that multi-scale contextual information plays a crucial role in capturing morphological changes in the collecting system and suppressing interference from complex backgrounds. More importantly, removing TEA further reduced the accuracy to 91.73% ± 1.06%, indicating that without explicit decoupling and adaptive fusion of the three types of evidence—stone structure, hydronephrosis morphology, and infection texture—the model is more prone to unstable discrimination in transitional states (such as kidney stones combined with hydronephrosis, or the evolution of hydronephrosis into pyonephrosis) and scenarios where multiple signs coexist. Meanwhile, the decrease in no_se was relatively small, suggesting that, given the strong discriminative representations already established by PPM/ASPP and TEA, channel recalibration provides only a marginal improvement.

The comparison results with mainstream models further highlight the necessity of this design. MSF-TEA Net achieved a test accuracy of 94.40% ± 1.13%, which is higher than InceptionResNetV2 (92.31% ± 1.36%), Xception (91.73% ± 1.02%), DenseNet201 (90.48% ± 2.16%), and ResNet50 (88.97% ± 0.54%). The weighted Precision/Recall/F1 scores also consistently show superior performance, indicating that its advantage does not stem from a deeper or larger network size, but rather from the structural improvements of “multi-scale modeling oriented towards pathological evidence + adaptive fusion.” In particular, VGG16 showed significant instability in this task (accuracy only 52.21% ± 23.16%), which indirectly demonstrates that traditional feature representations are not suitable for NCCT diagnostic scenarios involving multiple diseases, varying scales, and ambiguous boundaries between categories; while MSF-TEA Net achieved more robust overall performance under the same settings, validating its generalization and robustness in the unified multi-disease recognition task.

In summary, this study organically integrates “enhancement strategy screening—multi-scale feature extraction-three-evidence attention fusion—multi-task supervision” into a unified process, achieving stable joint identification of kidney stones, hydronephrosis, and pyonephrosis within the same framework. The contributions of the key modules and enhancement strategies were validated in multiple experiments.

Although MSF-TEA Net achieved favorable performance, this study still has some limitations. The dataset was collected from a single center and no independent external institutional validation was performed. Therefore, the reported results mainly reflect internal validation under a patient/case-level split, and the generalizability of the model beyond the study site remains to be further confirmed. In addition, the dataset was imbalanced, with pyonephrosis having the smallest sample size, which may affect the stability of class-level performance estimation. Future studies will include external multi-center validation, larger pyonephrosis cohorts, multi-slice or 3D NCCT information, and robustness evaluation across different scanners and acquisition protocols.

Conclusion

This study proposes a unified NCCT diagnostic framework based on multi-scale feature modeling and three-evidence adaptive fusion, achieving automatic four-category classification of normal kidneys, kidney stones, hydronephrosis, and pyonephrosis. Experimental results show that the model maintains stable performance under different image enhancement strategies and significantly outperforms several mainstream convolutional and hybrid network structures under optimal configuration, validating the effectiveness of multi-scale contextual modeling and evidence-level fusion mechanisms in the recognition of complex kidney lesions. This method can simultaneously account for diverse imaging features such as tiny stones, collecting system dilation, and infection-related textures within the same framework, demonstrating good generalization potential and promising clinical applications. It can provide reliable technical support for intelligent assisted diagnosis of urinary system diseases.

Future studies will further include external multi-center validation, larger pyonephrosis cohorts, multi-slice or 3D NCCT information, and robustness evaluation across different scanners, acquisition protocols, and clinical populations.
