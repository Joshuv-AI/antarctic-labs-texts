Initial Injury Type and Violent Reinjury
Authors: Nicholas Szoko, Daphne Lew, Taylor Kaser, Zoe M. Miller, Matt Vogel, Michael A. Mancini, Lindsay M. Kranker, Christopher Behr, Vicki Moran, Rachel Ancona, Kristen Mueller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionHow does risk of violent reinjury vary across different initial injury types?FindingsIn this cohort study of 29 810 patients with violent injuries, the probability of reinjury at 7 years was highest among those with index blunt assault (17%) and stabbing (20%) injuries. Individuals with index firearm injuries had the highest risk of firearm reinjury (13%).MeaningThe findings of this study suggest that preventing violent reinjury requires understanding different injury contexts to better address root causes of repeated violence exposure.

Introduction

In 2022, there were more than 40 million emergency department (ED) visits for violent injuries (ie, blunt assaults, stabbings, firearm injuries), which remain the leading cause of death among children and young adults in the United States.1,2 Violent injuries disproportionately impact communities with social vulnerability due to historical oppression and concentrated disinvestment.3,4 Most individuals survive their injuries, however, and often experience impacts beyond their physical wound, such as lost employment, disability, and increased mental health challenges.5 There may also be secondary effects on family members and other loved ones.6 Because of this, violent injury has profound individual and societal implications, leading to population-level costs exceeding $4 trillion annually.7 Unfortunately, violent injury also increases the risk of subsequent injury (ie, violent reinjury),8,9 necessitating strategies that interrupt repeated episodes of violence.

Numerous theoretical models have been applied to better understand the recurrent nature of violence, which span the fields of social psychology and child development. Many scholars situate violent reinjury within a life course framework,10 which acknowledges the profound developmental and psychosocial impacts of early experiences of violence (eg, experiencing abuse, assault, or bullying). For example, individuals impacted by abuse in childhood are more likely to experience intimate partner violence (IPV) as young adults.11,12 In addition, there is evidence that the severity of violence increases with each exposure. Children with abuse have increased risk of severe injury and mortality with each subsequent maltreatment report,13 and prior IPV is a known predictor of IPV homicide.14 The burden of violent reinjury falls disproportionately on individuals from minoritized backgrounds, particularly Black youths, who are the most likely to experience multiple episodes of violence over time.15 Living in areas with high rates of community violence may further contribute to reinjury risk.16,17,18 While the mechanisms for these trends are complex, growing work highlights the role of multilevel (eg, peer, family, neighborhood) contextual factors,19 rather than individual characteristics, on driving these patterns.

When a patient presents to the ED with a violent injury, it is important to consider how reinjury risk may vary by injury type and context. Prior work has demonstrated increasing injury severity with each hospital visit,20 which aligns with theoretical models of reinjury. Similarly, repeat violent injury is a risk factor for mortality.21 That said, most studies of violent reinjury fail to provide information about whether escalating injury mechanisms (eg, blunt assault escalating to firearm injury) are driving this trend. Characterizing different injury mechanisms could offer insights into aspects of injury context that are not captured by severity score alone. This may include nuances such as how weapon access influences injury patterns, what types of violence occur among specific subgroups, and whether intent differs across injury mechanisms. Each of these considerations could have important implications for prevention efforts.

The majority of studies focused on violent reinjury have only assessed reinjury risk for a single injury type (eg, a firearm reinjury after an initial firearm injury),22,23 and most are limited by small sample sizes and short follow-up intervals.24 Rowhani-Rahbar et al23 examined 5-year violence trajectories using a combination of administrative and legal system data, demonstrating high rates of firearm injury and violence-related arrests after an index firearm injury. Although their arrest outcome included both firearm and nonfirearm incidents, it was focused on subsequent perpetration of violent crime and not reinjury by other mechanisms. Only one study by Nygaard et al25 explicitly investigated increasing injury severity across different injury mechanisms (ie, blunt assault, stabbing, firearm), and the authors found that while severity score increased among firearm-to-firearm–injured patients, there was no escalation of injury mechanism with repeat injuries. That said, those with blunt and stabbing injuries were more likely to experience reinjuries overall, suggesting distinct reinjury trajectories. Notably, this study was restricted to a single institution, and it did not include patients who were discharged directly from the ED.

To build on this work, we sought to examine the risk of recurrent violent injury across multiple injury types (ie, blunt assault, stabbing, firearm) using data from a multi-institutional data repository spanning 4 level I trauma centers in the St Louis region. We hypothesized that individuals with a blunt index injury would have a higher risk of reinjury by a more severe mechanism (ie, stabbing or firearm) and that individuals with an index firearm injury would have the highest risk of reinjury overall. In describing these patterns, we aimed to better understand patterns of repeated violence to strengthen prevention interventions for individuals across differing injury types.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a multicenter cohort study of patients presenting to 1 of 4 St Louis level I adult trauma centers (ATCs) and pediatric trauma centers (PTCs) for a violent injury between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2023. These centers represent all American College of Surgeons–verified level I trauma centers in the St Louis area and serve a wide catchment area of racially and socioeconomically diverse patients in Missouri, Illinois, and other surrounding states. Hospitals are located within 3 miles of each other, with an ATC and PTC in adjacent buildings on each campus. Research procedures followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines for cohort studies.26 The study was approved by the Washington University institutional review board (IRB), which serves as the umbrella IRB for participating institutions. This study posed no more than minimal risk to participants and met all federal requirements for a waiver of informed consent as per 45 CFR 46.116 (d).

Data Source

We leveraged administrative data from the St Louis Region-Wide Hospital-Based Violence Intervention Program Data Repository (STL-HVIP-DR),27 which includes encounter-level data for patients presenting to ATCs and PTCs with assault injuries. The dataset includes demographic characteristics, injury information, and disposition. All 4 participating hospitals share data with the Missouri Hospital Association (MHA), a nonprofit organization representing all acute-care hospitals in the state. The MHA provides a stable universal identifier linking patients across systems over time. Our data sharing agreement, methods for data collection, and full list of injury diagnosis codes have been previously published by our team.16,27

Study Participants

We included patients in the STL-HVIP-DR with an index violent injury between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2023 (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). Injuries were operationalized into 3 categories based on International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) external cause of injury diagnostic codes: blunt assault, stabbing, and firearm (eTable 1 in Supplement 1). We excluded encounters with missing cause of injury codes. We used a supervised machine learning algorithm to classify encounters as acute visits or follow-up from a prior injury (eg, pain management), which has demonstrated high sensitivity (95%) and specificity (89%) in the sample.16,28 Although data collection for the repository began in 2010, we limited the current study to 2016 onward, given the model has not yet been validated on nonfirearm injury classifications in the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision period. For recurrent injury analyses, we excluded participants with fatal index encounters (772 [2.6%]).

Variables

Demographic characteristics were abstracted from index injury encounters and included age (<18 years, 18-26 years, and >26 years), sex at birth (male and female), race (Black, White, and additional groups [American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, other, and multiracial], and unknown or patient declined to disclose), ethnicity (Hispanic or Latino and non-Hispanic), and state of residence (Illinois, Missouri, and other). Race and ethnicity were determined from the STL-HVIP-DR and were included in the current analysis as a proxy for structural discrimination. We used the primary payment method to assess insurance status (public [Medicare, Medicaid], private, and none). We used patient zip code at index injury to determine Social Vulnerability Index (SVI), which was operationalized into 3 categories, as has been done in prior literature (<0.25, low; 0.25-0.75, average; >0.75, high).29,30 Because we used the 2022 SVI for zip code tabulation area, values may differ for other index injury years. We classified hospital type as adult or pediatric. We used the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services Chronic Conditions Data Warehouse (CCW) codes to identify alcohol use disorder, depression, and substance use disorder,31 due to known correlations with violent injury.

Classification of Time to Reinjury

We defined index injury as the first violent injury encounter in the STL-HVIP-DR. For recurrent injuries, we calculated the time in years from the index injury to (1) the first recurrent violent injury of any type and (2) the first recurrent violent injury of each type (blunt assault, stabbing, and firearm). We then stratified the sample into 3 index injury type subgroups: blunt assault, stabbing, and firearm. For each subgroup, we similarly calculated the time to any recurrent violent injury and to each injury type (eFigure 2 in Supplement 1). For all time to reinjury calculations, persons with no recurrent injuries at all or of a particular type were censored on January 1, 2024, after the end of the study period. To further improve specificity for reinjury, we excluded encounters less than 7 days from index injury, as these likely represented follow-up rather than new acute injuries.32

Statistical Analysis

We summarized demographic characteristics using means and SDs for normally distributed continuous variables, medians and IQRs for skewed continuous variables, and frequencies and proportions for categorical variables. We present characteristics of the overall sample, as well as stratified by reinjury status and index injury type. We compared patient characteristics across index injury types using analysis of variance and χ2 tests for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. We used a significance threshold of α = .05, and all tests were 2-sided.

For overall reinjury and each index injury type, we used Kaplan-Meier time-to-event analysis to estimate probability of reinjury. Because an individual could experience multiple injury types in the study period (eFigure 2 in Supplement 1), for injury-specific analyses, we did not treat reinjuries of a different type as competing risks; however, we conducted sensitivity analyses treating different reinjury types as competing risks. We calculated estimated reinjury probabilities and 95% CIs at 1, 5, and 7 years from index injury. All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.5.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing) using the following packages: dplyr, ggplot2, survival, and tidyr.

Results

Cohort Description

Between 2016 and 2023, 29 810 individuals experienced an index injury across participating hospitals (Table 1). Adults older than 26 years represented the majority of the sample (17 228 [57.8%]), followed by young adults aged 18 to 26 years (7690 [25.8%]) and pediatric patients younger than 18 years (4724 [15.8%]). Most were male (19 933 [66.9%]), and there were 21 126 (70.9%) Black and 7031 (23.6%) White individuals, with 792 (2.7%) belonging to an additional racial group, having unknown race, or declining to disclose race. Public insurance was the most common primary payer (14 096 [47.3%]), followed by no insurance (10 913 [36.6%]) and private insurance (4792 [16.1%]). Most (24 919 [83.6%]) presented to an ATC. The majority of patients (21 515 [72.2%]) resided in areas with high social vulnerability at the time of their index injury, and 29 078 (97.6%) lived in the bistate catchment area (ie, Illinois or Missouri) of participating hospitals. Blunt assault was the most common index injury type (17 002 [57.0%]), followed by firearm (10 264 [34.4%]) and stabbing (2544 [8.5%]) injuries. Index violent injuries generally decreased over the study period (4332 [14.5%] in 2016 to 3253 [10.9%] in 2023), with the exception of firearm injuries, which increased during the COVID-19 pandemic and declined thereafter (1274 [12.4%] in 2016, 1651 [16.1%] in 2020, 1014 [9.9%] in 2023). The proportion of individuals with CCW codes for alcohol use disorder, depression, and substance use disorder was 6.5%, 5.8%, and 9.5%, respectively.

Patient characteristics varied across index injury types (Table 2). While adults (>26 years) were the most frequently injured across all injury mechanisms, patients younger than 18 years represented a larger proportion of blunt assaults (3172 of 17 002 [18.7%]) relative to other injury types (firearm: 1398 of 10 264 [13.6%]; stabbing: 154 of 2544 [6.1%]), whereas young adults (18-26 years) represented a larger proportion of firearm injuries (3428 [33.4%]) compared with other injury types (blunt: 3641 [21.4%]; stabbing: 621 [24.4%]) (P < .001). Male patients were more likely than female patients to experience injuries overall, but this proportion varied across injury types (blunt: 9509 [55.9%] vs 7490 [44.1%]; firearm: 8569 [83.5%] vs 1684 [16.4%]; stabbing: 1855 [72.9%] vs 689 [27.1%]; P < .001). Similarly, while Black individuals represented the largest proportion of each index injury, there were significant differences across injury types (blunt: Black, 10 968 [64.5%]; White, 5188 [30.5%]; firearm: Black, 8284 [80.7%]; White, 1327 [12.9%]; stabbing: Black, 1874 [73.7%]; White, 516 [20.3%]; P < .001). Adult hospitals vs pediatric hospitals saw the largest proportion of injuries across all injury types (blunt: 13 578 [79.9%] vs 3424 [20.1%]; firearm: 8959 [87.3%] vs 1305 [12.7%]; stabbing: 2382 [93.6%] vs 162 [6.4%]; P < .001), while blunt injuries represented the majority of index injuries among pediatric hospitals (n = 3424). Substance use disorder CCW codes were most prevalent among those with index firearm injuries (1197 [11.7%]; blunt: 1275 [7.5%]; stabbing: 141 [5.5%]; P < .001), and alcohol use disorder CCW codes were most common among individuals with stabbing index injuries (263 [10.3%]; blunt: 1270 [7.5%]; firearm: 415 [4.0%]; P < .001). Fatality was highest for firearm injuries (720 [7.0%]; blunt: 35 [0.2%]; stabbing: 17 [0.7%]; P < .001).

Risk of Any Reinjury by Index Injury Type

Of 29 038 patients (97.4%) who survived their index injury, 3899 (13.4%) experienced any reinjury in the study period. Median (IQR) time to reinjury was 1.0 (0.3-2.5) years, and median (IQR) observation time was 4.2 (2.1-6.2) years for censored patients. For any type of index injury, the estimated risk of reinjury was 6.9% (95% CI, 6.6%-7.2%), 15.6% (95% CI, 15.1%-16.1%), and 18.1% (95% CI, 17.5%-18.7%) at 1, 5, and 7 years, respectively (Table 3 and Figure 1). For those with blunt index injuries, the risk of a recurrent injury of any type was 6.7% (95% CI, 6.3%-7.1%), 14.8% (95% CI, 14.2%-15.5%), and 17.4% (95% CI, 16.6%-18.2%) at 1, 5, and 7 years (Figure 2). Compared with blunt index injuries, those with an index stabbing had a higher risk of any reinjury at 5 years (16.6% [95% CI, 14.9%-18.5%]) and 7 years (19.5% [95% CI, 17.5%-21.8%]). Reinjury risk was lower among those with an index firearm injury at all time points (1 year: 6.5% [95% CI, 5.9%-7.0%]; 5 years: 14.6% [95% CI, 13.7%-15.5%]; 7 years: 16.9% [95% CI, 15.9%-18.1%]).

Risk of Specific Reinjuries by Injury Type

For any index injury, the risk of a recurrent blunt injury was highest (7 years: 10.0% [95% CI, 9.6%-10.4%]), followed by firearm (7 years: 7.0% [95% CI, 6.6%-7.4%]) then stabbing (7 years: 1.6% [95% CI, 1.5%-1.8%]) (Table 3 and Figure 1). Among individuals with blunt index injuries, the probability of experiencing a recurrent blunt injury was higher at all time points compared with other reinjury types (7 years: blunt, 13.2% [95% CI, 12.6%-13.8%]; firearm, 4.0% [95% CI, 3.6%-4.4%]; stabbing, 1.5% [95% CI, 1.3%-1.8%]) (Figure 2). Individuals with an index stabbing injury also had higher risk of recurrent blunt injury relative to other reinjury types (7 years: blunt, 9.9% [95% CI, 8.6%-11.4%]; firearm, 6.6% [95% CI, 5.4%-8.0%]; stabbing, 4.9% [95% CI, 4.0%-5.9%]) as well as a higher risk of recurrent stabbing compared with other index injury categories (7 years: stabbing index injury, 4.9% [95% CI, 4.0%-5.9%]; blunt index injury, 1.5% [95% CI, 1.3%-1.8%]; firearm index injury, 1.0% [95% CI, 0.7%-1.4%]). Individuals with index firearm injuries had the highest risk of recurrent firearm injury, with an estimated probability of 5.4% (95% CI, 5.0%-5.9%), 11.0% (95% CI, 10.3%-11.7%), and 12.6% (95% CI, 11.8%-13.4%) at 1, 5, and 7 years. Sensitivity analyses using a competing risks model yielded no meaningfully different conclusions (eTable 2 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

Over the 8-year study period, more than 29 000 patients received care for violent injuries at level I trauma centers in St Louis. We found an alarmingly high risk of violent reinjury, with 18% of patients estimated to have another injury within 7 years. Black male patients were overrepresented in the cohort relative to the demographics of the St Louis metro area.33 St Louis has a history of disinvestment impacting Black populations, which others have linked to higher concentrations of community violence.34 In addition, our reinjury estimates were somewhat higher than other samples,35,36,37 underscoring pervasive impacts of community violence in the region.38 That said, survival after violent injuries was high (>97%), highlighting the robust trauma management provided by participating centers. When we examined reinjury across different injury types, several nuanced patterns emerged.

Based on evidence that mortality increases with repeat injuries,14,21 we hypothesized that individuals with blunt index injuries would experience reinjuries with escalating mechanism (ie, stabbing or firearm). This was not the case, and we instead noted a higher risk of recurrent blunt injury compared with other reinjury types. This aligns with the prior study by Nygaard et al,25 which also found no escalation in injury mechanism with repeat injuries. Notably, that study also found that experiencing blunt assault or stabbing predicted a higher odds of recurrent violence compared with other injury types. We observed a similar pattern, as those with blunt and stabbing index injuries had the highest risk of recurrent injury overall; however, firearm reinjury risk in our sample remained high. This may be due to different injury intents (eg, IPV causing blunt assaults vs community violence causing firearm injuries),39 compounded by a high burden of community firearm violence in our region. This finding is important, because it indicates the need for systemic strategies to address violence spanning multiple socioecological layers.40 Our blunt injury category was also broad, which may have led us to capture a larger scope of violent injury or violence-related diagnostic coding than other mechanisms.

While there is some overlap in risk factors for blunt and firearm assaults, important differences exist. Interpersonal conflicts, a tendency toward emotional dysregulation, and alcohol use proximal to injury are more highly associated with blunt assaults.41 Living in areas of socioeconomic vulnerability, involvement in organized criminal activity, and social networks promoting violence are stronger predictors of firearm injury.42,43 The availability of firearms (or lack thereof) may be one reason why blunt assaults did not increase risk for subsequent firearm reinjury in our study. Increased firearm availability, particularly through firearm ownership by parents and caregivers, can also lead to an increase in firearm injury and deaths.44,45 Clearly, more research examining these phenomena are needed. Regardless, our findings reinforce the need for tailored interventions to address the specific needs (eg, interpersonal, psychological, economic) of patients experiencing various types of violent injury. In particular, normalizing discussions of firearm ownership and reviewing safe storage practices remain a critical strategy for primary and secondary injury prevention.46

Patients with index stabbing and firearm injuries also showed distinct trajectories. Although overall reinjury rates were similar, individuals with an index stabbing injury were at the highest risk for another stabbing. In addition, while substance use disorder CCW codes were more common among individuals with firearm injuries, alcohol use disorder CCW codes were more prevalent for those with stabbing injuries. Other studies have shown that stab wound survivors are more likely to die of a drug or alcohol overdose than those experiencing firearm injuries.47 Furthermore, violently injured individuals with concurrent alcohol use are at higher risk for continued violence.48 While links between drug and alcohol use and violence are well-established,49 work exploring differences in injury mechanism across substance types is limited. That said, alcohol remains the strongest predictor of interpersonal violence,50 and concurrent use of illicit substances, such as cocaine and opioids, predicts more severe injuries.51 These findings reinforce the importance of synergistic approaches to violence prevention that simultaneously address related risk factors (eg, mental health conditions, lack of social support, financial instability), particularly ensuring access to substance use intervention, treatment, and harm reduction, especially for those with a severe violent injury.

Our findings have key research and practice implications. Conceptually, our results highlight parallel but distinct reinjury trajectories that vary across injury mechanisms. This provides important qualitative information about injury context that severity scores do not capture. For example, blunt assault or stabbing injuries may be more common in individuals impacted by interpersonal violence (eg, IPV), whereas firearm injuries represent disparate exposure to community violence. Reinjury risk in these subpopulations may be driven by unique mechanisms, necessitating prevention approaches that are tailored to the root causes of continued injury. Hospital-based violence intervention programs, which offer longitudinal case management and mental health support to individuals with violent injuries, may be well positioned to address interpersonal violence through directed outreach and resource provision, while continued exposure to community violence may reflect broader social, economic, and political factors requiring systemic reform. Thus, beyond mitigating an individual’s risk for future reinjury, efforts to prevent violence necessitate attention to larger sociostructural drivers involved.

Future work should explore qualitative differences between injury trajectories to identify subgroups who would benefit from specific interventions. For example, latent trajectory analysis would enable characterization of multiple reinjuries (ie, second, third, fourth, and so on) in a given timeframe, building on insights from a traditional time-to-event approach. Evaluating differences in reinjury across gender, racial, and sexual identities may also provide insights into distinct risk and protective factors. Further delineating clinical (eg, substance use) and contextual (eg, relationship between perpetrator and survivor) moderators of reinjury risk could also illuminate opportunities for intervention. It is also essential to improve measurement of injury intent to better contextualize observed reinjury trends. Linking across data sources (eg, health system, administrative, legal system) can also increase the richness of analyses, allowing characterization of related outcomes.23 Overall, conducting comprehensive assessments of injury trajectories can guide practitioners in supporting those at highest risk for future mortality, strengthening efforts to promote healing and longevity.

Limitations

There are several limitations to our study. First, our administrative data repository relies on diagnostic codes to define injuries, which could result in misclassification, particularly for injury intent.52 We lacked information on injuries that occurred before the study period. Because of this, the index injuries in our study may not be the first violent injury someone experienced. While our data captured information across 4 hospitals, we did not measure out-of-hospital deaths, including those due to violence, nor did we capture reinjuries occurring at other health systems, potentially underestimating reinjury incidence. Although we examined injury patterns across different injury types, we lacked information on injury severity, another important predictor of recurrent injury risk.21 Blunt assault also acted as a catch-all category for any nonfirearm and nonstabbing assaults, which could explain the higher risk of recurrent blunt injuries in certain subgroups. If blunt assault injuries were less severe, patients may also seek care in a non-ED setting, leading to underestimation of true incidence. That said, our study’s strengths include a larger sample size and longer follow-up period than previous studies and characterization of multiple injury types.

Conclusions

In this cohort study, individuals who survived an initial violent injury had a nearly 20% probability of violent reinjury within 7 years. Reinjury risk varied based on initial injury type. These findings suggest that violent injuries have specific contexts that should be understood when developing programs to address repeated exposure to violence.
