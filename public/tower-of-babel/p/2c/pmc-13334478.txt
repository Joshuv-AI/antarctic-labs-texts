Aggregation and surface-state modulated fluorescence quenching of lemon-derived carbon quantum dots for the sensitive detection of 4-aminophenol
Authors: Unzila Saeed, Sunnia Tariq, Sara Riaz, Muhammad Nasir, Usman Latif, Akhtar Hayat, Cong Kong, Mian Hasnain Nawaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
4-Aminophenol (4-AP) is the primary degradation product of paracetamol (PA), resulting from its hydrolytic cleavage within the human body and is known for its nephrotoxic, hepatotoxic, and teratogenic nature. Its enhanced levels may cause mutagenic responses and DNA damage in living organisms; therefore, the accurate detection of 4-AP is important for public health monitoring. In this study, a facile, green, and one-step approach for the synthesis of carbon quantum dots (CQDs) using lemon juice as a sustainable carbon precursor and avoiding toxic reagents and harsh conditions is reported to efficiently quantify 4-AP in aqueous media. The prepared CQDs were comprehensively characterized by UV-visible spectroscopy, zeta potential, DLS, FTIR spectroscopy, Raman spectroscopy, XRD, and FESEM. Furthermore, to enhance detection performance, the fluorescence intensity of CQDs was optimized by varying the excitation wavelength, concentrations of CQDs and 4-AP, pH and ionic strengths. The probe exhibited good fluorescence stability over a wide pH range and in the presence of different salts (NaCl, KCl, and MgCl2) and superior selectivity even in the presence of several potential interfering species. Moreover, high sensitivity towards 4-AP was demonstrated, with a superior limit of detection (0.71 µM) and a wide linear detection range (50 to 2500 µM). The practical applicability of the probe was further validated through spiked serum samples, demonstrating satisfactory recovery rates and good reproducibility. Overall, the proposed method provides a robust platform for the detection of 4-AP and demonstrates strong potential for applications in clinical research, diagnostics as well as biomedical and environmental monitoring.

Introduction

Recently, a plethora of advanced materials has been designed to offer superior intrinsic properties compared to conventional materials; however, the applications of these materials are hindered because of their biohazardous nature, lack of biocompatibility, and complex manufacturing requirements.1 To address these challenges and promote sustainable practices, a new class of carbon-based materials, carbon quantum dots (CQDs), has emerged, with size less than 10 nm. CQDs stand out for their favorable photoluminescence, low toxicity, stable emission, water solubility, eco-friendly nature, resistance to photobleaching, and excellent biocompatibility. Due to their intrinsic superior properties, CQDs bear enormous potential for a variety of applications, including drug delivery,2 gene delivery and development of bioimaging probes, energy storage devices,3 optical sensors,4,5 fluorescent inks,6 light-emitting diodes,7 and photocatalysts.8 Recently, several techniques have been reported for the synthesis of CQDs, such as pyrolysis,9 hydrothermal10 and solvothermal methods,11 acidic oxidation,12 laser ablation,13 and microwave/ultrasonic passivation.14 However, many methods require toxic reagents, high temperatures, prolonged reaction time, specialized equipment and multiple processing steps, depending on the precursor and synthesis route employed. To address these limitations, green synthesis approaches involving the use of renewable, inexpensive and biocompatible carbon-based sources have gained increasing attention, which minimize the environmental burden, reduce risk to human health, and enhance process efficiency and reproducibility.15 Based on this approach, several natural precursors, such as apple juice,16T. indica leaves,17 radish,18 milk, egg,19 bamboo leaves,20 garlic,21 orange peel,22,23 and honey,24 have been used to synthesize CQDs.

On the other hand, paracetamol (PA), a globally used analgesic and antipyretic drug, is typically considered safe; however, high doses of PA may produce harmful metabolites, which can cause significant health issues.25–27 Hydrolysis of PA within the body results in different metabolites, including 4-aminophenol, which is nephrotoxic, hepatotoxic, and teratogenic in nature, is poisonous and causes irritation to the eyes, respiratory system, and skin.28 The enhanced levels of 4-AP may cause mutagenic responses, which may consequently cause DNA damage in living organisms; therefore, the selective and sensitive detection of 4-AP concentration in serum using a rapid method is critical for a safe and healthy life. Previously, different techniques have been used for the analysis of 4-AP in different samples, including surface-enhanced Raman scattering,29 electrochemical response measurements,30,31 high-performance liquid chromatography,32,33 gas/liquid chromatography,34 and capillary electrophoresis,.35 However, these techniques require harsh synthesis conditions, difficult detection procedures, skilled personnel, and complicated carbon sources.

In this work, to address these limitations, the green synthesis of highly fluorescent CQDs from lemon juice was performed via a hydrothermal method at a moderate temperature. The size, morphology, and optical properties of the synthesized CQDs were systematically characterized, followed by their application as an efficient fluorophore for the quantification of 4-AP through fluorescence quenching. Notably, under UV irradiation, CQDs showed intense blue fluorescence, which was blue shifted on the addition of 4-AP. The designed sensor platform was anticipated to deliver superior analytical performance for both qualitative and quantitative determination of 4-AP, even in complex biological environments.

Experimental section

Chemicals and reagents

4-AP was purchased from Daejung Chemicals & Metals Co., Ltd. Glucose, fructose, tween 20, potassium chloride, uric acid, dopamine, tyrosine, hydroquinone, cysteine, and hydrogen peroxide were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Urea, acetaminophen, and glutathione were purchased from Merck. All the reagents used in this work were of analytical grade, and all the standard solutions were prepared using deionized (DI) water for the analysis.

Instrumentation

The surface morphology was assessed by field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) using an Apreo S instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Eindhoven, The Netherlands). Charge distribution and average particle size were assessed using a Zetasizer Nano-ZS90 instrument (Malvern PANalytical, UK). To study the surface functionalities, Fourier Transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was recorded in the spectral range of 4000–400 cm−1 with a Nicolet-6700 FTIR spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The phase crystallinity of the synthesized CQDs was studied using a PANalytical X'pert powder diffractometer (Malvern, UK), over a scan range of 20°–80° with a step size of 0.02°, and 10° speed/minute. Vibrational modes were investigated using an in via Raman microscope (Renishaw, UK) with a 514 nm excitation wavelength of the laser source, and UV-visible absorption spectra were investigated using a Multiskan SkyHigh Microplate Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) over a scan range of 200–800 nm. Fluorescence spectra of samples were measured with a Cary Eclipse Fluorescence Spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, USA) with emission and excitation slits set at 5 nm, using a quartz cell.

Synthesis of CQDs

CQDs were prepared from lemon juice (lemons were collected from a local market) using a hydrothermal process at a comparatively moderate temperature, as shown in Scheme 1. Deionized water (25 mL) was mixed with pulp-free (filtered) fresh lemon juice (100 mL), filtered using a 0.22 µm syringe filter, and the resulting solution was then poured into a stainless-steel autoclave lined with polytetrafluoroethylene (Teflon) and heated for sixteen hours (16 h) at 160 °C to ensure sufficient carbonization and the formation of fluorescent CQDs. Afterwards, the reaction system was allowed to cool naturally at room temperature. The resulting dark brown solution was filtered with 0.45 µm membrane filters to remove large carbonaceous aggregates and unreacted bulky particulates formed during the thermal treatment of lemon juice. This filtration was primarily employed as a preliminary purification step for eliminating bulky impurities. Though trace amount of byproducts (low molecular weight) may remain in the solution, the successful formation of CQDs was confirmed through microscopic and spectroscopic characterizations, including FESEM, FTIR spectroscopy, UV-vis spectroscopy, and fluorescence analysis. Therefore, the collected filtrate was used for subsequent characterization and sensing studies. The obtained CQDs were stored at 4 °C.

Schematic of the synthesis of CQDs using a simple hydrothermal method.

To demonstrate the 4-AP induced fluorescence quenching of the probe material, a standard solution of analyte was prepared and further diluted to various concentrations ranging from 50 µM to 2500 µM using DI water. Typically, for 4-AP detection, 200 µL of CQDs and varying concentrations of 4-AP (50 µM–2500 µM) were added to the reaction mixture, and DI water was added to make the final volume 3 mL. The mixture was then incubated for 10 minutes at room temperature, followed by measurement of fluorescence spectra at an excitation wavelength of 350 nm.

For real sample analysis, serum samples were collected from a local diagnostics laboratory. For the detection of 4-AP, 200 µL CQDs were added to the pretreated serum samples, which were then spiked with different concentrations of 4-AP. Briefly, 1000, 1500, and 2000 µM of standard 4-AP solutions were added to 200 µL of the probe solution and then mixed with 300 µL of the pretreated serum sample. Fluorescence spectra of the spiked samples along with the probe were recorded. The same procedure was applied for all three concentrations of 4-AP in human serum.

Results and discussions

Green-synthesized CQDs from lemon juice contain two types of functional groups on the surface, namely, hydroxyl and carboxyl groups. The sensing response of CQDs toward 4-AP is likely attributed to arise from a combination of hydrogen bonding, surface adsorption, and electron-transfer interactions. The amino and hydroxyl groups of 4-AP can interact with oxygen-containing functionalities present on the CQD surface, including carboxyl, hydroxyl, and carbonyl groups. These interactions promote the adsorption of 4-AP onto the CQD surface and facilitate electron transfer between the analyte and the fluorescent nanomaterial. Owing to its para-substituted aromatic structure, 4-AP exhibits enhanced electron delocalization and stronger interaction with the CQD surface, which may facilitate non-radiative electron-transfer processes and consequently alter the fluorescence intensity. Therefore, the selective fluorescence response is attributed to the unique structural and electronic characteristics of 4-AP rather than solely to the presence of amino or hydroxyl functional groups. The structural and morphological properties of the synthesized CQDs and CQDs/4-AP were confirmed by FESEM, as shown in Fig. S1. As evidenced by the micrographs of CQDs, an urchin-like microstructure consisting of rod-like secondary structures represents an enhanced surface area. The morphology of CQDs was retained even in presence of the analyte, which indicates the retention of the backbone structure of CQDs, as evidenced in Fig. S1(B).

Moreover, in Fig. 1A(a) the XRD pattern of CQDs showed a single broad diffraction peak positioned at 2θ = 23.46°, demonstrating the presence of characteristic graphitic plates, which is consistent with the (002) interlayer spacing of the carbon-based material. The lattice spacing of 3.78 Å specifies a partially amorphous structure and the presence of additional oxygen-containing functional groups in CQDs. The XRD pattern of CQDs (Fig. 1A(a) shows distinct diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 9.3°, 15.02°, 16.8°, 19.3°, 22.05°, 25.24° and 30.18°. Following the interaction with 4-AP, a significant enhancement in the intensity of these diffraction peak was observed, Fig. 1A(b). This increase in peak intensity is attributed to the formation of a more ordered long-range structural arrangement among the CQD molecules, indicating enhanced crystallinity of the material.

Similarly, Fig. 1B(a) shows the Raman spectrum of CQDs, which demonstrates two broad peaks at 1364 cm−1 and 1584 cm−1, corresponding to the disordered band D (sp3 hybridization) and crystalline band G (sp2 hybridization), respectively. The intensity of the D band is lower than that of the G band, indicating the sp2 carbon-based composition of CQDs with fewer sp3 carbon defects. The intensity ratio of the peaks of the D to G band (ID/IG) is 0.5, which indicates a partial amorphous carbon structure of CQDs with fewer lattice defects. Hence, distinct D and G bands indicate the development of graphitic domains and successful carbonization of the precursor under selected reaction conditions. These observations suggest the synthesis of structurally stable and fluorescent CQDs. To investigate the functional groups on the surface of CQDs, FTIR spectroscopy was used in the smart attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode, and spectra are shown in Fig. 1C. The strong, broader peak at 3300 cm−1 was attributed to the stretching vibration of the hydroxyl group (O–H). The peaks positioned at 1670 cm−1 and 1467 cm−1 were attributed to stretching vibrations of the carbonyl group (CO) and the deformation vibrations of the –CH2, respectively. Additionally, a minor peak at 1191 cm−1 was observed which could be attributed to the stretching vibrations of C–OH. Consequently, the results justify the presence of hydroxyl (–OH) and carboxyl groups (–COOH) on the surface of CQDs; moreover, two new peaks at 3208 cm−1 and 1358 cm−1 were present in the FTIR spectra of CQDs/4-AP due to the stretching vibrations of N–H and C–N, respectively, and a peak at 1491 cm−1 indicated the bending vibration of N–H. All these results demonstrate the successful binding of 4-AP on CQDs.

Similarly, in terms of surface charge calculations via zeta potential measurements, CQDs showed a high positive zeta potential of 261 mV, while the zeta potential of 4-AP was negative, i.e., −0.73 mV, and the zeta potential of CQDs/4-AP was found to be 244 mV, as shown in Fig. 1D. This reduction in the zeta potential of CQDs was due to the conversion of some functionalities (–COOH and –OH) upon interaction with 4-AP. Similarly, the size distribution of the synthesized CQDs was confirmed using DLS, as shown in Fig. 1E. The increased size of CQDs/4-AP as compared with that of pristine CQDs ensured efficient 4-AP anchoring and complemented the above-mentioned zeta potential results.

Furthermore, the synthesized CQDs showed an absorption peak at 281 nm, as shown in Fig. 1F(a), which could be assigned to the π → π* transition system of sp2 hybridized CC and CO bonds. The absorption spectra of 4-AP and CQDs/4-AP, are shown in Fig. 1F(b and c), respectively. The attachment of 4-AP to the CQD surface caused a decrease in the band gap due to increased particle size, resulting in a red shift. Also, in Fig. 1F(c), an additional absorption band was observed around 450 nm, which was absent in the individual spectra of CQDs and 4-AP. This is likely due to the formation of electronic interactions between 4-AP and the surface functionalities of CQDs. Such interactions may alter the surface electronic states of CQDs and give rise to new absorption transitions. Therefore, the observed absorption feature may also be influenced by aggregation-related electronic coupling between neighboring CQDs. Based on the available experimental evidence, the band at ∼450 nm is tentatively attributed to a combination of surface-state modulation and aggregation-induced electronic interactions following the binding of 4-AP to the CQD surface. Moreover, the inset in Fig. 1F represents the yellowish-brown aqueous solution of CQDs in daylight, which appeared blue under UV radiation, confirming the fluorescence nature of the prepared CQDs.

Fig. 2A shows that the emission intensity of CQDs (Fig. 2A(a)) was highly quenched in presence of 4-AP (Fig. 2A(b)), and the same proof-of-concept was used to optimize the different analytical parameters for sensor fabrication. Moreover, to demonstrate the selectivity of the system, different possible interferants, including various biomolecules, salts, and several amino acids, present in human serum, were analyzed as part of the interference study. The probe material gave no significant response against these species, which include glucose (Glu), fructose (Fru), Tween 20 (TWN), potassium chloride (KCl), uric acid (UA), dopamine (DA), tyrosine (Tyr), hydroquinone (Hqn), cysteine (Cys), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), urea (Ur), acetaminophen (AP), and glutathione (GSH), in comparison to 4-AP under similar experimental conditions, as shown in Fig. 2B. The high selectivity of CQDs toward 4-AP is likely attributed to its unique substituted aromatic structure and the synergistic effect of para-positioned amino and hydroxyl groups, which promote efficient interaction and charge transfer with CQDs surface. Although other interfering species possess amino and/or hydroxyl functionalities, differences in molecular structure, functional group orientation, interaction strength and electronic properties result in significantly weaker interactions and negligible fluorescence changes.

To achieve optimal performance of CQDs, different excitation wavelengths and concentrations of the CQD-based fluorescent probe were also optimized. Fig. 3A demonstrates the fluorescence emission spectra of CQDs with excitation wavelength ranging from 300 nm to 400 nm, with an increment of 10 nm. CQDs exhibit strong fluorescence with increasing emission intensity up to 350 nm, which further periodically decreased from 350 to 400 nm; such variation in the emission intensity of CQDs at distinct wavelengths can be attributed to the surface functionalities and polydispersity of CQDs. The wavelength with maximum emission intensity (350 nm) was used for further studies. In Fig. 3B, the fluorescence intensity of CQDs gradually decreases with increasing CQDs concentration (from 200 µL to 2000 µL). This behaviour is likely attributed to aggregation-induced quenching, where the reduced interparticle distance at higher concentrations promotes stronger particle–particle interactions and facilitates non-radiative relaxation pathways, resulting in decreased fluorescence emission. Furthermore, aggregation may alter the surface electronic states of CQDs and affect the distribution of emissive surface sites. Moreover, the sensitivity towards 4-AP was determined by recording fluorescence spectra of CQDs with several concentrations of 4-AP (50, 100, 200, 400, 500, 600, 800, 1000, 1500, 2000, 2500 µM). As shown in Fig. 3C, the fluorescence intensity of CQDs progressively decreased with increasing concentration of 4-AP, indicating efficient fluorescence quenching. This quenching is likely associated with interactions between the 4-AP and the surface functional groups of CQDs through hydrogen bonding, surface adsorption, and possible electrostatic interactions may further modify the surface states and contribute to fluorescence quenching. To further investigate the quenching behavior, Stern–Volmer analysis was performed according to the following equation:where Fo and F represent the fluorescence intensities of CQDs in the absence and present of 4-AP, respectively; Ksv is the Stern–Volmer quenching constant, and [Q] is the concentration of 4-AP. The corresponding Stern–Volmer plot (Fig. 3D) showed a linear relationship between F0/F and 4-AP concentration over the range of 50–2500 µM with a correlation coefficient (R2) of 0.999, and the Stern–Volmer quenching constant (KSV) was calculated to be 7.77 × 10−4 µM−1, indicating efficient quenching of CQD fluorescence by 4-AP. The strong linearity of the Stern–Volmer plot suggests a concentration-dependent quenching process and demonstrates the suitability of CQDs for quantitative detection of 4-AP. Based on the Stern–Volmer plot data, the limit of detection (LOD) was calculated to be 0.71 µM. Therefore, the observed fluorescence response is proposed to arise from a combination of aggregation-induced non-radiative decay and surface-state modulation caused by the interaction of 4-AP with the CQD surface.

To demonstrate the stability of CQDs under different experimental conditions, the influence of pH (4,7, and10) and salts (NaCl, KCl, and MgCl2) on the fluorescence intensity of the prepared CQDs was evaluated. Altering the pH from 4 to 7 resulted in an increase in fluorescence intensity, while at pH 7 the maximum fluorescence intensity was observed, followed by a decrease in fluorescence from pH 7 to 10, as shown in Fig. 4A. This behavior could be attributed to the protonation of the carboxylic (–COOH) and hydroxyl (–OH) groups on the CQD surface, which subsequently could causes quenching of the fluorescence intensity. Protonation of the surface functionalities can disrupt the π-electron conjugation, resulting in decrease in fluorescence intensity. In contrast, basic conditions (pH 10) could lead the deprotonation of these surface functionalities, resulting in an increase in surface charge and inducing electrostatic interaction within the solution. These interactions cause energy transfer from CQDs to other species, which can deactivate the excited state and result in fluorescence quenching. However, no apparent change in the fluorescence properties of CQDs was found in presence of different salts (NaCl, KCl, and MgCl2), as shown in Fig. 4B. These salts did not interact with the surface functionalities of CQDs; instead they dissociate into their respective ions in water and preferentially interact with solvent molecules rather than the surface functionalities of CQDs. Consequently, the fluorescence intensity of CQDs was not significantly affected even in different saline environments. Additionally, the repeatability, applicability, and stability were systematically investigated. The repeatability was evaluated by measuring 8 successive readings in the presence of 540 µg mL−1 of 4-AP, and the RSD value was calculated to be 2.34%, as shown in Fig. 4C. Furthermore, the synthesized CQDs were incubated for 36 days, and the emission spectra were observed at regular intervals, showing apparently unaffected results, as shown in Fig. 4D. The daily recorded studies showed only a 1.59% RSD value, which confirms the higher stability of the prepared CQDs response against 4-AP and excellent suitability for analytical applications.

To demonstrate the practical usage of the designed platform, CQDs were effectively applied for the detection of 4-AP in real serum samples. Human serum from a local diagnostic laboratory was collected and used for real serum analysis following the standard addition method.36 After the pre-treatment, fluorescence spectra of serum samples were recorded with CQDs, followed by the analysis of CQDs with serum sample spiked with three different concentrations of 4-AP (i.e., 1000 µM, 1500 µM, and 2000 µM). After carefully examining the fluorescence spectra, the % recoveries were calculated, which were found to be between 98.5% and 107.1%, and the relative error ranged between 1.06% and 4.87% (Table 1). The obtained acceptable, precise, and accurate error percentages, the satisfactory level of statistical parameters indicate the reliability and accuracy of the results in the detection of 4-AP even in complex biological matrices.

Conclusion

In summary, a simple one-step hydrothermal approach was employed to synthesize highly fluorescent carbon quantum dots (CQDs) using lemon juice as a green precursor. The synthesized CQDs demonstrated sensitive and selective interactions with 4-AP, offering a low limit of detection (0.71 µM) along with a wide linear range. The fluorescence quenching mechanism was attributed to the interaction with 4-AP, enabling rapid detection within 3 minutes for a single analysis. Importantly, the entire process adhered to the principles of green chemistry, eliminating the use of hazardous reagents. The label-free, biocompatible, and highly emissive probe showcased excellent performance for 4-AP detection in complex biological matrices with satisfactory recovery results, highlighting its potential for applications in analytical and biomedical fields.
