Prevalence and inequalities of missed opportunities for intermittent preventive therapy (IPTp) of malaria among pregnant women: an analysis of 2018 Nigeria DHS
Authors: Salisu Muhammed Ladan, Natália P. Lima, Adebola E. Orimadegun, Yohannes Dibaba Wado, Joshua O. Akinyemi, Abdullahi A. Jatau, Cauane Blumenberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalaria in pregnancy and its devastating consequences can be prevented with the use of adequate doses of intermittent preventive treatment with sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (IPTp-SP). Missed opportunities for IPTp-SP are defined as pregnant women who attended four or more antenatal care (ANC) visits but did not receive the recommended three or more IPTp-SP doses during ANC. Missed opportunities for IPTp-SP remain high, despite modest antenatal care (ANC) coverage in Nigeria. However, there is a paucity of evidence on the level of inequalities of missed opportunities for IPTp-SP use among pregnant women in Nigeria. This study aimed to assess the prevalence and inequalities of missed opportunities for IPTp-SP of malaria among pregnant women in Nigeria.MethodsA secondary analysis of the 2018 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) datasets was conducted. The study analysed data on 12,498 women aged between 15 and 49 years with a live birth two years before the survey who were recruited based on a stratified two-stage cluster sampling method. The prevalence and socioeconomic disparities in IPTp-SP missed opportunities were determined using descriptive analysis, and a Sankey diagram was developed to facilitate the identification of patterns of missed opportunities for IPTp-SP. Pearson's chi-squared test for heterogeneity and logistic regression analysis were used to assess inequalities and intersectionality of variables, respectively.ResultsThe study revealed a high prevalence (75%) and a pro-rich/pro-educated inequality of missed opportunities for IPTp-SP among pregnant women in Nigeria. Among women who attended four or more ANC visits, 75% received fewer than three IPTp-SP doses; this represents 42.5% of the total sample analyzed in this study. It showed an inverse relationship with prevalence increasing as education and wealth decrease. The prevalence of worst-case scenario (< 4 ANC visits and < 3 IPTp-SP doses) was highest among the oldest (40–49 years) age group with a prevalence of 44.7%, among rural residents (50.4%), women with no formal education (61.6%), and those in the poorest wealth quintile (64.0%) (all p < 0.001). The prevalence of the best-case scenario (4 + ANC visits and 3 + IPTp-SP doses, with at least one dose administered during an ANC visit) was highest among women in the intermediate age group 35–39 (14.1%, p = 0.001). The probability of being in the worst-case scenario was 75% among the poorest women with no education versus 5% among the wealthiest with higher education, indicating significant intersectional inequality, with education having a stronger effect than wealth in reducing missed opportunities.ConclusionThe high prevalence and socioeconomic inequality favoring the wealthy and educated of missed opportunities for IPTp-SP among pregnant women in Nigeria, suggesting a significant deficiency in IPTp-SP delivery during ANC, and deserve urgent attention from policymakers. Deliberate policy approaches and programmatic actions targeting the poor and less educated pregnant women may address the observed disparities. Further studies are required to understand the influence of demand and supply-side factors on the prevalence and inequalities of missed opportunities for IPTp-SP among pregnant women in Nigeria.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05840-0.

Background

Malaria remains a disease of global public health importance with attendant high morbidity and mortality despite the availability of curative and preventive therapies and interventions. In 2022, there were an estimated 249 million malaria cases in 85 malaria-endemic countries, an increase of 5 million cases compared with 2021 [1]. In contrast, globally, malaria deaths declined steadily from 864,000 in 2000 to 576,000 in 2019—which, in terms of mortality rate, would be a reduction from 29 to 14 per 100,000 population at risk [1, 2]. Due to the disruption of malaria control efforts by the COVID-19 pandemic, the mortality rate increased again to 15.2 in 2020, before decreasing slightly to 14.3 in 2022 [1].

Between 2000 and 2019, Africa’s malaria mortality rate decreased by 60%, from 143 to 57 deaths per 100,000 population at risk, although it rose to 61 in 2020, likely due to COVID-19 disruptions. It declined again to 56 in 2022 [1]. In 2022, four Sub-Saharan African (SSA) countries accounted for almost half of all malaria cases globally; Nigeria led the pack with 27% of cases, followed by the Democratic Republic of the Congo with 12%, Uganda, and Mozambique with 5% and 4%, respectively [1]. Similarly, four countries accounted for just over half of all malaria deaths globally in 2022: Nigeria (31%), the Democratic Republic of the Congo (12%), Niger (6%), and the United Republic of Tanzania (4%) [1]. Leading the burden of cases and deaths, Nigeria has always been a country in which the prevention and treatment of malaria were a challenge, due to limited access to antimalarials, cultural and behavioral impediments, and sustainability problems [3].

Population groups with a high disproportionate risk for malaria infection include children under the age of five years, non-immune migrants, People living with HIV/AIDS on account of reduced immunity, and pregnant women [2]. In 2022, there were an estimated 35.4 million pregnancies in Sub-Saharan Africa, of which 12.7 million (36%) were exposed to malaria infection during pregnancy [1]. The prevalence of exposure to malaria during pregnancy was highest in West (39.3%) and Central Africa (40.1%) and lowest in East and Southern Africa (27.0%) [1].

Malaria in pregnancy (MiP) poses substantial risks for the mother, her fetus, and the newborn [4]. In areas with moderate to high transmission of Plasmodium falciparum, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends a package of interventions for controlling malaria spread and its effects during pregnancy, which includes the promotion and use of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), the administration during pregnancy of intermittent preventive treatment with sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (IPTp-SP), and appropriate case management through prompt and effective treatment of malaria in pregnant women [5]. In 2004, the WHO recommended at least two doses of IPTp-SP during pregnancy [5]. This recommendation was updated in 2012, increasing to at least three doses of IPTp-SP for all pregnant women after the first trimester. These doses should be given to women at each scheduled antenatal care (ANC) visit until the delivery time, provided that the doses are given at least one month apart [6]. Nigeria adopted this policy nationwide in 2014 [6] and has been implementing it as part of its malaria prevention program.

Evidence from SSA countries shows that the uptake of IPTp-SP among pregnant women has reduced low birth weight by 42%, neonatal deaths by 38%, and placental malaria by 65% [4]. A meta-analysis result revealed the full benefits of reduced risk of placental malaria, low birth weight, and neonatal deaths when pregnant women receive three or more IPTp-SP doses [4]. Missed opportunities for IPTp-SP are instances where pregnant women who attended four or more antenatal care (ANC) visits but did not receive the recommended three or more IPTp-SP doses during ANC, contrary to WHO guidelines. In turn, several studies have reported missed opportunities for IPTp-SP among pregnant women. Studies from Nigeria and Tanzania revealed that some pregnant women were unaware of its benefits [7, 8]. Other studies from Burkina Faso, Ghana, Mali, Malawi, Kenya, Nigeria, Sierra Leone, and Uganda concluded that sociodemographic factors such as age, religion, region, area of residence, marital status, and socioeconomic factors like wealth, education, and employment were also determinants of missed opportunities of IPTp-SP among pregnant women [9–11]. However, a gap exists in understanding and characterizing missed opportunities of IPTp-SP among pregnant women in Nigeria, the country with the highest burden of malaria cases and deaths in the world.

This study aimed to estimate the prevalence of missed opportunities for intermittent preventive treatment of malaria in pregnancy with sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (IPTp-SP) and assess the socioeconomic inequalities associated with these missed opportunities among pregnant women in Nigeria using data from the 2018 Demographic and Health Survey (DHS).

Methods

Data source

This study involved the secondary analysis of data from the 2018 Nigerian National Demographic and Health Survey (DHS), a nationally representative population-based cross-sectional survey involving data collection from selected locations across the 36 states of the Federation and the Federal Capital Territory. The survey was conducted between 14 August and 29 December 2018. The study population in the Nigeria DHS was randomly selected using a stratified sampling method. Stratification was achieved by separating each state into urban and rural areas. Enumeration areas were used in the first stage, and a fixed number of households were selected using equal probability sampling for the second stage.

The authors registered and received permission to download and use the DHS datasets from the DHS Program website (https://dhsprogram.com). The datasets provided to the researchers were anonymized.

Study population

The study sample included women aged 15–49 who were either permanent residents of the selected households or visitors who stayed in the households the night before the interview and whose most recent pregnancy resulted in a live birth two years preceding the survey.

Missed opportunities for IPTp-SP framework

We created an analysis framework for malaria prevention based on the number of ANC visits (two categories: less than four and four or more ANC visits), the number of IPTp-SP doses received (two categories: less than three and three or more IPTp-SP doses), and the source of the antimalarial (two categories: during and not during ANC visits) to assess the coverage and identify missed opportunities for malaria prevention. The ANC visits were categorized based on the WHO’s focused antenatal care (FANC) model, which considers four visits as the minimum standard for adequate care. IPTp-SP doses were categorized according to WHO’s 2012 recommendation that at least three doses of SP be administered after the first trimester for effective malaria prevention during pregnancy [6]. The combination of the categories from these variables comprised our framework and primary outcome, which classified women into six groups:(i)Less than 4 ANC visits and less than 3 IPTp-SP doses(ii)Less than 4 ANC visits and three or more IPTp-SP doses, none of which were administered during an ANC visit(iii)Less than 4 ANC visits and three or more IPTp-SP doses, with at least one dose administered during an ANC visit(iv)4 or more ANC visits and less than 3 IPTp-SP doses(v)4 or more ANC visits and three or more IPTp-SP doses, none of which were administered during an ANC visit(vi)4 or more ANC visits and three or more IPTp-SP doses, with at least one dose administered during an ANC visit

Group i represents the worst-case scenario, where women attended less than 4 ANC visits and received less than three doses, failing to meet the recommended IPTp-SP dosage. Group vi reflects the ideal scenario: women who attended four or more ANC visits and received three or more IPTp-SP, with at least one dose during an ANC visit, ensuring the recommended treatment was administered during the ANC. According to the WHO definition, women who attended less than 4 ANC visits are considered not to have had the opportunity to receive the treatment (groups i, ii, and iii) [12]. Still, some women reported receiving at least one IPTp-SP dose during an ANC visit (group iii). Any situation where women who had 4 or more ANC visits and received less than three SP doses, or received three or more SP doses with none administered during an ANC visit, is considered a missed opportunity for treatment (groups iv and v).

This classification of missed opportunities for IPTp-SP was guided by the conceptual framework developed by Andrews et al. (2015), which considers frequency of ANC visits and the number and timing of IPTp-SP doses received.

Dimensions of inequality

The outcome was described according to selected sociodemographic characteristics, including age, parity, area of residence, educational level, and household wealth index. These dimensions of inequality were analyzed as categorical variables and operationalized according to the definitions presented in Additional file 1: Table S1.

Data analysis

We conducted data cleaning to identify and address missing values, outliers, and logical inconsistencies. Women with missing data on ANC attendance, number of IPTp-SP doses received, or the timing of IPTp-SP administration were excluded. Logical checks ensured consistency between reported ANC visits and receipt of IPTp-SP during ANC. Contradictory records were removed from the analytical sample.

Categorical variables were described as absolute and relative frequencies. A Sankey diagram was developed to represent the malaria prevention framework, facilitating the identification of patterns of missed opportunities for treatment.

Inequalities in the distribution of ANC visits and IPTp-SP doses were assessed by comparing the prevalence of the outcome across categories of each inequality dimension using Pearson's chi-squared test for heterogeneity. Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons.

A logistic regression analysis assessed the intersectionality of variables, including interaction terms. Marginal effects were calculated to examine how the outcome probability varied across different groups, and confidence intervals were computed.

The data were extracted from women’s individual recode datasets for the 2018 NDHS. STATA version 18 (STATA Corp, College Station, TX, USA) was used for data extraction, cleaning, and analysis. All analyses accounted for the complex survey design of the DHS using the ‘svy’ suite of commands in STATA. Sampling weights were applied to produce nationally representative estimates. We adjusted for stratification and clustering at the enumeration area (EA) level using the identifiers provided by the DHS. The survey design was declared using the svyset command with appropriate variables for weight (v005/1,000,000) and strata (v022). All analyses considered a 5% significance level.

We constructed a Sankey diagram to create a visualization representing the flow of women through the IPTp-SP framework based on ANC attendance and the number of SP doses received. This allowed us to identify patterns of missed and non-missed opportunities for IPTp-SP. The width of each flow segment in the diagram corresponds to the number of women in each category. This facilitates the understanding of cascade gaps. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and multicollinearity was evaluated through variance inflation factors.

The worst-case scenario (< 4 ANC visits and < 3 IPTp-SP doses) was most prevalent among the oldest (40–49 years) and youngest (15–24 years) age groups, with a prevalence of 44.7% and 43.5%, respectively (p < 0.001). It was also more common among rural residents (50.4%), women with no formal education (61.6%), and those in the poorest wealth quintile (64.0%) (all p < 0.001). An inverse pattern was observed with educational level and wealth, with prevalence increasing as education and wealth decreased.

In contrast, for the best-case scenario (4 + ANC visits and 3 + IPTp-SP doses, with at least one dose administered during an ANC visit), the prevalence was higher among women aged 35–39 (14.1%), followed by the 25–34 age group (13.1%) (p = 0.029), representing the intermediate age groups. Women living in urban areas also had a higher prevalence (17.3%) compared to those in rural areas (p < 0.001). Prevalence increased with higher levels of education and wealth, being higher among women with higher education (22.2%) and those in the wealthiest quintile (21.4%) (both p < 0.001). A similar pattern was observed for the group that attended four or more ANC visits but took less than three IPTp-SP doses.

Probability of worst-case scenario in the IPTp-SP framework

The indirect relationship between wealth and education and the probability of being in the worst-case scenario was further investigated by intersectionality analyses presented in Fig. 2. This probability was as high as 70% for the poorest women with no education, compared to 5% for the wealthiest with higher education. Figure 2 also shows the effects of achieving higher educational attainment and wealth. The reduction in the probability of being in the worst-case scenario in the IPTp-SP framework is greater within wealth groups as education increases, compared to the reduction observed within education groups as wealth increases. For instance, among women with no education, the probability of being in the worst-case scenario reduced from 61% for those in the second wealth quintile to 23% among the wealthiest—equivalent to a 62% reduction. In turn, among women in the second wealth quintile, the reduction in probability for those with no education compared to those with higher education was 72% (61% vs. 17%, respectively)—greater reductions in probability were observed as education level increased, compared to increases in wealth. An analysis of the intersectionality between area of residence and education yielded similar results, with a greater effect on education (Fig. 3). Discussion

This study aimed to estimate the prevalence and assess inequalities of missed opportunities for IPTp-SP in Nigeria. Our findings revealed a very low prevalence of non-missed opportunities for IPTp-SP treatment (13.1%) in Nigeria in 2018. Also, almost half of the sample (43.7%) did not attend at least four ANC visits and did not receive the recommended number of IPTp-SP.

The higher prevalence of the best-case scenario among women aged 35–39 may be due to a more established health-seeking behavior, more familiarity with maternal health services from previous pregnancies, and increased freedom in decision-making compared to younger or older age groups. Younger women may face access or awareness barriers, while older women may delay ANC utilization.

These results revealed a worrying situation regarding the limited access of pregnant women to antenatal care and a concerning situation regarding the content of antenatal care visits—as most women who attended four or more ANC visits during pregnancy did not receive the recommended number of SP doses for malaria treatment and prevention.

The limited coverage of antenatal care in Nigeria is a chronic problem. The 1999 Nigeria DHS reported that the national coverage of pregnant women attending at least four ANC visits was 47.7% [13], reaching 56.3% in the 2018 Nigeria DHS [14] for our analytical sample and 58% nationally [15]. This is consistent with a 0.4 percentage point yearly increase in ANC4 prevalence—half the increase observed in other West & Central African countries[16]. This slow pace of improvement in ANC4 coverage is influenced by multiple factors, such as socioeconomic barriers like poverty, lack of access to healthcare facilities, poor infrastructure in rural areas, low maternal education levels, cultural beliefs, inadequate awareness about the importance of ANC, long distances to healthcare centers, high cost of services, and limited availability of skilled healthcare providers [17].

This study found a high ANC4 coverage among the wealthiest (88.8%) and highly educated (92.5%) women, consistent with the findings of studies in Uganda [18] and Togo [19]. However, the large ANC4 coverage does not fully capture the challenges of implementing the IPTp-SP prevention program, as our findings indicate that only 21.4% of the wealthiest and 22.2% of the highly educated women did not miss the opportunity for IPTp-SP treatment during antenatal care. This suggests that high ANC attendance alone does not guarantee a low prevalence of missed opportunities for IPTp-SP. Apart from the coverage, the quality and content of antenatal care are also areas of concern. The ANCq is an antenatal care quality indicator operationalized as a score ranging from 0 to 10 that aims to capture interventions related to contact with services and content of care provided during ANC visits [16]. Only 34% of women in Nigeria scored eight or more points in 2008, scaling to 43% in 2018—a 0.9 percentage point annual increase in 10 years, nearly half the increase in other countries in the region (1.6 percentage points per year) [16].

Other studies have also documented the drivers of the large prevalence of missed opportunities for IPTp-SP treatment [20]. These include operational aspects, such as drug stock-out, and personnel issues, such as poor health worker attitude and poor staff remuneration [20]. However, these aspects affect subgroups of the population differently, as evidenced by our inequality analyses.

The prevalence of non-missed IPTp-SP opportunities differed between subgroups of women’s age, educational level, area of residence, and wealth. Women residing in urban areas had a 1.8 times higher prevalence of non-missed opportunities than those living in rural areas. The difference was even larger regarding the level of education, the odds being 3.6 times higher among highly educated compared to non-educated women (22.2% vs. 6.2%). Also, comparing the wealthiest to the poorest, the prevalence was 3.1 times higher among the better-off. Demographic and socioeconomic inequalities exist prominently in Nigeria across all settings (urban and rural), with the wealthiest and highly educated enjoying unfettered access to the most scarce services and resources [21]. However, access to services is not the only problem since the prevalence of women attending at least four ANC visits but who received less than three SP doses was larger among the non-educated, poorest women living in rural areas. Hence, awareness campaigns targeting socioeconomically disadvantaged pregnant women should be implemented to increase their access and to educate them to hold healthcare workers accountable for providing good-quality care during antenatal care.

The intersectionality analysis also revealed the superiority of attaining higher educational qualifications over wealth in reducing the probability of being in the worst-case scenario in the IPTp-SP framework. Hence, it is equally important to encourage women to attain higher educational qualifications to reduce the probability of falling into the worst-case scenario and reduce the burden of missed opportunities for IPTp-SP.

The strength of this study is that it uses a nationally representative sample from the 2018 DHS survey, and the findings from this study are generalizable to pregnant women in Nigeria.

The limitation of this study is that the 2018 Nigeria DHS datasets lack variables on supply-side and service provider factors, such as quality of service delivery, SP stock-outs, and healthcare worker performance, which may explain the observed inequalities.

. Therefore, it is important to conduct further research on the influence of the supply-side and service-provider factors on the prevalence and inequalities of missed opportunities. Also, the 2018 Nigeria DHS datasets did not provide variables for all three doses of IPTp-SP received during ANC contact; we used at least one dose of SP received during ANC contact for our evaluation of prevalence. Therefore, the 13.1% prevalence of respondents meeting the WHO recommendation of three doses of IPTp-SP is likely even lower than what we observed in our study. Lastly, the DHS data on missed opportunities for IPTp-SP among pregnant women is subject to recall bias because it is a self-report received from respondents.

Conclusion

The findings of this study revealed a high prevalence of missed opportunities for IPTp-SP among pregnant women in Nigeria in 2018. It is deeply disturbing that the inequalities favor wealthy and highly educated pregnant women residing in urban areas, not only regarding access to antenatal care but also to the content and quality of the care provided. The study also revealed the superiority of education over wealth in reducing the probability of being in the worst-case scenario in the IPTp-SP framework. Hence, policy-makers must strengthen policy approaches and design programmatic actions targeting the poor and less educated pregnant women to address the observed disparity in IPTp-SP uptake. Further research is therefore recommended to understand the influence of demand and supply-side factors on the prevalence and inequalities of missed opportunities for IPTp-SP among pregnant women in Nigeria.
