Implementation of a pragmatic pathway to ensure high-quality processing of heterogeneous remote health sensing data
Authors: Mina Ostovari, Natalie Crimp, William Ashe, Nutta Homdee, John Lach, Fitzgerald Marcelin, Emmanuel Ogunjirin, Sarah Ratcliffe, Virginia LeBaron
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRemote health monitoring systems (RHMS) can improve access to care and health outcomes by enabling continuous monitoring of an individual's overall health status outside traditional clinical settings. However, ensuring data quality and effectively cleaning and processing complex, heterogeneous RHMS sensing data collected in naturalistic environments can present unique challenges. This article presents a pragmatic approach to clean and process RHMS data received from multiple devices/sources (e.g., wearables, ambient environmental sensors) in various formats (e. g., JSON, CSV) during real-word deployments. Our proposed pathway is highly relevant given the growing use of RHMS to support home-based healthcare and provides a useful guide for others engaged in similar research. The pathway describes a series of key steps implemented by our interdisciplinary team, including data preparation, processing, enrichment, and quality assurance, all designed to ensure that the highest quality data are available for analysis. Each step is illustrated through examples from deployments of the Behavioral and Environmental Sensing and Intervention for Cancer (BESI-C), a remote health monitoring system designed to empower patients with advanced cancer and their caregivers to monitor cancer pain and distress at home. This paper fills an important gap in the literature by focusing on the “critical middle” of the RHMS data life cycle (data extraction to preparation for analysis) to ensure accurate conclusions are drawn from data output. Our proposed pathway is applicable to other RHMS, across various patient populations and contexts, that collect diverse data streams in real-world settings and offers a strategy to address those challenges and optimize health outcomes.Keywords: Remote health monitoring, Digital health, Cancer, Palliative care, Sensors, Data processing, Data quality

Introduction

The role and benefits of RHMS in healthcare

Over the past decade, remote health monitoring systems (RHMS) have been increasingly used to monitor health (Shaik et al., 2023; Ud Din et al., 2019). RHMS are available in diverse forms and broadly involve using wireless electronic devices, such as wearables, mobile applications (apps), and environmental sensors, to collect a range of health-related and contextual data with limited interference with patients’ daily lives (Hayes et al., 2023; Pramanik et al., 2019). For example, through the use of ecological momentary assessments (EMAs) — repeated brief assessments from end-users in real-time (Shiffman et al., 2008) — RHMS enable active data collection of self-reported symptom data (Heron & Smyth, 2010; Kraft et al., 2020). RHMS can also passively collect physiological data, such as heart rate, and ambient environmental data, such as room temperature (Boikanyo et al., 2023; Hilty et al., 2021).

RHMS can enhance access to care, particularly for individuals residing in remote rural areas with limited access to healthcare providers and clinical facilities (Banbury et al., 2014). RHMS can also improve patient outcomes by proactively anticipating declining health status and enabling 'just-in-time' interventions from healthcare providers (Thomas et al., 2021; Noah et al., 2018). Additionally, RHMS improve disease self-management by empowering patients and their caregivers to track physiological parameters (e.g., blood glucose, and blood pressure) and symptoms in their homes (Leo et al., 2022). Despite their potential to improve access to care and health outcomes, challenges related to processing complex, heterogeneous RHMS data (i.e., diverse forms of passively and actively collected data received in multiple formats from multiple devices) collected from individuals in their day-to-day lives can impact their effective implementation (Sagahyroon, 2017; Serrano et al., 2023).

This article summarizes challenges related to RHMS real-world data preparation and processing based on our experiences with the deployment of a home-based RHMS, the Behavioral and Environmental Sensing and Intervention for Cancer (BESI-C) (LeBaron et al., 2022). We discuss the analytic decisions our interdisciplinary team implemented within a stepwise pathway to ensure high-quality RHMS data to support various types of analyses, such as exploring correlations between ambient environmental data and patient symptoms. While we provide a brief overview of RHMS data collection challenges, the focus of this article is on the challenges related to preparing and processing complex RHMS data to support high-quality analysis. We acknowledge the myriad and important challenges related to collecting RHMS real-world data (i.e., initial data ingest) and analyzing cleaned RHMS data (i.e., generating final output, such as data visualizations). However, little attention has been given to the ‘messy middle’ of data preparation and processing that is essential to support robust conclusions from the analysis of RHMS data (Abdolkhani et al., 2019, 2023). Thus, this paper aims to fill this gap in the literature by offering a practical guide related to this critical step in the RHMS data pipeline – data preparation and processing – that can support others working with similarly complex RHMS data.

Brief overview of RHMS data collection challenges

Hardware and software issues

Challenges in utilizing RHMS often begin in the initial data collection stage, stemming from the hardware and/or the software components of the devices themselves. For example, RHMS often rely on ‘off-the-shelf’ devices, such as commercial wearables for data collection. While affordable and accessible, using these devices may introduce several issues, impacting data collection and quality (Dobson et al., 2023). For instance, commercial smartwatches receive scheduled operating system (OS) updates intended to improve the overall functionality of the device; however, the updates may inadvertently interfere with, or disrupt, custom applications (apps) installed specifically to collect health data from research participants. Additionally, varying accuracy of embedded sensors in commercial devices can result in data anomalies and outliers, complicating the process of deriving conclusions from physiological data, such as heart rate (Masoumian Hosseini et al., 2023).

Challenges related to RHMS data integration, cleaning, and privacy

As RHMS collect a range of data from different devices, such as wearables and mobile apps, each with different data formats and structures, the process of integrating and cleaning these diverse data become more complex (Philip et al., 2021). For instance, merging physiological data, such as heart rate and accelerometer data from wearable devices, with EMAs and environmental data can be a particularly challenging task due to the need to reconcile variations in time stamps and data formats across these heterogeneous sources. Furthermore, due to factors, such as unstable internet connectivity, device malfunction, or limited user engagement, gaps or missing data are common in real-world data collected via RHMS (Zhu, 2022). For example, when collecting physiological data (e.g., heart rate), motion artifacts due to walking or other user movements can alter the fit of the wearable device and affect the quality of the physiological signals being captured (Orphanidou, 2019; Roggen et al., 2013). Additionally, due to privacy and security regulations, such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)(Edemekong et al., 2023), cleaning and processing health data collected by RHMS requires robust data protection measures which can increase the complexity of data cleaning and processing procedures (Cohen, Gerke, and Kramer 2020).

Need for practical guidance for cleaning and processing RHMS data

The increasing heterogeneity of medical data (e.g., electronic health records, medical claims, and wearables) has prompted researchers to develop complex methods and frameworks that facilitate utilizing these diverse data sources. For instance, large language models (LLMs) have been increasingly used to handle outliers, missing data, and duplicate records in medical data (D. Biradar et al., 2024; Ilhanli et al., 2025; Mondal et al., 2024; Ni et al., 2024). Despite their extensive potential, LLMs still face several challenges, such as ethical and legal concerns especially related to sensitive health data, biases and errors due to the training data, and lack of contextual understanding(Ullah et al., 2024).

As described in section 1.2.2, RHMS which continuously collect multimodal data from participants in their daily lives present additional complexities. Interpreting the complex and context-dependent data collected using RHMS cannot solely rely on automated methods, such as LLMs. Furthermore, while previous frameworks designed for cleaning and processing health data, such as electronic health records, provide valuable guidance for anomaly detection and structured data processing (Kahn et al., 2012; Kotecha et al., 2022; Miao et al., 2023), they are not designed to address the unique challenges of cleaning and synchronizing multimodal sensor data in real-world remote health monitoring systems.

Existing frameworks that focus on RHMS interoperability and data transfer between system components (A. Alamri, 2012; Ahmad et al., 2009; F. Liu et al., 2016; G. Middleton et al., 2009; G. Sebestyen & Krucz, 2010) do exist; however, our proposed pathway is unique, and fills a gap in the literature, in that it provides practical advice for researchers to ensure data quality and effective processing of complex RHMS data collected in real-world settings. The pragmatic pathway we describe in this article extends prior work by detailing a pragmatic, field-tested sequence tailored to multimodal, dyadic in-home sensing and suggests steps researchers can adopt in similar systems.

Overview of the BESI-C data collection devices and system architecture

The Behavioral and Environmental Sensing and Intervention for Cancer (BESI-C) is an innovative example of a RHMS designed by our interdisciplinary team to empower patients with advanced cancer and their family caregivers (i.e., informal, unpaid ‘family’ caregivers (McGillivray et al., 2021)) to track and manage cancer-related pain and distress at home. BESI-C is designed to improve symptom management from the dyadic perspective (considers both patients and caregivers) and extend the reach of healthcare for individuals living in rural areas.

The BESI-C system has undergone numerous iterations but currently is comprised of: 1) smartwatches worn by both patients and their primary family caregivers programmed with our custom BESI-C application to facilitate the marking and characterization of pain and distress through on-demand and scheduled EMAs; the smartwatches also collect participant physiological (e.g., heart rate) and activity (pedometer, accelerometer) data; and 2) environmental sensors that unobtrusively collect ambient room-level environmental data in the home, including light, noise, barometric pressure, humidity, and temperature. Additionally, smartwatches receive Bluetooth signals from the environmental sensors to determine which room of the home the participant was in during a self-reported pain event (Fig. 1); this enables us to more fully contextualize the pain experience to specific environmental variables (e.g., pain events that occur within the kitchen may be influenced by the room temperature).

RHMS data cleaning and processing pathway

Fig. 2 describes the pathway our team developed to address the unique challenges posed by the heterogeneous nature of the data collected by BESI-C in naturalistic environments. Throughout the process, an interdisciplinary team reviews each step to ensure quality and comprehensiveness. The entire pathway consists of a series of steps, encompassing data ingest; data preparation; data processing; data enrichment; quality assurance; and generating data products. The interdisciplinary team's oversight at each stage ensures that the data are handled with the necessary expertise from multiple perspectives – from nursing to engineering to biostatistics – enhancing the overall reliability and utility of the processed data.

Pragmatic pathway for processing complex RHMS data. The process is highly iterative, indicated by dotted lines, allowing for revisiting and refining previous steps as needed, such as the need to address issues discovered during ‘quality assurance’ by returning to ‘data processing’.

Collaboration among these experts help resolve data interpretation challenges. For instance, variability identified in heart rate that the engineering team initially considered as valid data were recognized by clinicians as physiologically irrelevant “noise”. The collaborative approach ensured that data processing clearly reflected meaningful physiological results and enhanced the reliability of downstream analysis. As this paper focuses on data extraction and preparation, we will focus on steps 1 to 4 (Fig. 2) of the pathway, which include data preparation to the completion of final quality checks prior to generating data products.

Compared with other remote monitoring technologies, which primarily rely on mobile applications for symptom management and collect data from patients (Breen et al., 2015; Dibble et al., 2025; Maguire et al., 2021; Moradian et al., 2018), BESI-C is distinctive in integrating dyadic (patient-caregiver) in-home monitoring with physiological, behavioral, and ambient environmental data. While the specific features and architecture of the BESI-C system may be unique, the processing and quality assurance strategies that we present are generalizable as they provide steps that can be adapted for other symptom monitoring technologies.

Step 1: Data preparation

To assist with data preparation, we developed a systematic data preparation and verification pipeline, independent of the sensing modality and data type. In other words, whether the RHMS data are user-initiated/on-demand or scheduled EMAs, environmental, localization, or physiological data, they all go through the same process; this approach ensures consistency and quality across all data streams.

The first step of data preparation includes extracting RHMS data variables stored in JSON format from our real-time, secure cloud database using an R script and transforming it into a structured tabular format suitable for data analysis. We then compare the cloud-based data with the local data stored on smartwatches and environmental sensors to verify data accuracy and confirm that no data were lost during the data transfer process from local devices to the secure cloud. We perform this comparison by running an anti-join query (Morton et al., 2010) from the R dplyr package (Wickham et al., 2023) that returns rows from the first data frame that do not have a match in the second data frame to identify any missing data. If data exist in the cloud but is missing from local files, this discrepancy may indicate an issue with the local database write process, necessitating further investigation. Conversely, if data are present locally on the devices but missing from the cloud, we check for potential problems with the connection and upload process. After addressing these discrepancies, we remove any duplicate entries.

Data dictionaries

A foundational and critical step in our pathway involved creating a data dictionary to manage the diverse data collected by the BESI-C system and facilitate clear communication among our interdisciplinary team. Data dictionaries provide a comprehensive description of data elements (variables) within the dataset and establish a common language and understanding of the data. By clearly defining the field type (e.g., datetime) and data format (e.g., YYYY-MM-DD HH:MM:SS) and clarifying valid values for each data element, data dictionaries ensure consistency and accuracy across different users (Rashid et al., 2020). Importantly, clear and complete data dictionaries also facilitate knowledge transfer and orientation for new users of the data during engagement with the project; this can be particularly important within large, academic research teams where multiple students and trainees may be working with the data. Data dictionaries were discussed and validated among our interdisciplinary team (which includes clinicians) to reach consensus regarding variable definitions and determine appropriate inputs and ranges. Step 2: data processing

In this section, we describe our approach to processing various categories of data collected by the BESI-C system. By applying a structured processing pipeline consistently across all data types, we ensure the quality and integrity of our data, enabling robust analyses and accurate conclusions.

Determining watch wearing

A foundational step related to processing of our wearable data was the need to determine when the user was actually wearing the smartwatch in order to know what data to include for certain analyses. For example, to explore relationships between physical activity and patient self-reported pain reports, it would not be appropriate to classify times when the watch was not being worn by a patient as ‘low activity’ periods. Because we programmed the watch to turn off the flashing heart rate sensor to mitigate sleep disruption when the participant is resting (activated automatically when no pedometer activity is detected, or manually by user if desired), we anticipated accelerometer would be the logical variable to identify whether participants are wearing the watch. However, while existing algorithms that utilize accelerometer data to determine watch wearing time exist (Choi et al., 2011; Evenson & Terry, 2009; Schmidt et al., 2003; Troiano et al., 2008), correctly classifying a sedentary period remains a challenge (Vanhelst et al., 2019). Therefore, to minimize the risk of misclassification we developed a tailored approach to determine user ‘watch wearing’ status which considers multiple factors, including watch charging status, heart rate, and accelerometer data (Table 2).

We utilized a 10-min rolling window, which was an analytic decision based on accelerometer test data, and the sampling rate of the heart rate data (30 s of data every 5 min). Using accelerometer data only, an unmoving person is indistinguishable from a stationary unworn watch. However, even at rest, participants typically show some movement over a longer time window. We created ground truth test data (members of our research team performing common activities both active and sedentary), tagged with known periods of wearing and not wearing. We used this test data to identify the smallest possible window over which there is a clear separation in accelerometer vector magnitude variance between periods of wearing and not wearing. We trialed multiple combinations of rolling time windows (between 1 and 20 min) and thresholds of variance to develop the final model. We chose the 10-min rolling window and threshold at the 95th percentile of not wearing variance as this model showed 95% accuracy in predicting wearing/not wearing status in the test data.

This step illustrates how determining a ‘simple’ variable (‘watch-wearing’) can quickly become complex when working with heterogeneous sensing data, and how operationalizing such variables demands thoughtful attention to detail to ensure accurate analysis and conclusions. We determined ‘watch-wearing’ based on the following conditions.

Charging Status Check: Each 1-min data window was checked for charging status. If the watch was charging it was marked as not being worn. This result was smoothed over a 10-min window to reduce noise from brief charging events.

Charging Status Check: Each 1-min data window was checked for charging status. If the watch was charging it was marked as not being worn. This result was smoothed over a 10-min window to reduce noise from brief charging events.

Heart Rate Data Check: If the data showed the watch was not charging, or no charging data were available, we examined heart rate data. A 10-min rolling window was analyzed for heart rate values with high internal sensor accuracy (accuracy level of 2 or 3). The internal accuracy (i.e., confidence in the result) rating for the heart rate sensor is provided by the smartwatch with a rating of 0 to 3 where 0 is the least accurate and 3 is the most accurate according to the device's internal assessment. We also considered the internal sensor accuracy along with the actual heart rate numerical value (i.e., beats per minute, bpm). If any heart rate numerical value greater than 0 bpm was detected along with an accuracy of 2 or 3, we considered the watch as being worn.

Processing of ecological momentary assessment (EMA) data

While EMAs can provide helpful real-time symptom and health status reports, missing data can create challenges in knowing whether values are absent due to technical issues or related to user engagement. When we identify missing data with the system-generated, scheduled daily EMAs, we initially count the expected occurrences (e.g., we expect 14 daily EMA responses per patient and caregiver, one for each day of the 2-week data collection period) and then generate data visualizations using R programming language to identify possible reasons for missing data. This report identifies the status of the smartwatch during the expected response time, such as whether the EMA user-interface option was available but not interacted with, whether the smartwatch was on the charger and not being worn by the participant, or whether the watch was powered off.

If none of these scenarios explain the missing EMA data, we consult the device activity logs recorded on the watches for further troubleshooting. These logs are programmed into the BESI-C smartwatch application to track any actions performed on the smartwatch by a study participant. Examples of logged actions that our team has found particularly useful include configuring and activating physiological sensors; completing user-initiated EMAs; or whether the daily, scheduled EMA option/button appeared at the appropriate time. The system logs play an especially crucial role in our ability to identify and resolve issues related to missing or inconsistent EMA data.

Processing of physiological and activity data

RHMS devices, such as wearables, can record comprehensive physiological and movement data from individuals, such as heart rate, accelerometer, and pedometer data. To accurately process these data, we established expected value ranges for each variable based on two main criteria: 1) the technical minimum and maximum values provided by the device manufacturer; and 2) feasible clinical ranges determined in consultation with the clinical team. If any values fall outside the predetermined ranges, they are flagged for further team discussion. Additionally, we check the internal accuracy ratings for the heart rate and pedometer sensors provided by the smartwatch and retain only data points with a rating of 2 or 3 (where 0 is the least accurate and 3 is the most accurate according to the device's internal assessment). Given the high variability of heart rate values and the prevalence of low internal accuracy readings, we elected not to use heart rate as a clinically relevant physiological variable. Instead, we found the heart rate data most useful in conjunction with corresponding accelerometer data as a proxy to determine when participants are wearing their smartwatches (see Table 2).

In addition to being a proxy for watch-wearing, we also utilized the accelerometer data to identify the level of activity using three-dimensional vector magnitude before each self-reported pain event. The vector magnitude, which is the magnitude of the 3-axis accelerometer, is calculated as the square root of the sum of the squares of the accelerometer readings in the x, y, and z directions. For each patient, we applied a 5-min time window to the accelerometer data, using median preprocessing to smooth the readings. A 5-min window was chosen to balance temporal resolution with the need to capture meaningful activity states preceding a pain event. Median smoothing was selected for its robustness to short, spurious spikes in the accelerometer signal. To account for inter-patient variability in movement patterns, k-means clustering algorithm (Likas et al., 2003) was applied to each participant's full accelerometer dataset independently. This generated participant-specific centroids for low, medium, and high activity levels. Notably, the thresholds for these activity levels were individualized, with different cut-off points for each patient to account for variations in their movement patterns.

The pedometer data provided another measure of activity by counting the number of steps taken per 24 h. In cases when we identified unusual data, such as a sudden spike of 1500 steps recorded within a minute, we discarded those values as they exceeded the physical capabilities of a person to sprint, suggesting a technical malfunction of the pedometer sensor.

Processing of localization data

Many RHMS include the ability to track and record participant location, which can help establish important correlations between an individual's environment and their health status. Our team examines the Bluetooth Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI) (Pu, & PuLee, 2011) on the smartwatches from environmental sensors to determine which room of the home the participant was in during a self-reported pain event. This allows us to link the location of the pain event with the specific environmental context in which the pain event occurred, thus providing evidence for environmental factors that may impact the pain experience.

We have encountered several challenges in using RSSI for location identification. The localization data obtained from sensors can be noisy and fluctuate due to multiple factors, such as signal interference from other devices within the home (e.g., Wi-Fi routers) and physical obstructions, such as walls blocking the signal. To address these issues, we apply data smoothing techniques (Murray, 2016) to reduce irregularities in the localization data. Specifically, we established a small 2-s window over which we calculated the maximum RSSI. This was necessary due to the sampling rate of the Bluetooth signal from the environmental sensor which transmit pulses every 1 to 1.5 s. Within each 2-s window, we identified the strongest RSSI value. We used a larger 10-min window to sum up all the maximum RSSI values from the smaller windows. This allowed us to determine which environmental sensor had the most frequent and strongest RSSI values. By counting how many times the strongest RSSI values were detected for each sensor, we can identify which sensor the person was closest to most frequently, thereby determining their most likely location within the home. Any 10-min windows with ambiguous location data, as indicated by multiple environmental sensors having similar signal strengths, were flagged for further investigation to resolve the uncertainty.

Our on-demand/user-initiated EMAs also include a ‘ground truth’ question to capture the patient/caregiver's self-reported location in their home during each recorded pain event (e.g., kitchen, bedroom, family room, etc.). This provides one way to help resolve uncertainty with participant location. We compare the ground truth location recorded on the EMA with the localization data collected from smartwatch sensors to verify the participant's location. If these differ, we chose the location participants self-report on their EMA, recognizing that home floor plans or signal obstacles may cause errors in how we interpret sensor localization data.

Processing of environmental sensing data

During each deployment, environmental sensor data streamed to the secure cloud are continuously monitored to verify the sensors are functioning properly. If any unusual activity is detected, such as missing sensor data, the team attempts to troubleshoot the issue remotely, although some issues require intervention from the participants (e.g., improper equipment setup, switched off electrical outlets, etc.). After the deployment, the collected aggregate environmental sensor data undergo group review, and any unusual data points are discussed and, when possible, resolved. As one example, while working with the environmental sensor data, we realized that the temperature levels were higher than what would be expected in indoor environments (e.g., temperature readings approximating 96 °F/36 °C). This systematic offset was likely due to minor self-heating of the sensor components within the enclosed device casing. However, the differences and relative trends in readings over time (e.g., “deltas”) were preserved, due to the linear behavior of the temperature sensor (Texas Instruments, 2017) across the relevant temperature range and the consistency of the additional heat. Similarly, humidity recordings were lower than reference values but correctly matched trends.

We continue to iterate approaches to cleaning and processing the environmental data, including calibrating the signals to reference sensors and using relative deltas in place of absolute measurements to extract useful information for data analysis. Similar strategies have been previously incorporated for monitoring indoor/outdoor air quality (Levy Zamora et al., 2023). The approach of using relative deltas is particularly helpful when detecting the degree/amount of change is more important than measuring absolute values, such as when changes in environmental conditions can trigger behavioral or physiological responses. This focus on relative change when processing/analyzing streaming environmental data was also applied to barometric pressure, whereas light and noise levels were treated as event-based measures because the absolute magnitude was less critical than its presence or absence.

Step 3: Data enrichment and contextualization

Real-world data collected by RHMS requires contextualization. For example, missing BESI-C data may be due to limited participant engagement with the system, significant changes in health status, or power outages that impact internet connectivity. To examine discrepancies in the data collected with BESI-C, we implemented a systematic process to help contextualize the data which begins with our data analyst sharing any anomalies in the data with the broader team for further discussion and investigation. During these weekly discussions, we seek to gather relevant contextual information from various sources, including the Clinical Research Coordinator (CRC) who directly interfaces with participants and is the first point of contact for any difficulties encountered during deployments. For instance, if participants did not complete scheduled EMAs, the CRC may be able to provide additional information about the specific circumstances that could have impacted the participant's ability to complete the EMAs, such as deteriorated health status or hospitalization. To further the contextualization process, we investigate the Ground Truth Logs completed by both patients and caregivers at the end of each day during the deployment (Fig. 4).

Participant ground truth logs.

The Ground Truth logs include both checkboxes to indicate if something was done or occurred, as well as free-text options to provide additional details (Table 3). Participants are instructed to use these logs to record specific facts and circumstances that occurred during the day that may impact the quality of data (for example, if they experienced technical problems with their smartwatch or had a change in their health status). Prior to data collection, we explain to participants the purpose of the Ground Truth logs and strongly encourage their completion; to date, we have had a strong Ground Truth patient and caregiver completion rate of 93%. By cross-checking the collected sensing data with the corresponding Ground Truth logs, we gain additional insights into potential reasons for inconsistent or missing data. Although requiring additional time, the combination of contextualization through team discussions, the review of the corresponding Ground Truth logs, and the triangulation of data sources (e.g., comparing participant localization from environmental sensors to self-reported location via EMAs) enables us to thoroughly investigate and understand circumstances surrounding data collection to ensure a more accurate interpretation of the data. Implications

Preparing and processing complex RHMS data for analysis collected from multiple sources from real people as they live their daily lives can be challenging and becomes even ‘messier’ as a project progresses, more data are acquired, and team members delve deeper into the data. Seemingly straightforward data cleaning questions, such as ‘how do we know when someone is wearing their smart-watch?’ can be surprisingly complex – and take more time than anticipated to determine – given the inherent limitations of commercial embedded system algorithms and considerations of custom applications relevant for specific patient populations. Prospective discussions and investigations of unusual values, albeit often time consuming, represent a valuable investment of time as they serve to uncover software bugs and related data issues that are critical to solve and reconcile; it is essential to account for this in project planning timelines and during team meetings.

Establishing a systematic pathway with well-defined steps and involving an interdisciplinary team is crucial to ensure efficiency and most importantly to draw appropriate final analytical conclusions. The proposed pathway outlined in this paper offers an approach to streamline the data processing workflow and enhance the robustness and reliability of the final data products. By incorporating expertise from multiple perspectives at each stage, the pathway addresses the unique challenges posed by heterogeneous sensing data collected in naturalistic environments. This approach not only improves the quality of the processed data but also ensures that the insights drawn are both meaningful and actionable.

Beyond addressing technical challenges, the pathway outlines best practices for key stakeholders who utilize complex remote health monitoring systems. For engineers, our pathway emphasizes the importance of building well-documented quality assurance checks that can be adapted as systems evolve and are modified over time. For data analysts, the pathway highlights the need for rigorous and transparent steps in data cleaning and cross-validation to ensure reproducibility and reliability. For clinicians, the pathway underscores the importance of close collaboration with technical experts, as clinician input ensures relevance of data outputs to best support clinical decision-making.

This work also highlights the critical importance of contextualizing complex health sensing data in the processing and cleaning stages. During each stage of the pathway, our team continually discovered the necessity of contextualizing our data streams with the experience of participants engaging with the technology in their day-to-day lives. We found discussions with our Clinical Research Coordinator (who interacts directly with participants in the recruitment and data collection phases of the study), review of Ground Truth Logs, and triangulating data sources (e.g., comparing environmental sensing data with EMA responses) particularly helpful strategies to contextualize data as they were being prepared for analysis. While contextualizing RHMS data is always important, we argue it is critical when collecting highly dynamic sensing data from patients with serious illness that may be used to inform clinical decision making.

While conceptualized in the context of a specific RHMS (i.e., BESI-C) it is important to emphasize that our proposed pathway is broadly applicable to other systems designed for continuous monitoring of health conditions, for instance, diabetes(Salehi et al., 2020), heart failure (Bashi et al., 2017), or respiratory diseases (Baroi et al., 2018). The key steps described regarding data preparation, processing, enrichment, and quality assurance can be adapted for different types of sensing and data collection modalities and serve as a useful guidepost for researchers and data analysts working with a wide range of RHMS in various health monitoring contexts. Importantly, the broader adoption of this pathway could accelerate advancements in the use of RHMS data that can inform more personalized care strategies, interventions, and ultimately improve health outcomes.

Conclusions

We present a pathway to support the process of data preparation, processing, enrichment, and quality assurance related to the deployment of RHMS that collect heterogenous sensing data in real-world settings. Interdisciplinary collaboration and expertise, combined with a genuine commitment to thoroughly investigate complex and unusual data, are critical to ensure final data products are accurate and have the greatest potential to positively impact health outcomes.
