DiscoDivas: Leveraging genetic-ancestry continuum information to interpolate PRS for admixed populations
Authors: Yunfeng Ruan, Rohan Bhukar, Aniruddh Patel, Satoshi Koyama, Leland Hull, Buu Truong, So Mi Cho, Whitney Hornsby, Haoyu Zhang, Nilanjan Chatterjee, Pradeep Natarajan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGenome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics for training and individual-level cohorts for fine-tuning are essential for constructing predictive polygenic risk score (PRS) models. However, the relatively low representation of admixed populations in both GWAS summary statistics and individual-level datasets hinders the development of PRSs and equitable clinical translation for admixed populations. Prior work indicates that the most informative PRS model for a genetically homogeneous sample varies linearly in an ancestry continuum space. Guided by these observations, we introduce a genetic-distance-assisted PRS combination pipeline for diverse genetic ancestries (DiscoDivas) to interpolate a harmonized PRS for diverse, especially admixed, genetic ancestries. DiscoDivas leverages multiple PRS models fine-tuned within existing samples, which are mostly of single ancestry, and genetic distance. It provides a new approach to generate genetic-ancestry-specific PRSs when a suitably matched individual-level fine-tuning cohort is unavailable or underpowered. DiscoDivas treats genetic ancestry as a continuous variable and does not require shifting across different models when calculating PRSs for different ancestries. We generated PRSs with DiscoDivas and the current conventional method, i.e., fine-tuning multiple GWAS PRSs using the matched or similar genetic-ancestry samples. DiscoDivas generated a harmonized PRS, performing comparable to or better than the conventional approach, with the greatest advantage exhibited in admixed individuals.

Introduction

Individuals of non-European ancestries remain underrepresented in genome-wide association studies (GWASs), which partly explains why polygenic risk score (PRS) performance is generally reduced in this population when compared with individuals of European ancestry.1 Within the constraints of existing data, the current principal approach to increase the PRS performance among non-European individuals is to construct a PRS model combining SNP effect sizes derived from GWAS summary statistics (discovery data) from multiple populations or multiple traits.2–6 Hyper-parameters and combination coefficients of the combined PRS models are then further optimized with an individual-level fine-tuning sample of genetic ancestry matched with the target cohort (testing sample).7–9 However, PRS performance decays as the genetic distance between the fine-tuning and testing samples increase.10 Relative to the vast diversity across the genetic-ancestry continuum, the existing and near-term individual-level datasets that can be used for fine-tuning PRS combinations remain sparse. Most existing individual-level genotype data are collected from single-ancestry populations, and, therefore, admixed populations are underrepresented or largely excluded from analysis.11–14 Additionally, fine-tuning and testing samples that are labeled as being from the same superpopulation are often truly genetically heterogeneous,13,15–18 leading to variable performance within such samples.

PRS analysis across diverse ancestries may also be limited by inconsistency. The raw PRS distributions of the same model vary by ancestry, and, therefore, the raw PRS values for individuals of different genetic ancestries should not be directly compared without ancestry correction.19–21 Although prior research19,21,22 has shown that regressing out the top principal components (PCs) of ancestry from the PRS can standardize the PRS distributions of different ancestries (i.e., the mean and standard deviation of corrected PRSs sampled from different populations become more comparable), the performance of a PRS model after PC adjustment still varies across different genetic ancestries. When applying PRSs across diverse ancestries, the options include using a single PRS model for all the individuals or several discrete PRS models for different individuals approximating superpopulations, both leading to inconsistent PRS modeling and performance.

Given these challenges and the increasing clinical use of PRSs,23–25 PRS generation for diverse and admixed genetic ancestries with more consistent performance and more unified PRS distributions is critically needed. Several methods have been developed to incorporate local ancestry inference information into PRS models to increase performance in admixed populations.26,27 However, the effectiveness of these approaches is constrained by the lack of widely accepted standards for local ancestry analysis and by their reliance on reference panels.28,29 Moreover, methods for fine-tuning PRS models have advanced rapidly in recent years, including having the ability to use summary-level genetic data.30 The advances in PRS methods and the ongoing effort to collect genetic data from diverse populations allow PRSs to be trained in cohorts with ancestry profiles that differ from those of the reference panels, presenting challenges for reference-dependent methods to fully utilize these models. Finally, if causal SNPs and their effect sizes can be accurately identified and estimated, neither observed marginal effect sizes across populations nor PRS performance decay across populations may pose a challenge in constructing PRS for diverse populations. However, the current genetic data for many traits are insufficient to reach this ideal. Therefore, it remains important to consider population differences and the notion of PRS performance decay with genetic distance when constructing PRSs across multiple populations.

In light of the present challenges and opportunities, we devised a method, a genetic-distance-assisted PRS combination pipeline for diverse genetic ancestries (DiscoDivas), to generate PRSs across the genetic-ancestry continuum directly from the fine-tuning cohorts, which eliminates the need to align individuals with external reference panels and further enhances PRS performance beyond what is achieved by existing methods based on a single fine-tuning dataset. DiscoDivas is based on the recent observations10,31 that the PRS performance in the testing data decays approximately linearly as the genetic distance between the fine-tuning data and testing data increases. Further, the genetic distance can be approximated by Euclidean distance of PCs based on global ancestries.10 Evidence supports that the performance of a PRS model trained in a European population declines linearly as the proportion of European genetic ancestry decreases in African American testing samples32 and that the PCs of ancestry can be used to determine genetic-ancestry proportion.33 Together, these observations indicate that PCs can provide meaningful information for modeling and anticipating PRS performance, most plausibly via a linear relationship. Based on the above observations, we assumed that the correlation of the most informative PRS models for two samples of different ancestries decreases linearly as the Euclidean distance of global ancestry-based PCs between them increases. Thus, in the basic derivation setting, the most informative PRS model for a genetically homogeneous sample can be linearly interpolated from the currently available PRS models that are fine-tuned in the ancestries surrounding it in the global ancestry-based PC space, with the interpolation coefficients mainly determined by the Euclidean distance of the PCs. The proposed method can be further extended to accommodate multiple genetically homogeneous sub-groups as well as heterogeneous testing samples.

In summary, DiscoDivas calculates PRSs for diverse and admixed genetic ancestries, whose currently available genetic data may not be sufficiently powerful alone to train a PRS model, by linearly interpolating the multiple PRS fine-tuned in ancestries whose genetic data are more available. We evaluated the performance of DiscoDivas in simulated and empirical data.

Methods

Methodological details of DiscoDivas

Informed by genetic-distance information, DiscoDivas interpolates PRS of testing individuals from diverse ancestries according to the testing individual’s PRS calculated using a PRS model fine-tuned in single-ancestry fine-tuning datasets. The pipeline consists of two parts: harmonizing the input PRS data and interpolating the PRS.

Data harmonization

To reduce the bias in the interpolation, the PRS and PC analysis (PCA) information should be on a unified and harmonized scale. First, all the individuals in the fine-tuning datasets and the testing datasets are projected in the same PC space based on balanced reference data covering the global genetic-ancestry continuum. The reference data are essential to avoid skewed correlation between genotypic similarity and genetic distance and to ensure that the PC Euclidean distance can present consistent genetic distance. The results presented in this study were based on PCA calculated using pruned SNPs of 1000 Genomes34 samples. A detailed description of calculating PCA and Euclidean distance in this research is provided in the Notes S1 and S2, and supplemental methods section entitled “Calculation of 1000 Genomes-based PCA and Euclidean distance.”

Second, all the PRS input should be transformed to a comparable scale. We regressed out the top 10 PCs from the PRS and then standardized the PRS residuals to mean = 0 and standard deviation = 1.

PRS interpolation

The overall mathematical model of DiscoDivas is a linear combination of PRS based on the PRS model fine-tuned in different fine-tuning datasets: PRSi=ai∑wi,kPRSi,k, where PRSi,k is the standardized PRS of individual i trained in fine-tuning dataset k; wi,k is the interpolation coefficient, which is a function of genetic distance between the individual i and dataset k and other factors; and ai is a constant for individual i such that ai∑wi,k=1.

The essential factor contributing to wi,k is the reciprocal of Di−k, the genetic distance between individual i and dataset k. Different options to estimate the value of rk

Under the additive assumption, the maximum accuracy of a PRS is achieved when the PRS R2 approximates narrow-sense heritability h2 and the PRS is saturated (namely, adding more samples in the discovery GWAS would not further increase the PRS accuracy if other conditions remain the same). Previous research used percentage of heritability explained to present the accuracy of the PRS so that PRSs predicting traits of different heritability and binary traits of different prevalence can be compared.35,36 Here we recommend using the proportion of narrow-sense SNP heritability in the target sample explained by the PRS, or the relative accuracy, as the first choice of rk rk=RPRS2h2.

However, the accurate estimation of h2 requires sufficient sample size, which may not always be available for the individual-level data. If h2 is unknown or cannot be accurately estimated, an alternative approach is provided to approximate rk. Assuming that most of the PRS was far from saturation and the performance of the PRS R2 was roughly linearly correlated with the sample size of the discovery GWAS Nk, we weighted the PRS by the square root of Nk, which is also similar to weighting effect size using the Z scores in fixed-effect-size meta-analysis: rk=Nkdiscovery.

If the trait or genetic ancestry used in the discovery GWAS does not match that of the fine-tuning and testing datasets, which are collectively referred to as the target data, the PRS accuracy will also depend on the heritability of the trait in the discovery GWAS hkdiscovery2 and the genetic correlation between the discovery GWAS trait and the target trait rgkdiscovery-target, rk=Nkdiscovery*hkdiscovery2*rgkdiscovery−target.

In a common and more ideal scenario, discovery data are a set of multiple GWASs across different genetic ancestries and possess decent and similar statistical power for each genetic ancestry. In situations where the heritability of the target trait in the fine-tuning sample is unknown or cannot be accurately estimated, the relative accuracy of the PRS fine-tuned in each single-ancestry sample is hard to estimate yet likely to be similar. Therefore, rk can be omitted or equivalently set to a default constant value of 1.

In the DiscoDivas script provided, the rk is set to be the default value 1 unless defined by the user; otherwise, ai,Di−k,dk is automatically calculated from the PCA information provided by the user.

Data

UK Biobank

The UK Biobank (UKBB) is a volunteer sample of approximately 500,000 adults aged 40–69 years upon enrollment living in the United Kingdom recruited since 2006.37 UKBB data used in this research were first quality controlled by removing the individuals meeting the criteria that indicate low genotype quality or contamination: (1) have missing genotype rate larger than 0.02; (2) have genotype-phenotype sex discordance; (3) are identified as having excess heterozygosity and missing rates; (4) are identified as putatively carrying sex chromosome configurations that are not either XX or XY; and (5) appeared to have unreasonably large numbers of relatives. From the remaining samples, only one individual from a group of multiple individuals that are closer than third-degree relatives was retained. 415,402 individuals were left after the quality control. 390,037 were self-identified as EUR, 7,039 African (AFR), 8,652 non-Chinese Asian (ASN), 1,430 Chinese (CHN), 6,572 unknown or not answered, and 1,672 as admixed (MIX). The genetic ancestries inferred from PCs were largely correlated with the self-reported ancestries, with total individuals including 385,038 EUR, 7,450 AFR, 8,298 South Asian (SAS), 2,163 East Asian (EAS), 669 admixed American (AMR), and 11,784 “other” (OTH), which indicates admixed ancestry. Detailed procedures for genetic-ancestry inference are provided in the supplemental information (see section entitled “genetic-ancestry inference” in the supplemental methods). A comprehensive comparison of PCs among OTH, EUR, and AMR individuals is described in the Note S5.

In the PRS test, UKBB samples were grouped by their genetic ancestry (see supplemental methods). The fine-tuning datasets for the single-ancestry populations (AFR, EAS, EUR, and SAS) were based on 1,300 randomly selected samples whose self-report ancestry matched with their genetic ancestry and the probability of random forest = 1 (see Figure S3). The fine-tuning dataset for “other” or admixed ancestry (OTH) is 1,300 randomly selected samples of individuals of OTH genetic ancestry. AMR did not have a corresponding fine-tuning dataset due to its small sample size, and, therefore, we used OTH fine-tuning datasets as a proxy since the two genetic ancestries had similar PCs (see Figures S13–S15). The remaining individuals of UKBB were used as testing data.

The quantitative traits of the UKBB samples were the first measurement collected after enrollment. The lipid trait measurements were adjusted for cholesterol-lowering medication by dividing total cholesterol (TC) by 0.8 and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) by 0.7 as described before.38 Cases of coronary artery diseases (CAD) are defined using the definition described previously24; cases of type 2 diabetes (T2D) were defined as meeting any of the following: (1) International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 of E11 or self-reported diagnosis of diabetes with available T2D first diagnosis date, or (2) HbA1C ≥ 6.5%, or (3) insulin use at enrollment, or (4) use of oral glucose-lowering medication excluding isolated metformin prescription at enrollment. Controls were defined as (1) without any diabetes or prediabetes, and (2) hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) <5.7%. Cases of type 1 diabetes—characterized by (1) ICD of E10 or self-report diagnosis of diabetes mellitus (DM) with first diagnosis date of type 1 diabetes, (2) insulin initiation within 1 year of diagnosis, and (3) insulin use at enrollment—and prediabetes (without diabetes and HbA1c ≥ 5.7% and HbA1c < 6.5%) were excluded. For the quantitative UKBB traits, PRS performance was evaluated using the regression effect size (BETA), its standard error, corresponding p value, and the coefficient of determination (R2) derived from linear regression models adjusted for covariates (top 20 PCs, sex, age, and genotyping batch).

The access to UKBB data was obtained upon application. UKBB participants provided consent in accordance with the primary institutional review board (IRB) protocol, and the Massachusetts General Hospital IRB approved the present secondary data analysis of the UKBB data under UKBB application 7089.

Mass General Brigham Biobank

The Mass General Brigham Biobank (MGBB) is a volunteer sample of approximately 142,000 participants receiving medical care in the Mass General Brigham health care system recruited since 2010. MGBB data access was obtained upon application. 53,306 MGBB participants underwent genotyping via Illumina Global Screening Array (Illumina, CA). MGBB genotype data were quality controlled, imputed, and assigned one of the populations AFR, AMR, EAS, EUR, or SAS using a K-nearest-neighbor model as described previously.39 The phenotype data of CAD and T2D were drawn from PheCodes based on ICD-9 and ICD-10, from the EHR as described previously.39 MGBB participants provided consent in accordance with the primary IRB protocol, and the Massachusetts General Hospital IRB approved the present secondary data analysis. For the binary MGBB traits, PRS performance was evaluated using the regression effect size (BETA), its standard error, corresponding p value, and the Nagelkerke R2 derived from logistic regression models adjusted for covariates (top 20 PCs, sex, age, and genotyping batch).

All of Us research program

The All of Us (AoU) research program is a volunteer sample of more than 1 million United States residents recruited since 2016. AoU aims to engage communities previously underrepresented in biomedical research in the United States and beyond.40 AoU data access was obtained upon application, and the analysis was performed in workspace “PRS_diverse_ancestry” in the cloud-based analysis environment AoU Researcher Workbench (https://workbench.researchallofus.org/). In the present analysis, genetic data from the v7 245,394 participants who were genotyped using short-read whole-genome sequencing (srWGS) data were used. Hapmap3 SNPs were extracted for the callset with the threshold of AF > 1% or population-specific allele count (AC) >100. Related individuals were pruned according to the information provided by AoU. We did not exclude individuals whose self-reported gender was different than their assigned sex at birth and used the combination of self-reported gender and assigned sex as a single factored covariate. The predicted ancestry information was provided by AoU.41 The phenotypes were defined as described in previous research by Buu et al.42 For the binary AoU traits, PRS performance was evaluated using the regression effect size (BETA), its standard error, corresponding p value, and the Nagelkerke R2 derived from logistic regression models adjusted for covariates (top 20 PCs, the combination of assigned sex and self-reported gender, age, and genotyping batch). AoU participants provided consent in accordance with the primary IRB protocol, and the Massachusetts General Hospital IRB approved the present secondary data analysis.

Simulated data

The simulated GWAS summary statistics were derived from simulated genotype data based on 1000 Genomes reference.6 Only Hapmap3 SNPs were included in the simulation. Causal SNPs were randomly selected from the Hapmap3 SNPs and simulated per allele effect size following a normal distribution. The non-genetic factor was set to follow the same normal distribution across populations. We simulated the gradient of causal SNP numbers at 100, 1,000, 3,000, and 10,000 and set the heritability for each population to 0.6. The causal SNP effect sizes were simulated as either constant across populations or varying linearly in the PC space.

The phenotype is the sum of genetic burden and non-genetic factor: phenotypei=∑βjxj,i+Ei, where the phenotypei and Ei were the phenotype and non-genetic factor of individual i,βj was the effect size of causal SNP j, and xj,i was the number of risk alleles of individual i in SNP j.

We used PLINK243 to calculate the genetic burden based on the simulated causal SNPs and effect size. We used R to simulate the non-genetic factors by generating a normal distribution, scale the genetic burden and non-genetic factor, and generate a phenotype of heritability set to 0.6. We used up to 100,000 individuals per population to generate the summary statistics GWAS as the PRS discovery data. The remaining simulated data were left out as the fine-tuning and testing datasets. The summary statistics GWASs were generated based on the simulated genotype data and phenotype data using the “—glm” function of PLINK2.

In addition to the simulated data, we generated more realistic fine-tuning and testing datasets of a wider genetic-ancestry range by using the quality-controlled UKBB genotype data described in the section UKBB. We simulated the genetic burden, non-genetic factor, and phenotype based on the real-life UKBB genotype data with the same pipeline and parameters. A more detailed description of simulating the data is provided in supplemental methods section entitled “generating data for simulation analysis.” For the simulated quantitative traits, PRS performance was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R2) derived from linear regression models adjusted for covariates (top 20 PCs).

Results

Overview of DiscoDivas

DiscoDivas combines PRS fine-tuned in different fine-tuning samples—generally from different single-ancestry populations—to linearly interpolate PRS for individuals of diverse genetic ancestries, treating ancestry as a continuous variable. Based on the recent observations that the PRS model performance linearly decayed as the genetic distance between fine-tuning and testing sample increased,10,31–33 we assumed that an optimal PRS model (i.e., an SNP effect size vector for PRS calculation) for an ancestry representation can be linearly interpolated from other PRS models fine-tuned in other ancestries with the additional consideration of the genetic distance between the samples (right panels of Figure 1).

Under the same principle of interpolating the PRS models, the best PRS for an individual can be interpolated from PRS calculated using the PRS models fine-tuned in other ancestries for this individual. Since generating individual-specific PRS models in a testing dataset causes redundant calculation and given the difficulty of normalizing information from different datasets, we combine the PRS instead of the SNP models. The PRS of individual i PRSi in the testing sample is a linear combination of PRS based on the PRS models fine-tuned in different fine-tuning samples: PRSi=∑wi,kPRSi,k PRSi,k=βk→⋅xi→ where xi→ is the genotype vector of the testing individual i, PRSi,k is the PRS of the testing individual i calculated using the PRS model βk→ fine-tuned in the fine-tuning sample k, and wi,k is the combination coefficient mainly based on the reciprocal of the PC Euclidean distance between the testing individual i and median point of the fine-tuning sample kDi,k. Note that the input PRS and PCs should be properly harmonized: all the individuals are projected onto the same PC space based on a global ancestry reference panel and the PRS input PRSi,k is the raw PRS with the top PCs regressed out and then standardized to mean = 0 and SD = 1. Additionally, we recommend including all available discovery GWAS when generating the input PRS PRSi,k to maximize the PRS performance, as indicated in Figure 1. DiscoDivas is a flexible framework that accommodates sets of input PRS PRSi,k that are derived from different discovery GWASs and are generated using different PRS methods.

In addition to the PC distances, other factors are included in the model. First, since some fine-tuning samples are more correlated than others (e.g., EAS and SAS are more correlated than AFR and EUR), the combination coefficients should be further modified by these correlations, which can also be extracted from the PC Euclidean distances. Second, since PRS fine-tuned in each of the fine-tuning samples may be of differing qualities (e.g., when the PRS model fine-tuned in different samples are based on GWASs of different sample sizes or populations), the quality of the PRSs trained with each of the training data will vary and should be considered when combining the PRSs. Thus, the combination coefficient wi,k in the previous formula is a function of multiple factors: wi,k=f1Di,k,G,rk, where 1Di,k is the reciprocal of PC Euclidean distance between the individual i and the fine-tuning sample k, G is the matrix of PC Euclidean distance between fine-tuning samples, and rk is the parameter describing the quality of training fine-tuning samples. A more detailed description of defining wi,k is given in the methods section entitled methodological details of DiscoDivas.

The PRS input for DiscoDivas was the multi-GWAS PRS fine-tuned in AFR, EAS, EUR, and SAS fine-tuning samples with the conventional method pipeline as mentioned in the following section titled overview of multi-population GWAS PRS model. The interpolation from these four PRSs is based on the PCs calculated using the 1000 Genomes34 reference panel. For most of the PRS analysis conducted in this study, the input PRSs of DiscoDivas are based on the same set of discovery GWASs and the fine-tuning datasets are sufficiently large to generate a stable result. Therefore, we assumed that all the input PRSs can be viewed as of equal quality and their parameter for PRS quality rk can be viewed as a constant value.

Overview of multi-population GWAS PRS model

A common approach for constructing PRSs is to include as much GWAS summary statistic data as possible, usually from multiple populations and sometimes across multiple traits, in the discovery data.5,42,44 The GWAS data are typically then processed by PRS methods that will adjust the SNP effect size using a set of hyper-parameters. Individual-level data from an independent fine-tuning sample is then used to fine-tune the hyper-parameters across PRS methods and the combination of the fine-tuned PRSs. The resulting PRS derived from multiple GWASs is expected to perform the best in samples of matched ancestry with the fine-tuning sample.

The current approach, as shown in the left panels of Figure 1, is to use the multi-GWAS PRS fine-tuned in the matched sample or the closest approximation when the matched sample is unavailable. The pipeline of adjusting SNP effect sizes and combining information from different GWASs varies widely. Without loss of generality, we built the following pipeline as a representation of the current conventional method: we first adjusted the SNP effect size of each of the summary statistical GWAS datasets by a Bayesian method and then chose the most predictive PRS from all the PRSs generated under different hyper-parameters. For simulated GWAS data, we used PRS-CS45 to adjust the SNP effect size and LDpred246 for real GWASs. Then we used the fine-tuning data to both select the most predictive PRS based on each GWAS and train the linear combination of the most predictive single-GWAS PRS with a linear regression model. The final PRS model generated from each of the fine-tuning datasets is a linear combination of PRSs. For the empiric dataset, the PRSs were fine-tuned controlling for the following covariates: top 20 PCs, sex, and age. The individuals from real-life biobanks and simulated individuals based on real-life biobank genotype data were clustered into genetic ancestries including AFR, EAS, EUR, SAS, AMR, and OTH. Depending on data availability, either OTH or AMR was used to evaluate PRS performance in admixed populations. In the conventional PRS generation pipeline, fine-tuning datasets were drawn from the major ancestry groups (for single ancestry, AFR, EAS, EUR, SAS; for admixed ancestry, OTH and/or AMR depending on availability). More detailed description of generating PRS models from the a single fine-tuning dataset are provided in supplemental methods section entitled “methodological details of PRS construction using a single fine-tuning dataset.” Detailed metrics of PRS performance are provided in the supplemental data.

Simulated data results

Summary-level GWASs used as discovery data were generated based on simulated genotypes of AFR, EAS, EUR, and SAS populations based on 1000 Genomes34 reference as previously described.6 Fine-tuning and testing samples were simulated based on UKBB genotype data. From each ancestry group of AFR, EAS, EUR, SAS, and OTH for admixed individuals, 1,300 individuals were used as the fine-tuning datasets (see supplemental methods section entitled “generating data for simulation analysis”). The phenotypes of discovery, fine-tuning, and testing data were generated using the same pipeline and parameters: the phenotypes of 100, 300, 1,000, or 10,000 causal SNPs, a non-genetic factor following a normal distribution across populations and overall phenotypic heritability = 0.6 was simulated. Scenarios of shared causal SNPs with effect sizes constant across different ancestries and shared causal SNPs with effect sizes varying across populations were both simulated. We used up to 100,000 simulated individuals from AFR, EAS, EUR, and SAS to generate the discovery summary statistics GWAS dataset with PLINK243 and used 10,000 of the remaining samples as fine-tuning dataset in other downstream analyses.

We primarily focused on the PRS performance in the OTH testing cohort. DiscoDivas, which is based on PRS fine-tuned in AFR, EAS, EUR, and SAS, was compared with the conventional PRS fine-tuned in the matched admixed fine-tuning sample in scenarios of different causal SNP numbers, different discovery GWAS sample sizes, and different causal SNP distribution across ancestry (see Figure 2).

Although the comparison between DiscoDivas and the conventional method of fine-tuning PRSs with matched ancestry samples in a single test iteration usually showed no statistical significance due to the small numeric differences, a paired t test comparing DiscoDivas R2 and the conventional PRS R2 over the 20 iterations better clarified significant differences. When effect sizes of causal SNPs were held constant across different ancestries (Figures 2A–2C), the PRS generated by DiscoDivas had comparable performance with the PRS fine-tuned using matched data. We noticed that, when the sample size of non-European discovery GWAS dropped and the dataset was more Eurocentric, the advantage of DiscoDivas became less statistically significant (Figures 2B and 2C). In Figure 2D, we compare DiscoDivas and the conventional PRS method of fine-tuning the PRS with matched ancestry in the scenario where causal SNPs were shared across all populations, but the effect sizes varied linearly in the PC space. The advantage of DiscoDivas over the conventional PRS method was more obvious in this scenario than when the effect sizes were constant across populations (Figure 2D versus Figure 2A), presumably because personalized PRS combination with DiscoDivas better captured the changing effect sizes for the admixed testing sample. In all the scenarios tested, the advantage of DiscoDivas was least statistically significant when the number of causal SNPs was 10,000 but still significant when the number of causal SNPs was 1,000. Notably, the performance of both DiscoDivas and the conventional PRS method was the lowest when the number of causal SNPs was 10,000 (Figure S1), indicating that the difference of the two PRS methods became less obvious when the input data became increasingly underpowered.

When predicting the individuals that are usually classified as single ancestries (i.e., AFR, EAS, EUR, and SAS), DiscoDivas showed no statistically significant difference or a slight advantage over the conventional PRS method (Figure S2). When predicting AMR individuals, we used admixed fine-tuning data (OTH) to fine-tune the conventional PRS due to the small sample size of the AMR dataset. The PRS performance when testing in the AMR dataset was similar as in admixed data, but the statistical significance was weaker, potentially due to the small sample size and the high heterogeneity of the AMR dataset. Besides, DiscoDivas showed its clearest advantage over the conventional method of fine-tuning PRS with a single dataset when the testing data and the fine-tuning data for the conventional method were of different ancestries (Figure S2). This result underscores the advantage of DiscoDivas in delivering a harmonized and robustly accurate PRS for samples spanning a wide range of genetic ancestries compared to relying on a single PRS model or switching between multiple discrete PRS models. We evaluated the performance of DiscoDivas when the input PRSs were based on different GWASs. The evaluation is detailed in Notes S3.

Biobank data results

We downloaded publicly available summary statistics of body-mass index (BMI), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), TC, triglycerides (TGs), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), CAD, and type 2 DM (DM2) (see Tables S3 and S4) and adjusted the SNP effect size using LDpred2 as described previously.5

For the quantitative traits, we used the fine-tuning samples of AFR, EAS, EUR, SAS, and admixed (OTH) ancestry in the UKBB to fine-tune the model. The remaining UKBB samples were used as the testing data. The results for empiric quantitative trait data were highly aligned with the simulation results (Figure 3): DiscoDivas showed a robust advantage over the conventional PRS method of fine-tuning PRS with matched or similar ancestry samples when compared across the seven traits in the OTH testing dataset. When predicting AFR, EAS, EUR, and SAS, DiscoDivas had similar or slightly improved performance compared to the conventional PRS method. The results of both methods in AMR testing dataset had large deviations due to the small sample size and greater genetic heterogeneity of the AMR data. Consistent with the simulated data, DiscoDivas showed a greater advantage over PRS models fine-tuned on datasets of different ancestry from the testing samples (Figure S17). This further supports the potential of DiscoDivas to construct a harmonized and robustly accurate PRS for individuals across a wide range of ancestries.

For the binary traits CAD and DM2 (Figure 4), we used the AFR, EAS, EUR, SAS, AMR, and OTH samples from the AoU research program as the fine-tuning data and tested in AFR, EAS, EUR, SAS, and OTH individuals in UKBB and AFR, EAS, EUR, SAS, and AMR individuals in the MGBB. The DiscoDivas PRSs were based on the PRSs fine-tuned in AFR, EAS, EUR, and SAS and used the default assumption that the PRS fine-tuned from all the samples were of similar quality even though the sample sizes of both discovery GWAS and the fine-tuning samples were not balanced across different ancestries. AMR in UKBB was excluded because of the small sample size (N = 669).

The DiscoDivas PRS and the PRS fine-tuned in different single samples had similar performances. It also appeared that some of the fine-tuning samples could be underpowered: generally, we expect the PRS fine-tuned in the matched sample to perform the best in the testing samples, but PRS fine-tuned in larger fine-tuning data performed better than PRS fine-tuned in smaller fine-tuning data. For example, the PRS fine-tuned in EAS AoU data performed worse than other PRSs in both MGBB and UKBB EAS data and had low performance in other testing data. The CAD PRS fine-tuned in EUR performed better than all the other PRSs in all the testing data, and the effective sample size of the EUR CAD fine-tuning data was much larger than all the other fine-tuning data.

Discussion

We propose a new method, DiscoDivas, to interpolate the PRSs for diverse ancestries, especially admixed, with a generalized framework that does not require binning into discrete ancestries. Our results show that the performance of DiscoDivas was comparable to or greater than the conventional method (i.e., fine-tuning using the matched population sample when available). In addition, when generating PRSs for a wide range of ancestries, DiscoDivas did not require shifting from several sets of PRS models fine-tuned in discrete samples while remaining matched with the ancestry information. DiscoDivas is a flexible framework that allows input PRS models to be generated using other advanced methods, further enhancing the final PRS performance across diverse genetic ancestries. Our method provides a new solution to generate PRSs for underrepresented, generally admixed, populations and to generate a harmonized PRS model across different ancestries.

The performance of our method depends on the quality of both the discovery GWAS data and the fine-tuning data. As shown in the simulation test, discovery GWAS datasets that represent diverse ancestries with sufficient sample size will increase the performance of interpolated PRSs generated by DiscoDivas. On the contrary, Eurocentric and underpowered discovery GWAS datasets limit the advantage of DiscoDivas over the conventional PRS method. This might partly explain the limited advantage of DiscoDivas when predicting binary traits: the discovery GWAS datasets were highly Eurocentric, while the GWASs, especially the non-European cohorts, might be more underpowered than quantitative trait GWASs. Furthermore, the PRSs fine-tuned in fine-tuning datasets of insufficient sample size will be overfitted and cannot be used to fairly evaluate the performance of either the conventional PRS method or DiscoDivas. We aimed to address this issue by only using traits that (1) had effective sample sizes larger than 200 in all the fine-tuning samples, and (2) had high-quality phenotyping data in both the fine-tuning datasets and the testing datasets, However, Asian populations were largely underrepresented in the current public biobanks: the effective sample size of many binary traits in EAS or SAS can be as small as <200 even in AoU, the most diverse and large-scale largely publicly available biobank. This limited binary traits selected to only CAD and DM2.

The difficulty of gathering data for our research underscores the notion that non-European populations, both admixed and single-ancestry populations, remain largely underrepresented in the existing genetic data. Furthermore, potential extensions of our method are not possible until more diverse and larger datasets are collected. First, our method has not been designed or tested for extrapolating data (e.g., generating PRS for continental African samples based on African American, European, and Asian samples). Even though it is mathematically plausible to alter our method to extrapolate the PRS, we lack data, such as continental African samples, to test the method. Secondly, we currently only consider the assumption that the most informative genome-wide PRS model shifts linearly in the PC space and only consider interpolating genome-wide PRS models instead of SNP- or gene-specific interpolation. Although more complicated PRS interpolation (e.g., interpolation guided by local ancestry information,27,44,47 pathway-specific48,49 and annotation-guided2 PRS models, and polynomial interpolation50,51) can possibly further improve the PRS performance, training such complicated models would require collecting much larger and more diverse datasets as well as additional relevant metadata. Finally, additional biological insights could be revealed by interpolating PRS if genetic data from all diverse ancestries are of sufficient power. In this case, the differences between interpolated PRS and the PRS trained using the matched ancestry would indicate the population- or sample-specific factors absent in the interpolation model (e.g., population-specific genetic variance,52 complicated population stratification involving cofounding factors,53,54 sample/ancestry-specific modifiers such as local adaptation,53 gene × environment interactions,55 or other factors that contribute to the genetic variant frequency or effect size in these samples/ancestries).

DiscoDivas does not consider local ancestry information, which improve PRS predictions in various research,27,44,47 especially PRS prediction of newly admixed populations.26 This may result in suboptimal performance in newly admixed populations. However, this design choice makes our method highly applicable and robust in settings where the input PRS model is fine-tuned using cohorts whose genetic ancestries deviate from those in local ancestry-inference reference panels.

In conclusion, DiscoDivas provides a new option to treat ancestry information as a continuous variable and interpolate a harmonized PRS for diverse ancestries. Notably, although our method was developed primarily to calculate PRS when the matched fine-tuning datasets were unavailable, our research showed that successfully interpolating PRS required sufficient input data and highlighted the need to collect genetic data for underrepresented populations. We believe that more diverse and larger data collected will enable the development of new methods of interpolating PRS and elucidate the genetic basis of complex traits.
