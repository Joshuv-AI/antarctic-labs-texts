Clinical Development, Regulatory, and Reimbursement Review Duration of New Drugs in the US and European Countries
Authors: Simon Hediger, Camille E.G. Glaus, Stacie B. Dusetzina, Ulrich-Peter Rohr, Thomas J. Hwang, Kerstin Noelle Vokinger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionWhat was the duration of clinical development, regulatory review, and reimbursement review of new drugs over time in the US, France, Germany, and Switzerland?FindingsIn this cross-sectional study of 519 drugs in the US, 412 in France, 412 in Germany, and 395 in Switzerland, median duration from clinical trial initiation until reimbursement remained stable over time (2014–2024) across all countries, with overall shorter duration for cancer drugs.MeaningDespite pressure and policy changes to speed access to new drugs, results of this study suggest that median total duration remained similar over time; drivers should be analyzed to develop targeted policies to accelerate access while also enabling safety.

Introduction

Rapid availability of new drugs targeting unmet medical needs is of high interest to patients and society.1 The following 3 key periods play an important role: clinical development (time from human testing until regulatory submission), regulatory review (time from regulatory submission until regulatory approval), and reimbursement review (time from regulatory approval until reimbursement).1,2,3,4

While regulatory approval enables market entry and availability of new drugs, insurance reimbursement is a key determinant of patient access.2 Reimbursement systems vary across countries. In the US, Medicare is required to cover all new drugs in protected classes (antineoplastic agents, immunosuppressants for prophylaxis of organ transplant rejection, antidepressants, antipsychotics, anticonvulsants, antiretrovirals) but has substantial flexibility in how to design formularies for drugs outside these protected classes.2,5 Drugs are covered if they are reasonable and necessary for treatment.6,7 European countries, such as France, Germany, and Switzerland, incorporate a health technology assessment process to evaluate the added clinical benefit of a new drug compared with existing therapies.2,8 These outcomes serve as a basis for reimbursement decisions and price negotiations.

Regulatory agencies and reimbursement bodies face pressure to approve and reimburse drugs more quickly.4,6,9,10,11,12,13,14,15 Recently, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) announced that a one-trial requirement (instead of two) will be the FDA’s new default standard, with the objective of further speeding drugs to market.16 To understand times to access of new drugs to patients, we analyzed the duration for the 3 key periods (clinical development, regulatory review, and reimbursement review) and assessed whether duration of these periods changed over the past decade in the US and European countries (Germany, France, Switzerland).

Methods

Data

This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cohort studies. The study was exempt from institutional review board approval and the requirement for informed consent by the Vanderbilt University Medical Center Institutional Review Board because it used nonidentifiable data and did not constitute human participants research. We used publicly available databases for the US (FDA), France and Germany (the European Medicines Agency [EMA]), as well as publicly and nonpublicly available databases for the Swiss Agency for Therapeutic Products (Swissmedic) to identify all new drugs (ie, new molecular entities in the US, new active substances in the EU and Switzerland) first approved between January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2024.17,18,19 We excluded diagnostics, vaccines, generic and biosimilar products, and drugs used to treat or prevent COVID-19. If multiple indications were approved as first indications, all were included in our study cohort. For simplicity, we used the term drugs instead of drug indications.

We extracted the following information from the databases of the FDA, EMA, and Swissmedic: drug name, active substance, investigational new drug application date (representing the start of human clinical testing), regulatory submission date, approval pathway, and regulatory approval date. If the investigational new drug application date was not available, we extracted the trial start date from ClinicalTrials.gov or the European Union Drug Regulating Authorities Clinical Trials (EudraCT) database. We excluded drugs for which the trial start date could not be extracted. For drugs with more than one first indication, we extracted for each indication separately the trial start date, submission date, approval date, and approval pathway. For drugs with multiple pivotal clinical trials, we used the earliest trial start date. Drugs were categorized into therapeutic areas based on the World Health Organization Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical Classification system.20 For the US, the following approval pathways were categorized as expedited programs: accelerated approval, breakthrough, fast track, and priority review. In France and Germany, the approval pathways conditional marketing authorization, accelerated assessment, priority medicines, and exceptional circumstances, and in Switzerland fast track authorization and temporary authorization procedures were qualified as expedited pathways. Throughout, we refer to fast track (Switzerland) and accelerated assessment (Europe) as priority review and temporary authorization (Switzerland) and conditional marketing authorization (Europe) as accelerated approval.

Reimbursement dates were extracted from Medicare for the US,21 the Federal Joint Committee for Germany,22 Ministry of Health and Prevention for France,23 and the Federal Office of Public Health for Switzerland.24 For the US, a drug was considered reimbursed when it was either covered by a Medicare Part D plan formulary (measured on quarterly basis for self-administered drugs, regardless of whether it was dispensed to a patient) or the first time a unique billing code was listed in the Part B average sales price file (published quarterly for clinician-administered drugs). For France, we extracted the reimbursement dates following publication of the health technology assessment decisions. For Germany, we assessed the time from EMA approval to the Federal Joint Committee decision, and for Switzerland the time from Swissmedic approval to decision by the Federal Office of Public Health. For drugs with multiple indications, reimbursement dates were extracted for each indication for Germany, France and Switzerland. For the US, the reimbursement date of the first indication was extracted.

Statistical Analysis

For each included country, the duration for each key period was calculated for each included drug. For each country and each key period, we conducted individual Kaplan-Meier analyses to calculate the median duration in years for clinical development, regulatory review, and reimbursement review. We conducted separate Kaplan-Meier analyses to calculate the median total duration from trial initiation to reimbursement. We conducted these analyses for all drugs; in addition, we conducted analyses restricted to cancer drugs.

To assess temporal trends, we defined 3 time periods of equal length and repeated our analyses. The first time period included drug approvals from January 1, 2014, until August 31, 2017, the second period included drugs approved from September 1, 2017, until April 30, 2021, and the third period included drug approvals between May 1, 2021, and December 31, 2024. In addition, we categorized drugs into 2 approval type groups: standard approval and approval involving at least one expedited pathway for analyses. Finally, sensitivity analyses were conducted for a restricted cohort of drugs that were approved in all 4 countries (US, France, Germany, Switzerland), referred to as commonly approved drugs, and separately biologics and small molecule drugs. All statistical analyses were conducted using R, version 4.5.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing).

Results

Overview

Our study cohort included 519 drugs (483 active substances) in the US, 412 drugs (377 active substances) in France, and 412 drugs (377 active substances) in Germany, and 395 drugs (362 active substances) in Switzerland. Most of the drugs were approved for treatment of cancers (ie, 157 cancer drugs [142 active substances] in the US, 137 [121 active substances] in France and Germany, and 130 drugs [116 active substances] in Switzerland).

Of drugs included, 99 of 519 (19%) were granted accelerated approval in the US, 68 of 412 (17%) in France and Germany, and 39 of 192 (20%) in Switzerland (because the temporary authorization was introduced in Switzerland in 2019, only those drugs approved between 2019 and 2024 were included for this analysis). Of these, 78 were approved for cancers in the US, 46 in France and Germany, and 30 in Switzerland. Further, 361 of 519 (70%) of drugs were granted priority review in the US, 84 of 412 (20%) in France and Germany, and 65 of 395 (16%) in Switzerland. Of these, 140 were approved for cancers in the US, 35 in France and Germany, and 32 in Switzerland.

A total of 404 of 519 (78%) drugs were reimbursed in the US, 261 of 412 (63%) in France, 368 of 412 (89%) in Germany, and 300 of 395 (76%) in Switzerland. 132 of 157 were for cancer treatments in the US, 85 of 137 in France, 128 of 137 in Germany, and 105 of 130 in Switzerland.

Total Duration and Duration Differences Between Clinical Development, Regulatory Review, and Reimbursement Review

The median total duration from clinical trial initiation until reimbursement was 9.3 (95% CI, 9.0-9.8) years in the US, 11.1 (95% CI, 10.2-12.0) years in France, 8.9 (95% CI, 8.3-9.5) years in Germany, and 9.8 (95% CI, 9.6-10.5) years in Switzerland (Figure 1 and Table 1).

Duration for Clinical Development, Regulatory Review, and Reimbursement Review Over Time

Abbreviation: NA, not applicable.

In all 4 countries, median clinical development time accounted for the largest proportion of the total duration from clinical trial initiation until reimbursement, with 6.7 (95% CI, 6.4-7.2) years in the US, 6.0 (95% CI, (5.5-6.7) years in France and Germany, and 6.8 (95% CI, 6.5-7.4) years in Switzerland.

In the US, median duration for regulatory review was 0.7 (95% CI, 0.7-0.9) years and for reimbursement review was 0.8 (95% CI, 0.8-0.9) years. In France, median duration for regulatory review was 1.2 (95% CI, 1.1-1.2) years and for reimbursement review was 2.2 (95% CI, 2.0-2.5) years. In Germany, median duration for regulatory review was 1.2 (95% CI, 1.1-1.2) years and for reimbursement review was 0.6 (95% CI, 0.6-0.7) years. In Switzerland, the median duration for regulatory review was 1.3 (95% CI, 1.2-1.3) years and for reimbursement was 0.7 (95% CI, 0.6-1.0) years (Figure 1 and Table 1).

When limiting to commonly approved drugs in all 4 countries (US, France, Germany, and Switzerland), the findings were similar (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). The findings were also similar when differentiating between biologics and small molecule drugs (eFigure 2, eFigure 3 in Supplement 1).

Changes Over Time in Total Duration and Duration of Clinical Development, Regulatory Review, and Reimbursement Review

In all 4 countries, except for Germany, the median total duration increased slightly between 2014 and 2024. The median total duration from clinical trial initiation until reimbursement increased in the US from 9.0 (95% CI, 8.43-9.90) years in the first period (January 2014-August 2017) to 9.35 (95% CI, 8.75-11.56) years in the most recent period (May 2021-December 2024). This was mainly attributable to the clinical development (plus 0.19 years) and reimbursement review (0.09 years) periods and to a lesser extent for the regulatory review (plus 0.02 years) (Figure 2, Table 1, Table 2).

In France, the median total duration increased from 9.82 (95% CI, 8.88-11.41) years to 12.18 (95% CI, 10.12-14.78) years and in Switzerland from 9.42 (95% CI, 8.2-10.17) years to 10.05 (95% CI, 9.39-12.32) years. The reimbursement time increased for both (plus 0.35 years in France and plus 0.79 years in Switzerland). In Germany, the median total duration decreased from 9.27 (95% CI, 7.67-10.6) years to 8.7 (95% CI, 7.92-9.57) years (Figure 2, Table 1, Table 2).

Changes Over Time for Drugs Granted Standard vs Expedited Approval

In all 4 countries, the median total duration was shorter for drugs granted at least one expedited approval pathway vs those granted standard approval (Figure 1 and Table 1). However, this gap decreased over time. In the US, in the most recent period, drugs following standard approval have even a slightly lower median total duration compared with drugs following at least one expedited approval pathway. More specifically, median total duration was 9.17 (95% CI, 8.34-10.62) years for drugs approved through the standard approval pathway between 2014 and 2017 vs 8.93 (95% CI, 7.85-10.23) years for drugs approved via at least one expedited approval pathway, corresponding to a difference of 0.24 years. For drugs approved through the standard approval pathway between 2021 and 2024, median total duration was 9.22 (95% CI, 8.7-14.58) years vs 9.35 (95% CI, 8.72-11.59) years for drugs approved via at least one expedited approval pathway, corresponding to a difference of −0.13 years (Figure 2, Table 1, Table 2).

Similar temporal patterns were observed for commonly approved drugs in all 4 countries (eFigure 4, eTable 1, eTable 2 in Supplement 1). Also when differentiating between biologics and small molecule drugs, similar patterns in both groups were observed (eFigure 5, eFigure 6, eTable 3, eTable 4, eTable 5, eTable 6 in Supplement 1).

Total Duration for Cancer Drugs

When focusing on cancer drugs, the median total duration from clinical trial initiation until reimbursement was 7.9 (95% CI, 7.4-8.7) years in the US, 10.8 (95% CI, 9.2-12.3) years in France, 7.5 (95% CI, 6.9-8.7) years in Germany, and 9.3 (95% CI, 8.3-9.9) years in Switzerland (Figure 3). In all 4 countries, median clinical development time accounted for the largest proportion of the total duration from clinical trial initiation until reimbursement (Figure 3). In all 4 countries, the median total duration was shorter for drugs granted at least one expedited approval pathway vs those granted standard approval (Figure 3).

Discussion

Median total duration from clinical trial initiation until reimbursement for new drugs was 9.3 years in the US, 11.1 years in France, 8.9 years in Germany, and 9.8 years in Switzerland. The smallest proportion in duration was attributable to regulatory review in the US, and reimbursement review in Germany and Switzerland. The median total duration showed only minimal variation over time. In the US, drugs approved through at least one expedited pathway had a similar median total duration compared with standard approved drugs. In France, Germany, and Switzerland, drugs approved through at least one expedited pathway had shorter total duration compared with drugs approved through standard approval pathway. This time difference decreased over the past decade, with expedited approvals showing an increase in duration. Overall, cancer drugs showed a shorter median total duration compared with all drugs.

The findings indicate that despite pressure and policy changes to speed access of patients to new drugs, the overall median duration from clinical testing to reimbursement remained similar over time. Various reasons could explain the median duration increase in clinical development duration over the past decade in the US, France, Germany, and Switzerland. Outsourcing of clinical development and the increasing conduct of trials in emerging markets are possible drivers for longer clinical development durations.1,25 For example, a prior study found that median time to completion was 9 months longer for international trials compared with national trials.26 Increases in international trials could therefore also contribute to longer clinical development times. By contrast, the increase in number of approved biologics over the past years did not explain the findings.27,28 Our analysis showed that overall median duration was similar for biologics and small molecule drugs. It would be beneficial to analyze the reasons why access to new drugs has not become faster despite pressure to accelerate access.4,6,9,10,11,12,13 This would help to understand which targeted policies that enable to accelerate access to new drugs would be helpful while adequately balancing safety with speed.

Cancer drugs showed a shorter median total duration compared with all drugs, especially in the US and Germany while less pronounced in Switzerland and France. Previous studies showed that accelerated approval is most common in oncology, with more than 80% of all accelerated approvals being granted for cancer drugs.29,30,31 However, the study findings showed that when comparing cancer medicines with all drugs, duration was not only faster for cancer drugs granted expedited approval, but also for cancer drugs approved through the standard approval pathway. Indeed, even before the FDA’s announcement in February 2026 that a one-trial requirement (instead of two) will be the FDA’s new default standard, the FDA has historically granted oncology approvals based on a single premarket study.16 This could be one possible driver for more rapid access of cancer drugs compared with all drugs. However, rapid approval of products with limited evidence can increase risks to patients and, in some cases, result in their withdrawal after they have entered the market.32

The median reimbursement review duration increased over the past decade in Switzerland. However, it is notable that in the first time period (January 2014–August 2017) the median reimbursement review duration was shortest in Switzerland in comparison with the other included countries with a median duration of approximately 3 months. A possible reason for time increase in Switzerland could be the increased application of rebates and managed entry agreements, especially for high-priced drugs and drugs for which the evidence was based on surrogate end points, which may provide only limited evidence required for the assessment of therapeutic value.33,34 Despite increased median reimbursement review duration in Switzerland over the past decade, the reimbursement review duration in the most recent period (May 2021–December 2024) was comparable with that in the US and was shorter compared with France, and reimbursement review times were overall shorter than clinical development and regulatory review times.

Limitations

Our study has limitations. Dates for self-administered drugs were only available quarterly and on drug level (in contrast with indication level) in the US, which may overestimate the total duration and duration of reimbursement review, specifically. For drugs with multiple first indications, the reimbursement date of the first indication was included for the US, which may underestimate the duration of reimbursement review for drugs with more than one first indication. We did not consider postapproval withdrawal decisions by the FDA, EMA, or Swissmedic, and also did not consider reversion of priority review status by the EMA. Furthermore, we only included approved drugs.35 The study findings show that median duration of clinical development was shorter in France and Germany (but not Switzerland) compared with the US despite prior studies showing that drug applications were often first submitted to the FDA and later to the EMA and Swissmedic (submission gap).4,36 In the primary analysis, we did not limit our study cohort to commonly approved drugs in all 3 jurisdictions but rather included for each jurisdiction those drugs that were approved between 2014 and 2024: the drug cohorts for the US, EU (France and Germany), and Switzerland were different. Because we defined clinical development as the time from human clinical testing until regulatory submission, the clinical development time in European countries includes the submission gap between US and European regulatory agencies for those drug applications submitted for approval by FDA before other regulators. Finally, reimbursement in the US reflects formulary coverage of a drug by any Medicare Part D plan and may not reflect insurance coverage in the commercial or Medicaid markets.

Conclusions

The findings of this study suggest that the median duration from clinical development until reimbursement of new drugs showed only minimal variation over time. Moving forward, it would be beneficial to analyze factors contributing to overall median duration for new drugs, particularly in light of policy changes that have aimed to speed access to new drugs for patients. This would help to understand which policies would enable to accelerate access to new drugs, ideally adequately balancing safety with speed.
