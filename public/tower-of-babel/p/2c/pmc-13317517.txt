Dynamic bilateral vertebral artery occlusion owing to os odontoideum mimicking basilar artery occlusion: illustrative case

Acute vertebrobasilar occlusion is typically caused by a thromboembolic mechanism and, when accompanied by severe neurological deficits, is managed with emergency endovascular therapy (EVT) for basilar artery occlusion (BAO).1 Patients with hyperacute BAO require emergency EVT under the presumption of a thromboembolic mechanism. However, dynamic mechanical compromise of the vertebral artery (VA) owing to upper cervical instability represents a rare alternative etiology that closely mimics thromboembolic occlusion.

Os odontoideum2,3—defined as separation of the odontoid process from the axis—can cause atlantoaxial instability and has been implicated as a structural cause of bow hunter syndrome (BHS).4–14 BHS is characterized by motion-induced VA compression resulting in posterior circulation ischemia.4,5

We report a case of acute bilateral VA occlusion initially managed as presumed thromboembolic BAO in which intraoperative angiographic findings, particularly persistent to-and-fro flow, prompted reconsideration of the mechanism. Subsequent CT angiography (CTA) with bone-window reconstruction identified os odontoideum with atlantoaxial instability as the underlying cause, leading to definitive occipitocervical fusion for mechanical stabilization.

Illustrative Case

A 73-year-old man experienced three infarctions in the posterior circulation at another institution during a 5-month period (Fig. 1A–E). He had residual left homonymous hemianopsia but maintained functional independence. He was then referred to our hospital for etiological evaluation. Prolonged cardiac monitoring, transesophageal echocardiography, and thrombophilia testing revealed no embolic source. Because each prior event had occurred during cervical rotation (e.g., golfing or turning while driving), BHS was suspected. However, carotid and VA ultrasonography during neck rotation demonstrated no significant flow reduction (Fig. 2A–F). The events were classified as embolic stroke of undetermined source, and aspirin therapy was initiated.

D and E: MR angiograms demonstrating no stenotic lesions in the bilateral vertebral or subclavian arteries.

Spectral Doppler ultrasonograms obtained during head rotation, demonstrating preserved bilateral VA flow without significant positional changes in velocity. A–C: Right VA. D–F: Left VA. Each artery is evaluated during right rotation, in the neutral position, and during left rotation.

Three days after discharge, he collapsed while golfing and struck his forehead. He was initially responsive but became apneic before emergency medical services arrival and required bag-valve-mask ventilation during transport. On arrival, he was apneic and comatose (Glasgow Coma Scale score E1V1M1). His pupils were 2.5 mm bilaterally, and he exhibited severe quadriparesis (manual muscle testing grade 1/5) with bilateral Babinski signs. The National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score was 40. Arterial blood gas analysis showed severe hypercapnia (partial pressure of carbon dioxide 89.5 mm Hg) and acidemia (pH 7.080), requiring intratracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation. Head CT showed no traumatic hemorrhage. CTA demonstrated bilateral VA occlusion at the vertebrobasilar junction and bilateral superior cerebellar artery occlusion (Fig. 3A), and emergency EVT was initiated for presumed BAO.

D:Fluoroscopic image showing full expansion of the stent retriever across the presumed occlusive segment (outlined for clarity). E:Right vertebral angiogram demonstrating severe stenosis distal to the C2 transverse foramen (arrow). F:Right internal carotid angiogram showing collateral flow to the posterior circulation via the posterior communicating artery.

Digital subtraction angiography revealed left VA occlusion at C1–2 (Fig. 3B). After lesion crossing, stagnant contrast at the occlusion site exhibited to-and-fro flow extending to the left posterior inferior cerebellar artery (Video 1, Fig. 3C). The stent retriever achieved full expansion (Fig. 3D) with preserved aspiration backflow, and no thrombus was retrieved. Additional passes targeting the basilar artery top also failed to yield any thrombus, and the to-and-fro flow persisted. Right VA angiography demonstrated severe stenosis distal to the C2 transverse foramen (Fig. 3E), whereas right internal carotid angiography showed collateral flow via the posterior communicating artery without intracranial large-vessel occlusion (Fig. 3F).

Persistent to-and-fro flow during EVT and the absence of a retrievable thrombus prompted reconsideration of the underlying mechanism. Reevaluation of the bone-window reconstructed CT angiograms revealed os odontoideum with a displaced odontoid-like ossicle deviated toward the clivus (Fig. 4A). Given the intraoperative neck extension, dynamic bilateral VA compression was suspected and a cervical collar was applied to limit further extension.

B:Postoperative image revealing bilateral cerebellar infarctions in the superior cerebellar artery territories. C: Both VAs and the basilar artery are well visualized without evidence of occlusion. D: Sagittal cervical MR image demonstrating atlantoaxial instability with spinal cord injury at the C1–2 level (arrow). E: CT angiogram obtained during neck extension at the time of acute neurological deterioration, demonstrating bilateral VA occlusion, with no visualization of both VAs below the C1 level. F–H:Postoperative CT angiograms obtained after posterior occipitocervical fixation, demonstrating good visualization and patency of the bilateral VAs. Compared with panel F, panel E shows loss of VA visualization caudal to C1.

Follow-up imaging demonstrated cerebellar infarctions within the bilateral superior cerebellar artery territories (Fig. 4B), while both VAs and the basilar artery were patent (Fig. 4C). Cervical MRI showed C1–2 spinal cord injury (Fig. 4D). Although his level of consciousness improved, respiratory failure and quadriparesis persisted. He was able to track visually and communicate by eye opening and closing; however, he remained quadriparetic. Owing to a markedly weak cough reflex and insufficient spontaneous respiration, he required tracheostomy and mechanical ventilation and remained ventilator dependent at discharge.

Retrospective review of the initial CT angiograms demonstrated dynamic bilateral VA occlusion under neck extension, with restoration of flow after restriction of extension (Fig. 5). It also demonstrated loss of bilateral VA opacification below the C1 level (Fig. 4E), supporting extension-dependent vascular compromise. To stabilize the craniocervical junction, he subsequently underwent occipitocervical fusion, after which the VAs remained patent (Fig. 4F and G).

At presentation, axial (A) and coronal (B) views reveal an os odontoideum, and neither VA is visualized at the V4 segment. Sagittal views (C and D) show an absence of contrast enhancement in the bilateral VAs below the C1 level. After restriction of neck extension, axial (E), coronal (F), and sagittal (G and H) views demonstrate improved visualization and restoration of flow in the bilateral VAs.

Discussion

Observations

This case highlights atlantoaxial instability owing to os odontoideum as a rare but critical mechanical mimic of BAO that precipitated acute bilateral VA occlusion and life-threatening ischemia in the posterior circulation. Acute BAO is most commonly thromboembolic in origin, and severe deficits in the hyperacute setting appropriately prompt emergency EVT under this assumption. However, as illustrated in the present case, not all vertebrobasilar occlusions encountered during EVT are thromboembolic.

Os odontoideum is a well-recognized cause of atlantoaxial instability that may produce a broad clinical spectrum of symptoms ranging from asymptomatic disease to myelopathy and vertebrobasilar insufficiency.2,3 Although VA occlusion related to os odontoideum has been reported,7,9,13,15,16 to our knowledge, there are no prior reports of acute bilateral VA occlusion mimicking BAO and prompting emergency EVT.

A pivotal diagnostic clue in the present case was the persistent to-and-fro flow observed during EVT. Angiographic mimics of large-vessel occlusion have been increasingly recognized in the endovascular era; nonthrombotic mechanisms, such as intracranial atherosclerosis or arterial dissection, may closely resemble embolic occlusion yet require distinct management strategies.17 Although the underlying mechanism in the present case was dynamic mechanical compression rather than intrinsic arterial pathology, it similarly illustrates that not all angiographic occlusions encountered during EVT are thromboembolic.

Despite the prior events being associated with neck rotation, ultrasonography during rotation alone failed to demonstrate significant flow reduction. VA compression related to upper cervical instability may not be elicited by rotation alone; therefore, multidirectional and posture-dependent assessment improves the diagnostic sensitivity.18 Ultrasonographic evaluation under neck extension might have revealed positional flow compromise in the present patient. A retrospective review of the initial CT angiograms obtained under neck extension demonstrated loss of bilateral VA opacification below the C1 level, supporting the presence of extension-dependent vascular compromise. Cervical extension during airway management might have exacerbated the occlusion.

The clinical deterioration was likely multifactorial. In addition to posterior circulation ischemia, concomitant cervical spinal cord injury at C1–2 likely contributed to hypoventilation, resulting in severe hypercapnia and neurological deterioration. When the degree of respiratory failure or neurological impairment appears disproportionate to imaging-confirmed brainstem or cerebellar ischemia, concomitant cervical cord injury should be considered. It is therefore essential to carefully reassess the extracranial VAs and their positional relationship to cervical osseous structures in cases of vertebrobasilar occlusion, particularly when a clear embolic source is not found.

In the present case, temporary cervical immobilization provided immediate stabilization, but definitive fixation was required. Occipitocervical fusion was selected over C1–2 fusion given the severe instability with concomitant C1–2 spinal cord injury and dynamic bilateral VA occlusion.2,3,19 Neutral cervical alignment was maintained intraoperatively to preserve VA patency. This approach resulted in durable patency and prevention of further ischemic events, highlighting the importance of early recognition of mechanical instability and timely surgical management.

Lessons

Os odontoideum may present not only as recurrent posterior circulation ischemia but also as acute bilateral VA occlusion. Therefore, vertebrobasilar occlusion should not automatically be presumed to be thromboembolic. Persistent to-and-fro flow during EVT should raise the suspicion of a dynamic, nonthrombotic mechanism. When neurological deterioration is disproportionate to the extent of ischemic findings, particularly in the presence of unexplained hypoventilation, concomitant cervical cord injury must be considered. Early recognition of cervical instability and timely definitive fixation are crucial for durable management.
