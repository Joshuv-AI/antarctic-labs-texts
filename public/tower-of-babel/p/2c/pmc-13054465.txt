Precision Local Strain Engineering in 2D Semiconductors and Their van der Waals Heterostructures
Authors: Byeong Chan Kim, Yoona Kim, Gwan‐Hyoung Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTwo‐dimensional (2D) semiconductors and their van der Waals (vdW) heterostructures are readily tuned by strain, an approach known as straintronics, owing to their atomically thin, vdW‐bonded layered structures. Small external or environmental perturbations induce strain in adjacent 2D semiconductors, yet sustaining large elastic deformations without fracture. This makes strain a uniquely powerful lever for reconfiguring lattice structure and deterministically tuning band structure, carrier transport, and excitonic responses. In this review, we survey experimental methodologies for quantifying and mapping local strain in 2D semiconductors, and summarize state‐of‐the‐art strategies for imparting strain, including substrate‐mediated deformation, patterned stressors, and interlayer‐interaction‐driven strain in stacked vdW heterostructures. Building on these foundations, we discuss how advances in precise local strain control are informing new device concepts and outline pathways for integrating strain programmability into scalable architectures. We conclude by identifying key challenges and opportunities toward robust, wafer‐compatible deployment of local strain engineering in 2D semiconductor technologies.Keywords: 2D semiconductors, local strain, strain characterization, straintronics, van der Waals heterostructures

Introduction

Two‐dimensional (2D) semiconductors have gained significant attention for their atomically thin structure, high surface‐to‐volume ratio, and tightly coupled electrical, optical, and mechanical responses [1, 2]. Since the isolation of graphene, an expanding family of 2D semiconductor materials, most notably transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs), has been systematically investigated, unveiling layer‐number‐dependent electronic, optical, and mechanical properties that are qualitatively distinct from their bulk counterparts. These materials offer exceptional potential for next‐generation electronic, optoelectronic, and quantum devices, largely due to their band structure tunability [3], mechanical flexibility [4], and compatibility with heterogeneous [5] and wafer‐scale integration [6].

Despite a‐few‐atom‐thickness, they can sustain substantial mechanical deformations, often exceeding a large strain of >10% without undergoing fracture [7, 8, 9]. This exceptional strain tolerance at the atomic scale enables strain engineering to serve as a deterministic knob to modulate the intrinsic properties of 2D semiconductors [10]. While global strain engineering approaches, such as substrate or membrane bending, have been widely used to modify material properties, their device‐level applicability is limited by poor spatial selectivity [11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. Uniform strain cannot readily program complex strain gradients or locally reconfigurable functions. In contrast, local (or site‐specific) strain engineering, where mechanical deformation is applied to targeted nanoscale regions, offers precise control over the strain distribution, enabling local functionalities, such as exciton funnels and pseudo‐gauge fields, that are unattainable with uniform strain fields [16, 17, 18]. In this context, precision signifies the capacity to deterministically induce strain at targeted coordinates with nanoscale accuracy, effectively transforming mechanical deformation into a programmable, layout‐defined design parameter.

Local strain provides site‐selective tunability of material responses and enables engineered strain gradients [19], strain‐induced heterojunctions [20], and nanoscale confinement [21] that can be exploited in diverse device architectures. For example, in electronic devices, spatially localized strain modulates carrier concentration and mobility [22], generating pseudo‐electric fields [23] and confining potentials that realize quantum dots [24]. In optoelectronic systems, strain tunes the bandgap and excitonic landscape, shifting excitonic resonances and enhancing photoluminescence [25, 26]. Moreover, the surrounding unstrained regions act as functional contrasts, enabling design principles that exploit spatially programmed, strain‐induced property variations.

In this review, we focus on recent advances in precise local strain engineering of 2D semiconductors, highlighting quantitative techniques for characterizing and mapping local strain, fabrication strategies for imparting spatially programmable strain, and representative device concepts and system‐level applications (Figure 1). By detailing how precisely controlled strain is measured, implemented, and functionally leveraged, we outline the design space and emerging opportunities for strain‐programmable functionalities in 2D semiconductors and their vdW heterostructures.

Raman Spectroscopy

Raman spectroscopy offers a simple, non‐destructive, and sensitive probe to confirm and quantify strain in 2D materials. When strain is applied, the lattice parameters change, which perturbs phonon dispersions and shifts the vibrational frequencies (phonon modes) of the crystal lattice [38]. Monitoring the strain‐induced Raman peak shifts and splitting of characteristic Raman modes enables quantitative assessment of strain [39]. At small strain, the frequency shift is often approximately linear and can be described using mode‐dependent Grüneisen parameters, allowing the slope (dω/dε) to serve as a practical gauge factor for estimating strain [40]. In addition, the strain symmetry, biaxial or uniaxial, can be inferred from spectral features, such as peak shifts and degeneracy lifting. For example, in MoS2, biaxial tensile strain induces a redshift of the in‐plane E’ phonon mode while preserving its degeneracy (Figure 2a) [41]. In contrast, uniaxial strain lifts the degeneracy and splits E’ into E− and E+ (Figure 2b) [42]. The magnitude of the phonon‐mode shift scales linearly with strain amplitude, allowing estimation of the strain percentage (Figure 2c,d). However, since Raman peak positions are also susceptible to doping, more accurate strain quantification is achieved by deconvolving strain‐ and doping‐induced shifts (Figure 2e) [27, 28, 29]. Spatially, micro‐Raman mapping via point‐by‐point scanning visualizes the distribution of local strain, while tip‐enhanced/nano‐Raman techniques further improve the spatial resolution (Figure 2f) [42, 43]. Furthermore, polarization‐resolved Raman spectroscopy captures the strain‐induced anisotropy via intensity variations between shifted peaks, revealing the strain orientation (Figure 2g–i).

Photoluminescence

Photoluminescence (PL) provides a non‐destructive optical probe of band‐structure modifications in 2D semiconductors, where neutral excitons and defect‐bound emission collectively report changes in the optical bandgap and recombination pathways under strain. In strained 2D semiconductors, PL peak energies track not only band‐edge shifts but also changes in exciton binding energy via strain‐modified screening and effective masses. Therefore, PL gauge factors should be interpreted as an excitonic response rather than a pure bandgap change [44]. A major microscopic origin of this excitonic response is the strain‐induced reordering of band‐edge valleys, which in many TMDs is commonly discussed in terms of the K/Γ/Q points. Because mechanical strain perturbs the relative energies at these valleys, PL readily captures strain through peak‐energy shifts, intensity modulations, and linewidth broadening, enabling both qualitative and quantitative estimation of strain via excitonic gauge factors while remaining compatible with micro‐PL mapping for spatial visualization [26, 45]. Furthermore, tip‐enhanced photoluminescence can push the spatial resolution down to the nanoscale by leveraging plasmonic near‐field enhancement, allowing for the precise mapping of local strain variations that are otherwise obscured in far‐field measurements [46]. In TMDs, monolayers typically exhibit strong direct K–K emission, whereas few‐layer films acquire an indirect character and weaker PL [1]. Under tensile loading, 1L‐MoS2 exhibits a redshift of the A‐exciton as the bandgap narrows, and the emission undergoes a direct‐to‐indirect transition that reduces radiative recombination (Figure 2j) [47]. Consequently, PL quenching under tensile strain can serve as a practical indicator of a strain‐driven crossover in the band extrema, which is highly relevant for strain‐tunable photodetectors and excitonic devices that rely on maintaining a bright direct‐gap transition. Consistently, a calibration plot of A‐exciton energy versus tensile strain in MoS2 shows an approximately linear redshift, allowing direct conversion of measured peak positions into local strain percentages (Figure 2k). In few‐layer WSe2 (2‐4L), tensile strain can induce the opposite trend of an indirect‐to‐direct transition, thereby enhancing deterministic tuning of the fundamental optical transitions in 2D semiconductors [48]. In practice, PL‐based strain metrology is most robust when coupled with Raman and simple polarization controls, with co‐mapping offering a complementary view of lattice‐phonon versus band‐gap/excitonic responses.

Second‐Harmonic Generation

Second‐harmonic generation (SHG) is a sensitive and non‐linear optical probe of crystal symmetry in non‐centrosymmetric 2D materials. Because the SHG signal originates from the second‐order susceptibility, it is highly sensitive to symmetry breaking, making it suitable for quantitative strain metrology [49, 50]. Polarization‐resolved SHG (PR‐SHG) yields angular intensity patterns that directly reflect lattice orientation and distortion in strained 2D materials, allowing simultaneous determination of crystal axes and the strain direction [31]. Under uniaxial strain, the intrinsic threefold rotational symmetry of monolayer 2H‐MoS2 is broken, distorting the characteristic six‐lobed PR‐SHG pattern so that selected lobes are enhanced while others are suppressed (Figure 3a,b). This distortion reflects a strain‐induced lowering of symmetry that reweighs the effective contributions of the second‐order nonlinear susceptibility tensor. The resulting anisotropy provides a direction readout of the principal strain axis (Figure 3c), as clearly illustrated by the contrast between the unstrained crystal and the 1.0% strained state, where the intensity redistributes according to the strain direction. In contrast, equal biaxial strain modifies the overall SHG intensity while largely preserving the six‐lobed pattern, whereas unequal biaxial loading or finite shear components introduce subtler lobe‐to‐lobe asymmetries that can be discriminated via careful fitting [51]. A key advantage of SHG is its non‐invasive, wafer‐compatibility, enabling large‐area strain visualization with sub‐micrometer spatial resolution by analyzing PR‐SHG patterns across the field of view.

Grazing‐Incidence X‐Ray Diffraction and Selected‐Area Electron Diffraction

The most direct approach to quantify strain is to measure lattice spacings by X‐ray diffraction (XRD) [52, 53]. However, ultrathin 2D materials show no dominant XRD peaks. Therefore, grazing‐incidence XRD (GI‐XRD) has been used as a precise determination method for in‐plane lattice constants of 2D materials. Strain‐induced peak shifts directly reflect lattice expansion or compression [54]. Compared with spectroscopic techniques, such as Raman or SHG, which infer strain through phonon or symmetry changes, GI‐XRD reveals reciprocal space structural information, averaging strain over a large areas, and can probe 2D materials even beneath metal overlayers, where Raman peak shifts may be confounded by overlayer‐induced effects and thus less straightforward for precise quantification (Figure 3d,e) [53]. These capabilities make GI‐XRD particularly effective for assessing epitaxial strain, lattice commensurability with substrates, and the areal strain uniformity across extended films. Nevertheless, it lacks nanoscale spatial resolution. Selected‐area electron diffraction (SAED) in transmission electron microscopy (TEM) overcomes this limitation by offering local reciprocal‐space measurements with high spatial precision [55]. By selecting specific micro‐ to nanoscale regions within a flake or across an interface, SAED patterns capture the local reciprocal lattice, enabling quantitative assessment of lattice parameters. Therefore, strain can be quantified by comparing diffraction‐spot positions or spacings before and after deformation (Figure 3f) [56]. Because of nanoscale probing capability, SAED resolves heterogeneity and crystallographic anisotropy of strain, e.g., defect‐ or interface‐localized distortions, providing a direct bridge between nanoscale structural strain and localized electronic or optical property variations.

Atomic‐Scale TEM for Geometric Phase Analysis

TEM offers a direct high‐resolution method for probing strain in 2D materials, ranging from atomic‐resolution imaging to advanced diffraction‐based mapping. These techniques are particularly powerful for correlating lattice deformation with functional properties in device‐relevant contexts. High‐resolution TEM (HR‐TEM) provides real‐space visualization of the atomic lattice, enabling identification of local distortions introduced by strain [57]. Strain‐induced changes in interatomic distances and lattice fringes can be qualitatively assessed, while quantitative information can be obtained through fast Fourier transform (FFT) analysis of HRTEM images [58]. Conceptually analogous to SAED, this FFT approach yields local lattice‐parameter variations and orientation maps at high spatial resolution. Because such analyses often require extended high‐dose imaging, careful optimization of electron beam energy, dose rate, and acquisition conditions is essential to minimize knock‐on damage and ensure metrological accuracy, particularly for mono‐ and few‐layer specimens.

Geometric phase analysis (GPA) is a Fourier‐based post‐processing technique applied to HR‐TEM or scanning TEM (STEM) images to extract quantitative in‐plane strain and lattice‐rotation fields. By analyzing the phase of selected diffraction spots in the Fourier transform, GPA resolves subtle lattice distortions and in‐plane strain distributions (Figure 3g,h) [59, 60]. This capability makes GPA effective for mapping dislocation networks and interfacial mismatch in heterostructures [61, 62]. Although its sensitivity depends on image quality, signal‐to‐noise ratio, and filtering strategy, GPA is widely regarded for precise nanoscale strain mapping method.

To overcome the inherent limitations of 2D projections, 3D electron diffraction has recently emerged as a powerful tool for probing the local strain landscape within complex vdW architectures. Unlike conventional methods, it can independently quantify out‐of‐plane strain components and local interlayer spacing variations by reconstructing the 3D reciprocal space [63]. This capability provides a holistic view of the strain state in twisted or multi‐layered heterostructures, where local lattice reconstructions and moiré‐induced deformations govern the material's properties. Building upon these high‐dimensional insights, four‐dimensional STEM (4D‐STEM) combines nanobeam scanning with diffraction pattern acquisition at every probe position, generating a dataset with co‐registered real‐ and reciprocal‐space information. Tracking diffraction‐spot displacements across the scanned area allows construction of strain and rotation maps with high spatial resolution (Figure 3i,j) [64, 65]. Beyond strain quantification, 4D‐STEM captures local symmetry breaking, phase transitions [66], and structural heterogeneities [67], offering a versatile platform for correlating strain with material functionalities. When used in combination, these techniques provide a comprehensive toolkit for analyzing local strain in 2D materials across multiple length scales. Each method offers distinct trade‐offs in spatial resolution, sensitivity, and device compatibility, and judicious method selection is critical for robustly correlating structural deformation with functional property changes.

Strategies to Induce Strain in 2D Semiconductors

Owing to their atomic thickness, mechanical flexibility, and high sensitivity to lattice distortion, 2D semiconductors are particularly prone to accumulate pronounced local strain under external stimuli. In general, strain in 2D semiconductors has been introduced via mechanical deformation (bending, stretching, compression) [68, 69], thermal treatment (differential thermal expansion or localized heating/cooling) [56, 70], or applied contact forces (tip‐induced strain, indentation, hydrostatic pressure) [71, 72], each of which offers distinct control over strain amplitude and spatial distribution. Among the available strategies, environment‐mediated approaches that exploit interactions between a 2D semiconductor and its surroundings have emerged as particularly versatile and device‐compatible. These interactions include: i) substrate‐induced local strain arising from mismatch of thermal expansion coefficients, nanoscale surface patterning, or mechanical deformation of flexible supports, and ii) overlayer‐induced local strain generated by materials deposited, synthesized, or transferred onto the 2D surface, ranging from high‐stress thin films and metal/metal‐oxide layers to epitaxially grown or transferred 2D overlayers. Such environment‐driven methods enable precise spatial and directional control of the strain, opening pathways to engineer the structural, electronic, and optical properties of 2D semiconductors for advanced device applications.

Substrate‐Induced Local Strain

2D semiconductors are commonly synthesized on diverse substrates, such as silicon, sapphire, quartz, and polymers, using chemical vapor deposition (CVD). Depending on the applications, as‐grown 2D semiconductors can be released and transferred onto other substrates or stacked on other 2D layers. It should be noted that TMDs typically require high growth temperatures to achieve high crystalline quality. During this high‐temperature growth process, a mismatch in thermal expansion coefficients (TECs) between the TMD film and the substrate leads to residual strain upon cooling. This residual strain can be interpreted as TEC‐mismatch‐induced strain, which has been systematically investigated in monolayer WSe2 grown by CVD on different substrates with distinct TECs [56]. As illustrated in Figure 4a, the temperature‐dependent TECs (αsub for the substrate and α2d for the 2D material) lead to three regimes: negligible residual strain for αsub = α2d, tensile strain for αsub < α2d, and compressive strain for αsub > α2d. The induced strain was quantitatively analyzed through PL measurements, showing bandgap shifts in monolayer WSe2 (Figure 4b). These results effectively highlight a clear correlation between ΔTEC and the residual strain in the CVD‐grown TMDs (Figure 4c). Beyond synthesis, thermal treatment after transfer can also impart strain through differential contraction across the vdW interface. Upon cooling, a transferred 2D film, TEC mismatch with the host substrate induces strain [70]. For MoSe2 monolayers transferred onto substrates with different TECs (e.g., SiO2 and polypropylene (PP)), low‐temperature PL revealed ΔTEC‐dependent shifts of the A‐exciton energy, consistent with tensile/compressive loading (Figure 4d,e). Introducing an hBN buffer layer mitigated cooling‐induced strain, with thicker buffers providing more effective mechanical decoupling (Figure 4f,g). As discussed above, TEC‐mismatch approaches impose structural deformation that modifies TMD bonding and electronic structure. In particular, deformation affects hybridization between the d xy and d z2 orbitals of metal and the p (x+y) orbitals of chalcogen, thereby tuning the bandgap. Besides thermal routes, the atomic thinness, mechanical flexibility, and high stretchability make them well‐suited to substrate‐mediated strain via patterned protrudes [73, 74, 75, 76] or flexible polymers [22, 77]. For example, nanoimprint‐assisted strain engineering involves transferring 2D films onto molds with designed patterns to create fully 3D and tunable strain (Figure 4h,i) [78]. By adjusting imprint pressure and mold geometry, the areal distribution and amplitude of strain can be precisely controlled. PL mapping confirms periodic strain that follows the mold pattern in the MoS2 film (Figure 4j). Beyond discrete control, these patterned substrates facilitate the large‐scale integration of local strain architectures with high spatial uniformity. For instance, transferring 2D semiconductors onto periodic nanopillar arrays enables the deterministic formation of strain hotspots across wafer‐scale areas, providing a scalable route to create high‐density arrays of localized quantum emitters [79]. Flexible‐substrate approaches offer another controllable route [80]. In one such example, a flexible polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) film was pre‐stretched to form built‐in strain, after which TMDs were transferred onto the film (Figure 4k). Pre‐stretching a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) substrate and then transferring a TMD onto it stores elastic energy; upon relaxation, the substrate drives wrinkle formation in the TMD, producing localized strains up to 2%. These strained wrinkle crests exhibit enhanced PL, consistent with an exciton‐funneling mechanism that concentrates carriers/excitons in regions of higher tensile strain, thereby boosting radiative recombination relative to flat, unstrained films.

Stressor‐Induced Local Strain

Early efforts in strain engineering for 2D semiconductors primarily utilize substrate‐film interactions arising from epitaxy‐ or transfer‐induced lattice mismatch, transfer onto nanostructures, macroscopic bending of flexible substrates, and self‐assembled nanoscale wrinkling, where internal/external stresses at the 2D‐substrate interface generated strain. Recently, a powerful alternative, process‐induced strain, has emerged, in which high‐stress thin film “stressors” are deposited directly on 2D layers to introduce and control nanoscale strain [81, 82]. Such stressors can exert substantial thin‐film forces (>20 N/m), thereby transferring tensile stress and inducing strain in the underlying material [81]. When patterned magnesium oxide (MgOx) stressor films were deposited on monolayer MoS2 to induce process‐driven strain (Figure 5a), possible damage during deposition was mitigated by inserting a thin AlOx or HfOx passivation layer between the stressor and the 2D surface [83]. Patterned MgOx layers of varying thickness (0–60 nm) were subsequently deposited on the passivated MoS2, with the respective contributions of strain and doping deconvolved by Raman spectroscopy (Figure 5b). The analysis revealed that the passivation layer slightly increased n‐type doping while inducing negligible strain, whereas further MgOx deposition generated progressively larger tensile biaxial strain with minimal additional doping. The linear dependence of strain magnitude on stressor thickness confirmed that relaxation of the compressive stress in MgOx transferred a controllable tensile load to the underlying MoS2. Strain line profile across square stressor shows a strain minimum at the center, increasing the edges (Figure 5c). Near the edge, the strain inverts, producing a narrow compressive zone that extends outside the stressor. These results establish a foundation for predicting strain fields in 2D semiconductors integrated with arbitrary thin films and fabrication processes.

Stressor‐based techniques have been utilized to selectively induce strain for control of strain symmetry (uniaxial or biaxial) [84]. One of such approaches is to pattern stripe‐shaped regions by lithography, then subsequently stack Al2O3/MgF2/Al2O3 stressors using e‐beam deposition. MoS2 flakes with partial tensile‐stressor coverage show large tensile strains near free edges and compressive strain at the center of the stressor strip, with both in‐plane and out‐of‐plane strains (Figure 5d). By varying strip width and MoS2 thickness, the geometric distribution of tensile and compressive strained regions can be controlled. Polarized Raman spectroscopy further resolves strain directionality: ∼0.85% uniaxial strain arises at strip edges for 25N/m film force, while the same stack produces biaxial strain at strip centers. Raman spectra at the A and B points show distinct E2g1 and A 1g peak shapes, indicative of locally varying strain orientations (Figure 5e). Taken together, patterned stressed thin films enable deterministic, layout‐defined strain fields in 2D semiconductors, selectively introducing tensile/compressive loading, uniaxial/biaxial symmetry, and crystallographic alignment, thereby opening routes to engineered deformation landscapes for electronic, photonic, and superconducting applications.

A representative application of stressor‐based engineering is the reduction of contact resistance (R c) of 2D semiconductor transistors [85]. Ni contacts serve as thin‐film stressors on monolayer WS2, imparting compressive stress beneath the metal pads and inducing compensating tensile stress in the uncovered channel. The resulting uniaxial strain distribution aligned with the current flow depends on the contact length (L c) relative to the channel length (L ch) (Figure 5f). For long‐channel devices (L ch = 1 µm), tensile strain peaks near the contact edge and decays toward the channel center. As L ch is reduced, the peak tensile strain at the contact edge increases from ∼0.18% (L ch = 1 µm) to ∼0.49% (L ch = 30 nm) (Figure 5g). Conversely, for short contacts (L C = 0.1 µm), the maximum tensile strain is significantly smaller (0.017%–0.03%) (Figure 5h). These trends indicate that the overall strain profile is primarily governed by the ratio of L ch to L C. Electrical characterization reveals that high tensile strain concentrated near the contacts lowers the conduction band edge of WS2, increasing electron density in the channel and under the contacts and thereby reducing the Schottky barrier height (SBH) for electron injection. Devices with longer contacts exhibit a threshold‐voltage shift of ΔVT ≈ −0.27 V and a reduction of 78% in R C. These findings are consistent with previous reports that tensile strain in monolayer MoS2 or WS2 suppresses intervalley scattering in the conduction band, enhancing carrier mobility [76, 81].

Local Strain in CVD‐Grown Vertical and Lateral Heterojunction of 2D Semiconductors

Depositing bulk metals or metal oxides onto 2D layers enables deterministic control of the in‐plane strain, thereby tuning their electrical, optical, and mechanical properties. Similarly, extensive efforts have focused on synthesizing secondary 2D materials on top of a primary 2D material to form vertical hetero‐multilayers [86] or promoting lateral growth to realize in‐plane heterostructures [87, 88], both of which yield emergent properties governed by interfacial coupling. A key advantage of a 2D template is the formation of vdW epitaxy, which ensures a dangling‐bond‐free interface with weak interlayer interactions and minimized defects. When graphene was used as a template to epitaxially grow MoO3 (Figure 6a), spherical aberration‐corrected TEM (Cs‐TEM) confirmed the epitaxial relationship of the stack (Figure 6b,c) [86]. FFT diffraction revealed a rhombohedral MoO3 pattern (blue dashed line in the inset of Figure 6c) aligned with the hexagonal graphene pattern (orange dashed line), with the MoO3 (200) plane parallel to the graphene surface, evidencing an epitaxial relationship between MoO3 and graphene. This epitaxial growth induces compressive strain in graphene via strong interlayer coupling at the interface. The compressive strain arises from the TEC mismatch between MoO3 and graphene, along with high lateral stiffness and interfacial friction of MoO3 that mechanically clamps the graphene lattice. The strain amplitude in graphene increases with the MoO3 thickness, although the strain increase gradually diminishes with the addition of more layers. An elongated superlattice at the MoO3/Gr heterointerface, together with strong interlayer interactions, suggests a crystal‐orientation‐dependent modulation of graphene's properties. Angle‐resolved polarized Raman spectroscopy results revealed that the G peak of 3L‐MoO3/Gr heterostructure measured at four specific angles splits into two components of G+ (1621.7 cm−1) and G− (1623.2 cm−1), corresponding to the bi‐directional strain, evidence of uniaxial strain in graphene (Figure 6d). These polarized Raman spectra confirm that vdW‐epitaxial MoO3 imposes anisotropic strain in graphene, implicating vdW‐interface‐driven strain as an effective knob to tailor the mechanical and electrical properties of monolayer graphene (Figure 6e).

Beyond vertical vdW epitaxy, strain engineering in laterally stitched TMD heterostructures is rapidly advancing. Monolayer WS2/WSe2 coherent lateral superlattices were synthesized by MOCVD (Figure 6f) [89]. Despite a large lattice mismatch of 4%, dislocation‐free, registry‐matched atomic layers were obtained. SEM images of three representative WS2/WSe2 heterostructures with controlled supercell dimensions show dark (WS2) and bright (WSe2) domains, with equilateral triangular units of uniform width in both materials, defined by nanometer‐precision lateral heterojunctions (Figure 6g). Strain accommodation within the coherent superlattice results in tensile (compressive) strain localized in the WS2 (WSe2) region (Figure 6h), and the strain magnitude increases with the supercell dimensions (Figure 6i). Specifically, a smaller width of WS2 or a larger width of WSe2 increases tensile strain in WS2 while decreases compressive strain in WSe2, bringing the lattice constants (a // and a ⊥) closer to intrinsic values of WSe2. The associated band structures of both WS2 and WSe2 are strongly influenced by the imposed strain, with tensile (compressive) loading decreasing (increasing) the bandgap, thereby enabling wide tunability of optical transitions through superlattice design. This strain‐dependent band structure allowed for the extensive tuning of optical properties through superlattice design. Whereas strained thin films often relax via out‐of‐plane buckling, false‐color SEM images of five coherent WS2(blue)/WSe2(yellow) superlattice variants show that the WS2/WSe2 coherent supercells grown on SiO2 remain largely planar, because strong vdW adhesion between the substrate and the atomically thin superlattice suppresses edge‐driven warping despite alternating compressive/tensile segments (Figure 6j), which is in agreement with AFM measurements that show a peak‐to‐valley height of ∼1–2 nm (Figure 6k).

Local Strain in Transferred 2D Layers

For epitaxial co‐growth of dissimilar 2D crystals, the lattice mismatch inherently restricts the range of material combinations, limiting the formation of heterostructures with minimal dislocations or defects. In contrast, when crystalline 2D TMDs are assembled by exfoliation and transfer, lattice mismatch constraint is greatly relaxed, allowing for broader material pairings and facile formation of high‐quality vdW heterolayers. Motivated by this flexibility, we now focus on local strain phenomena in transfer‐assembled vdW heterostructures [90, 91]. A representative example is using stacking to imprint anisotropy by inducing “effective” anisotropy in otherwise isotropic TMDs through coupling to a low‐symmetry layer. This approach aims to induce artificial anisotropy in isotropic 2D semiconductors with structural symmetry. For example, when WSe2 with honeycomb symmetry is stacked on insulating CrOCl with twofold rotational symmetry, interlayer coupling and polarization break the in‐plane symmetry of WSe2 and render the heterostructure anisotropic (Figure 7a) [90]. Polarized Raman spectroscopy measurements reveal that, with increasing polarization angle, the intensity of the E 1 2g mode decreases and exhibits a two‐lobed pattern in the polar plot, with the A1g mode exhibiting a similar angle‐periodic response (Figure 7b,c). Consistent trends appear in PL, where the peak intensity diminishes from 0° to 90°, yielding a distinct two‐lobed directional selectivity (Figure 7d–f). These observations demonstrate that simple vdW stacking can transmit and reconfigure interfacial strain/coupling to endow the heterostructure with anisotropic optical responses that are absent in the constituent isotropic 2D layer.

Another example of transfer‐induced local strain can be found in isotropic/anisotropic vdW heterostructures, where a symmetric TMD is dry‐transferred onto a low‐symmetry layered crystal to generate uniaxial lattice deformation [91]. In a MoS2/Ta2NiSe5 stack, monolayer MoS2 is transferred onto a Ta2NiSe5 flake to form the heterojunction (Figure 7g). Raman spectra taken on the overlapped region show clear softening of MoS2 phonon modes, where the E 1 2g and A 1g peaks redshift by ∼3.20 and 1.97 cm−1, respectively (Figure 7h). Since the E 1 2g mode is strain‐sensitive and tensile strain leads to its softening, these redshifts indicate that tensile strain is introduced into monolayer MoS2 upon stacking. Consistently, polarization‐resolved Raman further reveals that the MoS2 E 1 2g mode develops a pronounced two‐lobed angular dependence in the heterostructure (Figure 7i), evidencing symmetry breaking and an anisotropic response that is absent in pristine symmetric MoS2. These results demonstrate that transfer‐assembled vdW stacking can impose interfacial uniaxial strain and thereby convert an originally isotropic TMD into a directionally selective optical medium.

Moiré‐Pattern‐Induced Local Strain

In multilayer 2D stacks, twist‐angle‐dependent periodic features, most notably moiré patterns and moiré superlattices, emerge and govern the resulting electronic and mechanical properties. Recently, moiré superlattices in small‐angle twisted or hybrid bilayers have attracted rapidly growing interest, where local strain and lattice relaxation are increasingly discussed as key ingredients for emergent phenomena such as flat‐band formation and correlation‐driven optical signatures [92, 93]. Motivated by this trend, this subsection summarizes practical routes to introduce and tune moiré‐related strain through experimentally accessible knobs such as twist‐angle control, transfer‐induced shear, and interlayer coupling. A combined experiment‐molecular dynamics study of twisted MoS2/MoSe2 hetero bilayers showed that small twist angles (θ < 2°) drive substantial atomic reconstruction, resulting in large moiré periods, and generate high local strain levels (εlocal ≈ 1%) (Figure 8a) [94]. Moiré superlattice formation is promoted by reconstruction within the stacked registry domains, producing complex strain textures comprising uniaxial, biaxial, and shear components. At larger twist angles (θ > 10°), reconstruction is suppressed and the moiré period shortens, leading to negligible strain. Polarization‐resolved Raman corroborates these complex strain distributions in hetero‐bilayers at small twist angles (θ < 10°) through splitting of the E2g1 mode in the MoS2 top layer induced by atomic reconstruction. In TMD heterostructures with θ < 10°, such deformation patterns serve as fingerprints of lattice reconstruction. For MoS2/MoSe2 heterostructures, zero twist angle yields the largest moiré period, while ε local increases from θ = 0° to 1°, reaching a maximum of ∼1.15% near θ = 1°, and then decreases with further rotation. Beyond θ > 10°, the moiré period decreases and ε local drops to ∼0.15% on average. Thus, the strain symmetry and magnitude are determined by the twist angle, which in turn tunes the moiré wavelength and potential landscape, enabling deterministic control over the structural and optoelectronic properties of twisted 2D heterostructures.

It was theoretically demonstrated that synchronously applied biaxial strain applied to a graphene flake sandwiched between two pre‐strained hBN layers induces an in‐plane rotation of the graphene within a large range of ∼10° (Figure 8b) [95]. This strain‐driven rotational relaxation programs local strain within the graphene, and the resulting moiré superlattice serves as a quantitative reporter of the relative twist angle and lattice registry between graphene and hBN (Figure 8c). Because moiré reconstruction and stacking configuration are governed by rotational stability and the interlayer energy landscape, moiré patterns provide a sensitive probe for interpreting twist stability and interfacial energetics. Accordingly, moiré lattices can be leveraged to track strain‐mediated atomic rearrangements in lattice‐mismatched TMD vdW heterostructures. In case of TMD homo‐ and hetero‐bilayers, monolayer TMD crystals of WSe2 and MoSe2 were aligned into R‐stack (parallel) and H‐stack (anti‐parallel) bilayers, encapsulated with hBN or graphene, and then subjected to high‐temperature annealing (Figure 8d); the R‐stack relaxed into a 3R fully commensurate (FC) structure (R‐FC), while the H‐stack transformed into a 2H FC structure (H‐FC) [96]. High‐angle annular dark‐field STEM (HAADF‐STEM) and selected‐area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns of the moiré patterns before and after annealing revealed atomic rearrangements of WSe2/MoSe2 bilayer into H‐FC structure regardless of the twist angle (Figure 8e). Figure 8f summarizes this behavior, showing that atomic reconstruction in twisted TMD bilayers depends on stacking sequence and twist angle. Below a critical twist angle (θc ≈ 0°), R‐ and H‐stack bilayers spontaneously relax into periodic 3R‐ and 2H‐type domains to minimize total vdW energy. At larger twist angles, the bilayers remain incommensurate at room temperature but undergo thermally driven reconstruction into R‐FC or H‐FC structures upon annealing.

Local strain engineering in 2D semiconductors relies on direct manipulation of their atomic lattices through mechanical deformation, thermal modulation, and interaction with surrounding layers. Substrate‐ and stressor‐driven strain controls its magnitude and direction, tuning the structural, electronic, and optical properties of 2D layers. Emerging approaches such as vdW epitaxy, lateral heterojunction growth, and moiré superlattices use interfacial coupling to drive reconstruction and anisotropy at the atomic scale.

Strain‐Induced Modulation of Properties in 2D Semiconductors

Strain engineering in 2D semiconductors provides a powerful mean to tailor their intrinsic properties by directly modulating the lattice structure [89, 90], thereby reshaping the band structure [10, 97, 98], governing carrier transport dynamics [73, 99, 100], and excitonic behaviors [101, 102, 103]. To complement experimental demonstrations, multiscale computational approaches are often employed to describe nanoscale strain fields and connect them to electronic or optical responses. Continuum mechanics provides device‐scale strain distributions under realistic boundary conditions, atomistic simulations capture reconstruction‐driven nanoscale strain textures, and first‐principles calculations quantify strain‐induced changes in band‐ and exciton‐related observables [47, 94, 104]. This section reviews how local strain impacts electrical, optical, and structural properties, with emphasis on representative mechanisms and reported experimental demonstrations.

Strain‐Induced Modification of Electrical Properties

Applying local strain to 2D semiconductors can significantly alter their electronic band structure, which in turn affects carrier transport. Depending on symmetry and magnitude, strain can lower effective masses of carriers, suppress electron‐phonon scattering, or generate carrier trapping potential, ultimately influencing carrier mobility. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations showed a significant effect of tensile strain on the conduction band of monolayer WS2 [99]. The unstrained WS2 exhibits two conduction band minimum (CBM)/s near the K valley due to spin splitting, meanwhile applying uniaxial (blue dotted lines) or biaxial (orange dashed lines) tensile strain of 1% raises the CBM at Q relative to K valley, increasing K–Q valley separation of ΔEQK (Figure 9a). As shown in Figure 9b, ΔEQK increases linearly with strain, with biaxial strain exhibiting a much higher rate of increase (169 ± 2.1 meV/%) compared to uniaxial strain (86 ± 1.9 meV/%). The larger valley separation under biaxial strain effectively suppresses K–Q intervalley scattering, enhancing carrier mobility. For unstrained WS2, the calculated ΔE QK of ∼87 meV, including spin‐orbit coupling, agrees with the previous report [105]. These results suggest that biaxial tensile strain is more effective than uniaxial strain for improving electron transport in WS2 devices.

Building on this concept, a deterministic method to introduce localized biaxial tensile strain in monolayer MoS2 was demonstrated (Figure 9c) [74]. Grayscale nanopatterned dielectric substrates were prepared by thermal scanning probe lithography (t‐SPL). The MoS2 flakes were conformally adhered to the patterned dielectric, yielding periodic sinusoidal strain profiles across the channel. This nanoscale strain modulation suppressed electron‐phonon scattering, as confirmed by both electrical measurements and theoretical modeling. The strained devices exhibited remarkable device performance, achieving an eight‐fold increase in on‐current and a high room‐temperature mobility of 185 cm2/V·s (Figure 9d). Temperature‐dependent mobility analysis further revealed a substantial reduction in phonon‐limited scattering; the strained devices maintained a low power‐law exponent (γ ≈ 1.1) even at T > 200 K (Figure 9e), indicating sustained high mobility under thermal stress. Similarly, the effect of substrate‐induced nanoscale rippling on the electronic transport properties was investigated by transferring MoS2 onto a rough SiN substrate (Figure 9f) [106]. A dramatic enhancement in carrier mobility up to ∼900 cm2/V·s was achieved, significantly exceeding those of flat MoS2 devices on a smooth substrate. DFT calculations indicated that increasing ripple height raises both in‐plane and out‐of‐plane components of the intrinsic dielectric constant (ε) of MoS2 (Figure 9g). Experimentally, rippled MoS2 exhibits an effective dielectric constant nearly an order of magnitude higher than flat MoS2 (Figure 9h). This enhanced dielectric screening suppresses electron‐phonon scattering, shifting the dominant mobility limiter to carrier‐impurity scattering. Under such conditions, mobility increases as ε 2, consistent with the observed improvement. Interestingly, temperature‐dependent mobility measurement exhibits a negative power‐law exponent (γ), further supporting the suppression of phonon scattering in the rippled structures (Figure 9i). Beyond mobility, nanoscale strain can also lower the SBH at metal‐semiconductor contacts. In monolayer WS2 transistors, stressor‐induced tensile strain in the channel reduces the CBM relative to the Fermi level, facilitating electron injection and lowering contact resistance by ∼78% [85]. While the precise mechanisms behind strain‐induced performance improvements are complex and multifactorial, the accumulated evidence strongly supports local strain engineering as an effective mean for enhancing the performance of 2D devices.

Strain‐Induced Phase Transitions

It has been reported that strain can induce structural phase transitions in TMDs, enabling reversible switching between semiconducting and metallic phases [107]. In particular, MoTe2 exhibits a semiconducting 2H phase and a semimetallic 1T’ phase, with the energy barrier between them small enough to be modulated by moderate tensile strain. This opens opportunities for non‐volatile switching and phase‐change memory applications of TMDs. Localized AFM‐tip‐induced tensile strain can trigger a reversible semiconductor‐to‐metal (2H →1T′) transition in MoTe2 at room temperature (Figure 10a,b) [108]. Conductive AFM measurements revealed a conductivity increase of up to four orders of magnitude during the phase transition, accompanied by a change from double‐Schottky to Ohmic current–voltage (I–V) behavior, which reversely returned upon strain release. Systematic temperature‐force phase diagram of Figure 10c indicates that tensile stress lowers the transition temperature at a rate of ∼0.16 K/MPa, in agreement with Clausius–Clapeyron relation. The latent heat of transition was estimated at ∼330 cal/mol, consistent with differential scanning calorimetry measurements. These findings confirm that even sub‐percent tensile strain can effectively modulate the thermodynamic free‐energy landscape of MoTe2, enabling low‐energy phase switching. In device applications, strain engineering was integrated into a vertical MoTe2 phase‐change memristor architecture (Figure 10d) [109]. By depositing a high‐stress metal contact layer, uniaxial tensile strain was generated beneath the contact edge, locally reducing the energy difference between the 1T’ and 2H phases. This pre‐strained region acted as an active switching channel, enabling electric‐field‐driven 1T’‐to‐2H transitions without the need for an electroforming step. Device performance was strongly orientation‐dependent; both DFT calculations and experimental statistics showed that uniaxial tensile strain aligned along the zigzag crystallographic direction was most effective for inducing phase change, with optimal switching observed for alignment angles within ±15° of the zigzag axis (Figure 10e–g). Furthermore, the extent of the strained active layer, consequently the ON/OFF ratio and switching voltage, could be tuned by adjusting the film force (product of intrinsic stress and film thickness). This approach yielded ultralow switching voltages of ∼90 mV, on/off ratios exceeding 108, and sub‐nanosecond switching speeds. These findings highlight the potential of strain engineering for enabling versatile phase‐transition‐based device applications in 2D semiconductors.

Strain‐Induced Modulation of Optical Properties

2D materials possess exotic optical characteristics stemming from their atomic‐scale thickness, thickness‐dependent band transitions and bandgaps, and pronounced excitonic effects due to reduced dielectric screening [110, 111]. These features render their optical responses highly sensitive to external perturbations, such as strain [52], dielectric environment [112, 113], and interlayer interactions [90, 119]. Among them, strain engineering offers a versatile and non‐invasive approach to tune the electronic band structure and excitonic landscape at the nanoscale. Moreover, spatially varying strain can guide exciton diffusion [17], modulate photoluminescence [26, 47, 48], and even form quantum‐dot‐like potentials that enable single‐photon emission. Such capabilities make strain engineering of 2D semiconductors as a promising approach toward advanced optoelectronics and quantum photonics.

Strain Engineering of Exciton Behaviors in 2D Semiconductors

Exciton funneling defined by strain was demonstrated in monolayer MoS2 by employing pre‐strained elastomeric substrates [114]. Upon transfer, the flakes developed wrinkle‐ and dome‐like morphologies that act as local bandgap minima (Figure 11a). PL of strained MoS2 regions exhibited pronounced redshift and intensity enhancement owing to exciton drift toward the lowest‐gap regions before recombination. Raman spectroscopy results, interpreted jointly with the PL results, provided further evidence: while systematic downshifts of phonon modes confirmed tensile strain, subtle mismatches between the Raman and PL spatial profiles indicated strain‐driven exciton redistribution, i.e., strain‐induced funneling (Figure 11b).

Based on this mechanism, site‐controlled single‐photon emitters (SPEs) were realized in monolayer WSe2 by transferring crystals over lithographically patterned nanopillar arrays (Figure 11c) [115]. The nanopillars induced nanoscale tensile strain at their tips, generating localized emission centers with sharp, spectrally discrete lines (Figure 11d,e). Second‐order autocorrelation measurements confirmed single‐photon statistics (g(2)(0) < 0.5) at cryogenic temperatures, establishing strained regions as deterministic SPEs. Furthermore, recent strategies involving the creation of laterally confined nanodot structures have demonstrated significantly enhanced excitonic interactions, providing a means to precisely control quantum emission through structural confinement and local strain engineering [116]. Importantly, the emitter position and density were dictated by the array geometry, enabling scalable integration of quantum light sources into photonic circuits.

Complementing these experimental demonstrations, a theoretical framework was proposed for strain‐mediated exciton control in nearly lattice‐matched MoX2/WX2 heterobilayers (X = S, Se) stacked with zero twist angle [117]. This model predicts that hydrostatic strain is concentrated at the nodes of the domain‐wall network, forming deep, C3‐symmetric quantum‐dot‐like potentials (Figure 11f). These traps host discrete excitonic states with s‐ and p‐like character and yield red‐shifted emission relative to the surrounding domains (Figure 11g). Furthermore, the emission energy and polarization can be tuned via twist angle and stacking orientation, providing design guidelines for strain‐programmable quantum emitters in vdW heterostructures. Beyond spatial confinement, mechanical strain can be further exploited to tune the intrinsic excitonic species and their spin‐valley dynamics. Recent work has demonstrated that strain‐induced exciton hybridization allows for the deterministic control of valley polarization dynamics in 2D semiconductors, providing a novel route for strain‐programmable valleytronic applications [118].

Strain Engineering for Photovoltaics and Photodetectors

Inherently strong light–matter interaction of 2D materials has long been considered as a promising characteristic for high‐performance photovoltaics and photodetectors [119]. However, their atomically thin nature inherently limits light absorption, often resulting in lower‐than‐expected conversion efficiencies. Recent studies have shown that strain‐induced modification of band structures in 2D materials enhances their optical properties with high efficiency [19, 120, 121]. Moreover, when non‐centrosymmetric 2D materials are subjected to strain, they generate piezoelectric polarization charges that can be utilized to further improve their optoelectronic properties owing to improved light–matter interaction and accelerated carrier dynamics [122].

Utilizing this concept, non‐centrosymmetric trigonal 3R‐MoS2 was used to enhance the bulk photovoltaic effect [123]. The 3R‐MoS2 flakes were transferred onto a patterned substrate with 200‐nm‐high steps spaced 5.5 µm apart, and a PDMS stamp was then used to deflect the flake so that it was stretched perpendicular to the step edges. In the unstrained state (Figure 12a), the device exhibited negligible short‐circuit photocurrent (I sc) under illumination, showing only a photoconductive response. In contrast, uniaxially strained 3R‐MoS2 (∼0.18%) generated a significant I sc of ∼170 nA under the same illumination (Figure 12b). The photocurrent increased linearly with incident light intensity, confirming the strain‐induced enhancement of the bulk photovoltaic effect. To further elucidate the role of crystal orientation, spatial photocurrent mapping was conducted across armchair‐ and zigzag‐oriented regions of strained 3R‐MoS2, as well as 2H‐MoS2 references (Figure 12c). The results revealed that the pronounced bulk photovoltaic response emerges exclusively in armchair‐oriented strained 3R‐MoS2, while zigzag‐oriented and 2H‐MoS2 samples showed negligible signals. This indicates that applying uniaxial strain along specific crystallographic directions is a critical key to optimize the optical performance of such piezoelectric 2D systems.

Additionally, strain is also powerful for 2D‐material‐based photodetectors since strain‐induced band modulation enables detection of a broader spectral range [124, 125]. A self‐powered lateral PN homojunction photodetector was demonstrated by introducing p‐type doping into monolayer WSe2 via N‐ion implantation, resulting in an N‐WSe2/WSe2 homojunction on a flexible PET substrate (Figure 12d,e) [126]. The device exhibited broadband photodetection from 500 to 850 nm (Figure 12f). When uniaxial tensile strain was applied by stretching the PET substrate, the maximum responsivity band shifted significantly from 550 nm in the unstrained state to 800 nm under tensile strain of 1.04% (Figure 12g). This strain‐induced redshift demonstrates the feasibility of mechanically tunable photodetectors, wherein deformation serves as a reversible control knob for the detection bandwidth without altering device structure or material composition.

Conclusion and Perspectives

Precision strain engineering has matured from a proof‐of‐concept laboratory curiosity into a practical toolkit for tuning the electrical, optical, and structural properties of 2D semiconductors. This review surveyed i) multiscale characterization methods that resolve strain from the micron down to the atomic limit, ii) diverse strategies to introduce strain via substrate interactions, stressed thin films (stressors), and interlayer coupling in vertical/lateral heterostructures, and iii) representative device‐level manifestations, including mobility/contact‐resistance improvements, strain‐induced phase transitions, and optical functionalities ranging from exciton funneling to single‐photon emission and strain‐tunable photodetection. Looking ahead, several materials‐ and process‐level challenges should be addressed to translate these advances into manufacturable technologies. On the materials side, wafer‐scale single‐crystal 2D films are a prerequisite for reliable and uniform strain fields and device‐to‐device reproducibility [127]. However, deterministic control of strain magnitude, gradient, and orientation across large areas remains an open target. On the process side, there is a clear need for CMOS‐compatible, transfer‐free routes to local strain. While many demonstrations rely on polymer transfer or lab‐scale actuation, industry adoption will favor methods, such as lithographically patterned high‐stress films, direct 2D‐on‐2D growth to harness interlayer interactions, and in situ strain programming within backend thermal budgets. Equally important are overlay/alignment accuracy, contamination control, and integration with standard metallization and dielectrics. From a device perspective, stability and reliability under realistic operating conditions remain central: strain drift under thermal cycling, humidity‐induced slippage at vdW interfaces, mechanical fatigue, hysteresis/creep, and encapsulation/packaging strategies all require systematic study. In addition, compact models that capture strain‐dependent transport, exciton behaviors, and phase transition would accelerate design‐technology co‐optimization. Finally, researchers would find rich opportunities at the intersection of scalable strain patterning (e.g., stressed‐film lithography, nanoimprint, TEC‐mismatch engineering), dynamic actuation (piezoelectric or MEMS on chip for real‐time tuning), and inverse design (data‐driven optimization of strain fields for modulation of band structures, exciton funnels, and phase landscapes). With continued advances in single‐crystal growth, CMOS‐compatible strain induction, and robust multi‐scale metrology, local strain engineering is poised to move from bespoke demonstrations to reliable and scalable building blocks for next‐generation electronic, optoelectronic, and quantum technologies.

He received his B.S. in the Department of Materials Science and Engineering from Hanyang University in 2023. His research focuses on the physical and electrical properties of 2D materials under strain and on 2D‐material‐based heterostructure devices.

Yoona Kim is a Ph.D. candidate in Materials Science and Engineering at Seoul National University under the supervision of Prof. Gwan‐Hyoung Lee. She received an M.S. in Chemistry from Yonsei University in 2014 and a B.S. in Chemistry from Hanyang University in 2012. Her doctoral research focuses on 2D material growth, including graphene and TMDs, with particular emphasis on strain‐induced modulation of their physical properties and the development of electronic devices with clean interfaces. Since 2014, she has also been working as a research engineer at Samsung SDI, specializing in the development of semiconductor materials.

Gwan‐Hyoung Lee is a professor at Seoul National University. He received a Ph.D. in Materials Science and Engineering from Seoul National University in 2006. Then, he worked for Samsung Electronics. In 2010, he joined Columbia University as a postdoctoral researcher. After five years at Yonsei University, he is now with Seoul National University since 2019. His research activities include the investigations of electrical, mechanical, and optical properties of 2D materials as well as 2D‐material‐based heterostructure devices for electrical and optoelectronic applications.
