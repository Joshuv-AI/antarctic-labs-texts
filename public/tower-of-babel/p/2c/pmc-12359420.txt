Implementation of a brief training programme to reduce mask leak with a respiratory function monitor
Authors: Neaha Patel, Ludovic Chassin, Burak Salgin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRespiratory function monitors (RFMs) can provide real-time data including expiratory tidal volumes and interface leak during delivery room (DR) neonatal resuscitation. However, concerns have been raised within previous randomised controlled trials and systematic reviews that effectuating RFMs routinely for DR resuscitation is limited by lack of comprehensive staff training. The International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation have also recently declared a knowledge gap in terms of the training required to be competent in utilising an RFM during neonatal resuscitation. We conducted an experiment with thirteen medical students who had no prior resuscitation experience to see if visual feedback from an RFM could improve mask leak when ventilating an extremely preterm manikin. Following a brief training session with hands-on practice, 260 breaths were analysed and median mask leak improved from 45% (IQR 25-62%) during the first breath to 0% (IQR 0-1%) by the twentieth and final breath. The median expiratory tidal volume was 3.6 mL (IQR 3.4-3.8 mL) and remained stable. This manikin experiment has demonstrated that even a short and experiential training session with an RFM for participants with no prior resuscitation training can be effective in reducing mask leak. Keywords: Neonatology, Resuscitation

Respiratory function monitors (RFMs) can be used in the delivery room to provide real-time ventilation data during neonatal resuscitation. Access to this information may help resuscitators optimise mask ventilation, reducing both mask leak and airway obstruction.1 2 Excessive leak around the face mask can lead to suboptimal tidal volume delivery and inadequate lung aeration, thereby impairing resuscitation effectiveness.1 However, a major limitation to RFM implementation has been the lack of comprehensive training in interpreting and applying displayed data. The International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation acknowledges that the training needed to achieve proficiency with an RFM remains unknown.3

We evaluated whether a brief training format with an RFM could reduce face mask leak in a preterm manikin among a naïve cohort. Thirteen medical students visiting from Nanjing Medical University in China participated, with no prior neonatal, paediatric or adult resuscitation training. The training intervention comprised a teaching session on neonatal resuscitation (postnatal transition, indications for positive pressure ventilation and interpretation of ventilation parameters), followed by a 10-min unstructured peer-learning practice session. The latter included practising mask ventilation on a preterm manikin with real-time RFM feedback, followed by each participant performing a trial of 20 ventilation breaths (aiming for approximately 30 breaths per minute) with the RFM display visible. Participants were instructed to focus exclusively on targeting the lowest leak percentage by adjusting their airway technique. The face mask was then removed and reapplied to the manikin, and each participant repeated the task with another 20 breaths under the same conditions. The second set of breaths was recorded by the RFM for analysis.

All ventilations were performed using an X-small resuscitation mask (Fisher & Paykel Healthcare Limited, New Zealand) with a Micro-Preemie manikin (Lifecast Body Simulation Limited, London, UK) and a Neopuff T-piece resuscitator (Fisher & Paykel Healthcare Limited) set to peak inspiratory pressure (PIP) 25cmH2O and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) 5cmH2O, as recommended by Newborn Life Support guidelines.4 The manikin was new and had been checked to ensure no intrinsic air leaks. A development prototype RFM running prototype software version 0.D (Europlaz Technologies Limited, Southminster, UK) was used with a hot-wire anemometer flow sensor. The flow sensor was zero-calibrated to ambient conditions before each use. Measured real-time parameters included expiratory tidal volume (mL), PIP (cmH2O), PEEP (cmH2O), mask leak (%) and inflation rate (breaths per minute) (figure 1). Data were extracted via Tera Term (an ASCII terminal programme) by USB-to-TTL cable connection to a laptop for breath-by-breath output analysis at 2 m/s intervals. Data were subsequently analysed in IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences V.29. Because of skewed distributions, results are presented as median (IQR).

Screenshot of the respiratory function monitor (RFM) interface in use. The display uses colour-coded bar graphs for key parameters showing expiratory tidal volume (blue bar), respiratory rate (yellow bar) and mask leak (pink bar). Green horizontal lines indicate the target value for each parameter, while red lines mark the acceptable range thresholds. The RFM was positioned adjacent to the manikin, and no auditory feedback was provided.

All 13 participants completed the protocol, yielding a total of 260 analysed breaths (20 per participant). On the first delivered breath, the median mask leak was 45% (IQR 25-62%) (figure 2). By the 20th and final breath, the median leak had improved to 0% (IQR 0%–1%). The median expiratory tidal volume was 3.6 mL (IQR 3.4–3.8 mL), remaining stable after the first breath (figure 3).

Previous studies have identified insufficient training as a barrier to RFM use in neonatal resuscitation, including 45% of staff from the MONitoR trial.5 The optimal training format for achieving competency with RFM devices remains unclear, which limits its perceived value. A recent 2024 study employed a similar RFM-guided training approach, though the total training duration was not reported.6 Our findings suggest that even a brief training session with hands-on practice using the RFM can help novice providers markedly reduce mask leak while maintaining stable tidal volumes. We omitted a control group in order to observe within-subject improvements, which may limit the strength of conclusions about causality. However, incorporation of this RFM teaching format within neonatal resuscitation training curricula could provide a practical strategy for improving resuscitation quality.
