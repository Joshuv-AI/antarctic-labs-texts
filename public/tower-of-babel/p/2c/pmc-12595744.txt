Externalizing behaviors in children living near coal-fired power plants
Authors: Kristina M. Zierold, John V. Myers, Guy N. Brock, Charlie H. Zhang, Lonnie Sears
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProximity to coal-fired plants exposes children to neurotoxic metals, which can negatively impact their behavioral health. Our research team previously reported an association between coal fly ash exposure and depression. However, other neurobehavioral outcomes have not been assessed. We hypothesized that the presence of fly ash would be associated with increased externalizing behaviors in children aged 6–14 years old.MethodsThis was a community-basedcross-sectional study. To investigate the association between externalizing behaviors and fly ash, five externalizing behavior scales and the composite Externalizing Problems scale from the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) were examined. Air samplers were placed in children’s homes to collect fly ash which was determined by scanning electron microscopy. Generalized linear regression with a negative binomial distribution and logistic regression were conducted to assess the association between fly ash and the results from the CBCL.ResultsApproximately 43% of children had detectable levels of fly ash in their homes. The odds of exhibiting clinically significant externalizing problems (t-score ≥ 65) after being exposed to fly ash was 2.66 times higher (95% confidence interval (CI) 1.08 to 6.57) compared to children who were not exposed. Though not statistically significant, Conduct Problems were 34% higher (95% CI: 0.885, 2.021) and Rule-Breaking Behavior was 22% higher (95% CI: 0.872, 1.696) among children exposed to fly ash compared to those who were not exposed.ConclusionsBased on our findings, fly ash may be a potential risk factor for externalizing behaviors in children living near coal-fired power plants. While a cross-sectional study design does not provide the opportunity to prove a causal relationship, it can generate hypotheses that inform more rigorous epidemiological studies for further investigation of the observed associations.

Introduction

Coal-fired power plants are responsible for approximately 36% of the electricity used globally [1]. The use of coal for energy production varies tremendously throughout the world. For example, in 2022, China’s coal-fired power plants accounted for 61% of electricity generation [2], the United States used coal for 19.5% of electricity generation [3], and Canada’s coal-fired power plants accounted for only 5.8% of electricity generation [4]. Coal-fired power plants produce large amounts of gaseous and particulate air pollution. One of the main pollutants that is generated by burning coal is coal ash. Coal ash, is comprised of many components, however, fly ash is generated in the greatest quantities [5–8].

Fly ash particles are comprised of glass spheres with diameters ranging in size from ≤ 0.1 micrometers (µm) to >100 μm, that contain neurotoxic metals, such as arsenic, lead, and manganese [6–12]. Smaller fly ash particles have the greatest potential to enter the alveoli of the lungs, enter the bloodstream, and cross the blood-brain barrier. Furthermore, fly ash particles can directly enter the brain through the olfactory bulb [13]. As fly ash particles decrease in size, metal concentrations increase, resulting in highly concentrated glassy spherical particles that are within the respirable range [14]. Although parent coal is known to contain neurotoxic metals, fly ash particles have much higher concentrations of metals and radioactive substances [14–16]. Yao et al., (2015) reported that trace element levels in Chinese coal ash may be 4–10 times higher than those found in the original coal [17]. Verma et al. (2016) reported that the concentration of lead (Pb) in coal was 4 ppm but was 35 ppm in fly ash [16]. Furthermore, the authors reported that the concentration of chromium was 8 ppm in the parent coal and 65 ppm in the fly ash [16]. Papastefanou (2010) reported that radionuclides such as 238U (uranium) was greatly increased in fly ash, compared to the parent coal [18]. Fly ash captured in air pollution control devices is often stored in uncovered landfills and unlined storage impoundments, frequently located near densely populated residential neighborhoods that are disproportionately comprised of low-income communities and communities of color [19–21]. Many coal ash storage sites are not capped, allowing fugitive dust to escape into the surrounding environment, posing potential public health risks [22], including neurobehavioral disorders and decreased social competency in children [23–27].

Neurobehavioral disorders, such as externalizing behavior problems, have been associated with pollution. Externalizing behavior problems refer to children’s behaviors directed toward others and include oppositional, aggressive, and rule breaking behaviors [28, 29]. Children and adolescents diagnosed with externalizing behavior problems may have poor school performance and have a lack of ability to form relationships which in turn impacts their social development [29, 30]. These behaviors lead to parenting challenges and are a common mental health concern in children and youth referred for treatment.

Conduct disorder, an extreme externalizing behavior problem, is one of the costliest disorders to society due to the violence and property destruction [31]. The worldwide prevalence of conduct disorder is estimated to be 2.1% [32]. Commonly known risk factors include genetic factors [33], social - familial stressors [34] and more recently environmental exposures.

Chen et. al, (2018) described a “pollution-induced psychopathology” involving disrupted emotion regulation and cognition following acute smog exposure [35]. Younan et al., (2018) reported that children and adolescents’ long-term exposure to PM2.5 increased delinquent behavior [36]. In a recent study, Reuben et al., (2023) reported that in joint models of PM2.5 and nitrogen dioxide (NO2), children exposed to NO2 experienced externalizing behaviors during their evolution to adulthood [37]. In addition to air pollution, several researchers evaluated the association between metal exposure and externalizing behavior problems, but the research is limited. Using data from the National Health and Examination Survey, Braun et al., (2008) found that children in the fifth quantile of Pb exposure had an approximately nine-fold times greater odds of being diagnosed with conduct disorder compared to children that were in the fourth quantile of exposure [38]. Furthermore, Nkomo et al. (2018) found a positive association between elevated lead levels and direct aggression [39]. In 2023, Liu et al., (2022) reported that children with higher blood Pb levels experienced greater social adversity and higher levels of child and parent reports of externalizing behaviors [40]. Most recently, in a study that investigated the joint effects of metals on externalizing behavior problems and aggressive and intrusive behaviors, Oluyemi et al., (2024) reported that males were more likely to exhibit these behaviors, largely driven by exposure to Pb copper, and chromium, compared to females exposed to the same mixture of metals [41].

Researchers examining the health of children residing near coal-fired power plants have reported a range of neurobehavioral problems among those living in close proximity to the facilities. In a study of neighborhoods adjacent to a coal-fired power plant, Sears and Zierold [42] found that children living near a coal-fired power plant had a higher prevalence of behavioral problems (38%), compared with children living farther away from such facilities (4%). Additional researchers have investigated neurobehavioral problems in children using maternal cord blood from mothers’ living near coal-fired power plants or maternal proximity to coal-fired power plants and have found that children who were living close to coal-fired power plants experienced poorer neurobehavioral outcomes compared to children not living near the plants [25–27]. However, these researchers did not directly measure coal ash exposure, but used a proxy for exposure.

Given the established associations between environmental toxins and externalizing behaviors in prior research, the overall objective of this research was to investigate whether in-home fly ash exposure was linked to several externalizing behaviors in children. Our research study used a novel measure of exposure to fly ash in the homes of children living near coal-fired power plants.

Materials and methods

Participants

Children and their parents/guardians were invited to participate in the study by social media advertisement, mailings, and foot recruiting [43]. Inclusion criteria for this study were: living within 10 miles of the centroid between the Cane Run or Mill Creek power plants located in Jefferson County, KY (Louisville); residing in the study area for at least two years; being aged 6 to 14 years old; be willing to fill out a series of questionnaires, and being fluent in English. Additionally, parents/guardians had to agree to smoke outside of the home during the week of air sampling. Children with an identified neurologic disorder were excluded. The participants in this study were determined based on those parents/guardians who responded to recruitment materials [44]. Overall, a total of 283 participants aged 6 to 14 years (male = 136 (48.1%), female = 147 (51.9%)) were recruited for the study. Before the study began, parents provided written consent and children provided written assent to participate in the study.

This study was performed in accordance with the human subjects’ research requirement of the University of Louisville (UofL) Human Subjects Protection Program as set forth and approved by the UofL Institutional Review Board. This study was approved by the UofL Institutional Review Board (IRB# 14.1016). The University of Alabama at Birmingham (UAB) Institutional Review Board also evaluated the study when the principal investigator moved to UAB (UAB (IRB#:300003807)). However, the IRB of record is maintained by the UofL.

Externalizing behavior measures

To assess externalizing behaviors, the child’s primary caregiver completed the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) [45]. The CBCL is a widely used tool used by clinicians and researchers alike. The school-aged CBCL is used for children aged 6 to 18 and consists of 113 questions that are scored on a Likert scale (0 = “absent,” 1 = “occurs sometimes,” and 2 = “occurs often”). The CBCL questions are associated with problems that fall into eight syndrome scales and six Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) scales. To identify externalizing behaviors, scales for Rule-Breaking Behavior, Conduct Problems, Oppositional Defiant Problems, Aggressive Behavior, and Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) problems were used. Additionally, the composite scale, Externalizing Problems was used. This composite scale combines measures of Rule-Breaking Behavior and Aggressive Behavior. The internal reliability based on the Cronbach’s alpha of these scales are high (Rule-Breaking Behavior = 0.85, Conduct Problems = 0.91, Oppositional Defiant Problems = 0.86, ADHD Problems = 0.84, Aggressive Behaviors = 0.94, Externalizing Problems composite = 0.94) [46].

Raw scores are generated based on parent/guardian responses to the CBCL. In order to assess the mental health of a child, the raw scores are converted to t-scores for clinical use. The t-scores are derived from norms for raw scores based on age and sex from a sample that included neurotypical children and children with a clinical mental health diagnosis. Once the parent/guardian completed the CBCL, the responses were entered into a scoring software program that resulted in a t-score for the composite Externalizing Problems scale. Children with a t-score cutoff ≥ 65 on the scale were considered to have clinically meaningful externalizing problems [47].

Indoor fly ash air sampling and analysis

The novelty of this study is that fly ash was measured in the homes of the participants. Other studies that have investigated the impact of coal ash on children’s neurodevelopment used proxies for coal ash exposure, such as distance from the plant or toxins in maternal cord blood [25–27]. The research methods for this study are further detailed in "Air sampling" and "Analysis of the filters for fly ash" sections. Air sampling was conducted in 12 ZIP codes throughout the Louisville area.

Air sampling

All air sampling was conducted in the homes of children. There are no air monitors that specifically capture fly ash. Therefore, particulate matter with diameters ≤ 10 micrometers (PM10) was collected and inspected visually for fly ash. PM10 was collected because it captures fly ash within the total particulate matter. Single stage personal modular impactors (PMI, SKC, Inc.) with polycarbonate filers and pumps (AirChek XR5000) were placed in a room where the child spent most of his/her time within their homes. This location was determined by talking with the parent/guardian and the child. Most (99%) parents/guardians and their child noted that the main room/den/living room was where the child was most of the time.

The PMI consisted of a cassette that held a polycarbonate filter and a plastic disk. The 37 mm polycarbonate filter that collected the PM10 sat below a 25 mm pre-oiled disposable disc which was placed to decrease particle bounce and allow for more efficient sampling. Although there are many types of filters, polycarbonate was selected as the filter material because it has properties that allow for optical microscopy techniques.

The pump (AirCheck XR5000) was connected to the PMI by ¼ inch tygon tubing. Before going into the field to set up the air sampling train (PMI and AirCheck XR5000 pump), each pump was calibrated using a MesaLabs DryCal Defender 510. Calibration of the pump was done in the laboratory before the sampling train went into the field. Once the pump was returned to the laboratory after sampling, the flowrate was checked. The pumps ran at 3.0 to 3.05 L per minute (L/min), which is the optimal flowrate for the PMI.

The AirCheck XR5000 pumps were utilized because they allow for flowrates between 1 L/min and 5 L/min. Additionally, the pump has a built in sensor that can detect differences between the flowrate in the field versus that at which the pump was calibrated. In the case of backpressure, when the filter became overloaded, the AirCheck XR5000 is designed to stop after 15 s. The pump attempts to start again five times, every fifteen seconds. If the filter became too overloaded, the pump would stop. In these situations, the research team would bring a new sampling train to the home to re-start the sampling. The sampling was conducted for one week. This time period was determined by experimental testing to collect enough mass on the filter so that it could be analyzed by SEM without the filter becoming too overloaded.

When the research team set the sampling train in a home, the PMI was placed on a tripod that was set at a height of approximately 3–5 feet to represent the breathing zone of a child. The sampler was placed away from windows, doors that opened to the outside, fireplaces, heating or cooling vents and stoves to prevent re-suspension of particles.

Analysis of the filters for fly Ash

Gravimetric analysis was used to determine the mass of PM10 collected. Prior to the polycarbonate filter being inserted into the PMI, it was weighed three times on an analytical microbalance. The initial mass of the filter was determined by taking the average of the three weights. The initial mass was measured to assess if there were trace levels of elements on the filter prior to being placed in the PMI. When the PMI was returned to the lab after a week of air sampling, the filter was weighed in triplicate and averaged. To determine the total mass of the PM10 collected on the filter, the average of the initial weight of the filter was subtracted from the average of the final weights. Using the average of the measured initial and final flowrates (each measured three times), and the final mass on the filters, concentration of PM10 was determined. Filters were then sent to Elemental Analysis Inc. (Lexington, KY) for analysis using scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Fly ash is identified by its chemical and morphological properties. Fly ash particles are spherical in nature, compared to most particles that are irregularly shaped (See Fig. 1). Additionally, fly ash particles have a different metal fingerprint than other metallurgic particles. The main metals in fly ash include silicon, iron, and aluminum. Futhermore, fly ash particles contain concentrations of neurotoxic metals, such as arsenic and mercury [7–12, 48].Fig. 1Fly ash cluster

Fly ash cluster

SEM is a surface area analytical technique that generates high resolution images. Initially, the scanning electron beam generates primary electrons which when hitting the surface area of the filter generates secondary electrons. By measuring the low intensity beams of the secondary electrons, a surface image is made. In our research, general optical microcopy was conducted first, to locate areas of the filter where most fly ash particles were located or where the particles were clustered together. Then to identify fly ash, a 1 cm2 slice of the polycarbonate filters were cut and mounted on a gold-plated slide. SEM was operated at 20 keV in the back-scattered electron imaging mode. Fifty fields of view (total area = 12.5 mm2) for each filter were scanned at a low magnification. The particles were examined at a higher magnification when a grouping of spherical particles of appropriate contrast was found. SEM can visualize the number of particles on the slice of the filter, providing counts of particles, however, particle count was stopped at 10 and clusters of particles can be difficult to count individual particles. Therefore, a binary variable “yes/no” was created as the exposure variable.

Distance to coal power plants

To assess whether distance to the coal-fired power plants impacted externalizing behaviors, data was examined using geographic information systems (GIS) to geocode participants’ home addresses on maps. Straight-line distance from each child’s home to their closest coal-fired power plant was calculated and used as a proxy measure to determine if externalizing behaviors were related to living in proximity to the coal-fired power plants. Straight-line distance (or “Euclidean distance”) to environmental polluting facilities such as coal-fired power plants is often used as a proxy for exposure to contaminants emitted by the plants [49–52]. While distance doesn’t perfectly capture actual pollution levels, it’s a convenient and relatively straightforward measure of proximity to a source of pollution. Additionally, using distance helps standardize the exposure measurement across study participants. This allows researchers to control for various confounding variables, such as socioeconomic status or other environmental factors, by comparing individuals or populations living at different distances from power plants.

Covariates

The effects of fly ash on externalizing behaviors were adjusted in our model by including factors potentially impacting behavior problems including age, sex, self-reported race, maternal education, and neighborhood traffic. In addition, recent research has shown that air pollution might be a factor in externalizing behaviors, so we included indoor PM10 as a covariate in the model.

Age

Risk factors for behavioral disturbances vary by age [31], therefore the models were adjusted for age of the child. Age was used as a continuous variable.

Sex

Prior research has suggested that boys are more likely to display externalizing behaviors and conduct disturbance [53]. Therefore, the model was adjusted for sex.

Race

The model was adjusted for self-identified race based on heath inequities resulting from social determinants of health [54].

Maternal education

For this study, maternal education level was used as a proxy for family income which has been suggested to be related to child behavior problems [34]. Maternal education was reported by caregiver participants and was categorized as: high school or some college and college degree or more. Scholars have found that of all the components of socio-economic status, maternal education has the strongest association with a child’s cognitive development [55–57]. Furthermore, scholars report that mother’s education is a strong predictor of financial insecurity and maternal depression, which in turn impacts a child’s cognitive development [57].

Neighborhood traffic

Traffic exposure was used as a covariate based on evidence of traffic proximity being a predictor of child behavior problems [58]. The traffic exposure variable (cars/meter) was extracted from the Environmental Protection Agency’s Environmental Justice (EJSCREEN) database (epa.gov/ejscreen). Traffic exposure is calculated based on the average annual daily traffic at major roads within 500 m of a neighborhood (i.e., census block group) divided by the distance to the roads (in meters). The square root of traffic exposure was used in analyses due to the right-skewed nature of the data (range = 0, 9831; median = 231).

Indoor PM10

As recent research is beginning to address the association between air pollution and externalizing behaviors in children, we adjusted our models for indoor PM10, which was collected during our in home sampling.

Statistical and geospatial analyses

Two different statistical methods were utilized to assess the CBCL raw scores from the five externalizing behavior scales (Rule-Breaking, Conduct Problems, Aggressive Behavior, Oppositional Defiant Problems, and ADHD Problems) and the t-score of the composite Externalizing Problems scale. The relationship between the composite Externalizing Problems t-scores and fly ash was assessed through binary logistic regression. For the composite Externalizing Problems scale, scores that are clinically significant are defined as t-scores ≥ 65 [47]. The logistic model was adjusted for age, sex, self-reported race, mother’s education as a proxy of socioeconomic status (dichotomized as having a college degree or higher vs. no college degree), square root of neighborhood traffic exposure, log of PM10, and distance to the nearest coal-fired power plant in miles. Model results included odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

In addition to assessing the composite Externalizing Problems scale with logistic regression, CBCL raw scores for Rule-Breaking Behavior, Conduct Problems, Oppositional Defiant Problems, ADHD Problems, and Aggressive Behaviors were analyzed. According to the Achenbach System of Empirically Based Assessment (ASEBA), statistical analysis must be conducted on the raw scores of the externalizing behaviors because the t-scores are censored at 50 [59]. To assess the relationship between fly ash exposure and the raw scale scores, generalized linear regression with a negative binomial distribution was used. Models were adjusted for the same covariates as the composite Externalizing Problems logistic regression model. Results were reported as incidence rate ratios (IRRs) along with 95% CIs.

In addition to the binary fly ash exposure, distance to the nearest coal-fired plant was investigated as a potential exposure. The unadjusted models were univariate models aiming to explore the relationships between each externalizing behavior scale and the composite Externalizing Problems scale and proximity to the closest coal-fired power plant. Logistic regression was used for the composite Externalizing Problems scale and generalized linear regression with a negative binomial distribution was used for the five externalizing behavior scales (raw scores). The adjusted models accounted for the effects of age, sex, self-reported race, mother’s education, square root of traffic exposure, and log(PM10).

All analyses were conducted in R 4.3.3 (R Core Team (2024)) [60]. The glm function was used for logistic regression, and glm.nb from the MASS package was used to model negative binomial models. False discovery rate (FDR) adjustment, as described by Benjamini and Hochberg [61], was used to adjust p-values to account for multiple comparisons. This method was selected due to the conservative nature of other adjustment methods such as Bonferroni correction.

Results

Due to missing variables in the regression analysis, 271 participants had complete data for analysis of the relationship between fly ash and the externalizing behaviors investigated in this study. Some children were missing data due to incomplete responses. This may have been due to incomplete answers to questionnaires or missing outcome data. Twelve children were excluded in this manner. Two children were missing CBCL scores, five were missing information on mother’s education, and eight were missing traffic information. These were not exclusive; children could miss more than one of the variables listed. In our study population, there were more males (51.7%) than females (48.3%), the median age was 11 years old, and the majority were white (73.8%). Approximately 40% of the mothers graduated college or graduate school. The majority of children (> 93%) in the study lived in their residence for their entire life.

A total of 118 children out of 271 (43.5%) were exposed to fly ash in the home based on filter sampling and SEM analysis. Figure 2 consists of a series of violin plots of externalizing behavior raw scores and the composite Externalizing Problems t-score stratified by the presence or absence of fly ash. Violin plots approximate the probability function, which provides more information about the data distribution than a box plot. For violin plots, a box plot is overlayed over the density, thus providing a view of the median and interquartile range (IQR). As shown in the composite Externalizing Problems scale (bottom plot on the right side), there is a difference in data distribution for externalizing problems. Specifically, the density is noticeably larger among the fly ash population above the t-score ≥ 65. This aligns with the results of our externalizing behavior analysis and the proportion of children above the t-score ≥ 65 (12.7% fly ash vs. 5.89% no fly ash).

For Rule-breaking Behavior and Conduct Problems, the third quartile (upper end of box plots) is slightly higher in the fly ash population. Furthermore, the density is greater above the third quartile in the fly ash samples than non-fly ash samples. Oppositional Defiant Problems, Aggressive Behaviors, and ADHD problems have very similar distributions.

Discussion

Children with externalizing behaviors may perform poorly in school, be aggressive towards others, and have a lack of ability to form relationships, all of which can adversely impact their development [28, 30]. In the United States, millions of children live near coal-fired power plants, which are major sources of fly ash and airborne pollutants that contains neurotoxic metals.

Our research found elevated rates of Rule-Breaking Behavior and Conduct Problems along with elevated odds of a clinically significant score on the composite Externalizing Problems scale in children exposed to fly ash. However, none of these findings remained statistically significant after adjusting for multiple comparisons.

While our results did not achieve statistical significance, the elevated effect sizes do warrant discussion for clinical treatment. First, there are millions of children that live near coal-fired powers and/or those coal-ash storage sites, which can emit fly ash at levels exceeding the National Ambient Air Quality Standard for PM2.5, as regulated by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency [62]. Externalizing behaviors are severe and need care by child psychologists and psychiatrists. In the United States, there are approximately 975 trained clinical psychiatrists per 100,000 children, leaving options for diagnoses and treatment difficult for parents to obtain help for their children [47]. Often pediatricians and other child treatment providers are in the position to determine clinical psychological conditions in children [63]. The CBCL is an easy tool that can be utilized for assessment of children who may need referral to a child psychiatrist [47]. The CBCL has been translated into over 90 languages and is low cost, so those that physicians working in populations that do not speak English as their first language have access to this validated instrument. The CBCL is easy to use and can be hand scored if computer software is not available [63]. Furthermore, the CBCL can be sent home with parents if they need more time or the office staff can help the parent fill out the form [63].

The t-scores quantify the severity of emotional or behavioral disorders making it easy for a treatment provider to make assessments [47]. The higher the t-scores, the more meaningful it is to providers who need to make referrals, based on clinical judgement regarding emotional and behavioral problems in children. Research has shown that there is very good agreement between CBCL scales and clinical diagnoses, especially for attention problems for ADHD, aggressive behavioral scale for oppositional defiant disorder, the rule-breaking scale for conduct disorder, the anxious/depressed for anxiety, and the withdrawn/depressed for depression [47].

For the composite Externalizing Problems scale, the t-score for a neurotypical child on the CBCL is 50, therefore, t-scores ≥ 65, can be indicative of meaningful emotional or behavioral problems. In our research, 9% of children in our study had a clinically meaningful score for Externalizing Problems. Our findings present increased rates, particularly for children who are in need of treatment living near coal-fired power plants, where access to care may be lacking for a multitude of factors. Externalizing behaviors are some of the costliest disorders to treat and often persist into adulthood [64–66]. Yet, due to provider shortage in the United States, many children are not treated as needed.

Interestingly our study indicated that using proximity to coal-fired power plants was not a good predictor of externalizing behavior outcomes The studies that have used proximity to coal-fired power plants may have had different results if they had investigated a child’s actual exposure. Further research is needed to more accurately characterize the dispersion of fly ash in the communities surrounding coal-fired power plants.

From a public health perspective, there is concern for populations exposed to fly ash. Throughout the world coal-fired power plants operate daily and continue to be constructed. In December 2015, the Paris Agreement was ratified by 195 countries of the United Nations to limit global warning by reducing CO2 emissions, which mostly arise from coal-fired power plants. The Agreement stipulated that coal-fired power plants throughout the world be retired by 2040. Countries in the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD), Eastern Europe, and the former Soviet Union were expected to phase out coal by 2031, followed by Latin America (2032), the Middle East and Africa (2034) and then non-OECD Asia by 2037 [67].

Globally, in 2016, coal power capacity (amount of electricity that a power plant can produce) was 1965.8 GW, but has increased 13% over time to 2174.8 GW. While countries in the European Union (EU) and North America are phasing out coal-fired power plants, developing countries, particularly China and India, are constructing coal-fired power plants to help with the continuing demand for power. In 2016, the coal power capacity in China was 922.1 gigawatts (GW), which increased to 1174.5 GW as of 2024. China has 421 GW coal power capacity under development, leading the world in construction of coal-fired power plants. In comparison, the United States of America, which has the third highest coal power capacity in the world has reduced from 286.6 GW in 2016 to 194.1 GW in 2024. To date, the USA is newly developing 0.8 GW [68].

Research has shown that in-home exposure to fly ash is associated with depression and problems in social competence in children [23, 24]. Although our research indicated that residential location to coal-fired power plants was not associated with externalizing behavior problems in children, other research has reported associations between residential proximity and poor birth outcomes, premature deaths, and poor respiratory function [69].

Fly ash is a public health threat. The composition, small size, and shape of particles in fly ash allow for ease of travel to the alveoli of the lungs, entering the blood stream, and impacting multiple organ systems throughout the body [6–12]. Although poor health outcomes among humans have been highlighted in research throughout the world, in most countries, fly ash is not considered a “hazardous waste” [8]. For example, in the US, fly ash falls under Subtitle D of the Environmental Protection Agency’s Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA), declaring it a “non-hazardous solid waste;” the same category as general household trash [8, 70]. China also does not consider it a “hazardous waste,” but classifies it as a “solid industrial waste” [8]. Since coal ash is not considered hazardous, the ability to develop and enforce regulations regarding the disposal and storage of coal ash is complicated, relying on multiple agencies and communities. Commonly stored in communities where environmental and social injustices interweave with lack of access to quality health care, populations residing near coal-fired power plants and coal ash are more likely to suffer from long-term health effects [71–75].

A major strength of this study is that this is the first to examine the association between fly ash exposure and externalizing behaviors in children using in-home exposure data collected in the vicinity of two coal-fired power plants. In-home measurements found that 43% of children had fly ash in their homes, which provided us the opportunity to explore externalizing behaviors. More robust epidemiological studies are needed to determine the true causal relationship between exposure to fly ash and externalizing behaviors. While a cross-sectional study design does not prove causality, it does allow for generating hypotheses for future research using more rigorous study designs that are longitudinal in nature.

Furthermore, this study was a community-based research effort conducted across 12 ZIP codes, a noteworthy strength that underscores its relevance and reach. We were able to gather a large sample for a community-based study that required parents/guardians and children to do multiple and a variety of different tasks. We were able to gather primary data of fly ash exposure in the homes of participants, whereas most other studies investigating exposures from coal-fired power plants relied on secondary pollution data or proxies for exposure. Another strength of this study is the use of the CBCL, a widely used behavior assessment tool for both researchers and clinicians that demonstrates excellent diagnostic accuracy and reliability [76, 77].

Research has demonstrated that air pollution is associated with increased oxidative stress and inflammation of the brain, both of which are contributing factors to the development of mental health disorders [78]. Fly ash which consists of small particles with neurotoxic metals can cross the blood brain barrier. Research has shown that fly ash contains concentrated levels of heavy metals known to be neurotoxic and may have the potential to contribute to externalizing behaviors, based on recent studies [38–41]. Since there are no studies on the impact of fly ash on externalizing behaviors, this research adds to a large gap in knowledge about fly ash.

Our study was originally powered to detect differences in neurobehavioral symptoms ranging from 20 to 30% in the exposed group and 5–10% in the unexposed group, with a planned sample size of 300 subjects [43]. Rates of clinically elevated externalizing problems were 12.7% in children exposed to fly ash and 5.8% in the unexposed group, below the anticipated prevalence in the exposed group for our power calculations. This coupled with our sample size being 10% below our original goal indicates our study may be underpowered to detect true underlying associations of interest. As such our results should be considered suggestive and hypothesis-generating in nature.

One limitation of this study is that fly ash exposure was only monitored in participants’ homes for a one-week period. Children may have been exposed to varying levels of fly ash in both indoor or outdoor settings such as schools and parks or playgrounds, thus, the data we collected may not fully reflect the total exposure of the children. Furthermore, the children had lifelong exposures not captured in our weeklong measurements. To address this limitation, we recruited participants who had lived in their current homes for two years. Notably, most (93%) of the children in our sample had only lived in one home, indicating that our measurements most likely reflect a history of the participants’ exposures to coal ash. Another potential limitation of this study was that the concentrations of PM10 may have been affected by seasonality. However, we attempted to recruit the same number of participants by season and when we assessed the difference of in-home PM10 concentrations and outdoor concentrations, there was not a significant difference of PM10 by season or year. Also, to mitigate this limitation, we adjusted our models for PM10 that was collected in the home during the sampling period. An additional limitation is the reliance on parent reports for assessing children’s externalizing behaviors which may have introduced biases. However, the CBCL, which is completed by parents/guardians, is highly predictive of various externalizing behaviors [46]. Finally, externalizing behaviors are a result of multiple factors and other confounding factors that we did not measure in this study. For example, this study did not assess the possible interactions between genetic and social/familial factors that other studies have suggested [31]. However, we did adjust for maternal education. Scholars have found that of all the components of socio-economic status maternal education has the strongest association with a child’s cognitive development. Furthermore, scholars report that mother’s education is a strong predictor of an unstable family [55–57].

An additional limitation of this research is the use of Euclidian distance as the measure of proximity to the coal-fired power plants. Emissions from coal-fired power plants are heavily influenced by wind characteristics, temperature, humidity, stack heights, and plume characteristics. There are models that can take into account concentration gradients impacted by these factors, however, since we had measured fly ash and PM10 concentrations and did not need gradients, we used a simple, but frequently used measure for power plant proximity. Further research is needed to investigate distance to coal-fired power plants using advanced geospatial methods and atmospheric modeling to better characterize exposure based on distance and environmental transport mechanisms.

Despite these limitations, the findings in this novel study help expand the existing literature by enhancing our understanding of the associations between fly ash from coal-fired power plants and externalizing behaviors in children. This has not been investigated by other researchers and provides hypotheses for researchers using more robust epidemiological studies that can highlight causal relationships. One important finding from our study is that we discovered that proximity to coal-fired power plants, using Euclidian distance as a proxy does not predict externalizing behaviors. This also can lead researchers from using ecological study designs or others based of proximity to recognize that more robust designs are needed to determine if a statistical relationship exists.

Conclusion

In our study of exposure to fly ash and externalizing behaviors, we found that based on parent/guardian report, the effect size of the Rule-Breaking Behavior, Conduct Problems, and the composite Externalizing Problems scales were greater in children with in-door fly ash on their filters, compared with those children who did not have fly ash on their filters. Although not statistically significant at p ≤ 0.05, these findings have clinical value to providers who may treat children with emotional and behavioral problems. Further, this cross-sectional study design can lead to hypotheses about exposure from coal-fired power plants and externalizing behaviors that can be better evaluated by a more robust epidemiological design.
