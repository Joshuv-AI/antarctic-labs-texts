Randomised controlled trial of a low plastic diet and lifestyle intervention for adults with cardiometabolic risk factors: the Plastic Exposure Reduction Transforms Health (PERTH) trial – a protocol
Authors: Andrew Lucas, Amelia Harray, Lelinh Duong, Susan Herrmann, Philip Vlaskovsky, Michelle Trevenen, Dick Chan, Hannah Papendorf, Tony Smith, Lilith Flint, Alex Liu, Silvana Gaudieri, Xianyu Wang, Jochen F Mueller, Kevin V Thomas, Kevin Murray, Christos Symeonides, Sarah Dunlop, Gerald Watts, Michaela Lucas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPhthalates and bisphenols, chemicals commonly used in the production of plastic products, exhibit endocrine disrupting properties linked to obesity and systemic inflammation. Given the ubiquitous use of plastic chemicals, their adverse impact on human health is of great importance. In this protocol, we describe a randomised controlled trial aimed at testing whether minimising exposure to plastics and plastic-associated chemicals (PACs) in community-dwelling adults with cardiometabolic risk factors can reduce urinary excretion of PACs and improve cardiometabolic health.Methods and analysisThe study will recruit (n=60) community-dwelling adults (18–60 years) with cardiometabolic risk factors, characterised by a body mass index of ≥30 kg/m2 and waist circumference of ≥88 cm in women and ≥102 cm in men. Participants will be randomised to a control (n=30) or an intervention group (n=30) receiving a 4-week diet and lifestyle modification designed to reduce plastic exposure, which includes the replacement of all food, kitchen utensils and equipment, personal care and cleaning products. The primary outcome is a reduction in urinary excretion of bisphenols after the 4-week intervention compared with the control arm. The secondary outcomes are the reduction in urinary excretion of low and high molecular weight phthalates. Finally, tertiary outcomes investigate improvements to cardiometabolic biomarkers, body composition, waist circumference and blood pressure. Participants will self-collect urine, stool and nasal lavage samples a day before beginning the intervention and at the end of each week. Fasting blood samples and health assessments will be collected during clinic visits: at baseline, mid-point and a day after the intervention period. Urinary PAC excretion and cardiometabolic health outcomes will be compared between the intervention and control groups.Ethics and disseminationThe PERTH Trial has ethics approval from the University of Western Australia Human Research Ethics Committee; 2021-ET001118. Results will be submitted for publication in peer-reviewed journals and presented at conferences.Trial registration numberNCT06571994.Keywords: Cardiovascular Disease, Clinical Trial, NUTRITION & DIETETICS, Obesity, Randomized Controlled Trial, PUBLIC HEALTH

Introduction

Bisphenols and phthalates are classes of plastic associated chemicals (PACs) that disrupt endocrine function by interfering with the activity of nuclear and steroidal hormone receptors.1 These widespread environmental toxins are linked to human infertility, autoimmune and metabolic conditions, among others.2 3 However, the full extent of their pathobiological impact is not known.4 Furthermore, whether endocrine disruption can be reversed by reducing exposure to plastics and PAC remains to be demonstrated.

Plastics are synthetic and semisynthetic materials, comprising high molecular weight polymeric molecules, with added chemicals used in processes of plastic or that alter the properties of the plastic. Bisphenol A (BPA) is used extensively as a co-monomer ‘building block’ in the production of polycarbonate plastics and epoxy and vinyl ester resins.5 Plastics containing BPA are commonly used in consumer products, such as the lining of food tins and in plastic food packaging, and BPA that is present as unreacted monomer or from degradation can leach out.6 Human exposure to BPA is widespread, and levels found in the general population (0.2–20 ng/mL) are associated with multiple adverse health effects.47,10 Another class of PAC is ortho-phthalate diesters, used as plasticisers in plastics, but also in other applications such as solvents in personal care products.11,14 Phthalate exposure is associated with adverse health effects, such as metabolic syndrome, obesity and insulin resistance.15,17 The excretion of phthalate metabolites and bisphenols is commonly in urine, with half-lives in the order of hours; however, there is evidence that BPA may be taken up and accumulate in adipose tissue,18,20 which could potentially increase long-term exposure and adverse effects.

Cardiometabolic syndrome encompasses a range of interrelated conditions that increase the risk of cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes, both of which are significant contributors to the global disease burden.21 Excess and dysfunctional adipose tissue can secrete inflammatory molecules, leading to systemic inflammation, insulin resistance and impaired glucose tolerance, which contributes to cardiometabolic syndrome.22 23 Thus, cardiometabolic risk factors include obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m2), abdominal obesity (≥88 cm in women and ≥102 cm in men), dysglycaemia, dyslipidaemia and arterial hypertension. Many of these factors are positively associated with increased exposure to BPA and/or phthalates in observational research.324,27 However, to better understand the causal pathways between PAC exposure and cardiometabolic health, a controlled intervention study is necessary.

Exposure to BPA and phthalates occurs most commonly through the oral consumption of food contaminated by PAC during farming, processing, packaging, storage or preparation, as well as through airborne particles and direct contact with personal care and cleaning products.28,36 Short dietary or hygiene interventions have been shown to be effective in decreasing exposure to BPA and/or phthalates, as evidenced by reductions in the urinary excretion of these chemicals.37,45 Our recently completed observational study and a 7-day pilot randomised controlled trial (RCT) of a low plastic diet and lifestyle intervention in healthy adults further demonstrate this approach can reduce urinary PAC (manuscript submitted for publication). However, whether this can be achieved in an adult population with cardiometabolic risk factors is yet to be determined. Furthermore, linking decreased PAC exposure and excretion to health outcomes is a current challenge due to the uncertainty regarding the metabolism and potential sequestration of PAC in tissue.46 To address this, the Plastic Exposure Reduction Transforms Health (PERTH) trial will implement a 4-week low plastic diet and lifestyle intervention to reduce PAC exposure in adults with cardiometabolic risk factors, extending the duration from our previous RCT to better assess potential improvements in cardiometabolic biomarkers.

Aims and hypotheses

The PERTH Trial aims to determine whether decreasing plastic exposure in adults with cardiometabolic risk factors will: (1) reduce excretion of urinary bisphenols, (2) reduce excretion of urinary phthalate metabolites and (3) improve cardiometabolic biomarkers. This will involve complete food, kitchenware, personal care and cleaning product replacement over a 4-week intervention period, accompanied by instructions for plastic-free food preparation and lifestyle practices to minimise plastic exposure. A control group with no changes to diet or lifestyle will serve as a comparator. We hypothesise that, after 4 weeks, participants receiving the low plastic diet and lifestyle intervention will show reduced excretion of urinary PAC, such as bisphenols and phthalate metabolites, and improved cardiometabolic biomarkers compared with the control group.

Methods

Trial design

The PERTH Trial is a single site, community-based RCT conducted in Perth, Western Australia. 60 participants will be randomised into two parallel groups, control (n=30) and intervention (n=30), which will receive an at-home intervention for four consecutive weeks (figure 1). The current trial builds on our completed observational study (n=211) (ACTRN12622001259730; prospectively registered on 20 September 2022) and 7-day pilot RCT (n=60) (ACTRN12622001252707; prospectively registered on 19 September 2022), both registered with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry.

Study setting

Volunteers will attend the clinic at the Harry Perkins Institute of Medical Research in Perth, Western Australia for eligibility screening and study procedures, including fasting blood collections for assessments. Guided self-collection of other biological samples, such as urine, stool and nasal rinses will take place in participants’ homes, and a screening interview will be conducted by video call (online supplemental material 1).

Recruitment

Social media, radio and print media advertisements will be used to promote the study across the Perth Metropolitan Area. Posters and flyers will be distributed in general practices, private health clinics, shopping centres, cafes and the University of Western Australia campus. Interested persons will contact the research team either through the contact information available on the project webpage or by completing the electronic Expression of Interest form. Volunteers meeting the initial criteria will receive the participant information and consent form (online supplemental material 2).

Screening

Following electronic consent, the research team will contact the participant via phone to discuss study requirements in more detail and arrange their first clinic visit. Participants will attend the research clinic (timepoint (T)1) for a full health assessment, including an ECG and vital signs, performed by the study doctor or nurse. Results will be reviewed by the study doctor to confirm eligibility (box 1). Additionally, volunteers will receive a body composition analysis using a portable bioimpedance machine (Seca m525; Seca, Hamburg, Germany) and will be referred to their local pathology provider for further blood tests in the fasting state (T2). If participants meet the inclusion criteria, a video call appointment (T4) will be scheduled, during which eligible and willing participants will be invited to continue to the intervention study (figure 1).

Randomisation and blinding

Six eligible participants per week will be randomised using the automated randomisation module in REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture),47 48 with a 1:1 allocation ratio. Allocation tables consisting of computer-generated random blocks of participants, unknown to the clinical team, will be used. Participants cannot be blinded to the low plastic food preparation practices inside and outside the home. Similarly, study investigators are not blinded as they will interview and provide instructions to participants receiving the intervention. However, the statisticians and laboratory personnel involved in the analysis of the biological samples will be blinded to participant status. Samples will be identified by a randomly generated trial number and will be sent for analysis in batches of both control and intervention samples.

Low plastic diet and lifestyle intervention group

Participants allocated to the low plastics intervention group will be given replacement food, food preparation equipment, utensils, personal care and cleaning products for the entire intervention period to maximise adherence. This group will receive food deliveries from an approved provider and be asked to consume this food exclusively over the intervention period. Participants will be encouraged to eat to appetite to maintain isocaloric intake. Once randomised, participants in the intervention group will select meals, snacks and beverages from a list of provided foods and ingredients. The farmers and food producers of each ingredient will undergo standardised and indepth screening by Accredited Practising Dietitians to identify and minimise all potential plastic contamination (online supplemental material 3). The participants’ food orders will be sent to an approved meal delivery service, who will receive, pack and deliver the foods and beverages to participants’ homes using plastic-free packaging, storage and transport. Participants will receive two deliveries of food every week of the 4-week intervention to ensure an adequate supply of foods and beverages.

In addition to complete food replacement, participants will be provided with plastic-free or low plastic cooking and food preparation appliances and utensils, including stainless steel pots and pans, glass cooking dishes and storage containers, wooden chopping boards, metal colanders, metal toasters, coffee plungers and kettles. Participants will receive both verbal and written instructions (online supplemental material 4) on how to handle and prepare food in their kitchen to minimise plastic exposure, such as lining refrigerator compartments with provided paper towels or not using their dishwasher and handwashing with the plastic-free dishwashing bar provided.

The intervention group will also be provided with replacement personal care products (PCP) and cleaning products to use exclusively during the 4-week intervention. All products will undergo a similar standardised screening process to minimise plastic exposure. The PCP will include shampoos, conditioners, soap, face moisturisers, cleansers, sunscreens, deodorant, shaving products, toothbrushes, toothpowder, dental floss and period products. Participants will be asked to avoid cosmetics and perfume for the duration of the intervention. Replacement cleaning products will include multipurpose spray, window cleaner, floor cleaner, toilet cleaner, hand soap and cloths. Participants will be asked to avoid using pest control products or scented candles during the intervention. Samples of each food, beverage, PCP and cleaning products will be obtained and frozen in glass jars for future PAC analysis.

Control group

The control group will not be provided food, kitchen equipment, PCP or cleaning products and will be asked to maintain their current behaviours. The control group will undergo the same assessments as the intervention group.

Retention and compensation

To minimise loss to follow-up, participants will be provided with a comprehensive schedule with reminders of clinic visits and biological sample collections. Participants will be encouraged to discuss any difficulties and concerns they may have with the research team. Additionally, participants will receive supermarket vouchers and reimbursements for parking for each clinic visit. All participants will be offered a free consultation with an accredited practising dietitian at the end of the trial.

Health measurements

Body composition (fat mass, fat free mass, visceral adipose tissue, skeletal muscle mass) will be measured using the portable Seca m525 body composition analyser. Anthropometry will be assessed using scales, a stadiometer and non-stretchable tape measure to obtain height, weight, waist and hip circumference measurements. Vital signs, including systolic/diastolic blood pressure, pulse, body temperature and respiratory rate, will also be collected. All assessments will be conducted by a trained study nurse. Physical activity levels will be assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form50 by a member of the research team (online supplemental material 5).

Urine, nasal lavage and stool samples

Participants will be provided with instructions on how to self-collect urine, nasal lavage and stool samples. Members of the research team will assist with any questions or difficulties participants experience with collection of these biological samples. As urine is the standard biological matrix for measuring PAC excretion, participants will collect spot urine (×3) between the hours of 06:00–12:00, 12:00–18:00 and 18:00–24:00. Samples will be stored in polypropylene containers. These samples will be frozen and delivered to the laboratory the following day. Once received by the study laboratory, the urine samples will be thawed and measured individually for creatinine concentrations to allow for the normalisation of PAC measures obtained from different time points. Each daily sample or multiple daily samples will be subsampled into glass tubes for long-term storage at −80°C at the study laboratory, and equal volumes of individual daily samples will be subsampled and pooled. Aliquots of this will be sent in batches to the Queensland Alliance for Environmental Health Sciences (QAEHS) Laboratory for quantification of urinary PAC (ng/mL) by mass spectrometry. The QAEHS is a purpose-built facility, equipped with a specialised plastics and additive-minimised clean room laboratory, designed to reduce background contamination of microsized and nanosized plastics and PAC by over 100 times compared with standard physical containment level-2 laboratories in the same building.51 QAEHS routinely participates in interlaboratory assessments, including the German External Quality Assessment Scheme, where their analytical methods for PAC including bisphenols and phthalate metabolites in human urine specimens are validated and certified. Nasal lavage52 is obtained by flushing the nares using a syringe containing isotonic solution. Nasal secretions and stool samples will be collected into glass containers. These samples will be retained at −80°C for future measurements of PAC, microplastic and nanoplastic content. Blanks will be established for all biological samples to control for leaching of PAC from the collection tubes.

Fasting blood samples

Fasting blood (80–90 mL) will be collected into standard polyethylene terephthalate Vacutainer SST and EDTA tubes, as well as glass Vacutainer acid citric dextrose tubes (all from Beckton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA). Aliquots of the collected blood will be sent to Sonic Healthcare Pathology for routine blood tests, including cardiometabolic and inflammatory biomarkers. Additionally, a portion of the blood will be sent to the Institute for Immunology and Infectious Diseases (IIID) at Murdoch University, for peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC), plasma and serum separation, as well as DNA and RNA extraction. Aliquots of liquid samples will be stored at −80°C and PBMC will be cryopreserved in vapour phase liquid nitrogen. Both Sonic Healthcare Pathology and IIID are National Association of Testing Authorities of Australia accredited laboratories. Non-routine tests, such as adiponectin and leptin, will be performed on serum or plasma samples in the study laboratory using enzyme linked immunosorbent assays based on established protocols. Stored samples will be used for post hoc exploratory analysis of inflammatory and other putative biomarkers, including gene expression and epigenetic modulations, sensitive to changes in PAC exposure.

Plastic exposure assessment tools

To assess exposure to plastics and monitor adherence to the protocol during the trial, three electronic questionnaires will be administered throughout the study; (1) the PACeQ; (2) the 24DR-PE49 and (3) the 24HR-PPR.

The Plastic-Associated Chemicals exposure Questionnaire (PACeQ)

The PACeQ is an 87-item self-administered questionnaire that was developed for the trial using a modified Delphi process with national and interventional experts (online supplemental materials 6 and 7). The PACeQ identifies behaviours among participants that may lead to plastic exposure. The instrument framework consists of three constructs: ingestion, absorption and inhalation, recognising that phthalates, bisphenols and microplastics have entry to the human organism by those routes. The PACeQ will be completed by participants on four occasions throughout the trial. This questionnaire takes 15–20 min to complete.

The 24-hour dietary recall – Plastic Exposure (24DR-PE)

The 24DR-PE will be used to assess dietary intake and associated plastic exposure over the preceding 24 hours.49,53 This tool is computer-assisted, dietitian-administered and is a modified version based on the validated automated multiple pass method, widely used in intervention and nutrition epidemiological research.54,56 We have adapted it to collect additional food exposures and dietary behaviours that could expose food to plastic, for example, how food and beverages are packaged, stored, prepared, cooked and consumed. The development of this tool is described by our research team.49 The 24DR-PE takes 45–60 min to complete, and this data will be piped into a nutrient analysis programme using an Australian food composition database to calculate energy, fibre, macronutrient and micronutrient intake throughout the trial.

The 24-hour personal care and cleaning product recall

The 24HR-PPR questionnaire was developed to monitor use of personal care and cleaning products for the preceding 24-hours and adherence to the personal care and cleaning products intervention (online supplemental material 8). The 24HR-PPR consists of 17 questions and takes 5 min to complete.

Post-trial tolerability and feasibility qualitative interview

Participants will be invited to take part in a short phone interview within 2 weeks post intervention to assess the tolerability and feasibility of the trial intervention. Trained interviewers will conduct the interviews using a guide consisting of a series of closed and open-ended questions. The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research criteria57 will be followed for data analysis and reporting. Each interview is expected to take 20–40 min to complete.

Outcomes

To determine the effectiveness of the low plastic intervention, the primary outcome is the reduction in urinary bisphenols (composite of BPA; and bisphenol S, BPS) at 4 weeks from baseline, comparing participants in the intervention and control groups.

In our previous studies of healthy adults, BPA was detected in over 91% of urine samples. Though BPA had a low intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.317, indicating greater within-individual variability, it had a large estimated effect size in response to the intervention observed in our 7-day pilot RCT (manuscript submitted for publication). This suggests that, although BPA baseline variability is higher, it may be more responsive to changes induced by the intervention. Similarly, BPS exhibited moderate within-individual variability (ICC=0.488) and a large estimated effect size. Bisphenol F, however, was not detected with sufficient frequency. To construct a meaningful, single primary outcome, we will opt for a composite, z-score like measure, calculated as the mean of the constituent bisphenol PAC z-scores, each calculated based on the corresponding reference distribution in the observational cohort.

Secondary outcomes are reductions in urinary excretion of BPA and BPS, individually, and low molecular weight (mono-ethyl phthalate; mono-n-butyl-phthalate; mono-(3-carboxypropyl) phthalate; mono-iso-butyl phthalate; mono-benzyl phthalate) and high molecular weight (mono-2-ethylhexyl phthalate; mono-(2-ethyl-5-oxohexyl) phthalate; mono-(2-ethyl-5-hydroxyhexyl) phthalate; and mono-(2-ethyl-5-carboxypentyl) phthalate) phthalate metabolites. Urinary creatinine and specific gravity will be measured for all pooled samples to correct for dilution differences. Tertiary outcomes include improvements in cardiometabolic biomarkers and health parameters. The list of measurable outcomes is shown in table 2.

Sample size calculation

No studies to date, to the best of our knowledge, have examined the effect of a low plastic diet and lifestyle intervention on urinary PAC excretion for this duration in the community. To estimate the expected reduction in PAC over 4 weeks, we used results generated from our previous observational study (ACTRN12622001259730) and 7-day low plastic diet and lifestyle pilot RCT (ACTRN12622001252707) on healthy adults (manuscript submitted for publication). A composite z-score measure of bisphenols (BPA and BPS) was used in a simulation-based power analysis using the R simr package58 with 1000 simulations. We assumed a medium standardised effect size of −0.6, based on reductions achieved in the pilot RCT. Results from our simulations indicate that a sample size of 30 participants per group would provide 80% power to detect the target effect size of −0.6 in the primary outcome at the 5% level of significance. We expect the power to detect the effects of interest to be greater than what is estimated due to the extended amount of time of the intervention and our selected study population, which should allow for a modest dropout rate of 5%.

Statistical analyses

Primary, secondary and tertiary outcomes will be summarised for each group depending on the measurement scale and data distribution (frequencies, means, SD, medians, IQRs, 95% CIs). To assess the intervention’s effectiveness, parametric (or non-parametric, depending on the data distribution) tests will be used for the groupwise comparisons after the fourth week (post intervention); estimated mean differences between groups and 95% CIs will be reported along with relevant p values. Linear mixed models will be used to model the urinary PAC excretions and cardiometabolic health outcomes with time (baseline to post intervention), group (treatment and control) and time by group interaction as fixed effects and participant ID as a random effect. Model assumptions will be tested and appropriate transformations employed if and where necessary. Analysis will be conducted with an intention to treat approach and a sensitivity analysis of non-ignorable missing data will be conducted as necessary. Statistical significance will be considered at p value < 0.05.

Project management software will be employed for document control, and REDCap will be used as a secure, web-based software platform supporting direct data entry using computer tablets. A record of protocol deviations will be kept, and the REDCap software will be employed to provide audit trails for data manipulation and data export tracking.47 48

The SampleManager Laboratory Information Management System (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) will be used to keep a record of samples collected, processed and their storage locations. Samples that may include genetic information will be treated as identifying data and will be stored securely. Access to data will only be granted to members of the research team based on authority from the trial manager.

A trial steering committee, consisting of the principal investigators, representatives of the funder and independent experts, meets every 4 months and provides trial oversight, support and recommendations as necessary. The trial management team, consisting of the principal investigator, clinical lead, laboratory lead and project manager, will maintain and oversee the trial by meeting monthly.

The trial may be suspended temporarily or terminated if there is reasonable cause. In such cases, the sponsor and investigators will promptly notify trial participants, the human research ethics committee (HREC), regulatory bodies and, if applicable, funding sources, explaining the reasons for the suspension or termination. Potential reasons for such actions include, but are not limited to, identification of an unexpected, significant or unacceptable risk to participants that constitutes a major safety issue; non-compliance with protocol requirements; incomplete or inadequate data or a determination of futility. If safety, protocol adherence or data quality concerns arise, the trial may be resumed once these issues are addressed to the satisfaction of the sponsor, HREC, funding sources and/or regulatory bodies.

The principal investigator will appoint an independent data safety monitoring board. Electronic data monitoring for quality and completeness will occur at regular intervals by an independent monitor. An independent statistician will review the data at predetermined intervals. Discussion

Scientific evidence demonstrates negative and direct effects of plastic contamination on our environment and human health.3 61 However, with few exceptions,62 the mechanisms by which PACs cause human diseases are unknown. There are few published intervention studies in humans, but most show that decreased exposure to either BPA or BPA and phthalates via a dietary or ‘exposure withdrawal’ intervention results in decreased excretion of those chemicals.3740,45 While this provides evidence that our approach is feasible, the effectiveness of this exposure withdrawal, especially for longer periods, as a means of affecting health outcomes has yet to be investigated.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to assess a 4-week low plastic diet and lifestyle intervention in people with cardiometabolic risk factors. We aim to determine whether such an intervention can significantly reduce urinary excretion of PAC metabolites over this timeframe. By focusing on individuals with cardiometabolic risk factors, our study is designed to test the effects of exposure withdrawal on cardiometabolic health outcomes.

As an RCT, the design of our study addresses some limitations of earlier studies. One possible limitation of this study is that, despite instructions to maintain isocaloric food intake, participants in the low plastic intervention group may unintentionally lose weight due to the substitution of their usual diet with minimally processed foods, higher in dietary fibre and antioxidants and lower in saturated fat and sodium, which could lead to improvements in cardiometabolic health.63,65 A change in daily energy intake during our 7-day pilot RCT (ACTRN12622001252707) with the same foods was not observed in the intervention or control groups (manuscript submitted for publication). Several studies have identified ultraprocessed food as a significant source of PAC,66,70 and controlling for this in the trial would require supplementation of PAC in a placebo group. This approach raises ethical concerns and therefore, we argue that a holistic approach of reducing plastic and PAC exposure, alongside a diet rich in minimally processed foods, is a more appropriate strategy for improving cardiometabolic health. Another limitation is that the study investigators and participants cannot be blinded due to the former being involved in educating the participants around low plastic food practices and the latter having to employ such practices inside and outside the home. However, the statisticians and laboratory personnel performing biological sample analysis will be blinded to the status of the participants.

Overall, the findings from this study are expected to aid in determining the efficacy of low PAC exposure as a means of reducing risk factors for cardiometabolic disease.22,24
