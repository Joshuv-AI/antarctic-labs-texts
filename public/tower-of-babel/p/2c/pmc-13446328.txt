Quantifying the Contributions of Food, Glucose, Sleep, and Microbiome Data to Personalized Glycemic Response Prediction
Authors: Yiheng Shen, Euiji Choi, Samantha Kleinberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndividual glycemic responses to foods vary and can be predicted using microbiome, activity, and dietary data. However, these data are expensive and invasive to collect, and it is not known how much each modality contributes to accuracy.ObjectivesWe aim to quantify the contributions of dietary, sleep, continuous glucose monitor (CGM), and microbiome features for glycemic response prediction; understand how much personal data are required for training; and evaluate how microbiome sample timing impacts model accuracy.MethodsWe used data from 8334 participants in the Human Phenotype Project cohort study who provided demographic, anthropometric, dietary, and CGM data. Participants self-reported meals in a dietary tracking application for a mean of 10.78 d, during which they wore CGMs. We trained CatBoost models to predict postprandial glycemic response (PPGR) using 2-h incremental area under the curve and peak 2-h postprandial glucose rise (Glumax). We conducted ablation studies with varied feature combinations to assess the contribution of each data modality. We used 3 train/test splits (split-by-meal, 5-d personal training, and split-by-person) to assess the impact of personal training data. Lastly, we evaluated accuracy as a function of microbiome sample timing (from before meal logs to ≤60 d after).ResultsThe model combining all features performed best, and CGM was the most informative feature. Models trained with more personal data had the best performance (PPGR split-by-meal R = 0.731; split-by-person R = 0.590), and personal training data had a larger effect on accuracy than microbiome. Microbiome features improved predictions most when collected within 7 d of meal logs and did not improve performance without personal training data or for samples collected >14 d after meal logs.ConclusionsAlthough CGM was the most important feature group, combining it with personal training data and timely microbiome samples led to the most accurate models in our analysis. These findings can help researchers understand the tradeoffs between the time and effort of data collection and how data types impact model performance.Keywords: personalized nutrition, postprandial glycemic response, gut microbiome, machine learning, feature selection

Introduction

Postprandial blood glucose management is important for maintaining metabolic health [1] and preventing chronic diseases such as type 2 diabetes (T2D) and cardiovascular disease [2]. In individuals with diabetes and prediabetes, postprandial glucose control is important for overall glycemic management and contributes to long-term outcomes such as hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) [3]. Even for individuals without diabetes, higher postprandial glucose excursions are associated with the increased risk of cardiometabolic disease and mortality [4]. However, because glycemic responses to specific foods vary significantly between individuals [5], population-level guidance may not be fully optimal [6]. Precision nutrition thus aims to deliver personalized dietary guidance mainly by predicting individual glycemic responses to meals, which can then inform dietary recommendations [7]. Randomized controlled trials have shown that personalized diets can improve HbA1c in individuals with T2D [8], prediabetes [9], and adults generally [10]. These findings suggest that improving the prediction of glycemic responses could support clinically relevant dietary interventions and improve metabolic control. Despite this, personalized nutrition has yet to be translated widely into practice.

At the core of personalized nutrition is machine learning to predict postprandial glycemic response (PPGR) based on physiologic data collected from an individual. Zeevi et al. [5] were the first to show that PPGR [defined as 2-h incremental area under the curve (iAUC) of glucose] could be predicted from gut microbiome, anthropometrics, physical activity, and clinical data. In that work, the correlation between actual and predicted PPGR was R = 0.70. Many other works since then have aimed to predict glycemic responses, showing that models with microbiome and other features such as activity and sleep generally outperform models based only on carbohydrates or energy of a meal [11,12]. For example, in a study of pregnant females, PPGR predicted from a model that included microbiome data had R = 0.72, whereas a model using only carbohydrates had R = 0.51 [13]. Tily et al. [14] achieved the highest accuracy so far (R = 0.80) using macronutrients, anthropometrics, sleep, activity, and microbiome features in a cohort of 550 people without diabetes. This value is close to the theoretical maximum for R, based on the intraindividual correlation in responses to the same foods [5]. However, other works have achieved high accuracy without microbiome data [15,16], and none have quantified the relative contribution of each modality.

Although these studies have predicted glycemic responses and thus laid the foundation for personalized nutrition, we still lack the foundational understanding needed to translate prediction to practice. Given the financial cost and participant burden added by each additional data modality [17], it is critical to understand the tradeoff in accuracy if some data types are omitted. Second, prior works have tested generalizability to new populations (e.g., from an Israeli cohort to a Midwestern United States one [11]) and found that personal data improves models, but they have not formally tested differing quantities of personal data. Yet, meal logging is burdensome for participants [18], making it important to understand how much data are truly needed. Finally, in prior works, the gut microbiome has been measured at a single timepoint, but gut microbiota composition changes over time [19] and in response to diet [20]. Thus, it remains an open question whether a single sample remains predictive in the long term, or whether regular samples will be needed, which is critical for translation to practice.

We aim to advance the science and translation of personalized nutrition by leveraging a large multimodal dataset with 8334 participants to provide, to our knowledge, the first systematic examination of 1) the contribution of each data modality to glycemic response prediction, 2) how much personalized training data are needed for high accuracy, and 3) whether the predictive value of microbiome data decreases over time.

Methods

We leveraged an existing prospective cohort study, the Human Phenotype Project (HPP), which contains multiple data modalities relevant to precision nutrition. Using these data, we conducted experiments to examine the information value of different data modalities, amounts of training data, and sample timing.

Data

We used data from the HPP [21], an ongoing large-scale observational study that aims to phenotype adults worldwide and currently contains data collected in Israel. Participants in the HPP were recruited via the study website (http://project10k.org.il/en) and screened for the presence of exclusion criteria: pregnancy or current fertility treatment, >3 hospitalizations in the last year, cardiovascular disease, chronic neurologic or psychiatric diagnosis, chronic respiratory disease, chronic kidney disease, chronic gastrointestinal disease, chronic metabolic diseases, antibiotic use in the past 3 mo, chronic antibiotic use, weight loss >5% in the last 12 mo without dieting, and previous or active malignancy [21]. As a result, the population may be healthier than the general population.

The current dataset includes participants aged 28 to 82 y who provided basic demographic information (gender, age), anthropometric measurements (BMI, hip circumference, and waist circumference measured by HPP), and meal logs. Meals were self-reported with meal time, food item, and size in grams using a mobile application within 30 min after meals. Because of the nature of self-reported data, it is possible that meals were misreported or omitted. Given the purpose of the study, we used all logged meals and did not remove any days or meals because of unusual reporting. As shown in Figure 1, a subset of 8334 participants had CGM data (mg/dL) collected using the FreeStyle Libre Pro iQ CGM (Abbott) (15-min sampling rate) for a mean of 10.78 d (SD = 3.26, range = 1–27 d). Within this subgroup, 7040 participants had stool samples collected for microbiome analysis, and taxonomic profiles were generated from shotgun metagenomic sequencing data using MetaPhlAn 4 [22]. Of these participants, 5735 provided sleep data.

Demographics

The demographics feature group consisted of age and gender, where age was calculated based on the recorded date of birth and date of each meal log.

Anthropometrics

Anthropometric features were BMI (kg/m2) and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), which is calculated as the waist circumference divided by the hip circumference.

CGM features

For CGM data, we followed the same processing approach as in our prior work on glycemic response prediction in people with diabetes [15]. Thus, we excluded meals if there was a continuous CGM gap of ≥30 min during the 30 min before and 120 min after the meal start time. Gaps <30 min were filled using linear interpolation [23]. CGM features consisted of base CGM value defined as the median CGM value during the 30 min before a meal; and premeal iAUC and glucose trend (slope relative to the base CGM value) over the 1-h, 2-h, and 4-h intervals preceding each meal [6]. In total, there were 7 CGM features for each meal.

Meal composition

Meal logs in the HPP include nutrients, energy, and location (home or restaurant). From this, we constructed the following features: carbohydrate (g), fat (g), protein (g), fiber (g), and the caloric ratios: carbohydrate-to-fat ratio, carbohydrate-to-protein ratio, and protein-to-fat ratio based on 9 kcal/g fat, 4 kcal/g for protein, and 4 kcal/g for carbohydrate [24]. Meal categories (breakfast, lunch, and dinner) were determined following the same rules as Shen et al. [15]: 3:00 to 11:00 for breakfast, 11:00 to 16:00 for lunch, and 16:00 to 3:00 for dinner. A binary indicator was used to denote whether a given meal was consumed in a restaurant setting. In total, there were 9 composition features per meal.

Prior dietary intake

To capture residual effects of earlier meals on glycemic response, we computed the total carbohydrates, energy, and fiber consumed in the 3, 6, and 12 h before the meal, leading to a total of 9 prior intake features per meal [5].

Food features

In prior work, we showed that even after including nutrient features, food names themselves contributed significantly to predicting PPGR [15]. To transform the thousands of unique foods in large-scale datasets into features, we used the hierarchical information criterion (HIC) [25], which uses the hierarchical nature of food databases and determines the optimal level of abstraction, such as selecting alcoholic beverages rather than beer, wine, and cocktails individually. Foods in the HPP dataset have a 3-level hierarchy with food category, short food name, and product name (e.g., drinks, coffee, and instant coffee, respectively) (see Figure 3). Following Shen et al. [15], we selected the top 20 food features for each glycemic response within each training fold using the 5 d of training and rest for testing setting.

A subset of the hierarchical food structure in HPP and the feature selection based on the HIC values on PPGR. HIC, hierarchical information criterion; HPP, Human Phenotype Project; PPGR, postprandial glycemic response.

HIC is based on mutual information between features and outcomes (here, foods and glycemic responses) and considers the number of samples of a feature and its level, with a preference for more general (higher) levels. Equation 1 is the mutual information formula, where yˆ is the predicted glycemic response, x is the food code, and x1,x2, and x3 are covariates (age, gender, and x’s quantity in grams).

HIC is defined in Equation (2), where β1 is obtained from Equation (1), and the subsequent terms represent the level (levelx is x’s level in the hierarchy and maxLevelx is the length of its branch, which is 3 for all items in this dataset), branch and tree z-score thresholds (zb, and zt), and their corresponding weights (branch weight wb and tree wt) that are used to weight tree-level statistics more for larger samples and branch-level statistics more for smaller ones, and sample size adjustment (N, number of instances of x in a dataset).

For example, drinks is a high-level food category, and instant coffee is a more specific item within the drinks category. Suppose instant coffee has a larger coefficient than drinks (β1= 0.8 and 0.45, respectively), but there are few meals that include it. The final HIC score for instant coffee may thus be reduced after accounting for the other terms, as follows: 0.8 (β1) – 0.2 (hierarchy penalty) – 0.15 (branch penalty) – 0.1 (tree penalty) + 0.05 (sample size) = 0.40. In contrast, drinks may have a higher score with a larger sample size and lower penalties, as follows: 0.45 (β1) – 0.1 (hierarchy penalty) – 0.05 (branch penalty) – 0.03 (tree penalty) + 0.25 (sample size) = 0.52. Therefore, HIC would select drinks instead of instant coffee.

Microbiome features

Microbiome features were derived based on prior literature linking gut microbiota composition or functional potential with host metabolic outcomes: 1) diversity indexes: Shannon index (within-sample richness + evenness) [12], and Simpson’s index (within-sample dominance) [26]; 2) functional proxies derived from taxonomic profiles [14], which have been associated with microbial metabolic activities relevant to short-chain fatty acid production and insulin sensitivity: propionate-producing bacteria [27], fucose metabolism score [28], and glutamine-producing bacteria [29]; 3) firmicutes-to-bacteroidetes ratio, a commonly reported but coarse summary measure of microbiome composition [12,30]; 4) microbiome balance score, a composite feature previously proposed for glycemic response prediction [14]; and 5) the 10 most prevalent genera based on relative abundance [14]. Because many taxa were rare across participants, including a large number of genera could substantially increase dimensionality without improving performance. To assess whether more taxa features and higher-resolution microbiome representations improved prediction accuracy, we conducted sensitivity analyses using the top 10 prevalent species, genus-level taxa present in ≥10% of participants, and species-level taxa present in ≥10% of participants.

Microbiome features were calculated at the participant level. When individuals had multiple microbiome samples, we used the one closest in time to the meal data for all experiments except the one specifically examining microbiome timing.

Sleep features

We derived 3 sleep features based on those used in prior work on sleep and glycemic response: sleep duration [31], deep sleep duration [31], and apnea–hypopnea index [32].

Predictive models and outcomes

We used CatBoost models in Python to predict 2 glycemic responses for all participants with CGM data: PPGR, the iAUC in the 2 h following a meal, and Glumax, the difference between glucose at meal onset and maximum glucose in the following 2 h (also called maximum glucose rise). We conducted hyperparameter tuning using scikit-learn, which selected 1000 iterations and a learning rate of 0.1. L2 Leaf Regularization was used to prevent overfitting (l2_leaf_reg = 5). For features with different amounts of availability (7040 of 8334 participants had microbiome, and 5735 of 8334 participants had sleep features), CatBoost handles missing values natively by learning how to route missing entries during tree construction, so we did not impute missing values. We use Pearson’s R and R2 to evaluate the relationship between predicted and actual glycemic responses, pooling results across all meals. We compared results across models for the same training split using paired 2-tailed t tests.

We used SHapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP) values [33] to examine which microbiome features contributed the most to glycemic response prediction across microbiome sampling windows.

Experiments

Our experiments aimed to answer the following 3 key research questions: how much does each feature group contribute to the accuracy of PPGR prediction, how does accuracy vary as a function of personal training data, and how does microbiome timing affect accuracy.

Feature contribution

First, we aimed to systematically examine how much each feature group contributes to performance both alone and in combination with other feature groups. To do this, we developed a base model (with demographics, anthropometrics, base CGM, and meal composition) and used this set of features as the base to which others were added. Because the base model contains the features used in Shen et al. [15] plus the waist-to-hip ratio, we additionally tested the model without that feature. Testing the addition of a single feature group, we used the base model plus food, microbiome, sleep, prior dietary intake, and CGM features (except base CGM, as it is in the base model), leading to 5 models, where each is base + 1 feature group. Testing the addition of 2 features, we used all permutations of food, microbiome, CGM, and prior dietary intake (leading to 6 models where each is base + 2 feature groups). We omitted sleep from further tests to reduce computational complexity because it contributed the least in the single feature group. To test additions of 3 features, we used all permutations of the features used in the 2-feature experiment (food, microbiome, CGM, and prior dietary intake), leading to 4 models where each is base + 3 feature groups. Finally, we tested the base model plus all 4 features. In total, there were 18 models developed for each glycemic response (PPGR, and Glumax).

Personalized training data

To evaluate how personalized training data influences the accuracy of predictions, we used 3 train-test splits: 1) 10-fold cross-validation (CV) split-by-person, 2) 5 d of data for training and the rest for testing (so the testing set ranged from 5–21 d of data per person, with mean = 10.79 d per person and SD = 3.28 d), and 3) 10-fold CV split-by-meal. Split-by-person CV means that each individual is either fully in the training or test data, and thus, there is no personal training data. The 5 d of data for training and the rest for testing setting reflects the scenario in which a CGM is worn for a short initialization period (e.g., 5 d) before beginning personalized glycemic forecasting. Lastly, the split-by-meal CV contains the most individual data. We tested all 18 models using all 3 splits.

Microbiome sample timing

To examine whether microbiome data become less informative with increasing time lags between sample collection and the time frame for glycemic response prediction, we leveraged that samples were collected from HPP participants at a variety of timepoints. Thus, by calculating the time difference between the sample and meal logs, we could assess accuracy as a function of timing. We used split-by-meal and split-by-person CV and compared our base model plus other features (prior dietary intake, CGM, and 20 foods) with and without the addition of microbiome. We used the following microbiome sample time windows and report number of participants (N) and number of meals (meals) in each subset: 1) before the start of meal logs (N = 136; meals = 6800), meaning before the first meal log entry; 2) before the end of meal logs (N = 171, meals = 10,650), meaning before the last meal log entry; 3) ≤7 d after the end of meal logs (N = 1745; meals = 123,741); 4) ≤14 d after the end of meal logs (N = 5179; meals = 352,719); 5) ≤30 d after the end of meal logs (N = 6804; meals = 426,958); and 6) ≤60 d after the end of meal logs (N = 6983; meals = 435,364). For subsets 3 to 6, these are up to and including the stated day. When multiple samples were available for an individual within the window, the one closest in time to the meal logs was used.

Sensitivity analyses

To evaluate whether differences in sample size across feature groups affected models, we conducted a complete-case sensitivity analysis restricted to participants with all data modalities available. Participants did not have microbiome data collected at all of the time windows tested, so we could not perform a complete-case analysis for the microbiome sample timing experiment. Instead, as a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the microbiome timing analysis using nonoverlapping windows (e.g., 8–14 d after meal logs instead of ≤14 d after) to assess whether the timing-related patterns remained consistent.

Results

Participants

The full cohort of 8334 individuals had a mean age of 52.44 y (SD = 8.04 y), with 52.29% of participants being female. Participants had a mean BMI of 26.16 kg/m2 (SD = 4.18).

Feature selection

The top 20 food features selected by HIC for PPGR and Glumax (using the 5 d of data for training and the rest for testing setting) are shown in Table 2. Features associated with higher glycemic responses were predominantly refined grain or sugar items, whereas lower responses were associated with vegetables, protein-rich items, and noncaloric sweeteners. For Glumax, there was greater emphasis on sweets, breads (e.g., pita/whole-meal rolls), and added fats and oils, whereas for PPGR, there was a broader set of starch-based foods and several protein-rich items (e.g., seafood, and protein powder).

Feature contribution

As shown in Table 3, the highest accuracy was obtained with the largest model (4 feature groups), and the single most useful feature group was CGM. Our addition of waist-to-hip ratio to the model of Shen et al. [15] led to a statistically significant improvement in both R and R2 for both PPGR and Glumax (P < 0.01 for all paired 2-tailed t tests). The single most important feature group was CGM, which led to significantly higher R and R2 for both glycemic outcomes compared with the next best-performing single feature model (all P < 0.01). Microbiome, 20 foods, and prior dietary intake all led to improvements compared with the base model (all P < 0.001), whereas sleep did not. When expanding the model to 2 feature groups, the best performance depended on outcome: prior dietary intake + microbiome performed best for PPGR (all P < 0.05), whereas prior dietary intake + CGM performed best for Glumax (all P < 0.01). Consistent with this, the combination of those 3 feature groups (prior dietary intake, microbiome, and CGM) was the best-performing model with three features. The maximum R and R2 were achieved by adding 20 foods to this group, and the increase was statistically significant (For R, P = 0.0036 for PPGR and P = 0.00072 for Glumax; For R2: P = 0.00048 for PPGR and P = 0.00089 for Glumax), though small. Higher-resolution microbiome features did not improve prediction accuracy (Supplemental Appendix C1).

The complete-case sensitivity analysis (Supplemental Appendix A2 and A3) showed a similar pattern: waist-to-hip ratio significantly improved model accuracy, CGM features contributed the most, and microbiome, 20 foods, and prior dietary intake each led to improvements. This suggests that the relative contribution of feature groups was not driven by differences in sample size or participant differences across models.

Prediction difficulty did not differ across participant subgroups (Supplemental Appendix E1–E3). No significant differences were observed based on demographics or anthropometrics (gender, age group, BMI, or WHR). However, there were significant differences for base CGM ranges and meal types. Meals with base CGM >150 mg/dL were more often classified as harder to predict, as was dinner (all P < 0.001).

Personalized training data

Prediction accuracy improved significantly with more personal training data, as shown in Figure 4. For the 4 feature-group model, accuracy was highest for a 10-fold split-by-meal (R = 0.731 for PPGR and R = 0.725 for Glumax), followed by 5 d for training (R = 0.661 for PPGR and R = 0.656 for Glumax), and lastly a 10-fold split-by-person (R = 0.590 for PPGR and R = 0.598 for Glumax). Using a 2-tailed t-test, the difference between split-by-meal and 5 d for training was significant (P < 0.001 for both PPGR and Glumax), as was the difference between split-by-person and 5 d for training (P < 0.001 for both PPGR and Glumax). The pattern for R2 was the same, with results being better with more personal training data. Supplemental Appendix A1 contains results for the full set of 18 models.

Microbiome sample timing

As shown in Table 4, microbiome sample timing had a significant effect on accuracy. For 10-fold CV split-by-meal, models with microbiome had significantly higher R and R2 for both glycemic responses with samples collected ≤7 d after meal logs (all P values <0.01). The highest accuracy and largest improvement were achieved when microbiome samples were collected before meal logs (R of 0.750 and 0.704 with and without microbiome, respectively). In addition, R for PPGR was higher for the model with microbiome samples collected ≤14 d after meal logs, but there was no difference for R2 or for Glumax. For both responses and metrics, models with microbiome had the same performance as models without for samples ≤30 and 60 d later. Microbiome features did not improve predictions at any sample timing for the CV split-by-person. Although N varied, performance without microbiome was stable across the groups (PPGR R split-by-meal ranging from 0.676 to 0.704; Glumax R ranging from 0.681 to 0.693), suggesting that the differences were not due to differences in the meals or participants. Furthermore, the contribution of the microbiome was smaller than the difference between the 2 CV settings.

Discussion

We systematically investigated how data types, timing, and quantity influence the accuracy of glycemic response prediction. We found that personalized training data provided the greatest contribution to accuracy, with even 5 d of data providing a significant increase. Among data types, CGM features were most important, with combinations of CGM, prior dietary intake, and microbiome features each improving model accuracy when added. Finally, we found that microbiome features only increased accuracy when personalized training data were used, and samples were close in time to the meal logs.

Personalized nutrition is inherently individual, but it has been an open question whether models can be trained once and then applied to new individuals, or whether they must be updated with individualized data. Prior work has shown that updating models trained in 1 geographic region with data from the target region (Israel and the United States) leads to better results [11], but diet and culture also change between regions, making it difficult to isolate the effect of personalized training data. Our results provide 2 new insights. First, we found that the relationship between accuracy and the amount of personal training data (meaning meal logs with glycemic responses) is not linear. Adding 5 d of personalized training data led to R halfway between split-by-person (no personal data) and split-by-meal (all meals but 1). This suggests that a middle ground may be possible, enabling personalized insights from a few days of meal logs. Second, we found that personalized training data are essential when using microbiome features, because there was no difference in prediction accuracy with and without microbiome in the split-by-person setting. This is consistent with other works showing high interindividual variation in gut microbiome, making it difficult to predict individual responses to dietary interventions [34].

Our findings on the different contributions of feature groups to accuracy are consistent with known mechanisms, yet provide new insights into the relative significance of each feature. CGM features (i.e., glucose prior to the meal and trends in the hours earlier) were the single most important feature group, reflecting that forecasts are made in the context of current glucose dynamics and that the effect of earlier meals carries over to later ones. Although it is known that current glucose dynamics strongly influence postprandial responses, this finding highlights the importance of incorporating recent glucose measurements to predict glycemic responses to meals. Prior dietary intake was one of the most informative features, which is consistent with the literature on second meal effects. Previous research found that the nutrient composition of dinner can affect glycemic response at breakfast the next day [35], and this effect has been observed at other times of day [36] and when comparing the glycemic response of lunch with and without the consumption of breakfast [37]. On the other hand, although sleep does affect insulin sensitivity [38], this feature group did not improve predictions. The effects may be small enough that this feature is less informative than others. Lastly, in prior work, we found that food features significantly improved glycemic response prediction [15], and now find that this feature only adds value when combined with microbiome, CGM features, and prior dietary intake. Our HIC feature selection identified foods that would be expected to have higher glycemic responses (e.g., refined grain and high-sugar items) as predictors of increased PPGR and Glumax, and foods with lower glycemic index (e.g., protein and fiber-rich foods) as predictors of lower glycemic responses. Notably, because the model includes meal composition already, the finding that food categories are informative reflects that there are other features, such as micronutrients, processing level, and physical properties of food, that may influence PPGRs.

In addition to the need for personal training data, to our knowledge, we provide the first results on how the timing of microbiome sampling affects the utility of this feature for predicting PPGRs. After 14 d, microbiome features did not lead to a statistically significant improvement in model accuracy. The gut microbiome may influence host glucose metabolism through mechanisms such as the production of short-chain fatty acids [39] and effects on host insulin sensitivity [40]. Microbial structure can shift over time [13] and fluctuate throughout the day [41] and in response to dietary intake [20]; however, it has not been known whether it remains informative at a long timescale for predicting glycemic responses.

Our results have several important implications for precision nutrition research and translation. First, whether for short- or long-term study, to obtain accurate predictions, it is important to collect a brief duration of personal CGM and meal log data. On the other hand, microbiome data only improved model accuracy when sampled within 14 d of meal logs and when combined with personalized training data, and for long-term studies. Collecting frequent samples may be cost and time-prohibitive using current technology, but future technologies such as smart toilets and other new sampling and processing devices could change this [42]. Additionally, individual differences in intestinal transit time may influence how well microbiome samples correspond to dietary intake recorded near the same time [20]. Finally, given the importance of prior dietary intake on prediction accuracy, real-world systems providing feedback on the glycemic effect of a meal should collect or query users about earlier dietary intake.

This study has several limitations that may impact generalizability. Participants self-reported meals within 30 min of consumption, which could introduce error due to inexact meal times, omitted meals, or errors in the recording of meal content and quantities. Furthermore, because of the nature of HPP, not all participants provided all data types, so sample sizes varied across analyses. Finally, the cohort comes from a single geographic region and includes only adults. Results may differ in regions with different dietary patterns or for cohorts with underlying conditions such as T2D. Future work is needed to validate models with more diverse cohorts and with objective ground truth.

Personalized nutrition could significantly improve population health, but has lacked a foundational understanding of what data modalities are required. In a large multimodal dataset, we showed that personalized data (meal logs, and CGM) are more important to prediction accuracy than the addition of any other single feature group tested. CGM features and dietary intake were consistently informative, whereas microbiome features only significantly improved accuracy when collected within 14 d of meal logs and when combined with personalized data. Our findings suggest that glycemic responses can be predicted with a limited set of features, and that there is a need for new, lower-burden methods to collect personalized meal data.
