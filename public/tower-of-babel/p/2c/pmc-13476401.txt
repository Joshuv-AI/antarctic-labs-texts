Body Mass Index Trajectories Among Patients with Drug-Susceptible and Drug-Resistant Tuberculosis in Northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Fasil Wagnew, Kefyalew Addis Alene, Matthew Kelly, Darren Gray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionUnderstanding predictors of body mass index (BMI) trajectories in patients with drug-susceptible tuberculosis (DS-TB) and drug-resistant TB (DR-TB) is crucial for optimizing treatment strategies. This study examined BMI trajectories and predictors among patients with DS-TB and DR-TB in northwest Ethiopia.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using clinical data from patients attending TB clinics at four public hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia. We modelled BMI trajectories after TB treatment initiation using linear mixed models, with adjustments for potential confounders.ResultsA total of 988 patients were included in the analysis, comprising 569 with DS-TB and 419 with DR-TB. In the DS-TB cohort, male sex was associated with higher mean BMI (β = 0.39; 95% CI: 0.12–0.65), while baseline underweight status (β = −4.07; 95% CI: −4.33, −3.82) and baseline anaemia (β = −0.27; 95% CI: −0.53, −0.02) were associated with lower mean BMI across follow-up. In the DR-TB cohort, men had a higher mean BMI than women across the follow-up (β = 0.64; 95% CI: 0.16, 1.12). Although participants who were underweight at baseline (β = −3.62; 95% CI −4.06 to −3.18) or anaemic (β = −0.54; 95% CI: −0.98, −0.10) had a lower mean BMI across follow-up, they gained BMI more rapidly over time (time*underweight β = 0.04, 95% CI: 0.01, 0.07; time*anaemia β = 0.05, 95% CI: 0.01, 0.08).ConclusionEarly nutritional assessment and tailored nutritional support should be strengthened as part of routine TB care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-026-01404-6.Keywords: Body mass index, DS-TB, DR-TB, Ethiopia

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Understanding body mass index (BMI) trajectories in patients with drug-susceptible tuberculosis (DS-TB) and drug-resistant TB (DR-TB) is important for improving treatment outcomes and guiding clinical management.This study examined BMI trajectories and their predictors among patients with DS-TB and DR-TB using longitudinal clinical data from four public hospitals in northwest Ethiopia. What was learned from this study? Male patients had higher mean BMI during follow-up, while baseline underweight status and anaemia were associated with significantly lower BMI levels over follow-up time.These findings highlight the importance of early nutritional assessment and support for patients with DS-TB and DR-TB to improve treatment outcomes.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is a major global health threat, causing an estimated 10.8 million cases and 1.25 million deaths annually [1]. Multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB), resistant to isoniazid and rifampicin, is a growing public health challenge, with poorer treatment outcomes and substantially higher costs than drug-susceptible TB [1, 2]. The decline in global TB incidence from 2015 to 2023 was only 8.3%, substantially below the World Health Organization (WHO) ‘End TB Strategy’ goals of 50% by 2025 and 90% by 2035 [1]. While strengthening biomedical interventions for TB is vital, the epidemic is largely driven by modifiable social risk factors that must also be addressed to reduce incidence and mortality.

Undernutrition is a leading risk factor for the TB epidemic, and its impact is particularly severe in patients with MDR-TB due to prolonged illness, poor appetite, gastrointestinal side effects of second-line drugs, and socioeconomic barriers to adequate food access [1, 3]. In high‑burden settings like Ethiopia, poverty, ongoing political instability, and widespread undernutrition exacerbate the TB burden by decreasing case detection, delaying treatment initiation, and disrupting continuity of care [4, 5]. A recent meta-analysis in Ethiopia showed that only 49.2% of TB patients had a healthy nutritional status, highlighting challenges in improving nutritional recovery during treatment [6].

Measures of body weight and body mass index (BMI) serve as practical biomarkers in clinical practice to assess TB risk and monitor treatment response. Previous studies [7, 8] have shown that a decline in BMI before TB diagnosis is associated with an increased risk of developing TB disease. Conversely, weight gain during treatment has been positively related to improved outcomes in patients with TB and MDR-TB [9–11]. Weight gain in the two months of treatment was strongly prognostic, with a 1% increase associated with a 13% lower risk of death, and a 5% increase associated with a 61% reduction [12]. Our previous study also demonstrated that each one-unit weight gain was associated with nearly a 5% reduction in the risk of unsuccessful DR-TB treatment outcomes [13]. Moreover, weight gain may necessitate TB drug dose adjustments to ensure adequate pharmacokinetic exposure and prevent resistance [14].

Despite their prognostic value, longitudinal patterns of BMI change during TB treatment are understudied, with most research relying on single-time measurements rather than capturing dynamic changes over time. BMI changes during TB are multifactorial, reflecting the combined effects of nutritional status, disease-related metabolic demands, inflammation, and treatment-related factors. While BMI gain after treatment initiation is expected, not all patients experience it, and the extent of gain varies substantially among individuals. Particularly, patients with DR-TB often present after a longer period of illness, experience notable inflammatory burden, and undergo prolonged second-line drugs that commonly cause gastrointestinal intolerance and metabolic disturbances. These factors may delay or attenuate nutritional recovery, leading to more heterogeneous BMI trajectories among patients with DR-TB. Nonetheless, comparative analyses of BMI trajectories between patients with drug-susceptible TB (DS-TB) and DR-TB remain scarce, despite their differing treatment regimens, durations, and side-effect profiles, which may influence nutritional recovery. Ethiopia, where undernutrition and TB are highly prevalent [15], offers an important setting to investigate these interactions. Therefore, this study aimed to identify predictors of BMI trajectories to inform early risk stratification and targeted nutritional interventions.

Methods

Study Design and Settings

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at four public hospitals in northwest Ethiopia: the University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialised Hospital (UoGCSH), Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialised Hospital (DMCSH), Debre Tabor Specialized Hospital (DTSH), and Finote Selam General Hospital (FSGH).

TB diagnosis in the study settings followed national protocols, primarily using GeneXpert MTB/RIF, with smear microscopy and clinical methods applied as needed. Patients with DS-TB received the standard six-month treatment regimen, while patients with DR-TB were treated with individualized or standardized regimens depending on drug susceptibility results.

As part of standard TB treatment protocols in the study settings, nutritional status was routinely assessed using BMI, a commonly used indicator in clinical practice. Nutritional support was provided according to national guidelines and clinical need throughout the study period. Severely underweight patients received therapeutic foods, moderately underweight patients received supplementary foods, and patients who were not underweight received nutritional counselling. All TB-related treatment and nutritional support were provided free of charge. Detailed descriptions of the study setting, diagnostic protocols, treatment regimens, and nutritional support guidelines are provided in our previously published reports [13, 16].

Study Population

The study included two cohorts of adult patients aged 15 years and above who were treated for DS-TB and DR-TB at four different hospitals. The DS-TB cohort included patients treated from August 2017 to July 2023, whereas the DR-TB cohort included patients treated from May 2015 to February 2022. The difference in time frames is due to the longer treatment duration required for DR-TB patients (up to two years). For both cohorts, patients were included if they had at least two recorded BMI measurements, at baseline and during follow-up. Patients who were pregnant, did not initiate treatment, transferred out, or lacked outcome data were excluded.

Data Sources

Data were extracted from medical records at directly observed therapy (DOT) clinics using a semi-structured data extraction tool adapted from the DS-TB and DR-TB treatment and follow-up guidelines and published literature [17–19]. The questionnaire was based on multiple sources, including DS-TB and DR-TB registration books, follow-up sheets, laboratory reports, and individual medical charts. Collected data included sociodemographic details (age, sex, residence, marital status); behavioural factors (alcohol use, smoking); clinical and treatment-related factors (nutritional status, comorbidities, TB type, treatment history); and anthropometric measurements (weight, height, BMI).

Eight trained healthcare professionals collected the data following a two-day orientation. Supervisors reviewed the extracted data daily, and the principal investigator provided regular feedback to ensure data quality was maintained.

Data Analysis

Data were cleaned, coded, and analysed using R version 4.4.3. Descriptive statistics summarized sociodemographic, behavioural, and clinical characteristics. Exploratory analyses assessed BMI patterns over time using individual profile plots, smoothed mean trajectories, and Q-Q plots for residual normality. Multicollinearity among predictors was checked using variance inflation factors (VIF). Missing values in predictor variables such as haemoglobin were addressed using multiple imputation with a multivariate normal imputation model, following confirmation that data were missing at random (MAR) using Little’s MCAR test.

To evaluate BMI change over time and identify associated predictors, a linear mixed-effects model (LMM) was fitted separately for DS-TB and DR-TB cohorts. This approach was selected to account for within-subject correlation arising from repeated BMI measurements and to accommodate varying follow-up durations across participants. The outcome variable was BMI, measured at baseline and 2 months after initiation of treatment for patients with DS-TB (corresponding to the end of intensive treatment phase, and the only time points recorded in this cohort), and monthly for up to 18 months for patients with DR-TB. Time was considered a continuous variable (in months), capturing the duration of follow-up from treatment initiation. Each model included random intercepts to account for within-subject correlation; for DR-TB, random slopes for time were also included to capture individual variation in the rate of BMI change. In the univariable analysis, variables with a p-value ≤ 0.25 were considered eligible for multivariable analysis, and statistical significance was set at a p-value of < 0.05. Results are presented as regression coefficients with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) and p-values. DS-TB and DR-TB are analyzed separately due to different BMI measurement schedules and distinct clinical characteristics. Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC).

Results

A total of 988 patients were included in the analysis, comprising 569 with DS-TB and 419 with DR-TB. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of participants according to BMI gain ≥ 1 kg/m2 are provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2 for the DS-TB and DR-TB cohorts, respectively.

At baseline, both patients with DS-TB and DR-TB had mean BMI values below the WHO normal threshold of 18.5 kg/m2. Patients with DS-TB gained an average of 0.82 BMI units (95% CI: 0.69–0.96; p < 0.001), while those with DR-TB gained 0.91 units (95% CI: 0.72–1.10; p < 0.001) after two months of treatment (Table 2). In addition, the prevalence of undernutrition declined from 57.1% at baseline to 46.7% at 2 months in the DS-TB cohort, and from 60.9% to 53.5% in the DR-TB cohort (Table 2).

BMI Trajectories Among Patients with DS-TB and DR-TB

BMI was measured at baseline and again at 2 months after initiation of treatment for patients with DS-TB, and monthly for patients with DR-TB over the follow-up period. Individual profile plots of 40 randomly selected participants showed considerable variation in BMI within individuals at treatment initiation and during follow-up. Among patients with TB, BMI generally increased during the intensive phase (the first 2 months of treatment). Factors of BMI Trajectories in Patients with DS-TB and DR-TB

We found sex, baseline nutritional status, and anaemia as significant predictors of BMI trajectories in both DS-TB and DR-TB cohorts.

In the DS-TB cohort, male patients had significantly higher mean BMI than female patients over the follow-up period (β = 0.39; 95% CI: 0.12, 0.65). Patients who were underweight at baseline had a markedly lower mean BMI throughout follow-up compared with those in the normal range (β = −4.07; 95% CI: −4.33, −3.82), and anaemic patients at baseline had slightly lower mean BMI across follow-up compared to non-anaemic TB individuals (β = −0.27; 95% CI: −0.53, −0.02) (Table 3).

Discussion

Our analysis showed consistent patterns across both DS-TB and DR-TB cohorts: at baseline, mean BMI levels among patients with DS-TB and DR-TB were below the WHO normal threshold of 18.5 kg/m2. Male sex was associated with higher BMI levels during follow-up, whereas baseline underweight and anaemia were associated with lower BMI levels. Among patients with DR-TB, however, those who were undernourished or anaemic demonstrated faster BMI gains over time, likely reflecting nutritional recovery or the effects of existing nutritional support during treatment.

Sex differences in BMI trajectories have been reported previously, with men showing a higher average BMI than women [22, 23]. It could be due to genetic variations or other nutritional factors. For instance, higher levels of household food insecurity have been observed in female-headed households, which may influence nutritional outcomes in vulnerable populations [24]. Women may also be more vulnerable to TB treatment-related side effects, including nausea and fatigue, which can reduce appetite and adherence, thereby limiting BMI recovery during therapy [25]. Further studies are needed to elucidate the biological, behavioural, and socioeconomic mechanisms underlying sex differences in BMI trajectories during TB treatment.

Underweight patients at baseline presented with a lower mean BMI at follow-up than their counterparts in the normal range, consistent with findings in a previous study among adults with HIV [26]. In people with TB, being underweight impairs cell-mediated immunity and increases vulnerability to infection and drug toxicity, and exacerbates nutritional stress and weight loss [27]. Importantly, underweight can be addressed as a modifiable risk factor through low-cost solutions. In our study, underweight patients with DR-TB demonstrated a higher rate of BMI increase during follow-up than those in the normal range, possibly reflecting the benefits of existing nutritional support and TB medications, such as improved appetite and reduced metabolic demands. According to the national nutrition guideline, all underweight TB patients are eligible to receive nutritional therapy such as Plumpy-Nut or Ready to Use Therapeutic Foods (RUTF) [17]. Strengthening nutritional interventions and ensuring close monitoring of underweight patients could substantially improve nutritional recovery and increase BMI gain during follow-up [22], with potential benefits for treatment outcomes, as supported by our previous study [13]. An effective TB response must therefore extend beyond biomedical interventions to address modifiable risk factors, which are essential to meeting global targets that current strategies cannot achieve. Nutritional support should be considered as essential as medication, serving not only to restore nutritional status but also to prevent disease among household contacts, mitigate food insecurity, and sustain patient engagement with care. However, projected reductions in international donor support, including from the United States Agency for International Development (USAID) and the Global Fund, threaten to undermine these efforts with potentially devastating consequences for TB and global health [28, 29]. Nutritional programmes are increasingly being sidelined in favour of biomedical priorities, despite their broad benefits that extend beyond TB.

Patients with baseline anaemia had lower mean BMI trajectories during TB treatment compared with non-anaemic patients. Previous studies have similarly shown that low BMI and weight loss are more frequent among anaemic patients than non-anaemic patients [30, 31]. This may help explain the high prevalence of anaemia in severe TB [32–34], with reported prevalence ranging from 30 to 94% [35]. Anaemia might also be associated with coexisting conditions such as parasitic or other severe infections, and the roles of malaria and soil-transmitted helminths should be considered in certain settings [36]. Despite these challenges, our analysis indicated that anaemic patients with DR-TB experienced a higher rate of BMI gain over time compared with their non-anaemic counterparts. This finding may suggest improved nutritional and clinical recovery among patients who were anaemic at baseline. Therefore, routine haemoglobin testing is recommended in patients with TB due to its prognostic importance.

An important implication of this study is the need for systematic, routine BMI measurement among patients with DS-TB at baseline, at the end of the intensive phase, and at the end of treatment, in line with national guidelines [17]. Given its simplicity and low cost, monitoring BMI provides a practical tool for tracking recovery, especially in extrapulmonary TB (EPTB) where other clinical markers are limited. Regular BMI measurement can help establish nutritional recovery targets and broaden the concept of treatment success beyond microbiological cure. While microbiological cure is often achieved before treatment completion, nutritional recovery frequently lags behind—a gap we also observed to be under-documented among patients with DS-TB in our study areas. Persisting undernutrition at treatment completion may signal an elevated risk of relapse, reinfection, or clinical deterioration [35]. Therefore, BMI measures should be reassessed at treatment completion to evaluate nutritional recovery. Another implication is that the common predictors of BMI trajectories identified in both patients with DS-TB and DR-TB support a unified intervention strategy integrating nutritional support, anaemia management, and gender-responsive care. Such an approach can enhance program efficiency, enable early risk stratification, and inform policy by integrating nutrition and anaemia indicators within TB programmes.

Limitations

Our study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. As the DS-TB and DR-TB cohorts were recruited in different time periods, changes over time in TB treatment and nutritional support could have affected BMI trajectories. Some important factors, such as food insecurity, treatment adherence, drug regimen, and drug absorption, could not be included due to data unavailability. In addition, anaemia was classified using standard WHO haemoglobin thresholds without altitude adjustment, as participants were recruited from four referral hospitals serving geographically diverse areas with varying elevations. Moreover, for DS-TB, only two BMI measurements were collected (baseline and 2 months), despite national guidelines recommending three measurements. As a result, our modelling for this group was restricted to short-term change, which limited our ability to evaluate longer-term BMI trajectories as undertaken for DR-TB, and highlights a programmatic gap in routine BMI monitoring during treatment.

Conclusions

Both patients with DS-TB and DR-TB showed marked BMI deficits, with baseline underweight and anaemia consistently associated with lower BMI throughout follow-up. However, underweight or anaemic patients with DR-TB showed faster BMI gains during treatment, underscoring the importance of early nutritional support to improve treatment outcomes. Moreover, prospective longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate the long-term impact of BMI changes during and after treatment on patients’ quality of life and post-TB sequelae.
