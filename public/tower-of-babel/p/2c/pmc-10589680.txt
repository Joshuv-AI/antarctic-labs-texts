Paralysis by analysis: Overcoming cardiac contraction with computer vision
Authors: Jan Christoph, Crystal M. Ripplinger
Published: 2023

Since the first recording of optical action potentials in a frog heart nearly 50 y ago (1), basic studies of cardiac electrophysiology and excitation–contraction coupling have relied heavily on optical measurements and fluorescence imaging techniques. Voltage- and Ca2+-sensitive indicators are now routinely used in optical mapping experiments to study and obtain high-resolution measurements of cardiac electrophysiology, intracellular Ca2+ handling, metabolism, and more recently, mechano-biology (2). However, accurately measuring fluorescent signals in moving and deforming cardiac tissue preparations has remained a major challenge. The contraction of cardiac tissue invariably introduces distortions to optical mapping signals, often referred to as motion artifacts. These artifacts can significantly diminish the quality of the optical measurements and impede further data analysis. As such, the contractile motion of cardiac tissue has typically been avoided during optical mapping studies and is most often inhibited using pharmacological excitation–contraction uncoupling agents such as blebbistatin (3, 4). However, studies using pharmacological paralysis are severely limited in that they have not allowed investigators to study cardiac electrophysiology in the context of the heart’s most important characteristic: contractile motion. Therefore, in recent years, researchers have adopted numerical motion tracking algorithms to measure voltage and Ca2+ in contracting tissues, including cardiac (5–11) and gastric tissues (12, 13), by tracking and compensating the motion numerically and measuring signals in a comoving frame of reference. In PNAS, Woodhams et al. (14) adopted a novel numerical motion tracking approach and applied it to induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocyte (iPSC-CM) monolayers and engineered tissues, which the authors termed "Virtual Blebbistatin." While numerical motion tracking is primarily used to compensate motion, the kinematic data that results from the motion tracking, i.e., displacement vectors, can also be used to calculate tissue strain and heterogeneity in contractile activity. In that sense, the approach used by Woodhams et al. (14) and others is more than just applying a digital "virtual" form of blebbistatin; rather, it is a quantitative approach that will increasingly be used in physiology and integrative biology studies to characterize tissue structure and function at high spatial and temporal resolutions.

One of the first applications of numerical motion tracking to study voltage–Ca2+-strain dynamics was in fibrillating swine hearts (5). In this study, similar to Woodhams et al. (14), the tracking data were used to calculate strain maps, which were subsequently correlated with voltage and Ca2+ waves measured in the corresponding motion-stabilized optical maps. However, one of the key challenges in deriving tissue strain from tracked displacement data is obtaining a smooth displacement vector field that describes the motion and deformation of the tissue accurately. The displacement vector field needs to be differentiated in order to calculate strain (e.g., deformation gradient tensor) and discontinuities or small inaccuracies in displacement estimation can become amplified through numerical differentiation. Therefore, spatio-temporal smoothing or additional regularization techniques are often required. By contrast, Woodhams et al. (14) adopted a strain estimation technique (15), which directly estimates strain fields without first calculating tissue displacements, circumventing the necessity for smoothing or regularization techniques. Their approach is likely to have advantages over previous methods that rely on displacement vector field differentiation.

Indeed, Woodhams and colleagues (14) took advantage of the mechanical data and used local deformations to calculate and plot contractile strain versus Ca2+ fluorescence to assess regional Ca2+-contraction coupling in contracting human iPSC-CM monolayers and engineered tissues. The data reveal striking regional inhomogeneities in Ca2+-contraction coupling in the monolayers that are almost completely absent in engineered tissues. The authors attribute the monolayer heterogeneity to more dyssynchronous contraction, whereby quiescent cells are tugged on by contracting neighbor cells, which appears as an essentially uncoupled Ca2+-contraction relationship. In contrast, Ca2+-contraction coupling was much more spatially uniform in the engineered tissues, with physiological increases in Ca2+ associated with expected contractile responses. As the authors point out, these different contractile environments may have significant implications for iPSC-CM maturation processes and suggest that virtual blebbistatin may be a valuable tool for monitoring and assessing maturation of excitation–contraction coupling in iPSC-CMs. The virtual blebbistatin approach also robustly identified significant contractile deficits and Ca2+-contraction uncoupling in iPSC-CM engineered tissues harboring a cardiomyopathy-prone mutation. These findings highlight that virtual blebbistatin could easily be applied to iPSC-CMs with a variety of patient-specific mutations for physiological phenotyping or drug-screening studies. Furthermore, Woodhams and colleagues (14) introduce an interesting concept to characterize Ca2+-contraction coupling using circular phase diagrams, which could be useful to identify pathophysiology. Lastly, the study provides additional evidence that blebbistatin can be associated with side effects, as it documents a disturbance of the sarcomere structure in cardiomyocytes following blebbistatin treatment.

While virtual blebbistatin can effectively reduce motion artifacts in two-dimensional monolayers and engineered cardiac tissue, it remains to be determined how it will perform with stronger motion and in three-dimensional tissues, including intact isolated hearts. Three-dimensional motion and deformation cannot be characterized adequately using a single camera, as it limits the analysis to tracking two-dimensional displacements within the video image plane. Measuring three-dimensional motion and deformation requires either volumetric imaging or multicamera optical mapping and three-dimensional tracking (7, 8). Furthermore, numerical tracking and motion stabilization alone cannot eliminate all sources of motion artifacts. For instance, “illumination artifacts”, which become particularly severe with large motion and illumination gradients, arise due to the relative motion between the tissue and the excitation light source. This relative motion persists in real physical space even with virtual blebbistatin, and therefore, motion tracking combined with ratiometric techniques may be required to better inhibit motion artifacts (7, 10). Virtual blebbistatin also cannot compensate for density, absorption, and reflection changes of the tissue as it contracts. Despite the efficacy of numerical motion tracking and its promising future in physiological research, these remaining issues need to be carefully considered in future studies.

The development of new computational tools such as virtual blebbistatin also highlights the varied approaches and algorithms that can be implemented for analysis of cardiac fluorescent optical mapping data (both with and without motion correction). While for a long time, there was no community standard or commonly used software for the processing of optical mapping data, more recently, several post-processing and analysis software packages have been developed specifically for this purpose. These packages include ElectroMap (16), KairoSight (17), Rhythm (18), Unimapper (19), ORCA (20), COSMAS (21), Spiky (22), Sparkmaster (23), Musclemotion (24), and optimap (25) among others (Table 1). Some of these packages are optimized for the analysis of cellular fluorescence data, whereas others are equally suitable for monolayers, tissues, or intact hearts. Thus far, optimap and Virtual Blebbistatin are the only packages that provide processing routines for the tracking and compensation of motion artifacts. By making these software tools open-source, investigators are continually helping to improve the standardization, transparency, and reproducibility of cardiac optical mapping data analysis, and as a result, the rigor of the physiological findings revealed by these studies. As the field continues to develop, it will hopefully address the robustness and accuracy of the algorithms by rigorous testing and benchmarking. Further advancements in computer vision, combined with lower-cost hardware and processing capabilities, will no doubt lead to novel approaches and algorithms for overcoming cardiac contractile artifacts and virtual paralysis by analysis.
