Visceral obesity as a predictor of ileal pouch reachability in ulcerative colitis: A prospective single-center study
Authors: Yuki Horio, Motoi Uchino, Masato Kiriki, Yusuke Tomoo, Kazunori Nomura, Kentaro Nagano, Kurando Kusunoki, Ryuichi Kuwahara, Kei Kimura, Kozo Kataoka, Masataka Ikeda, Hiroki Ikeuchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimObesity has been reported to increase the risk of technical inability in performing ileal pouch–anal anastomosis (IPAA) in patients with ulcerative colitis (UC). This study aimed to prospectively investigate the association between visceral fat and the feasibility of pouch reach in hand-sewn IPAA.MethodsWe prospectively enrolled patients with UC scheduled for two-stage restorative proctocolectomy with a preoperative plan for hand-sewn IPAA. Patients were categorized into a conversion group that required conversion to stapled IPAA intraoperatively and a non-conversion group. Preoperative visceral fat area and anatomical indicators were measured using computed tomography (CT). Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify predictors of conversion surgery.ResultsA total of 106 patients were included, with 12 (11.3%) in the conversion group. Compared with those in the non-conversion group, patients in the conversion group had significantly higher body mass index (BMI), greater visceral fat, and a longer distance between the ileal end and the anal verge (p < 0.01). Logistic regression identified visceral fat (per 10 cm2 increase: odds ratio [OR] 1.19, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.02–1.39, p = 0.01) as a significant predictor of conversion surgery, whereas BMI was not (OR 1.03, 95% CI 0.77–1.21, p = 0.72).ConclusionsVisceral fat is an independent risk factor for conversion surgery in patients undergoing hand-sewn IPAA. Preoperative CT-based measurement of visceral fat may provide a more accurate prediction of pouch reachability than BMI alone. These findings may help surgeons decide preoperatively whether a hand-sewn IPAA is feasible.

Introduction

The standard recommended surgical procedure for ulcerative colitis (UC) is restorative proctocolectomy with ileal pouch–anal anastomosis (IPAA) [1]. Two basic techniques are available for IPAA: the double-stapling method (stapled IPAA) and mucosectomy with hand-sewn anastomosis (hand-sewn IPAA). Stapled IPAA is commonly performed because it is technically simpler, allows better maneuverability, and is associated with improved anal function [2]. In contrast, hand-sewn IPAA is recommended in cases involving lower rectal cancer or dysplasia [3, 4]. Compared to stapled IPAA, hand-sewn IPAA poses greater technical challenges than stapled IPAA due to the limited reach of the ileal pouch to the anastomotic site [5]. Additionally, the feasibility of performing hand-sewn IPAA cannot always be determined preoperatively and is usually assessed intraoperatively by evaluating whether the apex of the pouch extends beyond the pubic symphysis [6]. If this cannot be achieved, abdominoperineal resection with a permanent ileostomy becomes necessary.

UC induces chronic inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract through immune-mediated mechanisms, often leading to malnutrition and weight loss. However, recent reports have identified a rising trend of obesity among patients with UC [7, 8]. In general, obesity—typically defined by body mass index (BMI)—is a known risk factor for perioperative complications [9, 10]. Although the relationship between obesity and postoperative complications in UC remains controversial, obesity has been associated with a higher risk of postoperative complications such as anastomotic leakage [11, 12]. Moreover, obesity has been linked to a greater likelihood of technical challenges in performing IPAA [13]. However, BMI alone cannot distinguish between subcutaneous and visceral fat, which represents different types of obesity. Technical difficulty during IPAA may be related more to increased visceral adiposity and mesenteric shortening, both of which limit pouch reach. In addition, achieving adequate perfusion and a tension-free anastomosis is essential for successful IPAA. Previous studies have reported that patients with UC and high mesenteric tension are more likely to experience anastomotic stenosis and pouch failure. Notably, the rate of hand-sewn IPAA was higher in the high-tension group [14].

Given these findings, we hypothesized that preoperative measurement of visceral fat using computed tomography (CT) could serve as a more accurate predictor of technical difficulty in pouch reach compared to BMI. Therefore, this study aimed to prospectively investigate whether CT-based visceral fat measurements are associated with difficulty in reaching the pouch during hand-sewn IPAA in patients with UC.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

We enrolled patients with UC who underwent two-stage restorative proctocolectomy with IPAA at Hyogo Medical University between April 2017 and October 2024. All patients were scheduled to undergo hand-sewn IPAA during the preoperative phase. A formal sample size calculation was not performed due to the exploratory nature of this study and the limitations of recruiting patients at a single institution. UC was diagnosed based on surgical specimens obtained at the time of surgery. Histological features consistent with UC included lymphocytes and plasma cells in the lamina propria, neutrophils in the lamina, crypts, or surface epithelium, changes in surface topography, epithelial damage, metaplastic changes, and mucin depletion. Patients diagnosed with or suspected of having Crohn’s disease (CD) based on histological findings were excluded. Colectomy specimens were screened to ensure the absence of Crohn’s-like features such as granulomas, transmural lymphoid aggregates, or fissures.

Data collection

Collected data included sex, age at surgery, duration of disease, disease severity, disease extent, laparoscopic surgery, surgical indication, cumulative prednisolone (PSL) dose, preoperative PSL dose, use of immunomodulators (thiopurines, including azathioprine and 6-mercaptopurine), calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus), Janus kinase inhibitors (tofacitinib), biologics (infliximab, adalimumab, golimumab, and vedolizumab). Operative time, blood loss, intraoperative blood transfusion, height, weight, BMI at admission, and pouch-related complications were also recorded. Severe disease was assessed primarily according to clinical features using Truelove and Witts’ criteria: six or more bloody stools per day and at least one of the following: or more of the following: a hemoglobin (hb) < 105 g/L, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) > 30 mm/h, fever > 37.8 °C, or pulse rate > 90/min [15]. Total corticosteroid exposure was converted to PSL equivalents based on the dose used since UC onset. Immunosuppressive agents administered within 72 h before surgery were recorded, regardless of dosage. All infusions administered within 12 weeks before surgery were classified as biologic therapy. Pouch-related complications were defined as leakage, anastomotic stricture, pouchitis, and pouch failure. The diagnosis of leakage was determined by enema examination and abdominal CT scan. The anastomotic stricture was defined as the inability to insert the index finger into the anus and the need for postoperative dilation. Pouchitis was diagnosed by modified Pouchitis Disease Activity Index ≥ 5 [16]. Pouch failure was defined as permanent ileostomy, pouch excision, or loss of pouch function requiring refunctioning. Cuffitis was defined as chronic inflammation in the residual rectal cuff after IPAA, characterized by clinical and endoscopic features similar to UC.

Surgical procedure

At our institution, two-stage restorative proctocolectomy with hand-sewn IPAA is the standard first-line procedure. Stapled IPAA is reserved for elderly patients or those with concerns regarding anal sphincter function. Laparoscopic approaches were introduced in April 2018 and were indicated for elective or urgent cases (e.g., refractory disease or cancer/dysplasia) in patients with stable general condition. Emergent cases such as fulminant disease, perforation, toxic megacolon, or massive bleeding were excluded from laparoscopic indication.

The ileal pouch was constructed with preservation of the ileocecal artery (ICA) and vein to maintain oral and cephalic perfusion. The mesentery was fully mobilized, and the peritoneum was divided at the inferior border of the pancreas. If the apex of the pouch did not extend below the pubic symphysis, the ileal branch of the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) was divided while preserving blood flow to achieve a tension-free anastomosis. However, if the apex still did not reach the anastomotic site, the procedure was converted intraoperatively from hand-sewn IPAA to stapled IPAA. In this study, conversion surgery was defined as a change in anastomosis method from hand-sewn to stapled IPAA due to inadequate pouch reach. The decision to convert was made jointly by two experienced colorectal surgeons, based on intraoperative assessment of anatomical limitations (inadequate pouch descent below the pubic symphysis) and perfusion status. In cases requiring conversion, mucosectomy was omitted, and a double-stapled anastomosis was performed using a circular stapler.

Primary outcomes

The primary outcome was to determine whether visceral obesity predicted conversion surgery, as defined above. Visceral fat area (cm2) was measured on preoperative CT images at the umbilical level during expiration using Ziostation System 1000™ (Ziosoft Inc Tokyo, Japan) on a CT system (SOMATOM Definition AS, Siemens Medical Solutions, Forchheim, Germany) (Fig. 1). Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test, analysis of variance (ANOVA), or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were expressed as medians with ranges and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test or ANOVA. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for all variables via univariate analysis. Multivariate logistic regression was performed for variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis. To avoid multicollinearity, variables with correlation coefficients > 0.7 were excluded. To determine the optimal cutoff value of visceral fat for predicting conversion surgery, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed. The cutoff was defined as the point with the highest Youden index. All analyses were conducted using JMP version 17 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

To minimize selection bias, all consecutive patients scheduled for two-stage restorative proctocolectomy with hand-sewn IPAA were prospectively enrolled. Measurement bias was reduced by using standardized CT-based software and defined anatomic criteria. The decision to convert was made jointly by two colorectal surgeons. Although this was an observational study, multivariate analysis was performed to account for potential confounders.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 106 patients with UC were included in the study, of whom 12 (11.3%) required conversion surgery. Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The mean age at surgery was 47.2 ± 13.5 years, with no significant difference between the conversion (46.1 ± 13.5 years) and non-conversion (47.4 ± 13.5 years) groups (p = 0.74). Median disease duration was 172.6 months, with no significant difference (p = 0.90). The incidence of severe disease did not differ significantly between the groups (8.3% in the conversion group vs. 14.8% in the non-conversion group, p = 0.39). Laparoscopic surgery was performed in 74 patients (69.8%), with no significant difference between the conversion (75.0%) and non-conversion (69.1%) groups (p = 0.67). Preoperative pharmacotherapy usage between the conversion and non-conversion groups was similar. Median operative time was significantly longer in the conversion group than that in the non-conversion group (306 vs. 392 min, p < 0.01). Primary outcome

Table 3 compares conversion and non-conversion groups. Weight, abdominal wall thickness, and abdominal circumference were significantly higher in the conversion group as compared to the non-conversion group and correlated well with BMI (r ≥ 0.7). The SMA-to-ileum distance was significantly shorter in the conversion group than that in the non-conversion group (p = 0.01), whereas the ileum-to-anal verge and ileum-to-anal canal distances were significantly longer (p < 0.01, p < 0.01). These anatomical factors were inversely correlated (correlation coefficients: 0.80 and 0.86, respectively). The ischial spine distance tended to be shorter in the conversion group, but the difference was not statistically significant. All fat-related parameters measured by CT were significantly higher in the conversion group than in the non-conversion group. Among these, most fat-related values showed strong correlations with BMI (correlation coefficient ≥ 0.7), whereas visceral fat (< 0.7) demonstrated a weaker association. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent risk factors for conversion surgery (Table 4). After excluding variables with strong intercorrelation, three factors—BMI, ileum-to-anal verge distance (ileal end-AV), and visceral fat—were identified as clinically and statistically significant. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this prospective study is the first to explore the association between visceral adiposity and difficulty in achieving pouch reach for hand-sewn IPAA in patients with UC. Our findings demonstrated a significant association between CT-measured visceral fat and the need to convert to stapled IPAA, suggesting that high visceral adiposity may impede the feasibility of performing hand-sewn IPAA. These results support our hypothesis that preoperative visceral fat is a more accurate predictor of pouch reachability than conventional indices such as BMI.

The association between obesity and technical difficulty in IPAA has been reported in previous studies, highlighting the increased surgical risk associated with obesity [13, 17]. Khasawneh et al. reported that the likelihood of IPAA failure increased from 2.0% at a BMI of 30 to 5.7% at a BMI of 35, and to 15.0% at a BMI of 40 [13]. Browning et al. reported that 43% of the IPAA discontinuations were due to technical issues, including severe obesity, mesenteric obesity, and elevated mesenteric tension [17]. Moreover, in a systematic review, obese patients undergoing IPAA had longer operative times, increased operative blood loss, and higher complication rates such as leakage and incisional hernias [11]. We previously reported that higher BMI was associated with an increased risk of anastomotic leakage and stricture following IPAA [18]. These reports suggest that increased visceral fat contributes to mesenteric shortening and increased anastomotic tension, which together elevate the risk of pouch-related complications. In our study, visceral fat was a significant predictor of intraoperative conversion from hand-sewn to stapled IPAA, reflecting its anatomical impact on pouch reach. Importantly, however, pouch-related complications did not significantly differ between the conversion and non-conversion groups. These findings suggest that when pouch reach is insufficient, converting to stapled IPAA may be a safe and reasonable alternative, rather than persisting with technically challenging hand-sewn IPAA in patients with high visceral fat. From an anatomical perspective, stapled IPAA may offer improved reach compared to hand-sewn IPAA, as it eliminates the need for mucosectomy and therefore does not require the pouch apex to extend to the dentate line. Previous reports have indicated that hand-sewn IPAA requires an additional 2–3 cm of reach to accommodate the lower anastomosis at the dentate line. In contrast, stapled IPAA can be performed at the upper anal canal, providing greater flexibility in cases with a short mesentery or high tension [5]. The cutoff value derived from our ROC analysis demonstrated good discriminatory ability and closely aligned with the Japanese diagnostic criterion for visceral obesity (≥ 100 cm2) used in the definition of metabolic syndrome [19]. This concordance suggests that the predictive value of visceral fat may extend beyond the specific context of ileal pouch reachability and may have broader clinical relevance. While BMI demonstrated higher sensitivity, it had lower specificity and was not an independent predictor in multivariate analysis. In contrast, visceral fat showed a slightly higher AUC, greater specificity, and was independently associated with conversion risk. These findings suggest that visceral fat provides more anatomically relevant information and may better identify patients who truly have poor pouch reachability. Nevertheless, our study was conducted in a Japanese population, where BMI and visceral fat levels are generally lower than those observed in Western countries. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings may be limited. However, the identified cutoff aligns with the Japanese diagnostic criteria for visceral obesity, which are based on metabolic risk, and may serve as a useful reference point for further validation in Western populations.

Beyond obesity, other anatomical indicators of difficulty have also been investigated. Ohira et al. assessed the location of the junction between the ileocolic artery (ICA) and the limbal artery in relation to the anal verge on CT and found that the pouch reach became problematic when this distance exceeded 21 cm [20]. Emoto et al. measured the distance from the root of the SMA to the upper border of the anal canal on contrast-enhanced CT and reported that a distance exceeding 11 cm was associated with difficulty in achieving pouch reach [21]. Both studies highlighted that reachability improved when the vascular branching point was located more caudally. Logically, the end of the ileal branch of the SMA is close to the apex of the pouch, making it a better anatomical indicator; however, given that the pouch itself is generally 15 cm in length, both indicators are clinically valuable.

Yokose et al. constructed a deformable three-dimensional model in patients undergoing IPAA and reported that the distance from the base of the SMA to the mesenteric tip was longer in the hand-sewn IAA group than in the stapled IPAA group [22]. Similarly, Adams et al. identified the distance from the base of the SMA to the mesenteric tip as a risk factor for IPAA difficulty [23]. In our study, preoperative contrast-enhanced CT was not routinely performed except in cancer cases. Therefore, we measured the distance from the root of the SMA to the ileal end and the distance from the ileal end to the anal verge or anal canal. Although these factors were not identified as independent predictors in the multivariate analysis, they were significant in the univariate analysis and may be useful when considering surgical modifications involving the pubic symphysis. Additionally, Emoto et al. examined pelvic dimensions, including the distance between the sciatic bones and from the coccyx to the pubic bone. These distances were not statistically significant predictors of pouch reach [21]. Similarly, in this study, the potential effect of a narrow pelvis—measured via the distance to the ischial spine—was considered, but the results were not statistically significant. Because sagittal CT images were not available for all patients, we could not evaluate distances from the sacral promontory to the superior pubic margin or from the coccyx to the inferior pubic border. Whereas narrow pelvic dimensions, including the distance between the sciatic spines, have been considered when planning pubic symphysis modification, our findings suggest that they may not be relevant predictors of anatomic feasibility. These pelvic parameters should be further explored in future studies to assess their impact in cases where hand-sewn IPAA is technically challenging in practice, rather than converting to surgery.

This study has some limitations. First, it was a single-center study with a small sample size. Second, as mentioned earlier, contrast-enhanced CT was not routinely performed, limiting our ability to measure the vessel-to-anal distances between the vessels, such as SMA or ICA, which may have offered stronger predictive value. Third, we defined conversion surgery based on failure of the pouch apex to cross the pubic symphysis, which may not fully capture all instances of technically challenging hand-sewn procedures. In other words, although hand-sewn IPAA was technically feasible in some cases and total proctocolectomy was avoided, intraoperative anastomosis was highly challenging. Further analysis of the risk factors associated with these cases is warranted in the future. Fourth, although we preserved the ICA to maintain perfusion during pouch creation, alternative mesenteric extension methods, such as non-preservation of the ICA, have been reported, and outcomes may vary depending on the vascular anatomy [24].

Conclusions

Visceral fat was identified as a significant predictor of technical difficulty in performing hand-sewn IPAA. Preoperative evaluation of visceral adiposity using CT may provide a more accurate and clinically useful predictor of pouch reachability in patients with UC than BMI alone.
