Leveraging digitization to decarbonize healthcare - medication prescribing information as an exemplar
Authors: Nazneen Rahman, for the National Academy of Medicine Action Collaborative on Decarbonizing the U.S. Health Sector and the Sustainable Medicines Partnership
Published: 2025

- Glossary of Key Terms

Consumer Medication Information (CMI): Independent medication information provided to patients, produced by third-party organizations and not approved or reviewed by regulators.

Decarbonization: The process of reducing carbon emissions, particularly those related to fossil fuels, to mitigate climate change.

Digital-First System: A communication model where digital information is the primary source with printed versions provided when needed.

Digitalization: The use of digital technologies to change processes, typically aimed at improving efficiency and user experience.

Digitization: The process of converting information from physical (e.g., paper) format into a digital format.

Dual System: A communication model that requires both digital and printed information, with both holding equal status as the primary source.

Food and Drug Administration (FDA): The U.S. regulatory authority responsible for the approval of prescription drugs and their labeling.

Healthcare Professional (HCP): A licensed individual who provides health services including doctors, nurses, and pharmacists.

Instructions for Use (IFU): Detailed instructions provided for medications that require specific, sometimes complex, usage.

Labeling: The overarching term used in the U.S. to describe medication information, including the Prescribing Information, Medication Guides, Patient Package Inserts.

Legislative rider: A provision added to a bill that directs or restricts government actions on a specific issue. Riders can be used to prevent regulatory changes by prohibiting the use of funds for certain activities.

Medication Guide: FDA-approved information that must be provided with certain prescriptions to inform patients about serious risks and adherence instructions.

Patient Package Insert (PPI): Information provided with certain types of prescription medicines, such as oral contraceptives, to educate the patient on safe use.

Prescribing Information (PI): Official, FDA-approved documentation intended to provide healthcare professionals with essential scientific and clinical details on a medication's safe and effective use.

Print-First System: A communication model in which printed information is the primary source, and digital versions may be available but are not required.

Supplier-Choice System: A communication model where the information provider has the flexibility to decide how information is presented, without specific regulatory requirements.

Introduction

The healthcare sector bears a critical responsibility in safeguarding the health and well-being of individuals and communities worldwide. Increasingly this encompasses acute and chronic health challenges caused by climate change including impacts from air pollution, extreme heat, wildfires, flooding, shifts in infectious diseases, and food and water insecurities [[1], [2], [3]].

Globally, healthcare contributes significantly to carbon emissions. In the U.S. the sector is linked to approximately 8.5 % of national emissions, primarily through indirect sources such as energy use, manufacturing, and supply chain activities [3,4]. Consequently, there is a growing impetus to reduce healthcare’s negative environmental footprint, and mounting interest in cost-effective innovations that align emission reductions with improved patient care [3,5].

Digitalization has strong potential to deliver the triple benefit of better outcomes, fewer emissions, and lower costs. Compared with interventions to mitigate upstream emissions, such as in pharmaceutical manufacturing, digital reforms can be simpler to scale and can deliver meaningful, near-term environmental benefits. In recent years, digitalization has transformed healthcare through streamlined administrative processes, improved patient management, real-time monitoring, remote consultations, advanced diagnostics, and advances in analytic and research capabilities. Virtually every facet of healthcare has been touched by the digital revolution [6]. Many digital advances also reduce environmental impacts. Virtual consultations reduce patient journeys and the associated carbon emissions, while electronic health records diminish the need for producing and storing paper records, thus lowering resource consumption and emissions [7,8]. However, digital health interventions should not be assumed to be more carbon-efficient and may increase emissions if they are simply an addition to existing systems.

In this perspective, we use provision of medication information as a case study to illustrate the potential of digitization to support healthcare decarbonization alongside efficiencies in healthcare delivery.

What is medication information?

Every country has regulated processes to provide information about prescription medicines [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. This information encompasses a wide range of content and serves three audiences: regulators, healthcare professionals, and patients. Regulators need to ensure clear, balanced, evidence-based information is available on the safe and efficacious use of medications. Healthcare professionals (HCPs) need to know the medication's purpose, development process, proven efficacy, approved uses and prescription guidelines. HCPs and patients need instructions on correct usage and details of side effects, contraindications, and any precautions that should be taken when using the medication.

Labeling is the overarching term used to describe medication information in the U.S. and is required for all prescription medicines. Labeling is proposed by the medicine manufacturer and reviewed and approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA). Approval of a new medicine is contingent on its labeling being approved. Healthcare professional information

Prescribing Information (PI) serves to inform and educate HCPs and summarizes essential scientific and clinical information necessary for the safe and effective use of the medication. It must be non-promotional in tone and updated when new information becomes available. All FDA-approved prescription medicines have a PI, which contains three sections [13]:

Patient information

Various types of medication information are written for patients. Certain prescriptions require FDA approved information, but this is not required for most medicines as described below [14,15]:

•Medication Guides are required if the FDA determines such information could help prevent serious adverse reactions; if the drug has serious risks of which the patient needs to be aware to make an informed decision on whether to take the medicine; or if adherence to specific use instructions is crucial for drug effectiveness [15].

Global medication information

The fundamental principles guiding provision of medication information are universal, but the terms used, content required, and models of communication vary between countries [12]. For example, in the European Union, the Prescribing Information is called the Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPC) and is available online [10]. An approved, printed Patient Information Leaflet is derived from the SmPC and is provided with all prescriptions.

Dual system

The dual system provides medication information in both print and digital formats, with both having equal status as the primary source. Both versions must be identical, up-to-date, and available with every prescription. In the U.S., Prescribing Information follows the dual system. Draft content is submitted electronically to the FDA for review and revision. Once approved, the information is added to the DailyMed and FDA Label websites, and a printed copy must accompany every prescription [17,18].

Digital-First system

The digital-first system uses digital information as the primary source, and it must contain the most up-to-date approved content. Printed copies are available upon request. Most countries outside the U.S., including the European Union, Japan, Canada, Australia, and the UK, use the digital-first system for healthcare professional (HCP) medication information [12]. Some countries, like Japan and Singapore, are also adopting the digital-first system for patient information, which can be accessed by scanning a barcode or QR code on the medicine packet [19,20].

Print-First system

The print-first system uses printed information as the primary source, which must accompany every prescription. Digital versions may be available, but they are not required. Patient information for prescribed medicines in many countries, including the European Union, follows the print-first system [12]. In the U.S., some patient information also follows this model (Table 2).

Supplier-choice system

The supplier-choice system allows flexibility in what information is provided and how it is delivered. In the U.S., patient information for many medications follows this model. Online information is widely available, but much of it is unregulated. Some manufacturers voluntarily provide Patient Package Inserts, and independent Consumer Medication Information is also common. Pharmacies have discretion over what patient information they provide and how it is delivered. Australia also uses the supplier-choice system for patient information [9].

Why is the dual system still used to communicate prescribing information?

The U.S. is a leader in digital provision of medication information. Since 2003, the FDA has required all drug labeling to be submitted for review in electronic format [21]. Since 2006, all approved Prescribing Information and updates have been available digitally on the FDA Label website and on DailyMed which is operated by the U.S. National Library of Medicine [17,18]. As of Feb 1st, 2025, DailyMed contained information on 152,117 submitted labels. Additionally, the full Prescribing Information must be provided in printed form with every prescription. Hence the dual system has been in place for 18 years.

In 2014, the FDA proposed a rule entitled ‘Electronic Distribution of Prescribing Information for Human Prescription Drugs, Including Biological Products,’ which proposed a shift to a digital-first system for Prescribing Information [22]. This change proposed to substitute the requirement of providing printed information with every prescription with systems to ensure those without reliable access to digital information could get printed information. Despite the FDA’s recommendation, the U.S. has not implemented the rule, setting it apart from most other countries that have transitioned to digital-first approaches. Here, we examine why the U.S. has not transitioned to digital-first Prescribing Information and assess whether the concerns raised a decade ago still have relevance today.

Legislative requirement for printed prescribing information

The principal reason the dual system is still in place is because there has been a legislative rider to the annual congressional bill since 2014, which prevents the FDA from changing it. Specifically the rider in H.R.4366 - Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2024 s 722 states: “None of the funds made available by this Act may be used to propose, promulgate, or implement any rule, or take any other action with respect to, allowing or requiring information intended for a prescribing health care professional, in the case of a drug or biological product subject to section 503(b)(1) of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (21 U.S.C. 353(b)(1)), to be distributed to such professional electronically (in lieu of in paper form) unless and until a Federal law is enacted to allow or require such distribution.” [23].

This rider was first introduced in 2014 in response to three main concerns about the FDA proposal.

Reduced access to prescribing information

In 2014, internet access in U.S. pharmacies, particularly in rural areas, was believed, by some, to be too limited and too unstable to serve as the primary means of prescribing information provision. Now, virtually all pharmacies have reliable digital access, primarily driven by the general increase in internet penetration over the last 10 years, from 73 % in 2014 to 97 % in 2024 [24], (Fig. 1). Additionally, most prescriptions are now filled electronically—94 % by 2021—and all States are making e-prescribing mandatory for controlled substances, as part of measures to tackle the opioid overdose epidemic that was enacted into law in the 2018 SUPPORT Act [[25], [26], [27]]. Thus, reliable pharmacy internet access is now the norm. Contingency planning for information provision in adverse weather conditions, or other causes of breakdown, would be required in a digital-first system, as for all services with digital infrastructure. Such planning is already routine in healthcare, as most systems are dependent on reliable internet access [28].

Reduced access to patient information

Patient-specific approved information is not required for most medications in the U.S., unlike many other nations. Instead, dispensers of medications can choose what information to provide. Some choose to give the relevant parts of the Prescribing Information, either by cutting out those sections or giving the full Prescribing Information with patient-relevant areas highlighted. There were concerns that removing the printed Prescribing Information would place an added burden on pharmacists to print information for patients. The 2014 rule was solely about prescribing information and did not address its unofficial use as patient information. However, FDA has recently proposed amendments to patient information [29], offering an opportunity to coordinate the revision of prescribing and patient information to mitigate this concern (see Section 6.3. below).

Negative impact on paper industry

Many in the paper industry, particularly from smaller mills, were concerned the 2014 rule would affect their livelihoods. Healthcare and pharmaceutical digitization and innovations over the last decade has led to substantial changes within the sector, including potential new opportunities for paper use, which mitigates this concern [30].

Benefits of digital-first prescribing information

Regulators across the world, including the U.S., have recommended using the digital-first system for disseminating medication information to healthcare professionals and this has been successfully implemented in many countries. This strong consensus comes from the triple benefits of the digital-first system in delivering healthcare, cost savings, and reduced environmental impact.

Healthcare benefits of digital-first prescribing information

The most important benefit of a digital-first system is in providing clarity regarding the primary source of information. The current dual system, in which both digital and print versions are considered equally valid, is challenging to deliver safely because Prescribing Information content is dynamic and often updated. The processes and timelines for digital and print updates differ substantially, leading to inconsistencies in the available information and risks of confusion, errors, and patient harm. Prescribing Information updates must be submitted digitally and are made immediately available on approval, through the FDA Label and DailyMed websites. In contrast, changes to print versions take much longer, typically 8–12 months from development to delivery. It is also difficult to ensure previous versions of printed Prescribing Information are not being used, whereas the most up-to-date digital version is always the default version available. In a digital-first system it would be clear to HCPs that they should refer to the digital version for the current information and that the current digital version should be printed whenever a physical copy was required.

Cost benefits of digital-first prescribing information

The digital-first system for Prescribing Information offers a substantial cost benefit, primarily by reducing the direct costs of printing, distributing, and disposing of billions of physical copies. Indirect costs are also reduced, for instance from less wasted medicines stock resulting from Prescribing Information updates and from fewer increases in lead time and machine downtime related to production [31]. An analysis in 2007 estimated that switching to digital-first Prescribing Information could produce up to $74 million annualized net savings (using a 7 percent discount rate) [32].

Environmental benefits of digital-first prescribing information

The environmental benefits of the digital-first system come from reducing the environmental impacts of printing Prescribing information. A digital-first system will still carry environmental burdens from provision and storage of digital information, but these are lower than printing information. Moreover, fully maintaining both systems in parallel is inherently more resource-intensive than a digital-first, print-as-required model.

Transitioning to digital-first prescribing information

Digital-first Prescribing Information offers substantial benefits for patients, healthcare, and the environment with previous concerns now reduced and manageable. The digital-first system also has strong support from regulators and most healthcare professionals and has been successfully implemented in many countries. Drive legislative change

The first, and essential, step towards transitioning to digital-first Prescribing Information is obtaining legislative approval that allows the FDA to implement the change and removes riders preventing transition. To achieve this, the H.R. 7559 Prescription Information Modernization Act of 2022 was introduced to the House in April 2022 but did not advance beyond the committee stage [35,36]. In response, a new version—H.R. 1503, Prescription Information Modernization Act of 2023—was introduced and has 31 co-sponsors [35]. Successful passage of the Act will enable transition to commence. If the Act does not pass and the rider preventing transition remains, consideration should be given to initiating studies to provide formal evidence that previous concerns are now manageable, and to further articulate the benefits of the digital-first system and potential harms of the dual system.

Maintain access to printed prescribing information

The digital-first system requires robust systems that ensure printed information is readily accessible whenever needed. These must encompass both routine and emergency situations when digital access is unavailable or compromised. Ensuring provision of printed information is core to the 2014 FDA rule and the 2023 Prescription Information Modernization Act [22,36]. There are various approaches by which this could be achieved, and a stipulation of the 2023 Act is for a public workshop to be held not later than two years after enactment to explore and optimize the format, accessibility, and usability of Prescribing Information. One simple, practical measure would be to establish a dedicated freephone number to request printed Prescribing Information.

Provide approved patient information

In countries where digital-first medication information for healthcare professionals has been successfully implemented, the provision of approved, patient-specific medication information is also standard practice. For instance, in the EU, patient information is derived from the HCP information and communicated through a print-first system for all prescription medicines (Table 1; Table 2). In contrast, in the U.S., patient information provision is variable and approved patient information is limited. The absence of approved patient information has led to some pharmacists giving the Prescribing Information to patients. To maintain this practice in a digital-first system would require such pharmacists to print out the PI to give to patients, which many considered an unacceptable burden. Furthermore, the PI is not designed for patients and there has been strong advocacy for simple, patient-tailored information to be routinely provided with prescription medicines [37].

In response to this need, in 2023, the FDA proposed a new rule entitled ‘Medication Guides: Patient Medication Information’ [29] which would mandate medicine manufacturers to provide a new type of patient information for all outpatient prescription medications, called Patient Medication Information (PMI). Patient Medication Information will consist of a standardized, one-page document approved by the FDA, that would be available in both printed and digital formats. If the rule is implemented it would greatly facilitate transition to digital-first Prescribing Information, as it would remove the need for any pharmacist to provide patient information through printing the PI.

Support stakeholder transition

A successful transition from a dual system to a digital-first approach will require clear and comprehensive communication to HCPs and other stakeholders. Proactive engagement, with clarity on the requirements and timelines of the new processes, will enable a seamless shift to the digital-first system that maintains stakeholder confidence and PI provision.

Under the stipulations of the 2023 Patient Medication Information Act, should it be enacted, the effective date for the transition is set as two years after enactment [36]. During these two years, efforts must be directed towards educating and preparing stakeholders for the upcoming changes, and in building robust, clear processes for PI provision if internet access is inadequate or compromised, to ensure uninterrupted access, and to safeguard patient care in all circumstances.

Supporting stakeholder transitions should also address concerns of paper manufacturers that they may suffer economic impacts due to reduced paper demand. To mitigate potential adverse effects, dialogue and collaborative efforts to identify new opportunities for paper usage within the pharmaceutical industry and beyond would be helpful. Embracing sustainable solutions that optimize paper resources and reduce single-use are already transforming the paper industry, aligning it with the evolving digitization and decarbonization of healthcare [30]. Innovative applications are also gaining momentum, for example, using paper industry waste as a source of chemical precursors that could be used by the pharmaceutical industry [38].

Furthermore, paper is a vital commodity that has been subject to shortages and supply chain challenges in recent years, with minimizing unnecessary paper use becoming increasingly important [39]. The digital-first system promotes responsible paper usage that optimizes the balance of digital and paper medication information provision.

Anticipate future opportunities and risks

The shift to a digital-first system for Prescribing Information presents numerous opportunities to optimize medication information for HCPs and patients. Anticipating and preparing for these, where possible, will allow the benefits to be maximized and risks to be minimized.

Opportunities

Digital information offers inherent advantages, being easily searchable, interoperable, and translatable. It is crucial to design and implement initiatives that capitalize on these existing benefits for medication information. Expanding search functionality and seamless integration with electronic health records and pharmacy systems can enable tailored content to be readily accessible. The potential for global analyses and collaboration to scrutinize and optimize medication information systems will grow in scope and impact [40].

Artificial intelligence (AI) is actively transforming healthcare and offers exciting possibilities for improved healthcare, user experience, and resource utilization. AI-driven chatbot interfaces powered by large language models (LLMs) are being introduced to provide interactive, user-friendly support for HCPs and patients. Such systems can improve accessibility of digital content, provide more detailed information, analyze usage patterns, and identify and address comprehension challenges [41]. For example, LLMs are being evaluated in postmarketing surveillance of medical products, by identifying adverse event signals from electronic health records, potentially improving safety communications and regulatory decisions [42].

Risks

Reliance on digital platforms introduces risks such as technical issues, cyberattacks, and internet connectivity disruptions that could impede access to vital information. To mitigate these risks, robust back-up solutions and contingency plans must be established, and be subject to ongoing review to ensure they continuously adapt to evolving threats.

While a digital-first system offers many opportunities to improve inclusivity, accessibility, and equity, it also risks disadvantaging those with limited digital access or capabilities, including social barriers such as low digital literacy. Identifying and providing tailored solutions to these individuals will be essential to ensure equity in access to medication information. Additionally, although a digital-first approach will reduce environmental impacts compared with a dual system, the environmental impacts may still be substantial and/or avoidable. This needs to be monitored and addressed, where possible [43].

By proactively addressing these future opportunities and risks, medication information can be at the forefront of innovation and sustainability, enhancing patient safety and responsible healthcare decision-making.

Conclusion

The transition from a dual system to a digital-first approach for Prescribing Information offers a significant opportunity to streamline healthcare practices, reduce environmental impacts, and improve patient care. By embracing proposed legislative and regulatory changes, maintaining access to printed information, and providing approved patient-centric information, the healthcare sector can align with global standards and leverage the power of digitization. The potential benefits extend beyond Prescribing Information, with the broader healthcare landscape, such as regulatory submissions and health records, poised for comprehensive dual-to-digital-first transformations, that may provide opportunities to advance healthcare delivery, promote equity and build more environmentally responsible, and sustainable health systems.
