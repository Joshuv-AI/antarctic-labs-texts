Types identification, patterns characterize and pathway optimization of synergistic development between urbanization and pollution control in Chinese urban agglomerations
Authors: Yushu Qin, Hongtao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAgainst the backdrop of China’s carbon peaking and neutrality goals, the way urban agglomerations coordinate rapid urbanization with pollution control has direct implications for energy use and the low-carbon transition. Yet evidence at the urban-agglomeration scale remains limited: synergy is often reduced to an overall score or coarse category, within-agglomeration spatiotemporal patterns are rarely integrated, and policy implications stay generic because outcomes are seldom linked to subsystem-level drivers. To address these gaps, this study examines nineteen major urban agglomerations in China and develops a unified evaluation and diagnostic framework that supports comparable city-level type identification, agglomeration-scale spatiotemporal mapping, and subsystem-based explanation of coupling outcomes. Using a consistent indicator system, we first apply the entropy–TOPSIS method to measure city-level urbanization and pollution control, then construct a coupling–coordination typology and track the spatiotemporal evolution of coordination types within and across agglomerations. We further decompose subsystem influences to identify binding constraints and derive agglomeration-specific optimization directions. The results show that: (1) most cities are concentrated in medium-level development patterns, with clear within-agglomeration heterogeneity and core–periphery gradients; (2) coupling coordination improves over time but remains generally weak, with progress concentrated in core cities and slow diffusion to surrounding cities, so maladjustment persists widely; and (3) coordination outcomes are constrained by asymmetric subsystem influences, meaning that at least one subsystem in most agglomerations persistently acts as a bottleneck. Overall, the study provides a reproducible basis for diagnosing where and why synergy remains weak and for designing differentiated, subsystem-targeted pathways to support greener and lower-carbon development in China’s urban agglomerations.Keywords: Urban agglomerations, Synergistic development, Low-carbon transition, Optimization pathwaysSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

Over the past forty years, China‘s urbanization rate has risen by about 1.04 percentage points per year, a pace that is rare globally1. This expansion has been underpinned by fossil-fuel-intensive industry, construction and transport, which has driven up energy demand and aggravated air and water pollution in many cities2,3. The traditional growth-first model is now difficult to sustain under the country’s carbon peaking and neutrality targets and the “14th Five-Year Plan”, which call for greener urbanization, cleaner energy systems and tighter environmental constraints4. These domestic targets are closely aligned with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), which emphasize that urban development must be embedded in effective social governance at the city level and in social governance structures that internalize environmental responsibilities5,6. Pollution control has therefore moved from a corrective add-on to a core requirement for high-quality development, directly linked to how cities use, save and substitute energy. In this transition, urban agglomerations have become the main spatial platform. They concentrate population, industry, infrastructure and governance capacity, and thus simultaneously amplify the environmental pressures created by dense energy use and provide the scale advantages needed for cleaner technologies and coordinated regulation. In this setting, the relationship between urbanization and pollution control is neither purely positive nor purely negative and is characterized by substantial uncertainty typical of sociotechnical systems7. At early stages, rapid land expansion, industrial clustering and motorization often intensify emissions and environmental stress. With rising income, stronger regulation and cleaner energy structures, the two systems can move toward synergy, where improvements in urbanization quality coincide with better pollution control and lower emission intensity. Yet why does synergy remain weak or fragile in many urban agglomerations even when basic pollution indicators improve? How do asymmetries among economic, population, social and spatial subsystems of urbanization shape coordination outcomes across different development stages? These questions are central to designing governance strategies that match differentiated urbanization paths with appropriate pollution control instruments. Against this background, this paper examines nineteen major urban agglomerations in China to assess the coupled evolution of urbanization and pollution control. By constructing a unified evaluation framework for both subsystems and applying coupling–coordination and spatiotemporal analysis, we identify prevailing development patterns, reveal where coordination remains weak or imbalanced, and derive differentiated optimization pathways for improving system performance8. The aim is to provide an evidence base for city-cluster strategies that can simultaneously support urbanization, strengthen pollution control and ease the energy- and carbon-intensity pressures of China’s path toward sustainable development. Literature review

To quantify the size of the existing evidence base, we conducted three separate searches in the Web of Science under restricted search expression. For studies on the coupling and coordination between urbanization and pollution/environmental governance, we identified 43 related articles. At the urban-agglomeration scale, focusing on the spatiotemporal patterns of synergy, only 2 directly relevant articles were found. For research on subsystem influence and diagnostic pathways within the coupled system, we identified 9 articles. These figures suggest that evidence on agglomeration-scale integration and subsystem-level diagnostics remains relatively scarce.

In the course of socioeconomic transformation, urbanization and pollution control form two complex systems that advance in parallel and shape each other. Existing studies often conceptualize them as an urbanization system and a pollution control system and examine how their interactions change across development stages9. As high-quality urbanization, ecological civilization, and regional collaborative governance have become more prominent, research has moved beyond single factor explanations toward multidimensional assessment. Urbanization measures now extend beyond the urbanization rate to capture population dynamics, economic development, public services, and spatial form. Measures of pollution control have also expanded from single pollutant emissions or environmental quality indicators to integrated evaluations of governance inputs, governance capacity, and governance performance, with closer attention to institutional design and policy implementation.

Research on how urbanization affects pollution control or environmental quality is increasingly organized around mechanism-based linkages10. City expansion, the concentration of transport and industrial activity, and rising energy use can increase pollution pressure through a scale effect, raise governance costs, and generate cross boundary externalities. At the same time, industrial upgrading, technological progress, energy structure optimization, and public infrastructure improvements can reduce emissions intensity through structural and technological effects and improve governance performance. Many studies also document nonlinear and stage specific patterns. The relationship between urbanization and pollution is often marked by thresholds and turning points as income levels, industrial structure, and governance capacity change, which is consistent with an environmental Kuznets curve type trajectory11. Pollution outcomes and governance performance also exhibit strong spatial dependence. Pollution diffusion, industrial relocation, and uneven policy enforcement can amplify spillovers, which weakens the ability of a single administrative unit to reach an internal optimum and raises the importance of cross regional collaboration12.

On the pollution control to urbanization, the literature has shifted from treating pollution control mainly as a constraint to highlighting its role in shaping pathways toward high quality urbanization13. Improved air quality and public health can enhance livability and human capital accumulation, which can strengthen a city’s attraction for people and investment and improve competitiveness. Environmental regulation and governance investment can also affect firm costs, innovation incentives, and location decisions, which can guide industrial restructuring and factor reallocation and reshape the speed, quality, and spatial pattern of urbanization. This view recognizes that pollution control is not only a cost. Under certain conditions, it can serve as institutional support and a public goods base for high quality urban development. The magnitude and direction of these effects often depend on governance capacity, fiscal affordability, industrial endowments, and marketization, which leads to substantial heterogeneity across places14.

As bidirectional mechanisms have become clearer, research has begun to focus on co evolution between urbanization and pollution control15. One stream uses composite indices and related methods to describe spatiotemporal differentiation and to show how synergy levels reflect mutual constraints between the two subsystems and stage specific dominant mechanisms. Results commonly point to a shift from low synergy toward medium to higher synergy. Another stream brings in inter-city network linkages, regional integration, and policy coordination. It shows that connection strength, core periphery structure, and cross boundary governance arrangements can shape synergy efficiency and can reduce administrative fragmentation and improve collaborative governance performance16.

The existing literature offers a strong basis for understanding interactions between urbanization and pollution control, but several gaps remain, summary in Table 1. Current work often describes synergy in terms of overall level or broad categories, which limits fine grained and comparable type identification across cities and urban agglomerations. Evidence on spatiotemporal patterns is also less cohesive at the urban agglomeration scale. Many studies document spatial dependence or general trends, but they less often present an integrated picture of agglomeration level distributions, core periphery gradients, and stage specific evolution. This leaves key questions unresolved, including how synergy patterns form, how they change over time, and which areas persist in maladjustment. Policy implications also tend to remain general. Few studies trace overall outcomes back to the direction and strength of subsystem effects within urbanization and pollution control, which weakens diagnostic guidance and makes it difficult to design optimization pathways that target specific weak links in different urban agglomerations. Since urban agglomerations have become the primary spatial carriers of China’s new type urbanization, a framework that supports systematic measurement, type identification, spatiotemporal analysis, and subsystem-based diagnosis is needed to clarify bottlenecks and improvement directions for cross regional collaborative governance.

Measuring urbanization and pollution control in urban agglomerations and examining what shapes their synergy are essential for addressing practical questions about why the interaction matters, how a synergy system can be built, and what obstacles hinder progress. This study contributes in three ways. It develops a comparable typology of coupling coordination between urbanization and pollution control across urban agglomerations. It establishes a unified standard for synergy types and identifies and compares city level types within each agglomeration, which strengthens structured interpretation of heterogeneity and status differences. It then documents spatiotemporal differentiation in synergy at the urban agglomeration scale through visualization and time series comparisons. This analysis highlights spatial distribution, stage-based change, and center periphery differences, and it shows an overall shift from maladjustment toward coordination with persistent unevenness. Finally, it provides diagnostic pathway recommendations that reflect subsystem influence. It quantifies the direction and magnitude of effects from the four urbanization subsystems and the three pollution control subsystems on overall coupling coordination, identifies key hindering and driving factors, and proposes optimization pathways that target weak subsystems in different urban agglomerations. This design links measurement, mechanism diagnosis, and policy recommendations in a coherent sequence.

Theoretical mechanisms

Urbanization is a complex, multidimensional process that reshapes a region’s economic, demographic, social, and spatial structure17. Decisions in this process are made under high uncertainty and involve interacting subsystems, as is typical in complex system reliability contexts18. Prior research often frames urbanization along four dimensions, economic, population, social, and spatial19,20. Following this line of work, we construct four subsystems to assess urbanization in urban agglomerations. These subsystems include economic urbanization, population urbanization, social urbanization, and spatial urbanization. Economic urbanization reflects the growth engine of regional development. Population urbanization captures the concentration and transformation of the labor force. Social urbanization reflects changes in lifestyles, value orientations, and public services and safeguards. Spatial urbanization represents the physical expression and carrier of economic and population urbanization.

Environmental governance refers to institutions and actions that aim to improve ecological conditions by using pollution control to reduce emissions and related impacts. Pollution control in China involves broad scope, layered interactions, and complex relationships. We adopt the PSR framework21,22 and build an evaluation system for urban pollution control that includes three subsystems. These are pollution control pressure, pollution control state, and pollution control response. Pollution control pressure captures the relationship between human activity and governance demands within the study unit. Pollution control state describes the prevailing environmental conditions relevant to governance. Pollution control response reflects measures taken by governments at different levels to address pollution.

Separate measurement of urbanization and pollution control also provides a basis for examining coupling and synergy and for analyzing their determinants. Figure 2 illustrates that urbanization connects with pollution control through economic development, population growth, social progress, and spatial expansion. Economic urbanization is reflected in shifts in industrial structure and production scale, and it can influence pollution control pressure in two directions. Expansion of nonagricultural activity raises resource use and can intensify pressure. Rising pressure also increases ecological protection costs, and emission constraints may limit economic expansion over a period, which can slow economic urbanization9. Social urbanization is reflected in changes in lifestyles, values, and service systems. As it advances, it can strengthen pollution control responses, and governance responses can also feed back into social change. Population urbanization is reflected in higher population density and a larger share of employment in secondary and tertiary industries. These shifts increase demand for environmental and resource inputs and can affect the pollution control state. The environmental conditions faced in pollution control also shape local carrying capacity and, in turn, the scale and structure of population concentration.

Conceptual model of coordinated development of urban agglomeration urbanization and pollution control.

Economic and population urbanization also reshape local landscapes through boundary expansion and higher construction density, which is captured by spatial urbanization23. As economic activity and population concentration grow, developable land tends to become scarcer. Unplanned sprawl can directly worsen the pollution control state and intensify pressure24. Poor alignment among urban space, the economy, and population concentration increases environmental burdens and reduces the integration of ecological elements in built environments, which can constrain progress across urbanization dimensions. As social urbanization improves, more scientific spatial regulation can also mitigate some negative effects of urbanization on pollution control. These dynamics suggest that interaction stress between urbanization and pollution control is expressed mainly through the use, depletion, and governance of available space. Spatial urbanization acts as a key mediator that links economic and population urbanization to pollution control state and pressure through indirect pathways. Social urbanization has a more direct relationship with pollution control response. For this reason, this study analyzes urbanization and pollution control both separately and as a coupled system, and it examines how each subsystem contributes to overall coupling coordination.

Classification of development patterns

To examine temporal differences in the development patterns of urbanization and pollution control across cities within urban agglomerations, this study classifies and summarizes different cities’ urbanization and pollution control development patterns in China’s urban agglomerations. We first calculate, for each year and each urban agglomeration, the mean value of every subsystem index of urbanization (population, economic, social and spatial) and pollution control (pressure, state and response) across all cities in that agglomeration as the thresholds. Then, compare each subsystem scores with these corresponding mean values and classify development patterns according to the number of subsystem indices that are above the mean, the stronger the development type assigned. On this basis, urbanization is divided into five types (from “strong” to “weak” as the count falls from four to zero), and pollution control into four types (from “strong” to “weak” as the count falls from three to zero), as summarized in Table 2.

Long-term analytical maintenance and reproducibility

To enhance reproducibility and support long-horizon applications, we provide a brief note on long-term analytical maintenance for the entropy–TOPSIS evaluation and coupling coordination framework. Indicator-weight recalibration: because entropy weights are data-driven and depend on the information dispersion of indicators within the sample, adding new year or cities requires re-estimating entropy weights on the updated dataset. Threshold drift: for pattern classification steps that rely on relative thresholds such as mean-based comparisons, thresholds may shift as the sample composition or time horizon changes. This study adopts a relative scheme to capture within-period heterogeneity, but if stronger intertemporal comparability is desired, it can additionally implement fixed benchmark thresholds or rolling-window thresholds. Temporal smoothing: all indices in this study are computed annually without smoothing to preserve observed year-to-year variation. For longer horizons or more volatile series, simple smoothing procedures such as moving averages can be applied to the subsystem and composite indices prior to coupling calculations to reduce noise. Together, these practices clarify how key parameters can be transparently updated as new data emerge and allow consistent replication and extension of the analysis over long time spans.

Taken together, these dimensions provide a concise yet comprehensive picture of both the level and the internal structure of urbanization at the urban-agglomeration scale, and make it possible to identify structural imbalances and stage-specific differences in urbanization paths. Within each subsystem, we select indicators that are representative of its core connotation, sensitive to changes in urbanization quality, and comparable across agglomerations and years, while avoiding redundancy among highly collinear variables. Population urbanization captures population concentration and related development and serves as the fundamental carrier of urbanization. Economic urbanization reflects the main driving force and core content of urbanization. Social urbanization indicates residents’ living standards and the diffusion of civilization in the study area, which broadens the meaning of urbanization. Spatial urbanization refers to changes in land use structure and provides a direct indication of the level of urban construction.

From the perspective of pollution control, this study draws on the PSR model to develop an evaluation index system for urban pollution control. The PSR model, originally developed to describe linkages within ecological systems and now widely applied in environmental assessment, is generally regarded as a systematic and scientifically grounded way to organize environmental information22. It is particularly suitable here because it mirrors the basic governance logic of “emission pressure–environmental state–policy and management response”.

In our application, pollution control pressure captures pollution impacts and loads generated by natural factors or human activities that must be offset or mitigated. Pollution control state describes the current environmental conditions that governance must address in the study area. Pollution control response reflects the actions and efforts undertaken by the study area and governments at different levels to address environmental pollution. Considered together, the three dimensions provide an integrated view of the balance between pollution generation, environmental outcomes and governance effort, and thus of the overall pattern and stage of pollution control in each urban agglomeration. Indicator selection follows the principles of comprehensiveness, systematic coverage, representativeness and data availability. Within each PSR category, we choose indicators that are closely aligned with its core meaning, sensitive to changes in pollution control performance, and comparable across agglomerations and years, while avoiding redundancy among highly collinear variables. Because urban agglomerations and their constituent cities differ markedly in scale, location and natural endowments, we rely primarily on per capita and percentage indicators and structure the evaluation system into three PSR-based subsystems. Data sources

Based on development planning documents for the nineteen urban agglomerations approved and issued by the State Council, the National Development and Reform Commission, and provincial, including municipalities and autonomous regions governments, this study uses China’s nineteen major urban agglomerations as the units of analysis. The data are drawn primarily from the China City Statistical Yearbook (2011–2020) and the China Regional Economic Statistical Yearbook (2011–2020), as well as annual statistical bulletins on national economic and social development released by prefecture-level cities within the study period. In some urban agglomerations, county-level cities administered by prefecture-level cities or directly governed by provinces are included. To avoid overlapping samples, these county-level cities are excluded from the statistics. In addition, cities such as Danzhou and Turpan are excluded due to excessive missing data. Following these procedures, 202 prefecture-level cities are ultimately retained as the data units. China’s 1:1,000,000 fundamental geographic dataset, published by the National Geomatics Center of China (http://www.ngcc.cn) in 2020, provides the vector administrative boundary information used in this study. Based on these data, we used ArcGIS 10.8 to merge city-level administrative units and delineate the boundaries of each urban agglomeration, thereby generating the corresponding vector maps.

Figure 3 reports urbanization patterns. Among the five national level urban agglomerations, the Pearl River Delta performs best overall. With the exception of Zhaoqing, Jiangmen, and Huizhou, all cities are classified as relatively strong urbanization or above. Most Pearl River Delta cities fall in the top tier among national level agglomerations. The Chengdu Chongqing urban agglomeration performs less strongly. Only Chengdu reaches strong urbanization once, which sets it apart from the other cities. Among the eight regional level urban agglomerations, performance is generally weak, except in the Northern Slope of the Tianshan Mountains urban agglomeration. In most regional level agglomerations, relatively strong urbanization is concentrated in the core city or provincial capital, while many other cities are classified as moderate or weak. The six local level urban agglomerations show a similar pattern. Only the core city or provincial capital performs relatively well, and the overall level remains lower than in regional level agglomerations. In aggregate, cities in national level urban agglomerations tend to show stronger urbanization than those in regional level agglomerations, while local level agglomerations are most often at the lower end. Within each agglomeration, the central city typically advances faster than surrounding areas. Across all agglomerations, urbanization shows a clear declining gradient from east to west.

Figure 4 reports pollution control patterns. Among the five national level urban agglomerations, the Pearl River Delta again performs best overall. Aside from Zhaoqing, Zhongshan, and Jiangmen, the remaining cities are classified as strong pollution control. Many observations also fall in relatively strong pollution control or above, and most Pearl River Delta cities are in the top tier among national level agglomerations. The Beijing Tianjin Hebei urban agglomeration performs somewhat less strongly. Only Beijing and Hengshui are classified as strong pollution control, and they form the leading group within this agglomeration. Among the eight regional level urban agglomerations, the West Coast of the Taiwan Strait agglomeration performs best. Wenzhou, Xiamen, Putian, Shantou, Shanwei, and Chaozhou record relatively strong pollution control in more than half of the observations. The Central and Southern Liaoning urban agglomeration performs poorly. None of its cities exhibit strong pollution control, and most observations fall in moderate pollution control or below. Among the six local level urban agglomerations, the Lanxi agglomeration performs relatively well. Dingxi and Haidong exceed half in the count of relatively strong pollution control. Cities in the Jinzhong and Dianzhong agglomerations are mostly classified as moderate or weak, and none are identified as strong. Overall, regional central cities tend to outperform surrounding areas in pollution control. Better performance is more common in cities with a tertiary industry-oriented structure or a light industry base, while weaker performance is more common in resource-based cities and those reliant on heavy manufacturing in the secondary sector. At a broader scale, better performance is more common in eastern coastal areas than in western inland regions, and more common south than north of it, result aligned with previous literatures25,26.

Table 6 shows that general urbanization is the most common pattern across cities in China’s urban agglomerations, while strong urbanization is relatively rare. The shares of relatively strong, medium, and weak urbanization cities are more balanced. The Yangtze River Delta and the Pearl River Delta stand out, with more strong urbanization cities and a combined share of relatively strong urbanization and above exceeding 40%. This suggests comparative advantages in population, economic, social, and spatial urbanization relative to other urban agglomerations nationwide. In eight urban agglomerations, weak urbanization cities account for more than 30%. The shares are particularly high in the Harbin Changchun and Central and Southern Liaoning urban agglomerations, where they reach about 50%.

For pollution control, Table 7 indicates that cities classified from general to relatively strong pollution control account for the largest share, creating a distribution that is concentrated in the middle and thinner at the extremes. The Pearl River Delta, Chengdu Chongqing, and West Coast of the Taiwan Strait urban agglomerations include more cities with relatively strong pollution control and above, and each exceeds 50%. These agglomerations perform well in addressing pollution control pressure, improving pollution control state, and strengthening pollution control response. Urban agglomerations with higher shares of weak pollution control cities are mainly found in areas that remain dependent on heavy industry or where structural upgrading has lagged, such as the Central and Southern Liaoning, Jinzhong, and Dianzhong urban agglomerations. In these cases, the share of weak pollution control cities exceeds 30%, and it approaches 50% in Jinzhong, indicating the weakest overall pollution control performance.

Table 8 shows that, over the study period, the mean counts across China’s urban agglomerations are 9.6 for strong urbanization, 33.6 for relatively strong urbanization, 24.1 for medium urbanization, 83.7 for general urbanization, and 51 for weak urbanization. Most urban agglomerations follow a general urbanization path. Regional development remains poorly coordinated, resources are concentrated in central cities, and many cities within urban agglomerations still have substantial room to advance. From 2010 to 2019, the count of strong urbanization cities increases steadily, while other patterns show partial shifts over time. By 2019, the number of weak urbanization cities shows no clear decline from 2010 after several fluctuations. This suggests that regional development imbalances were not eased during 2010 to 2019. Areas with stronger foundations continued to progress, while weaker areas remained largely stagnant, widening the gap11.

Table 9 indicates that, from 2010 to 2019, the mean counts across China’s urban agglomerations are 86.8 for general pollution control, 59.1 for relatively strong pollution control, 40.9 for weak pollution control, and 15.2 for strong pollution control. Most cities fall into the general pollution control category, indicating that pollution control challenges remain serious at the national level. Using the criteria for pollution control patterns, the period can be divided into three stages. The first stage is 2010. No cities are classified as strong pollution control, and weak pollution control accounts for about 85%, which makes 2010 distinct from later years. The second stage covers 2011 to 2014. The number of weak pollution control cities declines relative to 2010, while overall changes remain limited. The third stage spans 2015 to 2019. Strong pollution control becomes more common, weak pollution control declines, and pollution control levels begin to show regular up and down fluctuations that are not observed in the earlier stages.

Spatiotemporal differentiation in coupling coordination levels in urban agglomerations

Using formula 17, we calculate the coupling coordination degree index between urbanization and pollution control for each city in China’s urban agglomerations. This provides an overall view of the spatiotemporal evolution of coupling coordination. We then visualize coupling coordination levels for cities in four selected years using ArcGIS 10.8, as shown in Fig. 5. In most prefecture level cities, the coordination degree between the urbanization and pollution control systems remains in basic maladjustment. The overall trajectory is upward over time. Areas of severe maladjustment gradually shrink and eventually disappear, while areas of advanced coordination become more widespread. Combined with the results in Figs. 5 and 6, the Yangtze River Delta and the Pearl River Delta include the largest number of cities at basic coordination and above, and they remain the strongest performers. This pattern indicates that places with stronger urbanization and stronger pollution control are also more likely to reach basic coordination or higher. The Beijing Tianjin Hebei urban agglomeration shows a different profile. Urbanization has advanced in many cities, but pollution control has lagged, leaving most cities in basic maladjustment. Only Beijing and Tianjin reach advanced coordination and basic coordination, respectively. Overall, higher coordination does not stem from the exceptional performance of a single system. It requires urbanization and pollution control to improve in tandem. It also requires diagnosing the key drivers through subsystem performance so that targeted optimization pathways can raise coupling coordination across cities within urban agglomerations.

Coupling coordination types in urban agglomerations

After obtaining the coupling coordination degree index for cities in urban agglomerations, we classify each city into a coupling coordination type based on the corresponding criteria and visually presented by ArcGIS 10.8. Figure 6 shows that most cities in China’s urban agglomerations fall into the basic maladjustment category. Within this range, most cities exhibit system balance, while relatively few are classified as urbanization constrained or pollution control constrained. Among cities in the basic coordination category, pollution control lagging and system balance account for similar shares, and relatively few cities are classified as urbanization lagging. Within the advanced coordination category, pollution control lagging is the dominant type, system balance is less common, and no cities are classified as urbanization lagging. This pattern suggests that under coordinated conditions, pollution control exerts a stronger influence on overall coordination than urbanization. Under maladjustment, both systems face comparable constraints.

From 2010 to 2019, the Chengdu Chongqing urban agglomeration shows the largest change. In 2010, it has the highest number of severely maladjusted cities among all urban agglomerations. By 2013, all cities move out of severe maladjustment and then remain largely stable within the basic maladjustment range, with system balance as the dominant type. The Yangtze River Delta and the Pearl River Delta perform best overall. They include the largest number of cities at basic coordination and above, and they also have the highest shares. Over time, more cities reach basic coordination and above, but these gains are concentrated in regional central cities and a subset of surrounding small and medium sized cities. Most areas within urban agglomerations remain in basic maladjustment. Regional inequality remains pronounced, and the study period shows no clear sign of broad improvement. This finding is broadly consistent with prior studies on closely related topics, thereby providing evidence for the robustness of our results27.

Subsystem influence decomposition for urban agglomerations

Building on the preceding analysis, we decompose the influence of urbanization and pollution control subsystems within urban agglomerations. The decomposition traces how the four urbanization subsystems affect the overall coupling coordination degree in both magnitude and direction. We examine subsystem influence trends using four benchmark years, 2010, 2013, 2016, and 2019, as shown in Figs. 7 and 8. On the urbanization side, the positive contribution is concentrated in a single component and remains visible across all agglomerations, while the remaining components are mostly negative or near-neutral; this produces an “asymmetric support” structure in which the urbanization system can assist coordination but rarely does so uniformly across its internal dimensions. On the pollution control side, the influence profile is typically “source-and stock-dominated”: the components reflecting ongoing emission loads and accumulated environmental conditions are persistently negative, whereas the component reflecting governance effort is positive but comparatively smaller and, in several interior agglomerations, becomes unstable in later years.

A first group consists of high-output, densely connected coastal megaregions, including the YRD, PRD, SP, and the YMZ. In these agglomerations, the negative influence associated with ongoing environmental loads can be very pronounced, reflecting the sheer scale of production, freight flows, and energy consumption concentrated in globally connected manufacturing service networks. Yet the governance effort component is also consistently present and more visible than in most inland agglomerations. Structurally, these regions combine stronger fiscal capacity, denser monitoring and regulatory infrastructures, and faster diffusion of cleaner technologies through integrated supply chains and competitive local policy regimes. This configuration explains why the coordination trajectory can improve even when pressure-related influences remain strongly negative: governance capacity and technological options are sufficiently strong to partially counterbalance scale-driven loads, rather than eliminating them. On the urbanization side, the influence profile in these megaregions is comparatively concentrated: supportive effects are clear, while the other components remain small in magnitude and often negative, consistent with a mature urban system in which marginal improvements depend on fine-grained adjustments rather than broad-based gains across all urbanization dimensions.

A second group comprises old industrial bases and heavy-industry corridors, including BTH, CSL, HC, the CP. Their influence profiles display a different structural logic. Negative influences tied to environmental loads and environmental stock conditions remain consistently large, which is compatible with persistent coal dependence, a high share of steel/petrochemical/power and related upstream–downstream chains, and the legacy effects of long-term industrial emissions on local environmental baselines. Meanwhile, the governance-effort component is present but does not expand enough to visibly reweight the overall profile. On the urbanization side, these agglomerations show broader negativity across multiple urbanization components, which is consistent with industrial path dependence and adjustment frictions: upgrading is more capital-intensive, land and infrastructure investment may be used to sustain growth during restructuring, and public-service improvement can lag behind the speed of physical expansion and industrial relocation. In other words, the subsystem pattern reflects a structural setting where the economic base generating environmental pressure and the institutional capacity to offset it are both constrained by legacy assets and transition costs, producing a high-inertia coupling environment28.

A third group includes resource-dependent, ecologically constrained, and late-developing inland agglomerations, such as HBEY, LX, NAYR, the NSTM, CY, CG, and the BG. Here, the influence decomposition points to a distinct mechanism: negative effects on the pollution-control side are often anchored not only in emission loads but also in ecological carrying-capacity constraints and fragility of baseline environmental conditions, so the state component remains persistently negative even when response efforts are observable. In addition, several of these agglomerations show instability in the governance-effort component in later years, suggesting that governance responses are more sensitive to fiscal cycles, investment discontinuities, or institutional capacity limits than in coastal megaregions. On the urbanization side, influence magnitudes are often smaller and less diversified, consistent with a development structure where growth relies more on resource extraction, administrative investment projects, and corridor-based expansion, while diversified producer services and innovation-led upgrading remain thinner.

To develop optimization pathways for the synergistic development of urbanization and pollution control in urban agglomerations, this study computes the 2010 to 2019 mean coordination influence for each subsystem and treats these means as path coefficients, as reported in Table 10. The results show that the sum of the three pollution control subsystems is negative for all urban agglomerations. The sum of the four urbanization subsystems is negative for all urban agglomerations except Central and Southern Liaoning, Shandong Peninsula, and Ningxia along the Yellow River. Further synthesis shows that, in the Central Plains and the Middle Reaches of the Yangtze River urban agglomerations, the mean values of all pollution control subsystems exceed the corresponding subsystem means across all urban agglomerations. In the Central and Southern Liaoning and Shandong Peninsula urban agglomerations, the mean values of all urbanization subsystems exceed the corresponding subsystem means across all urban agglomerations. In the remaining urban agglomerations, at least one subsystem has a path coefficient below the cross agglomeration mean for that subsystem.

Overall, within the urbanization subsystems, population urbanization and spatial urbanization operate in opposite directions. Population urbanization generally contributes positively to coordination because, in most urban agglomerations, higher levels of population urbanization are associated with a greater concentration of non-agricultural employment, more complete integration of rural migrants as urban residents, and broader coverage of basic public services. These changes raise residents’ expectations for better urban services and strengthen the fiscal and economic base of cities. Consequently, population urbanization tends to act as a force that supports, rather than undermines, more balanced and higher-quality urban development. By contrast, spatial urbanization tends to exert a persistently negative influence: in many urban agglomerations, urban land expansion is still characterized by extensive, low-density development and lagging functional integration, so each additional unit of built-up area increases energy use and emissions faster than it improves the conditions for joint pollution control. The overall coordination degree is therefore largely determined by the tension between these two subsystems.

Within the pollution control subsystems, pollution control pressure and pollution control state exert relatively large and consistently negative effects on the overall coordination degree. This pattern reflects the inertia of industrial and energy structures that continue to generate high pollutant pressure and the cumulative nature of environmental state, which adjusts only slowly even when new control measures are introduced. As a result, the “source” side of the system remains misaligned with the goals of synergistic governance, and pressure and state repeatedly appear as structural constraints rather than supportive forces in the coordination process.

These patterns also help to explain why the evolution of individual subsystems does not always move in step with the aggregate coordination index. By construction, the coordination influence indices capture the deviation of each subsystem’s coordination level from the overall coordination degree. Even when the overall index improves over time, a subsystem can continue to exert a negative influence if its own coordination with the other side lags behind the system average or improves more slowly. In addition, stronger performance in other subsystems can partially offset this deficit and lift the aggregate index, so subsystem trajectories that remain weak or only slightly improved may coexist with an upward trend in overall coordination. In this sense, persistent negative coordination influence values for specific subsystems reveal hidden imbalances in the coordination pattern that are not immediately visible from the aggregate coupling coordination index alone.

Optimization pathways based on pollution control subsystems

Pollution control. For pollution control pressure, subsystem influence is below the mean in the BTH, YRD, YMZ, PRD, BG, HBEY, and NSTM urban agglomerations. These urban agglomerations should prioritize balancing human activities, energy use, and environmental loads during development. They should also reduce excessive reliance on natural resources and mineral energy embedded in production structures and practices. Urban agglomeration growth is often accompanied by rapid population expansion and rising energy demand. In line with energy conservation and emissions reduction, these regions should gradually substitute new energy sources for traditional energy use. They should also accelerate the shift from labor intensive, low value added, and low barrier processing industries toward advanced manufacturing and modern services.

For pollution control response, subsystem influence is below the mean in the Guanzhong, YMZ, PRD, CC, BG, CY, CG, and LX urban agglomerations. These urban agglomerations should strengthen public learning and acceptance of pollution control concepts and practices. Governance should not remain confined to meeting discussions and written documents. Cities within each urban agglomeration should build shared norms of joint responsibility. They should share governance costs and share governance gains. Governance decisions should be adapted to local conditions. Pollution control research, industrial division of labor, and the allocation of human and material resources should be arranged in a coordinated and efficient manner to strengthen joint response capacity within the region.

For pollution control state, subsystem influence is below the mean in the BTH, CSL, YRD, SD, PRD, CS, HBEY, CY, LX, and NAYR urban agglomerations. These urban agglomerations should prioritize maintaining and increasing per capita water resources and the stock of ecological and environmental areas. They should also maintain sustained control over pollutant emissions. Pollution control state captures the environmental conditions that urban agglomerations face in practice. It is more visible than pressure and response, and residents can readily perceive deterioration and improvement in daily life. Urban agglomerations can use this visibility as an entry point. They can develop more people centered governance plans based on direct monitoring of pollutant emissions and environmental resource stocks.

Optimization pathways based on urbanization subsystems

Urbanization. For population urbanization, subsystem influence is below the mean in the BTH, YRD, PRD, HBEY, and NSTM urban agglomerations. These urban agglomerations should focus on easing the tension between rapid population growth during urbanization and the land resource supply demand balance. They should also raise the urbanization rate and increase the shares of employment in the secondary and tertiary sectors in cities beyond the central city. This can strengthen the spillover role of the central city and support a development pattern in which surrounding cities grow around the center and become more closely connected.

For economic urbanization, subsystem influence is below the mean in the BTH, CP, CS, YMZ, PRD, BG, CS’, CY, CG, and LX urban agglomerations. These urban agglomerations should place greater emphasis on per capita levels and accelerate growth in cities beyond the central city. They should improve the business environment and support industrial upgrading in the central city, while enabling reasonable and well-designed industrial transfer to surrounding small and medium sized cities. They should also increase the share of high value industries in the fiscal revenue structure to support healthier local revenue growth. Reliance on revenues from highly polluting industries and the real estate industry chain should be reduced.

For social urbanization, subsystem influence is below the mean in the CP, CS, YMZ, MRYR, CC, BG, CG, LX, and NAYR urban agglomerations. These urban agglomerations should prioritize improvements in residents’ quality of life. Urbanization calls for more than economic growth and infrastructure expansion. It also requires stronger urban soft power, including education resources, health care resources, and social welfare protection. These regions should also encourage healthier daily practices and norms, including rational consumption and healthy consumption, to build a virtuous cycle.

For spatial urbanization, subsystem influence is below the mean in the CS, CC, HC, BG, CS’, CY, CG, and LX urban agglomerations. These urban agglomerations should rely on scientific and sound planning to balance population growth, economic development, and urban spatial construction. They need adequate resource capacity to support population growth and economic activity. They also need to plan ahead and reserve sufficient land and related resources to sustain long term development in urban agglomerations.

Conclusion

This study builds a unified evaluation framework for urbanization and pollution control in China’s urban agglomerations and implements a coherent empirical workflow from entropy–TOPSIS index construction and development pattern classification to coupling coordination identification and subsystem influence decomposition, present not only whether coordination improves, but also where it improves, why it remains weak in many places, and which components act as the binding constraints. The main conclusions are as follows.

First, both urbanization and pollution control cluster around mid-level patterns and display a clear core–periphery divide within most agglomerations. Core cities consistently outperform surrounding smaller cities, and pollution-control strength tracks industrial structure: stronger performers are typically service- or light-industry-oriented, while weaker ones are more resource-based or heavy-industry-dependent. This manuscript analyzed the city-level pattern classification, rather than a single index, makes this within-agglomeration heterogeneity visible and helps explain why seemingly similar agglomerations deliver very different governance outcomes.

Second, Synergy between urbanization and pollution control increases over time, but coordination remains weak and is concentrated in regional core cities. Many cores shift from maladjustment to basic coordination, yet spillovers to surrounding cities are slow, leaving most members still maladjusted. By using spatiotemporal tracking of coupling types, it further shows that the national uptick is driven by localized gains rather than broad convergence, which helps explain why regional inequality in coordinated development remains persistent even as average coordination indicators rise.

Third, subsystem influence results show that weak coordination stems less from “low levels” than from structural misalignment across components. Coordination improves most when urbanization and pollution control progress in step, yet nearly every agglomeration has at least one subsystem that persistently drags the coupled system below the national benchmark for that component. By turning influence decomposition into subsystem-specific diagnostics pinpoints the binding constraints and offers clear leverage points for differentiated, agglomeration-tailored policy design.
