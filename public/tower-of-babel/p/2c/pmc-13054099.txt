Provision of palliative and end-of-life care by community health nurses: A scoping review on the challenges and potential solutions
Authors: Sophie Pask, Sarah Greenley, Chung Y Ip, Georgina Macdonald, Emel Yorganci, Alison Waring, Katherine E Sleeman, Miriam J Johnson, Catherine Walshe, Mark Pearson, Catherine J Evans, Fliss EM Murtagh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe demand for home-based palliative care is increasing, driven by ageing populations and a rise in multiple long-term conditions. Community health nurses are pivotal to enabling people to be cared for and die at home, yet their contributions to palliative and end-of-life care remain under-recognised and poorly understood. Previous international reviews have focused on specific themes but in-depth synthesis of evidence from one national health system has not been undertaken.AimThis scoping review aimed to identify, map and synthesise evidence within a single national health system on the community health nursing workforce in palliative and end-of-life care provision for adults living at home (including their role and responsibilities, and identifying challenges and potential solutions).MethodThis scoping review followed the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and the PRISMA-Scoping Review checklist statement. We systematically searched thirteen electronic databases and grey literature sources, from 2009 to 2025. UK-evidence reporting on community health nurses’ provision of palliative and end-of-life care to adults living at home was included. Data were charted using a structured data extraction form and grouped thematically to understand the nature of their roles and responsibilities, the challenges faced in providing such care, and potential solutions to mitigate these challenges.ResultsA total of 250 items/reports reflecting 239 included items/reports (11 merged as same project) were included. The main challenges community health nurses are facing in palliative and end-of-life care provision are driven by the rising demand and increasing complexity of the needs of persons dying at home. This requires rapid role evolvement, expertise, and effective integration with multiple providers and services, and impacts on staff wellbeing and morale. To mitigate these challenges, requires recognition of and training to support their rapidly evolving roles in palliative and end-of-life care and to meet the increasingly complex needs of persons dying at home and their families. There is limited understanding of safe caseloads and data to demonstrate the extent of palliative and end-of-life care provided by community health nurses.ConclusionsCommunity health nurses are essential to the delivery of palliative and end-of-life care at home. Addressing workforce capacity, role clarity, and training gaps is critical to meeting future demand and ensuring equitable, high-quality care. This review highlights the urgent need for these challenges in community health nursing to be addressed to support sustainable palliative and end-of-life care delivery.Keywords: Palliative care, End-of-life care, Community health nursing, Community medicine, Scoping review

Introduction

Palliative care aims to improve quality of life for people with advanced illness and their families through holistic care (including early identification, comprehensive assessment, symptom management and addressing social and spiritual needs). End-of-life care is an important part of this and aims to support people to live and die as well as possible in the last year of life (Adair, 2021). Community health nurses provide highly skilled holistic general nursing care within people’s homes and communities (McBride et al., 2024; Walshe and Luker, 2010), and are one of the main providers of palliative and end-of-life care within multidisciplinary teams worldwide (Rosa et al., 2022). They play a crucial role in supporting people to be cared for and die at home (McBride et al., 2024; Gomes et al., 2015; Costa et al., 2016). Their frequent visits and long-term relationships with patients and their families uniquely position them to manage fluctuations in care needs and deliver responsive person-centred care (Walshe and Luker, 2010). This relational approach to care allows for continuity, a good knowledge of health situations, and rapport that is grounded in respect, trust and compassion (Walshe and Luker, 2010; Lamph et al., 2023).

There is an increasing need for home palliative care to support current trends in deaths (driven by ageing populations and multiple long-term conditions) and to address inequitable access to palliative care (Bone et al., 2018; Sleeman et al., 2019; Clark et al., 2020; World Health Organisation 2020). Community health nurses are critical to universal provision of palliative care (Rosa et al., 2022) and access to home nursing care is central to health policy in many countries (Jarrín et al., 2019, UK Government 2025). Home is typically the preferred place for end-of-life care and place of death for people with advanced illness. (Pinto et al., 2024) Yet there is substantial global variation in the proportions with home deaths (Adair, 2021, Pivodic et al., 2016) and care for people at the end of life accounts for a significant proportion of hospital activity. (Leniz et al., 2022, Williamson et al., 2023, Robinson et al., 2016) Although a home death is not desirable or feasible for everyone, expanding the availability of home-based services is likely to better support people to die at home (if preferred and/or possible) (Bone et al., 2018, Sleeman et al., 2019, Leniz et al., 2022).

Community health nursing is shaped by the needs of the populations they serve, political influences, and health and care system organisational changes (McBride et al., 2024, L Polak et al., 2025, Sørstrøm et al., 2024, Bowers et al., 2025). However, the work of nurses is often invisible as care is delivered in people’s homes (Walshe and Luker, 2010). Several international reviews have explored aspects of community health nursing and palliative and end-of-life care, such as: where home was the preferred place of death (Sørstrøm et al., 2024); the experiences/needs of nurses providing home-based palliative care (Wu et al., 2023); focusing on role and contributions (Walshe and Luker, 2010, Moran et al., 2024); specific aspects of care delivery (e.g. communication) (Wittenberg et al., 2024); and identifying challenges (but not solutions) in palliative care delivery (Weston et al., 2023). These reviews also highlight challenges with multiple health systems. Therefore, this scoping review aims to build on previous reviews by capturing evidence on the challenges and solutions within one national health system in relation to several aspects of community health nursing in palliative care.

Aim

This scoping review aimed to identify, map and synthesize evidence within a single national health system on the community health nursing workforce in palliative and end-of-life care provision for adults living at home.

Objectives

1.To understand and describe the role and responsibilities of community health nurses in delivering palliative and end-of-life care.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology for scoping reviews, and reported in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and meta-analysis: extension for Scoping Reviews (Tricco et al., 2018, Peters et al., 2020). The protocol was registered in advance at the Open Science Framework website (Pask et al., 2024).

Eligibility criteria

Included articles met the pre-specified criteria described below and were constructed using the Joanna Briggs Institute population, concept, and context (see Supplementary file 1 for detailed description of inclusion and exclusion criteria).

Population

The target population comprised community health nurses and their immediate team (e.g. nursing assistants) who care for an adult caseload (aged ≥18) with palliative and end-of-life care needs. Palliative and end-of-life care provision for adults living and dying at home with advanced illness is one part of the general nursing care they provide across the lifespan (see Supplementary file 2 for detailed definitions of palliative and end-of-life care, and nursing role titles and their respective definitions).

Concepts

Concepts included the roles and responsibilities of community health nurses in palliative and end-of-life care (role is defined as the overarching nature of the nurses’ function; responsibilities are the tasks they are required to do) (Hasson et al., 2022), challenges in delivering this care, and related skills and expertise (including palliative and end-of-life care training needs). We also considered concepts which extended beyond palliative and end-of-life care, but which can impact heavily, including workforce (numbers, recruitment and retention), caseload/workload (including palliative and end-of-life care), and integration with other services in the delivery of palliative care.

Context

Context was considered in terms of settings of care and the broader context of country. Community health nursing is principally positioned in the home and more broadly in the community, such as community clinics, communal living facilities (e.g. care home with no on-site nursing), and nursing care for those with no fixed abode. International transferability of findings can be challenging due to country variation of community healthcare provision and legislation, and differences in workforce roles and responsibilities (Walshe and Luker, 2010). However, our focus on a single defined national health system (community health nursing in a UK-context) offers an in-depth analysis of evidence whilst providing applicable insights for other countries with similar community nursing models. The UK also has a clear policy direction with shifting care to the community (UK Government 2025) and a breadth of evidence available (Walshe and Luker, 2010, Moran et al., 2024).

Types of evidence

A range of research designs were considered for inclusion, including experimental, quasi-experimental, observational, cross-sectional, qualitative and case studies (with a clear methodology of development), review-level evidence, and conference abstracts published within the last 4-years (to identify full-text papers). Review-level evidence was considered to understand the types of evidence available (i.e. the number of relevant reviews). Other than mapping to the themes we identified, data extraction was not completed for this type of evidence to avoid duplication. Grey literature was also considered (e.g. reports and dissertations). Sources of evidence were limited to evidence written in English.

Search strategy

The search strategy aimed to locate a variety of potential sources of evidence (both published and unpublished) using a comprehensive process. First, an initial focused search of MEDLINE (All via OVID) was conducted to identify articles on the topic, guided by a realist review by Walshe and Luker (2010). We reviewed the indexing of any studies that cited this paper, recommended papers from the research team and used existing filters for UK studies and Palliative Care to inform the search strategy. Nine studies were used to test the draft MEDLINE search and highlighted the need to supplement the main search with a broader search for review articles to determine evidence on nursing roles in palliative care (where community nursing or the UK setting may not be specified in the title/abstract). Therefore, search terms focused on concepts of ‘Palliative Care’ AND ‘Community or district nursing’ AND ‘United Kingdom’, with a broader supplementary search for review articles of ‘Palliative Care’ AND ‘Nursing’ or ‘Community or district nursing’ AND ‘Workforce/roles’. Once agreed, the initial Medline search strategy was translated into other databases (such as changing the Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) into other controlled vocabulary and adjusting the search syntax for different platforms. Please see Supplementary file 1 for more information and full search strategies.

Systematic searches were conducted on 13 electronic databases: MEDLINE, EMBASE, Emcare, PsycINFO, KSR Evidence, Health Management Information Consortium and Allied and Complementary Medicine Database (all via OVID), CINAHL (via EBSCO), British Nursing Database and Applied Social Sciences Index and Abstracts (via ProQuest), Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews and Cochrane Central Register of Trials (via The Cochrane Library), and Epistemonikos by an information specialist. These were cross-checked against Google Scholar for additional relevant unique results.

Unpublished and grey literature were identified through targeted searches of UK-based organisations and policy bodies by the same information specialist. A Google Advanced search for reports from other organisations (e.g. focused search for PDF documents on websites ending in ‘.org.uk/.nhs.uk’) was conducted.

Further sources were identified by asking experts in the field and by performing forward and backward citation searching based on five relevant review articles identified during scoping searches (using Citation Chaser).

No language limitations were applied at the search stage but database searches were limited to citations published after 2009 to build on the realist review by Walshe and Luker (2010) (which accounted for legislative changes to district nursing in 1990 and an incorporated review that included literature up to 2009). The main database search was performed on 22nd April 2024 and re-run on 10th July 2025.

Selection of sources of evidence

All identified citations and grey literature were uploaded into EndNote 21, 2025 (Clarivate Analytics, Pennsylvania, USA) and duplicates removed. Remaining references were managed in Covidence systematic review software, 2024 (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) for screening and data extraction. On screening title and abstracts, 15% of evidence retrieved from the original search was dual screened to ensure agreement and minimise the risk of discrepancies between two reviewers (SG and SP), with a proportionate agreement of 85% (greater than the ≥75% recommended in Joanna Briggs Institute). The remaining titles and abstracts were single screened independently by the two reviewers against the eligibility criteria. Each full text was reviewed by a researcher, and areas of uncertainty were discussed as a team. Reasons for exclusion were recorded at full-text review. Disagreements at each stage were resolved with another research team member (FEMM).

Data charting process and synthesis

Six authors (SP, SG, JI, EY, GM and AW) used a data extraction form in Covidence (piloted on a subset of evidence and modified as necessary) to extract data from included items/reports (see Supplementary file 1). Extracted data included details about the source of evidence (e.g. author, year), population, context, methods, and critical findings. Data were grouped using the topics outlined in the ‘Concept’ section. This enabled a logical/descriptive summary of results, aligned with the review objective, and facilitated the identification of gaps in the literature.

A researcher (SP) compared results of each source of evidence until preliminary themes were identified. These were discussed with the wider research team, a Community Nursing Panel and a Patient and Public Involvement Group as data extraction and synthesis progressed to strengthen the interpretation of extracted data.

Involving patients, families and professionals

A Community Nursing Expert Panel and Patient and Public Involvement Group were configured to support a wider programme of work on community nursing in palliative and end-of-life care. The respective groups comprised community health nurses (senior nurses, nurse managers, or nurses in advanced roles), and patients and family carers. Regular meetings were held online focusing on different aspects of the work, including protocol development (e.g. developing the search strategy and identifying relevant search terms reflecting community health nursing roles), identifying potential sources of evidence, sense-checking the data and interpretation of the findings (see Supplementary file 3), and dissemination of the research.

The included sources of evidence took a broad range of approaches, with the majority having a qualitative approach (n = 84), mixed methods (n = 35), cross-sectional (n = 32), reviews with a systematic approach (n = 28), reports (n = 15), standards/frameworks (n = 11), service evaluation (n = 6), case study (with clear methodology) (n = 5), service development (n = 5), audit (n = 4), longitudinal (n = 3), non-systematic review (n = 3), pilot study (n = 2), case-control study (n = 1), Delphi study (n = 1), modelling study (n = 1), non-randomised experimental (n = 1), randomised controlled trial (n = 1), and toolkit (n = 1).

Synthesis of results

Notions reflecting community health nursing and palliative and end-of-life care provision were clustered into four themes: (1) roles and responsibilities: the nature of community health nursing in palliative and end-of-life care; (2) challenges and potential solutions: the expertise needed to respond to the increasing complexity of care; (3) challenges and potential solutions: the rising demand and caring under pressure; and (4) challenges and potential solutions: implications for effective integration with other services. Challenges and potential solutions are discussed within each respective theme.

(1) Roles and responsibilities: the nature of community health nursing in palliative and end-of-life care

First, we consider the overarching role of community health nurses in palliative and end-of-life care. The overarching role of community health nurses in palliative and end-of-life care

The overarching role of community health nurses in palliative and end-of-life care

Palliative and end-of-life care is delivered as part of the comprehensive holistic care that community health nurses provide to people with advanced illness and families in their own homes (Bowers et al., 2025, Maybin et al., 2016, NHS Benchmarking Network 2024, McDonald and Hasson, 2024). They are one of the main providers of hands-on palliative and end-of-life care (distinct from that provided by palliative care specialists) and are well-placed to do so because of their closeness and regular contact with patients and their families (Arrey et al., 2019, Glogowska et al., 2016, Hoe et al., 2023, Walshe, 2020). This is a highly valued aspect of their role that provides a sense of fulfilment, (Smith and Porock, 2009, Ganga, 2022, Pottle et al., 2020) as they recognise the lasting impact of their care at the end of life (enabling patients and families to have choice and dignity) (Ferguson et al., 2023, Harrison Dening et al., 2020). Ensuring a “good” death is seen as part of their responsibility and goals of care (Smith and Porock, 2009, Ganga, 2022).

Community health nurses are highly skilled in relational care and using empathy to support patients and families towards the end of life (e.g. providing reassurance and knowledge about what to expect) (Arrey et al., 2019, Hoe et al., 2023, Walshe, 2020, Ferguson et al., 2023). Characteristics of ‘good care’ included caring for the person as a whole, continuity of care, and the personable manner of staff (Bowers et al., 2025, Maybin et al., 2016), as well as valuing and involving carers, acting as coordinators/advocates, and providing clinical expertise (Maybin et al., 2016, Hoe et al., 2023, Tomison and McDowell, 2011). Predictable visiting times, reliability, and being able to contact services between appointment times were considered important characteristics to patients and their families (Maybin et al., 2016).

Community health nurses’ responsibilities in palliative and end-of-life care

Community health nurses’ responsibilities in palliative and end-of-life care

There was a strong notion of professional duty in the evidence – what nurses should deliver and what a nurse must be (both in relation to general nursing and palliative and end-of-life care, see Supplementary file 5). This was evident in qualitative research and the standards and competency frameworks that guide their practice and education (Bowers et al., 2025, Nursing and Midwifery Council 2018, Nursing and Midwifery Council 2022).

Standards and competency frameworks help to define levels of practice and support the nursing care provision in the community where uncertainty can be high, but with limited mention of palliative and end-of-life care (Nursing and Midwifery Council 2018, Nursing and Midwifery Council 2022, Nursing and Midwifery Council 2018, Chief Nursing Officer Directorate 2017). Although they outline responsibilities and are designed to support practice (including palliative and end-of-life care), standards and competency frameworks are often inconsistently understood, applied, and valued (The Queen's Nursing Institute 2015, Carter et al., 2018, Simpson et al., 2022). Their adoption is influenced by local adaptation, clarity, and perceived relevance to daily work (The Queen's Nursing Institute 2015, Carter et al., 2018, Simpson et al., 2022). If they are to effectively guide practice, they need to resonate with patients and families’ needs, be comprehensive, practical, relevant, well-explained, streamlined and easy to follow, and regularly reviewed (Carter et al., 2018).

Nursing activities and responsibilities are contingent on banding (i.e. a pay system that categorises based on skills, experience and responsibilities), with more expected from more senior, advanced or specialist nursing roles (e.g. symptom management in palliative care patients and enhanced communication skills to manage uncertainty) compared with the wider community nursing team (e.g. healthcare assistants and nursing associates who are under direction from registered professionals) (Nursing and Midwifery Council 2018, Nursing and Midwifery Council 2018, Chief Nursing Officer Directorate 2017, Dalgarno et al., 2025). Although, some community health nurses felt that their pay grade was not commensurate with their responsibilities (Bowers et al., 2025, Marangozov et al., 2017).

Community health nurses’ role and responsibilities are dynamic and demand-led; shaped by changes in patient need (e.g. rise in multiple long-term conditions and increasing complexity) and clinical context (e.g. the COVID pandemic) (Bowers et al., 2025, Dalgarno et al., 2025, Polak et al., 2025). This means they can change rapidly and often expand in scope. Evidence highlights the variety of new responsibilities or extensions to existing responsibilities, in particular following the COVID pandemic, with inferred expectation that these would be embedded in their clinical practice going forwards (Bowers et al., 2025, McDonald and Hasson, 2024, Polak et al., 2025). Advance care planning conversations, querying patient and family preferences and completion of cardiopulmonary resuscitation/advance care planning documentation, clinical decision-making around end-of-life care management, and verification of death, have all increased and/or been added to nursing responsibilites (Bowers et al., 2025) – in addition to the complexities of their role as palliative care key workers (McDonald and Hasson, 2024). There is complex picture of how new and established roles and responsibilities have extended and deepened, balancing the positives of demonstrating palliative and end-of-life care expertise with frustration of the demands of additional tasks/responsibilites (Bowers et al., 2025, McDonald and Hasson, 2024, Polak et al., 2025).

Despite this central role in palliative and end-of-life care, there remains limited understanding of the complexity of community health nurses’ role and their contributions by both the public and other professionals (Bowers et al., 2025, McDonald and Hasson, 2024). They may perceive themselves as forgotten, undervalued and under-supported, as much of the work they do is invisible, behind closed doors (Bowers et al., 2025, Royal College of Nursing 2024, Walshe et al., 2011, Wilson and Seymour, 2017). The rapid evolution may contribute to lack of understanding or clarity around their roles and responsibilities (Bowers et al., 2025, McDonald and Hasson, 2024).

(2) Challenges and potential solutions: the expertise needed to respond to the increasing complexity of care

The expertise needed by community health nurses to respond to the increasing complexity of palliative and end-of-life care needs were described in 179 included items/reports.

Complex care needs within the home environment

Complex care needs within the home environment

More people are living with multiple long-term conditions, non-malignant disease, uncertain trajectories and complex care needs (Hoe et al., 2023, Dalgarno et al., 2025, Turner et al., 2023, Mitchell et al., 2021). Care for some conditions is considered more complex at the end-of-life compared to others, and less easy to predict (e.g. dementia compared to cancer) (Hoe et al., 2023, Tomison and McDowell, 2011, Andrews and Seymour, 2011). Community health nurses may encounter challenging situations that further complicate palliative and end-of-life care delivery (such as complex or dysfunctional family relationships or alcohol/drug abuse) (Hoe et al., 2023, Manyimo and de Vries, 2023, Wilson et al., 2014). For these reasons, as more care is provided closer to home, community health nurses are increasingly required to work at a ‘specialist’ level across diverse clinical areas, relying on their training as generalist adult nurses (Maybin et al., 2016, Hoe et al., 2023, Tomison and McDowell, 2011, The Queen's Nursing Institute 2009). It is often assumed that a qualified nurse has all the relevant clinical expertise needed (Maybin et al., 2016, Andrews and Seymour, 2011, Gott et al., 2012, Carter et al., 2019).

Palliative and end-of-life care education and training needs

Palliative and end-of-life care education and training needs

The delivery of effective palliative and end-of-life care by community health nurses requires the necessary skills and expertise through education, training, and learning by experience, and these must be maintained through continuous professional development (Bowers et al., 2025, Ganga, 2022, Tomison and McDowell, 2011, Bailey et al., 2016). A benefit to their evolving roles (as outlined above), as prompted by the pandemic, was sudden task-shifting – demonstrating their skills and building confidence (Bowers et al., 2025). Although they recognise the importance of knowing the limit of their skills and expertise (Johnson, 2015). There is some evidence that introducing or creating roles that reflect the advanced level of care (such as advanced nurse practitioners) may enhance role development, develop career pathways and provide the support to deliver increasing complex care needs (Dalgarno et al., 2025). Adopting essential criteria for advanced nurse practitioners can provide a clinical career pathway that helps retain expert knowledge and experience, foster professional respect and deliver effective partnership working (Dalgarno et al., 2025).

Upskilling and investing in the current health and care workforce (including community nurses) is important to meet the growing need for community-based care (Ganga, 2022, Tomison and McDowell, 2011, Arnold et al., 2025, Mitchell et al., 2022), mirroring changes in the population (Ganga, 2022, Arnold et al., 2025). Training must be provided, reviewed and updated to ensure that staff feel appropriately qualified to deliver holistic community-based palliative care effectively (Maybin et al., 2016, McDonald and Hasson, 2024, Smith and Porock, 2009, Tomison and McDowell, 2011). Staff must not be deskilled or devalued, but provided with the necessary opportunities to enhance confidence, knowledge and skills (McDonald and Hasson, 2024, Ferguson et al., 2023, Byron and Hoskins, 2013, Harrison Dening et al., 2019). Training can support the emotional aspects of their work, how they appraise situations, and how they respond (Byron and Hoskins, 2013, Morton-Nance and Schafer, 2012, Kingsley et al., 2023, Brighton et al., 2018).

Community health nurses draw on a broad range of expertise in the provision of palliative and end-of-life care to persons dying at home and their families but can lack confidence in some aspects of care and mixed levels of experience are noted (McDonald and Hasson, 2024, Arrey et al., 2019, Hoe et al., 2023, Arnold et al., 2025). They demonstrate strengths in the provision of physical and practical aspects of care but less so with psychological symptoms (Glogowska et al., 2016, Hoe et al., 2023, Griffiths et al., 2010, Nagington et al., 2016). Despite UK national guidance recommendations (Wilson and Seymour, 2017, The National Council for Palliative Care 2011, Lawler et al., 2020), pre-registration training, with its focus on the acute setting, has not always adequately prepared nurses in community-based palliative and end-of-life care (Ali et al., 2011, Reed, 2011). In addition, palliative and end-of-life care is not standardised in pre- and post- registration training programmes (Reed, 2011). Palliative and end-of-life care training is often cursory or limited to mandatory task-based training, such as syringe driver management (McDonald and Hasson, 2024, Cross Party Group on Hospices Palliative Care 2018). Training, where present, is often brief and infrequent (Griggs, 2010, Dean, 2017), and additional opportunities would be welcomed by community nurses (McDonald and Hasson, 2024, Bailey et al., 2016, Dean, 2018). Access to additional palliative and end-of-life care training is a longstanding issue, despite increasing demand for community-based care (Maybin et al., 2016, McDonald and Hasson, 2024, Dean, 2017, Dean, 2019). This leaves teams without enough skilled and experienced team members (Maybin et al., 2016, Smith and Porock, 2009).

These barriers and confidence issues can hinder the integration of evidence-based practices. For training to be effective, it should be locally driven (reflecting training needs/reflect the area’s needs), embedded in existing programmes, and informed by examples of good practice and local audits (Schichtel et al., 2021, King et al., 2010, Baylis et al., 2023, Cassam et al., 2014).

Community health nurses develop competencies and capabilities through formal education, experience, and reflective practice, which must be regularly assessed and maintained (Chief Nursing Officer Directorate 2017, Health Education England 2020, Davies et al., 2010, Bowler et al., 2009). Confidence in delivering palliative and end-of-life care is supported by varied learning approaches, such as: peer support, mentorship, supervision/joint home visits (i.e. apprenticeship style learning), multidisciplinary collaboration, reflection, decision-making tools, and evidence-based and experiential learning (Bowers et al., 2025, Harrison Dening et al., 2019, Ramplin, 2019, Steven et al., 2014). Community health nurses recognised they could be more evidence-based in their approach when caring for someone at the end of life (Johnston et al., 2012, Wheeler et al., 2014). Community health nurses, and their wider team, find it useful to access disease-specific specialist nurses or those with specialist palliative care training when additional expertise is needed (Hoe et al., 2023, Wye et al., 2014, Johnson et al., 2012, Boland et al., 2014). Effective training combines evidence-based content with practical tools, diverse formats, and locally relevant resources, though no single method has been found more effective (Smith and Porock, 2009, Dowding et al., 2009, Steven et al., 2014, Wheeler et al., 2014).

146 items/reports reflect the increasing need for care, workforce shortages, equipment delays, and funding issues that community nursing faces. Although palliative and end-of-life care specific data were limited, it has implications for care delivery.

Caseload and workload

Caseload and workload

There is a longstanding emphasis on improving the availability of community-based palliative and end-of-life care to improve access to care within limited resources (Maybin et al., 2016, Cross Party Group on Hospices Palliative Care 2018, Burt et al., 2010). However, the increasing number of people being cared for and dying in the community has implications for community health nurses’ caseload and daily workload (Maybin et al., 2016, McDonald and Hasson, 2024, Arnold et al., 2025, Royal College of Nursing 2023), as does their contributions to the wider multidisciplinary team (Polak et al., 2025, Bowers et al., 2025). They often work in their own time to feel satisfied with the quality of their work (Bowers et al., 2025). The absence of patient number controls, large caseloads and limited resources means that community health nurses must prioritise aspects of care delivery (Polak et al., 2020). This involves balancing more critical cases, and patient and family expectations of care provision (Polak et al., 2020). Although they strive to prioritise those needing palliative and end-of-life care both in and outside of normal working hours, variations in access to or timely response are noted (Polak et al., 2020, Burt et al., 2010, Pask et al., 2022, Bowers et al., 2024). One contributing aspect is the lasting impact of the COVID pandemic, where there was a steep rise in demand and understaffing was at its worst for many services (including community-based staff) (Mitchell et al., 2021, All-Party Parliamentary Group for Hospice End of Life Care 2023, Chalk et al., 2021, Antunes et al., 2020).

Caseloads fluctuate over time (Harrison Dening et al., 2020, Polak et al., 2020) and vary by team size and composition (Griffiths et al., 2010, Griffiths et al., 2015), locality, and profession (e.g. community matrons) (Cole et al., 2019). Benchmarking data demonstrated a steady rise in referrals and caseloads, with minimal flexibility to manage this rising demand (Maybin et al., 2016, NHS Benchmarking Network 2024, NHS Benchmarking Network 2023). Most services cannot close their caseloads and contact time with patients is often below recommended levels (minimum 30 min to assess, plan, implement and evaluate care) (NHS Benchmarking Network 2024, NHS Benchmarking Network 2023). Care can be left undone or deferred due to limited capacity (NHS Benchmarking Network 2024, Lawler et al., 2020, Royal College of Nursing 2023, Senek et al., 2022), and may be handed over to already stretched out-of-hours services (Royal College of Nursing 2023, Hospice, 2025). It is unclear how much palliative and end-of-life care is left undone or deferred, as existing data does not explore this (Senek et al., 2022).

Palliative and end-of-life care has been an area of increased demand, especially since the COVID pandemic (Maybin et al., 2016, Glogowska et al., 2016, Mitchell et al., 2022, Dean, 2019). Data relating to palliative and end-of-life care mostly came from primary studies and focused on how many of their patients are palliative or at the end of life, their typical stage of involvement (e.g. last days or weeks), or number of contacts (NHS Benchmarking Network 2024, Northway et al., 2019, Ore and Carver, 2020, Filipe et al., 2024). Opportunities to deliver palliative and end-of-life care are being missed and care is more reactive (Maybin et al., 2016, Johnson, 2015, Lawler et al., 2020). This can negatively impact on staff morale (Maybin et al., 2016, The Queen's Nursing Institute 2024, The Queen's Nursing Institute 2016) and the patient and family experience (The National Council for Palliative Care 2011, Burton et al., 2024, Marie Curie, 2023). Frustrations occur from busy caseloads, witnessing poor end of life experiences and limited time to develop a therapeutic connection crucial for quality care provision (Ganga, 2022, Arnold et al., 2025, Morton-Nance and Schafer, 2012, Goodrich et al., 2025).

Most community health nurses expect caring for the dying to be a main part of their work; reflected in how they often manage palliative and end-of-life care needs (Glogowska et al., 2016, Smith and Porock, 2009, Gott et al., 2012, Healthcare Financial Management Association 2022). Palliative care is time-consuming, often at odds with limited working hours, limited resources and staffing levels, and substantially increased workloads (Hoe et al., 2023, Johnson, 2015, Dean, 2019, Willis and Sutton, 2013). The high demand challenges implementation of the holistic approach required in palliative care (Disler and Jones, 2010). Nurses (including those based in the community) feel they have insufficient time to provide high-quality end-of-life care and build therapeutic relationships with persons dying at home (Bowers et al., 2025, Arnold et al., 2025, Dean, 2019), and to support family carers (Johnson, 2015, Wye et al., 2014).

Despite growing caseloads, there is no clear definition of a safe or optimal workload (NHS Improvement on behalf of the National Quality Board 2017, Leary and Bushe, 2022). Identifying methods for calculating safe caseloads of community health nurses is challenging as much of their work involves in-the-moment autonomous decision-making. Additional time requirements for palliative and end-of-life care is difficult to quantify, measure or predict (NHS Improvement on behalf of the National Quality Board 2017). Inability to close caseloads, daily deferrals of care (including end-of-life care), high staff turnover, and sickness absence signal risks to patient safety and system resilience (Leary and Bushe, 2022, The Queen's Nursing Institute 2019). End-of-life care is often obscured within contracting arrangements, making it difficult to assess how many patients can be supported at home and the timing of care delivery (Baylis et al., 2023). Improved data collection, outcome-based accountability, and tools like care profiles are needed to enhance transparency, support sustainable service planning, and ensure equitable access to both generalist and specialist palliative care (Cross Party Group on Hospices Palliative Care 2018, Baylis et al., 2023, Gandy et al., 2012).

The demand-capacity gap

The demand-capacity gap

Evidence highlighted the persistent gap between the demand for care (i.e. the volume of need/people referred) and the capacity of services to meet this demand (Bowers et al., 2025, Maybin et al., 2016, NHS Benchmarking Network 2023, Senek et al., 2022). This demand-capacity gap is not a new problem for community nursing services. However, it is an increasingly acute issue, worsening rather than improving over time (Maybin et al., 2016, Lawler et al., 2020, Royal College of Nursing 2023, The Queen's Nursing Institute 2016). The workforce currently available is insufficient for the number of patients in the ‘clinical queue’ (Lawler et al., 2020, Cross Party Group on Hospices Palliative Care 2018, Steven et al., 2014, Royal College of Nursing 2023). It reflects a dissonance between the policy drive to move care out of the hospital setting and capacity issues with nursing services in the community and makes providing timely, good quality palliative and end-of-life care difficult (Maybin et al., 2016, NHS Benchmarking Network 2024, Royal College of Nursing 2024, All-Party Parliamentary Group for Hospice End of Life Care 2023). A recent survey highlighted decreases in nursing budgets despite an increase in demand (NHS Benchmarking Network 2024). Equitable, safe and effective delivery of palliative and end-of-life care in the community depends on the availability of an appropriately qualified workforce locally (Cross Party Group on Hospices Palliative Care 2018). Currently, some areas are unable to provide sufficient community nursing services (Royal College of Nursing 2023, Pask et al., 2022, Department of Health Northern Ireland 2018). There was limited evidence modelling the daily community nursing time needed to manage end-of-life care, and only in the context of the pandemic (Chalk et al., 2021).

The significant shortfall in workforce has impacted the demand-capacity gap and increased workloads (Maybin et al., 2016, Royal College of Nursing 2023). Many services do not operate with the full number of staff (Maybin et al., 2016, NHS Benchmarking Network 2024, NHS Benchmarking Network 2023, Senek et al., 2022). Fewer nurses are covering large geographical areas on nightshifts, with consequent increased wait times (Royal College of Nursing 2023, Pask et al., 2022, Hospice, 2025). Workforce shortages have led to a more task-based approach, and at times, the provision of basic care when short on time (Maybin et al., 2016, Royal College of Nursing 2023, Dyer et al., 2021, House of Commons Health and Social Care Committee 2022). Shortages result in unequal access to palliative and end-of-life care and in achieving care preferences (Cross Party Group on Hospices Palliative Care 2018). Evidence suggests that contact with community health nurses is positively associated with achieving the patient’s preference to die at home (Gott et al., 2012, Bannon et al., 2018). Greater availability of community nurses per capita increased the probability of having at least one contact (Filipe et al., 2024). Addressing the gaps within the community nursing workforce should be treated as a priority, including the distribution of staff with palliative and end-of-life care skills, unfilled vacancies, and succession planning (Firth et al., 2025, Cross Party Group on Hospices Palliative Care 2018, Hospice, 2025, Green, 2021). Workforce planning and a range of skill mix are needed to ensure that responsibilities can be delivered, and palliative and end-of-life care is safe and effective (The Queen's Nursing Institute 2016, Leary and Bushe, 2022, Department of Health Northern Ireland 2018).

The gap between demand for care and capacity to deliver care adversely affects nurses’ wellbeing and moral distress, which is further impacted by a lack of formal support (Bowers et al., 2025, Ganga, 2022, Kingsley et al., 2023, Goodrich et al., 2025). Issues with staff wellbeing and being mentally prepared for daily challenges when delivering palliative and end-of-life care were evidenced, as was the need for more mental health support (Smith and Porock, 2009, Ganga, 2022, Pottle et al., 2020, Mitchell et al., 2021). Time to reflect, clinical supervision, informal support from colleagues, and cathartic activities are self-care strategies adopted to manage their emotions (Ganga, 2022, Brighton et al., 2018, Griffiths et al., 2010, Laker, 2022). It is important that these emotional challenges are recognised and addressed at an organisational level (Ganga, 2022, Pottle et al., 2020, Mitchell et al., 2021, Brighton et al., 2018).

Workforce

Workforce

The decline in nursing staff is linked to an ageing workforce nearing retirement, evolving roles, and changing work environments, leading to persistent challenges in replacing those who have left or reduced their hours (Maybin et al., 2016). In 2023, a staff turnover rate of 15% was noted in community health nursing services (NHS Benchmarking Network 2023). As experienced staff exit the profession, newly qualified nurses face heavier workloads with limited support (Maybin et al., 2016, The Queen's Nursing Institute 2009, Dean, 2018). Variations in team composition, skill mix, and regional staffing levels highlight the need for service establishments to reflect actual care demand and account for staff absences (Carter et al., 2018, NHS Benchmarking Network 2023, The Queen's Nursing Institute 2016, House of Commons Health and Social Care Committee 2022). Other contributing factors to the shortfall in staff numbers and recruitment could relate to the inadequate number of training places, a decline in those seeking the District Nursing Specialist Practice Qualification and dissatisfaction with pay not reflecting the level of responsibility and intensity of the job role (Polak et al., 2025, Maybin et al., 2016, Marangozov et al., 2017).

Staff vacancies were highlighted as a longstanding issue across evidence (Maybin et al., 2016, NHS Benchmarking Network 2024, Royal College of Nursing 2023, Senek et al., 2022). Increasing national difficulty in recruiting staff into community health nursing is a longstanding issue, particularly with more senior band roles (Royal College of Nursing 2023). There is a reliance on bank and agency staff, rather than permanent recruitment (Maybin et al., 2016, NHS Benchmarking Network 2024, Royal College of Nursing 2023, Leary and Bushe, 2022). However, gaps are not always filled even by bank and agency staff (Royal College of Nursing 2023). A significantly higher proportion of community-based staff (22%) report staff sickness (i.e. unexpected absences) compared to those working in other care settings (Royal College of Nursing 2023). Data specifically related to community health nursing services are limited; median staff sickness was 6.7% in 2023 and 6.9% in 2024 (NHS Benchmarking Network 2024, NHS Benchmarking Network 2023).

Pressures on community nursing services risk moving away from a high quality model of care and can have a profound impact on the wellbeing of staff (Maybin et al., 2016). Low staffing levels often leave nurses without time to seek support or debrief, contributing to feelings of isolation and low morale (Maybin et al., 2016, Royal College of Nursing 2024, Dean, 2019, All-Party Parliamentary Group for Hospice End of Life Care 2023). Prolonged recruitment periods and rising stress levels are prompting some to leave the profession or shift to roles with more manageable workloads (Maybin et al., 2016, House of Commons Health and Social Care Committee 2022). This creates a cycle of workforce shortages, making community health nursing less attractive and highlighting the urgent need for improved workforce planning, recruitment, and resource allocation (Maybin et al., 2016, Royal College of Nursing 2023).

Workforce planning tools needed to guide how services are commissioned, and careful consideration is needed in their development (Maybin et al., 2016, Baylis et al., 2023, Leary and Bushe, 2022, The Queen's Nursing Institute 2013). Commissioning arrangements for palliative and end-of-life care have evolved organically, and there can be multiple approaches within a single area (Baylis et al., 2023). There are limited data and analytic tools available to demonstrate increased patient contacts and case complexity to commissioners, making consistent collection data essential (Maybin et al., 2016). Service establishments should reflect actual care demand, accounting for planned and unplanned staff absences (Leary and Bushe, 2022).

Palliative and end-of-life care as a multi-disciplinary effort

Palliative and end-of-life care as a multi-disciplinary effort

Palliative and end-of-life care is a multidisciplinary effort, requiring collaboration and good communication between different health and care providers to avoid disjointed care (Turner et al., 2023, Gott et al., 2012, Firth et al., 2025, Johnson et al., 2012). It is assumed that health and care providers share a common understanding of what good care requires and possess the necessary capabilities to achieve this, but this is not always the case (Seymour et al., 2010, Griggs, 2010, Baylis et al., 2023). Commissioners need to continue to review and develop approaches to palliative and end-of-life care to ensure the fundamentals are addressed and that there is a shared understanding of what this should look like (Baylis et al., 2023).

Evidence reflected complex interactions and working relationships, not only between the wide array of community-based nursing roles, but with other professionals (e.g. general practitioners, acute care staff, care home staff, and specialist palliative care). Community health nurses are a vital part of the multidisciplinary team and provide support to other professionals when caring for palliative and end-of-life care patients (McDonald and Hasson, 2024, Cruickshank et al., 2010). Although independent in their role, they appreciate advice and support from the wider team (Bowers et al., 2025, Dean, 2019), particularly with managing complex needs (see Table 1). However, a perceived lack of support from other professionals was sometimes noted (Bowers et al., 2025, Wilson et al., 2014, Baylis et al., 2023), such as issues with accessing specialist palliative care (Disler and Jones, 2010, Dean, 2019, Pask et al., 2022, Campling et al., 2022) or less/limited support from general practitioners (Polak et al., 2025, Bowers et al., 2025).

Close working relationships of community health nurses with general practitioners in the delivery of palliative and end-of-life care were often described (Polak et al., 2025, Bowers et al., 2025, Bowers and Redsell, 2017). Changes to the ways general practitioners work impacted community nurses (e.g. increases in remote working, reduced proximity/co-location of services) (Polak et al., 2025, Bowers et al., 2025, Wilson and Seymour, 2017, Arnold et al., 2025). In terms of acute care, there can be a disconnect between referral information provided in hospital settings or overpromising the care community nurses can provide, as well as involving them too late in the discharge process (Wilson et al., 2015, Jack and O'Brien, 2010, O'Brien and Jack, 2010). Community nurses value support from specialist palliative care (Baylis et al., 2023, Wye et al., 2014, Campling et al., 2022, Dobie, 2010), but noted that they also have limited capacity and are not always easily accessed (Arnold et al., 2025, Wilson et al., 2015, Baylis et al., 2023, Campling et al., 2022).

Community health nurses play a central role in coordinating, managing, and reviewing care alongside and between other professionals (McDonald and Hasson, 2024, Firth et al., 2025, Baylis et al., 2023, Goodrich et al., 2022)—often acting as a link between different providers and settings of care (McDonald and Hasson, 2024, Department of Health Northern Ireland 2018, Law, 2009). Yet, they are not always involved as early as they would like (Tomison and McDowell, 2011, King et al., 2010). Liaising with and signposting to other members of the multidisciplinary team can be time consuming and requires knowledge and availability of local services (Bowers et al., 2025, Ferguson et al., 2023, Lawler et al., 2020, Dyer et al., 2021). Poor coordination and communication between community health nurses and other teams can lead to issues with the delivery of palliative and end-of-life care (e.g. fragmented care) and worse care outcomes (Ferguson et al., 2023, Firth et al., 2025, Burton et al., 2024, Brogan et al., 2018). Good communication enables timely access, smoother transitions and better coordination across services (Glogowska et al., 2016, Bailey et al., 2016, Wilson et al., 2015, Baylis et al., 2023).

Expectations within the multi-disciplinary team

Expectations within the multi-disciplinary team

Differences in professional opinion and role conflict can lead to care-related decisions being challenged (Hoe et al., 2023). Trust and mutual respect of skills and expertise are essential to effective collaboration (Arrey et al., 2019, Bowers and Redsell, 2017, Bowers et al., 2024, Campling et al., 2022). Being treated as a valued member of the multidisciplinary team provides a sense of professional dignity for community health nurses (Bowers et al., 2025, Maybin et al., 2016, Amador et al., 2016), but their concerns about patients’ palliative care needs could sometimes be dismissed by other professionals (Wilson and Seymour, 2017) or were not accorded enough acknowledgement/respect by colleagues (Bowers et al., 2025). Trust between professionals may be challenged by poor understanding of each other’s roles and responsibilities (Wilson and Seymour, 2017). Hierarchical attitudes may be present and can lead to community nurses feeling undervalued (Wilson and Seymour, 2017).

Professionals hold perceptions of the roles and responsibilities of different providers in palliative and end-of-life care (McDonald and Hasson, 2024, Arrey et al., 2019, Andrews and Seymour, 2011, Morton-Nance and Schafer, 2012), and may be uncertain of their own role in the provision of such care (Morton-Nance and Schafer, 2012, Schichtel et al., 2021, King et al., 2010, Handley et al., 2014). Limited understanding of, shifts in, confidence with or overlapping roles can lead to further confusion about who is responsible and when or concerns about encroaching on another professionals remit (Schichtel et al., 2021, King et al., 2010, Brogan et al., 2018, Handley et al., 2014). Some aspects of care are considered a shared responsibility between community nurses and other professionals (e.g. advance care planning) (McDonald and Hasson, 2024, Thorpe et al., 2021, Minto and Strickland, 2011). Though, there may be a global reticence amongst providers when discussing some aspects palliative and end-of-life care (e.g. cardiopulmonary resuscitation) (Minto and Strickland, 2011, Connolly et al., 2015). A clear understanding of roles in palliative and end-of-life care is crucial to collaborative working, good inter-disciplinary communication, streamlining care and avoiding duplication of work (Wilson and Seymour, 2017, Thorpe et al., 2021, Agnew et al., 2011, Hackett et al., 2018).

Timely involvement and information sharing

Timely involvement and information sharing

Community nurses often receive limited patient information, leaving them unprepared for complex situations (Morton-Nance and Schafer, 2012, Campling et al., 2022, O'Brien and Jack, 2010). Poor information sharing—especially during handovers and out-of-hours—can hinder access to care and disrupt collaboration (McDonald and Hasson, 2024, Pask et al., 2022, Campling et al., 2022, Asprey et al., 2013). Sharing advance care plans, patient referral and discharge processes, and patient records between services is considered crucial to the delivery of care and an enhanced sense of partnership working (McDonald and Hasson, 2024, Turner et al., 2023, Goodrich et al., 2022).

While shared electronic records support the sharing of patient information amongst professionals (Goodrich et al., 2022, Ingold and Hicks, 2015, Antunes et al., 2022), technical issues, accuracy and consistency of information, multiple electronic systems with limited or no inter-operability, transferring information between in and out-of-hours, and information sharing arrangements/permissions limit their effectiveness (Firth et al., 2025, Goodrich et al., 2025, Pask et al., 2022, Wye et al., 2016). Improving access, consistency, and professional engagement with these tools is essential for better continuity and quality of care. Tools to support information sharing need to be clear in their function, otherwise they might not be adopted (Asprey et al., 2013). The quantity and quality of information shared can be of a better standard when the service has a specific interest in palliative and end-of-life care (Asprey et al., 2013). Virtual technology was used more flexibly during the pandemic due to changes in policy guidance (e.g. remote multidisciplinary team meetings) (Turner et al., 2023, Mitchell et al., 2022, Antunes et al., 2022). The use of virtual technology also resulted in a loss of personal connection, poorer relationships with colleagues, and disengagement in collaborative working, and further reinforced the reliance on community nurses (Turner et al., 2023, Mitchell et al., 2021).

Better support structures and more joined-up care are essential to continuity of care, addressing current capacity challenges and avoiding duplication of work (Maybin et al., 2016, Harrison Dening et al., 2020, Piercy et al., 2018), and is desired by health and care professionals (Lawler et al., 2020). Approaches to support collaborative working between community health nurses and others include multidisciplinary meetings (Leniz et al., 2022, Turner et al., 2023, Ramplin, 2019, Hackett et al., 2018) and care pathways (Alsop, 2010, Addicott, 2009). In addition, the adoption of palliative care guidance (e.g. Gold Standards Framework; a practical and evidence-based end-of-life care service improvement programme) (Sawkins and Bawn., 2025) was perceived as helpful in adopting a more proactive approach of collaborating with other services and across settings of care (Byron and Hoskins, 2013, Reed, 2011, Hackett et al., 2018, Badger et al., 2012). It also relies on professional engagement with the guidance and meetings, and ensuring early involvement of all relevant professionals (Reed, 2011, Handley et al., 2014, Badger et al., 2012). The overall picture of whether any one structural approach to the integration of community nursing services with other care providers is superior remains unclear (Evans et al., 2021, Wyatt et al., 2022).

Discussion

This scoping review systematically draws together evidence about community health nursing in palliative and end-of-life care, providing in-depth insights from one national healthcare system. Palliative and end-of-life care provision by community health nurses in the UK is challenged by the rising population demand, increasingly complex palliative and end-of-life care needs, and considerable workforce pressures. This has led to rapid evolution of their roles and responsibilities (and corresponding lack of clarity), a need for more advanced skills and expertise but limited opportunities for training, and challenges with effectively integrating with other services. Potential solutions to mitigate these were identified, which are discussed within the context of wider literature below.

The limited recognition of the level of palliative and end-of-life care that community health nurses provide is long-standing, as much of their work occurs behind closed doors (Walshe and Luker, 2010, Moran et al., 2024). New and extended roles are a more prominent narrative in recent evidence and particularly since the COVID pandemic (Bowers et al., 2025), but these additional responsibilities have been incorporated into a heavy workload, with little recognition, training or support (Bowers et al., 2025, Wittenberg et al., 2024). This review highlights the need for greater recognition of and clarity around their roles and responsibilities in delivering palliative and end-of-life care, for nurses themselves and for the wider multidisciplinary team. Palliative and end-of-life care should have more representation in the core standards and competency frameworks that define levels of practice and guide education programmes, which is potentially reflected by the more recent introduction of field specific standards for palliative and end-of-life care for advanced level practice (The Queen's Nursing Institute., 2026, Cornick, 2023). In terms of the wider multidisciplinary team, clear discussion within and across teams regarding role competencies and boundaries within palliative and end-of-life care provision would support more effective multidisciplinary approach (Chapman et al., 2022, Borgstrom et al., 2024).

As recognised in global nursing literature (Rosa et al., 2022, World Health Organisation 2020), investment in community health nurses’ palliative and end-of-life care education and training is essential to support equitable access. Education and training must reflect the increasingly complex needs of patients with palliative and end-of-life care needs on their caseloads. This review identifies six domains (see Table 1) where community health nurses’ confidence is more limited. These domains highlight the types of complexity that community health nurses struggle to manage that could benefit from integration with and support from specialists in palliative care. Our findings are consistent with previous reviews and demonstrate the need for further evidence-based training, the importance of offering specialised palliative care training, and the need to consider existing training gaps (Walshe and Luker, 2010, Weston et al., 2023). Community health nurses need protected time for further education and continuous professional development, and standardised curriculum and development pathways that build a confident, skilled workforce capable of meeting the future demands for palliative and end-of-life care.

Novel analysis of UK-cost data demonstrates that the community health nursing component of costs in the last year of life in 2024 is very low at <4% of all formal healthcare costs (Clarke et al., 2025). Investment is needed in the workforce to achieve policy ambitions of shifting care from the hospital to the community and ensuring equitable access to palliative and end-of-life care (UK Government 2025, Robinson et al., 2016, World Health Organisation 2020, Sleeman et al., 2021). The stressors of general nursing care demands are more well-known and data to illustrate are captured as part of national benchmarking (NHS Benchmarking Network 2024, NHS Benchmarking Network 2023, World Health Organisation 2020). This review highlights a clear need for data on palliative and end-of-life care in community health nursing. Specifically, the nature of palliative and end-of-life care delivered (related nursing activities and their duration), how this care is managed and prioritised within their wider caseload (e.g. care left undone/deferred) and what constitutes a safe caseload/workload in the context of palliative and end-of-life care.

The challenges identified with workforce shortages and the increasing demand for community health nurses is echoed in other literature (Pressley and Garside, 2023), with clear implications for palliative care delivery (Rosa et al., 2022). Understanding why nurses stay in the profession, or not, is important in addressing this issue (Pressley and Garside, 2023). In terms of palliative and end-of-life care, community nurses need time to develop a therapeutic relationships to deliver the kind of care they would like. Palliative care delivery is often at odds with the limited time to deliver care (Weston et al., 2023) leading to task-based approaches to care. More formal support is needed for nurses’ emotional and moral wellbeing and to enhance professional resilience. Capacity building and the creation of roles that will foster staff retainment are needed (Dalgarno et al., 2025). Roles need to reflect the advanced skills of community health nurses and the growing need for palliative care (Dalgarno et al., 2025).

Effective integration and working relationships with other services are seen as a core health provision concept, both within and outside of palliative care (Rosa et al., 2022, UK Government 2025, World Health Organisation 2020). Mirroring previous literature (Chapman et al., 2022, Wallerstedt et al., 2019, Joshi et al., 2025, Borgstrom et al., 2025), effective integration requires a shared understanding of palliative and end-of-life care and roles in care delivery, and professional engagement. Our findings highlight the importance of involving nurses early on to better support care coordination and integrated working. UK national guidance stipulates that adults approaching the end of life should have care coordinated between different providers and services to collaborative working (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence 2019). The community health nurse often assumes this role, but ambiguity around this can lead to disjointed care or duplication of work (McDonald and Hasson, 2024, Reeves et al., 2020). Partnerships with and support from other professionals could be improved by enabling access both in- and out-of-hours (e.g. 24/7 advice specialist advice line), improving information sharing, and addressing changes in the ways of working (e.g. lack of co-location/more remote working by general practitioners).

Main research gaps identified

Several research gaps were identified; the main research gaps are summarised below:

1.Understanding and recognition of community health nursing roles in palliative and end-of-life care: There remains a lack of clarity around what community health nurses do, how their roles and responsibilities are evolving and (including the impact of new nursing roles and responsibilities), and how this may impact on expectations within the multi-disciplinary team.

2.The scale and nature of palliative and end-of-life care community health nurses provide: There is limited or no robust evidence defining what constitutes a safe caseload, how much of their time is spent on palliative and end-of-life care, and how often this kind of care is left undone or deferred. These are impacted by inconsistent reporting of nursing roles. It is important that studies clearly define the nursing role and title and consider skill mix.

Strengths and limitations

The focus on the UK-context enabled an in-depth exploration of the evidence. The findings inform wider national and international community-based palliative and end-of-life care provision, including workforce planning, training and education, and integration across providers and services. To identify relevant evidence, we conducted a comprehensive search that encompassed both published literature and grey literature. Therefore, included items/reports vary in detail necessitating results which are descriptive, but provide an overall picture. A Patient and Public Involvement Group and Community Nursing Expert Panel informed the development of the review and provided guidance with evidence synthesis. This brought experiential, practice, and academic knowledge together to ensure the relevance of results.

One limitation may be the use of conference abstracts to identify full papers rather than for inclusion, as conference abstracts often provide timely information on emerging research trends and findings. This may limit the comprehensiveness of the review’s findings. However, this approach was deemed appropriate due to the breadth of the review and to support the data extraction. Additionally, in our aim to be inclusive of the variety of nursing roles and titles, there may be some variation in the relevance of the findings to these different groups of nurses and nursing support staff.

Conclusions

Community health nurses are essential to the delivery of palliative and end-of-life care at home. Addressing workforce skills and capacity, role clarity, and integration with wider services is critical to meet future demand and ensure equitable, high-quality care. This review highlights the urgent need for these challenges in community health nursing to be addressed to support sustainable palliative and end-of-life care delivery.

Implications of the findings

We acknowledge the limitations of drawing implications for policy and practice from the findings due to no formal assessment of methodological quality taking place. However, the challenges and potential solutions identified in this review may have relevance to policymakers and practitioners.

Implications for research

Further research, building on analyses of UK-cost data (Clarke et al., 2025), is needed to quantify and evaluate the cost contribution of palliative and end-of-life care activities undertaken by community health nurses and define a safe caseload. It would be useful to explore the impact of evolving nursing roles and responsibilities on service delivery and workforce retention or how role ambiguity affects multidisciplinary collaboration.

Implications for policy

The size and skill mix of the community health nursing workforce needs to be addressed supported by increased investment, to keep pace with rising demand and complexity of care needs. There is a need for national standardised data collection to better understand the amount of palliative and end-of-life care community health nurses provide (activities, duration, and care left undone/deferred).

Implications for practice

Palliative and end-of-life care must form a significant component of pre- and post-registration training, so necessary skills and expertise can be embedded. Infrastructures like the standards and competency frameworks need to ensure that palliative and end-of-life care is sufficiently included. Community health nurses need to be supported to attend education and training and have a clear pathway for clinical progression.
