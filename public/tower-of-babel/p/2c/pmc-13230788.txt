Structure and isomer-specific femtosecond real-time relaxation dynamics of hydrated electron motifs in water cluster anions
Authors: Sejun An, Sang Kyu Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We reexamined hydrated electron motifs in water cluster anions using photoelectron and electronic absorption spectroscopies under identical ion source conditions. The structural motifs, previously characterized as four, are now established as five in the present work, thereby refining the framework of hydrated electron isomers. Time-resolved photoelectron spectroscopy revealed motif-dependent relaxation dynamics, with certain surface-bound species undergoing more efficient excited-state autodetachment than others. Extending the temporal probing window uncovered long-lived vibrationally excited ground-state anions (τ ∼ 340 ps), far exceeding prior expectations, while increasing cluster size enhanced internal conversion efficiency. These results closely align with theoretical predictions. More broadly, they demonstrate that excited-state dynamics are highly sensitive to local structure, giving rise to distinct relaxation pathways under different hydration environments. The structure–dynamics relationships established here provide a foundation for extending such studies to other hydrated species, advancing fundamental insight into electron solvation and relaxation in aqueous systems.

Introduction

The gas-phase water cluster anion, (H₂O)(n)⁻, has been both intensively and extensively investigated for many recent decades as a prototypical model system for elucidating structure and dynamics of the hydrated electron. The hydrated electron, a key intermediate in electron transfer processes, plays a pivotal role in redox chemistry, radiation-induced chemistry, photosynthesis, water/air interface, and numerous biological reactions1–6. Dynamics of hydrated electrons are actually strongly governed by their diverse structural motifs in various environments. These include geminate recombination, formation of contact-pairs, or anionic chemical reactions in the gas phase, bulk medium, or gas/liquid/solid interfaces. For instance, surface-localized electrons exhibit rapid relaxation at the water/air interface whereas those undergo relatively slow geminate recombination in the bulk6. Furthermore, as the simplest hydrated electron system, detailed study of the water cluster anions should provide a solid platform for thorough understanding of the otherwise ambiguous hydration dynamics of electrons in general.

Early investigations of hydrated electrons primarily sought to connect gas-phase studies of water cluster anions with bulk-phase behavior, focusing in particular on determining their vertical binding energies (VBEs). In 1990, Coe et al. measured photoelectron spectra of size-selected (H₂O)(n)⁻ and reported a linear dependence of VBE on n−1/3 7. By extrapolating these data to the bulk limit (n → ∞), they estimated VBE of the hydrated electron in aqueous solution. Subsequent studies by Ayotte et al., employing electronic absorption spectroscopy, demonstrated that water cluster anions may host surface-bound electrons as well as internally solvated ones8. This insight challenged validity of the extrapolation approach, as it was presumed that the excess electron in the water cluster anion is internally solvated (fully encapsulated within a water cavity) as in the bulk phase. As a result, research efforts have increasingly focused on determining whether specific (H₂O)(n)⁻ cluster isomers localize the excess electron internally or at the surface.

In this context, it is noteworthy that Verlet et al9. reported photoelectron spectroscopy of water cluster anions generated under various ion source conditions, identifying three distinct motifs, designated I, II, and III. Among these, isomer I exhibited the highest electron binding energy and was ascribed to the so-called ‘cavity isomer’. This assignment particularly supports the extrapolation method proposed by Coe et al., which yielded a VBE value of 3.3 eV for liquid water7. More recently, direct measurements using liquid microjet photoelectron spectroscopy have reported the VBE in bulk water, ranging from 3.3 to 3.7 eV10–13, consistent with the early estimates derived from water cluster anions. However, a later high-resolution photoelectron spectroscopy for the low-temperature water cluster anions by Ma et al14. revealed that the photoelectron band which had been assigned to the cavity isomer (I) could be further resolved into two distinct photoelectron peaks. These are attributed to two different structural motifs designated as Ia and Ib, with the latter of the slightly higher binding energy to be assigned as the cavity isomer. Indeed, more recent liquid microjet measurements have reported a refined VBE for bulk water of 3.76 ± 0.05 eV15, which aligns more closely with the value extrapolated from the photoelectron peak of Ib (3.60 ± 0.03 eV) than with that of I16. Moreover, infrared spectroscopic studies by Johnson and co-workers reported that the isomer I can also adopt a surface-bound configuration17–19, supporting that the isomer I may not be entirely attributed to the cavity isomer. These experimental findings consistently support that the initially-categorized isomer I could be further classified into Ia and Ib isomers under certain ion source conditions.

Alongside these experimental advances, theoretical studies have provided predictions for diverse structural motifs of hydrated electrons, occasionally implying the existence of additional structural motifs beyond those experimentally identified2,20–27. Notably, Jacobson et al., employing hybrid quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics (QM/MM) models, proposed four distinct isomeric structures for water cluster anions26. These theoretically predicted structures were classified as (i) dipole-bound surface, (ii) proper surface, (iii) partially embedded surface, and (iv) cavity isomers (Fig. 1). Among these, the calculated VBEs for the dipole-bound surface and cavity isomers correspond to the experimentally observed isomers III and Ib, respectively, whereas the proper and partially embedded surface isomers show no clear correspondence. Instead, their VBEs cluster near the Ia region but remain markedly different from that of the II isomer. To address this longstanding issue, we have performed photoelectron and electronic-absorption spectroscopies of water cluster anions under identical ion source conditions. Notably, a recent study by Herburger et al.28, using an ion cyclotron resonance (ICR) mass spectrometer, resolved two distinct absorption bands that were attributed to surface isomers. However, because no photoelectron spectrum was recorded under the same ion-source conditions, it should be emphasized that the bands could not be assigned definitively within the established isomeric framework (Ia, Ib, II, and III), and thus the structural classification remains unchanged29. By implementing a tailored voltage approach in the velocity-map imaging (VMI) apparatus30, we enabled the concurrent acquisition of photoelectron and absorption spectra without altering the experimental setup31–34. Such simultaneous measurements were crucial, as isomer distributions are highly sensitive to source conditions9,35,36. This combined approach provides a direct spectroscopic connection between the absorption features and specific photoelectron bands recorded under strictly identical conditions. Consequently, we unambiguously show that the two previously observed absorption bands both originate from the Ia isomer, which must therefore be resolved into two distinct motifs, Ia1 and Ia2, expanding the established structural landscape from four to five isomers. This conclusion is enabled only by integrating insights from earlier absorption studies (which had already excluded any involvement of Ib and III in the two absorption bands under consideration) with our photoelectron measurements, which further allow us to rule out contributions from isomer II.

Consequently, based on the combined spectroscopic evidences, the number of structurally distinct motifs for water cluster anions is now proposed to be five. These findings prompt a re-evaluation of earlier experimental and theoretical assignments, offering a more comprehensive understanding of the hydrated electrons in water cluster anions. Furthermore, we performed time-resolved photoelectron spectroscopy (TRPES) to probe excited-state dynamics of water cluster anions with isomer-specific resolution. Although excited-state dynamics of water cluster anions have been previously investigated over different cluster sizes37–40, isomer-dependent dynamical behavior for the same-sized cluster remains insufficiently characterized. Additionally, we have identified a sub-nanosecond decay component, which is likely attributable to processes such as water evaporation and/or autodetachment from the ground state. This observation suggests that the lifetime of photoexcited water cluster anions may be substantially longer than previously estimated 37.

Results and discussion

Spectroscopic evidence for Ia1 and Ia2 motifs in water cluster anions

Absorption spectra of size-selected (H₂O)(n)⁻ clusters, obtained by monitoring the depletion of the parent anion signal as a function of excitation energy (Fig. 2a), reveal two distinct spectral features. These features could be well reproduced by convolution of two Gaussian line shapes, suggesting the presence of at least two different isomeric forms. In water cluster anions, electronic transitions are hardly accompanied by dramatic structural changes, since the excess electron in mid-sized anionic clusters is expected to be largely delocalized rather than localized. Accordingly, as above, we assign the two absorption features to two distinct isomeric forms, each exhibiting the s → p excitation of the excess electron. Under identical ion source conditions, photoelectron spectra were recorded at a photon energy of 1.57 eV to estimate VBEs of corresponding isomers (Fig. 2b). It should be noted that the noisy feature in the high binding (low kinetic) energy region originates from instrumental detection error. The VBEs of motifs I and II reported by Verlet et al.19 are indicated in Fig. 2b for comparison with our photoelectron spectra though it should be noted that linear extrapolation was used for several clusters whose VBEs were not directly measured. Small spectral features associated with the II motif are observed for several clusters (Fig. 2b). Except for the n = 13 cluster, however, the contribution from II isomers is negligible. This observation strongly indicates that both experimentally observed absorption bands predominantly originate from I isomers across the entire cluster-size range investigated.

The above argument that the population of isomer II is extremely small under the present experimental conditions, and that its contribution to the absorption spectra is therefore negligible, is further supported by the difference photoelectron spectra discussed below. Specifically, because the photoelectron spectra measured at 1.57 eV contain overlapping contributions from autodetachment, direct detachment to the continuum, and background noise, isolating the signal originating solely from isomer II is nontrivial. To address this issue, two different temporal sequences of femtosecond laser pulses were employed. One pulse was set to 1.57 eV (probe), while the other was fixed at 0.84 eV (pump). At a pump–probe delay time (Δt) of −120 fs, the autodetachment signal is expected to be substantially reduced, as the pump pulse may depopulate the corresponding metastable excited state, whereas the contribution from direct detachment by the probe remains largely unaffected. In contrast, at zero delay (Δt = 0 fs), the pump pulse may depopulate the ground state, leading to a depletion of the entire photoelectron signal. The difference spectrum obtained by subtracting the spectrum measured at Δt = 0 fs from that at Δt = −120 fs, therefore, highlights the direct detachment contribution while suppressing autodetachment. In addition, this procedure significantly reduces spectral noise, enabling clear identification of photoelectron features associated with both isomers I and II (Fig. 2b and 2c).

It should be noted that the cavity isomer (Ib) is not expected to exhibit a blue shift in the absorption maximum with the increase of the cluster size21,25,26,28. Namely, when the electron is confined within a cavity, its radius of gyration remains nearly constant for different cluster sizes, resulting in minimal variation in the energy spacing between s and p quantum states. For this reason, the same absorption band in the earlier study by Herburger et al. was assigned to the surface isomers28. Barnett et al. theoretically predicted the absorption maximum of the cavity isomer would lie slightly above 2 eV, without a significant shift with cluster size, whereas that of the surface isomer would be around 1 eV for n = 18–6421. Furthermore, quantum/classical molecular dynamics simulations by Turi et al. predicted that the cavity isomer is not anticipated to be thermodynamically stable for water cluster anions with n ≤ 100 and may exist only under low-temperature conditions due to kinetic trapping24,25. Taken all together, these theoretical predictions and experimental observations suggest that the absorption and photoelectron spectroscopic features observed in Fig. 2 most likely represent two variants of the Ia surface isomer. In other words, Ia may encompass two distinct structural motifs, hereby designated as Ia1 and Ia2.

For proper and partially embedded surface isomers of water cluster anions with n < 40 (or n–1/3 > 0.29), the VBE difference was predicted to be less than 0.2 eV (Fig. 1)26. These subtle VBE differences, despite they may diverge with increasing the cluster size, are hardly resolved in photoelectron spectra. In contrast, however, two distinct absorption features well indicate the presence of different hydrated electron motifs. We therefore assign the higher- and lower-energy absorption bands to the Ia1 and Ia2 isomers, respectively, which most likely correspond to partially embedded and proper surface structures26. It should be emphasized that, although the assignment by Herburger et al.28 appears broadly consistent with the present interpretation, the II motif could not be excluded in their analysis of the absorption profiles, as no photoelectron spectra were recorded under identical experimental conditions. Consequently, their interpretation could not extend beyond the four previously identified isomeric motifs (Ia, Ib, II, and III)28,29 nor allow refinement of their relative contributions. In this sense, the assignment by Herburger et al.28 relied exclusively on absorption spectral features and did not assess alternative isomeric motifs; accordingly, while largely aligned with our conclusions, it requires logical refinement in light of the present results.

Taken together with prior theoretical and experimental studies, our results then support a refined classification of water cluster anion isomers. In addition to the cavity isomer Ib, we identify now four distinct surface isomers: Ia1, Ia2, II, and III. The Ia1 and Ia2 species represent subdivisions of the previously defined Ia surface isomer. The expected order of the radius of gyration is Ib < Ia1 < Ia2 < II < III, with smaller values reflecting greater electron embeddedness. Isomer III was not observed in our experiment, most likely due to the rapid autodetachment of dipole-bound states32,41–44, which would render this species even less populated than isomer II under our experimental conditions. It should be emphasized again that the two surface-bound isomers predicted by QM/MM calculations26 should now be reassigned as Ia1 and Ia2, rather than the previously assigned motifs Ia and II. The Ib and III isomers predicted by theory are consistent with the experimental observations. On the other hand, the experimentally identified II motif does not match the theoretical prediction. Therefore, although only four motifs were theoretically proposed, water anion clusters should be classified into five distinct motifs.

Upon photoexcitation of (H₂O)₁₄⁻, the excited-state signal (C) rises and decays very rapidly with τ = 140 ± 10 fs, while the ground-state spectral features corresponding to BIa and BII populations undergo prompt depletions at the delay time of zero. Simultaneous decreases of both signals indicate that the 0.84 eV pump pulse indeed excites both Ia2 and II isomers. Recovery of the ground-state population of BIa or BII occurs on a sub-picosecond timescale, but it is apparently incomplete which at the first sight may suggest that the excited-state population does not fully return to the ground state38,39. However, it should be emphasized that incomplete recovery of the depleted ground state may also arise from various factors other than just the excited-state relaxation alone. For instance, the ground-state dynamics could be influenced by the change of the photoelectron kinetic energy distribution following internal conversion. The BIa recovery occurs with τ = 230 ± 30 fs, which is slower than the decay of the excited-state signal (140 fs), and thus it most likely reflects structural reorganization of the cluster which is consistent with a TRPES study previously reported by Paik et al.37 Such reorganization dynamics had also been observed in transient absorption spectroscopy of bulk water45 and liquid microjet TRPES46 with similar time constants (200–400 fs). In addition, as pointed out in a previous study by Bragg et al.38, it should be pointed out that such an incomplete recovery could be consequence from the direct detachment by the pump laser pulse.

As shown in Fig. 3c, the BII transient shows a much larger extent of recovery compared to BIa. According to the TRPES study by Bragg et al.38, the BI photoelectron peak does not overlap with BII neither through isomerization nor from other time- dependent behaviors, and thus the transient recovery is most likely attributed to ground state dynamics of BII. Therefore, despite the above-mentioned complexity (see above), at least in small water cluster anions (n = 14), internal conversion of the Ia2 isomer is less efficient than that of the II isomer. In other words, as internal conversion and autodetachment are kinetically competing, it could be said that ESAD may be relatively more dominant over internal conversion for Ia2 in comparison to II.

For comparison of the excited-state dynamics between Ia2 and Ia1, time-resolved photoelectron spectra were also acquired for (H₂O)₁₄⁻ at a pump energy of 1.09 eV (Fig. 3d–f). At this pump energy, isomer Ia1 is predominantly excited. Similar to the case of the 0.84 eV excitation, recovery of the bleached Ia1 is also incomplete at 1.09 eV, and it is fair to state that the overall temporal dynamics of Ia1 is quite similar to those of Ia2. Notably, however, the extent of recovery is a bit smaller for Ia1 than that of Ia2, suggesting that the ESAD is relatively more competitive for Ia1 compared to Ia2. Indeed, in the photoproduct action spectra obtained with the nanosecond laser, more pronounced ESAD is observed within the absorption band of Ia1. At 0.84 eV, observed photoproducts are mostly GSAD and fragmentation from the ground state, whereas at 1.09 eV, ESAD becomes the dominant pathway (vide infra). In contrast, isomer II consistently displays the largest recovery ratio to the ground state (~88% at 0.84 eV and ~80% at 1.09 eV), which is in stark contrast to the substantially lower values observed for the Ia isomers (~35% for Ia2, ~14% for Ia1).

Meanwhile, the photoelectron band A near zero kinetic energy, attributed to GSAD, ESAD, and/or direct detachment, displays a slow-rising component following an initial prompt increase caused by rapid relaxation of the p-like state with τ ~ 140 fs (vide supra), Fig. 3. This behavior arises because autodetachment intrinsically produces low–kinetic-energy electrons, while direct detachment at the pump energy of 0.84 eV likewise generates electrons with near-zero kinetic energy. The band A consistently exhibits a slow-rising component across a wide range of cluster sizes, yielding an average rise-time constant of 340 ± 50 ps, with no systematic dependence on cluster size (Supplementary Data). Such a slow-rising component is unlikely to originate from direct detachment or ESAD, and is therefore most plausibly assigned to GSAD, competing with fragmentation. Considering that the water monomer binding energy in water cluster anions is approximately 0.42–0.47 eV47,48, the slow rising component observed at the pump energy of 0.84 or 1.09 eV is therefore most likely due to internal conversion from the p-like state, followed by slow GSAD. This finding contrasts with a previous report suggesting that vibrationally excited states relax within only a few picoseconds37, indicating instead that vibrationally hot water cluster anions may persist for substantially longer times than previously recognized.

We have also examined influence of the cluster size on the excited-state dynamics of water cluster anions using TRPES across a range of cluster sizes, as shown in Fig. 4a–b (Supplementary Data). As the cluster size increases, the recovery of the BIa peak becomes more pronounced (Fig. 4d), indicating that relaxation into the s-like ground state becomes increasingly efficient in larger clusters at both pump energies. This trend is the consequence from the increase of the electron binding energy with increasing the cluster size, suppressing ESAD. The effect of ESAD is also reflected in the lifetime of the C transient (Supplementary Data). Namely, up to the cluster size of n = 20, the lifetime remains nearly unchanged whereas it decreases for the larger clusters, which is consistent with earlier studies38,39. For clusters with n < 20, the recovery yield is larger at 0.84 eV compared to that at 1.09 eV (Fig. 4d). This is just because the Ia1 isomer is relatively more selectively excited at 1.09 eV compared to 0.84 eV (vide supra). For n = 14, for instance (according to deconvoluted absorption features), the 0.84 eV excitation selectively excites Ia1 or Ia2 by ~23 or ~77%, respectively, whereas Ia1 is expected to be predominantly excited (~85%) at 1.09 eV (Supplementary Data). Incidentally, it would be of interest to investigate the electron dynamics in deuterated water cluster anions. For (D₂O)₁₅‒, a BIa transient recorded at 0.75 eV has been reported previously,³⁸ and its recovery rate is very similar to that observed for the (H₂O)₁₅‒ transient measured at 0.81 eV (Supplementary Data). It is also notable that the excited-state dynamics probed by TRPES are corroborated by nanosecond photoexcitation spectra (Fig. 4c). In these measurements, the photoelectron and photofragment signals were recorded separately as functions of excitation energy, and the absorption features were deconvoluted into electron and fragment contributions. This separation allows the relative branching ratios between autodetachment and fragmentation to be directly evaluated for each cluster size. In addition, because GSAD electrons exhibit a characteristic long tail along the time-of-flight axis (Fig. 4f), their relative contribution as a function of photon energy could also be assessed. Overall, larger clusters show greater contributions from photofragmentation relative to photoelectron signal, indicating that internal conversion becomes more efficient with increasing cluster size, consistent with our TRPES results. To the best of our knowledge, this work presents the first photoexcitation action spectra of both photoelectrons and photofragments from water

cluster anions, although an earlier ICR study reported only photofragment distributions28. Notably, the scaled sum of photoelectron and photofragment yields as a function of excitation energy closely reproduces the absorption profiles of individual clusters. This demonstrates that autodetachment (ESAD or GSAD) together with subsequent fragmentation pathways dominate the excited-state relaxation dynamics of water cluster anions.

Conclusion

Isomeric motifs of hydrated electrons in water cluster anions were reexamined by combining photoelectron and electronic absorption spectroscopies under identical ion source conditions. The four previously characterized isomeric structures are now expanded to five in the present work, thereby refining the framework of hydrated electron isomers. Notably, our experimental results corroborate theoretical predictions, underscoring the essential role of computational modeling in advancing the otherwise formidable understanding of hydrated electrons. Time-resolved photoelectron spectroscopy (TRPES) reveals isomer-specific excited-state relaxation dynamics. By tuning the excitation energy, we probed the dynamics of distinct surface-bound motifs (Ia1, Ia2, and II) across different cluster sizes. In particular, isomers Ia1 and Ia2 undergo more efficient excited-state autodetachment compared to II, offering an additional structural property that can be used to distinguish hydrated electron motifs. Extending the temporal probing window further reveals that vibrationally excited ground-state anions persist for much longer than previously recognized (τ ∼ 340 ps). With increasing cluster size, internal conversion becomes more efficient for both Ia1 and Ia2, as evidenced by femtosecond transient measurements. Our findings highlight a strong consistency between theoretical predictions and experimental observations, reinforcing confidence in previous computational approaches and emphasizing their value for future studies. Moreover, the results demonstrate that excited-state dynamics are highly sensitive to structural motifs, with distinct relaxation pathways emerging from different hydration environments. This structural sensitivity becomes particularly relevant in anisotropic environments such as aerosols or the water/air interface, where electron localization is strongly governed by local geometry. Looking forward, the structure–dynamics relationships established here provide a foundation for systematic extensions to other hydrated species, offering a broader platform for understanding electron solvation and relaxation in aqueous systems.

Methods

Water cluster anions were generated by expanding water vapor seeded in neon gas (backing pressure of 5 bar) through a 150 μm nozzle using a pulsed Even-Lavie valve equipped with a filament ionizer. Thermal electrons emitted from the filament ionized the neon gas via electron impact, producing secondary slow electrons that were subsequently attached to water molecules to form anions. The resulting water cluster anions were accelerated toward the laser interaction region using a Wiley–McLaren time-of-flight (TOF) mass spectrometer, which enabled mass selection based on arrival time differences. When the desired size of water cluster anions arrived between the repelling and extractor electrodes of a home-built velocity map imaging (VMI) spectrometer, a laser pulse was applied to induce photoelectron and/or photofragment formation. Photoelectrons were imaged using chevron-type microchannel plates (MCPs) backed by a phosphor screen and CCD camera. Images were reconstructed into photoelectron kinetic energy distributions using the BASEX method, which performs an Abel inversion through a basis-set expansion. Using a photomultiplier tube, photofragments were recorded in a mass-selective manner based on their arrival times at the MCP, which varied according to the electric field given in the VMI setup. Especially, the contribution of GSAD could be separately identified at the edge of the long tail of photoelectron signal along the time-of-flight axis49,50. For the photoelectron spectroscopy, the voltage ratio of the repeller to that of the extractor was set to 0.71, while it was adjusted to 1.23 for the photofragment detection. Electronic absorption spectra were obtained by monitoring depletion of the parent anion signal as a function of photon energy while it is normalized with respect to the laser intensity. Tunable nanosecond laser pulses were provided by an Nd:YAG pumped optical parametric oscillator (OPO) system (NT342, Ekspla). All photoelectron spectra of water cluster anions were recorded using a Ti:sapphire regenerative amplifier (Legend Elite-P, Coherent) seeded by a femtosecond oscillator (Vitara-T-HP, Coherent). For time-resolved photoelectron spectroscopy (TRPES), the femtosecond output at 1.57 eV was split into two parts; one was used as the probe (~200 mW) whereas the other was converted to 0.84 or 1.09 eV using an optical parametric amplifier (TOPAS, Light Conversion) to be used for the pump pulses (~60 mW for both). All beams were loosely focused to a spot size of approximately 3 mm in the laser–ion interaction region. The delay between pump and probe pulses was controlled using a 220 mm long optical delay stage (DDS 220, Thorlabs) equipped with a retroreflector (UBBR2.5-1S). The instrument response function (IRF) was determined to be approximately 100 fs from time-resolved spectra of I₂34.
