Workflows for stable cell line generation: Chemical transfection and viral transduction
Authors: Chanhyuk Min, Jongsik Kim, Gwangrog Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStable mammalian cell lines with sustained transgene expression are indispensable for functional genomics and long–term genetic studies. However, transient approaches often suffer from inconsistent efficiency and expression instability across diverse cell types. This MiniResource addresses these challenges by providing a comprehensive synthesis of gene delivery strategies and optimized, systematic workflows for establishing robust cell lines via chemical transfection and viral transduction. By distilling complex procedures into accessible, standardized guidelines, this work serves as an essential practical resource for achieving uniform and durable expression in both commonly used immortalized cell lines and challenging primary or immune cell populations.Keywords: Antibiotic selection, Chemical transfection, Stable cell line, Transgene expression, Viral transduction

INTRODUCTION

Stable cell lines characterized by sustained transgene expression serve as indispensable tools for reproducible functional assays, long–term genetic perturbation, and high-throughput screening in mammalian systems (Bui et al., 2023, Cabaniols et al., 2009, Tandon et al., 2018, Wurm, 2004). While a vast array of transfection and gene delivery strategies has been developed to introduce exogenous nucleic acids, delivery efficiency remains highly variable across different cell types. This variability is often constrained by intrinsic factors, including cell lineage, differentiation state, and innate immune signaling pathways (Chong et al., 2021, Fus-Kujawa et al., 2021, Kim and Eberwine, 2010). Consequently, transient transfection frequently yields heterogeneous and ephemeral expression profiles—a limitation particularly pronounced in primary and immune cells, such as macrophages. Such instability severely restricts the utility of transient approaches for applications requiring consistent, long–term gene expression (Dokka et al., 2000, Herb et al., 2024, Keller et al., 2018). In contrast, the establishment of stable cell lines bypasses these hurdles by facilitating uniform and durable transgene expression through genomic integration and subsequent selection (Dong et al., 2020, Liew et al., 2007). In this MiniResource, we summarize fundamental gene delivery strategies for stable cell line generation and present standardized workflows illustrating the achievement of stable transgene expression via chemical transfection and viral transduction.

OVERVIEW OF GENE DELIVERY STRATEGIES FOR STABLE CELL LINE GENERATION

Gene delivery strategies for mammalian systems are broadly classified into chemical, physical, and biological (viral) methodologies. Each of these approaches has been tailored for the establishment of stable cell lines, achieved either through direct genomic integration or via continuous selective pressure. Although a significant portion of these techniques was initially engineered for transient transgene expression, the transition to stable cell establishment necessitates the long-term persistence of the introduced genetic material. Consequently, generating stable cell lines entails critical considerations that extend beyond initial delivery efficiency, focusing instead on the stability and durability of the introduced genetic material within host cells.

Chemical Transfection

Chemical transfection utilizes synthetic carriers designed to condense nucleic acids and facilitate cellular internalization, predominantly via endocytic pathways. Established methodologies encompass cationic lipid-based systems (lipofection), polymer-based reagents such as polyethylenimine, calcium phosphate precipitation, and dendrimer-mediated delivery, as well as nanoparticle-based platforms (Boussif et al., 1995, Cullis and Hope, 2017, Felgner et al., 1987, Graham and van der Eb, 1973, Kukowska-Latallo et al., 1996). While chemical approaches are valued for their technical simplicity and scalability across numerous mammalian lineages, they do not intrinsically catalyze genomic integration. Consequently, the establishment of stable transgene expression relies on infrequent, stochastic insertion events, necessitating rigorous antibiotic selection and subsequent clonal screening to isolate high-performing, stably integrated cell populations.

Physical Transfection

Physical transfection modalities achieve intracellular nucleic acid delivery by transiently permeabilizing the plasma membrane through the application of exogenous physical forces. This category includes diverse techniques such as electroporation, microinjection, biolistic delivery (gene gun), sonoporation, laser-assisted optoporation, and magnetofection, alongside emerging microfluidic platforms that utilize mechanical deformation (Capecchi, 1980, Mehier-Humbert and Guy, 2005, Neumann et al., 1982, Sanford et al., 1987, Scherer et al., 2002, Sharei et al., 2013, Tsukakoshi et al., 1984). These methods offer the distinct advantage of being largely independent of endogenous cellular uptake machinery, making them particularly effective for primary cells and other transfection-refractory populations. However, their implementation often demands specialized instrumentation and meticulous optimization to balance delivery efficiency with cellular viability. Similar to chemical methods, stable cell line generation via physical delivery is dependent on stringent post-transfection selection unless integrated with targeted genomic insertion systems.

Viral Transduction

Viral transduction offers a highly efficient mechanism for gene delivery and remains a cornerstone in the generation of stable mammalian cell lines. Retroviral and lentiviral vectors are particularly advantageous as they facilitate stable genomic integration, ensuring sustained transgene expression; notably, lentiviral systems extend this utility to both proliferating and quiescent (nondividing) cells (Miller and Rosman, 1989, Naldini et al., 1996). In contrast, adenoviral, adeno-associated viral, and herpes simplex viral vectors primarily mediate episomal expression, rendering them less effective for establishing long–term stable cell lines (Daya and Berns, 2008, Glorioso and Fink, 2004, Graham and Prevec, 1995). While viral platforms provide superior delivery efficiency, their implementation necessitates rigorous biosafety protocols and may introduce phenotypic variability due to the stochastic nature of genomic integration sites.

Integration-Focused Strategies

To enhance the efficiency and predictability of stable transgene expression, several integration-focused strategies have been developed. Transposon-based systems, such as piggyBac and Sleeping Beauty, enable robust non–viral genomic integration and are frequently employed to generate stable cell pools or monoclonal lines with high efficiency (Ding et al., 2005, Ivics et al., 1997). Alternatively, site–specific integration technologies—including clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/Cas9-mediated knock-in and recombinase-mediated cassette exchange—allow for targeted insertion into predefined “safe harbor” genomic loci. These precision approaches significantly mitigate position effects and enhance expression uniformity, although they typically involve lower throughput and more extensive screening requirements compared to random integration methods (Cong et al., 2013, Schlake and Bode, 1994).

STABLE CELL GENERATION VIA CHEMICAL TRANSFECTION

Chemical transfection serves as a viable strategy for establishing stable mammalian cell lines when coupled with rigorous antibiotic selection and systematic clonal isolation, notwithstanding the inherently low frequency of spontaneous genomic integration (Wurm, 2004). Consequently, the success of this methodology is predicated on stringent selection pressures and the meticulous validation of individual clones, rather than the initial transfection efficiency alone (Kaufman et al., 2008, Zahn-Zabal et al., 2001). The following workflow delineates the essential procedural steps for establishing stable cell lines following plasmid–based chemical transfection.

Antibiotic Kill Curve Determination

To ensure the effective selection of stably transfected cells, the minimal lethal concentration (MLC) of the specific selection antibiotic—typically puromycin or G418—must be empirically determined (Kaufman et al., 2008, Tandon et al., 2018). Cells are seeded into multiwell plates and subjected to a gradient of antibiotic concentrations. The culture medium is replaced every 2 to 3 days; after an incubation period of 7 to 8 days, the lowest concentration that induces complete cell mortality is identified. This optimized concentration is subsequently employed for selection during the stable cell line generation process.

Transfection and Selection

Cells are plated 24 hours before transfection to achieve optimal confluency at the time of gene delivery. Plasmids encoding the transgene of interest, along with an integrated antibiotic resistance cassette, are introduced using a chemical transfection reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Approximately 24 hours post-transfection, the culture medium is replaced with fresh medium supplemented with the predetermined MLC of the antibiotic. Selection pressure is maintained by refreshing the antibiotic-containing medium every 3 to 4 days to eliminate nonresistant cells and debris. Under these stringent conditions, distinct antibiotic-resistant colonies typically emerge within 2 to 3 weeks.

Isolation, Expansion, and Validation of Clones

Before isolation, a 24-well plate is prepared with fresh medium containing the selection antibiotic. Single, well-isolated colonies are harvested via localized trypsinization and transferred into individual wells. These clones are expanded for approximately 1 week until they reach appropriate confluency. For downstream validation, each clone is split into 2 parallel cultures: one for continued expansion and the other for expression analysis. Stable transgene expression is validated via fluorescence microscopy or flow cytometry for fluorescent reporters, or through western blotting for nonfluorescent proteins. Only those clones exhibiting robust and sustained expression levels are selected for further experimental applications (Fig. 1).

STABLE CELL GENERATION VIA VIRAL TRANSDUCTION

Viral transduction represents a highly efficient strategy for establishing stable cell lines by facilitating the genomic integration of exogenous transgenes via integrating viral vectors (Naldini et al., 1996). Retroviral and lentiviral systems are among the most prevalent platforms for this application, as they utilize a conserved framework for virus production, transduction, and subsequent selection (Biasco et al., 2012, Dull et al., 1998). The following workflow delineates the essential procedural steps for establishing stable cell lines using integrating viral transduction approaches.

Virus Production

HEK293T packaging cells are seeded 24 hours before transfection to ensure they reach approximately 70% to 90% confluency at the time of gene delivery. On the following day, a mixture of packaging plasmids and the transfer vector is introduced via chemical transfection. To mitigate cytotoxicity and maximize viral titers, the culture medium is replaced with fresh medium 6 to 8 hours post-transfection. Viral supernatants are harvested at 24 and 48 hours post-transfection, clarified by low-speed centrifugation, and passed through a 0.45-µm membrane filter to eliminate cellular debris. The resulting clarified supernatants may be utilized immediately for transduction or stored in aliquots at −80ºC (Gándara et al., 2017).

Viral Transduction

Target mammalian cells are seeded 1 day before infection to achieve an optimal confluency of 30% to 50% at the time of viral exposure. Pre–warmed viral supernatant is added in culture medium at the desired multiplicity of infection (MOI) and applied to the target cells. Transduction efficiency can be further augmented by the addition of polybrene or through the application of spinfection (centrifugation-assisted infection), with parameters empirically optimized to maintain cellular viability. Following a 24- to 48-hour incubation period, the viral inoculum is replaced with fresh culture medium.

Selection and Clonal Isolation

Stably transduced populations are enriched either through stringent antibiotic selection—as previously described—or, in cases where a fluorescent reporter is utilized, via fluorescence–activated cell sorting to isolate individual clones. Subsequent characterization of these clones for transgene expression facilitates the establishment of stable cell lines with highly uniform expression profiles (Fig. 2).

CONCLUDING REMARKS

This MiniResource has provided a comprehensive synthesis of the fundamental strategies and standardized workflows essential for the generation of stable mammalian cell lines characterized by durable transgene expression. The methodologies delineated herein offer a robust and versatile framework that can be readily adapted to accommodate diverse cell lineages and a wide array of experimental applications in molecular and cellular biology.
