Phrenic Ampulla Emptying Dysfunction in Patients With Esophageal Symptoms
Authors: Sujin Kim, Walter Marquez-Lavenant, Ravinder K Mittal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/AimsPharyngeal pump, esophageal peristalsis, and phrenic ampulla emptying play important roles in the propulsion of bolus from the mouth to the stomach. There is limited information available on the mechanism of normal and abnormal phrenic ampulla emptying. The goal of our study is to describe the relationship between bolus flow and esophageal pressure profiles during the phrenic ampulla emptying in normal subjects and patient with phrenic ampulla dysfunction.MethodsPressure (using topography) and bolus flow (using changes in impedance) relationship through the esophagus and phrenic ampulla were determined in 15 normal subjects and 15 patients with retrograde escape of bolus from the phrenic ampulla into esophagus during primary peristalsis.ResultsDuring the phrenic ampulla phase, 2 high pressure peaks (proximal, related to lower esophageal sphincter and distal, related to crural diaphragm) were observed in normal subjects and patients during the phrenic ampulla emptying phase. The proximal was always higher than the distal one in normal subjects; in contrast, reverse was the case in patients with the retrograde escape of bolus from the phrenic ampulla into the esophagus.ConclusionsWe propose that a strong after-contraction of the lower esophageal sphincter plays an important role in the normal phrenic ampullary emptying. A defective lower esophageal after-contraction, along with high crural diaphragm pressure are responsible for the phrenic ampulla emptying dysfunction.Keywords: Esophageal dysphagia, Esophageal motility disorders, Manometry

Introduction

The major function of esophagus is to transport the swallowed material into the stomach. Each swallow induces a wave of primary peristalsis that traverses from the cranial to the caudal end of the esophagus. Initial inhibition followed by excitation are the 2 essential elements of the peristaltic wave.1,2 Initial inhibition results in receptive relaxation/accommodation of the esophagus which allows it to accept the bolus and flow through it with minimal resistance. At the end of relaxation and at each location in the esophagus, the contraction wave sweeps the bolus from the cranial to the caudal end of the esophagus. From the onset of swallow to the arrival of contraction wave in the most distal esophagus (also known as contraction deceleration point [CDP]) is the time period during which bolus travels through the esophagus.3 Studies shows that the esophagus distends in the shape of an “American Football,” as bolus traverses along the length of the esophagus, which implies that similar to contraction, inhibition wave also traverses sequentially along the length of the esophagus.4,5 Therefore, the inhibition and excitation components of peristalsis are tightly coupled to each other during the passage of bolus through the esophagus.4

Using concurrent multiple intraluminal impedance, manometry, and X-ray fluoroscopy, Lin characterized 4 phases of liquid bolus flow through the esophagus, tied to the pressure topography landmarks.6 Phase I represents the time between the opening and closing of the upper esophageal sphincter during which bolus is propelled by oropharyngeal contraction (pharyngeal pump) into the esophagus.7 Phase II represents the time period between the upper esophageal sphincter closure, to the arrival of contraction wave in the transition zone. No bolus leaves the esophagus during Phase I and II. Phase III (esophageal emptying phase) is the time during which the contractile wave front propagates to the CDP, and finally Phase IV (ampulla emptying phase) is the time from the CDP to the completion of bolus transit into the stomach. While in Phase II and III, sequential contraction of the esophagus plays an important role in the clearance of bolus from the esophagus, and in Phase IV the phrenic ampulla emptying is related to the descent of an ascended esophagus or possibly the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) back to its resting position within the esophageal hiatus (Fig. 1).6,8,9

Phrenic ampulla emptying time is the period from the contractile decelerating point to the descent of an ascended esophagus or possibly the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) back to its resting position within the esophageal hiatus that pushes the bolus into the stomach.

Impaired clearance of bolus during Phase II and III is well described in patients with motility disorders such as achalasia esophagus and ineffective esophageal motility disorders, we observed that some patients referred to the esophageal manometry laboratory for esophageal symptoms assessment have normal bolus clearance during Phase II and III but have abnormalities of emptying from the phrenic ampulla (Phase IV). Instead of emptying into the stomach, bolus escapes from the phrenic ampulla back into the esophagus. The goal of our study is to describe the relationship between bolus flow and pressure gradient across phrenic ampulla in 15 patients observed to have dysfunction of the phrenic ampulla emptying, and compare the phrenic ampulla pressure and emptying profile of these patients with those of normal healthy subjects.

Materials and Methods

Twenty-eight patients with esophageal symptoms initially thought to have retrograde movement of bolus from the phrenic ampulla into the esophagus were analyzed. These patients were referred to the esophageal motility center from Jan 2020 to Mar 2023 for an esophageal manometry study. Careful inspection of these recordings revealed that in 11 of these patients, abnormal bolus clearance occurred during Phase III of bolus clearance, and not the phrenic ampulla emptying phase. Additionally, 2 patients were found to have reflux from the hiatus hernia (Fig. 2). Therefore 15 patients with dysfunction of the phrenic ampulla emptying were analyzed in this study and the findings were compared with 15 healthy subjects (5 males; mean age 35.6 ± 12 years) with no history of gastrointestinal disease or surgery. These normal subjects were studied as part of other ongoing studies in the senior author’s laboratory.

The high-resolution manometry impedance (HRMZ) studies were performed following a 6-hour fast using an HRMZ catheter (Medtronic Inc, Minneapolis, MN, USA) with 36 circumferential pressure transducers (1 cm-interval) and 19 impedance electrodes (2 cm-interval). The HRMZ catheter was placed through the nose after local anesthesia with liquid lidocaine spray and viscous lidocaine orally and nasally. Eight to 10 swallows with 5 mL, 0.5 N saline warmed to 37℃ were performed in the supine position. The HRMZ studies were analyzed using the ManoView software (Medtronic Inc) to display impedance and pressure recordings. Impedance recording were viewed as line tracing, as well as purple color heat map (Fig. 2). To assess bolus movements through the distal esophagus and phrenic ampulla, 4-5 impedance electrodes were positioned, 1 cm apart, above the LES in the distal esophagus and phrenic ampulla. The relationship between pressure wave and impedance wave, as the 2 progress from the distal esophagus through phrenic ampulla into the stomach was examined by moving the red line (timeline) of the software, from the onset of swallow to the completion of peristaltic contraction through the esophagus (Fig. 3). The phrenic ampulla on the high-resolution manometry (HRM) record was an approximately triangular region between the CDP and the reconstitution of the LES back to its baseline position in the diaphragmatic hiatus.8 The escape of bolus from the phrenic ampulla into the esophagus was determined based on the impedance line recordings located above the LES, as a drop in impedance value of more than 100 Ohms. The bolus escape could also be seen on the impedance purple color map as the retrograde escape of bolus into the esophagus.10 The duration of phrenic ampulla formation was measured in seconds (Fig. 4). The distance between the lower edge of the LES and CDP was determined in centimeters. The LES pressure at the end of the completion of peristaltic contraction was measured using the e-sleeve function, and its cranial-caudal length (length of high pressure zone) was determined as shown in Figure 4. In normal subjects and patients, the pressure profile (Fig. 4) at different time points, as the impedance wave traversed from the distal esophagus and phrenic ampulla was either single, or double (cranial and caudal) pressure peaks. We analyzed swallows with double pressure peaks. The amplitude of the 2-pressure peaks during the phrenic ampullary emptying phase were determined using the e-sleeve function of the Manoview program. A composite image of the impedance line recording and topographical pressure plot, along with pressure and impedance wave profile at different time points during the phrenic ampulla emptying phase was built, as shown in Figures 3 and 5. The protocol for the study was approved by the human investigation committee (Protocol No. 202106X). The Helsinki Declaration was followed.

Statistical Methods

Parametric data are shown as mean ± standard deviation and non-parametric data are reported as median and interquartile range. Student t test for parametric data and Mann-Whitney U test for non-parametric data were used to compare continuous variables between the groups. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

All but 1 of the 15 normal subjects showed complete clearance of bolus during the esophageal and phrenic ampulla phases. The impedance line tracings from the distal esophagus and phrenic ampulla during bolus clearance revealed sharp upstroke or an increase in the impedance values close to the time of the onset of pressure or the contraction wave, at all locations in the distal esophagus, as well as phrenic ampulla (Fig. 3). The temporal relationship between pressure wave and impedance wave in the esophagus show that the onset (caudal end) of the bell-shaped pressure wave was aligned with the end (cranial end) of the impedance wave with a minimal overlap between the 2 waves (Fig. 3, top panels). In 1 normal subject, similar to the other patients described in this report, initial upstroke of the impedance line recording was followed by a drop in the impedance values and retrograde escape of the bolus into the distal esophagus during the period between the formation of CDP and return of LES pressure back to its baseline position. In patients with phrenic ampulla dysfunction, there was retrograde escape of bolus into the distal esophagus during the phrenic ampulla emptying phase, seen on both, the impedance line tracings as well as the purple color impedance topography (Fig. 5). Schematic of the pressure and impedance during the phrenic ampulla emptying phase in patients, unlike normal subjects revealed overlap of the pressure and impedance waveform and impedance wave extending cranial to the pressure waveform (Fig. 5 top panel).

The phrenic ampulla length was longer in patients than normal (2.8 ± 0.7 and 2.0 ± 0.9 cm, P = 0.017) (Fig. 6A). The phrenic ampulla emptying time was not significantly different (3.2 ± 1.0 seconds and 3.3 ± 1.0 seconds, P = 0.591) between the 2 groups (Fig. 6B). The high pressure zone length (cranio-caudal length) at the end of swallow was longer in patients than healthy subjects (median = 5 cm [interquartile range 4-6] and 4 cm [interquartile range 3-4], P = 0.029) (Fig. 6C). The LES pressure at the completion of swallow (at the end of phrenic ampulla phase), also known as LES after-contraction was lower in patients compared to healthy subjects (38.4 ± 19.1 mmHg and 68.8 ± 30.8 mmHg, P = 0.003) (Fig. 6D).

Schematic showing the relationship between the progression of pressure wave and impedance wave through the phrenic ampulla revealed 2 pressure peaks in both groups (Fig. 3 and 5). The amplitude of proximal pressure peak during the phrenic ampulla emptying phase in the patients was 37.0 ± 19.5 mmHg, significantly lower than the amplitude of pressure peak seen in the normal (63.0 ± 26.3 mmHg, P = 0.005) (Fig. 6E). In addition, there was a difference in the relationship between the amplitude of 2 pressure peaks between the normal and patients. In normal subjects, the proximal pressure peak was always higher than the distal one during entire phrenic ampulla emptying phase. On the other hand, in patients the distal pressure peak was higher than the proximal one, specifically at the moment of retrograde escape (57.7 ± 43.9 mmHg and 37.8 ± 17.0 mmHg, P = 0.038) (Fig. 6F).

In 12 patients, all 10 recorded swallows in each subject were associated with retrograde escape of bolus from the phrenic ampulla into the distal esophagus. There were 3 patients in whom retrograde bolus escape was seen with some but not all the swallows. The mean LES pressure (LES after-contraction) at the completion of swallows that were associated with the retrograde escape of bolus was lower compared to the swallows that were not associated with the retrograde escape (Patient 1, [55 mmHg vs 73 mmHg]; Patient 2, [21 mmHg vs 37 mmHg]; and Patient 3, [35 mmHg vs 51 mmHg]).

Discussion

In summary, we found that a group of patients with esophageal symptoms have abnormal phrenic ampulla emptying dysfunction. The type of symptom, ie, dysphagia, regurgitation or heartburn was not specific to the phrenic ampulla dysfunction. Our findings provide unique insight into the mechanism of phrenic ampulla emptying function and dysfunction in health and disease.

First of all, what is phrenic ampulla? For a long time, radiologist have observed that when visualized on X-ray barium swallow studies, the most distal part of the esophagus has a saccular rather than tubular appearance.6,11,12 Whether the saccular appearance is because it is a herniated stomach into chest, or a stretched and relaxed smooth muscle LES, or a combination of the above 2 has been debated.6 The CDP, felt to be the most distal most point of the esophagus, is an important landmark observed on the HRM studies.3 The velocity of esophageal peristalsis slows down at the CDP. The HRM correlation of X-ray-fluoroscopy observed phrenic ampulla is approximately a right-angle triangular region on the HRM recording, located between the CDP at its cranial end, crural diaphragm (CD) at the caudal end, and reconstituted LES following LES relaxation back to its baseline state (Fig. 4). The phrenic ampulla emptying, similar to the speed of peristalsis following CDP, is also slow. It is felt to be related to the re-elongation of the esophagus following its axial shortening during peristalsis, rather than the distally propagating circular muscle contraction of the esophagus. The re-elongation of the esophagus could be related to the relaxation of the spiral circular, and longitudinal muscles of the distal esophagus. We suspect that CDP is the distal edge of the LES, rather than the distal most point of the esophagus and that a reconstituted LES with high pressure (after-contraction) descends down during the phrenic ampulla emptying phase back to its baseline location in the hiatus (CD). The HRM recordings show that the CD location does not change significantly during the esophageal peristalsis and LES relaxation. A rhythmically contracting (inspiration) and relaxing (relaxing) CD provides resistance or no resistance to the bolus flow, respectively.13,14 The phrenic ampulla emptying into the stomach only occurs during a relaxed CD or the expiratory phase of respiratory cycle. Furthermore, it can only occur when the phrenic ampulla pressure is lower than that of the contracted LES. In normal subjects, at the end of swallow-induced LES relaxation, the LES pressure are higher than pre-swallow periods (LES after-contraction). We propose that an after contraction of the LES plays an important role in the phrenic ampullary emptying and a weak after-contraction, as observed in our patients, predisposes to impaired phrenic ampulla emptying.

The temporal relationship between an abnormally travelling pressure and impedance can be observed in the Manoview program schematic, as one moves in time through the esophageal and phrenic ampulla emptying phases. In normal subjects, the onset of contraction at each location in the esophagus (caudal point of the bell-shaped pressure waveform) is closely aligned with the tail end of impedance waveform (cranial point of impedance waveform), and the 2 travel sequentially during the esophageal and phrenic ampulla emptying phases in normal subjects.4 On the other hand, in patients with phrenic ampulla dysfunction one can see the impedance wave travelling in the reverse direction at the point of retrograde escape. The pressure profile at the point of retrograde escape reveals important finding that points to the mechanism of phrenic ampulla dysfunction. The axial pressure profile at each time point is made up of distally propagating esophageal contraction a descending after-contracted LES (during the phrenic ampulla phase), herniated stomach pressure and CD pressures.15 In normal subjects, one can see either one (during expiratory phase of respiratory cycle) or 2 (during the inspiratory phase of respiratory cycle) pressure peaks in the axial pressure profile during the phrenic ampulla stage. The proximal pressure peak is generally higher than the distal peak in normal subjects. On the other hand, in patients with phrenic ampulla dysfunction, the reverse is the case, ie, the distal pressure peak is higher than the proximal one at the point of retrograde escape. Our finding that the LES pressure at the end of swallow is lower in patients indicates a defective post-relaxation after-contraction of the LES, along with the greater herniated stomach and/or CD pressure in patients. The reason for the higher phrenic ampulla pressure in the patients is not evident from our study and requires further investigation.

Retrograde escape of bolus from the hiatus hernia into esophagus following complete clearance of bolus from the esophagus with peristalsis has been described previously.13 It is interesting that the axial pressure profile in that setting was similar to what we describe in our patients with phrenic ampulla dysfunction, ie, high phrenic ampulla pressure in this report. The difference between the phrenic ampulla and sliding hiatus hernia is that the former is transient and is only seen with swallow-induced peristalsis, while the latter is present in between the swallows. Recently, we also described hiatus hernia as a 2-stage pressure pump of the gastroesophageal reflux, which is relevant in the setting of reflux disease.16 The phrenic ampulla dysfunction that we describe in this paper is observed with primary peristalsis and one would expect it to be relevant for dysphagia symptom, which was present in 10 of the 15 patients in the current study. However, in the remainder, regurgitation and heartburn were the predominant symptoms.

Formation of the phrenic ampulla and reconstitution of LES following its relaxation is a complex event that involves axial shortening of the esophagus, LES, CD, proximal stomach, and phrenoesophageal ligament.9 Our understanding of the phrenic ampulla formation, emptying and dysfunction would require better understanding of the biomechanical and functional properties of all of the above structures. We believe that the phrenic ampulla holds an important key for better understanding of many esophageal motility disorders and esophageal symptoms. For example, the phrenic ampulla is absent in patients with achalasia esophagus. The sliding hiatus hernia is observed infrequently in patients with achalasia esophagus.17,18 We found that there is a tight anchoring between the LES and CD, and hiatal fibrosis in patients with achalasia esophagus.18,19 In this study we found that the height of phrenic ampulla is significantly higher in patients with phrenic ampulla dysfunction as compared to normal subjects. Patients with sleeve gastrectomy, where the fundus of the stomach is removed, are likely to have altered dynamics at play at the esophagogastric junction and in the formation of phrenic ampulla, which may be an important reason why many patients develop significant dysphagia following sleeve gastrectomy.20

There are many limitation of our study. (1) The pressure sensors on the manometry catheter are located 1 cm apart and impedance sensors 2 cm apart, which arguably limits the accuracy of our measurement to within those distances. Manoview software interpolates data in a seamless fashion between the actual recorded data points. Our measurements are based on the interpolated data and hence can be questioned. However, we analyzed all data in normal subjects and patients in an identical fashion and found differences between the 2 groups. (2) Whether patients with a defective after contraction of the LES represents a spectrum of patients with reflux disease with ineffective peristalsis of the distal esophagus is not clear from our study since we do not have Bravo pH and endoscopy data in all of our patients. (3) Whether CD dysfunction that results in high phrenic ampullary pressure indicates that these patients have some kind of pulmonary disorder is also not clear from our study. (4) Finally, our patient population is older and have a higher body mass index as compared to controls. Withstanding above limitation, the description of phrenic ampullary dysfunction that we report should encourage investigators to pay attention and examine phrenic ampullary dysfunction in more detail in patients with esophageal symptoms.
