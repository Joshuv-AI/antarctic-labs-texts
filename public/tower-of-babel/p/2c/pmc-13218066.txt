Moderating role of executive function in the relationship between specific white matter changes and long-term delayed recall in aMCI patients
Authors: Ling-Ling Li, Jie Ma, Jia-Jia Wu, Xin Xue, Yi-Yan Sun, Xu-Yun Hua, Qi-Hao Guo, Jian-Guang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdvances in connectomics and network neuroscience have opened new avenues for the in-depth study of complex brain connections and networks. The neuromodulation of connectivity-based circuits and networks is consistent with the underlying complex mechanisms of episodic memory processes involving multiple brain regions, offering significant potential for future therapeutic interventions. However, the relationships among brain changes at multiple levels (single region, fiber tract, and network), episodic memory, and other cognitive domains in patients with amnestic mild cognitive impairment (aMCI) remain unknown. The objectives of this study were (1) to elucidate the characteristics of gray matter microstructure (single region level), specific white matter (fiber tract level), and large-scale brain network (network level) changes in aMCI patients and (2) to explore the hypothesis that other cognitive domains can moderate the relationships between these brain changes at multiple levels and long-term delayed recall.MethodsA cross-sectional design was used, with neuropsychological assessments and multimodal MRI scans conducted on 30 aMCI patients and 34 healthy controls. Moderation analysis was performed to explore how other cognitive domains influence the relationships between brain changes and long-term delayed recall.ResultsaMCI patients presented significant brain changes at multiple levels (single region, fiber tract, network). Furthermore, moderation analysis based on cross-sectional data revealed that other cognitive domains, particularly executive function, moderate the relationship between specific white matter changes and long-term delayed recall in aMCI patients.ConclusionThis study provides important insights into the complex relationships between multilevel brain alterations and cognitive function in aMCI patients. In particular, the cross-sectional findings suggest that executive function moderates the relationship between specific white matter alterations and long-term delayed recall. These findings may inform future targeted interventions aimed at improving episodic memory and delaying the progression of aMCI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13195-026-02030-7.

Introduction

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is an intermediate stage between normal aging and dementia and is characterized by cognitive deficits, as reported by the patient and substantiated through objective assessments, whereas daily living abilities remain largely intact [1]. Amnestic mild cognitive impairment (aMCI), the most common subtype, is likely to progress to dementia, with an 80% probability of progressing within 6 years of diagnosis [2]. The memory process, involving encoding, storing, and retrieving information, requires the coordinated involvement of multiple brain regions [3]. For example, the hippocampus plays a crucial role in the encoding and initial storage phases of memory [4]. Over time, memories are transferred to the neocortex for long-term storage [5, 6]. Furthermore, the prefrontal cortex regulates and controls the entire memory process, ensuring the accurate retrieval and utilization of information [7]. White matter tracts play crucial roles because they connect different regions of the brain, providing essential information transmission pathways and enabling different brain regions to work together efficiently [8]. Studies have shown that the integrity of white matter tracts is essential for proper operation of memory processes [9, 10]. This intricate synergy renders the memory process highly dynamic and adaptable and capable of responding to diverse circumstances and demands. Episodic memory impairment constitutes the core symptom of aMCI, and long-delayed recall serves as a critical criterion for detecting episodic memory [11]. Through early detection and intervention of long-delayed recall, the progression of dementia can be delayed or prevented.

Current studies suggest that the loss of neurons, synaptic dysfunction, and pathological proteins, such as abnormal phosphorylation of tau protein and the aggregation of β-amyloid protein, are significant contributors to cognitive decline [12]. However, the role of specific white matter tracts in the aforementioned cognitive processes remains unclear. A recent study published in Science indicated that neuroimaging can noninvasively measure the connectivity structures and functions between brain regions in the living human brain, providing new insights and directions for research in humans [13]. Furthermore, Professor Fox proposed in the New England Journal of Medicine that dysfunction should be understood from the perspective of the entire functional network rather than solely focusing on the lesion, emphasizing the importance of mapping symptoms to brain networks [14]. This implies that the neuromodulation of connectivity-based circuits and networks aligns with the intricate mechanisms of episodic memory processes involving multiple brain regions, which may have significant potential in future therapeutic interventions.

Despite significant progress in aMCI research, various technical and methodological challenges remain. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) is the most commonly used diffusion model within diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) and serves as an effective, noninvasive method for observing and tracking white matter tracts in the brain [15]. DTI is based on the theory that water molecules diffuse freely in a Gaussian manner, which is inconsistent with the actual microenvironment within brain tissue [15]. For white matter, the primary limitation of DTI lies in its ability to resolve only a single fiber orientation within a given imaging voxel, making accurate representation of crossing fibers challenging. This limitation introduces challenges to the precise analysis and interpretation of white matter voxels because up to 90% of voxels in the adult brain comprise multiple distinct fiber populations [16]. It also suffers from low specificity in diffusion tensor metrics and an inability to detect the microstructure of gray matter. For example, a decrease in fractional anisotropy (FA) observed in the white matter tracts of aMCI patients can be attributed to various factors, including axonal diameter, density, and myelin content [17]. Overall, these limitations can result in potentially unreliable interpretations of DTI-related diffusion tensor metrics.

Recently, advancements in various neuroimaging and neurophysiological techniques have facilitated the construction and visualization of the human connectome and macroscopic brain networks [18]. Neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging (NODDI) categorizes the tissue microenvironment into three-compartment diffusion models (intracellular, extracellular, and cerebrospinal fluid), enabling more precise measurements of neurite density and orientation dispersion [19]. In particular, the neurite density index (NDI) reflects the density and number of synapses, whereas the orientation dispersion index (ODI) reflects the angular variation in neurites. The ODI indicates the degree of fiber dispersion in white matter and the complexity of the dendritic structures in gray matter [19]. To overcome the limitation that up to 90% of brain voxels contain crossing fibers [16], fixel-based analysis (FBA) utilizing high angular resolution diffusion imaging (HARDI) provides more specific diffusion tensor metrics, such as fiber density (FD), fiber cross-section (FC) and fiber density and cross-section (FDC) [20]. In particular, a decrease in FD indicates axonal loss, a decrease in FC indicates fiber tract atrophy, and a decrease in FDC indicates both axonal loss and fiber tract atrophy [20]. Moreover, conventional functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI)-based functional networks calculate the correlation coefficient of brain regions within the atlas, potentially resulting in redundant connections that do not correspond to actual neural circuits [21]. This study aimed to address this problem by constructing a functional network with genuine white matter structural connection constraints. In this multimodal MRI study, volumetric analysis based on structural MRI (sMRI) was used to assess cerebral atrophy as a potential confounder.

Numerous studies have demonstrated a robust correlation between various cognitive domains and long-term delayed recall [22, 23]. The objectives of this study were (1) to elucidate the characteristics of gray matter microstructure (single region level), specific white matter (fiber tract level), and large-scale brain network (network level) changes in aMCI patients and (2) to explore the hypothesis that other cognitive domains can moderate the relationships between these brain changes at multiple levels and long-term delayed recall. Overall, this study aimed to provide novel insights and potential strategies for clinical intervention. Furthermore, advanced brain imaging and mathematical modeling algorithms can be utilized in medical research to demonstrate that the memory process involves multiple brain areas, not merely a single brain region.

Materials and methods

Participants

An a priori sample size calculation was performed using G*Power 3.1 for the moderation analysis. Specifically, the “F tests: Linear multiple regression: Fixed model, R2 increase” option was used to estimate the sample size required to detect the additional variance explained by the interaction term [24]. The parameters were set as follows: significance level (α) = 0.05, statistical power (1 − β) = 0.80, an anticipated medium effect size (f2 = 0.15), one tested predictor (interaction term), and six total predictors (three covariates, one independent variable, one moderator, and one interaction term). The analysis indicated that a minimum total sample size of 55 participants was needed. For this cross-sectional study, we initially recruited 110 participants from the Memory Clinic of Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital and local communities in Shanghai between January 2020 and October 2023. Among them, 42 (38.18%) participants did not meet the eligibility criteria, 3 (2.73%) participants had incomplete neuropsychological testing, and 1 (0.91%) participant’s image exhibited severe artifacts, resulting in their exclusion. Finally, 30 (27.27%) aMCI patients and 34 (30.91%) healthy controls (HCs) matched for age, sex, and education were included. Approval for the study was obtained from the local ethics committee (NO. 2019-041), and all participants provided informed consent. The participant screening flowchart is shown in Fig. 2.

The inclusion criteria for the aMCI group were as follows: (1) met the Jak/Bondi’s diagnostic criteria (Supplemental Material 1) [25]; (2) had a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score > 24 points [26]; (3) were aged 55–80 years; (4) had objective memory impairment, Auditory Verbal Learning Test Long-Term Delay Recall (AVLT-N5) and Recognition (AVLT-N7) scores falling below 1.0 standard deviation (SD) from the age-corrected normative mean [27]; (5) had complex Instrumental Activity of Daily Living (IADL) ability that might have been slightly impaired while still maintaining independent daily living [27]; (6) had a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) memory score of 0.5 points [28] but did not meet the diagnostic criteria for dementia set by the National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer’s Association (NIA-AA) [29]; and (7) were right-handed.

The inclusion criteria for the HCs group were as follows: (1) lacked complaints of cognitive decline; (2) had neuropsychological testing results that did not meet Jak/Bondi’s diagnostic criteria [25]; (3) had a normal ability to perform activities of daily living; and (4) lacked a family history of dementia.

The exclusion criteria for all participants were as follows: (1) had a history of mental illness, such as delirium, mania, depression, or anxiety; (2) had contraindications for MRI examination; (3) had less than 6 years of education; (4) had severe aphasia and audio-visual impairment; (5) had severe medical diseases, such as cardiopulmonary insufficiency, liver, or renal insufficiency; and (6) had other diseases that caused cognitive impairment and white matter hyperintensity lesions, such as cerebrovascular disease, craniocerebral trauma, hydrocephalus, brain tumors, or intracranial infection.

Neuropsychological testing

All participants underwent comprehensive neuropsychological testing conducted by three senior neuropsychologists who were blinded to the clinical diagnosis. Comprehensive assessments of general cognitive function (MMSE, Montreal Cognitive Assessment-Basic (MoCA-B), Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination III (ACE-III)) [26, 30, 31] and individual cognitive domains (AVLT, Digit Span Test (DST), Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT), Shape Trails Test (STT), Animal Verbal Fluency Test (AFT), Boston Naming Test (BNT), Judgment of Line Orientation (JLO), Silhouette Test (ST)) [32–40] are provided in Supplementary Table 1. For each cognitive domain, the raw scores from all the neuropsychological tests were converted to z scores and averaged to generate a domain composite score, which was used in subsequent moderation analyses [41].

Data acquisition

A 3.0 Tesla Magnetom Prisma scanner (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) was used for MRI scanning, employing a head coil for scans ranging from the cranial vertex to the level of the foramen magnum. The MRI protocols included fMRI, diffusion MRI (dMRI) and sMRI. The participants were required to be free from recent colds and inflammation, maintain a quiet state for 30 min before the scan, and wear earplugs. During the scan, they were asked to lie quietly in the scanner, close their eyes without falling asleep, and try to keep their heads as still as possible. The detailed MRI protocols are provided in Supplemental Table 2.

Data preprocessing

All data preprocessing and processing were conducted by three senior neuroradiologists who were blinded to the clinical diagnosis. The standard pipeline of Statistical Parametric Mapping version 12 (SPM12, https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/) was used for resting-state fMRI data preprocessing. This data preprocessing pipeline included the exclusion of the first 10 volumes, slice timing correction, realignment, normalization, detrending, regression covariates, and filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz) [42]. Participants were excluded if their head movement exceeded 2.5 mm of displacement or 2.5° of rotation [42]. Similarly, the standard pipeline of MRtrix3 (https://www.mrtrix.org/) [43] was used for dMRI preprocessing. This standard pipeline included denoising, Gibbs ringing removal, motion correction, eddy-current correction, topup correction of susceptibility distortions, and bias field correction. Meticulous data quality checks were performed by three senior neuroradiologists during preprocessing and all subsequent processing steps to ensure the images’ integrity and precision. In addition, the dwi2tensor was used to calculate the diffusion tensor metric FA.

Gray matter-based spatial statistics (GBSS)

The pipelines for GBSS are illustrated in Fig. 3A. First, the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) fraction in each voxel was obtained directly using three-compartment diffusion models of accelerated microstructure imaging via convex optimization (AMICO, https://github.com/daducci/AMICO/) [44], whereas the white matter (WM) fraction was acquired by segmenting FA images using the Atropos segmentation tool in Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs, version 2.1.0) [45]. Consequently, the gray matter (GM) fraction for each voxel was calculated by subtracting the CSF fraction and WM fraction from one. The volume fractions of the three tissues were subsequently multiplied by their respective weights (WM = 2, GM = 1, CSF = 0) to generate a “pseudo T1-weighted” image, which was then used to create a pseudo T1 population template [46]. The NDI, ODI, and gray matter fraction images from individual diffusion spaces were nonlinearly warped to the population template using the deformation field generated during template construction. Next, the GM fraction images from the population template space were averaged to create a mean GM image, which was then skeletonized using the tbss_skeleton tool in the FMRIB software library (FSL, http://www.fmrib.ox.au.uk/fsl, version 6.0.1) [46]. Finally, the local maxima of the GM fraction for the NDI and ODI were projected onto the mean GM skeleton, and the GM skeleton was thresholded (GM fraction > 0.65 in > 70% of participants) [47].

FBA and tract-based analysis

The pipelines for FBA and tract-based analysis are illustrated in Fig. 3B. First, the MSMT-CSD model was used to calculate the individual response function and the average response function for dMRI48. The commands mrgrid and dwi2mask were used for upsampling and generating individual masks. The fiber orientation distribution (FOD) in each voxel was subsequently estimated, followed by global intensity normalization using mtnormalise [48, 49]. The population FOD template was generated using iterative symmetric diffeomorphic nonlinear registration [50], and the individual FOD template was mutually warped to the population FOD template. Next, twenty million probabilistic streamlines were generated throughout the individual brain space using second-order integration over fiber orientation distributions (iFOD2) and the anatomically constrained tractography (ACT) framework [51]. Spherical deconvolution informed filtering of tractograms (SIFT) was applied to filter out streamlines with poor model fit and improve the reproducibility of fixel-fixel connectome construction [52]. Descriptions of the diffusion tensor metrics (FD, FC, and FDC) are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

In addition to whole-brain FBA, the TractSeg pipeline was utilized to segment the population template into 72 tracts (Supplementary Table 4) [53]. TractSeg is a deep learning-based tool that uses pretrained models to automatically process high-resolution dMRI data and generate probability maps of various brain tracts, achieving high-precision segmentation without the need for manual labeling or region of interest (ROI) definition [53]. At the same time, diffusion tensor metrics (FD, FC, and FDC) were measured across 72 tracts.

Construction of a functional network with structural connections

The pipelines for the construction of the functional network with structural connections are illustrated in Fig. 3C. First, the average time series for each brain region in the automated anatomical labeling (AAL) atlas can be extracted. Subsequently, the Pearson correlation coefficient between two brain regions in the AAL atlas was computed, followed by Fisher’s Z transformation. Notably, this study focused on functional networks with structural connections to reduce redundancy and enhance robustness [27]. The structural connections, represented by streamline counts, were derived from whole-brain probabilistic fiber tracking of the FBA. In the absence of a structural connection between two brain regions, the corresponding position in the functional connectivity matrix was set to zero. Ultimately, weighted functional networks constrained by structural connections were constructed.

Network topological properties

The Graph Theoretical Network Analysis (GRETNA, http://www.nitrc.org/projects/gretna/) toolbox [54] was used to calculate the network topological properties of each participant’s functional network with structural connections. The network topological properties included the following: (1) global efficiency (Eg); (2) local efficiency (Eloc); (3) clustering coefficient (Cp); (4) characteristic path length (Lp); (5) small-world index including normalized characteristic path length (λ), normalized clustering coefficient (γ), and small-world property (σ); (6) assortativity; and (7) hierarchy. Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses of the clinical characteristics were conducted using SPSS version 27.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Intergroup comparisons for continuous variables were carried out through a two-sample t test, whereas categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test. A two-tailed P < 0.05 indicated that the difference between two groups was statistically significant.

GBSS, FBA, tract-based analysis, and network analysis were performed using general linear models with age, sex, and education as covariates. For GBSS, voxelwise statistical analysis based on the nonparametric random permutation test (n = 5000 permutations) [58] was performed on the GM skeletonized diffusion metric (NDI and ODI). The threshold-free cluster enhancement (TFCE) method was used to investigate intergroup differences while controlling for multiple comparison errors using family wise error (FWE) correction (P < 0.05, FWE corrected) [59]. For FBA, fixel-level statistical analysis based on the nonparametric random permutation test (n = 5000 permutations) [58] was performed on the WM diffusion metric (FD, FC and FDC). The connectivity-based fixel enhancement (CFE) method was used to investigate intergroup differences while controlling for multiple comparison errors using FWE correction (P < 0.05, FWE corrected) [60]. For tract-based analysis, FWE correction was applied to the 72 tracts. For network analysis, the sparsity of the functional network with structural connectivity was calculated (0.05:0.01:0.45). Network topological properties were calculated the area under the curve (AUC) across all sparsity thresholds for intergroup comparisons, demonstrating high robustness.

All differential results of the GBSS, FBA, tract-based analysis and network analysis were subjected to partial correlation analysis with neuropsychological testing while controlling for age, sex, and education as covariates.

The moderation effects of other cognitive domains on differential brain changes at multiple levels and long-term delayed recall were analyzed using linear regression and Hayes’ bootstrap method. Age, sex, and education were included as covariates to account for their potential confounding effects on brain structure and cognitive function. Episodic memory impairment, particularly long-delayed recall, was a core symptom of aMCI [11]; therefore, we conducted an exploratory moderation analysis using the AVLT-N5 scores. Furthermore, Hayes’ bootstrap method was utilized to generate 5,000 random samples for testing and to calculate the 95% confidence intervals (CI). Moderation analysis was performed using SPSS version 27.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and the significance of the mediation effect was assessed by determining whether the 95% CI excluded zero.

GBSS (brain changes at the single region level)

The NDI represents the density and number of synapses, whereas the ODI quantifies the angular variation in neurites, indicating the complexity of dendritic structures in gray matter. The whole-brain GBSS revealed that aMCI patients exhibited significantly lower NDI and ODI in the frontal lobe, temporal lobe and parietal lobe (P < 0.05, FWE corrected) (Fig. 4).

FBA and tract-based analysis (brain changes at the fiber tract level)

A decrease in FD indicates axonal loss, a decrease in FC indicates fiber tract atrophy, and a decrease in FDC indicates both axonal loss and fiber tract atrophy. The whole-brain FBA revealed that aMCI patients exhibited significantly lower FD, FC, and FDC than HCs (Fig. 5). In particular, aMCI patients exhibited significantly lower FD in the left superior longitudinal fasciculus, left inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus, left anterior thalamic radiation, and left inferior longitudinal fasciculus than HCs (P < 0.05, FWE corrected) (Fig. 5A). Additionally, they exhibited significantly lower FC in the right superior longitudinal fasciculus, right inferior longitudinal fasciculus, right thalamic radiation, and right inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (P < 0.05, FWE corrected) (Fig. 5B). Moreover, aMCI patients exhibited significantly lower FDC in the right inferior longitudinal fasciculus, thalamic radiation, left superior longitudinal fasciculus, and inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (P < 0.05, FWE corrected) (Fig. 5C).

Network analysis (brain changes at the network level)

Within the sparsity range of 0.05 to 0.45 (step = 0.01), both groups demonstrated small-world topological properties. Network analysis revealed that aMCI patients exhibited significantly lower hierarchy and σ and greater assortativity than HCs (ηp2 = 0.08, ηp2 = 0.08, ηp2 = 0.15, P < 0.05) (Fig. 6). However, there were no differences between the two groups in terms of Eg, Eloc, Cp, Lp, γ, or λ (P > 0.05) (Fig. 6).

Partial correlation analysis

Partial correlation revealed that FD of the left superior longitudinal fasciculus II, FD of the left superior longitudinal fasciculus III, FDC of the left superior longitudinal fasciculus III, FC of the right inferior longitudinal fasciculus, and FDC of the right inferior longitudinal fasciculus were closely related to episodic memory, executive function, and attention function (P < 0.05, FDR corrected) (Supplementary Fig. 2–3).

Moderation analysis at the single region, fiber tract, and network levels

Moderation analysis at the fiber tract level revealed that executive function significantly moderated the relationship between FD of the left superior longitudinal fasciculus II and AVLT-N5 scores (β = 0.275, P = 0.037) (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 7). However, the moderation analysis conducted at both the single-region and network levels did not reveal any statistically significant effects.

Discussion

This study elucidated the characteristics of gray matter microstructure(single region level), specific white matter (fiber tract level), and large-scale brain network (network level) changes in aMCI patients and their relationships with cognitive functions. Additionally, the study explored whether other cognitive domains moderate the relationship between these brain changes at multiple levels and long-term delayed recall. The results showed that aMCI patients experienced significant brain changes, which were strongly associated with cognitive function, particularly memory and executive functions. Executive function can also moderate the relationship between specific white matter changes and long-term delayed recall.

This study revealed significant reductions in the NDI and ODI in the frontal, temporal, and parietal lobes of patients with aMCI compared with HCs. These reductions in the NDI and ODI suggest a loss of dendritic and axonal integrity in these regions, which are critical for maintaining the neural networks involved in various cognitive functions, including memory and executive functions. The observed reductions in the NDI and ODI are consistent with the findings of previous meta-analyses that identified microstructural abnormalities in aMCI patients [10]. Zhang introduced NODDI in 2012, thereby extending the application of diffusion imaging from white matter to the microstructure of gray matter [19]. A reduction in the NDI indicates a decreased density of dendrites and axons, which could impair synaptic connectivity and neural communication. Previous studies have indicated that the deposition of pathological tau protein in the gray matter of white matter projections results in a decrease in axonal density [61]. Similarly, the ODI can be used to quantitatively estimate the dendritic distribution, thereby directly reflecting the complexity of gray matter regions and changes in tissue structure. A study by Boros suggested that neurodegenerative changes are associated with a decrease in dendritic branching and loss of dendritic spines in the gray matter of the brain, consequently resulting in a reduction in dendritic complexity [62]. Most studies have previously utilized the DTI ellipsoid model to examine cortical mean diffusivity (MD), revealing an increase in MD within cortical regions exhibiting macrostructural atrophy, including the medial and lateral temporal lobes, the superior medial frontal lobe, the supramarginal gyrus, the posterior cingulate gyrus, and the precuneus [63]. The temporal lobe, particularly the hippocampus, plays a crucial role in memory formation and retrieval [64]. Microstructural damage in this area may lead to ineffective storage and retrieval of memory information, resulting in impaired long-term delayed memory. Moreover, the basal temporal and medial frontal regions are among the initial areas where amyloid protein deposits form, subsequently affecting the associative neocortex, primary sensorimotor areas, and medial temporal lobe [65].

Compared with the DTI ellipsoid model, the FBA demonstrates greater specificity and accuracy in resolving crossing fibers [20]. FBA revealed significant reductions in FD, FC, and FDC in key white matter tracts, including the superior longitudinal fasciculus, inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus, anterior thalamic radiation, and inferior longitudinal fasciculus. In conjunction with tract-based analysis, the superior longitudinal fasciculus II and III exhibited significant axonal loss and fiber bundle atrophy, which are closely related to both memory function and executive function. These findings indicate that damage to white matter fiber tracts in aMCI patients not only disrupts the efficient transmission of neural signals but also contributes to significant deficits in multiple cognitive functions. In particular, the superior longitudinal fasciculus, which connects the frontal lobe with the parietal, occipital, and temporal lobes, is essential for the multimodal neural networks that establish the foundational cascades of memory, language and attention [66]. The superior longitudinal fasciculus II originates from the posterior part of the inferior parietal lobule (angular gyrus) and terminates in the dorsal part of the premotor and prefrontal areas, where it is involved primarily in working memory, spatial attention, and spatial information retrieval [67]. Research has shown that the anatomical lateralization of the superior longitudinal fasciculus II is linked to the processing speed and asymmetry of the right hemisphere during visuospatial processing tasks [68]. The traditional horizontal segment of the superior longitudinal fasciculus III originates from the anterior part of the inferior parietal lobule (supramarginal gyrus) and terminates in the ventral part of the premotor and prefrontal areas, where it is involved primarily in working memory and articulation regulation [67]. Notably, owing to the highly complex structure of the superior longitudinal fasciculus, there are discrepancies in its classification and definition among different studies [69]. A study utilizing diffusion spectrum imaging (DSI) and Q-ball residual bootstrap fiber tracking confirmed the connection of the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus between the frontal and occipital cortices, highlighting its role in semantic processing and semantic memory and demonstrating its linkage to regions implicated in semantic retrieval and cognitive control [70]. Anterior thalamic radiation is a crucial component of the cortico-thalamo-cortical circuit and plays a key role in the execution and planning of complex behaviors [71]. Additionally, damage to the anterior thalamic nuclei, similar to hippocampal damage, results in impaired spatial memory [72]. However, the anterior thalamic nuclei function independently of the hippocampus, contributing equally to memory and cooperating with the hippocampus and cortex to support episodic memory [72].

Currently, complex network analysis serves as an indispensable tool for delineating brain anatomy and functional connections. Network analysis revealed significant alterations in the topological properties of brain networks, specifically lower hierarchy and σ and greater assortativity, in aMCI patients than in HCs, indicating a shift toward less efficient and more locally clustered network organization. Many studies have shown that the human brain’s efficient network systems are characterized by higher levels of local aggregation (i.e., larger Cp) and fewer connections with “wiring costs” (i.e., shorter Lp) [73]. Small-world properties can be likened to metrics balancing “cost” and “benefit”, reflecting the balance between network functional integration and separation. Although both groups exhibited small-world properties, patients diagnosed with aMCI demonstrated a decrease in local information processing ability within the structural network and in specific brain regions involved in the overall integration and transmission of information. Consequently, these results no longer adhere to the “low-cost, high-benefit” model of information processing, which is consistent with the results of previous functional networks [74, 75]. Episodic memory, especially long-term delayed recall, relies not on the isolated function of a single brain region but on coordinated interactions among the hippocampus, frontal and parietal cortices, and their associated networks [76]. A reduced σ value may reflect an impaired balance between functional integration and segregation within the brain network, which could diminish the efficiency of distributed information integration and retrieval, thereby contributing to poorer performance in long-term delayed recall [77, 78]. The brain is a hierarchical system composed of highly interconnected, large, complex networks [79]. Functional integration refers to the ability of the brain to integrate specialized information from various brain regions, whereas functional segregation refers to the ability to specialize in information within multiple connected brain areas [80]. A fundamental characteristic of the human brain, which is crucial for generating complex cognitive processes, is its ability to dynamically and adaptively reconfigure its network structure in response to various environmental stimuli [81]. That is, the brain regulates information transfer by balancing functional integration and segregation, and various cognitive tasks may necessitate varying degrees of integration and segregation [82]. The observed reductions in hierarchy within the brain suggest a decline in the efficient integration of information across its regions. This decline in integrative capacity aligns with the observed impairment in interregional information integration during episodic memory tasks in patients with aMCI, as the formation and retrieval of episodic memory rely on the effective coordination of large-scale brain networks [83]. A higher assortativity indicates that nodes in the network are more likely to connect with other nodes possessing similar characteristics [84]. In particular, nodes with similar degrees formed distinct clusters, suggesting that the functional network may undergo reorganization, thereby increasing the segregation of the overall functional network. The formation of such localized and homogenized clusters may partially compensate for connectivity impairments in specific brain regions and preserve basic local information processing, but it also severely constrains long-range information exchange across distributed brain areas, including the hippocampus, prefrontal cortex, and parietal cortex. Moreover, the consolidation and retrieval of episodic memory, as well as the cognitive flexibility and task coordination associated with executive function, depend critically on this cross-regional synergy [83, 85]. However, other topological properties of the brain networks demonstrated no significant differences between groups, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies [86, 87]. Given the susceptibility-related distortions in the frontal and temporal regions inherent to EPI acquisitions, some residual distortions may remain in the fMRI data, potentially impacting the precision of functional connectivity estimates in these regions. Future research should acquire inverse phase-encoded images or field maps and perform specialized magnetic susceptibility distortion correction to increase data accuracy.

Moderation analysis revealed that executive function moderated the relationships between FD in the left superior longitudinal fasciculus II and long-term delayed recall. This phenomenon underscores the critical role of executive function in moderating memory function, particularly in cases of damage to specific white matter tracts. However, it is important to note that this moderating effect may also be influenced by other factors, such as disease severity, cognitive reserve, or residual confounders. Individuals diagnosed with aMCI frequently experience white matter tract degradation, which adversely impacts memory and other cognitive domains [10]. However, the enhancement of executive function appears to provide a buffering effect, reducing the negative impact of specific white matter fiber damage. These findings have important implications for the development of targeted interventions aimed at enhancing executive function to improve memory in aMCI patients. Notably, the moderation findings in this study, which were derived from a cross-sectional design, are exploratory and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Although the present study met the minimum sample size requirement for detecting a medium interaction effect, moderation analyses are often less sensitive to detecting small effects and typically require larger sample sizes to achieve adequate statistical power. Moreover, bootstrap resampling improves the precision of confidence interval estimation but does not eliminate the limitations associated with a relatively modest sample size. Therefore, future longitudinal studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to further confirm the robustness of the observed moderation effect and clarify its temporal dynamics.

Limitations

Despite the significant findings of this study, several limitations remain. The small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings and diminish the statistical power of the study. Future research should involve larger cohorts to validate and expand these findings. Longitudinal studies are necessary to establish the temporal relationship between brain changes at multiple levels and long-term delayed recall in aMCI patients and to determine causality. Additionally, despite controlling for confounding factors such as age, sex, and education, other unmeasured variables, such as white matter hyperintensities, microbleeds, cerebral atrophy and genetic predispositions, might influence both brain structure and cognitive function. Future research should include a broader range of covariates to better isolate the effects of aMCI on brain structure and cognitive function. Finally, this study was conducted at a single center with participants from a specific geographic region, potentially introducing selection bias. Multicenter studies with diverse populations are necessary to enhance the external validity of the findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides critical insights into the intricate relationships between brain changes at multiple levels and cognitive function in aMCI patients. Moreover, this study demonstrated that other cognitive domains, particularly executive function, can moderate the relationship between specific white matter changes and long-term delayed recall in aMCI patients. These findings have important implications for the development of targeted interventions to enhance cognitive function and delay aMCI progression. In addition, this study utilized advanced brain imaging and mathematical modeling algorithms in medical research and demonstrated that the memory process involves multiple brain areas, not merely a single brain region.
