Introduction

Primary cardiac tumours represent a very rare clinical entity. According to autopsy series, the estimated frequency of primary cardiac tumours ranges from 0.0017% to 0.33%.1,2 Even within this limited patient population, benign tumours account for approximately half of the cases, with myxomas constituting approximately three-quarters of benign tumours.1 Primary malignant cardiac tumours account for approximately one-tenth of all cardiac tumours, with a reported incidence ranging from 0.0017% to 0.07%.1,3

Intimal sarcoma, also known as spindle cell tumour or malignant fibrous histiocytoma, is an extremely rare mesenchymal malignancy that occurs more commonly in large vessels, such as the aorta or pulmonary trunk, rather than in the heart.4–8 These tumours are thought to originate from subendothelial multifunctional stem cells, which enable differentiation into various histological subtypes.9

Case presentation

A 74-year-old Caucasian woman was admitted to our institution with exertional dyspnoea and palpitations associated with short-term decompensated arterial hypertension. The patient denied any chest pain. Her medical history revealed an episode of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation nine months earlier with spontaneous conversion to sinus rhythm. Three years earlier, the patient experienced a stroke, confirmed by magnetic resonance imaging. The patient had a history of hypertension, well controlled with long-term therapy, hyperlipidaemia, and chronic venous insufficiency of the lower limbs.

Upon admission to the hospital, the patient was normotensive (120/85 mmHg). The electrocardiogram documented atrial fibrillation with a controlled ventricular response of approximately 90 b.p.m. Transthoracic and transoesophageal echocardiography (TTE and TEE) revealed a tumour in the left atrium measuring 4.5 × 5 cm, predominantly occupying the left atrial outflow tract (Figure 1). The tumour partially obstructed the mitral orifice with a mean transvalvular gradient of 6 mmHg. Tumour lobes partially extended into the left atrial appendage (LAA) and the left superior pulmonary vein (LSPV) (see Supplementary material online, Videos S1 and S2). Left ventricular ejection fraction was preserved (60%), and no pericardial effusion was detected. Coronary angiography showed normal coronary arteries without stenosis. Although neovascularization was not described in the original angiographic report, retrospective review of the angiographic images revealed contrast enhancement consistent with neovascularization in the left atrial region during left coronary artery injection (Figure 2). Computed tomography further documented a lobulated hypodense mass within the dilated left atrium, with subtle post-contrast vascularization (Figure 2). On computed tomography, the mass protruded into the mitral orifice and partially obstructed the pulmonary veins as well as the left atrial appendage. The mass was considered most consistent with a myxoma; however, thrombus and sarcoma could not be excluded. Despite the absence of a clear suspicion of malignancy, the therapeutic strategy remained unchanged, and surgical resection was indicated regardless of the final diagnosis.

Cardiac surgery was performed with a median sternotomy. Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) was established, and the left atrium (LA) was opened. A lobulated tumour with multiple haemorrhagic areas was identified in the atrium. The tumour originated from and infiltrated the posterior wall of the left atrium and protruded through the mitral annulus. A large portion of the tumour was excised, leaving the component infiltrating the posterior atrial wall (Figure 3). Due to tumour invasion, the MAZE procedure was abandoned; the atrium was sutured, and CPB was terminated. Intraoperative TEE showed a residual tumour measuring 1.9 × 2.5 cm, located in the posterior wall in the region of the coumadin ridge. The tumour partially extended into the LSPV, where accelerated pulsed-wave Doppler flow was recorded (PW S wave: 1.13 m/s, D wave: 1.48 m/s) (Figure 4). In combination with colour Doppler turbulence, these findings indicated mild pulmonary vein stenosis. Only trivial mitral regurgitation was noted.

Histological examination documented the presence of high-grade (Grade 3) intimal sarcoma with rhabdomyosarcomatous differentiation. Marked cytological atypia and a high mitotic rate (25 per 10 high-power fields) were observed. Immunohistochemical analysis showed negativity for SMA, h-caldesmon, CD34, CD117, and S100, with positivity for desmin, EMA, AE1/AE3, MDM2, CDK4, and p16, borderline p53 expression, and a Ki-67 proliferation index of 75% (Figure 5). Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) analysis demonstrated extensive diffuse amplification of the MDM2 gene, which represents a characteristic molecular finding in intimal sarcoma. The post-operative course was uneventful with respect to surgical complications but included a prolonged stay in the intensive care unit due to the requirement for inotropic support.

Discussion

Cardiac tumours may remain asymptomatic for prolonged periods, often resulting in delayed diagnosis. In advanced stages, this silent course may progress to the classic triad of symptoms, including obstructive, embolic, and systemic manifestations.10 The dominant clinical symptom, as observed in the present case, is dyspnoea. Tumour infiltration into the walls of the cardiac chambers may also lead to arrhythmic manifestations.11 In the presented case, atrial fibrillation was observed; however, without direct evidence of tumour-related causality.

Imaging modalities used in the diagnosis of cardiac tumours include echocardiography, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging.12,13 The identification of a mass within a cardiac chamber necessitates a differential diagnosis including benign tumours, malignant tumours, thrombus, vegetations, metastases from tumours at other locations, or connective tissue diseases.10 In the diagnostic process of cardiac tumours, the sensitivity of TTE reaches approximately 93%, while that of TEE reaches up to 97%.3 Heterogeneous echogenicity on TTE or TEE may indicate areas of haemorrhage.14 Computed tomography enables more accurate tumour staging and improved visualization of calcifications, fat content, and especially vascularity.10 In the presented case report, computed tomography confirmed the presence of a tumour, but the most probable diagnosis based on location was a myxoma. The method of choice for differential diagnosis of cardiac tumours is 18F-FDG positron emission computed tomography (PET/CT). This modality infers the presence of a malignancy based on increased FDG uptake, with intimal sarcoma typically demonstrating high FDG avidity.1,12,13 Increased FDG uptake reflects elevated metabolic activity, a characteristic feature of malignant tumours.7 Features indicating a higher risk of malignancy include a tumour larger than 50 mm, the involvement of more than one heart chamber, haemorrhagic pericardial effusion, exophytic masses with a broad base extending from the cardiac chamber wall, and tumour progression through the cardiac chamber wall into the pericardial space.1,15 In the presented case, only a broad tumour base and partially lobulated extension into the LAA and the LSPV were confirmed among these features. Non-left-sided localization, tumour heterogeneity, first-pass perfusion, and heterogeneous enhancement are also independent factors in predicting the malignancy of cardiac tumourous processes.14 Tumour localization on the posterior or lateral wall of the left atrium additionally increases the likelihood of the presence of highly malignant mesenchymal cardiac neoplasia, which is consistent with the findings observed in the present case.11

Patient’s perspective

The prognosis following incomplete resection is poor. Oncological consultation recommended adjuvant chemotherapy administered on an outpatient basis with a combination of anthracyclines and ifosfamide, which the patient agreed to undergo. The patient was discharged in stable condition to outpatient follow-up. At discharge, anticoagulation therapy consisted of low-molecular-weight heparin, with a planned transition to rivaroxaban for atrial fibrillation. At the time of manuscript preparation, 8 months after surgery, the patient exhibits no haemodynamically significant intracardiac obstruction.
