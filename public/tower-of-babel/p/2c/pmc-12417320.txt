Isolated Stiffness as a Predominant Manifestation of Wilson Disease: A Case Report
Authors: Ahmed Abdulhussain Shahatta, Ali Azeez Shlaka, Sajjad Ghanim Al‐Badri, Ali Saad Al‐Shammari, Khadija Naeem, Ali Saif Ali, Hussein Rizgar, Nabeel Al‐Fatlawi, Muna Alogaili, Sabry Babiker H Sayed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEarly recognition of atypical Wilson disease (WD) presentations, such as isolated stiffness, is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and facilitate timely intervention. A comprehensive evaluation, including advanced imaging and biochemical analysis, can help differentiate WD in cases where common neurological symptoms are absent, thus preventing progression to severe neurological impairment.Keywords: case report, copper storage disease, hepatolenticular degeneration, neurodegenerative diseases

Introduction

Wilson disease (WD) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in the ATP7B gene, leading to impaired copper transport and accumulation in various organs, primarily the liver and brain [1]. This toxic buildup results in hepatic and neuropsychiatric symptoms, with patients often presenting with liver dysfunction, movement disorders, and psychiatric changes [2]. Neurologic manifestations are seen in about 40%–50% of cases and typically involve movement disorders such as tremors, dystonia, and dysarthria, often manifesting during adolescence or early adulthood [3].

However, the presentation of WD can vary significantly, with certain neurological signs appearing atypically. Isolated stiffness, especially without other common symptoms like tremors or dysarthria, is extremely rare, posing a diagnostic challenge and often delaying clinical evaluation [4]. This report presents an unusual case of isolated stiffness as the predominant manifestation of WD in a young patient, underscoring the importance of recognizing atypical presentations for timely diagnosis and intervention.

Case History/Examination

The 12‐year‐old boy with no significant past medical or surgical history presented with progressively worsening stiffness over 6 months, initially affecting his lower limbs and eventually leading to generalized rigidity, double sphincter incontinence, and severe jaw spasms. He had been assessed multiple times by different physicians but remained without a definitive diagnosis and was prescribed supplements. His vaccination records lacked complete documentation of age‐appropriate immunizations, and his family history was unremarkable. The patient was initially evaluated by an internal medicine physician, who referred him for an outpatient neurology assessment. Following further evaluation, neurologists contacted our team, leading to his hospital admission. This process spanned approximately 1 month. Prior to this, he had undergone multiple evaluations without a clear diagnosis. Neurological examination revealed marked hyperreflexia without sensory deficits or nystagmus, while MRI findings demonstrated bilateral symmetric T2 and FLAIR hyperintensities in the basal ganglia, midbrain, and dorsal pons, confirming neuro‐Wilson disease. Liver function tests (LFTs) were assessed twice and were completely normal, which is an atypical finding in Wilson's disease, where hepatic involvement is commonly observed. Treatment was initiated with d‐penicillamine (starting at 250 mg/day and titrated to 500 mg/day) for copper chelation, alongside zinc acetate (50 mg/day) to inhibit further copper absorption. Symptomatic management included baclofen (10 mg TID) for muscle spasticity, clonazepam (0.5 mg BID) for jaw spasms, and physical therapy to maintain joint flexibility and mobility, while a low‐copper diet was implemented to prevent further accumulation. Regular 24‐h urinary copper excretion tests, serum ceruloplasmin levels, and liver function tests were conducted to monitor treatment efficacy, while neurological evaluations were performed at 3‐month intervals. Given the absence of liver dysfunction, the decision to assess serum ceruloplasmin and urinary copper excretion was prompted by the presence of unexplained progressive dystonia, jaw spasms, and characteristic MRI findings. At the 3‐month follow‐up, the patient demonstrated partial improvement in lower limb stiffness, reduced jaw spasms, and increased urinary copper excretion, though upper limb rigidity persisted, and fecal incontinence remained. By the 6‐month follow‐up, further improvements were noted, including enhanced fine motor function, partial self‐feeding ability, improved bladder control, and stable liver function tests, though residual gait instability and upper limb stiffness necessitated ongoing rehabilitation. Despite normal LFTs, the diagnosis was reinforced by the biochemical markers and neuroimaging findings characteristic of Wilson's disease. Given the progressive nature of neurological WD, a long‐term multidisciplinary care plan emphasizing continuous chelation therapy, dietary modifications, physical therapy, and routine follow‐ups was established to prevent further deterioration and optimize functional recovery. This case underscores the importance of early diagnosis and targeted intervention in atypical WD presentations, demonstrating that timely treatment can lead to significant neurological improvement, though sustained management remains crucial for long‐term outcomes.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment)

The differential diagnosis considered Stiff Person Syndrome, Isaac Syndrome, and tetanus, given the progressive stiffness, motor limitations, and emerging neurological signs. Tetanus was deemed less likely due to the prolonged symptom duration of 6 months, which is inconsistent with its typical acute presentation. On physical examination, the patient exhibited generalized body stiffness with marked hyperreflexia in all limbs, while sensory function remained intact and nystagmus was absent. These findings helped refine the differential diagnosis by excluding other metabolic or neurodegenerative conditions commonly associated with sensory deficits or nystagmus. Laboratory investigations were pivotal in identifying the underlying condition. The patient exhibited significantly low serum ceruloplasmin levels, measured at < 3.0 mg/dL (normal range for males: 15–30 mg/dL).

Additionally, his 24‐h urinary copper excretion was markedly elevated at 129.2 μg (with a urine volume of 3400 mL), exceeding the normal reference range of 5–25 μg/24 h. The decision to evaluate ceruloplasmin and urinary copper excretion was prompted by the unexplained progressive dystonia, rigidity, and jaw spasms in the absence of sensory deficits, along with characteristic MRI findings suggestive of basal ganglia involvement. These findings are characteristic of WD, with low ceruloplasmin and elevated urinary copper levels being hallmark indicators of this disorder. Further confirmation was provided by a slit‐lamp examination, which revealed the presence of Kayser‐Fleischer (KF) rings—an ophthalmologic feature nearly pathognomonic for WD. Imaging played a critical role in assessing the neurological impact. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain displayed bilateral hyperintensities in the thalami, putamen, midbrain, caudate, and globus pallidus (Figures 1 and 2). These findings are indicative of acute neuro‐Wilson disease, differing from the chronic phase that often presents with the ‘face of the giant panda’ sign. The observed hyperintensities underscored the neurological impact of copper accumulation in motor‐related brain regions, correlating with the patient's progressive motor symptoms. An MRI of the spine was not ordered, as the clinical presentation primarily suggested a central nervous system etiology without features warranting spinal pathology evaluation.

Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Based on the clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and MRI imaging, a diagnosis of neuro‐Wilson disease was confirmed. The presence of KF rings and specific MRI findings, along with copper metabolism abnormalities, supported the diagnosis. The patient's outcome and long‐term follow‐up would likely focus on copper chelation therapy to prevent further neurological decline and improve quality of life.

Discussion

WD is an autosomal recessive disorder causing abnormal copper accumulation, primarily in the liver and central nervous system. Neurological symptoms occur in 40%–50% of cases, often including tremors, dystonia, dysarthria, and psychiatric disturbances [5]. While commonly hepatic, WD can present with varied neurological features [6]. Some cases show atypical symptoms like progressive stiffness and spasticity without tremor or dystonia, emphasizing its clinical variability. Rarely, isolated spasticity complicates diagnosis, necessitating careful evaluation in atypical cases [7]. Spastic paraparesis, characterized by lower limb stiffness and weakness, can arise from genetic disorders and vitamin deficiencies, complicating neuro‐WD diagnosis [8]. WD's neurological damage results from copper deposition, primarily in the basal ganglia, but also affects the thalamus, brainstem, and corticospinal tracts. The predominant stiffness in our case suggests corticospinal and basal ganglia involvement in muscle tone regulation. Copper toxicity induces oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuronal injury.

Disrupted GABAergic inhibition in the basal ganglia may exacerbate hypertonia, explaining why some neuro‐WD cases present with rigidity and paraparesis instead of typical movement disorders. Our patient developed progressive lower limb stiffness, later involving the upper limbs, severe jaw spasms, and double sphincter incontinence, posing a diagnostic challenge. Predominant stiffness in neuro‐WD is rare, especially without other movement disorders. This highlights the need for clinical suspicion in unexplained progressive stiffness when standard assessments are inconclusive [9]. Although WD is known for hepatic and neurological symptoms, isolated spasticity is extremely uncommon. Such atypical presentations complicate diagnosis, often mimicking hereditary spastic paraplegias or multiple sclerosis. This case emphasizes the broad clinical spectrum of neuro‐WD and the importance of considering it in progressive spasticity among young adults.

Early diagnosis is vital to prevent irreversible damage. Ali and Ahmad reported WD with predominant neurological symptoms, particularly dystonia, without hepatic involvement. Their findings—basal ganglia hyperintensities, low ceruloplasmin, and elevated urinary copper—highlight neuro‐WD's diagnostic variability [10]. Patel et al. and others expanded its clinical and radiological spectrum, documenting classic MRI features like basal ganglia, thalamic, and brainstem hyperintensities, along with “face of the giant panda” and “bright claustrum” signs. Patel et al. also noted atypical findings, including middle cerebellar peduncle involvement and extensive white matter changes in severe cases [11]. Our case uniquely presents isolated progressive stiffness and spasticity, further broadening WD's movement disorder spectrum. Our patient exhibited basal ganglia, thalamic, and brainstem hyperintensities without typical movement disorders, highlighting WD's heterogeneity. These findings emphasize the need for clinical suspicion in unexplained neurological symptoms, even without hepatic dysfunction. MRI showed bilateral hyperintensities, indicating acute copper accumulation and a high risk of deterioration [12]. Low serum ceruloplasmin and high urinary copper excretion confirmed WD and guided treatment. These markers reflect copper overload severity and treatment effectiveness [13], with elevated urinary copper aiding diagnosis despite the atypical presentation. Due to its rarity and similarity to other spastic disorders, WD with predominant stiffness is often diagnosed late. MRI findings, including bilateral basal ganglia, thalamic, and brainstem hyperintensities, help detect copper‐related damage. In our case, these findings, along with low ceruloplasmin and high urinary copper, enabled early diagnosis and intervention. While copper chelation may stabilize or improve symptoms, severe neurological cases often have prolonged or incomplete recovery. Close monitoring and long‐term follow‐up are crucial for optimal outcomes. A reported case of a 20‐year‐old woman with progressive motor decline highlights WD's diverse manifestations and the importance of early diagnosis [14]. Neuro‐WD variability stems from acute copper accumulation, disrupting motor pathways and causing oxidative stress, demyelination, and neurological dysfunction, sometimes presenting as isolated stiffness [15].

This case expands WD's spectrum and underscores vigilance in unexplained progressive stiffness. MRI findings, along with biochemical and ophthalmologic markers, aid early diagnosis. By highlighting WD's inclusion in spastic paraparesis differentials, especially in young adults, it reinforces the need for thorough evaluation in atypical cases.

Conclusion

This case of WD presented with isolated progressive stiffness, lacking typical movement disorders, which led to diagnostic challenges and delayed intervention. It underscores the importance of considering WD in patients with unexplained stiffness and highlights the role of biochemical markers and neuroimaging in achieving early and accurate diagnosis to prevent irreversible neurological damage.
