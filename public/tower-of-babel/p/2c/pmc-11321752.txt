Incorporating Patient Values in Large Language Model Recommendations for Surrogate and Proxy Decisions
Authors: Victoria J. Nolan, Jeremy A. Balch, Naveen P. Baskaran, Benjamin Shickel, Philip A. Efron, Gilbert R. Upchurch, Azra Bihorac, Christopher J. Tignanelli, Ray E. Moseley, Tyler J. Loftus
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Surrogates, proxies, and clinicians making shared treatment decisions for patients who have lost decision-making capacity often fail to honor patients’ wishes, due to stress, time pressures, misunderstanding patient values, and projecting personal biases. Advance directives intend to align care with patient values but are limited by low completion rates and application to only a subset of medical decisions. Here, we investigate the potential of large language models (LLMs) to incorporate patient values in supporting critical care clinical decision-making for incapacitated patients in a proof-of-concept study.Methods:We simulated text-based scenarios for 50 decisionally incapacitated patients for whom a medical condition required imminent clinical decisions regarding specific interventions. For each patient, we also simulated five unique value profiles captured using alternative formats: numeric ranking questionnaires, text-based questionnaires, and free-text narratives. We used pre-trained generative LLMs for two tasks: 1) text extraction of the treatments under consideration and 2) prompt-based question-answering to generate a recommendation in response to the scenario information, extracted treatment, and patient value profiles. Model outputs were compared with adjudications by three domain experts who independently evaluated each scenario and decision.Results and Conclusions:Automated extractions of the treatment in question were accurate for 88% (n = 44/50) of scenarios. LLM treatment recommendations received an average Likert score by the adjudicators of 3.92 of 5.00 (five being best) across all patients for being medically plausible and reasonable treatment recommendations, and 3.58 of 5.00 for reflecting the documented values of the patient. Scores were highest when patient values were captured as short, unstructured, and free-text narratives based on simulated patient profiles. This proof-of-concept study demonstrates the potential for LLMs to function as support tools for surrogates, proxies, and clinicians aiming to honor the wishes and values of decisionally incapacitated patients.

KEY POINTS

Question: This proof-of-concept study aimed to explore the potential of large language models (LLMs) in clinical decision-making that incorporates the values of decisionally incapacitated patients in generating recommendations for imminent treatment decisions.

Findings: Pre-trained LLM were applied to simulated patient clinical notes and value profiles to generate treatment recommendations. Adjudication by domain experts found these decisions were generally plausible, reasonable, and matched the patients’ values, especially when those values were encoded as short, unstructured, and free-text narratives.

Meaning: LLMs have potential in assisting surrogates, proxies, and clinicians in honoring the wishes and values of decisionally incapacitated patients.

Approximately 90% of ICU patients lose decision-making capacity (1), resulting in medically, ethically, and emotionally complex situations where treatment decisions need to be made. Such decisions are ideally made by patient-designated surrogates but are often made by proxies according to a next-of-kin hierarchy, which varies by state, and defaults to an appointed proxy for patients who lack next-of-kin or another surrogate or proxy. Each of these types of decision-makers (henceforth referred to as proxies) should attempt to make decisions that align with patients’ wishes and values (2, 3). An advance directive (AD, also known as an advance care plan) is a written and witnessed statement describing patients’ wishes regarding substantive treatment preferences and appointment of a proxy (4). State law normally addresses evolution of patients’ preferences via legal rules about when later directives invalidate a prior one. Additional legal provisions may enable patients, healthcare facilities, physicians, and other interested parties to invalidate or supersede an AD, sometimes expedited judicial intervention. However, the prevalence of such plans is low, with an estimated two-thirds of the adult population never completing an AD (5). These rates are even lower among minorities and those with low health literacy (6, 7). When ADs are available, proxies and clinicians together must often interpret and implement this information while one or both parties are under conditions of time constraints, uncertain prognostication, and heightened stress and emotion (8, 9). ADs also vary greatly in format, with current mediums criticized for lacking key information that applies broadly to decision-making tasks and general guidance about patients’ true wishes (8, 10). Thus, it is not surprising that ADs are variably honored (4, 11), and ensuing treatment decisions are substantially impacted by emotional stress or personal bias (2, 12, 13).

Large language models (LLMs) trained on large bodies of clinical and nonclinical text have shown potential to learn patterns in clinical data for prediction, interpretation, and decision-making (14). LLM deployment in critical care decision-making frameworks could provide support for proxies and clinicians by making recommendations to align care with patient preferences (15–17). However, the complex, ethically sensitive nature of such technology regarding ADs and end-of-life treatment has limited the practical application of such technology thus far (15, 18, 19). In addition, few studies have attempted to produce treatment decisions for patients who have lost decision-making capacity (henceforth referred to as “incapacitated”) using artificial intelligence (AI) methods or to evaluate their performance relative to decisions made by healthcare professionals (16, 19). Although there has been some work to develop algorithms to predict patient preferences using population-level data (13) and individualized patient preference predictors (PPPs) have been discussed (17), the authors are unaware of any working examples of AI models that incorporate learned patient values in proxy decision-making tasks (20).

Our objective is to use simulated patient data to explore the potential that pre-trained LLMs may possess for supporting proxies and clinicians in making treatment decisions for incapacitated patients. We simulate patient clinical scenarios and value profiles to establish whether LLM can generate medically plausible and reasonable treatment recommendations (e.g., whether to initiate life-prolonging, curative treatment or to commence with palliative measures without curative intent) that reflect patients’ values. We also compare alternative formats for capturing patients’ wishes and values, which can be input to a clinical decision-making framework to produce the most appropriate treatment recommendation for each patient and scenario.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Scenario and Value Profile Generation

We generated 50 text scenarios representing simulated situations in which a treatment decision is being considered for an incapacitated patient (Supplementary Methods for a full description and scenario example, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B378), including many scenarios in which decisional incapacity was unexpected. The treatment decision was designed to generally have two outcomes: whether there should be escalation of care with curative or life-prolonging intent (e.g., surgery, parenteral nutrition, or chemotherapy) or whether to withdraw life-sustaining treatments and establish hospice care. We tested three methods of simulating patient value profiles: briefly, our first method involved simulating numeric answers to questions from commonly used value profile ranking questionnaires and then concatenating the answers and questions into a text paragraph. Our second method was similar, but we binned the numeric answers and assigned the scores to either “low,” “medium,” or “high” text values. Our final method was to simulate three alternate free-text narratives for each patient with alternate values: family and longevity of life, independence, career, and finances, or adventure, activeness, and being pain free (Table 1 and Supplementary Methods for more information http://links.lww.com/CCX/B378). Thus, for each patient, we had three value profile methods to test: question-answering (QA) ranking profiles, binned ranking profiles, and three alternative free-text profiles (Fig. 1).

Overview of the data processing and modeling framework. Three alternative methods for capturing value profiles were tested for each of the 50 simulated patient scenarios. The treatment decision being considered was extracted from text describing the clinical scenario using a pre-trained large language model. Next, all information was concatenated into a question format and fed into a deep learning text completion model to produce a final treatment recommendation. Q&A = question and answer.

Model Fitting

We used a deep learning modeling approach to generate treatment recommendations for each of the 50 scenarios (Fig. 1). We first used a pre-trained Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformer (BERT) QA model to extract the specific treatment being considered from the text. This involved first encoding each patient scenario using the “bert-large-uncased-whole-word-masking-finetuned-squad” tokenizer (21) from Hugging Face in Python, using a binary mask for the BERT (CLS) and (SEP) tokens to mark the start of the sequence and sentence separators. We then used the same BERT QA model to produce a single answer to the question: “What treatment is being considered by the doctors?” Our second step used OpenAI’s Application Programming Interface (API) through the “openai.Completion.create” function in Python to generate a treatment recommendation in response to a single text input prompt. This combined the extracted treatment under question from step one, with the original scenario text and generated value profile (Supplementary Methods for the prompt structure, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B378). The API provides an interface to some of OpenAI’s top-performing generative pre-trained transformers models. We chose to use the “text-davinci-002” engine, part of the GPT-3.5 series, as we found that later versions of these models such as “text-davinci-003” were less useful for our purpose because of the increasing levels of safe-guarding in response to medical questioning. We set the maximum number of output tokens at 300 and used a lower temperature value of 0.2 to produce less random and more precise outputs (higher temperature values are related to more creative and random tasks), and a lower top_p (nuclear sampling parameter) value of 0.3 to obtain outputs that are more focused and predictable.

Clinical Decision Adjudication

Accuracy of the BERT treatment extraction from each scenario was assessed by comparing the model’s extraction to the original text, using a binary system of either correct (1) or incorrect (0). Answers were considered correct if the general concept of the treatment was retained, for example, the extracted treatment “surgical” from “surgical intervention” was scored as correct. For each scenario where the treatment was extracted correctly, the model treatment recommendations were reviewed and scored for each value profile method by three separate board-certified adjudicators with extensive experience in clinical decision-making (one bioethicist, one internist, and one surgeon and intensivist). Scoring was performed using the Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, and 5 = strongly agree) (22). Each treatment recommendation was evaluated twice: once on the extent of agreement between recommended treatment and patient values and once on the extent to which the recommended treatment represents a medically plausible and reasonable decision. We also tested an alternate scoring system of binned Likert scores: a Likert score of 1 or 2 = low, a score of 3 = medium, and scores of 4 or 5 = high. Adjudicator scores were averaged across patient and value profile method. Inter-rater reliability between adjudicators was assessed using Gwet’s agreement coefficients (23) and Cohen’s Kappa (24) (Supplementary Methods for more information, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B378). None of the authors who generated the original scenarios were involved in the adjudication processes. All adjudicators were blinded to judgments made by other adjudicators.

RESULTS

Treatment Extraction

Of the 50 simulated patient scenarios, the model automatically extracted the correct treatment for 44 (88%). Among the six incorrect extractions, two resulted from no text being extracted at all, and four failed to select the correct parts of the text relating to the treatment decision being considered. On review of those six text scenarios (Table S1, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B394), at least three contained syntactic or semantic ambiguity regarding the clinical decision (i.e., the scenarios themselves did not contain a specific treatment option), which would likely remain a challenge under real-world conditions. Following this evaluation, 220 of the original 250 treatment decisions generated by the model were considered for adjudication (44 scenarios × five value profiles).

Adjudication of Model Treatment Recommendations

The average agreement across the three adjudicators that the recommended treatments aligned with patient values was 3.54 of 5 (± 0.95 sd), and the average agreement that recommended treatments represented a medically plausible and reasonable decision was 3.92 (±0.78 sd), based on the Likert scoring system of 1 (low agreement) to 5 (high agreement). The binned Likert scores (low, medium, and high agreement) showed similar levels of agreement: the average agreement that recommended treatments aligned with patient values was 2.48 of 3 (±0.75 sd); average agreement that recommended treatments represented a sound and sensible decision was 2.75 of 3 (±0.57 sd).

The three value profile recording methods (survey question ranking profiles, binned ranking profiles, and free-text profiles) resulted in scores that were relatively similar for matching patient values and for leading to plausible and reasonable recommendations. Overall, the highest scored method for the treatment matching the patient values was for free-text narratives, which produced the highest mean scores when using both the Likert and binned scoring systems (Figs. 2A and 3A). The free-text narrative-informed models were also scored the highest for producing a medically plausible and reasonable treatment decision when using the original ranked Likert scoring system (Fig. 2B). The only exception was when using the binned Likert scores to assess the plausibility and reasonableness of the treatment decision, in which Method 2 (the binned ranking profiles) achieved the highest mean score (Fig. 3B). However, it should be noted that in all cases, sds for all methods were relatively high and overlapping.

DISCUSSION

LLMs and other AI tools show promise for assisting with clinical decision-making for patients who have lost decisional capacity. In this proof-of-concept study, the use of clinical text extraction combined with prompt-style QA LLMs resulted in the generation of sensible, coherent treatment recommendations for most simulated patient scenarios and achieved alignment with many iterations of patients’ values. We also found that recording patient values as free-text unstructured narratives produced the highest scoring values of agreement between suggested treatments and patients’ wishes as assessed by the adjudicators. Assessment of model performance was compromised by relatively low inter-rater reliability, which also serves as a rationale for AI-enabled decision support to mitigate the untoward effects of variability in human judgment and decision-making. An alternative explanation is that humans have trouble aligning patient wishes with LLM-generated treatment recommendations. Human-machine teaming investigations are needed to discriminate between these explanations. To our knowledge, this is the first study that has tested the feasibility of leveraging LLMs for augmenting clinical decision-making by explicitly incorporating individual patients’ values.

Previous attempts to develop algorithms to predict patient preferences include the PPP (25, 26), whereby patient preferences are estimated using population-level data under the assumption that preferences correlate with certain characteristics and demographics of similar individuals. Such models have the capability to recommend decisions that are similar to or more accurate than those of surrogates (25). Lamanna and Byrne (27) proposed the “autonomy algorithm” in 2018, a machine learning method that estimates the confidence in the prediction of a patient consenting to a particular treatment based on electronic health record data and social media. Earp et al (17) build on these ideas in proposing a “personalized PPP” model that fine-tunes LLMs with information about each patient that can then be queried about their preferences. Although purely hypothetical, we believe that this is the first recorded suggestion in peer-reviewed literature of using generative models and individual level data to predict patient preferences. Our study carries these concepts further by simulating patient data and preferences to input into pre-trained generative models to produce and evaluate treatment recommendations in relation to their accurate reflection of patients’ values and wishes.

Patient values can be captured in a variety of nonstandardized mediums, which is one cause for their failure to be consistently implemented and honored (10, 28). Overall, we found that capturing patient values using unstructured free-text narratives was optimal, relative to numeric ranking questionnaires and text-based questionnaires. Such unstructured methods have been found problematic in the past due to their vague nature, leaving them open to variable interpretation by proxies and clinicians (29). However, the introduction of LLMs trained to identify textual patterns and relationships among documented patient preferences and possible treatment decisions could alleviate this burden. Alternative formats have been proposed that extend the written free-text narrative including audio or video recording of patients’ wishes. For example, younger (college-age) adults found that self-recording ADs was comforting and prompted further thoughts on defining their quality-of-life goals (30). Such mediums also allow capturing body language, speaking style, and emotion, which can provide additional information to the proxy to help confirm that they are making the right decision for the patient (31). These unstructured methods have been limited previously by their feasibility of deployment and use within a healthcare setting, but rapid developments in multimodal AI modeling (e.g., speech-to-text, text-to-image, etc) could streamline the processing of collecting and analyzing data from patients to feed into an AI-enabled framework within a clinical decision-making situation (32, 33).

There are substantial ethical, legal, and social concerns surrounding the uptake and deployment of generative decision models within healthcare settings, especially when considering decision-making for incapacitated patients (15, 20). Previous criticisms of the PPP include failing to respect patient autonomy and freedom of choice (34), and researchers have suggested that AI-enabled critical care decision support may not gain widespread acceptance due to the lack of explainability or transparency (15). Further issues relating to potential bias in the models from biased training data, liability, and data protection may also hinder the acceptance of AI-enabled decision support models for end-or life or resuscitation decisions (19, 35). Nevertheless, in a recent study, nearly 80% of surveyed patients revealed they would want the PPP to be used to help make their treatment decisions (26). Ensuring that such models respect patient autonomy, maintain transparency, and are monitored for bias and performance degradation will be crucial in their acceptance by patients, proxies, clinicians, and the public (15, 19).

Developing AI models to function as support tools rather than replacement decision-makers may be the first step in helping to retain human autonomy and gain acceptance in clinical decision-making. Other possible mitigation strategies might be ensuring the inclusion of direct patient values (e.g., advanced directives, asking patients about their values directly and documenting the conversation), as well as assessing training data for bias and using a diverse and representative dataset across different patient demographics. Establishing clear guidelines and oversight mechanisms for AI in healthcare decision-making, including ethical review boards and regulatory frameworks that define responsibility and accountability, and introducing ethical training for model developers and healthcare providers may also address concerns. Implementing continuous monitoring and feedback loops to assess model performance in real-world settings may allow for adjustments and improvements to ensure ethical compliance and patient safety. Nevertheless, the ethical and epistemological discussions are likely to continue.

LLMs are increasingly popular tools across many disciplines but the extensive computational power and data needed to train such models could hinder their use in critical care decision-making. We trialed pre-trained models including BERT and OpenAI’s ChatGPT 3.5, which have 336 million and 175 billion parameters, respectively, and are both computationally and financially expensive to train (36). Thus, training a new LLM de novo for a particular clinical purpose or with new data is likely to be costly and time-consuming; fine-tuning of foundation models on small volumes of task-specific training data is preferable. Luckily, there are increasing numbers of open-source models and model weights available through platforms like Hugging Face (37), that can be repurposed or fine-tuned for specific clinical tasks such as text extraction or QA, rapidly and at much lower costs. Many models are also now developed that have been trained on large amounts of clinical and medical text and data such as Clinical BERT (38), Clinical-Longformer (39), and GatorTron (40); future pre-trained models for healthcare purposes are likely to improve in their text processing and generation abilities and performance. For clinical applications, it would be critically important to use privacy preserving methods and LLMs that will not store or transmit patient data online.

Our study exclusively used simulated clinical scenarios and patient values, which may lack the depth, complexity, and variability of real-world data. We sought to mitigate this limitation by generating imperfect synthetic text (e.g., the specific treatment being considered was occasionally ambiguous). In the six scenarios where this type of text was used, models struggled to extract the correct clinical information, and thus were unable to generate sensible treatment suggestions. Such imperfections in real-world data are likely to impose similar penalties and could be problematic for the development and success of any kind of automated LLM-enabled pipeline for clinical decision-making. Real-world patient data will also likely contain data that are more difficult for LLMs to understand, including numeric measurements, units, medical annotations, and shorthand or abbreviated terms. Future work will use real, de-identified clinical notes and value profiles to develop a structured framework for the automatic processing and generation of clinical decisions for incapacitated patients using fine-tuned LLMs. A crucial step in this process will involve studying and pre-processing clinical data inputs to detect and mitigate potential shortfalls and problems, so that poor data quality or data sparsity do not hinder model performance.

A further issue observed during adjudication in some of the generated treatment decisions were instances where the generative model ascribed values to a patient that were absent from the value profiles or simulated scenarios, that is, values were either falsely generated, or they were inferred by the model from the available information. Quantification of these model hallucinations will be a key component of expanding this research, and we emphasize that AI-enabled clinical decision support systems are a long way from consistently making safe, effective recommendations. Increasing model transparency to understand why the model recommended one treatment over another may improve safety and feasibility. Nevertheless, such models as “information gathering tools” capable of summarizing and extracting patient values and wishes across extensive clinical notes and patient documents is another interesting avenue of research that we are concurrently exploring.

This study was also limited by low (Cohen’s Kappa) or moderate (Gwet’s AC2) inter-rater agreement among human adjudicators, especially for matching patient values, which may be partially attributable to heterogeneous training backgrounds (one bioethicist, one internist, and one surgeon and intensivist). Low inter-rater reliability simultaneously compromises certainty regarding model performance and demonstrates the variability inherent in human judgment and decision-making, which is an important rationale for augmenting clinical reasoning with objective, quantifiable results from modeling procedures: proxy decision-making is generally an entirely human process, reliant heavily on oral or written communication, and it is thus highly likely to include a similar level of variation. Anchoring such decisions within an objective, data-driven framework could provide substantial support to proxies and remove some uncertainty from such decisions. Obtaining quantifiable measures of certainty in our modeling and adjudication processes is an additional focus of our work going forward and will be key in providing confidence in LLM treatment recommendations to support proxy decision-making.

CONCLUSIONS

Proxies and clinicians frequently face difficult treatment decisions in caring for patients who have lost decision-making capacity, particularly in ensuring that care aligns with patients’ values. This study presents the first application of LLMs to generate treatment recommendations that explicitly incorporate patients’ values. Adjudication by clinical experts suggests that these models can produce sensible, coherent decisions that maintain consistency with patients’ values. LLMs performed best when patients’ values were conveyed in an unstructured, free-text format, which suggests opportunities to investigate alternative mediums for capturing patient value profiles via audio or video recordings that can be processed automatically into LLM inputs. Additional opportunities for further work include fine-tuning LLMs for proxy decision-making tasks, automatically extracting clinical scenarios and decisions from real-world data, and attending to ethical, legal, and social issues attendant to AI applications in healthcare settings, which are amplified when proxies and clinicians face the substantial burdens of caring for decisionally incapacitated patients.
