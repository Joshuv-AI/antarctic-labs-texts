Relationship Between Gut Microbiota and Cancer Neuro‐Immunity
Authors: Danyang Wang, Ruirui Cui, Chang You, Wanlin Zheng, Luyao Wang, Yingqi Yang, Hongkai Yu, Bota Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe nervous system and the immune system are integral components of the tumour microenvironment, and neuroimmune mechanisms play critical roles in tumour metastasis, immune evasion and metabolic reprogramming. However, the relationship between the gut microbiota and cancer neuro‐immunity remains poorly understood. This knowledge gap hampers our understanding of how these systems contribute to tumour progression and therapeutic resistance. This article systematically explores the interactions among the gut microbiota, the nervous system and the immune system in the regulation of tumour progression, with a particular focus on elucidating the pathways by which the gut microbiota and its metabolites modulate tumour phenotypes via neuroimmune mechanisms, and summarises the regulatory mechanisms through which cancer neuro‐immunity shapes gut microbiota composition. Additionally, this article summarises the interplay between immunotherapy and cancer neuro‐immunity, and explores the potential of microbiota‐based interventions, such as faecal microbiota transplantation, probiotics, prebiotics and synbiotics, to enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy through neuroimmune mechanisms.

Introduction

The gut microbiota refers to the microbial community that resides in the human gut and has a long‐term symbiotic relationship with the human body. The gut microbiota is diverse, with approximately 1000 species, and in adults, the ratio of gut microbes to human cells is about 1:1 (Sender et al. 2016). They form the largest and most complex ecosystem in the human body and establish a dynamic regulatory network with cancer neuro‐immunity, collectively influencing the body's homeostasis and health.

The gut microbiota is directly or indirectly related to tumorigenesis, and there is a feedback effect between them. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota promotes tumorigenesis through mechanisms such as chronic inflammation (Cheng et al. 2021), while the proliferation and metabolism of tumour cells damage the intestinal mucosal barrier, alter the gut microenvironment and affect the composition and metabolic functions of the gut microbiota (Feng et al. 2024) and modify microbiota composition and metabolic functions.

Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota promotes tumorigenesis through mechanisms such as chronic inflammation (Khanmammadova et al. 2023), while the proliferation and metabolism of tumour cells damage the intestinal mucosal barrier, alter the gut microenvironment and affect the composition and metabolic functions of the gut microbiota (Sarkar et al. 2013), and modifies microbiota composition and metabolic functions (Shurin et al. 2020).

The gut microbiota and cancer neuro‐immunity exhibit bidirectional interactions and mutual influences. The gut microbiota can modulate the immune system by regulating the functions of the central nervous system, autonomic nervous system and sensory nervous system, thereby influencing tumour progression (Kizil et al. 2024). Alterations in cancer neuro‐immunity can in turn affect the composition and function of the gut microbiota, and these changes may serve as indicators for evaluating immunotherapy efficacy and for implementing personalised cancer treatment. This review focuses on how the gut microbiota regulates the tumour immune microenvironment through different neural pathways, as well as how tumours alter the gut microbiota via the nervous and immune systems, and explores how microbiota‐based interventions (faecal microbiota transplantation, probiotics, prebiotics and synbiotics) may enhance the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy. In addition, this review summarises current advances and limitations in research on the relationship between the gut microbiota and cancer neuro‐immunity, and discusses potential directions for future investigations.

Interactions of the Gut Microbiota and Tumour Immunity via the Nervous System

An increasing body of evidence indicates that gut microbiota dysbiosis is not merely a concomitant feature of metabolic or immune abnormalities, but may also participate in tumour initiation, progression and therapeutic responses through coordinated regulation of the nervous and immune systems. Multiple clinical cohort studies have shown that even before tumour diagnosis or at early stages of disease, patients exhibit reduced gut microbiota diversity, depletion of beneficial commensal bacteria, and enrichment of pro‐inflammatory taxa, changes that are closely associated with increased subsequent cancer risk and poor prognosis (Nobels et al. 2025).

On the one hand, alterations in the structure and function of the gut microbiota can influence neuroendocrine activity, immune cell recruitment, inflammatory responses and the shaping of the tumour immune microenvironment via the central, autonomic and sensory nervous systems; on the other hand, tumours themselves, together with associated systemic inflammation and neuroendocrine changes, can in turn modulate gut microbiota composition. Together, these processes form a dynamic interaction network between the gut microbiota and cancer neuro‐immunity, which plays a critical role in tumour initiation and progression as well as in the regulation of systemic homeostatic imbalance.

Central Nervous System

The central nervous system (CNS), composed of the brain and spinal cord, serves as the core hub for integrating external stimuli and internal environmental signals and for coordinating neural, endocrine and immune responses. The gut microbiota exerts critical roles in maintaining immune homeostasis and in the initiation and progression of multiple cancers through central nervous system regulatory pathways such as circadian rhythm–related hypothalamic nuclei, the gut–brain axis, and the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis.

Hypothalamic Nuclei

Studies indicate that the regulation of tumour initiation and progression by the gut microbiota via the central nervous system does not rely solely on global neuroendocrine responses, but can also be mediated through neural circuits confined to specific brain regions and their functional nuclei, exerting more precise and direct effects. As a critical hub linking the nervous, endocrine and autonomic nervous systems, the hypothalamus integrates information derived from peripheral metabolic states, immune signals, and the gut microbiota, and regulates processes such as circadian rhythms, energy metabolism and stress responses through selective activation or inhibition of its functional nuclei, thereby playing a central role in tumour‐associated immune remodelling and tumour microenvironment formation.

The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) located in the hypothalamus functions as the master clock of human circadian rhythms and can influence cancer progression by regulating DNA damage, apoptosis, the cell cycle and senescence (Miro et al. 2023), proliferation (Ortega‐Campos et al. 2023), metabolism (Chen et al. 2014), as well as genomic instability and mutations (Zhou et al. 2020), while potentially reducing host sensitivity to antitumor therapies (e.g., glioma) (Wang and Chen 2022). Growing evidence suggests that fluctuations in the gut microbiota may serve as important drivers of host circadian rhythms and metabolism. Alterations in microbiota‐derived metabolites (such as ergothioneine) have been observed to be associated with circadian rhythm function (Vallianatou et al. 2021).

Studies have shown that in a mouse model of colorectal cancer (CRC), circadian rhythm dysfunction enhances H3K4 monomethylation of target genes and suppresses CHIP‐mediated PDL1 ubiquitination by influencing the gut microbiota and its derived metabolite taurocholic acid (TCA), thereby promoting epigenetic glycolysis in myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), inducing the accumulation of pulmonary MDSCs and dysfunctional CD8+ T cells, and ultimately facilitating lung metastasis of CRC (Liu et al. 2024).

In addition, the SCN drives the rhythmic release of melatonin from the pineal gland through γ‐aminobutyric acid (GABA) neuronal pathways, and the gut microbiota can regulate melatonin synthesis. Meanwhile, melatonin can increase the number of effector T cells, reduce the number of regulatory T cells and restrain tumour progression. In the circulation, melatonin binds to its receptors on immune cells and promotes the secretion of pro‐inflammatory and immunostimulatory cytokines, including interleukin‐1 (IL‐1), IL‐6, IL‐12, tumour necrosis factor (TNF) and interferon‐γ (IFN‐γ), and this cytokine cascade enhances immune activity (Socaciu et al. 2020). Therefore, gut microbiota–mediated disruption of the SCN‐regulated melatonin rhythm may promote tumorigenesis by weakening innate and adaptive immune responses.

In addition to circadian rhythm regulation, pro‐opiomelanocortin neurons in the arcuate nucleus (ARC) and the lateral hypothalamic area form a network that controls energy metabolism through coordinated signals from the paraventricular nucleus (PVN), ventromedial nucleus (VMH) and dorsomedial nucleus (DMH), thereby promoting tumour development (Mitchell and Begg 2021). For example, the gut microbiota can indirectly communicate with the central nervous system (CNS) through interactions with enteroendocrine cells (EECs), thereby promoting the secretion of appetite‐regulating hormones. Microbial metabolites, including short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs), bile acids (BAs) and indoles, influence the secretion of gut‐derived satiety hormones such as cholecystokinin (CCK), peptide YY (PYY) and glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) in intestinal epithelial cells. These hormones can activate vagal and spinal gut‐derived signals, which are then integrated in the nucleus tractus solitarius and subsequently induce orexin signalling in the hypothalamic arcuate nucleus (ARC). Activation of orexin signalling can induce apoptosis in pancreatic cancer cells (Dayot et al. 2018), whereas elevated serum leptin levels can promote tumour progression by suppressing hypothalamic orexin release. Environmental enrichment suppresses tumour growth and proliferation by upregulating hypothalamic brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) expression, inhibiting adipocyte‐derived leptin secretion and acting through the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and sympathetic neural output, an effect that has been validated in colorectal cancer and melanoma models (Cao et al. 2010). Therefore, targeting the BDNF–leptin signalling axis between the brain and peripheral tissues may provide novel therapeutic strategies for cancer treatment. In addition, in CRC models, catecholaminergic neurons in the ventrolateral medulla (VLM) accelerate tumour progression by suppressing CD8+ T cell activity (Zhang et al. 2021). Oxytocinergic neurons in the hypothalamic PVN communicate with the CRC microenvironment through the oxytocin–celiac–mesenteric ganglion signalling axis and suppress tumour progression by modulating HPA axis activity (Pan et al. 2021).

HPA Axis

Studies have shown that HPA axis function is closely coupled with the gut microbiota, and that dysbiosis can influence central neuroinflammation and stress responses through the tryptophan–kynurenine pathway, inflammatory cytokine release and the translocation of microbial metabolites across the blood–brain barrier. The CNS can integrate signals from the gut microbiota, the immune system and the tumour microenvironment via the gut–brain axis and the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, and translate this information into outputs that modulate tumour immune surveillance, inflammatory status and metabolic homeostasis. In the brains of mice treated with antibiotics, reduced expression of brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and activation of NF‐κB in the hippocampus have been observed, leading to neuroinflammation and anxiety‐like behaviours (Jang et al. 2018) and consequently amplifying glucocorticoid (GC)‐mediated immunosuppressive effects.

Studies have shown that germ‐free (GF) mice lacking gut microbiota exhibit increased secretion of stress hormones and reduced levels of brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) in the hippocampus, whereas gut bacterial colonisation can reverse these effects, and validation across different animal models has confirmed that alterations in the gut microbiota indeed modify stress‐related behaviours in diverse organisms (Neufeld et al. 2011; Nishino et al. 2013). Chronic stress is widely recognised as an important risk factor for poor prognosis in multiple types of cancer. Analyses of data from multiple large prospective cohorts indicate that long‐term psychological stress significantly worsens the prognosis of patients with digestive system cancers, including colorectal cancer (CRC) and pancreatic cancer (Obradović et al. 2019). Chronic stress persistently activates neurons in the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus (PVN), leading to sustained excitation of the HPA axis and elevated levels of glucocorticoids (GCs).

Glucocorticoids (GCs) promote tumorigenesis by activating glucocorticoid receptors (GR), thereby inhibiting dendritic cell maturation and antigen‐presenting functions (Kizil et al. 2024). On the one hand, GCs relieve GR‐mediated suppression of Ras signalling, activate the MAPK cascade and drive tumour invasion; on the other hand, they accelerate tumour progression by upregulating CDK1, inducing epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and enhancing stemness‐related pathways. Within the immune system, GCs markedly impair immune surveillance by suppressing antitumor immune cells and promoting the expansion of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) (Ma and Kroemer 2024). In addition, glucocorticoids bind to GR within dendritic cells and induce the expression of the glucocorticoid‐induced factor Tsc22d3, which blocks type I interferon (IFN) responses in DCs and IFN‐γ–mediated T‐cell activation (H. Yang et al. 2019), thereby impairing antigen presentation to T cells, hindering the initiation of adaptive immune responses and enabling tumours to evade immune surveillance.

In addition, nonclassical stress hormones also play important roles in tumour progression. Chronic stress influenced by the gut microbiota can also promote the release of oxytocin from the posterior pituitary into the circulation. Oxytocin drives melanoma metastasis through β‐arrestin 2–dependent activation of the extracellular signal–regulated kinase (ERK) cascade. Dopamine can also be secreted into the periphery to exert antitumor effects. Sciatic nerve stimulation enhances natural killer (NK) cell cytotoxicity through dopaminergic signalling and synergises with immunotherapy in the treatment of triple‐negative breast cancer (Zhi et al. 2025). Dopamine can also reduce intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) levels and block the activation of Src kinase by norepinephrine and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) (Kim‐Fuchs et al. 2014). Chronic stress influenced by the gut microbiota further increases hypothalamic corticotropin‐releasing hormone (CRH) secretion, modulates dopaminergic neurons in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and substantia nigra, reduces central dopamine production and weakens the antitumor effects of dopamine.

In summary, the gut microbiota may play an important regulatory role in tumour initiation and progression as well as in the efficacy of antitumor therapies through coordinated modulation of the central nervous system and the immune system. Further systematic elucidation of the underlying mechanisms is expected to provide new theoretical foundations and therapeutic targets for delaying tumour progression and optimizing cancer immunotherapy strategies.

Autonomic Nervous System

The autonomic nervous system mainly comprises the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems, which together govern visceral functions and coordinately regulate the activity of cardiac muscle, smooth muscle and glands to maintain internal homeostasis. In addition to indirectly influencing autonomic nervous system function by modulating central nervous system information integration, the gut microbiota and its derived metabolites, such as short‐chain fatty acids, can also directly regulate peripheral nervous system function (Zheng et al. 2022), affect immune responses, and induce inflammation or even carcinogenesis; these effects are not limited to gastrointestinal tumours but can also regulate the progression of distant solid tumours, such as breast cancer and melanoma, through coordinated actions of the nervous, endocrine and immune systems (Wong and Yu 2019; Loh et al. 2024).

Sympathetic Nervous System

The sympathetic nervous system has a wide distribution and innervates nearly all visceral organs, primarily mediating adaptive responses to stress stimuli in order to maintain internal homeostasis. In addition, both the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems maintain a certain level of basal tone under resting conditions, regulating the baseline functional state of target organs through continuous neural impulses.

In addition to chronic stress promoting tumour progression through effects on the gut microbiota (Ojeda and Ávila 2019; Li et al. 2026), gut microbiota dysbiosis can also drive abnormal chronic stress–related behaviours and enhance sympathetic nervous system tone (Qing et al. 2025). In ovarian cancer models, chronic stress–induced sympathetic activation promotes the expression of VEGF, MMP2 and MMP9, thereby enhancing angiogenesis and tumour metastatic potential (Thaker et al. 2006). Moreover, cancer patients often present with sympathetic nervous system dysfunctions such as chronic stress, cancer‐related fatigue (CRF) and sleep disturbances, suggesting that gut microbiota dysbiosis may regulate tumour progression via the sympathetic nervous system.

In addition, the gut microbiota can influence tumour progression by modulating host inflammatory responses. Studies have shown that formononetin (FMN) alleviates no‐reflow after myocardial ischemia–reperfusion by suppressing inflammation through regulation of the gut microbiota (Zhang et al. 2024). Plant tannins (Liu et al. 2023) and Litsea cubeba essential oil (Chen et al. 2023) can also markedly alter gut microbiota composition (e.g., affecting the abundance of Lactobacillus and Bacteroides) and improve host immune status by regulating immune factors, antioxidant enzymes and inflammation‐related indicators.

The gut microbiota and its metabolites, such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs), can activate downstream signalling pathways via free fatty acid receptors and toll‐like receptors (TLRs), thereby stimulating host immune responses through receptors widely distributed throughout the body (Su et al. 2023). In addition, nutrients derived from microbial metabolites can influence macrophage regional specialisation and may serve as important exogenous regulators of macrophage function (Wu et al. 2025), thereby affecting immune system activity and tumour initiation and progression.

Gut microbiota dysbiosis (e.g., an increase in Firmicutes and a decrease in Bacteroidetes) can alter metabolic profiles, whereby excessive lipopolysaccharide (LPS) upregulates N4BP3 expression, enhances K48‐linked ubiquitination of IκBα, activates the NF‐κB pathway, exacerbates inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) progression (Jiang, Yin, et al. 2025), and thereby increases the risk of gastrointestinal carcinogenesis. In addition, LPS can induce hyperactivation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, increasing the release of cortisol and other glucocorticoids (GCs) while elevating sympathetic tone, thereby promoting norepinephrine (NE) release. NE acts on immune cells and tumour cells via α/β‐adrenergic receptors and represents an important mechanism by which the gut microbiota influences distant tumours (Amit et al. 2025).

Gut microbiota dysbiosis can influence tumour progression by acting on the immune system, particularly immune cells, through norepinephrine (NE). NE activates β2‐adrenergic receptors (β2‐AR) on macrophages, triggering the cAMP–PKA–CREB pathway and promoting the recruitment of myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and M2 macrophage polarisation (Kiseleva et al. 2023), thereby inhibiting phagocytosis and enhancing the expression of IL‐10 and TGF‐β, which weakens antitumor immune responses (Huang et al. 2025) and establishes an immunosuppressive microenvironment that promotes tumour initiation, invasion and metastasis (Sloan et al. 2010; Lamkin et al. 2016; Jiang et al. 2019; Mohammadpour et al. 2019). NE also suppresses CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity, reduces IFN‐γ secretion and induces exhaustion markers such as PD‐1 and TIM‐3, while attenuating CD8+ T cell responses to anti–PD‐1 and anti–4‐1BB therapies, resulting in a significantly reduced tumour‐free survival rate. NE further regulates CXCL9 and adenosine secretion by tumour cells, thereby inhibiting chemotaxis and CD8+ T cell function and inducing resistance to anti–PD‐1 monoclonal antibody therapy in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) (Geng et al. 2023).

NE can also promote tumour progression by mediating neuro–stromal interactions through an adrenergic β2 receptor–nerve growth factor (NGF) feedforward loop (Kobayashi et al. 2025).β2‐AR activation stimulates cancer‐associated fibroblasts (CAFs) to secrete NGF, promoting intratumoral sympathetic neurogenesis and norepinephrine accumulation, which supports tumour growth or induces vascular collapse (Ferreiro et al. 2018), reduces therapeutic drug penetration (Hernández‐Miranda et al. 2010), remodels the extracellular matrix within the tumour microenvironment and accelerates cancer cell proliferation. In addition, increased sympathetic nerve–derived neurotrophic factors such as NGF and BDNF promote axonogenesis in breast (Zhao, Yang, et al. 2014) and prostate cancers (Ayala et al. 2008), enhance tumour innervation, further drive tumour growth and regulate epithelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) by activating transcription factors including ZEB1, Slug and Snail, leading to augmented adrenergic signalling and NE accumulation (Chimerel et al. 2014) and thereby enhancing tumour migratory and invasive capacities (Belulescu et al. 2020; Lv et al. 2021; Chai et al. 2025).

In addition, sympathetic adrenergic nerve terminals can regulate the expression of chemokines and adhesion molecules in vascular endothelial cells by releasing signalling molecules such as NGF, thereby controlling neutrophil migration to peripheral tissues and leading to immunosuppression and tumour progression. The gut microbiota can secrete histamine to activate histamine receptors on neutrophils, enhancing NADPH oxidase activity via the cAMP–PKA pathway and increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which eliminates pathogens but may also promote cellular malignant transformation. Moreover, ROS‐mediated chronic inflammation can remodel the tumour microenvironment and promote the infiltration of immunosuppressive cells, thereby creating favourable conditions for tumour initiation and progression (Nakai and Suzuki 2019) (Figure 1).

Therefore, gut microbiota dysbiosis can amplify neuro–immune regulatory signalling by increasing sympathetic tone, jointly shaping a pro‐tumour immune and inflammatory microenvironment during both the pre‐tumorigenic stage and tumour progression, and is closely associated with clinical manifestations commonly observed in cancer patients, including chronic stress, cancer‐related fatigue (CRF), and sleep disturbances.

Parasympathetic Nervous System

Although the parasympathetic nervous system is more limited in distribution than the sympathetic nervous system, it can modulate neuroendocrine and immune system functions, thereby influencing tumour progression, by directly releasing neurotransmitters and by controlling the enteric nervous system (ENS) to secrete multiple neurotransmitters and neuropeptides, including acetylcholine (ACh), serotonin (5‐HT) and vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP). Furthermore, the parasympathetic nervous system can directly regulate tumour growth, invasion and metastasis within the tumour immune microenvironment by mediating perineural invasion (PNI).

The gut microbiota serves as a key upstream regulator of parasympathetic nervous system activity. Short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs) produced by the microbiota can activate FFAR2/3 receptors on enterochromaffin cells, inducing 5‐HT release, which activates 5‐HT3 receptors and thereby enhances vagal afferent signalling (De Vadder et al. 2018). Elevated vagal tone can promote Treg differentiation and inhibit NK cell cytolytic activity, conferring tumours with an immune evasion advantage. Preclinical studies further show that reducing gastric parasympathetic activity can suppress gastric tumour development. Probiotics, such as Lactobacillus, by increasing SCFA levels, can inhibit vagus nerve‐mediated tumour innervation in pancreatic cancer models, thereby slowing tumour progression; correspondingly, vagotomy blocks cholinergic signalling, suppresses Hippo pathway activation in gastric cancer, and significantly impedes tumour growth (Hayakawa et al. 2017). In the ApcMin/+ mouse model of intestinal cancer, vagotomy reduced tumour area, indicating that exogenous innervation from the vagus nerve can promote tumorigenesis (Liu et al. 2015).

In addition, the gut microbiota can act on the vagus nerve to promote the release of acetylcholine (ACh). ACh activates the PI3K–AKT and Wnt signalling pathways in tumour cells through muscarinic acetylcholine receptors (mAChRs), promoting cancer cell proliferation and survival (Goehler et al. 2005; Zhao, Hayakawa, et al. 2014). Meanwhile, cholinergic signalling also regulates immune cell functions via nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs). Studies have shown that ACh can activate α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (α7nAChR) on macrophages, suppressing the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as TNF‐α and IL‐6, inducing M2 polarisation of macrophages, and promoting the expansion of regulatory T cells (Tregs), thereby weakening anti‐tumour immune responses and facilitating tumour growth and metastasis (Rosas‐Ballina et al. 2011; Baruch et al. 2025; Jiang, Cai, et al. 2025). Moreover, studies have found that the cholinergic system can directly modulate the immunoregulatory function of Tregs through specific expression of nAChRs, thereby influencing tumour progression (Nakata et al. 2022) (Figure 2).

In the tumour microenvironment, parasympathetic signals regulated by the gut microbiota not only modulate cellular functions via neurotransmitters but also cooperate with damaged enteric nerve fibres to mediate perineural invasion (PNI). This process directly promotes tumour invasion and dissemination and serves as an independent predictor of aggressiveness and poor prognosis (Schonkeren et al. 2021). In colorectal cancer (CRC), tumour cells migrate along infiltrating nerve fibres, forming ‘migration tracks’ that extend into surrounding tissues. As tumours progress, invasion and PNI exacerbate nerve damage, resulting in neuronal loss and dysfunction and establishing a positive feedback loop linking nerve injury, inflammation and tumour progression. Moreover, neurotransmitters released from nerve endings, including serotonin and glutamate, stimulate angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis, thereby facilitating tumour invasion and metastasis (Young et al. 2012).

The gut microbiota enhances dendritic cells (DCs) cross‐presentation of antigens through short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs). Chemokine CCL21 secreted by DCs can activate neuronal CXCR3 receptors, guiding lymphocytes toward the leading edge of neural invasion (Dong et al. 2023; Inamoto et al. 2023). Dysbiosis, such as the loss of Akkermansia, can impair this chemotactic axis, thereby exacerbating perineural invasion (Routy et al. 2018). Furthermore, single‐cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses revealed that in regions of high neural invasion (High NI), NLRP3 + macrophages and cancer‐associated myofibroblasts surround the invaded nerves, whereas T cells are enriched in low NI regions and co‐localise with non‐invaded nerves. GFBI + Schwann cells at the NI front are induced by TGF‐β signalling, which promotes tumour cell migration and is associated with poor prognosis (Chen et al. 2025).

In summary, the parasympathetic nervous system is not solely an anti‐inflammatory or pro‐tumour pathway; under the regulation of the gut microbiota, it collaborates with the enteric nervous system to modulate immune function and systemic neuroendocrine activity via neurotransmitters and perineural invasion. Its effects are highly context‐dependent across different tumour types and disease stages, providing a critical theoretical foundation for strategies targeting neural regulation combined with microbiota interventions to improve tumour treatment responses.

Sensory Nervous System

The sensory nervous system, especially nociceptive neurons, is a critical sensing and feedback pathway linking gut microbiota alterations to immune regulation, playing a key role in tumour initiation, progression and neuro‐immune regulation. Their nerve endings are widely distributed in the intestinal mucosa and around tumours, where they sense microbial metabolites and inflammatory signals, modulate local immune responses and influence the tumour microenvironment.

Chronic cancer‐related pain (CCRP), whether arising from primary or metastatic tumors or induced by anticancer treatments, is closely associated with sensory neuronal dysfunction (Swarm et al. 2019). Studies have shown that in paclitaxel‐treated cancer patients and rodent models, elevated secondary bile acid levels and increased gut Clostridium abundance promote deoxycholic acid (DCA) synthesis, exacerbating chemotherapy‐induced peripheral neuropathic pain (PIPN) (Zhong et al. 2023). DCA‐induced hyperalgesia in the dorsal root ganglia (DRG) is partially dependent on the CCL5/CCR5 signaling axis, and CCR5 blockade significantly alleviates paclitaxel‐associated neuropathic pain caused by elevated DCA (Zhong et al. 2023). Additionally, GABA‐producing Bifidobacteria can inhibit DRG excitability via GABA receptors, thereby reducing the severity of cancer‐related pain.

The gut microbiota and its metabolites can directly modulate sensory neuronal activity, thereby influencing tumour progression. Butyrate acts via GPR109a receptor activation and HDAC inhibition to induce macrophage polarisation toward a pro‐inflammatory M1 phenotype, which secretes TNF‐α and IL‐12, activating nociceptive TRPV1 channels and promoting substance P release to stimulate DRG neurons, thereby inducing pain (Routy et al. 2023). In pancreatic cancer models, oral administration of butyrate reduces M2 macrophage infiltration and decreases tumour‐associated nerve density. Additionally, cysteine proteases produced by dysbiotic microbiota can induce immune‐independent pain via the PAR‐2/Nav1.8 signalling pathway. These findings indicate that the gut microbiota can modulate cancer‐related pain through direct molecular crosstalk with sensory neurons, thereby influencing tumour progression.

Intestinal sensory neurons also play a critical role in regulating inflammatory disorders such as IBS and IBD, which are strongly associated with colorectal cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma and other malignancies. These sensory neurons detect mechanical and chemical stimuli and release neurotransmitters including CGRP, acetylcholine, tachykinins and neuromedin U. They cooperate with intrinsic primary afferent neurons of the gut to modulate mucosal immunity and inflammatory status, thereby contributing to cancer neuro‐immunity regulation (Rosenberg and Rao 2021).

Nociceptive neurons undergo significant expansion in the gastric cancer microenvironment via NGF‐dependent mechanisms. Segmented filamentous bacteria can induce Th17 cells to secrete IL‐17, thereby activating the CGRP‐RAMP1 signalling in sensory neurons (Ye et al. 2013), driving calcium signalling and promoting tumour proliferation (Zhi et al. 2025). CGRP can also functionally couple with signalling modules such as TRPV4 and TLR4‐JAK1 in the DRG and vagal afferent pathways to suppress CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity. In certain tumour microenvironments, CGRP may contribute to inflammation regulation, visceral pain sensitisation and the development of functional gastrointestinal disorders by inducing tumour angiogenesis and modulating other immune cell functions, thereby promoting tumour immune evasion (Ye et al. 2013; Adori et al. 2021). Nociceptive neurons release CGRP (calcitonin gene‐related peptide), which interacts with cancer‐associated fibroblasts (CAFs) to suppress NK cell function, thereby promoting PDAC progression and pain. Targeting CGRP signalling, for example with the anti‐migraine drug Rimegepant, can simultaneously inhibit tumour growth and alleviate pain, offering a dual therapeutic strategy for clinical application. Additionally, tachykinins, such as substance P, activate enteric glial cells via NK1R to induce pro‐inflammatory responses, while neuromedin U mobilises ILC2s through Nmu‐R1 to enhance mucosal immunity. Together, these mechanisms constitute the molecular basis by which sensory neurons regulate the tumour microenvironment.

Tumours can actively induce neural innervation and establish direct connections with sensory neurons. In prostate cancer (Ayala et al. 2008) and pancreatic cancer (Göhrig et al. 2014), cancer cells guide neural innervation by expressing chemotactic molecules. Head and neck cancer cells release EphrinB1‐containing exosomes to induce neurite outgrowth and can even drive sensory neuron reprogramming toward an adrenergic phenotype via miR‐43a (Madeo et al. 2018; Amit et al. 2020). Sensory neurons within the tumour microenvironment (TME) promote tumour growth, such as in melanoma, and inhibit the formation of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) (Vats et al. 2022). Structural and functional alterations in the enteric nervous system further reinforce the connection between sensory neurons and the tumour microenvironment. In colorectal cancer, non‐myelinated enteric nerve fibres are highly enriched, and enteric neurons express high levels of N‐cadherin and L1CAM, promoting tumour cell adhesion and migration (Duchalais et al. 2018).

In summary, current studies indicate that the sensory nervous system plays a critical role in mediating gut microbiota‐related signals to regulate the tumour immune microenvironment. The gut microbiota and its metabolites influence the excitability of nociceptive sensory neurons and neurotransmitter release, thereby modulating local inflammatory responses, immune cell functions and tumour‐associated neural remodelling, which contributes to tumour growth, immune evasion and cancer‐related pain. Tumour‐induced neural remodelling further amplifies this regulatory effect, providing a theoretical basis for integrated therapeutic strategies targeting sensory neural signalling.

Tumour Mediated Modulation of the Gut Microbiota via Neuro‐Immune Pathways

Patients with tumours such as colorectal cancer (Sears and Garrett 2014), breast cancer (Viswanathan et al. 2023) and gliomas (Cryan et al. 2019; Mehrian‐Shai et al. 2019) commonly exhibit significant alterations in gut microbiota composition, which are closely associated with tumour type, disease progression and treatment response. Furthermore, tumours can induce a chronic stress state that persistently activates the sympathetic nervous system (SNS), leading to prolonged elevations of norepinephrine and glucocorticoids and triggering a low‐grade but sustained systemic inflammatory response.

In glioma models, tumour growth significantly reshapes the composition of the host microbiota, characterised by a decreased Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio, an increased relative abundance of Verrucomicrobia, and notable enrichment of the genus Akkermansia, particularly Akkermansia muciniphila (Patrizz et al. 2020; Jiang et al. 2022). In glioma‐bearing mice, faecal short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and several neurotransmitters are altered. Targeted metabolomic analyses show a significant decrease in serotonin (5‐HT) and norepinephrine (Dono et al. 2020). These findings suggest that tumour‐driven changes in gut microbiota composition are mediated via neuro‐immune pathways, including gastrointestinal dysfunction, systemic inflammation and neuroendocrine dysregulation.

In neuro‐rich tumour microenvironments, chronic inflammation and neuronal damage synergistically drive pro‐tumour tissue remodelling. Damaged neurons secrete factors such as IL‐6 and VEGF, promoting the recruitment of myeloid‐derived suppressor cells and angiogenesis (Ren et al. 2023). This persistently activated neuro‐immune state not only exacerbates local microenvironment remodelling but also amplifies systemic inflammatory signals via autonomic nervous output, further disrupting gut microbiota homeostasis. In gastrointestinal tumours such as colorectal cancer, neuronal degeneration can disrupt neuro‐epithelial homeostasis and gut motility, impair barrier integrity and alter local immune surveillance, thereby modifying microbiota composition and amplifying tumour‐associated inflammatory responses (Boursi et al. 2016; Li, Peng, et al. 2022).

Tumour cells disrupt gut microbiota balance through small molecules such as neurotransmitters and neurotrophic factors. Tumour‐secreted nerve growth factor (NGF) and cytokines such as TNF‐α and IL‐6 can activate peripheral sympathetic nerve fibres to release norepinephrine (NE). Through β2‐adrenergic receptor signalling, this downregulates tight junction proteins such as ZO‐1 in intestinal epithelial cells, compromising gut barrier integrity and consequently disrupting microbiota balance (Calvani et al. 2015; Vaes et al. 2022). In colorectal cancer models, IFN‐γ secreted by CD8+ T cells stimulates enteric glial cells to release brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), promoting the growth of short‐chain fatty acid–producing bacteria such as Roseburia in the gut. Additionally, neurons in the tumour microenvironment release calcitonin gene‐related peptide (CGRP), which activates gut nociceptive neurons, inducing mast cell degranulation and histamine release, thereby suppressing Lactobacillus proliferation (Sánchez et al. 2023).

Additionally, tumours can disrupt gut microbiota balance by modulating neuroendocrine function through effects on the immune system. Tumour‐infiltrating CD8+ T cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs) can influence gut microbiota composition by modulating intestinal epithelial cell activity through secretion of interferon‐γ (IFN‐γ) or IL‐10. IL‐10 secreted by Tregs can suppress 5‐HT synthesis in enterochromaffin cells, which, via 5‐HT4 receptor–dependent signalling, reduces the colonisation capacity of spore‐forming bacteria such as Clostridium (Palomares et al. 2014; Shin et al. 2019). Experiments in a breast cancer mouse model showed that oral administration of the Hedgehog pathway inhibitor Vismodegib increased the abundance of proliferative CD8+ T cells in the colonic epithelium, lamina propria and mesenteric lymph nodes, while reshaping tumour‐altered microbiota, significantly reducing the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio and decreasing pulmonary metastases (Hinshaw et al. 2022).

In summary, tumours may influence the structure and function of the gut microbiota via the neuro‐immune axis, further exacerbating intestinal immune dysregulation and impacting treatment efficacy and patient survival. Understanding how cancer neuro‐immunity affects the gut microbiota is critical for tumour diagnosis and personalised therapy (Chang et al. 2023).

Interactions Between Gut Microbiota and Cancer Neuro‐Immunity in Immunotherapy

Studies have shown that the gut microbiota can regulate tumour progression not only by directly affecting the nervous and immune systems but also by modulating the neuro‐immune axis, thereby influencing the efficacy of multiple immunotherapies, including immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., PD‐1/PD‐L1 and CTLA‐4 inhibitors), CAR‐T cell therapy, and haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (De Marco et al. 2023; Schepisi et al. 2023). Moreover, microbial peptides (Naghavian et al. 2023) and other metabolites derived from the gut microbiota can regulate the function and phenotype of multiple immune cells, including dendritic cells, macrophages, T cells and neutrophils. They act via epigenetic reprogramming, receptor‐mediated signalling cascades and cross‐talk with neuro‐immune factors, dynamically maintaining the balance of the tumour microenvironment (Pitt et al. 2016). This multi‐layered regulatory mechanism provides a new theoretical perspective for understanding interindividual variability in immunotherapy responses and for mitigating associated adverse events.

Gut Microbiota Regulation of Immunotherapy Through Cancer Neuro‐Immunity

Clinical and preclinical studies have confirmed that gut microbiota diversity and the abundance of specific commensal bacteria are closely associated with the response of various tumours, such as melanoma, to immunotherapy. In patients receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors, reduced microbiota diversity is generally associated with lower response rates and shorter progression‐free survival, whereas patients enriched in Bifidobacterium, Faecalibacterium, Ruminococcus or Akkermansia muciniphila are more likely to benefit from anti‐PD‐1 and anti‐CTLA‐4 therapies. Faecal microbiota transplantation studies further demonstrate that the microbiota from responders can restore sensitivity to therapy in resistant models, confirming a causal role (Baruch et al. 2021; Davar et al. 2021). Metabolites derived from Lactobacillus gallinarum can remodel T cell function via the IDO1/Kyn/AHR pathway, thereby enhancing the efficacy of anti‐PD‐1 therapy in colorectal cancer (Singh et al. 2014; Kikuchi et al. 2020; Cassano et al. 2023; Fong et al. 2023). Meanwhile, clinical studies suggest that targeting neural signals, such as with β‐adrenergic blockers or vagus nerve stimulation (VNS), can modulate the tumour immune microenvironment and synergise with immunotherapy. This approach holds promise as an anticancer strategy, potentially allowing reduced doses of immunotherapeutic drugs, alleviating patient discomfort, and effectively inhibiting tumour growth in a coordinated manner (Shi et al. 2022).

Meanwhile, the gut microbiota may mediate anti‐tumour immune responses through neuro‐related pathways. Polysaccharide A secreted by Bacteroidetes can drive M2 macrophage polarisation via the TLR2‐STAT3 pathway, inducing TGF‐β secretion that activates Schwann cell ERK/MAPK signalling and promotes Netrin‐1–mediated tumour neural sprouting. GDNF released by M2 macrophages can activate sensory neurons via the RET receptor, inducing CGRP‐dependent CD8+ T cell exhaustion, whereas targeting β‐adrenergic signalling can restore M1/M2 balance and enhance immunotherapy responses (Gao et al. 2022). The microbial metabolite butyrate can also induce pro‐inflammatory M1 macrophage polarisation via the GPR109a receptor and HDAC inhibition, leading to TNF‐α and IL‐12 secretion, activation of TRPV1 channels in nociceptive neurons, substance P release and enhanced NK cell cytotoxicity, thereby promoting tumour immunity (Routy et al. 2023). Lactobacillus activates dendritic cells through TLR2, promoting CD8+ T cell responses. In contrast, Bifidobacterium stimulates IL‐12 secretion via TLR2/MyD88 signalling, and its extracellular polysaccharides inhibit PD‐L1 expression on dendritic cells, thereby reversing T cell exhaustion (Jeon et al. 2012; Hu et al. 2021) (Figure 3).

Metabolites derived from the gut microbiota serve as key mediators of the dialogue between the microbiota and host cancer neuro‐immunity. Secondary bile acids can modulate inflammatory responses through TGR5 signalling in dendritic cells (Hu et al. 2021). Microbiota‐derived tryptophan metabolites, such as kynurenine, can induce T cell exhaustion via activation of the AhR pathway (D'Amato et al. 2015; Novikov et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2021), and their plasma levels are significantly associated with clinical outcomes of immunotherapy (Li et al. 2019; Karayama et al. 2021). In contrast, short‐chain fatty acids, such as butyrate, enhance the antigen‐presenting capacity of dendritic cells, thereby promoting T cell infiltration during anti‐PD‐1 therapy. The microbiota can also indirectly modulate immunity via neural reflex circuits. For example, in melanoma patients responding effectively to therapy, Lactobacillus abundance positively correlates with peripheral IFN‐γ levels and the proportion of CD8+ T cells. Anti‐PD‐1 treatment can activate the vagus–enteric neural reflex, establishing a positive feedback loop that promotes probiotic growth (Baruch et al. 2021; Davar et al. 2021; Smith et al. 2022).

This regulatory network also affects other immunotherapies. Dysbiosis induced by broad‐spectrum antibiotics can impair CAR‐T cell therapy efficacy (Stein‐Thoeringer et al. 2023), whereas probiotics or faecal microbiota transplantation can modulate the immune microenvironment and enhance the synergy between CAR‐T cells and immune checkpoint inhibitors (Guillot et al. 2023). A healthy microbiota can help reprogram tumour‐associated macrophages toward an antigen‐presenting phenotype, promoting T cell activation, whereas dysbiosis may disrupt this neuro‐immune regulation, leading to therapy resistance (Lin et al. 2024).

In summary, the gut microbiota actively shapes the tumour microenvironment through multiple mechanisms, including metabolic products, direct immune regulation and neural reflexes, profoundly affecting the efficacy and tolerability of immunotherapy. Therefore, combined microbiota‐targeted interventions are expected to enhance immunotherapy responsiveness and reduce toxicity via neuro‐immune pathways (Ciernikova, Sevcikova, and Mego 2024; Ciernikova, Sevcikova, Novisedlakova, and Mego 2024; Zhou et al. 2025).

Adverse Events Induced by Immunotherapy via Neuro‐Immune Pathways

While activating antitumor immune responses, immunotherapy may also trigger a range of adverse effects through neuro‐immune regulatory networks. The gastrointestinal tract is one of the most commonly affected sites, and this involves direct activation of local neuro‐immune axes. Studies indicate that during immunotherapy, tumour‐associated macrophages release inflammatory factors such as IL‐17, which activate TRPV1+ nociceptive sensory neurons in the gut mucosa, promoting the release of the neuropeptide substance P. Substance P, via NK1 receptor signalling, inhibits intestinal epithelial mucin secretion, disrupts barrier function and microbial spatial segregation, thereby facilitating colonisation by pathobionts and increasing the risk of immune‐related colitis (Innao et al. 2020).

Notably, intrinsic mechanisms of neural injury in certain tumours may be pathologically linked to adverse effects of immunotherapy. Studies have found that in tumours such as glioblastoma, cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, melanoma and gastric cancer, cancer cells can directly interact with neurons, degrade axonal myelin, induce neural injury and release lipids that support tumour growth. Meanwhile, damaged neurons release factors such as IL‐6 to initiate repair programs, and this repeated injury‐repair cycle induces chronic inflammation, maintaining an immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment (Kloosterman et al. 2024; Baruch et al. 2025). Preclinical studies show that inhibiting neural injury signals or IL‐6–mediated inflammatory pathways can reduce immunosuppression and restore the effectiveness of PD‐1 inhibitors, indicating that tumour‐intrinsic neuro‐immune abnormalities may underlie treatment‐related adverse events.

Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota can further amplify the systemic effects of immunotherapy. Impairment of regulatory T cell function allows gut commensal‐induced, antigen‐specific CD4+ T cells to migrate to the central nervous system, where they become reactivated, secrete IL‐17A and GM‐CSF, and activate microglia, thereby initiating neuroinflammation (Xie, Liu, et al. 2024). Immune checkpoint inhibitors, by relieving T cell suppression, may exacerbate this ‘gut‐brain’ regulatory process, leading to the onset or worsening of central nervous system‐related adverse events.

Furthermore, systemic immune activation induced by immunotherapy can indirectly affect gut homeostasis via neuroendocrine pathways. For example, cytokine storms induced by CAR T cell therapy can activate the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, elevating glucocorticoid levels, which in turn suppress TLR4 signalling in dendritic cells within the intestinal Peyer's patches, reduce the secretion of the antimicrobial peptide Reg3γ, and ultimately lead to gut microbiota dysbiosis (Alexander et al. 2017).

These findings collectively indicate that immune‐related adverse events result from multi‐level activation and amplification of tumour neuro‐immune abnormalities. The neuro‐immune network contributes to diverse physiological processes, ranging from local intestinal responses to central neurotoxicity, through mechanisms including direct activation, microbiota‐mediated modulation and neuroendocrine regulation. Understanding this network can facilitate the development of interventions targeting neuro‐immune pathways, enhancing immunotherapy efficacy while effectively managing associated toxicities.

Effects of Microbiota‐Based Interventions on Cancer Neuro‐Immunity

Probiotics, Prebiotics and Synbiotics

Synbiotics are mixtures containing live microorganisms and substrates selectively utilised by host microbiota that confer health benefits (Swanson et al. 2020), primarily by promoting the growth of beneficial microbes, inhibiting harmful microbes and maintaining gut homeostasis (Yao et al. 2025). Studies have shown that synbiotics can alter gut microbiota composition, effectively increase the abundance of beneficial lactic acid bacteria, improve the intestinal environment (Kanazawa et al. 2021) and consequently influence cancer neuro‐immunity.

Probiotics are live microorganisms that benefit the host, including Lactobacillus, Lactococcus and Streptococcus species, which can regulate gut microbiota balance and enhance host neuro‐immune function (Abd Ellatif et al. 2022). Bifidobacterium pseudolongum can promote CD8+ T cell infiltration in tumours and their differentiation into memory cells via its metabolite L‐arginine, directly killing cancer cells (Nan et al. 2025). Additionally, Bifidobacterium longum SX‐1326 can restore gut microbiota diversity in colorectal cancer mice by modulating the gut–brain axis, increase the abundance of Akkermansia, resist intestinal inflammation and enhance cancer neuro‐immunity (Yue et al. 2024). In glioma studies, tryptophan supplementation modulated gut microbiota, and D. dubosii was shown to enhance the cytotoxicity of tumour‐infiltrating CD8+ T cells, thereby improving tumour immunotherapy efficacy (Kim et al. 2025). In summary, probiotics primarily enhance cancer neuro‐immunity by modulating gut microbiota composition and boosting CD8+ T cell function.

Prebiotics are components that are indigestible by the host but selectively promote the growth and activity of beneficial gut microbes. Their impact on cancer neuro‐immunity primarily involves stimulating beneficial bacterial growth, regulating gut microbiota, improving anticancer therapy efficacy—for example, inulin promotes the growth of Faecalibacterium and Bifidobacterium—protecting the intestinal barrier, and enhancing cancer neuro‐immunity (Xie, Liu, et al. 2024). Moreover, novel prebiotics also play a role in cancer neuro‐immunity. A study revealed that plant‐derived nanoparticles (PNPs), as a new form of prebiotics, can modulate gut bacterial metabolic pathways, enhance the efficacy of PD‐1/PD‐L1 inhibitors and improve tumour immunotherapy outcomes (Teng et al. 2025).

Microbiome Engineering

Microbiome engineering refers to manipulating naturally occurring microbial communities or constructing synthetic consortia to achieve specific functions. It represents an approach that can improve and innovate various clinical applications (Eng and Borenstein 2019). Conventional cancer therapies, such as surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy, often fail to completely eliminate tumour cells in the hypoxic microenvironment of tumours. The gut probiotic Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 has been used as a targeted delivery vector for p53 and Tum‐5 drugs to hypoxic solid tumours, aiming to achieve therapeutic effects (He et al. 2019).

Immunotherapy resistance arises from the immunosuppressive state of the tumour microenvironment. Microbiome engineering can remodel and reprogram the composition and function of the gut microbiota (Aggarwal et al. 2023), representing a novel therapeutic strategy to restore TAM‐mediated immunosuppression and enhance tumour sensitivity to immunotherapy (Kuhl and Tangney 2025). In pancreatic cancer, under anaerobic conditions, engineered bacteria actively colonise pancreatic tumours and bind to cancer cells via aptamer‐mediated interactions, providing targeted delivery and synergistic therapeutic effects (Yu et al. 2025). Furthermore, engineered live bacterial therapy, which involves genetically modifying attenuated or non‐pathogenic bacteria (e.g., Salmonella or E. coli ), enables self‐proliferation and active targeting of tumour hypoxic regions, allowing sustained, in situ drug delivery and reducing resistance (Gurbatri et al. 2022). Engineered bacteria can release cytokines to convert immunosuppressive M2 tumour‐associated macrophages into pro‐inflammatory M1 phenotypes, thereby enhancing anti‐tumour immunity, optimizing the tumour immune microenvironment, reversing immunosuppression and reducing tumour resistance. Engineered E. coli can also convert tumour‐derived metabolic amines into L‐arginine by deleting the ArgR gene and expressing feedback‐resistant ArgA, enhancing T cell activation and memory formation, overcoming tumour metabolic barriers and improving immune cell function (Wang, Xu, and Wang 2025). Therefore, combining engineered microbes with immunotherapy can overcome treatment resistance and enhance therapeutic efficacy.

Faecal Microbiota Transplantation

Animal studies indicate that faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) can delay aging and reduce cognitive impairments by altering the composition of microbial metabolites. Similarly, gut microbiota‐derived metabolites may influence tumour‐related processes through the nervous system (Cheng et al. 2022). As indicated by the complex interaction between the tumour‐neuroimmune axis and the gut microbiota, improving the composition of the gut microbiota can have a positive effect on cancer neuro‐immunity, preventing and reducing treatment‐related toxicity and enhancing treatment efficacy (Kaźmierczak‐Siedlecka et al. 2020). Faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), by delivering the functional gut microbiota from healthy donors to the patient's gastrointestinal tract, reshapes the microbiota ecology to achieve synergistic regulation of the host immune‐neuro axis, offering a new strategy for enhancing tumour treatment while reducing toxicity (Khoruts and Sadowsky 2016).

Studies have found that Faecal Microbiota Transplantation (FMT) can positively affect the mechanisms associated with tumour neuroimmune pathways when combined with methods such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy and immunotherapy. FMT conducted prior to palliative chemotherapy in metastatic gastric‐oesophageal cancer patients involved the transplantation of gut microbiota from either allogeneic healthy obese donors or autologous sources. The results showed that, compared to the autologous group, patients in the allogeneic group had a higher disease control rate at 12 weeks, a longer median overall survival, and an extended progression‐free survival, suggesting that FMT from overweight or obese donors may improve chemotherapy response and survival time in metastatic gastric‐oesophageal cancer patients (de Clercq et al. 2021). Lachance et al. found that the transplantation of gut microbiota from prostate cancer patients stimulates the occurrence and progression of prostate cancer in mice. Supplementation with LCFA‐3‐MAG‐EPA reduced the number of gut ruminal bacteria and lowered faecal butyrate levels, potentially inhibiting prostate cancer growth in mice through the aforementioned neuroimmune pathways (Lachance et al. 2024).

Research has found that one of the key factors contributing to radioresistance in hepatocellular carcinoma patients is the disruption of the gut microbiome. In a dysbiotic state, the bacterial metabolite Cyclic‐di‐AMP acts as an agonist of the STING pathway, activating the cGAS–STING–IFN‐I signalling axis, thereby inhibiting antigen presentation and weakening the antitumor activity of effector T cells, ultimately leading to impaired antitumor immune responses. Therefore, gut microbiota transplantation, as a means of regulating microecological balance, holds promise as a new strategy to enhance the sensitivity of hepatocellular carcinoma to radiotherapy (Li, Peng, et al. 2022; Li, Zhang, et al. 2022).

Results from Phase I clinical trials in advanced melanoma show that the objective response rate for FMT combined with PD‐1 therapy is 65%, with four cases achieving complete remission (Routy et al. 2023). Microsatellite stable metastatic colorectal cancer shows a low response to PD‐1 inhibitors, but the disease control rate can be increased to 95% with the FMT combined with ICI regimen (Zhao et al. 2023). Furthermore, in mice without colorectal cancer (CRC), Ruminococcus flavefaciens (R.f) and Faecalibacterium saccharivorans (F.s) were significantly enriched, while Enterococcus durans (E.d) was notably reduced. Functional studies further indicate that R.f and F.s have independent protective effects in inhibiting CRC development, while E.d contributes to the initiation of CRC by promoting inflammatory responses (Zhou et al. 2025).

Several clinical trials (Baruch et al. 2021; Davar et al. 2021; Routy et al. 2023) indicate that faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) can convert patients with initially ICI‐resistant melanoma or renal cell carcinoma into responders, accompanied by favourable changes in microbial communities and immune biomarkers. Regarding the effects of probiotics, prebiotics and synbiotics on immunotherapy, the clinical efficacy of probiotics remains controversial. A study led by Spencer found that administering probiotics to mice could impair their response to ICI‐based therapy (Spencer et al. 2021). High‐dose probiotics may reduce the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors, suggesting that maintaining a balanced and diverse gut microbiota is more important than simply consuming specific ‘beneficial’ bacteria (Yan et al. 2024). Prebiotics enhance gut microbiota by promoting the growth and activity of Bifidobacterium, increasing the production of short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and stimulating the immune system. However, their anticancer adjuvant effects depend on a healthy gut microbiota and metabolic environment (Chattopadhyay et al. 2021), indicating that maintaining gut health is crucial for maximizing immunotherapy efficacy. Synbiotics, a combination of probiotics and prebiotics, exert both probiotic and prebiotic effects, enhancing the antitumor activity of the immune system. Synbiotic interventions have been shown to improve responses to checkpoint blockade therapy across various cancer types (Knisely et al. 2023).

Although FMT shows promise, its implementation faces challenges, including patient selection criteria, timing of intervention, donor screening requirements and safety considerations (Haifer et al. 2020). Recent clinical trials (Routy et al. 2023) and guidelines (‘[Expert consensus on clinical application management of fecal microbiota transplantation (2022 edition)],’ 2022) indicate that patient selection for FMT should be based on a comprehensive assessment of disease status, functional reserve and immune risk. Patients receiving antibiotics or probiotics, as well as those with absolute contraindications, are excluded. Antibiotic use is closely associated with CAR‐T efficacy, which is mediated by the gut microbiota; for example, mice treated with broad‐spectrum antibiotics exhibit reduced CAR‐T tumour suppression (He et al. 2022). Furthermore, patients and their families should be informed in advance about treatment goals, risks, precautions and potential complications, with appropriate postoperative follow‐up. The optimal timing for combining FMT with immune checkpoint inhibitors is shortly before initiating or resuming therapy, allowing the transplanted microbiota to establish promptly and modulate the immune microenvironment (Lin et al. 2025). Regarding donor selection, guidelines recommend screening across six dimensions, including physiological, psychological, personal history and stability (‘[Expert consensus on clinical application management of fecal microbiota transplantation (2022 edition)],’ 2022). Stricter criteria should be applied, prioritizing donors with beneficial microbiota profiles. The “Expert Consensus on Fecal Microbiota Transplantation for Inflammatory Bowel Disease (2025 edition)” (‘[Consensus of Chinese experts on gut microbiota and fecal microbiota transplantation in inflammatory bowel disease (2025 edition)],’ 2025) emphasises that gut dysbiosis can directly or indirectly trigger mucosal immune dysregulation and promote inflammation. Prior to FMT, patients' gut microbiota should be assessed to prevent adverse events and ensure treatment safety (Du et al. 2025).

Limitations of Current Research and Future Directions

Limitations of Current Research

The interaction between the gut microbiota and cancer neuro‐immunity involves multiple factors, including host genetic background, diet and lifestyle (Park et al. 2022; Simpson et al. 2023) and the mechanisms of their synergistic effects remain unclear. Currently, the methods used to study the gut microbiome include 16SRNA sequencing and shotgun metagenomics, but they fail to capture the full complexity of the microbiome's temporal and spatial variations (Han et al. 2024). Furthermore, although various techniques have been employed to study the role of the microbiota‐gut‐brain axis in tumour immunity‐related diseases and the impact of these diseases on the gut microbiota, including germ‐free (GF) studies, infection surveys, probiotics research, antibiotic studies and faecal transplantation studies, the specific mechanisms of interaction between the gut microbiota and cancer neuro‐immunity remain to be explored (Ashique et al. 2024).

Given the highly individualised nature of the human gut microbiota, the composition of faecal material inherently depends on the donor (Larsen and Brummer 2024). Donor–recipient matching in faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) remains challenging. The ‘super‐donor’ phenomenon suggests that certain donor samples confer superior efficacy, yet precise biomarkers for optimal matching are lacking, making it impossible to overcome immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) non‐responsiveness in all patients (Blake et al. 2024). Successful colonisation of transplanted strains is a prerequisite for efficacy; thus, anti‐inflammatory treatment should be administered during periods of active inflammation before FMT; otherwise, engraftment may fail (Porcari et al. 2023).

Regarding immune‐related adverse events, the recipient's immune system may mount abnormal responses to newly introduced microbes. Microbial transplantation can induce new dysbiosis or metabolic disturbances (DeLeon et al. 2025). Changes in microbial composition may reduce the number and function of Treg cells, disrupt immune system coordination, break immune tolerance and trigger or exacerbate autoimmune diseases (Cheru et al. 2023). A healthy gut represents a stable ecosystem, where the resident microbiota resists colonisation by new microbes, such as Salmonella, through nutrient competition and secretion of antimicrobial substances (Deng and Wang 2024). Transplanted microbes may fail to establish in the recipient's gut due to rejection by the resident microbiota, potentially resulting in treatment failure. Definitions and regulatory policies for FMT vary across countries (Merrick et al. 2020). Although regulations aim to ensure quality and safety, the absence of standardised guidelines can create confusion. For example, in the United States and Canada, FMT is classified as a biological product and drug. In the United Kingdom, it is regulated as a medicinal product. In Australia, it is considered a biological preparation, whereas in Italy, the Netherlands and Belgium, it is categorised as a tissue and regulated under the EU Tissues and Cells Directive (Yadegar et al. 2024).

Future Directions

Given the current limitations in understanding the mechanisms and signalling pathways, future research should focus on revealing the complex interactions between the gut microbiome and the immune and nervous systems in their spatiotemporal changes. This includes exploring how the composition of the microbiome and its metabolites dynamically regulate immune cell functions, neurotransmitter release and the polarisation of immune cell phenotypes in response to changes in the tumour microenvironment 89. At the same time, there is an urgent need to deeply analyse the mechanisms of cross‐regulation among multiple pathways, particularly the interactions between the microbiome, immune system, and nervous system, to understand how they collaborate during tumour occurrence, progression and immune evasion. For instance, how gut microbiota, such as Faecalibacterium, increases the proportion of CD4 T cells in peripheral blood and the production of serum CD25, while reducing the proportion of Treg cells (Lu et al. 2022; Xie and Liu 2024).

Additionally, the development of more accurate 3D cell culture techniques to simulate the tumour microenvironment (TME) is an important area of cancer research (Spill et al. 2016). In 3D cell culture, tumour cells grow in an environment that more closely simulates the 3D structure and complexity of in vivo tumours. This approach provides a more accurate representation of the TME and allows for the study of tumour behaviour and treatment responses in a more physiologically relevant environment, fundamentally transforming cancer research (Abuwatfa et al. 2024). With the steady development of co‐culture models, microfluidic technology, bioprinting and other techniques, researchers are hopeful of fully replicating the dynamically changing TME in vitro (Ao et al. 2022).

Under 3D culture conditions, the development of patient‐derived tumour organoid models using primary human cancer tissues can effectively simulate the patient's primary tumour microenvironment (Zeng et al. 2024). The novel 3D Flipwell system can also layer the simulation of the microbial layer, mucus layer, epithelial cells and immune cells of the intestinal mucosa, thus more accurately reflecting the dynamic balance between the gut microbiota and host cells. In other words, 3D culture technology allows for high‐throughput testing of the effects of different microbial combinations or drugs on the gut microbiota, rapidly screening probiotics that can improve intestinal barrier function or regulate immune responses (Beamer et al. 2023).

CAR‐T cell therapy is applicable to tumours that do not respond to immune checkpoint inhibitors, making the combination of CAR‐T cell therapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors a promising area for further research in the combined strategies of gut microbiota and tumour immunotherapy. Chimeric Antigen Receptor‐T (CAR‐T) cells recognise and bind to specific antigens on the surface of tumour cells through the chimeric antigen receptor, activating T cell proliferation and cytotoxic potential (De Marco et al. 2023). Immune checkpoint inhibitors have become a standard treatment for various cancer types, with the blockade of immune checkpoints maintaining T cell activity through the binding of Cytotoxic T‐Lymphocyte‐Associated Antigen‐4 or PD/PD‐1 molecules (Schepisi et al. 2023).

Studies on neuroimmune signalling pathways have found that the nervous system influences lymphocyte migration and function through various mechanisms, such as NE acting on β‐adrenergic receptors (β‐AR) on macrophages (Udit et al. 2022). The vagus nerve activates cholinergic neurons in the spleen, releasing acetylcholine that acts on the α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (α7nAChR) on macrophages to inhibit the production of pro‐inflammatory factors, regulating the activity of tissue‐resident and lymph node immune cells (Chu et al. 2020). The combination of neuroactive drugs, such as propranolol, with immune checkpoint inhibitors, cytotoxic therapies or cancer vaccines, can enhance the body's anti‐tumour immune response by modulating the immune tumour microenvironment (Shi et al. 2022).

The efficacy of CAR‐T cell therapy is influenced by the composition of the donor and patient's gut microbiota, and the use of broad‐spectrum antibiotics disrupts the balance of the gut microbiota, leading to a reduced therapeutic effect of CAR‐T therapy (Stein‐Thoeringer et al. 2023). Supplementing with probiotics, faecal microbiota transplantation and other microbiota‐related therapies can enhance the synergistic anti‐tumour effect of CAR‐T cells and immune checkpoint inhibitors by modulating the immune microenvironment (Guillot et al. 2023).

Integrating multi‐omics data enables the construction of interpretable interaction networks to predict novel mechanisms. In one study, longitudinal multi‐omics data from participants, including metagenomics and immunomics, were integrated. Artificial intelligence models were applied to these diverse data types to predict clinical severity (Xiong et al. 2025). The BioMapAI technology identified relevant biomarkers, and the integration of multi‐omics data with AI modelling captured the connections between the microbiome and the immune system, enhancing understanding of their relationship. In the network of interactions between the gut and cancer neuro‐immunity, we aim to analyse the composition and functional dynamics of the gut microbiota using metagenomics (Liu and Wang 2025), and to investigate the mechanisms of neuro‐immune interactions through single‐cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics in neuro‐immune omics (Sankowski et al. 2024). Although each single‐omics approach has advanced microbiome research, integrating multiple omics provides a more comprehensive understanding of interactions between microbiota and the host (Yang et al. 2025). Artificial intelligence models also integrate patient faecal metagenomics, neuro‐immune omics and tumour transcriptomics (Xie, Liu, et al. 2024; Xie, Chen, et al. 2024) to build predictive models for cancer neuro‐immunity therapy (Mo et al. 2025), which may deepen our understanding of tumour immune evasion mechanisms. In gut‐targeted cancer neuro‐immunity therapy, combining multi‐omics integration with artificial intelligence is expected to predict the success of FMT and enhance treatment outcomes (Rozera et al. 2025).

Conclusion

In summary, the gut microbiota and its metabolites influence the neuroimmune processes of tumours by secreting neurotransmitters or neuroregulatory factors, which act on the autonomic nervous system or immune cells. At the same time, as tumours develop and progress, changes in the tumour neuroimmune metabolic axis also alter the composition of the gut microbiota. Therefore, the rational application of microbiota transplantation may help improve the efficacy of immune therapies such as PD‐1 inhibitors and CAR‐T. A deeper understanding of the microbiome characteristics in cancer patients may also provide new biomarkers for the early diagnosis of tumours.
