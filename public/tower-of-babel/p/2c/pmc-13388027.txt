Diagnostic Approach to Pediatric Collagenous Gastritis Based on a Case Series and Literature Review
Authors: Wonah Choi, Yiyoung Kwon, Yoon Zi Kim, Tae Jong Jeong, Soomin Ahn, Mi Jin Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeCollagenous gastritis (CG) is a rare pediatric disorder characterized by gastrointestinal symptoms and iron-deficiency anemia. Owing to its low prevalence and broad range of nonspecific manifestations, CG is frequently overlooked during diagnosis. This study aimed to propose a diagnostic algorithm for CG and systematically review the published literature.MethodsFour patients aged <18 years diagnosed with CG at a tertiary referral center in Korea between 2003 and 2023 were retrospectively analyzed. In addition, 73 pediatric cases reported worldwide between 1989 and 2024 were reviewed.ResultsAmong 77 cases, excluding one case without symptom data, 16 patients (20.8%) had no gastrointestinal symptoms other than anemia. Abdominal pain was reported in 37 patients (48.7%), gastrointestinal bleeding in five (6.6%), vomiting in nine (11.8%), and diarrhea in three (3.9%). Anemia was present in 62 patients, and 68 tested negative for Helicobacter pylori. Most patients demonstrated nodular or irregularly coarse gastric mucosa on esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD), although these findings were less apparent during the early disease stage.ConclusionCG remains a diagnostically challenging condition that requires a high index of suspicion. Early EGD should be considered in pediatric patients with recurrent gastrointestinal symptoms and treatment-refractory iron-deficiency anemia who test negative for H. pylori. Careful evaluation for gastric mucosal nodularity and acquisition of additional histologic biopsies are recommended in these cases.

INTRODUCTION

Collagenous gastritis (CG), first described by Colletti and Trainer [1] in 1989, remains poorly understood with respect to its pathophysiology, clinical course, and optimal treatment. Clinical manifestations are nonspecific and include epigastric pain, dyspepsia, vomiting, and hematemesis. Consequently, CG is frequently overlooked without appropriate endoscopic evaluation and histologic biopsy. Histopathologic findings include a subepithelial collagen band >10 µm thick accompanied by inflammatory cell infiltration within the gastric lamina propria [2]. Increased infiltration of mast cells and eosinophils may contribute to collagen deposition; however, the underlying mechanism, including the roles of inflammation, intrinsic cellular abnormalities, and autoimmunity, remains uncertain [3].

CG is distinct from collagenous colitis (CC) and collagenous sprue (CS). CC occurs primarily in adults and less commonly in children and is characterized by non-bloody diarrhea and subepithelial collagen deposition in the colon [4]. Although collagen deposition in CG may appear clinically indolent, a 12-year follow-up study of the index case by Winslow et al. [5] and Colletti suggested the possibility of secondary cellular injury and progression to carcinomatous lesions.

Because of the rarity of CG, randomized controlled trials are lacking, and no standardized treatment has been established. Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), iron supplementation, and corticosteroids remain the most commonly used therapies, whereas biologic agents have only recently been introduced [6]. Diagnostic evaluation is further complicated by overlapping clinical and laboratory features between CG and Helicobacter pylori–associated iron-deficiency anemia (H. pylori–IDA).

Between 2003 and 2023, four pediatric patients with CG were identified at our institution. This study presents a single-center experience together with a review of previously reported cases worldwide and proposes a comprehensive diagnostic approach for pediatric CG.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A retrospective review was conducted of patients aged <18 years diagnosed with CG between March 2003 and November 2023 at the Department of Pediatric Gastroenterology and Nutrition, Samsung Medical Center. Owing to the rarity of the disease, all patients with histopathologically confirmed CG were included. During the study period, 7,593 pediatric esophagogastroduodenoscopies (EGDs) were performed, and four patients were diagnosed with CG based on endoscopic biopsy findings. All biopsy specimens underwent Masson’s trichrome staining.

In addition, 73 pediatric CG cases reported in the literature from 13 countries, including South Korea, were reviewed. A PubMed search using the keyword “pediatric collagenous gastritis” identified reports published between the initial description in 1989 and 2024. Only cases with histopathologic confirmation of CG were included.

The diagnosis of CG was based on characteristic nodularity identified on EGD and confirmed by histopathologic biopsy. Irregular or coarse gastric mucosal nodularity, occasionally accompanied by hyperemia, hemorrhage, or ulceration, was considered suggestive of CG. The esophagus, duodenum, and small bowel were additionally evaluated to exclude differential diagnoses and identify coexisting abnormalities. Multiple biopsy samples were obtained from both grossly normal and depressed mucosal areas to assess subepithelial collagen deposition. Masson’s trichrome staining and gastric eosinophil density measurements were performed. Diagnostic laboratory evaluation included complete blood count, H. pylori antibody testing, and stool studies. Based on these clinical and histopathologic findings, a diagnostic algorithm for CG was developed.

The patients’ medical records were retrospectively reviewed after approved by the Samsung Medical Center Institutional Review Board. The requirement for informed consent was waived (IRB File No. SMC 2025-09-174).

Case 1

An 11-year-old male was referred for refractory iron-deficiency anemia persisting for 3 years. EGD demonstrated nodular elevations at the antral angle with friable, coarse mucosa in the fundus. This patient represented the first reported pediatric case of CG in South Korea [7]. Despite symptomatic improvement, CG remained localized to the greater curvature of the mid-antrum without substantial interval change. Between 2003 and 2023, the patient underwent 11 follow-up EGDs with biopsy.

Case 2

A 9-year-old male presented with an 8-month history of intermittent vomiting. EGD revealed diffuse gastric nodularity, and histopathologic examination confirmed CG with collagen deposition within the gastric lamina propria (Fig. 2). Follow-up EGD performed 6 months later showed no significant interval change.

Histopathologic findings of collagenous gastritis. (A) Hematoxylin and eosin stain, ×10 magnification (B) Masson’s trichrome stain, ×10 magnification. Arrows indicate thickened subepithelial collagen bands diagnostic of collagenous gastritis.

Case 3

A 15-year-old female had experienced hematemesis 2.5 years before presentation. An earlier EGD at another institution demonstrated gastric mucosal nodularity without an identifiable bleeding source. Repeat EGD at our center revealed diffuse nodularity with a small ulcer, and biopsy confirmed CG involving the pylorus (Fig. 1). The patient continues to undergo annual EGD surveillance.

Case 4

A 12-year-old male presented with pallor and iron-deficiency anemia. Initial EGD did not reveal definite nodularity but showed edematous gastric folds and multiple hemorrhagic lesions in the gastric body and fundus. Repeat EGD performed 2 years later demonstrated increased mucosal nodularity, and histopathologic evaluation confirmed mild subepithelial collagen deposition consistent with CG.

Review of patient characteristics from literature

Systematic review of combined cohort (institutional+literature cases)

A total of 77 pediatric patients, including 4 institutional cases and 73 literature cases, were analyzed (Appendix 1). Symptom data were unavailable for one patient, whereas 16 patients were asymptomatic except for anemia. The remaining 60 patients (78.9%) presented with at least one gastrointestinal or systemic symptom. Abdominal pain was the most common manifestation, affecting 37 patients (48.7%), with symptoms ranging from epigastric to retrosternal pain. Additional clinical manifestations included pallor, fatigue, palpitations, dyspnea, or tachycardia in 26 patients (34.2%); nausea or vomiting in nine (11.8%); hematemesis or hematochezia in five (6.6%); and non-bloody diarrhea in three (3.9%).

Overall, 67 patients (87.0%) had IDA, whereas 10 (13.0%) had age-appropriate hemoglobin concentrations. Histopathologic evaluation consistently demonstrated subepithelial collagen deposition >10 µm accompanied by inflammatory infiltrates. Most endoscopic findings showed nodularity and/or irregularly coarse gastric mucosa (n=67). Four patients (patients 4, 15, 60, and 63) demonstrated hemorrhagic or erosive gastritis with erythema, one (patient 26) showed gastric atrophy, and five (patients 18, 53, 65, 69, and 77) initially demonstrated normal or unremarkable gastric mucosa. Among these five patients, three did not undergo follow-up EGD. The remaining two patients (patients 18 and 53) subsequently developed characteristic gastric nodularity after 2 and 6 years, respectively. In addition, four patients (patients 4, 15, 60, and 63) initially demonstrated only edematous gastric folds, hemorrhagic gastropathy, or erosions before subsequent confirmation of CG.

Endoscopic reports for 22 patients described disease involvement as “diffuse” without specifying the extent of gastric involvement. Among the remaining 52 cases, 29 (55.8%) demonstrated antrum-sparing disease, whereas 23 (44.2%) showed antral involvement.

Testing for H. pylori infection was performed in 75 patients. Of these, 68 (90.7%) tested negative. Two patients initially tested negative and later became positive. Nevertheless, both demonstrated endoscopic findings more consistent with CG than with H. pylori–IDA (patients 4 and 12).

DISCUSSION

CG is a rare disorder that has remained poorly understood since its initial description in 1989. Among gastritis subtypes, its etiology is classified as “uncertain,” together with lymphocytic and eosinophilic gastritis. Consequently, standardized treatment strategies have not been established. Chronic inflammatory stimulation may promote collagen deposition, whereas impaired collagen degradation may contribute to disease persistence [8]. However, the reasons dietary restriction, PPIs, and corticosteroids frequently fail to induce remission, as well as the mechanisms underlying gastric localization, remain unclear. The lower incidence of CG in children and its distinction from CC, which occurs more commonly in adults, further complicate understanding of the disease. Although previous studies have suggested an association with autoimmune disorders, celiac disease and CS are exceptionally rare in South Korea, and no definitive relationship with CG has been identified. Therefore, the associations among CG, CC, CS, and autoimmunity remain unresolved.

Epidemiologically, CG demonstrates female predominance and occurs more frequently in European populations. Peripheral and mucosal eosinophil counts may play an important role, as recent studies have demonstrated upregulation of both T helper type 1 and T helper type 2 cytokines in CG mucosal biopsy specimens [9]. These findings suggest that targeted inhibition of Th2-mediated pathways may represent a future therapeutic strategy beyond symptomatic treatment alone. Although endoscopic findings differ between CG and eosinophilic gastrointestinal disease, potential overlap in therapeutic approaches warrants further investigation.

Multiple hypotheses regarding the pathophysiology of CG have been proposed. The theory that CG represents inflammation followed by tissue repair does not adequately explain all cases [10]. For example, the first pediatric patient diagnosed with CG in Korea (Case 1) has been followed for 20 years. Despite complete symptom resolution and normal laboratory findings, serial endoscopic and histopathologic evaluations have consistently demonstrated persistent gastric nodularity without interval improvement despite long-term dietary modification and medical therapy. This observation highlights the chronic and refractory nature of CG and indicates that symptomatic improvement does not necessarily correlate with endoscopic remission. Previous reports have suggested that pediatric CG typically spares the antrum, whereas adult CG more commonly involves the antrum [11]. However, this pattern was not confirmed in the present review, suggesting greater variability in disease distribution than previously recognized.

Analysis of 77 pediatric cases worldwide supports the need to consider EGD with biopsy in children presenting with unexplained IDA or nonspecific gastrointestinal symptoms. Persistent or recurrent symptoms despite PPI therapy, iron supplementation, or dietary intervention should further raise suspicion for CG. Initial diagnostic evaluation should include H. pylori antibody testing and iron studies, including serum iron, total iron-binding capacity, and ferritin levels, to confirm IDA. Early endoscopic evaluation should be considered in patients with unexplained IDA. During endoscopy, clinicians should carefully assess for gastric mucosal nodularity, irregularity, erythema, erosions, or hemorrhagic lesions, which may indicate CG.

Accurate interpretation of endoscopic findings and appropriate biopsy technique are essential for diagnosis. A diagnostic algorithm is proposed to facilitate clinical evaluation (Fig. 3). When gastric nodularity is observed in the absence of H. pylori infection, biopsy should be performed to confirm CG. Because H. pylori–IDA and CG share overlapping endoscopic features, eradication therapy should first be administered in patients with confirmed H. pylori infection, followed by urea breath testing, reassessment of anemia, and repeat EGD. Resolution of anemia after eradication supports a diagnosis of H. pylori–IDA, whereas persistent anemia raises suspicion for CG, particularly because both conditions may coexist. Furthermore, atypical findings such as edematous gastric folds, erythema, or erosions inconsistent with other forms of gastritis should prompt consideration of early-stage CG and warrant follow-up endoscopy.

The coexistence of CG and H. pylori infection remains poorly characterized. Although subtle differences in nodularity patterns may exist, concurrent disease is possible, and false-negative H. pylori results may occur, particularly during PPI therapy [12]. Therefore, biopsy, eradication therapy, urea breath testing, and follow-up endoscopy are essential to distinguish CG from H. pylori–IDA. Biopsy specimens should preferentially target depressed mucosal areas because nodularity may reflect pseudopolyposis or mucosal depression within inflamed regions [13]. Close collaboration with pathologists is critical for diagnostic confirmation and may prevent unnecessary investigations in pediatric patients.

Collectively, these findings emphasize the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for CG in pediatric patients with nodular gastritis. In particular, children with H. pylori–negative gastritis and refractory anemia should undergo evaluation for CG. Even in the absence of nodularity, erythematous, hemorrhagic, or erosive gastritis should not be disregarded because these findings may represent early disease. Follow-up EGD should be considered in patients with persistent symptoms or IDA, as several patients with initially normal-appearing gastric mucosa later developed nodularity and were subsequently diagnosed with CG. Differential diagnoses, including lymphocytic and autoimmune gastritis, should also be considered in patients with suggestive family histories or systemic manifestations [14]. In children presenting with marked postprandial symptoms, eosinophilia, or hypoalbuminemia, collaboration between pediatric gastroenterologists and allergists is essential to evaluate for eosinophilic gastritis. Whether IDA in CG results from malabsorption, occult gastrointestinal bleeding, or both remains unclear, and additional studies are needed to clarify its pathophysiology. Establishing standardized endoscopic criteria for grading gastric nodularity and evaluating correlations with IDA severity and growth parameters may provide important clinical insight. Future research should focus on elucidating the pathogenesis of pediatric CG and developing standardized diagnostic protocols and effective therapeutic strategies, including dietary interventions. Prospective studies and external validation of proposed diagnostic approaches are warranted.
