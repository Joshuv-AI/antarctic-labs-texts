Comparison of the characteristics of small intestinal fluid from white-feathered and yellow-feathered broilers
Authors: Mingqiang Song, Cong Ren, Youyou Liu, Xiaomeng Ye, Yuming Wang, Jingjing Xie, Feng Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe objective of this study was to compare the properties of small intestinal fluid (SIF) from white- and yellow-feathered broilers fed variable dietary CP to test the hypothesis that digestive enzymes sourced from the SIF of yellow-feathered broilers could serve as a viable alternative for preparing in vitro SIF specifically for white-feathered broilers. Ten Arbor Acres Plus broilers (32 days of age) and 20 Chinese yellow chickens (51 days of age) were fitted with jejunal cannulas and assigned to dietary treatments as follows: 1) white-feathered broilers (BW of 4.08 ± 0.39 kg) fed diet 1 with CP of 19.88 %, 2) yellow-feathered broilers (BW of 2.89 ± 0.27 kg) fed diet 1, and 3) yellow-feathered broilers (BW of 2.84 ± 0.20 kg) fed diet 2 with CP of 16.32 %. Each treatment contained 10 replicates with 1 broiler in each replicate. Digestive enzyme activities, ion concentrations, pH, 16 amino acid (AA, excluding Tyr and Trp) contents, molecular weight distribution (MWD) of protein, hydrolysis rates on wheat starch, corn starch, casein, and soybean protein concentrate, and in vitro digestible energy of corn, soybean meal, and corn gluten meal were evaluated for SIF. Activities of chymotrypsin and amylase, pH, concentrations (mg/mL) of total amino acid (TAA) and 13 AA (excluding Pro, Cys, and Glu), and hydrolysis rates in casein or soybean protein concentrate were greater (P < 0.05), whereas MWD of protein from 19 to 23 kDa was lower (P < 0.05) in the SIF of yellow compared to white-feathered broilers fed diet 1. Furthermore, activities of chymotrypsin and amylase, concentrations of Na+, and Cl−, MWD of protein from 15 to 18 kDa were greater, while concentrations of K+ were lower in the SIF of yellow-feathered broilers fed diet 2 compared to white feathered broilers fed diet 1. Activities of chymotrypsin and amylase, K+ concentration, pH, TAA and 13 AA (excluding Pro, Cys, and Glu), hydrolysis rates in casein or soybean protein concentrate increased (P < 0.05), while concentrations of Na+ and Cl− (P < 0.05) decreased with dietary CP in SIF from yellow-feathered broilers. This experiment demonstrated diet composition and breed modulate digestive enzyme activities, ions (Na+, K+, Cl−), pH, and AA concentrations in the SIF. Our findings revealed no substantial difference in the hydrolytic characteristics of SIF from white-feathered broilers fed diet 1 and yellow-feathered broilers fed diet 2. Consequently, it is feasible to use digestive enzymes extracted from the SIF of yellow-feathered broilers to prepare simulated SIF for in vitro digestion of white-feathered broilers.Keywords: Digestive capacity, Hydrolysis kinetics, Small intestinal fluid, White-feathered broiler, Yellow-feathered broiler

Introduction

Digestive enzymes enable researchers to simulate the digestion processes of the gastrointestinal tract of animals. The efficacy of simulation depends on the characteristics of main digestive enzymes (Oguchi et al., 2022; Rathi et al., 2024). Zhang et al. (2019) described that small intestinal fluid (SIF) prepared with digestive enzymes extracted from in vivo SIF can improve the accuracy of in vitro digestion models of duck compared to the digestive enzymes derived from pig and microbe. This indicates that the digestive enzymes from different animal species can greatly affect the effectiveness of simulated digestion to evaluate the nutrients digestibility in feed. The fast-growing white-feathered broilers and Chinese yellow-feathered broilers are 2 breeds of chicken with different growth rates. However, fast-growing white-feathered broilers lack stress resistance. Consequently, it is difficult to obtain in vivo SIF by cannulation in quantities adequate for extraction of digestive enzymes. Previous studies have shown that Chinese yellow-feathered broilers grow more slowly but are more stress-resistant than fast-growing white-feathered broilers (Ju et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2016). This prompts our hypothesis that digestive enzymes sourced from the SIF of yellow-feathered broilers could serve as a viable alternative for preparing in vitro SIF specifically for white-feathered broilers.

Few researchers have conducted rigorous comparisons of the chemical composition and properties of SIF across breeds of broilers. Talibov (2017) observed no differences in the hydrolysis kinetics of wheat starch catalyzed by α-amylase from pigs and yellow-feathered broilers. However, Xu (2021) demonstrated a greater ability for porcine-derived pancreatin to hydrolyze feed protein substrates that derived from SIF of yellow-feathered broilers when the activities of main digestive enzymes were identical. There was difference in the molecular weight of chymotrypsin between porcine pancreatin and that extracted from SIF of yellow-feathered broilers (Xu, 2021), indicating that there were species-specific variations in composition and properties of digestive enzymes. Furthermore, the dietary nutrient contents influenced both the chemical composition and the enzymatic characteristics of SIF of broilers. Ye et al. (2022) and Ye (2022) have reported that dietary CP levels influenced the activities of digestive enzymes and the concentrations of Na+, K+, and Cl− in SIF of broilers. Ghasemi et al. (2021) reported a positive relationship between dietary CP and activities of total protease and trypsin in intestinal chyme for broilers. These results indicate the chemical composition and characteristics of digestive enzymes can be influenced by both species of animal and diet composition, but the effect of breed or diet and its relationship with composition of SIF should be further investigated. Therefore, the objective of this study was to compare the digestive enzyme activities, ion concentrations, pH, 16 amino acid (AA, excluding Tyr and Trp) contents, molecular weight distribution (MWD) of protein, hydrolysis kinetics on starch and protein, as well as the in vitro digestible energy (IVDE) on feed ingredients in SIF from fast-growing white-feathered broilers and Chinese yellow-feathered broilers. These findings will provide insight into the suitability of using digestive enzymes from SIF of yellow feathered broilers to develop a new simulated digestion technique to determine nutrient digestibility of feed for white-feathered broilers. Its potential application is beneficial for rapidly and accurately evaluating the nutritional value of feed and improving the performance of broilers.

Materials and methods

All experimental procedures related to the use of live broilers were approved by the animal care and welfare committee of the Institute of Animal Science, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Beijing, China). The code of ethical inspection was IAS 2022-96.

Animal management and experimental diets

Ten Arbor Acres Plus broilers (32 days of age; market age of 42 days) and 20 Chinese yellow chickens (51 days of age; market age of 48 days) at the same relative growth phase were selected and housed individually in cages (0.9 × 0.4 × 0.5 m) under 16 h of light per day. A silicon T-shaped cannula was installed at 1 to 2 cm anterior to the Meckel's diverticulum (Zhao et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2021). Each cannulated broiler was fed 20 mL of 10 % (wt/vol) dextrose solution 3 times daily, the surgical site was disinfected for 7 days after the operation, and subsequently birds were offered free access to water and feed. Experimental diets 1 and 2 were formulated according to the nutrient requirements of Arbor Acres Plus broiler (Arbor Acres, 2019) or Chinese yellow chickens (China Agricultural Industry Standard, 2020), respectively (Table 1). After an adaptation period of 23 days, the experimental period of 20 days was dedicated to the collection of jejunal digesta.

Experimental design

The SIF was collected from 3 different experimental groups: white-feathered broilers (BW of 4.08 ± 0.39 kg) fed diet 1 with CP of 19.88 % (WFB-CP19.88 %), yellow-feathered broilers (BW of 2.89 ± 0.27 kg) fed diet 1 (YFB-CP19.88 %), and yellow-feathered broilers (BW of 2.84 ± 0.20 kg) fed diet 2 with CP of 16.32 % (YFB-CP16.32 %). Diets were assigned in a completely randomized manner within breed. Each treatment contained 10 replicates with 1 broiler in each replicate as the experimental unit.

Sample collection

Jejunal digesta was continuously collected from 09:30 to 19:30 every other day during the experimental period through the cannulas. Digesta was centrifugated at 1250 × g and 4 °C for 10 minutes according to the protocol outlined by Zhao et al. (2007) to prepare SIF. Subsequently, SIF was stored in a bottle at -20 °C for later determination of enzyme activities, ion concentrations, pH, AA contents, MWD of protein, and the hydrolysis capacity with feed ingredients.

Determination of digestive enzyme activities, ion concentrations, pH, and AA contents in the SIF

The amylase activity was determined using 3, 5-dinitrosalicylic acid to develop color according to the method described by Dahlqvist (1962). The trypsin activity was determined by the hydrolysis kinetics of p-toluenesulfonic acid-L-arginine ethyl ester described by Wrint (1974a). The activity of chymotrypsin was determined by hydrolysis kinetics of benzoyl-L-tyrosine ethyl ester described by Wrint (1974b). Digestive enzyme activity was expressed as Units/mL (U/mL). Concentrations of Na+, K+ and Cl− (mmol/L) were analyzed by Easy-Lyte Plus electrolyte analyzer (Madica, UK). The pH of SIF was measured by portable pH meter (HI 99163) at 41 °C. The AA content was determined according to GB/T 18246-2019 (China National Standards, 2019).

Molecular weight distribution of protein in the SIF

To assess MWD, SIF was mixed with SDS-PAGE loading buffer at a ratio of 4:1 (V/V) to prepare a 0.2 mL of sample. After shaking and mixing, samples were placed in a 95 °C metal bath for 5 minutes. Based on a benchmark of 0.1 mg of AA in the SIF, a quantitative amount of SIF was injected into the gel well (GenScript, 4-20 % Mops resolving gel). Electrophoresis was performed at 110 V until the loading buffer dye reached the bottom of the gel. The protein bands in the gel were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue for 30 minutes. The gel was decolorized using a solution of methanol: acetic acid: water = 1:1:8. Protein band imaging was performed using the GS-900 Calibrated Densitometer (Bio-Red Laboratories, CA, USA) imaging system. The molecular weight of the target protein was determined by comparing the target protein band with the standard protein band (Biostep TM Prestained Protein Marker, Tanon, 180-6003).

Hydrolysis kinetics of SIF on substrates

The hydrolysis kinetics of starch by SIF were determined according to procedures described by Dahlqvist (1962). A 2 % (wt/vol) solution of corn or wheat starch was prepared. Amylase activities in the SIF from different broilers were diluted to achieve activity of 0.23 U/mL and then completely mixed with the substrate solutions. After hydrolysis at 41 °C for 3, 6, 10, 20, and 30 min, respectively, the reaction was terminated using 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid. The amount of reducing sugar released was determined by measuring the absorbance at 530 nm. The hydrolysis kinetics of SIF on protein substrates were determined as follows: 1 % (wt/vol) solutions of casein or soybean protein concentrate was prepared. The total activities of trypsin and chymotrypsin in SIF were diluted to 2.20 U/mL and then completely mixed with the substrate solutions. After hydrolysis at 41 °C for 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 min, respectively, the reaction was terminated using 2 mL of 0.4 mol/L trichloroacetic acid solution. Mixtures were incubated in a water bath at 41 °C for 10 min, rapidly cooled to room temperature, and filtered. One mL of the filtrate was mixed with 5.0 mL of 0.4 mol/L sodium carbonate solution and 1.0 mL of Folin reagent, shaken, and incubated in a water bath at 41 °C for 20 min. After rapid cooling to room temperature, the absorbance was measured at 680 nm. The content of phenolic amino acids produced by enzymatic hydrolysis was calculated from a tyrosine standard curve. The procedures and solution preparations were in accordance with methods for the determination of protease activity (China National Standards, 2012).

In vitro digestive capacity of SIF on feed ingredients

The in vitro digestive capacity of SIF on corn, soybean meal, or corn gluten meal was determined by a computer-controlled simulated digestion system described by Zhao et al. (2014). The buffer solution was prepared based on the measured ion concentrations and pH of the SIF from each treatment. The digestion duration was 6 h according to the retention time of digesta in broilers (Ye et al., 2022), and was followed by a 12 h wash. After washing, the undigested residues in the dialysis tubing were transferred to a pre-weighted glass vessel. The residues were dried at 65 °C for 16 h, then dried at 105 °C for 5 h to achieve a constant weight. The gross energy of the undigested residues was measured by an adiabatic calorimeter (Parr 6400; Parr Instrument Co., Moline, IL). The blank of residue from the SIF was determined by the similar in vitro digestion procedure. The in vitro digestible energy (IVDE) and in vitro energy digestibility were calculated according to the formula described by Yu et al. (2021).

Statistical analysis

Summary statistics were calculated using the MEANS procedure of SAS 9.4 (SAS Inst. Inc., Cary, NC). Outliers were excluded based on the interquartile range criterion. Data were analyzed using the GLM procedure for a one-way ANOVA. Tukey-Honest significant difference was used for multiple comparisons of means. The REG procedure was used to analyze the first-order enzyme kinetics reaction. Statistical significance was declared when P < 0.05.

No statistical differences were observed in trypsin activity of SIF across the 3 treatments (Table 2). However, the activities of chymotrypsin and amylase were lower in SIF of white-feathered broilers fed diet 1 compared to other 2 treatments (P < 0.05), and greater for yellow-feathered broilers fed diet 1 than diet 2 (P < 0.05). Concentrations of Na+, K+, and Cl− were not statistically different in SIF from white or yellow-feathered broilers fed diet 1. However, the SIF from yellow-feathered broilers fed diet 2 contained greater concentrations of Na+ and Cl− (P < 0.05) and lower K+ concentration (P < 0.05) compared to those fed diet 1. Effects of broiler breed on the hydrolysis kinetics of digestive enzymes in the SIF

When the amylase activities in the SIF of the 3 treatments were adjusted to the same activity, first-order enzyme kinetics were observed on starch (from corn and wheat) within 30 minutes of hydrolysis (Fig. 2a and b). The cumulative release of maltose linearly increased with hydrolysis time (R² > 0.99, P < 0.01). There were no statistical differences in the hydrolysis rates in corn or wheat starch in SIF across treatments. Similarly, first-order enzyme kinetics were also observed on the casein and soybean protein concentrate within 30 minutes of hydrolysis by SIF (Fig. 2c and d). The cumulative release of phenolic amino acids linearly increased with hydrolysis time (R² > 0.99, P < 0.01). Hydrolysis rates of casein or soybean protein concentrate by SIF were slower for white compared to yellow-feathered broilers fed diet 1, but there was no difference for yellow-feathered broilers fed diet 2 (P < 0.05).

Discussion

The physicochemical properties of SIF in broilers varied across breeds and diets were investigated in the current study. These findings revealed greater activities of digestive enzymes and pH in SIF of yellow compared to white-feathered broilers when they fed the same diet. Similarly, others demonstrated greater activities of amylase, trypsin, chymotrypsin, and pH for yellow compared to white-feathered broilers (1.43, 1.06, 1.13, and 1.44 times, respectively, Ren et al., 2012; Ye et al., 2022). Such differences likely relate to greater average daily feed intake for white compared to yellow-feathered broilers. Ravindran and Abdollahi (2021) described increased feed intake surpassed the rate of digestive enzyme secretion and diluted the activities of digestive enzymes and pH per unit of SIF. Dietary nutrient contents or composition were another factors affecting the activities of digestive enzyme and pH of SIF (Zhao et al., 2007; Zheng et al., 2009; Zhao et al., 2010; Ren et al., 2012). In the current study, diets 1 and 2 were formulated according to the specific feeding standards for white-feathered broilers and yellow-feathered broilers, respectively. These 2 diets contained comparable metabolizable energy (3,047 kcal/kg vs. 3,021 kcal/kg) but differed in CP levels (19.88 % vs. 16.32 % for diet 1 and 2). The activities of amylase and chymotrypsin were greater for yellow-feathered broilers fed diet 1 vs. 2 indicating a positive relationship between dietary CP level and enzyme activity in SIF. This result concurs with reports by Fisinin et al. (2017). Our findings revealed no relationship between dietary CP and trypsin activity, which agrees with findings reported by Ding et al. (2021) and Ye et al. (2022) suggesting the activity of trypsin in SIF is not modulated by dietary CP.

Ion concentrations of SIF were influenced by diet composition, physiological development, and microbial activity in broilers (Wang et al., 2010; Ye et al., 2022). In the current study, the high proportion of soybean meal in the high-CP diet (diet 1) provided a richer source of K+ which led to increased K+ and decreased Na+ concentration in the SIF relative to the low-CP diet (diet 2). The protein in SIF consists of digestive enzymes, mucin, soluble dietary protein, and hydrolyzed protein from these sources. The pH and TAA concentration of SIF increased with increasing dietary CP for yellow-feathered broilers in the current study, and this may be linked to increased dietary soybean meal and basic amino acids, such as lysine, histidine and arginine relative to the low-CP diet. This change not only raised the pH of SIF, but also enhanced the activities of protease thus contributing to greater AA concentrations in SIF. Our results agree with observations by Yang et al. (2000) and Chrystal et al. (2020).

In the current study, the volume and proportion of protein in the SIF was independent of the dietary CP for yellow-feathered broilers. However, the volume and proportion of protein with lower molecular weight were greater for yellow compared to white-feathered broilers indicating the MWD of protein in SIF is breed-specific. Others reported greater volume and proportion of protein with lower molecular weight led to higher AA digestibility in yellow compared to white-feathered broilers (Xie et al., 2017). Yoshii et al. (2018) and Xu (2021) reported molecular weights of 55 kDa for amylase and 25 kDa for trypsin from porcine pancreas, which are similar to those in the SIF of broilers. The current study found comparable molecular weights across treatments for amylase, trypsin and chymotrypsin indicating the molecular weights of main digestive enzymes in SIF are independent of the broiler breed and diet. The hydrolytic capacity of SIF in broiler on feed substrates depends on the activities and the specific characteristics of digestive enzymes. In the determination of amylase, trypsin or chymotrypsin activity, the substrates were soluble starch (Dahlqvist, 1962), p-toluenesulfonic acid-L-arginine ethyl ester (Wrint, 1974a), or benzoyl-L-tyrosine ethyl ester (Wrint, 1974b). However, these substrates were different from the diet that served as the substrate in the body, so the activity of digestive enzyme could not reflect the true effectiveness of SIF on the hydrolysis of the diet. In addition, the sources of digestive enzymes could affect their specific characteristics. Xu (2021) observed the hydrolytic capacity of SIF from yellow-feathered broilers significantly differed from simulated SIF with reagent grade digestive enzymes (amylase from microbe, trypsin and chymotrypsin from pigs) despite equivalent activity of amylase, trypsin, and chymotrypsin suggesting an effect of enzyme source on the effectiveness of simulated SIF. In the current study, the hydrolysis rates of protein and starch were similar for SIF from white-feathered broilers fed high-CP diet (diet 1 with CP of 19.88 %) and yellow-feathered broilers fed normal-CP diet (diet 2 with CP of 16.32 %). However, hydrolysis rates increased with dietary CP for the SIF of yellow-feathered broilers in the current study because the dietary CP promoted secretion of chymotrypsin from the pancreas, thereby increasing the rate of protein hydrolysis (Zhang et al., 2019). This indicates that when yellow-feathered broilers are fed diet 1 and white-feathered broilers are fed diet 2, respectively, the major digestive enzymes in the SIF have similar enzymatic characteristics. In the current study, there was no statistical difference in the IVDE and energy digestibility of each corn, soybean meal, and corn gluten meal across SIF from the 3 treatments. This suggests that the digestive capacity of the SIF was similar regardless of breed and diet when the ratio of feed to SIF was consistent with the solid-liquid ratio of chyme in the body, and the digestion time was equal to the passage time of chyme. Consequently, the enzymatic characteristics of digestive enzymes are comparable in the SIF of white-feathered broilers fed diet 1 and yellow-feathered broilers fed diet 2.

Using digestive enzymes to simulate in vivo digestion was widely applied to evaluate the nutritional values of feed in pigs and roosters (Yu et al., 2021; Du et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2023). In the development of in vitro digestion procedure, the activities and sources of digestive enzyme as well as digestion conditions such as composition and pH of buffer, and time of enzymatical reaction in each phase were the key points (Boisen and Eggum, 1991; Zhang et al., 2019). These factors significantly influenced the relationship between digestible nutrient contents of diets determined by in vitro digestion and in vivo bioassay or growth response of animals (Peng et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022). This indicates that the development of in vitro digestion models must be make the in vitro digestive reactions at each stage as close as possible to in vivo digestion. Digestive enzymes were the most important materials to establish a new in vitro digestion technique. However, there was obvious species-specific variations in property of digestive enzymes (Zhang et al., 2019; Xu, 2021), which means commercial digestive enzymes from pigs or microbe are not the best material to simulate the digestion of feed for broilers. The current study showed there was a close property of digestive enzymes from 2 breeds of broiler, which indicates the digestive enzymes from 1 breed of broiler could be suitable to simulate the digestion for all other breeds of broiler. Therefore, using digestive enzymes from broilers for in vitro digestion is beneficial to improve the accuracy of predicting nutrient digestibility in feed for broilers. However, further validations are needed to successfully use digestive enzymes from yellow-feathered broilers to develop in vitro digestion methods for all other breeds of broiler.

Conclusions

This experiment demonstrated that the digestive enzyme activities, ions (Na+, K+, Cl−), pH, and AA concentrations in the SIF were influenced by broiler breed or dietary composition. Our findings revealed no substantial difference in the hydrolytic characteristics of SIF from white-feathered broilers fed diet 1 and yellow-feathered broilers fed diet 2. Consequently, it is feasible to use digestive enzymes extracted from the SIF of yellow-feathered broilers to prepare simulated SIF for in vitro digestion of white-feathered broilers. These findings will provide insight of using digestive enzymes from SIF of yellow feathered broilers to develop a new simulated digestion technique to more accurately determine nutrient digestibility of feed for white-feathered broilers. Its potential application is beneficial for accurate supply of nutrients and improving the production of broiler.
