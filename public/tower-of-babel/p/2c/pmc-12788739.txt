Revision to Tracheostomy Postcricothyroidotomy is Associated with Increased Rates of Laryngotracheal Stenosis
Authors: Sonia M. Slusarczyk, Blake Miller, Rachel Sarah Morris, Patrick Murphy, Lewis B. Somberg, Daniel N. Holena
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Cricothyroidotomy establishes an emergency surgical airway, but concerns persist that cricothyroidotomy only (CO) may be associated with higher rates of tracheal stenosis than revision to tracheostomy (RT). We characterized the incidence of RT and rates of tracheal stenosis.Methods:We identified patients aged 19–90 years of age undergoing cricothyroidotomy in the TriNetX database from January 1, 2011 to December 31, 2020. We divided patients into CO or RT groups. The primary outcome of interest was 1-year cumulative incidence of tracheal stenosis.Results:Six hundred and sixty-eight patients underwent cricothyroidotomy (mean age 50 standard deviation 19 years, 76% male, 34% with cardiac arrest, and 29% head injury) of whom 355 (53.1%) underwent RT. Accounting for 1-year mortality (56.7% CO vs. 34.5% RT, P < 0.001), the 1-year cumulative incidence of tracheal stenosis was higher in the RT group (13.0% vs. 4.9%, P < 0.001).Conclusion:Cumulative incidence of 1-year mortality is high in patients undergoing cricothyroidotomy, while RT is associated with increased risk of tracheal stenosis.

INTRODUCTION

Cricothyroidotomy is an emergent procedure used to establish a surgical airway in patients who cannot be endotracheally intubated and cannot be ventilated or oxygenated-representing a “cannot intubate, cannot ventilate/oxygenate” scenario-are in a life-threatening emergency. The compromised airway is a life-threatening emergency.[1] To reduce the risk of hypoxic injury, airway guidelines recommend cricothyrotomy after failed intubation attempts or a cannot intubate and cannot oxygenate situation.[2] Cricothyrotomy is a widely accepted method of establishing an airway as it is quick, effective, and relatively simple.[34]

Despite the lifesaving advantages of cricothyroidotomy, patients requiring surgical airways may require prolonged mechanical ventilation. Known risks of cricothyroidotomy include infection, bleeding, and longer-term complications such as laryngotracheal stenosis.[4] Because of perceived views of high rates of laryngotracheal stenosis with cricothyroidotomy, many have advocated for cricothyrotomy revision to tracheostomy (RT) within 72 h.[3] Recent literature has called into question the validity of this practice.[567] The conversion to tracheostomy is not without complications. Indeed, when compared to cricothyrotomy, RT may have greater incidence of subglottic stenosis and long-term complications.[18] However, the data for laryngotracheal stenosis are not consistent and many authors associate RT with lower incidence of laryngotracheal stenosis.[135] Given the rarity of emergent cricothyroidotomy in clinical practice, novel methods are required to investigate the comparison of a persistent cricothyroid endotracheal tube to a revision tracheostomy.

In this study, we sought to characterize the incidence of conversion to tracheostomy in patients undergoing cricothyroidotomy as well as corresponding rates of laryngotracheal stenosis using a large, multicenter database. We hypothesized that compared to cricothyroidotomy only (CO), RT does not reduce the risk of subsequent laryngotracheal stenosis.

METHODS

A retrospective cohort study using TriNetX, a comprehensive healthcare database and global research network that provides access to de-identified real-time clinical data from 71 healthcare organizations and research institutions across 24 countries, including electronic medical record and claims data was performed. All patients between the ages of 18 to 90 years who underwent cricothyroidotomy as indicated by the occurrence of clinical procedural code (CPT) 31,605 during a 10-year study period spanning January 1, 2011 to December 31, 2020 were included. Patients were excluded if they had any prior history of tracheal disease or tracheal procedures in the 2-year period before tracheostomy [Appendix A]. In the overall cohort of patients undergoing cricothyroidotomy, we sought to establish the incidence of subsequent tracheostomy within 30 days [Appendix B].

Patients were divided into two groups based on the primary exposure of interest: CO, indicating patients who underwent cricothyroidotomy without subsequent conversion to tracheostomy, and revision tracheostomy, defined for the purpose of this study as patients who underwent tracheostomy within 30 days of cricothyroidotomy. We compared the baseline demographics and diagnoses between the two groups. We limited our exploration to those diagnoses occurring in ≥10% of the group and those occurring with a TriNetX signal indicator strength of moderate or greater to represent diagnoses occurring in a majority of the group with a greater confidence interval. A signal of moderate strength indicates a 90% confidence interval between 1.5 and 3 (0.66 and 0.33).

The primary outcome of interest was the occurrence of laryngotracheal stenosis within 1 year after cricothyroidotomy, defined by the occurrence of an International Classification of disease or current procedural terminology code indicating laryngotracheal stenosis [Table 1]. Because many emergent surgical airways are performed in the context of other clinical situations associated with high short-term mortality (for example, cardiac arrest), we examined the risk of laryngotracheal stenosis in the CO and RT while considering death as a competing endpoint using Nelson-Aalen curves, a graphical representation used in survival analysis with competing risks analysis. All statistical analyses including competing risk analyses were performed using statistical tools within the TriNet X platform and Stata version 17.0 (College Station, TX, USA). As data were deidentified, this study was granted Institutional Review Board exemption.

RESULTS

A total of 1323 patients at 49 institutions who underwent cricothyroidotomy over the 10-year study were included, 655 were excluded due to prior laryngotracheal pathology or procedures leaving 668 patients. Patients were 76% male with a mean age of 50 (standard deviation 19) years. Associated diagnoses included cardiac arrest (34%), head injury (29%), and injury to the neck (20%). On average, the number of cricothyroidotomies performed in patients was 2.4 per month over the last 3 years of the study period. Of the 668 undergoing cricothyroidotomy, 355 (53.1%) underwent RT within the following 30 days with 94% of the 355 occurring within the first 3 days.

Baseline demographic characteristics were similar between patients undergoing RT or CO [Table 2], but there were some notable differences between the groups. Patients undergoing RT were more likely to have a diagnosis of cardiac arrest (50% vs. 19%, P < 0.001) and have traumatic injuries to the neck (25% vs. 15%, P < 0.001). In examination of the cumulative incidence of the competing outcomes of death and laryngotracheal stenosis, the rate of laryngotracheal stenosis was lower in the CO group than in the RT group (4.9% vs. 13.0%, P < 0.001), but the rate of mortality was higher in the CO group than in the RT group (56.7% vs. 34.5%, P < 0.001). The majority of deaths occurred within the first 90 days after cricothyroidotomy for both groups, but the cumulative incidence of death rose more steeply in the CO group. DISCUSSION

Conversion to tracheostomy after cricothyroidotomy was first popularized based on the work of Chevalier Jackson in the late 19th century, who observed 92.9% rate of subglottic stenosis in patients with cricothyroidotomy.[1] Because of these concerns, mandatory RT remains standard practice at some institutions. Contrary to this historic context and surgical dogma, our study found that cricothyroidotomy alone was associated with lower risk of laryngotracheal stenosis, although with increased rates of mortality within 1 year compared to tracheostomy.

Tracheal stenosis can be caused by a plethora of risk factors including prolonged intubation, neck trauma, surgical intervention, inflammatory, and autoimmune disease.[6] Comorbidities such as tobacco use, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and obesity increase risk for impaired wound healing that can lead to scarring and stenosis.[7] Patients with tracheal stenosis can suffer from impaired speech, swallowing, and dyspnea.[7] Treatment is aimed at voice and swallowing preservation through surgical excision of the stenosed tissue; however, rates of recurrence are high.[7] Although cricothyroidotomy and tracheostomy are life-saving measures, the effects of tracheal stenosis greatly impact the quality of life. As such, standard practice of conversion is aimed at reducing rates of laryngotracheal stenosis; however, based on our results, this conversion may not always be necessary.

While debate regarding the need for RT persists, there is a long-standing body of the literature that suggests it may not be necessary. In their 1984 study of 76 patients undergoing elective cricothyroidotomy for longer term airway access, Sise et al. found only 2 instances of subglottic stenosis, both in adolescents.[9] In a 2011 study of 95 patients undergoing emergency cricothyroidotomy, Graham et al. found that there were no clinically significant complications in patients undergoing decannulation after the indication for protected airway had resolved.[10] A 2022 meta-analysis of 1246 patients by Choi et al. found that the overall incidence of laryngotracheal stenosis after was not significantly different in patients undergoing CO versus RT and recommended against routine conversion based on these findings.[11]

While routine revision of cricothyroidotomy may not be universally indicated, certain patient populations could nonetheless benefit from conversion to tracheostomy. Early investigations by Boyd et al. suggested a correlation between the duration of prior intubation and the incidence of laryngeal complications in patients undergoing cricothyroidotomy;[12] however, this association was not supported by subsequent studies.[9] Although Jackson’s foundational work does not offer a detailed characterization of the patient cohort, his descriptions imply that some cases involved pediatric patients, particularly those treated for acute airway edema due to conditions such as diphtheria. Notably, Sise et al. reported that subglottic stenosis in their study occurred exclusively in adolescents, reinforcing concerns about the use of cricothyroidotomy in nonadult airways. Furthermore, the need for prolonged or permanent mechanical ventilation has been proposed as a justification for elective conversion to tracheostomy.[11]

In our analysis, we found that cricothyroidotomy alone was associated with rates of mortality three times greater than that of conversion to tracheostomy. Rates of mortality in cricothyrotomy are high in the literature with one study documenting survival rates of only 47.8%.[13] In an analysis of 76 critically ill patients who underwent a cricothyroidotomy, 61% died within 8.5 months.[14] The most common causes of death in patients with a cricothyroidotomy included cardiac arrest, brain hemorrhage, and hypovolemic shock.[13] We hypothesize that patients in the CO group had a worse prognosis and more severe injuries thus, they did not undergo conversion as is standard protocol.

As cricothyroidotomies are associated with high rates of mortality, the occurrence of death precludes the occurrence of laryngotracheal stenosis. Competing risks analysis is a model used to characterize the multiple events on a single outcome of interest.[14] Our primary event of interest was tracheal stenosis with mortality being a competitive event. Multiple statistical analyses exist to demonstrate this relationship such as Gray’s Test, Cause-Specific Hazard Function, and Cumulative Incidence. We demonstrated this relationship using a Nelson-Aalen curve, a graphical representation of the Nelson-Aalen estimator which estimates the instantaneous risk of an event occurring at a given time.

High-impact low-frequency events are rare occurrences that have considerable impact on patients. While infrequent, studying these events decreases errors and increases preparedness and success. Our findings underscore the difficulty of studying this high-impact low-frequency procedure, since the number of cricothyroidotomies performed at the 45 centers included in this study is less than 3 per month. Based on this sample size and the competing risk of death in this cohort, it is unlikely that a randomized controlled trial on the outcome of laryngotracheal stenosis will ever be performed successfully. Similarly, a prospective cohort study is not feasible; using TriNetX, which uses a retrospective data platform and pulls patients from multiple networks, allows for a large population to be studied.

There are several important limitations worthnoting. First, our data were collected through the TriNetX X database which gathers the patient data from Electronic Health Records. TriNetX does not provide specific patient or procedural details, limiting our analyses to general ICD and CPT codes. In addition, without direct access to raw data, we were unable to conduct further or independent analyses outside of those available within TriNetX. Given the low-frequency nature of surgical airway events, this allowed us to accrue a large sample size but at the cost of granularity. We were unable to determine the indications for procedures, including the decision to proceed with RT. It is likely that confounding by indication exists, perhaps explaining the higher mortality rate in the CO group as further procedures may not have been indicated in the setting of futility, such as hypoxic brain injury. Further, there are known risk factors for the development of laryngotracheal stenosis such as duration of mechanical ventilation, endotracheal and tracheostomy tube size, and other patient factors that we are unable to account for in this study. It is possible that some the decreased rates of TS seen in the CO group could be related to these factors. Prospective studies of rates of TS in patients undergoing CO versus RT are needed to further elucidate these issues. Finally, this work relied extensively on the use of CPT codes for the assessment of both exposures and outcomes and suffers the inherent limitations of this approach.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, our study found that cricothyroidotomy alone had lower overall rates of tracheal stenosis then the conversion to tracheostomy group. Decision to convert may not need to be standardized practice as it was not associated with improved rates of stenosis.
