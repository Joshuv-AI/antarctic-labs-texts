Evaluation of Porous Media Properties for the Performance of Sulfur‐Depolarized Electrolysis
Authors: Lukas Roessler Escudero, Ádám Székeli, Viktor Hacker, Merit Bodner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sulfur‐depolarized electrolysis (SDE) is an interesting alternative for H2 production. Lower operational voltage (<1.2 V) and a valuable by‐product offer an opportunity for economically competitive H2 production in sectors where traditional processes are not feasible or competitive. However, given its incipient state in development, several questions are yet to be answered to achieve stable, competitive operation. A definitive reaction mechanism for the anodic sulfur oxidation reaction has not been defined and therefore optimal properties for the diffusion media have not been established. In this study, the influence of various commercial gas diffusion layers (GDLs) on SDE performance and acid generation was systematically investigated. The choice of GDL was found to have a decisive impact on overall cell performance, in some cases determining whether stable operation was achievable. By optimizing the diffusion layer, current densities of 1.3 A cm−2 at 1.2 V were achieved, representing ≈30% improvement against state of the art. Based on observations during the study, a working hypothesis was proposed for the macroscale behavior of SDE during operation. These findings provide new insights into the anodic processes of SDE and identify critical parameters for developing efficient and durable electrochemical systems for low‐voltage, economical hydrogen production.

Introduction

Throughout the past decades, Europe—and the world—have shown an increase in interest for the establishment of a “hydrogen economy.” This translates to a projected demand for locally produced clean H2 of 10 million tons and another 10 million ton of imported clean H2 [1]. However, hydrogen production from electrolysis, to the date, still represents a minority fraction (1%) of the worldwide production [2] and the resulting costs make the produced H2 currently not economically competitive; 3–8 $/kg for “clean” compared to 0.5–1.3 $/kg for fossil‐derived hydrogen [3]. In this vein, sulfur‐depolarized electrolysis (SDE) has reemerged as an interesting alternative for the production of economically competitive clean H2 [4, 5]. Originally intended as part of the Westinghouse process [6] in the late 1970s, SDE is the process of electrochemically oxidize SO2 to H2SO4 (Equation (1)), electrolyzing water in the process, resulting in H2 production on the cathode of the cell (Equation (2)).

Thanks to the utilization of SO2 as the main reactive, SDE combines flue gas mitigation and H2SO4 production alongside H2 production, intrinsically adding value to the production process, therefore mitigating the cost of the produced hydrogen. Furthermore, the lower operational voltage directly translates in less specific energy consumption and eliminates the need for expensive iridium catalyst. Both these aspects currently represent the highest share in operational costs and capital cost, respectively, for proton exchange membrane water electrolysis (PEMWE) [7]. These conditions make the SDE technology an attractive alternative for H2 production in industries where SO2 abatement is necessary, providing a flue gas cleaning solution as well as two valuable products.

The SDE technology recently gained significant interest, after research was restarted by the Savannah River National Laboratory as part of the Nuclear Hydrogen Initiative by Colón‐Mercado in 2007 [8]. Since then, the interest has been gradually—but steadily—growing, with currently 5–10 articles published in the topic per year since, according to Nature research intelligence [9]. Despite this increasing interest, several critical aspects of SDE remain unresolved. While various reaction mechanisms have been proposed for the anodic sulfur oxidation reaction (SOR) [10, 11, 12], a definitive pathway has yet to be established. Furthermore—to the best knowledge of the authors—studies to date have relied on PEM electrolysis or fuel cell systems adapted for SDE operation, rather than purpose‐built SDE devices.

On the aspect of optimization of the SDE, the utilization of alternative proton exchange membranes has been previously evaluated; Colón‐Mercado evaluated sulfonated Diels–Alder polyphenylene (SDAPP) membranes as alternative to Nafion 115 and 212 [13]. Achieving 0.5 A cm−2 at 0.6 V proving competitive performance for in‐house alternative per‐ and fluoroalkyl substances‐free (PFAS‐free) membranes. Díaz‐Abad tested polybenzimidazole (PBI) and graphene oxide‐filled PBI membranes, proving performance of ≈0.4 A cm−2 with low SO2 crossover in intermediate temperature SDE electrolysis (110°C–140°C). Other, less conventional attempts have been made in an effort to achieve crossover‐free SDE; Stone et al. included an anode prelayer, reducing crossover sacrificing mass transport [14].

As well, multiple variations and combinations of catalysts have been evaluated including Pt, Pd, Rh, Ru, Ir, Cr, and Au, and combinations of the previous [8, 15, 16, 17]. In our previous studies, operational boundaries were established to ensure SO2 crossover‐free operation, without requiring component modifications to the system [18] and the use of gold as an alternative catalyst for the anodic reaction was proposed, and with it, a general reaction mechanism was proposed [19]. During these, the importance of the anode flow behavior in the overall operation was noted.

Diffusion media variations and combinations have been evaluated previously; at the Institute of Nuclear and New Energy Technology in Tsinghua, China, different assembly configurations and porous media were evaluated [20, 21]. However, both these studies were carried out using a liquid fed (H2SO4 + SO2(aq)) as anodic feed—which intrinsically changes the process, both complicating the process by necessitating extra steps, as well as several reports of modifying the system behavior—and multiple variables were simultaneously assessed, including flow field design, gasket type, and thickness were evaluated together with different diffusion media both in cathode and anode; therefore, assigning specific behaviors product of the diffusion media was hardly achieved. Alternatively, Steimke and Colón‐Mercado [22, 23] evaluated a wide arrangement of membrane electrode assembly (MEA) combinations. These combinations included type of membrane, thickness, diffusion media, flow field type, catalyst loading, and active area all at once. While the results of these studies show important advances in MEA fabrication, they still do not respond to the specifications of gas diffusion media properties, and which are optimal for gas‐fed SDE cells.

Given this background, the exact conditions inside the anode of the cell are still unknown, and therefore, the ideal gas diffusion layer (GDL) properties are yet to be defined. The unique conditions inside the electrode compartments of the SDE cell make this question particularly important. In the cathode, as much water as possible is needed to maintain proper humidification and induce osmotic drag to provide water in the anode side for the reaction. However, as H2 is being formed, it needs to be removed from it as efficiently as possible, to prevent drying out the cathode. On the anode side, gaseous SO2 is fed; however, it is not clear if the SOR is occurring at gaseous state, aqueous or dissolved in the formed H2SO4. In this study, we focus on the evaluation of a total of 25 possible combinations of commercial GDLs both from fuel cell and electrolysis applications, with the objective of assessing the optimal property combination, opening the door toward the manufacture of SDE‐specific GDLs.

Experimental Methodologies

The evaluated GDLs were all tested both in situ and ex situ, in an in‐house built SDE cell. The GDLs tested were selected to allow the possibility to “single out” the properties and attribute the changes to the modified properties as much as possible. In an ideal scenario, each GDL would be identical, each with just one property change; however, as this study is a first exploratory approach to establish design properties for future “tailored” SDE‐specific GDLs, commercial alternatives from Freudenberg Performance Materials and SGL Carbon are evaluated. In Situ Experimentation

All the in situ experimentation was carried out in an in‐house built SDE cell. The cell is constructed in a “sandwich” configuration, symmetrical from the PEM in the middle, except for the tested GDLs for each case. Figure 1 shows a generic exploded scheme of the constructed cell. From the outside to the inside, the components used are; stainless steel end plates (SS 316L) for structural rigidity with four holes for bolts in each corner. An insulating Teflon sheet was used to separate the end plates with the current collectors, gold‐plated copper current collectors, in house‐built graphite flow fields with serpentine‐type grooves and an active area, i.e., total area of each side's GDL, of 5 cm2, ice cube‐type sealing gaskets 35 FC‐PO 100 with a uncompressed thickness of ≈350 µm (Freudenberg SE), GDL selected for each experiment, from Table 1, and a catalyst‐coated membrane (CCM) with a 35 µm thickness and a 0.6/0.4 mgPt cm−2 loading in anode/cathode, respectively. Each experiment used a pristine CCM. The cell is assembled with 4 torque bolts at 3.6–4.0 Nm. After assembly, the cell is leak tested and evaluated for electrical resistance both between anode and cathode and between each current collector and its corresponding electrode to ensure correct and damage‐free assembly and proper tightness.

Generic depiction of the utilized cell, second current collector, insulator, and end plate not shown.

For the start‐up of the cell, our own established procedure is performed. Details of the start‐up procedure are given in our previous work [18]. In summary, to start up the SDE cell avoiding SO2 crossover and its induced degradation, the cell is operated in potentiostatic mode, as the reactive is never fed to the cell without an imposed voltage of >1.2 V, as this induces instant crossover. To start up the cell, 20 ml min−1 of ultrapure water (resistivity 17.6 MΩ cm, obtained with water purifier “E‐PURE,” BARNSTEAD) is fed to the cathode side with a microgear pump (ISMATEC) to humidify the cell for at least 1 h. During both humidification and operation, water is fed in excess, as only a fraction of said water will diffuse through the CCM to reach the anode and enable the reaction to occur, while the rest will be flushed out with the produced H2. Therefore, all the experiments start under a flooded anode condition. A voltage of 1.2 V is set using a Zahner electrochemical working station (ZenniumPro with a PP242 booster, Zahner Elektrik). Once the capacitive current decreases to a stable point (<50 mA and no change in current over time), the SO2 feed is set initially at 5 Nml min−1 controlled through a thermal mass‐flow controller (Bronkhorst High‐Tech). The system is kept until the initial current response stabilizes (≈800 mA for the initial flow). After a steady operation is achieved, the SO2 flow is increased stepwise, and the current response is recorded, until no corresponding response is observed. The last step recorded where the current response corresponds to the increase in SO2 flow, indicating the maximum operating point for the tested MEA. The procedure is done in this manner to keep the SO2 conversion at practically 100%, as this guarantees stable, crossover‐free operation. The cathodic product is separated from the cathode reservoir and passed through a desiccator to remove humidity, and dry measured by a thermal mass‐flow meter (Bronkhorst High‐Tech). The anodic product is collected in an anode reservoir and it is not recirculated.

In situ characterization techniques for SDE, unfortunately, do not translate one‐to‐one from other well‐established application such as PEMWE or fuel cells. Polarization curves are assessed as a measure of the performance of each combination, between 0.5 and 1.3 V versus RHE. However, given the intrinsic conditions of the SDE operation, naturally while the polarization curve is carried from 0.5 to 1.2 V versus RHE, the cell is operating under excess SO2 feed; therefore, crossover is induced and damage is made to the PEM. Consequently, for the case of SDE, polarization is not a nonintrusive characterization technique and thus was performed after the boundary experiment, maintaining the SO2 flow at the maximum value achieved during the test. Similarly, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed after the boundary experiments, in potentiostatic mode at 1.2 V versus RHE, with 10% amplitude and bidirectional sweeping frequencies from 1000 kHz to 100 mHz.

Ex Situ Experimentation

After experimentation, all the GDLs are evaluated ex situ both for pre‐ and postmortem comparison as well as to establish the characterization of each one, given that not all the properties are provided by the providers and often not in the same manner.

Mercury porosimetry is performed on each tested GDL using an automated low‐pressure mercury porosimeter (Pascal 140 Series, Thermo Finnigan) between 0.01 and 200 MPa to evaluate the porosity and mean pore diameter between the tested materials.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDX) are conducted to qualitatively compare the morphology and composition of GDLs before and after operation. Imaging is performed using an FEI XL20 microscope (Philips, Amsterdam) operated at 15 kV. EDX analysis is carried out to identify potential surface contamination (namely sulfur depositions); however, no significant differences nor depositions were observed between the pre‐ and postmortem samples (SI 1–14).

Contact angle measurements are performed to evaluate the wettability of each GDL. An Ossila L2004A1 goniometer (Ossila BV) is used to measure the static contact angle of ultrapure water droplets placed on the GDL surface. For each sample, three different positions are tested, and the reported value corresponds to the average contact angle obtained from the instrument software.

Product Characterization

Acid product collected during boundary testing is analyzed through acid–base titration using an automated titration device (Titroline 7800, SI analytics). 0.25 ± 0.001 mL of the anodic product is sampled and placed in a 100 mL beaker. The beaker is then filled with ultrapure water and titrated with a KOH solution 1.000 ± 0.001 M (Carl Roth GmbH). This procedure is repeated three times and averaged for each acid sample.

Results and Discussion

In Situ Testing: Symmetrical Gas Diffusion Layer Configurations

Initially, the five selected GDLs were tested in symmetrical configuration. From these however, combinations 2 and 3 (symmetric H24C5 and E35) did not perform altogether. The system was not able to achieve stable operation within the first stabilization step (5 Nml min−1 SO2). Therefore, the cell's current density did not even go past 100 mA cm−2. These results (Figure 2) already give a strong first indication of our iterative process for the definition of GDL parameters. Both failed combinations only coincide in the fact that neither has hydrophobic treatment. Based on our hypothesis presented in the introduction, one could expect that a hydrophilic diffusion media could be beneficial for the cathode side; however, having it in both sides simultaneously hinders the reaction, in this case, preventing it from occurring at all. Given that the operation with H24C5 and E35 was not stable enough to perform a boundary test, these results were not included in Figure 2A or B. The resulting polarization curves are shown in Figure 2C.

Symmetric GDL experimental run. (A) Boundary test, (B) EIS, and (C) polarization curves. EIS = Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy; GDL = gas diffusion layer.

Among the combinations that achieved stable operation, E35H, GDL10BA, and GDL39BB sustained steady performance at anodic feed rates of 22, 26, and 30 Nml min−1, corresponding to current densities of ≈0.6, 0.80, and 0.85 A cm−2, respectively. Notably, GDL39BB demonstrated the highest performance of the three and was the only GDL featuring a microporous layer (MPL). At first glance, one might assume that, given the complex reaction environment; particularly on the anode side, the presence of an MPL would impose excessive or unnecessary mass‐transport resistance. However, the results suggest that additional properties of the diffusion media play a significant role. These parameters, which must be assessed through ex situ characterization, likely contribute to the differences observed in operational behavior and help explain the superior performance of GDL39BB.

Lastly, for this series of experiments, the difference between the recorded impedances (Figure 2B) is significant between the carbon paper GDLs (GDL39BB and 10BA) with a distinguishable semicircle in the high‐frequency region (left) and diffusion element in the low‐frequency region (right) while the carbon‐felt GDL (E35H) shows sharp diffusion element that seems to “overshadow” all the rest of the phenomena, resulting in impedances orders of magnitude higher. This behavior suggests that mass transport, most likely at the anode, is significantly hindered when using E35H, likely due to its lower porosity and less favorable pore structure for gas–liquid transport. As with previous observations, ex situ characterization will be used to further interpret and confirm the origin of these differences.

It is important to note that the polarization curves (Figure 2C), as described in the experimental methodology, provide mainly qualitative rather than quantitative insight into SDE performance. This is due to the intrinsic operating characteristic of SDE, which ideally functions at near 100% SO2 conversion. When the cell is fed with excess SO2, crossover occurs almost immediately at lower cell voltages, causing a short, temporary boost in operation but placing the cell in a destructive operating regime. This explains the behavior shown in Figure 2C, where after reaching a current peak, the current response from the H24C5 configuration begins to decline.

While the trends in the polarization curves correlate with the relative effectiveness of the GDL combinations, these curves should not be interpreted as directly representative of steady‐state performance. Therefore, the boundary test results (Figure 1A) serve as the primary indicator of achievable and stable operating performance in this study.

In Situ Testing: Asymmetrical Gas Diffusion Layer Configurations

From the evaluated combinations, six GDL pairings were selected based on the performance observed in the symmetrical configuration tests (Figure 3). Each combination is consistently denoted as [cathode GDL] + [anode GDL]. Notably, the E35 + H24C5 configuration failed to achieve stable operation during the initial start‐up of the boundary test (5 Nml min−2) and was unable to reach 800 mA cm−2, consistent with the poor performance previously observed in the symmetrical experiments. Nevertheless, this configuration was sufficiently stable to allow EIS and polarization measurements (Figure 3B,C).

Asymmetrical GDL experimental run. (A) Boundary testing, (B) EIS, and (C) polarization curves. EIS = Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy; GDL = gas diffusion layer.

The impedance spectrum (Figure 3B) of E35 + H24C5 exhibits values several orders of magnitude higher than the other combinations, with an almost perfectly vertical response. This behavior has been previously observed, both by ourselves in previous studies [18] and by others under different scenarios where the electrode is either partially or completely blocked from contact with the reactive [24, 25]. This could therefore be explained by a dominant mass‐transport limitation, as H24C5 has a much finer microporous structure, originally designed for fuel cell operation, which is less suitable for the gas–liquid transport environment required in SDE. In contrast, the presence of a MPL on the anode improved performance in the symmetrical tests with GDL39BB. However, GDL39BB is significantly thicker, and during ex situ characterization revealed critical structural differences between its MPL and that of H24C5, which can explain the observed operational differences.

From the combinations that achieved stable operation, three—E35 + GDL39BB, GDL10BA + GDL39BB, and E35 + GDL10BA—outperformed the best symmetrical configuration (GDL39BB). The two others, E35H + GDL39BB and GDL39BB + GDL10BA, delivered notable performance, but did not exceed or only matched the reference level, respectively. These results allow several important hypotheses to be drawn.

As water transport from the cathode to the anode occurs exclusively via osmotic drag, maintaining sufficient water at the cathode is critical. Low‐porosity materials (e.g., E35H) or GDLs with MPLs (e.g., GDL39BB) hinder water retention when placed at the cathode and therefore negatively affect performance. In contrast, highly porous and comparatively hydrophilic GDLs, such as E35, promote water retention and efficient H2 removal, making them suitable cathode diffusion media.

On the anode side, the presence of an MPL generally provided operational benefits. It is important to point out that GDLs without MPL were significantly more difficult to assemble reliably, with fibers frequently piercing the membrane and causing electrical shorting during cell tightening. When proper assembly was achieved, the best anode performance was obtained using GDL10BA, whose lightweight, highly porous, and hydrophilic nature supports both acid formation and efficient mass transport, effectively acting as a “flow buffer” for the anolyte.

The highest overall performance was obtained with the E35 + GDL10BA configuration; result that initially appears counterintuitive. While E35 performed poorly when used symmetrically, its role exclusively as a cathode medium supported optimal water management. Provision of water exclusively from the cathode is rarely found in existing technologies and therefore finding comparable results in the literature is hardly accurate. Müller et al. proposed a PEMWE system with water fed exclusively from the cathode [26], observing an increase in performance and noting the porosity of the porous transport layer a major limiter on the performance. Considering that, during operation, E35 as a cathode media showed no signs of water starvation throughout the boundary testing, its pore volume and hydrophilicity might provide the better environment for water retention and H2 outflow, with minimal drag from the bubbles exiting the system.

Similarly, although GDL10BA was not the best‐performing symmetric configuration, its properties made it highly effective when used solely in the anode. Under simulation, tangentially similar observations were made for PEMWE by Shimpalee et al. [27], where the through‐plane permeability directly affects the redirection of the O2 in the anode. In the case of SDE however, the liquid media—H2SO4—presence in the diffusion media seems to be crucial for the performance of the cell, and therefore, redirection of the gas phase in the anode, in the case of SDE, could result in poor performance not only due to reactive loss, but also product of SO2 bubbles “dragging out” the anode H2SO4, destabilizing the anodic environment.

Hypothesis: Anodic Reaction Behavior and Ideal Operation

From both symmetric and asymmetric tests, several key indications emerge regarding the “ideal” properties of GDLs for SDE operation. Maximum water retention is achieved when a felt or woven carbon GDL is used on the cathode side, where high porosity and water permeability favor homogeneous water distribution and efficient H2 removal with minimal water drag. The inclusion of a MPL did not provide measurable benefits at the cathode; however, the complete absence of an MPL made assembly considerably more difficult and increased the risk of membrane damage. Consequently, the use of protective subgaskets between the GDL and the CCM might improve the assembly, preventing the edges of the GDL to be in contact with the CCM.

In contrast, the anode GDL exhibited behavior that, to the authors’ best knowledge, has not yet been described in the literature (Figure 4). During successful boundary tests, the anolyte outflow was consistent with a plug‐flow regime, in which the liquid acid flowed slowly, smoothly, and continuously, without SO2 bubbles (Figure 4B). When operation failed, the anode outlet instead displayed irregular, unstable flow, often accompanied by bubble formation or, in severe cases, splashing of liquid acid (Figure 4C). Even in experiments that initially appeared stable, the presence of bubbles at the anode outlet reliably preceded a sharp drop in current density, serving as an early indicator of performance instability.

(A) Hypothesis on the anode operation environment. (B) Anode outlet observations during successful operation. (C) Anode outlet operation during unsuccessful operation. (D) Current response during start‐up procedure of SDE cell. SDE = Sulfur‐depolarized electrolysis.

From these observations, we developed a working hypothesis to explain the behavior shown in Figure 4. As previously discussed, SDE operates stably under conditions of SO2 stoichiometric supply, where the anodic conversion approaches 100%. In contrast, operation with excess SO2 induces instability due to crossover and mass‐transport imbalance. Based on this, we hypothesize that the anodic diffusion medium must remain in a flooded state during steady operation. In this regime, the anode is fully saturated with H2SO4, through which SO2 diffuses to reach the catalyst layer, without displacing or “flushing out” the acid. This ensures the plug‐flow condition within the anode. When the SO2 flow rate increases beyond this equilibrium, even small excess is sufficient to “push” H2SO4 from the porous structure, destabilizing the system rapidly, resulting in a sharp current decay.

This hypothesis would help explain several previously unexplained observations. During stepwise start‐up (Figure 4D), the initial stage (5 Nml min−1 SO2) typically requires 10−20 min to reach steady state, while subsequent steps (2 Nml min−1) stabilize much faster. Similarly, large flow increments during boundary testing often led to abrupt current drops and apparent deactivation. Even at seemingly stable high‐current operation, visible bubble formation in the anolyte outlet often preceded gradual current decay, consistent with local SO2 oversupply disrupting the flooded equilibrium. Under this framework, even minor SO2 excess can progressively accelerate acid displacement, ultimately destabilizing the steady anodic environment established during start‐up. Furthermore, this explains the inability to “recover” the steady state once is lost, i.e., if a decline in current is observed when incrementing flow, the cell is normally unable to restabilize once the flow is reduced to the previously steady state.

To evaluate this, two cells are assembled with the same component configuration, and tested only varying the anodic flow between 28 and 30 Nml min−1 (minimum possible variation allowed by the mass‐flow controller as 1% of valve opening) and kept in operation at normal conditions: 80°C, 1.2 V versus RHE, while performing the same start‐up procedure (Figure 5). Both experiments have the same start‐up procedure and identical preconditioning.

Proof of concept experiment for the anode flow hypothesis. (A) Operation over 4 h, and picture of the anodic outlet. (B) End of testing EIS. (C) Anode product collected throughout the experiment. EIS = Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy.

As predicted, this slight change in operation translates to a significant difference, even in short‐term performance. Theoretically, both cells should have maintained steady operation at ≈800 mA cm−2; however, a change in the SO2 fed to the cell of only 2 Nml min−1 (≈7% of the total feed) is sufficient to destabilize the cell (Figure 5A), resulting in a current decay of ≈25 mA cm−2 h−1 over the short 4‐h span of the experiment (100 mA cm−2 total decay). No current decay was observed during the operation at 28 Nml min−1 during the span of the operation nor sulfur depositions in the cathode.

Interestingly, when performing EIS at the end of the 4‐h experiment (Figure 5B), the cell run at 28 Nml min−1 showed the low‐frequency impedance of 1.81 Ω, 137% over double the magnitude when compared to the 30 Nml min−1 of 0.77 Ω. While these results may appear negative toward the operation at 28 Nml min−1, there are several factors that affect the measurement of the EIS, that can explain this behavior. First, the spectroscopy is carried out to a lower limit of 100 mHz, while this value is normally adequate for most electrochemical devices; given the nature of SDE, it is possible that further diffusion effects, in a considerably bigger timescale, are not shown within these frequencies. However, due to the exponential increase of time in order to record values at 10 or 1 mHz would make the spectroscopy not only impractical, but unreliable as well, the cell operated at 30 Nml min−1 was not operating at steady conditions.

Most importantly though, the EIS in the experiment at 28 Nml min−1 is carried at anodic conditions where the reactant is significantly—if not completely—limited; therefore, a massive mass‐transport limitation' given that there is no more reactive to react, can explain the behavior shown in Figure 5B. On the contrary, the experiment at 30 Nml min−1 is, purposedly, not limited by anodic reactive.

Lastly, for this series, the anodic product was collected at the end of the 4‐h operation and before the EIS characterization. At the moment of collection, the anode side in the 28 Nml min−1 experiment showed the predicted behavior described in Figure 4B, while the 30 Nml min−1 showed a behavior closer to Figure 4C. It is relevant to point this distinction; both because it further validates our proposed hypothesis and directly affects the anode product measured. However, the pipes at the anode (and most likely the inside of the cell as well) were mostly empty for the 30 Nml min−1 experiment, whereas the 28 Nml min−1 had a completely filled pipe. Therefore, at the moment of removing the acid, it is likely that a non‐negligible fraction of it remained in the cell and not in the reservoir for the later.

Despite these differences, the total product for the cell at 28 Nml min−1 was slightly lower; 4.19 ± 0.03 g cm−2 h−1 against 4.33± 0.03 gcm−2 h−1 for the 30 Nml min−1. Yet the amount of pure H2SO4 collected, was slightly higher; 1.23± 0.009 g cm−2 h−1 against 1.21 ± 0.008 g cm−2 h−1. This resulted in acid concentrations of 29.3 ± 0.7% and 28.1 ± 0.6%, respectively. Considering that, in the case of the 28 Nml min−1, some acid is not accounted for as it remains in the pipes and cell, this configuration still managed to produce more pure acid. Analyzing the data of the H2 flow and the achieved current compared to the theoretical maximum values, the 28 Nml min−1 experiment achieved 95.83% Faradaic efficiency while the 30 Nml min−1 experiment only achieved 86.07%. This would both back up the superior performance of the flooded—starved—cell over the nonflooded, while providing further evidence that the remainder acid not accounted (≈3 ml of acid) is most likely found within the outlet anodic piping.

Ex Situ Characterization: Scanning Electron Microscopy Imaging

All the anode GDLs used for this study were analyzed and compared between a pristine sample and the used anode GDL sample (Figure 6). GDLs with MPL were analyzed both on MPL and fiber side, while the rest was analyzed only on the fibers. To properly portray the differences between the MPLs, 5000x magnification is needed to observe the morphology of the surfaces, therefore resulting in a rather large set of images. Image analysis provided key indications that help differentiate and explain some behaviors observed during the in situ testing. The MPL in the GDL39BB shows multiple “cracks” throughout its surface (Figure 6A1), each with average width of ≈44 µm. This morphology explains the performance observed in the symmetrical testing, and how this membrane still managed to provide good results when used both as a cathode and anode diffusion media. The presence of these “cracks” facilitates bulky transport from and into the membrane, while providing a “soft” surface that prevents the GDL from piercing the CCM and maintaining high contact area, as this would not be limited by the GDL fibers. Throughout the MPL area, there are visible pores of ≈0.1–0.2 µm in diameter (Figure 6A2). No major differences were observed between pristine and used on the fibers side (Figure 6A3).

In the case of GDL10BA (Figure 6B), only the carbon fibers are analyzed as there are no MPLs, no differences between pristine and used GDLs are visible. The difference in void spaces and rigidity of the fibers between the carbon paper GDLs (GDL39BB and 10BA) when compared to the much more densely packed and flexible carbon cloth GDLs (E35, E35H, and H24C5) is notorious (Figure 6C−E). This difference helps explain the complications faced during the assembly of the cells when using E35 or E25H, as the fibers are flexible and seemingly nonoriented, changes are higher of fibers piercing the membrane. On the contrary, the carbon paper GDL10BA fibers are all oriented in the same horizontal plane, making it difficult to find fibers in an angle to pierce the membrane.

The H24C5 GDL (Figure 6D), the worst performing, and only one that never managed to achieve stable operation shows a notoriously fine MPL, with no cracks in it throughout the totality of the surface analyzed (Figure 6D). These characteristics could explain the poor performance, water retention, and significant impedance observed throughout the testing. Furthermore, at 5000x magnification (Figure 6D), the pores on the MPL are not visible, indicating that these might be in the order of magnitude <100 nm. Given sulfuric acid's high viscosity ≈30 cp at the estimated anode conditions (80°C and 30–60 wt.% H2SO4), it is expected that diffusion will be limited in such a finely porous material. Furthermore, this GDL showed detaching of the MPL from the fibers postoperation (Figure 6D). During the preparation of the samples for SEM, every sample of the H24C5 was “fused” with the CCM.

The samples of E35 and E35H show similar results to each other (Figure 6C,E), with a very densely packed structure and almost no visible void spaces. Both these showed as well differences between the pristine and used samples (Figure 6C,E) with what appeared at first glance sulfur depositions; however, EDX analysis (SI 1–13) showed no sulfur agglomeration in the material. Therefore, these visual differences could be attributed to substrate material or additive in the GDL that aggregated during operation.

The characterization results were consistent with expectations derived from in situ performance and SEM observations. The carbon paper GDLs (GDL39BB and GDL10BA) exhibit roughly double the pore volume of E35, E35H, and H24C5, and also show the largest average pore diameters. Given that both belong to the same product series, this trend was expected. Their substantially higher porosity—and therefore larger void fraction within the diffusion media—likely explains their better ability to sustain the flooded acidic environment in the anode proposed in our hypothesis, facilitating efficient transport of H2SO4 into and out of the compartment.

Unexpectedly, E35 displayed a significantly lower total surface area than its hydrophobic counterpart; E35H. These differences may be explained by the dense packing of fibers in E35, although such a difference was not evident during operation nor in SEM imaging. An alternative explanation is that the intrinsic porosity of the PTFE used in the hydrophobic treatment of E35H contributes additional measurable surface area, thereby increasing the apparent value in porosimetry analysis.

Finally, the SEM observations of H24C5 were corroborated by the mercury intrusion results. Mercury failed to penetrate the MPL even at pressures up to 400 MPa. This strongly suggests that the anodic reactants—SO2, H2SO4, or both—would face similarly restricted diffusion through this layer. Consequently, the such a fine MPL is unsuitable for SDE operation.

Conclusions and Future Prospects

A systematic screening of possible diffusion media to establish the “ideal” GDL for SDE operation was performed. In situ results showed that an asymmetric combination between a “sponge like” hydrophilic carbon‐felt GDL—in this case E35—and a highly porous carbon paper—GDL10BA—might provide the ideal combination of properties for the operation of SDE, achieving 1.31 A cm−2 at 1.2 V versus RHE, approximately a 30% increase compared to literature's best reports.

It was observed during cell assembly that carbon‐felt GDLs without an MPL (E35 and E35H) were particularly difficult to install without risking membrane damage or short circuits. The presence of an MPL acts as a protective layer, preventing individual fibers from piercing the CCM. In situ testing with GDL39BB, which includes an MPL, demonstrated acceptable performance, reaching 0.9 A cm−2 at 1.2 V versus RHE when used on both the cathode and anode sides. However, this performance remains lower than that of the best MPL‐free cathode configurations. Specifically, current densities of 0.96 A cm−2 (E35 cathode, GDL39BB anode) and 1.17 A cm−2 (GDL10BA cathode, GDL39BB anode) were achieved. These results indicate that, although the MPL in GDL39BB provides sufficient water transport and practical advantages during assembly, it does not constitute the optimal diffusion‐media configuration for SDE operation.

After the screening, a working hypothesis was proposed, suggesting that, for ideal operation, the anode diffusion media requires to be flooded with H2SO4, creating an acidic environment that promotes stable operation; however, this state takes time to be created during the start‐up procedure and is very susceptible to be destabilized if SO2 is fed to the cell even at slight excess. To evaluate this hypothesis, two cells with identical configurations were operated at their maximum performance, but one with 2 Nml min−1 of excess SO2. Results after 4 h of operation showed ≈25 mA cm−2 h−1 decay in current for the cell operated in excess, while no decay was observed in the cell operated in starvation. These results support our proposed hypothesis, both visually and regarding the performance.

At the beginning of this study, the question established was: what are the required properties for the creation of an SDE‐specific diffusion media? After this exploratory approach, we have established that the requirements for such diffusion media are:

1.For the cathode side, hydrophilic media is capable of retaining as much water as possible, while enabling efficient H2 removal from the cell with minimal water drag to maintain humidification of the CCM and allow water diffusion through the membrane to guarantee enough water in the anode for the reaction to occur.2.For the anode side, the diffusion media must be able to retain H2SO4 and pose the least diffusion resistance possible, given the high viscosity of H2SO4.3.The inclusion of a > 0,2 nm pore MPL, with pathways for easy liquid transport, can have a positive impact during the cell assembly to prevent damage to the cell from the fibers, sacrificing, however, liquid transport resistance.

Altogether, these findings provide the first clear design guidelines for SDE‐tailored GDLs and lay the groundwork for the rational development of next‐generation diffusion media to enhance the performance, stability, and scalability of SDE. Future work will be dedicated to further prove our presented hypothesis and establish the ideal flow phenomena for the anode of the SDE cell. With these improvements, for the first time, tailored components for the SDE technology could be possible.
