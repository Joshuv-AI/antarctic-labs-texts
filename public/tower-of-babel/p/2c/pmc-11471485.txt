Short-term interactions between Longmaxi shale and carbon dioxide-based fracturing fluids
Authors: Guojun Liu, Delei Shang, Liang Zhang, Shuang Xie, Wenli Pan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The shale is a dense quarried rock material containing abundant shale oil/gas. Extracting shale gas usually requires fracturing the rock formation to speed up gas desorption and migration. Supercritical carbon dioxide fracturing, as an environmentally friendly and waterless fracturing technique of reservoir stimulation, gains increasing recognition in commercial exploitation of shale oil and gas. However, there is insufficient understanding on the interaction between carbon dioxide-based fracturing fluids and shale, as well as its influence on the mechanical parameters of shale, which are pivotal for determining injection parameters and forming the fracture network. This study focuses on the injection of carbon dioxide-based fracturing fluids to enhance shale gas extraction and carbon dioxide sequestration. Simulation tests of injection were conducted to investigate the influence of different fracturing fluids (deionized water, gaseous/supercritical carbon dioxide, gaseous/supercritical carbon dioxide mixed with brine) injected into shale reservoirs on the microstructure and mechanical properties of shale after short-term physicochemical interaction. This article adopts research methods such as uniaxial compression and uniaxial tensile mechanical experiments based on acoustic emission monitoring and various physical characterizations such as scanning electron microscopy, X-ray diffraction, X-ray fluorescence spectrometer, etc. Results show that following treatment with different carbon dioxide-based fluids, the uniaxial tensile/compressive strength, elastic modulus, fractal dimension of pore structure, and the brittleness index of shale exhibit varying degrees of decrease compared to those of dry untreated shale samples. After interacting with different carbon dioxide-based fracturing fluids, the mechanical parameters and brittleness index of shale decrease more significantly than that of the others as the addition of brine. This study shows that the water in carbon dioxide-based fracturing fluids is a controlling factor affecting the elastic modulus of shale. Additionally, the ductility of the shale increases and the acoustic emission emitting lag due to the coupling effects of brine and carbon dioxide. The change law of the elemental and mineral composition of the shale is consistent with the mechanical strength; and the change of element content and mineral composition is the most significant. Compared to gaseous carbon dioxide, the supercritical carbon dioxide has a greater impact on mineral composition of the shale. The change of mechanical strength and microstructure evolution mechanism caused by the short-term interactions between the shale and fracturing fluids provide theoretical references and implications for the determination of injection parameters and permeability transformation of the Longmaxi shale reservoirs.

Introduction

Shale gas, an unconventional natural gas, stands out as one of the most realistic alternatives to conventional oil and gas resources. With a technical recovery capacity of 26 trillion cubic meters, China's shale gas resources have enormous potential [1]. Utilizing carbon dioxide-based fracturing fluids, such as liquid, gas or supercritical CO2, emerges as a promising approach to stimulate shale reservoirs, resulting in efficient gas production and offering the potential for CO2 sequestration [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. In the stimulation process, the interaction between fracturing fluids and shale can influence the microstructure characteristics of shale [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]], and has significant effect on deposit state, seepage characteristics and migration modes of gas [19,20], as well as the mechanical parameters of shale [[21], [22], [23]]. Which are vital for the integrity of shaly caprocks and gas production after stimulating using the carbon dioxide-based fracturing fluids [24,25]. Shale is a sedimentary rock with a complex composition, but it usually has joints with small geometric aperture and permeability. It is mainly formed from clay sediments under high geostress and temperature, but it is mixed with quartz, feldspar debris, and other chemical substances.

However, there is a lack of sufficient understanding regarding the impact of multi-component carbon dioxide-based fracturing fluids on the mechanical properties of shale. Firstly, different minerals (including brittle minerals and clay minerals) in shale exert certain influences on the mechanical properties, pore-fracture structure, and CH4 adsorption capacity of shale. At the same time, different minerals exhibit disparate responses to temperature variations due to their distinct coefficients of thermal expansion. Consequently, temperature changes will significantly influence the pore structure of shale. Numerical studies have analyzed the microstructure and mineral composition of shale utilizing scanning electron microscopy (SEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), X-ray fluorescence spectrometer (XRF), BET specific surface area based on low-temperature nitrogen adsorption (BET), mercury intrusion porosimetry (MI), total organic carbon analysis (TOC), CT scanning, etc. [26,27]. Secondly, the presence of water and organic matter can significantly affect the adsorption capacity of shale [28]. The temperature and pressure conditions can influence on the water-rock interaction [29]. As temperature rises, the maximum volume expansion stress of shale decreases, manifesting in three distinct stages of strain: instantaneous compression, slow expansion, and stable strain [30]. Thirdly, extensive research has delved into the influence of hydration on the mechanical properties of shale, including freeze-thaw damage, moisture content, the mechanical properties after CO2-brine-rock interaction, and their respective anisotropic effects [[31], [32], [33], [34]]. Water-soluble minerals in shale, when exposed to water, undergo a weakening effect on the structure of shale due to water molecule infiltration and dissolution, reducing cohesion and strength but increasing the friction between particles within shale [35,36]. Guo et al. [37] conducted experiments involving CO2-water-shale reactions within a high-pressure vessel at 368.15 K and 15 MPa, which also investigated the influence of different water-rock contact modes in the reactions. The above studies examined the dissolved organic carbon in water and the morphological changes in rock surfaces before and after the reaction; The results indicated that under low water content conditions, the extraction amount of organic carbon by CO2 was 87 % higher compared to scenarios without water. In a word, current knowledge remains insufficient on the influences of carbon dioxide-based fracturing fluids on the mechanical parameters of shale, which is vital for numerical model of reservoirs evaluation and injection stimulation [[38], [39], [40]].

This study is conducted to focus on the injection of carbon dioxide-based fracturing fluids to enhance shale gas extraction and carbon dioxide sequestration. To further understand the interaction between carbon dioxide-based fracturing fluids and Longmaxi shale, as well as its influence on the mechanical parameters of the shale, section 2 will present experimental equipment, experimental program, and specimen collection and preparation. Based on acoustic emission (AE) monitoring and uniaxial compressive strength (UCS), section 3 will elaborate main contribution of damage and failure of Longmaxi shale, and investigate the uniaxial tensile strengths (UTS) before and after short-term interaction with the carbon dioxide-based fracturing fluids. According to results and analyses in section 3, section 4 will discuss the effect of carbon dioxide-based fluids on shale brittleness index, elemental content and mineral composition, pore structure of shale, and future research on shale gas production and CO2 sequestration. Finally, the study will summarize the change of mechanical strength and microstructure evolution mechanism caused by short-term interaction between the shale and fracturing fluids.

Experimental equipment and specimen preparation

The study region has continuous shale distribution, with a burial depth of 2–3 km and a thickness ranging from 100 to 500 m. The shale mainly deposit in a shallow-water to deep-water continental shelf environment, the Longmaxi Formation shale is composed of black and dark gray siltstone shale, matrix-rich (carbonaceous) shale, and siliceous shale interbedded with muddy siltstone. With an average total organic carbon content (TOC) of approximately 3.74 % and an average vitrinite reflectance (R.o.) of 2.4 %, the shale exhibits a significant level of organic richness, which is conducive to the formation of favorable gas conditions and gas storage space (TOC ≥2 %, 3 % ≥ R.o. ≥ 1 %).

The study program and procedures were presented in Fig. 1 to investigate the interaction between the shale samples and different carbon dioxide-based fracturing fluids. The study divided specimens taken from the same rock block into two parts; A part was drilled, cut, and ground to a standard rock sample of 50 × 100 mm for uniaxial compression test, with a specimen error of ±1 mm and a surface flatness of ±0.02 mm; The other part was drilled and cut into rock samples of 25 × 50 mm for Brazilian splitting test. Those processed specimens were further divided into six groups, as shown in Table 1. The development depth of shale reservoirs is usually over 1500 m. During the anhydrous fracturing process, the effect of groundwater may be encountered. A brine solution (10 % NaCl aq.) is designed to approximately simulate the geological environment of the reservoir to achieve research feasibility, and is used in the experiments to compare to dry, anhydrous, gaseous CO2, and supercritical CO2. Shale strata often act as an aquitard in groundwater distribution, and tends to soften when exposed to water. Using a 10 % NaCl brine solution instead of groundwater is more in line with the actual situation for this study. To prepare the CO2 + 10 % NaCl (aq.) fracturing fluids, the NaCl solution was first injected into the high-pressure chamber, followed by the introduction of helium gas at 7 MPa into the sample chamber to accelerate shale saturation. After 24 h of brine reaction, the helium gas was discharged, and the sample chamber was evacuated for at least 12 h. Results and analyses

Damage and failure behaviors of gas shale based on AE monitoring and UCS

From a geological perspective, eight main factors affect the UCS of rocks: moisture, organic matter content and distribution, microstructure, selectivity, mineral composition, bedding angle, porosity, and microcracks. From Fig. 3, the UCS of dry shale specimen was the greatest without any treatment, followed by treatment with gaseous carbon dioxide under 6 MPa drying conditions. Under brine or deionized water saturation conditions, the UCS of the shale decreased by 26.5 % compared to that of dry conditions. The influence of water on the UCS of the samples mainly lies in four aspects. First, clay minerals in shale undergo hydration expansion when exposed to water. Second, water can cause a 'slip effect’ in shale, reducing its cohesion and internal friction coefficient. Third, some minerals in shale dissolve in water, disrupting the shale microstructure. Additionally, water molecules can reduce crack propagation force. After treatment under CO2-brine-rock conditions, the UCS of specimen was lower than that of other treatment conditions. Reductions compared to dry conditions were 5.2 % (6 MPa gaseous CO2), 9.6 % (12 MPa supercritical CO2), 32.35 % (6 MPa gaseous CO2 + brine), and 29.33 % (12 MPa supercritical CO2 + brine). Notably, the UCS of the shale specimen treated with supercritical CO2 (12 MPa, 318.15 K) was lower than that of specimen treated with gaseous CO2 (6 MPa, 318.15 K), due to the dissolution of minerals in the sample by supercritical CO2, along with the rearrangement of particles within the rock mass [41]. After CO2-brine-rock interaction, the reduction in UCS observed in specimen treated with brine + gaseous CO2 (6 MPa) was slightly smaller than that of specimen treated with brine + supercritical CO2 (12 MPa), possibly because the geochemical and physical reactions that occurred after supercritical CO2 dissolved in brine are stronger than those of gaseous CO2, resulting in an increase in secondary quartz cement and an increasement in mechanical properties [42]. For example, the solubility of carbon dioxide in water is about 1.45 g/L at room temperature and pressure, but in the supercritical state, the solubility increases significantly to at least tens of grams per liter. As stress continued to increase, the rock sample entered the elastic stage, during which the stress levels were not sufficient to generate new microcracks, but sliding occurred between certain pre-existing closed fractures. Subsequently, During the latter part of the elastic stage, local stress concentration occurred within the rock sample, leading to the generation and propagation of new microcracks at the concentrated area. With continued pressure application the rock sample progressed into the post-destruction stress stage, where it underwent brittle failure directly without experiencing elastic-plastic deformation. Internal microcracks aggregated and connected under sufficient stress, leading to rapid fracture failure within a very short period, thereby forming a macroscopic fracture surface, and resulting in an instantaneous drop of the axial stress to zero. With increased stress, shale specimens enter the stable fracture stage. In the early phase of this stage, a small amount of AE occurs, possibly because the external stress on the shale is insufficient to create new cracks, but some previously closed cracks undergo shear slip, producing a small amount of AE. In the later phase of this stage, as stress continues to increase, new cracks form, and AE events significantly increase. As seen in Fig. 4, the stable fracture stage of shale specimens after interaction with different fluids is shorter compared to the original specimens. This is partly related to the delayed pore compaction stage after different fluid interactions and partly due to the reduced strength of shale after fluid interaction, lowering the stress value for failure damage. The stable fracture stage is significantly shorter in water-saturated specimens because of dissolution, hydration expansion, and water wedge effects between shale and water, transitioning the mechanical properties of shale from brittle to ductile, thereby reducing AE signals compared to the original specimens and shortening the stable fracture stage compared to pure CO2 fluids. The stable fracture stage is also shorter in shale treated with supercritical CO2 compared to gaseous CO2, due to the higher degree of damage caused by supercritical CO2. For specimens treated with CO2 + brine mixed fluids, the stable fracture stage is longer compared to deionized water-saturated specimens, due to the greater impact of CO2-brine-shale interaction on the microstructure of shale. However, it is important to consider the changes in shale strength after different fluid interactions when comparing the duration of the stable fracture stage across different specimens.

As stress continues to increase, pores within the shale expand and connect, causing the shale specimens to fail instantaneously upon reaching peak stress, with the axial stress dropping to zero, eventually forming a macroscopic fracture surface. During this stage, due to the expansion and penetration of microcracks, AE counts increase exponentially, reaching a maximum at failure. There is little difference in AE among different fluid interactions during this stage, and the duration of the unstable fracture stage also shows minimal variation.

AE is a typical physical phenomenon involving the release of elastic waves during the process of strain energy cracking, diffusion, and destruction in rock fractures in materials, and it can be utilized to observe the process of material failure at various stages of testing. AE dynamic non-destructive testing technology has been widely used in earth exploration, earthquake detection, and rock failure prediction [46]. In this experiment, AE was used to elucidate the sample's behavior across stages of crack closure, crack opening, and crack failure. From Fig. 4, it can be observed that the AE characteristics of rock samples in the three stages of uniaxial compression under different treatment conditions are generally similar. In the initial loading stage, that is, the pore compaction stage, the internal pore cracks of rock samples began to close and the coarse particles rubbed against each other, generating a small amount of AE. As the load continued to increase, the rock mass entered the elastic stage, and sliding occurred between the closed cracks inside the rock sample, producing a small amount of AE events. Subsequently, during the latter part of the elastic stage, stable crack extension commenced and the number of AE events increased significantly. In the failure stage, microcracks penetrated, the interaction between cracks increased, unstable crack extension occurred, and the release of AE energy increased exponentially, peaking at the moment of failure.

The time range for crack propagation in shale varies under different conditions. Under saturated conditions, crack propagation duration is shorter, possibly due to water filling the pre-existing micro-pores and reducing crack propagation stress. Exposure to gaseous CO2 and supercritical CO2 induces distinct effects. When exposed to supercritical CO2, the strong adsorption and expansion effect of supercritical CO2 expands pre-existing micro-cracks and pores in the rock mass, resulting in an extended time range for crack propagation. When subjected to gaseous CO2, the ratio of crack initiating with the addition of brine is lower compared to dry CO2 exposure. This can be explained by the intrusion of CO2 in a gaseous state, which generates many micro-cracks, potentially prolonging the duration of initial crack initiation. After the addition of brine, dissolution and enlargement of micro-crack pores occur in an acidic environment, shortening the time of sample compression and compaction. Compared to gaseous CO2, supercritical CO2 has lower viscosity and higher diffusivity. This may be result in fuller contact between supercritical CO2 and the sample, as well as more complete chemical reactions with specific minerals within the sample. Consequently, new minerals or binders may form, filling the original voids more thoroughly and thereby prolonging the compression stage of the sample.

Tensile strength before and after shale interaction with the carbon dioxide-based fluids

During the fracturing process, shale in the formation is susceptible to tensile failure. At the same time, when shale acts as a cap layer, the sealing of CO2 within the underground may induce splitting effects on the cap layer. Throughout the sealing process, the reaction between brine, CO2 and shale influences the tensile resistance of shale, necessitating experimental research on the UTS (σt) of shale before and after exposure. The UTS of the rock refers to the maximum tensile stress a rock can withstand before failure at its uniaxial tensile peak load. This experiment utilized the most famous Brazilian test in indirect experiments to test the UTS of shale before and after exposure. The failure mode of the rock sample under different treatment conditions is roughly the same as that observed in the shale uniaxial compression experiment. Before reaching the peak value, the loading value of shale exhibits a nonlinear relationship with experimental time. Once the tensile stress reaches its peak, it drops to zero instantaneously, exhibiting obvious brittle failure characteristics. Shale has the highest tensile strength under dry conditions. Following treatment with two forms of CO2, the tensile stress of shale decreases by 27.6 % and 40.2 % compared to dry conditions, respectively, indicating that CO2 immersion has deteriorated the tensile characteristics of shale to a certain extent. Owing to its strong adsorption and dissolution effect, supercritical CO2 can enter micropores inaccessible to gaseous CO2 molecules, adsorb more in the shale, and cause more significant swelling effects, significantly weakening the mechanical properties of shale. CO2 dissolved in brine creates an acidic environment, leading to mineral dissolution in shale, thereby reducing the bond strength between minerals and further diminishing the tensile capacity of shale. Compared to gaseous CO2, the tensile strength of shale increases by 17.9 % after treatment with supercritical CO2 in brine, similar to the UCS. The CO2-brine-rock interaction experienced by shale results in the secondary enlargement of quartz cement. Supercritical CO2 exhibits more extensive reactions than gaseous CO2, consequently enhancing shale's mechanical properties.

Discussion and future research

Effect of carbon dioxide-based fluids on shale brittleness index

This section focuses on the discussion and analysis of the changes in the brittleness index resulting from shale's interaction with different fluids. Research indicates that the fracturing effect of shale formations is influenced by geological factors such as rock brittleness, bedding and fracture development, and in-situ stress field. Shale brittleness is regarded as a crucial indicator for reservoir mechanics, as well as for the design and evaluation of rock fracturing. A higher shale brittleness index facilitates the formation of fracture networks, which can effectively improve reservoir permeability conditions [[47], [48], [49]]. Despite the absence of a unified definition and measurement method for brittleness, brittleness is recognized as both a deformation characteristic and a material property. Currently, more than tens of methods for measuring brittleness are employed domestically and internationally, mainly based on strength, stress-strain curve, loading and unloading experiments, hardness, fragment content, internal friction angle, penetration experiments, mineral composition, and other brittleness indicators [50]. The literature mainly identifies several types of brittleness indices, some based on strength ratios, some on stress-strain tests, and others on mineral composition and water content [51]. The author believes that using a functional formula of tensile and compressive strength when studying strength provides values closer to the actual ones. These findings indicate that shale specimens exhibit reduced brittleness and enhanced ductility following exposure to different fluids due to varying degrees of damage incurred to shale under the action of different fluids. Compared to the original specimen, the brittleness index of shale decreased by 10.95 % and 24.32 % upon exposure to gaseous CO2 and supercritical CO2 respectively, while the brittleness index of brine saturated specimens decreased by 27.83 %. Notably, the decrease in shale brittleness index after CO2 exposure is smaller than that following exposure to deionized water, indicating a lower degree of damage to shale incurred by CO2 compared to deionized water. This aligns with the result that the shale's strength after water exposure is lower than that following interaction with pure fluid CO2. When using supercritical CO2 as a fracturing fluid to enhance shale gas extraction, the fluid's impact on the mechanical properties (strength, brittleness) of shale is smaller, which is beneficial for maintaining wellbore stability and fracturing effectiveness. After interacting with gaseous CO2, shale exhibits a smaller brittleness index compared to interaction with supercritical CO2, which may lead to shale swelling resulting from supercritical CO2 adsorption, thereby reducing the mechanical properties of shale.

This fracturing fluids CO2 + brine produce hydrogen ions, creating an acidic environment that accelerates the dissolution of clay minerals and micropores, changes the microstructure of the shale and makes it porous and loose. Consequently, the initial strength of shale is weakened. In a word, hydrogen ions can partly affect the mechanical properties of shale samples and reduce shale brittleness index. In comparison, without brine in the fracturing fluids, the brittleness index following exposure to supercritical CO2 is less than that of the shale samples treated with gaseous CO2. This difference can be attributed to the stronger extraction effect of supercritical CO2, which dissolves clay minerals in shale and has a greater effect on the mechanical properties of shale. Additionally, when the brine is added to CO2, the brittleness index of shale exposed to brine + supercritical CO2 is slightly greater than that of the shale samples treated with brine + gaseous CO2. The possible reason for this may be the formation of brittle minerals such as dolomite and other secondary minerals arising from complex geochemical reactions after CO2 dissolves in brine, which increases the brittleness properties of shale.

The brittleness index of shale is influenced by various internal and external factors. The internal factors include minerals (especially clay minerals), pore pressure, groundwater in shale, mechanical factors, porosity, organic matter content, etc., while the external factors including stress effects, diagenetic form, fracturing fluids, temperature, and construction environment, etc. [53]. As seen in Fig. 6, the effect of water and CO2 on the brittleness of shale is more obvious. Water softens some minerals inside shale, thereby affecting its mechanical properties and brittleness index. Upon migration of CO2 into the shale, the brittleness index of shale decreases, with a greater reduction observed for supercritical CO2 compared to gaseous CO2. This phenomenon may stem from the adsorption and swelling of shale by CO2, which reduces the mechanical properties of shale. When shale serves as a cap layer for CO2 sequestration, it undergoes a series of complex geophysical-chemical reactions with groundwater and CO2, which affects the mechanical properties, brittleness characteristics, and other properties of shale. In a word, the brittleness index of samples shows a relatively less decrease as the shale samples exposure to CO2 and deionized water alone compared to exposure to CO2 + brine.

Under the action of supercritical CO2, its elastic modulus changes little compared to gaseous CO2, which is consistent with the change in strength properties of shale; this is due to the stronger adsorption between shale and supercritical CO2. Effect of carbon dioxide-based fluids on shale pore structure

The pore structure of shale is relatively complex. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) can valuably observe the pore types and changes in pore micromorphology on the shale sample surface. Fig. 10 displays SEM images of the untreated shale samples, revealing diverse pore types and varying geometric shapes of mineral particles. Shale pores can be divided into four types: intergranular pores, intragranular pores, organic matter pores and micro-cracks. The study found that organic matter pores and intergranular pores are widely distributed in shale samples, with organic matter pores exhibiting rounded openings and heterogeneity, likely resulting from the high thermal evolution of shale. Intergranular pores are mainly composed of pores between mineral particles. These pores, along with those within organic matter, serve as primary reservoirs for methane deposition. Micro-cracks are also observed on the surface of the shale sample, serving as the main channels for shale gas migration. Their formation is attributed to external factors such as tectonic stress and diagenesis, as well as internal factors like lithofacies, mineral composition, and thermal maturity level.Fig. 10SEM images of Longmaxi shale samples without treatment [43].Fig. 10

Effect of carbon dioxide-based fluids on shale gas production and carbon dioxide sequestration

After adding brine to the CO2 fluid, the brittleness index of shale decreased by 38.9 % (gaseous CO2 + brine) and 38.64 % (supercritical CO2 + brine), respectively, due to the interaction between CO2 fluids and brine. The brittleness index of shale is lower than that of shale exposed to pure CO2 fluid and deionized water. This is because the mechanical properties of shale are affected by the combined action of CO2 and water following the addition of brine. The combined effect of hydration, corrosion and dissolution caused by the mixture of CO2 and brine enhances shale damage, leading to a transition of shale mechanical properties from brittleness to ductility and a subsequent decrease in the brittleness index of shale [61]. Currently, most studies primarily focus on the influence of rock mineral composition and physical structure on their mechanical properties, often neglecting changes in external conditions, especially the impact of fluid intrusion on the mechanical properties and brittle characteristics of shale. The foregoing analysis indicates a significant influence of different fluid interactions on the brittleness of shale. Therefore, it is imperative to consider the impact of fluid composition on the mechanical properties and permeability or porosity of the shale during the injection of carbon dioxide-based fracturing fluids as the shale gas extraction.

Based on the analysis of NMR characteristic parameters before and after CO2-deionized water-shale interaction in different phases, the change rules of ratio and porosity are shown in Fig. 15. The permeability and porosity of shale have increased to a certain extent after the interaction of different phases of CO2-brine-shale, especially the effect of supercritical CO2-brine mixed fluid, the increment reached 30.48 %. The anisotropy inherent in the internal structure of shale implies the heterogeneity of pore structure distribution. Factors such as mineral composition and geological conditions determine the pore structure of shale, including pore size, pore size distribution, and connectivity, all of which directly impact the mechanical strength of shale. Shale's bearing capacity, including parameters such as strength and modulus, undergoes continuous alteration during interaction between shale and fracturing fluids. Compared to gaseous CO2, supercritical CO2 possesses greater density, solubility, and permeability. After interacting with supercritical CO2, alterations in shale's surface wettability (contact angle) occur due to the adsorption and displacement effects of carbon dioxide. This leads to non-uniform expansion deformation within the shale, affecting its permeability. These changes have significant implications for shale gas extraction and CO2 sequestration [29,35].Fig. 15Porosity(a) and permeability (b) change before and after short-term interaction between Longmaxi shale samples and different carbon dioxide-based fracturing fluids [54].Fig. 15

Due to the systematic reaction and interaction of carbon dioxide with fracturing fluid, groundwater and shale, some changes can occur in the formation system within a short period. As shale gas development progresses over time, the water-rock interaction process in the formation becomes more thorough. On the one hand, carbon dioxide increases the porosity and permeability of the formation to a certain extent, facilitating the desorption and migration of shale gas. On the other hand, as mining processes, the trapping effects of carbon dioxide in the formation near the well are weakened, affecting its sequestration. This can also trigger fault slip, although this process is very slow and can be ignored in short-term mining projects. However, if the development time of shale blocks is over decades, this process significantly alters the physical and mechanical parameters of the formation, increasing the mining risk. Therefore, future research should fully consider the impact of temporal and spatial scale changes on gas recovery and carbon dioxide sequestration strategies. To further elucidate the interaction mechanisms among different phases of CO2-brine-shale, future research could explore the following aspects: expanding the study scope to include variations in temperature and pressure, investigating the effects of CO2-brine on shale's microstructure, mineral composition, and mechanical properties under different temperature, pressure, and brine concentration conditions, and exploring the influence of different factors on the mechanisms of CO2-brine-shale interaction. Additionally, future studies could consider investigating the effects of different fluid interactions on shale mechanical properties under triaxial stress conditions.

Conclusions

The study employed a set of laboratory characterization methods to systematically study the pore structure, elemental content, minerals composition, and mechanical properties of the shale after being immersed and treated in different carbon dioxide-based fracturing fluids such as deionized water, gaseous CO2, supercritical CO2, gaseous CO2 + brine (10 % NaCl aq.), and supercritical CO2 + brine (10 % NaCl aq.). Further understanding and the main findings are listed as follows on the change law of mechanical strength and microstructure evolution mechanism.

The Longmaxi shale samples exhibited different UCSs and UTSs even after short-term interaction with different carbon dioxide-based fracturing fluids, in contrast to dry untreated shale sample. There was a remarkable difference in the UCS and UTS of the shales after treatment with different phases (gaseous and supercritical) of carbon dioxide. The reduction in UCSs/UTSs of the shales was 32.35 %/27.6 % (6 MPa gaseous CO2 + brine) and 29.33 %/40.2 % (12 MPa supercritical CO2 + brine) comparing to that of dry untreated shale sample. Moreover, the UCS and UTS of the shale treated with gaseous CO2 (6 MPa gaseous CO2, 318.15 K) are both greater than that of supercritical CO2 (12 MPa supercritical CO2, 318.15 K). The UCSs and UTSs of the shale is in the order: UCS/UTS (dry shale without treatment) > UCS/UTS (6 MPa, gaseous CO2) > UCS/UTS (deionized water) > UCS/UTS (12 MPa, supercritical CO2) > UCS/UTS (12 MPa, supercritical CO2 + brine) > UCS/UTS (6 MPa, gaseous CO2 + brine). The mechanical properties showed less noticeable difference between deionized water saturated and the CO2-brine fracturing fluids treated shale. Upon interaction with CO2-brine, different degrees of reduction in the brittleness index were observed as well. The brittleness index of samples shows a relatively less decrease as the shale samples exposure to CO2 and water alone compared to exposure to CO2 + brine. This study shows that the water in carbon dioxide-based fracturing fluids is a controlling factor affecting the elastic modulus of shale.

According to the microscopic observation and analysis, these changes in macroscopic mechanical properties are closely related to the changes in pore structure parameters, elements and mineral composition of shale, and the change trend of the parameters of the shale at different scales are in good agreement. The specific surface areas of micropores, mesopores and macropores all show a decreasing trend, which is in line with the changing trend of the total specific surface area of shale. After different fluid interactions with the shale samples, the content of shale elements mainly shows an increase in oxygen (O) and silicon (Si) content, while the content of metal elements such as calcium (Ca) and iron (Fe) decreases. The change law of the elemental and mineral composition of shale is consistent with the mechanical strength; and the change of element content and mineral composition of shale are the most significant. Compared to gaseous carbon dioxide, the supercritical carbon dioxide has a more pronounced impact on mineral composition of the shale. Therefore, it is imperative to consider the impact of fluid composition on the mechanical properties and permeability or porosity of the shale during the injection of carbon dioxide-based fracturing fluids as the shale gas extraction and carbon dioxide sequestration.
