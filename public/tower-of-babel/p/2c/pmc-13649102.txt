The Use of Blue Light as an Antimicrobial Treatment Against Staphylococcus aureus: A Systematic Review
Authors: Alexander Idrogo‐Lam, Kofi Owusu‐Ansah, Eric Cotter, Lisa Cotter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) is a major cause of skin, wound, and device‐related infections. Blue light (405–470 nm), shows promise for treating this organism. However, the translatability, dosing parameters, safety, and effectiveness remain unclear, and no systematic review currently exists.ObjectivesTo evaluate the antimicrobial efficacy and safety of blue light therapy against S. aureus across experimental models.MethodsAll relevant in vitro, in vivo, and clinical studies were identified through systematic searches of PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Scopus, and ClinicalTrials. gov. Studies evaluating blue light without exogenous photosensitizers reporting bacterial or clinical outcomes were included. Risk of bias was assessed using SYRCLE. This study was registered with PROSPERO (ID: CRD420251089518).ResultsA total of 33 articles met inclusion criteria: 20 planktonic models, 6 biofilm models, and 17 methicillin‐resistant S. aureus (MRSA) specific outcomes. The predominant wavelength tested was 405 nm (87.9%). Blue light reduced bacterial burden across models, though results varied by dose and experimental conditions. Additionally, 57.6% of studies did not report statistical significance.ConclusionsBlue light demonstrates antimicrobial activity against S. aureus, including MRSA, but heterogeneity in study design and limited in vivo data restrict clinical translation. We propose a standardized set of 8 reported parameters for future blue light studies.

Introduction

Blue light (BL), spanning 405–470 nm, has garnered attention in dermatology and orthopedics as an antimicrobial treatment [1, 2, 3]. This technology exerts its biocidal effects by photoexciting endogenous microbial chromophores, expressed to a greater extent in bacteria, leading to generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). ROS, in turn, causes oxidative cellular damage and ultimately the death of the microorganisms [4].

The use of BL as an antimicrobial treatment against Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) including methicillin‐resistant S. aureus (MRSA) is an important and emerging area of research, particularly due to its non‐antibiotic mechanism. These bacteria are often responsible for a substantial portion of skin infections, wound infections, and the formation of biofilms on medical devices. Previous research has demonstrated promising results in both laboratory and animal studies, showing that BL effectively reduces the viability of S. aureus [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. However, the translatability, dosing parameters, safety, and effectiveness of Blue Light Therapy (BLT) for S. aureus remain unclear.

To date, no comprehensive work has investigated treatment effect of BLT against S. aureus without the use of exogenous photosensitizers. Clinically, the use of photosensitizers is not optimal, as they can induce keratinocyte apoptosis, pain, erythema, and skin barrier breakdown [13, 14]. Therefore, this systematic review aims to aggregate existing data pertaining to the utilization of BLT, without the use of exogenous photosensitizers, as an antimicrobial treatment against S. aureus, identifying areas that warrant further investigation.

Materials and Methods

Literature Search

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, Scopus, and ClinicalTrials.gov from database inception through June 2025. This systematic review was registered in the PROSPERO database (ID: CRD420251089518). Search strings were constructed using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free‐text terms encompassing “Staphylococcus aureus,” “MRSA,” “blue light,” and “phototherapy,” with related synonyms adapted for each database. The search strategy adhered to no specific date restrictions and exclusively encompassed English‐language, peer‐reviewed publications. Two independent reviewers (A.I.L. and K.O.A.) conducted comprehensive title, abstract, and full‐text screenings. The risk of bias for in vivo studies was assessed using the SYRCLE Risk of Bias tool [15]. For ex vivo and in vitro studies, key methodological parameters relevant to reproducibility and dose delivery were reviewed, given the absence of validated Risk of Bias tools for these designs.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they specifically investigated the effectiveness of BL exposure at wavelengths ranging from 405 to 470 nm. Combined approaches were allowed if the effects of BL exposure alone could be distinguished. Additionally, included studies must have been focused on S. aureus, including MRSA. The study types included in our review were in vitro, in vivo, and clinical studies. Furthermore, each study must have reported a bacterial load, colony‐forming units (CFUs), or clinical infection resolution. Studies excluded were those that did not specifically report outcomes on S. aureus, explored photodynamic therapy with chemical photosensitizers, or did not separate the effects of BL exposure alone. Reviews, editorials, and conference abstracts were also excluded unless sufficient data was available. This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐analyses 2020 reporting guideline statement [16].

Data Extraction

For all included publications, two reviewers (A.I.L. and K.O.A.) independently screened the title and abstracts of included trials. Publications meeting screening criteria then underwent full text reviews. A third author (L.C.) facilitated a consensus discussion to resolve any disputes regarding the inclusion of literature. Extracted variables from the study encompassed the study type, organism, model type, and irradiation parameters (wavelength, dose, exposure time). The primary outcome of interest was the reduction in CFU counts following BL treatment. When CFU data was not reported, the overall trends in infection resolution or bacterial inhibition were assessed. The magnitude and statistical significance of the observed effects and the statistical methods used were also evaluated. Secondary outcomes encompassed the incidence of adverse effects, including cytotoxicity or other reported tissue responses. Statistical analysis was limited to descriptive interpretation, and no comparative or meta‐analysis was performed due to the heterogeneity of the included studies.

Results

A total of 4252 articles were extracted from the data, and 2105 articles were screened after removing duplicates. Out of these, 45 studies met the criteria for a full‐text review, but three articles were unable to be retrieved. Consequently, 33 studies met the criteria for inclusion, as seven were excluded due to the use of photosensitizers, and two were excluded due to incorrect BL wavelength (Figure S1).

A total of 33 publications met full eligibility criteria (Table S1). Notably, 17 publications (51.5%) reported MRSA specific outcomes. The most frequently employed wavelength was 405 nm, tested in 87.9% of the publications. Delivered doses varied significantly across studies, with the lowest dose being 1.08 J/cm2 and the highest being 3240 J/cm2. Thirty‐one publications utilized in vitro models (93.9%), with only four publications using in vivo models (12.1%). Publications with ex vivo experiments were among the least studied, with only one being examined (3.0%).

Planktonic Versus Biofilm Models

Across 20 planktonic S. aureus models (Table 1), BLT demonstrated consistent bactericidal activity. Reductions in bacterial burden were dose dependent, with 3–6 log10 CFU reductions observed across dosages of 36–720 J/cm2. Nevertheless, five experiments reported nonsignificant results (25%) and nine did not comment on statistical significance (45%). Both MRSA and MSSA strains exhibited overlapping susceptibility profiles, with no consistent differences in response attributable to methicillin resistance status.

One study reported the differences between static and dynamic biofilms, where dynamic biofilms, attempting to replicate in vivo environment, did not show a statistically significant reduction compared to static biofilms [31]. Biofilm maturity influenced responsiveness, with earlier‐stage biofilms demonstrating greater susceptibility than established or mature biofilms in this study [31]. Eleven of the 13 biofilm experiments required energy levels exceeding those typically considered clinically feasible (84.5%) as keratinocyte damage is reported around 100 J/cm2 [36, 37, 38].

Notably, one study directly evaluated both planktonic and biofilm conditions within the same experimental framework, demonstrating reduced susceptibility in biofilm‐associated bacteria relative to planktonic counterparts under the same BL parameters [29].

In Vivo and Ex Vivo Models

In vivo and ex vivo experiments were reported in five studies (Table 2). In vivo models primarily consisted of wound infection models in mammals.

All five in vivo and ex vivo studies demonstrated reductions in bacteria following BL exposure, with all but one reporting statistically significant decreases in CFU counts compared with untreated controls (20%). Some investigations noted side effects including: skin desiccation mitigated by hydration, thermal damage without cooling and skin necrosis at 20–30 mW/cm2 [29, 39, 40]. However some studies noted no visible apoptosis of mouse skin or thermal damage when BLT was coupled with a cooler [40, 41]. Risk of bias assessment was performed for in vivo animal models using the SYRCLE risk of bias tool. Most studies showed unclear risk of bias due to limited reporting of randomization, allocation concealment, and blinding. However, one study explicitly reported a lack of investigator blinding, resulting in a high overall risk of bias [39]. Selective outcome reporting and variability in irradiation parameters were common sources of potential bias.

MRSA‐Specific Outcomes

MRSA‐specific experiments comprised a substantial proportion of included studies (Table 3). BLT demonstrated antimicrobial activity against multiple MRSA strains across planktonic, biofilm, and in vivo models. Reported CFU reductions for MRSA were comparable to those observed for MSSA under similar irradiation parameters. However, none of the MRSA‐specific experiments report significance when compared to control.

Dose Thresholds and Low‐Dose Exposure

There were 21 studies that evaluated BL with doses under 100 J/cm2 (Table 4). This is an important limiting factor with regards to translation as keratinocyte damage has been reported around 100 J/cm2 [36, 37, 38]. Studies within this range mostly demonstrated BL being effective for reducing planktonic bacteria but less effective for biofilms. Exposure times varied from 2.5 min to 6 h, with prolonged exposure time demonstrating greater bacterial reduction.

Statistical Reporting and Outcome Measures

Statistical reporting was inconsistent across included studies. 19 of 33 publications (57.6%) did not report statistical significance measures for antimicrobial outcomes, which limits interpretability and cross‐study comparison. Outcome reporting also varied substantially, with efficacy expressed as CFU log10 reductions, percent survival, percent inhibition, or even qualitative descriptors of growth suppression. Of included studies, five did not report the dose of BL tested (15.2%) (Table 5).

Discussion

This systematic review summarizes current evidence evaluating BLT as a non‐antibiotic antimicrobial approach against S. aureus, including MRSA. Across 33 studies, BL exposure reduced bacterial burden in most experimental models, supporting its plausibility as an antimicrobial modality. However, substantial variation in study design, dosing parameters, and outcome reporting limits definitive conclusions regarding optimal treatment parameters or clinical applicability.

One key exclusion to this review was the omission of studies utilizing exogenous photosensitizers. This was done to isolate the antimicrobial effects of BL alone; although external chromophores may enhance ROS, clinical use of photosensitizers can induce keratinocyte apoptosis, pain, erythema, and skin breakdown [13, 14]. By focusing on BLT exposure alone, this review aimed to evaluate its intrinsic antimicrobial potential.

Planktonic Versus Biofilm S. aureus

A trend among included studies was the reduced susceptibility of biofilm‐associated bacteria compared with planktonic organisms. This observation aligns with established biofilm biology, in which extracellular polymeric matrices, altered metabolic activity, and reduced penetration of external stressors contribute to antimicrobial tolerance [49]. Biofilm‐associated infections represent a clinically relevant challenge, and the present findings suggest that BL monotherapy may not be ideal for eradication of biofilms at clinically acceptable doses. Nevertheless, BLT may still represent a viable strategy for decontamination of medical devices and implants, where keratinocyte safety is not a limiting concern.

Dose Considerations

One consideration noted in both in vivo and ex vivo studies was the balance between antimicrobial activity and tissue safety. While higher doses were generally associated with greater bacterial reductions, many studies demonstrating antimicrobial effects utilized doses that may approach ranges associated with keratinocyte injury or oxidative stress in experimental models [50, 51]. Importantly, antimicrobial effects were still observed in several studies at doses below 100 J/cm2, although results were less consistent, particularly in biofilm and in vivo systems. Future studies must prioritize evaluating antimicrobial activity and tissue safety to determine clinically relevant dosing parameters.

An additional limiting factor is that 5 of 33 studies did not report the dose of BL in the experiment, and no study mentioned the use of a light sensor to confirm the dose delivered in the experiment. Studies have shown that the dosing output of clinical phototherapy units can differ greatly from manufacturer specifications, with irradiance often measuring lower to even half the manufacturer specified dosing output [52, 53]. Without true dosing verification, light exposure in these clinical trials remains unclear, making dose–response comparisons between experiments and reproducibility nearly impossible.

Safety reporting was inconsistent across in vivo studies, and direct assessment of keratinocyte viability beyond visible inspection was rarely incorporated into study design. As a result, the existing evidence does not yet clearly establish whether doses capable of producing reliable reductions in bacterial burden can be delivered safely in human skin, particularly in the setting of repeated exposure. One included study investigated continuous low dose BLT (5 mW/cm2) as a safer alternative by maintaining bacteriostatic levels of BL on the skin, but exposure time (4–41 h) may be beyond what is clinically feasible [39]. On the other hand, one study found that sub‐lethal BL exposure may induce tolerance in S. aureus [23]. Further studies should be done to investigate if sub‐lethal exposure consistently promotes adaptive responses. The predominance of in vitro studies and limited in vivo data represents another significant gap. While in vitro models provide valuable mechanistic insights and dose‐response relationships, they cannot fully replicate the biological environment of a human infection.

Need for Standardized Reporting and Proposed Standardized Reporting Parameters

A major limitation identified across the literature was the lack of standardized reporting. Many studies reported wavelength without specifying dose. Since antimicrobial effects are dose dependent and influenced by delivery parameters, incomplete reporting limits reproducibility and prevents identification of optimal treatment protocols [12]. Similarly, variability in outcome measures prevented quantitative comparison or meta‐analysis. For this reason, to improve reproducibility and enable comparison, we propose a standardized set of reported parameters for future BLT studies:

Clinical Implications

BLT is already used clinically in dermatology, where antimicrobial effects against Cutibacterium acnes occur within dosing ranges tolerable to human skin, and is currently being investigated in orthopedics [1, 2, 3]. In contrast, application for S. aureus infections remains investigational. The findings of this systematic review suggest potential roles for BL in superficial infection control, wound decontamination, or adjunctive antimicrobial strategies, particularly where repeated noninvasive treatment is feasible. Combination approaches with conventional antimicrobials or other non‐antibiotic modalities may enhance efficacy while allowing lower BL doses.

BLT may also hold promise for patients with chronic inflammatory skin conditions associated with recurrent or persistent S. aureus colonization, such as epidermolysis bullosa [54]. In these populations, long‐term antibiotic use carries risks of antibiotic resistance and systemic side effects, making non‐antibiotic, topically applied antimicrobial modalities especially helpful. Additionally, while this review focused on S. aureus, the broader applicability of BLT to pseudomonas aeruginosa or other major causes of wound infections warrants ongoing investigation.

Limitations

This review is limited by the predominance of in vitro studies, limited in vivo data, and substantial heterogeneity in experimental design and reporting. Publication bias toward positive antimicrobial findings is possible. The lack of standardized safety assessment across studies limits definitive conclusions regarding clinical dosing thresholds. The exclusion of non‐English publications may have also introduced language bias.

Conclusions

BLT demonstrates antimicrobial activity against S. aureus, including MRSA, across experimental models. However, methodological variability, limited in vivo data, and uncertainty regarding the therapeutic window between antimicrobial effects and tissue safety limit clinical translation. Clarifying this therapeutic window through well‐designed studies is crucial for establishing BLT as a non‐antibiotic antimicrobial modality for S. aureus. Uniform adoption of the 8 proposed reporting parameters would enable meaningful meta‐analyses and support clinical translation.
