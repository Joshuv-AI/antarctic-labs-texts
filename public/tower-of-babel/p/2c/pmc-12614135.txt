MXene‐Supported Vanadium Sulfide Composites Reinforced by Tailored Carbon Nanotube Networks for High‐Performance Supercapattery
Authors: Rahul S Ingole, Snehal L Kadam, Kwangjun Kim, Minwook Kim, Yong Tae Kim, Jun Seok Lee, Jong G Ok
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe strategic engineering of multidimensional electrode materials is essential for next‐generation hybrid energy storage systems, specifically supercapatteries, which integrate the high energy density of batteries and high‐power density of supercapacitors. A rationally engineered composite comprising vanadium sulfide (VS4) nanosheets uniformly anchored onto 2D MXene (Ti3C2Tx) sheets through a facile solvothermal method, further reinforced with carbon nanotubes (CNTs) to construct a 3D conductive network, is demonstrated. The uniform dispersion of VS4 on MXene, facilitated by strong V─C interfacial bonding, mitigates MXene restacking, enhances electrical conductivity, and stabilizes the hybrid structure. Meanwhile, CNTs further improve electron mobility, reduce particle aggregation, and reinforce mechanical strength. This multidimensional design significantly boosts redox kinetics and cycle stability. The optimized VS4‐MXene‐CNT electrode delivers a high specific capacity of 802.46 C g−1 (1337.44 F g−1) at 0.3 A g−1 in 6 M KOH, retaining 97% capacitance after 5000 cycles. The fabricated asymmetric supercapattery device (ASD) exhibits a specific capacity of 148.18 C g−1 (246.97 F g−1) at 0.3 A g−1, achieving specific energy of 12.35 Wh kg−1 and specific power of 1856.43 W kg−1, with 93% capacitance retention over 5000 cycles. This work offers a promising route toward designing for durable, high‐performance supercapattery electrodes.Keywords: carbon nanotube, energy storage, multidimensional hybrid electrode, MXene, solvothermal synthesis, supercapattery, vanadium sulfide

Introduction

The global demand for efficient, sustainable, and high‐performance energy storage devices has catalyzed the development of novel hybrid technologies that bridge the performance gap between traditional batteries and supercapacitors.[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 ] Among these, hybrid supercapacitors, also called “supercapatteries,” that integrate the high‐power density and fast charge–discharge characteristics of supercapacitors with the high energy density of batteries, have emerged as a promising class of energy storage systems.[ 6 , 7 ] These devices operate with the battery‐type positive electrode as an energy source and the electric double‐layer capacitor negative electrode as a power source. They have been designed on the combination of Faradaic (redox) and non‐Faradaic (electrostatic) charge storage mechanisms, offering a well‐balanced electrochemical profile ideal for modern portable electronics, electric vehicles, and grid‐level energy applications.[ 8 , 9 , 10 ] However, to achieve the multifunctionality and scalability required for supercapatteries, the rational design of nanostructured battery‐type electrode materials with optimized surface chemistry, high conductivity, and mechanical integrity remains essential.[ 6 ] Recent research trends focus on tailoring hybrid architectures comprising transition metal sulfides, 2D materials, and carbon‐based additives to address these challenges and improve electrochemical performance.[ 7 ]

A low‐dimensional transition metal chalcogenide and its composites have been extensively studied for battery‐type electrode materials due to their outstanding electrochemical response and high theoretical and experimental specific capacity.[ 11 , 12 ] Among them, vanadium sulfide (VS4), has attracted considerable interest for energy storage applications due to its high theoretical capacity (≈1196 mAh g−1), multivalent redox nature (V4+/V5+), and unique linear chain crystal structure which allows efficient ionic intercalation.[ 13 , 14 , 15 ] However, its intrinsic limitations including low electrical conductivity, structural instability, and aggregation during cycling impair its rate capability and long‐term performance.[ 16 ] To overcome these drawbacks, MXenes (e.g., Ti3C2Tx), a class of 2D transition metal carbides/nitrides, have been explored as conductive and flexible matrices to support VS4 and suppress particle agglomeration.[ 17 ] MXenes exhibit exceptional electrical conductivity, hydrophilicity, and surface functional groups that promote electrochemical activity and interfacial interaction with sulfides.[ 18 , 19 ] The resulting MXene‐VS4 composites combine the pseudocapacitive behavior of VS4 with the conductive and mechanical support of MXenes. Despite these advantages, limitations such as restacking of MXene sheets, poor mechanical strength under repeated cycling, and insufficient electron pathways at high areal loadings still restrict their practical utility in supercapatteries.[ 18 ]

Several studies have demonstrated the potential of VS4/MXene composites in hybrid energy storage systems. For instance, VS4 anchored on Ti3C2Tx MXene‐based solid‐state device has exhibited a high specific capacity of 70.9 mF cm−2, with decent rate capability and capacity retention of 75% over 3000 cycles.[ 20 ] This performance enhancement stems from the effective dispersion of VS4 nanostructures, increased interfacial contact area, and improved ion diffusion enabled by the conductive MXene network.[ 15 ] MXene/VS4 composite electrode has been employed in an aqueous zinc ion flexible supercapacitor in another study, where the device delivers a maximum power density of 13.33 W cm−3, an energy density of 39.88 mWh cm−3, and a high capacitance retention of 88.5% of its initial capacitance after 10 000 cycles.[ 16 ]

Beyond supercapacitors, these composites have also shown promising performance as rechargeable batteries. For example, VS4/Ti3C2/C hierarchical nano‐micro hybrids have been synthesized via the hydrothermal method, exhibiting a high discharge capacity of 492 mA h g−1 at 50 mA g−1, an outstanding rate performance of 129 mA h g−1 at 1000 mA g−1, and excellent cycling performance over 900 cycles at 500 mA g−1 in Mg‐ion batteries.[ 21 ] Recently, a hollow VS4/C/rGO composite, constructed by using V2CTx MXene as a template with reduced graphene oxide coating as an anode material, has demonstrated discharge capacity of 378 mA h g−1 at 0.1 A g−1 over 100 cycles and 250 mA h g−1 at 1 A g−1 after 2000 cycles, with a remarkably high‐rate capacity of 202 mA h g−1 at 20 A g−1 for potassium‐ion batteries.[ 22 ] These results affirm the suitability of MXene‐supported VS4 composites as hybrid electrodes. However, the electrochemical stability and performance at high rates or under practical operating conditions remain suboptimal, prompting the need for further compositional engineering.

One promising strategy to further improve conductivity, mechanical strength, and active site accessibility is the integration of conductive carbon nanostructures, particularly carbon nanotubes (CNTs), into the VS4/MXene composite framework. CNTs offer a high aspect ratio, outstanding electrical conductivity (≈104 S cm−1), and mechanical robustness, forming interconnected conductive networks that act as charge highways across the electrode.[ 23 ] Their integration can prevent the restacking of MXene sheets, buffer volume changes of VS4 during cycling, and provide additional electrochemical pathways.[ 23 ] Hence, it is highly plausible that the incorporation of tailored CNT networks into the MXene‐supported VS4 composite not only improves the rate performance and specific capacitance but also stabilizes the electrode structure over prolonged cycling. Reports have shown that ternary VS4/CNT/rGO and VTe2/MXene/CNT composites‐based SCs demonstrate significantly enhanced energy densities 72.07 Wh kg−1 and 6.84 µWh cm−2 and prolonged lifespan (over 10 000 cycles) respectively, confirming the synergistic effect of multi‐component hybridization.[ 24 , 25 ]

In light of the above, this study presents the strategic engineering of MXene‐supported vanadium sulfide composites reinforced by tailored CNT networks for high supercapattery performance. By leveraging the high pseudocapacitance of VS4, the excellent conductivity and interfacial functionality of MXene, and the conductive bridging framework of CNTs, a multidimensional, high‐efficiency electrode architecture is constructed. This integration strategy uniquely addresses the key limitations of each component, boosting charge transfer kinetics, mechanical integrity, and long‐term cyclic stability. Attributed to the advantages of the synergistic effect among those complementary materials, our optimized VS4/MXene/CNT composite (later denoted as VS‐MX100‐CNT5) exhibits a high specific capacitance of 802.46 C g−1 (1337.44 F g−1) at 0.3 A g−1 in 6 M KOH, retaining over 96.89% of its capacitance after 5000 cycles. The asymmetric supercapattery device (ASD) fabricated by using this electrode delivers a specific capacity of 148.18 C g−1 (246.97 F g−1) at 0.3 A g−1, achieving a specific energy of 12.35 Wh kg−1 and specific power of 1856.43 W kg−1, with 93% capacitance retention of over 5000 cycles. This work offers a creative insight toward designing durable, high‐performance supercapattery electrodes for next‐generation energy storage systems.

Experimental Section

Materials

All chemicals used in this study were of analytical reagent (AR) grade and were not further purified. Ammonium metavanadate (NH4VO3, 99.90%), thioacetamide (C2H5NS, 99.0%), hydrofluoric acid (HF, 48%), potassium hydroxide (KOH, >85%), polyvinyl alcohol (PVA, 99.0%), polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), and the solvent n‐methyl‐2‐pyrrolidone (NMP, 99.5%) were obtained from Sigma–Aldrich Pvt. Ltd. Titanium aluminum carbide MAX‐phase (Ti3AlC2, 15 µm, >98.8%) was sourced from INNOMXENE Co., Ltd. Super P conductive carbon black was purchased from MTI Korea, while multi‐walled CNTs (average length of ≈5 µm) were acquired from Carbon Nanomaterial Technology Co., Ltd. Nickel foam (thickness of 1.6 mm, average pore diameter of ≈0.25 mm, MTI Korea) was used as the substrate due to its high electrical conductivity and porous architecture. These features make it well‐suited for ideal deposition platform for active materials and served as a current collector for electrodes.

Synthesis of Vanadium Sulfide (VS4)

Vanadium sulfide (VS4) was synthesized through a simple in situ solvothermal approach. Initially, 0.04 M of NH4VO3 was dissolved in 70 mL of N‐methyl‐2‐pyrrolidone (NMP) solvent. Subsequently, 0.2 M of C2H5NS was introduced into the solution while maintaining the ratio of 1:5, under continuous stirring. The final volume was adjusted to 70 mL, and the solution was stirred for 1 h to ensure uniform mixing. The homogeneous solution was then transferred into a 100 mL Teflon‐lined stainless‐steel autoclave and subjected to solvothermal treatment at 180 °C for 20 h. After cooling, the resulting product was collected via vacuum filtration and thoroughly washed with ethanol and DI water. Finally, the purified material was dried at 70 °C in a vacuum oven for 12 h.

Synthesis of MXene (Ti3C2Tx)

The MXene (Ti3C2Tx) was synthesized by selectively etching the aluminum layer from the titanium aluminum carbide MAX‐phase (Ti3AlC2) precursor, through a wet chemical etching method, as reported elsewhere.[ 26 ] In a typical synthesis, 1.0 g of Ti3AlC2 powder was finely ground using an agate mortar and pestle, then transferred into a plastic beaker. Next, 40 mL of 48% HF solution was slowly added to the beaker to initiate the etching process. The mixture was stirred continuously at room temperature for 24 h. After etching, the mixture was vacuum filtered and washed repeatedly with deionized (DI) water until the pH of the supernatant reached 6. Finally, the resulting multilayered MXene sediment was then vacuum dried at 70 °C for 12 h.

Synthesis of MXene (Ti3C2Tx)‐Supported Vanadium Sulfide (VS4) Composites

Figure 1a shows the overall fabrication procedure for the MXene‐supported vanadium sulfide (MXene/VS4) composites (denoted as VS‐MX50, VS‐MX100, and VS‐MX200) via an in situ solvothermal method similar to that used for the pristine VS4. In a standard procedure, 50 mg of MXene fine powder was added to the prepared VS4 precursor solution and dispersed by constant stirring, followed by ultrasonication for 30 min. The resulting uniform black dispersion was further stirred for an additional 30 min to ensure complete mixing. The total volume was adjusted to 70 mL before transferring the solution to a 100 mL Teflon‐lined stainless‐steel autoclave, which was then subjected to solvothermal treatment at 180 °C for 20 h. After completion of the reaction, the product (VS‐MX50) was retrieved by vacuum filtration, washed alternately with ethanol and DI water, and dried in a vacuum oven at 70 °C for 12 h. The same procedure was followed for synthesizing VS‐MX100 and VS‐MX200 by adding 100 and 200 mg of MXene powder, respectively, into the precursor solution.

a) Schematic representation of the synthesis route for MXene and MXene‐supported vanadium sulfide composites via an in situ solvothermal method, highlighting each stage from precursor preparation to composite formation. b) Illustration of the complete electrode fabrication process where the active material slurry is uniformly applied onto a nickel foam current collector using the slurry coating technique.

Fabrication of the Working Electrode

Figure 1b shows the working electrode fabrication process for electrochemical analysis. The process began by preparing a slurry composed of 80 wt.% active material, 10 wt.% PVDF binder, and 10 wt.% conducting additives. The conducting additive consisted of a mixture of carbon black (CB) and CNTs, with the CNT content systematically varied to reinforce the conducting network within the composite. Specifically, CNTs were incorporated at 1%, 3%, 5%, and 10% of the total conducting additive weight, denoted as CNT1, CNT3, CNT5, and CNT10, hereafter, with the remainder being carbon black. For instance, a 10 wt.% conducting additive with 3% CNT (CNT3) would consist of 0.3 wt.% CNT and 9.7 wt.% carbon black. To prepare the slurry, PVDF was first dissolved in N‐methyl‐2‐pyrrolidone (NMP) solvent under continuous magnetic stirring at room temperature. The conducting additive (CB + CNT blend) and active material were then added to the PVDF‐NMP solution, and the entire mixture was ground vigorously with agate mortar and pestle until a homogeneous, viscous slurry was obtained. The prepared slurry was subsequently coated onto a nickel foam. After coating, the electrodes were dried in a vacuum oven at 120 °C for 12 h to ensure complete removal of the NMP solvent. This process allows the systematic study of CNT‐enhanced conducting networks within the electrode architecture, contributing to improved electrical conductivity and electrochemical performance.

The details of physical and electrochemical characterizations, as well as relevant formulas used for calculation of specific capacitance and specific capacity, are fully described in the Supporting Information.

Fabrication and Evaluation of the Asymmetric Supercapattery Device (ASD)

Initially, 3 g of PVA was dissolved in 50 mL of DI water at 90 °C under continuous stirring till a clear gel was attained. In parallel, 6 g of KOH was dissolved in 10 mL of DI water and subsequently mixed dropwise into the PVA gel under sustained heating and stirring, resulting in a clear and homogeneous gel electrolyte. To fabricate the ASD, two electrodes were prepared with an area ratio of a positive electrode to a negative electrode of 1:1.2; VS‐MX100‐CNT5 (3 × 3 cm) served as a positive electrode, and activated carbon (AC) (3.6 × 3.6 cm) as a negative electrode. The gel‐polymer electrolyte was uniformly coated on the surface of each hybrid electrode and allowed to dry at room temperature. A 25 µm‐thick (Celgard‐2400) membrane pre‐soaked in the same gel electrolyte was used as a separator. The mass loading of active material for the ASD device was set to 96.96 mg. Results and Discussion

Physical Analysis

The structural and compositional characteristics of the synthesized materials, including the pristine MXene (Ti3C2Tx), vanadium sulfide (VS4), and their hybrid composite (MXene‐VS4), can be thoroughly examined using X‐ray diffraction (XRD) and X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). These techniques provide comprehensive insights into the crystalline structure, chemical bonding, and electronic environments of the constituent elements, which are essential for correlating the material structure with electrochemical behavior.

Figure S1a (Supporting Information) presents the XRD patterns of the precursor MAX phase and HF‐etched MXene. The diffraction peak observed at 2θ = 9.69° corresponds to the (002) plane of Ti3AlC2 (MAX phase), which shifts to 8.84° in the MXene phase (Ti3C2Tx), indicating successful removal of the Al layer and formation of few‐layer MXene.[ 27 ] Figure 2a compares the XRD profiles of the pristine MXene, pure VS4, and MXene‐VS4 composite (VS‐MX100). The characteristic peaks of MXene are observed at 8.84°, 18.06°, 27.42°, 34.21°, and 61.2°, corresponding to the (002), (004), (006), (008), and (110) planes, respectively, confirming the well‐defined layered structure of Ti3C2Tx.[ 23 ] The diffraction pattern of the VS4 nanosheets exhibits multiple reflections at 14.9°, 15.9°, 17.3°, 27.5°, 33.6°, 36.3°, 40.5°, and 47.6°, which correspond to the (002), (110), (020), (−211), (132), (−204), (−224), and (224) planes of monoclinic VS4, in agreement with standard reference data.[ 28 ] In the MXene‐VS4 composite (VS‐MX100), the XRD pattern reveals prominent peaks at 15.7° and 17.0°, attributed to the (110) and (020) planes of VS4, alongside the peak at 6.69°, assigned to the (002) plane of MXene. These features confirm the successful integration of VS4 onto the MXene substrate. Moreover, the presence of VS4 within the composite appears to increase the interlayer spacing between MXene sheets, likely acting as an intercalating spacer. This expanded interlayer spacing is advantageous for ion diffusion and mitigates restacking of MXene layers, thus enhancing reaction kinetics and accessibility of electroactive sites.[ 29 ]

a) XRD patterns of the pure MXene, vanadium sulfide (VS4), and MXene‐supported vanadium sulfide (MXene‐VS4) composites. b) XRD patterns of MXene‐VS4 composites synthesized with various concentrations of MXene.

Figure 2b displays the XRD patterns of the MXene‐VS4 composites synthesized with various MXene contents, labeled as VS‐MX50, VS‐MX100, and VS‐MX200. The characteristic peaks of both VS4 and MXene are retained across all samples, indicating structural integrity of each phase. However, the intensity of the VS4 peaks gradually decreases with increasing MXene concentration, which suggests a possible distortion or modification of the VS4 lattice due to higher MXene loading. This attenuation may be attributed to altered crystal growth dynamics or changes in the composite's microstructure, highlighting the influence of MXene content on composite formation and crystallinity.[ 27 ]

Now, the surface elemental composition and chemical states of the pristine MXene, pure VS4, and MXene‐VS4 composites are investigated through XPS analysis. This technique provides fundamental information on the oxidation states and chemical bonding environments of the constituent elements, which are essential for understanding their electrochemical characteristics. Figure 3a presents the wide‐scan XPS spectra of the pristine MXene (Ti3C2Tx), pure VS4, and VS‐MX100 composite, while the survey spectra for VS‐MX50, VS‐MX100, and VS‐MX200 are displayed in Figure S1b (Supporting Information). The survey spectra clearly confirm the presence of Ti 2p and C 1s peaks in the MXene sample, and V 2p and S 2p signals in the VS4 sample. In the case of the composite samples, characteristic peaks corresponding to V 2p, S 2p, Ti 2p, and C 1s are observed, which verifies the successful integration of VS4 nanosheets with the MXene matrix. Additionally, the survey spectra of the VS4/Ti3C2Tx composites confirm the presence of V, S, Ti, C, and O elements, as shown in Figure 3a and Figure S1b (Supporting Information), further supporting the formation of a heterostructured hybrid.

a) XPS survey spectra of the pure MXene, VS4, and MXene‐VS4 composite samples. b–e) High‐resolution XPS spectra of key elements present in the MXene‐VS4 composites: (b) Vanadium 2p spectrum; (c) Sulfur 2p spectrum; (d) Titanium 2p spectrum; and (e) Carbon 1s spectrum.

Figure 3b–e display high‐resolution XPS spectra for the primary elements in the VS4/MXene hybrid: (b) V 2p, (c) S 2p, (d) Ti 2p, and (e) C 1s. In Figure 3b, the V 2p spectrum exhibits two prominent peaks at binding energies of 516.5 and 524.0 eV, corresponding to V 2p3/2 and V 2p1/2 of V4+, respectively. The intensity and area ratio of these peaks indicate that vanadium predominantly exists in the +4 oxidation state.[ 28 ] Notably, the contribution from V─C bonding is more pronounced in the composite compared to pure VS4, indicating a strong interfacial interaction between VS4 and MXene, which contributes to enhanced structural stability of the composite.[ 29 ]

The high‐resolution S 2p spectrum in Figure 3c shows a doublet at 162.5 and 163.7 eV, corresponding to S 2p3/2 and S 2p1/2 of S2 2− species, suggesting the preservation of sulfide character within the composite.[ 28 , 30 ] These findings support the effective nucleation and growth of VS4 on both the surface and interlayer regions of the Ti3C2Tx nanosheets. Figure 3d reveals the Ti 2p spectrum, with distinct peaks at 455.4 eV (Ti─C), 458.3 eV (Ti2⁺), and 461.2 eV (Ti‐O), confirming the presence of MXene's typical surface terminations and partial oxidation states.[ 31 ] The C 1s spectrum, shown in Figure 3e, presents peaks at 281.5 eV (Ti─C), 284.5 eV (C─C/C═C), and 287.6 eV (C─O/C─S and O─C═O), indicating both intrinsic and surface‐modified carbon environments within the hybrid structure.[ 31 , 32 ] These spectroscopic results collectively verify the successful formation of a chemically bonded, heterogeneous VS4/MXene architecture with potential for high electrochemical performance.

The morphological characteristics and structural configuration of the pristine MXene, pure vanadium sulfide (VS4), and their composite hybrids can be thoroughly examined using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE‐SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Figure 4 shows the FE‐SEM images of the MXene, VS4, and their optimized composite VS‐MX100 structures, while supplementary Figures S2 and S3 (Supporting Information) display the MAX phase precursor and various composites with MXene concentration variation labeled as VS‐MX50, VS‐MX100, and VS‐MX200 at two magnifications, providing an in‐depth understanding of surface texture, morphology, and the degree of structural integration. Figure 4a,b reveals the characteristic multilayered, nanosheet morphology of exfoliated MXene. These sheets exhibit smooth surfaces with lateral dimensions in the microscale and stacked configurations typical of 2D lamellar materials.[ 26 ] Such a layered morphology ensures a high surface area and accessible interlayer spacing conducive to ion transport and efficient interaction with guest materials. The high‐magnification image confirms the preservation of the 2D structure, which plays a pivotal role in supporting redox‐active species and maintaining high electrical conductivity. The precursor MAX phase (Ti3AlC2), shown in Figure S2 (Supporting Information), exhibits a compact layered structure with well‐defined planes. Upon selective etching of Al layers during MXene synthesis, the resulting Ti3C2Tx retains the laminar framework while introducing surface terminations and interlayer gaps, crucial for enhanced charge storage and material dispersion.

FE‐SEM images taken at low (left column) and higher (right column) magnifications for the synthesized materials: a,b) Pure MXene; c,d) VS4; e,f) MXene‐VS4 composite (VS‐MX100).

Figure 4c,d, illustrates the morphology of pure VS4, which appears as vertically oriented, blade‐like nanosheets forming a porous, flower‐like architecture. This interconnected framework provides a high density of electroactive sites and interstitial voids, allowing efficient electrolyte infiltration and ion diffusion. However, due to its intrinsic brittleness and discontinuous structure, VS4 alone may exhibit limited electrical conductivity and mechanical durability during cycling, potentially compromising its electrochemical performance. In the case of the optimized hybrid VS‐MX100, shown in Figure 4e,f, VS4 nanosheets are uniformly distributed over the MXene layers. The composite maintains the sheet‐like morphology of VS4 while forming a hierarchically porous network with strong interfacial adhesion between the two components. This architecture facilitates effective electron and ion transport, restricts MXene sheet restacking, and accommodates volume variations during cycling. Such synergistic integration significantly enhances structural robustness, electrical conductivity, and electrochemically active surface area key attributes for high‐performance supercapattery electrodes.[ 28 ]

Figure S3 (Supporting Information) shows the morphological differences in the VS‐MX composites with various MXene concentrations. In the VS‐MX50 (Figure S3a,b, Supporting Information), the low MXene content leads to excessive VS4 accumulation and aggregation, disrupting interfacial uniformity, impeding ion diffusion, and risking structural degradation during cycling. The VS‐MX100 (Figure S3c,d, Supporting Information) exhibits an optimal MXene‐to‐VS4 ratio, enabling uniform dispersion of VS4 nanosheets, enhanced interfacial contact, and efficient charge transport, which are all important factors contributing to its superior electrochemical performance. In contrast, the VS‐MX200 (Figure S3e,f, Supporting Information), with excess MXene, shows sparse VS4 distribution and incomplete surface coverage, resulting in underutilized active sites and reduced redox activity. These findings confirm that the VS‐MX100 composite achieves the best balance of material distribution, structural integrity, and electrochemical accessibility, making it the most effective composition for supercapattery applications.[ 16 ]

This analysis elucidates the structural morphology, interfacial integration, and crystalline features of the individual and hybrid materials. Figure 5a–c shows the low‐ and high‐magnification images of MXene, revealing a stacked, layered nanosheet morphology with few‐layered thicknesses and smooth surfaces, typical of well‐exfoliated MXenes. The sheet‐like features with lateral extension in the microscale provide a large specific surface area and open interlayer spacing beneficial for electrolyte access and hybrid material anchoring. The HR‐TEM image (Figure 5c) displays well‐defined lattice fringes with high crystallinity, and the inset image of selected area electron diffraction (SAED) shows a sharp hexagonal spot pattern, confirming the ordered structure of the MXene.

Figure 5d–f display TEM images of the pure vanadium sulfide, exhibiting a vertically oriented, flower‐like nanosheet morphology with abundant porous features (Figure 5d). At higher magnifications, Figure 5e discloses randomly oriented VS4 nanocrystals with rough textures, and Figure 5f confirms the polycrystalline nature through distinct lattice fringes and concentric diffraction rings in the SAED pattern. The porous, interconnected morphology enables ample electroactive surface exposure and facilitates electrolyte penetration. Figure 5g–i, taken from the optimized composite (VS‐MX100), show a uniform distribution of VS4 nanosheets on the MXene surface, which confirms the successful formation of hybrid composites.[ 28 ] The low‐magnification image (Figure 5g) indicates intimate interaction between both components. Figure S4 (Supporting Information) presents TEM and HR‐TEM images of the VS‐MX composites fabricated with various MXene concentrations: VS‐MX50 (a–c), VS‐MX100 (d–f), and VS‐MX200 (g–i), to analyze their morphological features and interfacial structure. In the VS‐MX50, low MXene content leads to excessive VS4 accumulation, as seen in the aggregated domains (Figure S4a, Supporting Information) and irregular morphology (Figure S4b, Supporting Information). The HR‐TEM image (Figure S4c, Supporting Information) shows poorly defined interfaces and limited crystallinity, while the corresponding SAED pattern indicates partial hybridization. Such a morphology may hinder ion diffusion and reduce cyclic stability due to weak structural cohesion. In contrast, the VS‐MX100 exhibits uniform VS4 distribution on the MXene surface (Figure S4d–f, Supporting Information), revealing well‐aligned lattice fringes and coherent interfaces. The SAED pattern confirms the presence of both crystalline VS4 and MXene phases, indicating strong hybrid formation and improved ion/electron transport pathways that are key factors contributing to its high electrochemical performance. For the VS‐MX200, the excessive MXene content results in sparse VS4 coverage (Figure S4g, Supporting Information) and poor interfacial integration. HR‐TEM (S4(i)) and SAED analyses reveal weak crystallinity and limited contact between the components, leading to underutilization of active sites and diminished pseudocapacitive behavior.

Figure 6a presents a comprehensive analysis of the nitrogen (N2) adsorption–desorption isotherms for the MX, VS, and VS‐MX100 samples, providing valuable insights into their porous architectures and surface characteristics. As shown in Figure 6a, all three samples demonstrate characteristic type IV isotherms with distinct hysteresis loops, which are hallmark features of mesoporous materials.[ 16 ] These loops indicate the presence of capillary condensation within the mesopores, corroborating the development of well‐defined porous frameworks. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area analysis reveals values of 1.72 m2 g−1 for MX, 2.92 m2 g−1 for VS, and an enhanced 3.95 m2 g−1 for VS‐MX100, demonstrating that the integration of VS4 into the MXene matrix significantly improves the overall accessible surface area.

a) N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms and b) BJH pore size distribution curves of the MXene, VS4, and MXene‐VS4 composite (VS‐MX100) samples, confirming their mesoporous nature and enhanced surface area for improved ion diffusion and energy storage performance.

Further details are obtained from the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) pore size distribution analysis shown in Figure 6b, which determines average pore diameters of 6.29 nm (MX), 6.78 nm (VS), and 8.04 nm (VX‐MX100). The pore size distribution curves confirm that the majority of pores lie within the 6–10 nm range, characteristic of mesoporous frameworks capable of providing optimized pathways for electrolyte ion diffusion. The synergistic effect of increased surface area and optimized pore distribution in VS‐MX100 contributes to enhanced ion accessibility, reduced diffusion resistance, and efficient electron/ion transport. Such a hierarchical porous structure facilitates faster redox kinetics and improved electrochemical utilization of active sites, ultimately boosting the overall energy storage performance of the electrode materials.

As shown in Figure 7a, the CV curves recorded at a scan rate of 5 mV s−1 for the MXene, VS4, and VS‐MX100 electrodes reveal a significant enhancement in current response for the hybrid VS‐MX100. The expanded integrated area under the VS‐MX100 curve, relative to the individual components, underscores the improved charge storage originating from the synergistic interaction between the high conductivity of MXene and the Faradaic activity of VS4. The appearance of broad redox peaks in the hybrid confirms the presence of pseudocapacitive contributions from VS4, while the quasi‐rectangular shape reflects the capacitance behavior of MXene. This dual mechanism affirms the hybrid nature of the charge storage. To further optimize the electrode design, Figure S5a (Supporting Information) compares the CV profiles of VS‐MX composites with various MXene ratios. Among these, VS‐MX100 exhibits the highest current response and well‐defined redox features with the highest specific capacity of 489.54 C g−1 (815.91 F g−1). The VS‐MX50 shows diminished performance of 401.72 C g−1 (669.54 F g−1), due to VS4 aggregation from excess loading, while the VS‐MX200 presents distorted CV profiles with a lower capacity of 346.28 C g−1 (577.13 F g−1) and reduced current density, likely caused by poor VS4 dispersion and limited accessible surface area. These findings suggest that a balanced composition, as in the VS‐MX100, offers optimal redox accessibility and structural integrity.

The influence of CNT incorporation as a co‐conducting additive with CB on the electrochemical behavior of the VS‐MX100 composite is shown in Figure 7b. Various CNT concentrations (1–10 wt.%) were introduced into the VS‐MX100 composite. The CV curves demonstrate a progressive increase in current response with increasing CNT content, with the VS‐MX100‐CNT5 sample displaying the highest integrated area and highest specific capacity of 740.10 C g−1 (1233.5 F g−1) at 5 mV s−1. This enhancement is attributed to improved electron transport pathways, better interfacial contact with the electrolyte, and reinforced structural stability imparted by the CNT framework. The CV curves maintain quasi‐ideal shapes with subtle Faradaic features, indicating a well‐balanced capacitive and pseudocapacitive charge storage mechanism facilitated by the interconnected CNT‐CB network. Further, Figure 7c presents the CV profiles of the VS‐MX100‐CNT5 recorded at various scan rates from 5 to 100 mV s−1. The CV curves retain well‐defined, quasi‐rectangular shapes even at high scan rates, indicating excellent capacitive behavior, structural stability, and strong adhesion of the electrode in 6 M KOH electrolyte.[ 33 ] The observed decrease in current response with increasing scan rate suggests a diffusion‐controlled charge storage mechanism, where ion intercalation/deintercalation between the electrolyte and electrode material becomes increasingly limited at higher scan rates.[ 34 ] This indicates efficient electrochemical kinetics, rapid ion transport, and excellent reversibility, which are key attributes for high‐performance supercapattery electrodes.

Figure 7d compares the GCD behavior of the pristine MXene, VS4, and VS‐MX100. The VS‐MX100 hybrid exhibits the longest discharge duration and highest potential window (up to 0.6 V), in line with CV trends, indicating superior capacitance and energy storage capacity of 604.15 C g−1 (1006.92 F g−1). In contrast, the MXene and VS4 show shorter discharge times, resulting from lower pseudocapacitive storage capacity of 377.04 C g−1 (628.40 F g−1), and 513.42 C g−1 (855.71 F g−1), respectively, and limited electrical conductivity. As shown in Figure S5c (Supporting Information), the GCD profiles of VS‐MX composites further support the superiority of the VS‐MX100 composition with the highest capacity 604.15 C g−1 (1006.92 F g−1). While the VS‐MX50 exhibits rapid discharge and short duration due to limited active material and less capacity, the VS‐MX200 demonstrates irregular discharge curves suggestive of diffusion limitations and compromised charge transport from agglomerated VS4.[ 23 ] The optimized VS‐MX100 composite achieves the best compromise between redox kinetics and conductive network formation, leading to consistent and enhanced electrochemical behavior.

Figure 7e further investigates the effect of CNT addition on GCD performance of the optimized hybrid structure, VS‐MX100, at 0.3 A g−1 current density. The specific capacities of the VS‐MX100 and CNT‐reinforced VS‐MX100 electrodes are 604.15 C g−1 (1006.92 F g−1) for VS‐MX100, 489.85 C g−1 (816.43 F g−1) for VS‐MX100‐CNT1, 586.97 C g−1 (978.29 F g−1) for VS‐MX100‐CNT3. 802.46 C g−1 (1337.44 F g−1) for VS‐MX100‐CNT5 and 622.52 C g−1 (1037.53 F g−1) for VS‐MX100‐CNT10. This result shows that as the CNT content increases, discharge times increase progressively up to 5% CNT addition and decrease for higher CNT addition (10%), with the VS‐MX100‐CNT5 displaying the most extended discharge duration. This improvement is primarily attributed to CNTs acting as conductive scaffolds that bridge active sites, reduce internal resistance, and enhance ion diffusion. Among these electrodes, the VS‐MX100‐CNT5 electrode shows the highest storage capacity because of its optimized hybrid conductive network in the electrode.

As the CNT networks effectively integrate into the VS‐MX100 composite, the scaffold conductive network could ensure improved electrical conductivity and active material utilization. In contrast, the lower wt.% electrodes (1 and 3 wt.%; VS‐MX100‐CNT1 and VS‐MX100‐CNT3) decreased the capacity of the composite could be a result of an insufficient amount of CNTs. Moreover, the larger amount of CNT addition (10 wt.%; VS‐MX100‐CNT10) could encourage an irreversible capacity increase, and the thick CNT coating layer could distract facile redox ion pathways in the electrode.[ 35 , 36 , 37 ] Therefore, 5 wt.% of CNT is an optimal condition for an effective conductive network in the VS‐MX100 composite electrode (VS‐MX100‐CNT5).

Finally, Figure 7f presents the GCD curves of the VS‐MX100‐CNT5 across current densities ranging from 0.3 to 0.7 A g−1. The nearly symmetric profiles and consistent potential ranges across all current densities indicate excellent rate capability and structural stability. The low IR drop observed at higher currents further confirms the reduced resistance and superior conductivity of the CNT‐reinforced hybrid system, underscoring its suitability for high‐rate, long‐cycle supercapattery applications.

It is noteworthy that using CNTs as the sole conducting additive without CB may reduce the electrochemical performance because of their agglomeration. A set of control experiments using CNTs only as the conducting additive can confirm this point. Figure S6 (Supporting Information) shows the result; the CV profiles in Figure S6a (Supporting Information) and GCD curves in Figure S6b (Supporting Information) demonstrate that the CNT only cases overall show degraded performances compared to the CNT5 (i.e., CB + 5 wt.% CNTs) cases, for all the MXene, VS4, and VS–MX100 electrodes (Figure 7a,b,d,e). This suggests that CNTs used independently without CB base reduce electrochemical activity and overall capacitance, possibly by undesirably altering the surface morphology. This can be observationally confirmed by the FE‐SEM images shown in Figure S7 (Supporting Information), further clarifying how the surface morphology of VS–MX100 composite electrodes change depending on the type of conducting additives. Figure S7a (Supporting Information) with CB only shows the uniform and well‐distributed coverage of CB over the active material, enabling efficient electron transport. In contrast, Figure S7b (Supporting Information) with CNTs only reveals aggregation and clustering of CNTs, which can hinder ionic conduction and lower the electrode's performance. Figure S7c (Supporting Information) with the optimized CNT5 additive displays a homogeneous CB–CNT hybrid network, where CNTs act as conductive bridges connecting the active materials. This interconnected structure enhances charge transport, promotes better electrolyte accessibility, and leads to remarkably improved electrochemical performance.

Figure 8a presents the long‐term cyclic performance of the fabricated MXene, VS4, VS‐MX100, and VS‐MX100‐CNT5 electrodes over 5000 consecutive charge‐discharge cycles at a constant current density of 0.3 mA g−1. Among all tested configurations, the VS‐MX100‐CNT5 composite demonstrates exceptional cyclic stability, retaining ≈97% of its initial capacitance, indicative of excellent structural integrity and strong interfacial synergy among the VS4, MXene, and CNT network. In contrast, the pristine VS4 electrode exhibits significant capacity degradation, maintaining only ≈73% of its initial capacitance, primarily due to its poor intrinsic electrical conductivity and structural instability upon repeated cycling.[ 28 ] The improved electrochemical stability observed in the VS‐MX100 and VS‐MX100‐CNT5 electrodes can be ascribed to the combined effects of MXene's high electrical conductivity and CNTs’ mechanical resilience, which together create a robust conductive network that alleviates volumetric strain and facilitates efficient electron transport throughout cycling.

a,b) Electrochemical Performance Analysis: (a) Cyclic stability profiles, and (b) EIS results of the pristine MXene, pristine VS4, VS‐MX100, and VS‐MX100‐CNT5 electrodes. c,d) Kinetic Analysis: (c) Determination of the b‐values, and (d) Quantification of capacitive versus diffusion‐controlled contributions at various scan rates for the VS‐MX100 and VS‐MX100‐CNT5 electrodes.

Figure 8b shows the Nyquist plots derived from EIS, offering insights into charge‐transfer and ion‐diffusion behavior. In the high‐frequency region, the semicircle corresponds to an internal series resistance and charge‐transfer resistance, while the inclined line in the low‐frequency region indicates ion diffusion characteristics (Warburg impedance). The VS‐MX100‐CNT5 electrode exhibits the lowest internal resistance value of ≈0.15 Ω and a near‐vertical Warburg region, highlighting rapid interfacial charge‐transfer kinetics and enhanced ion accessibility.[ 38 ] In comparison, the pristine MXene and VS4 electrodes display higher impedance values, reflecting their limited charge mobility and sluggish ion diffusion. The significantly reduced impedance of the VS‐MX100‐CNT5 electrode emphasizes the beneficial role of CNTs in constructing a well‐interconnected 3D conductive network, promoting efficient charge carrier transport. In the corresponding results, Figure S8 (Supporting Information) presents the EIS fitting analyses and matched equivalent circuits of the electrodes obtained by using Zsimpwin simulation software. Figure S8a (Supporting Information) shows the fitted data and equivalent circuits for MXene, VS4, and the VS–MXene composite electrodes, while Figure S8b (Supporting Information) compares the simulation results for the VS–MX100 and VS–MX100–CNT5 electrodes. Both electrodes exhibit nearly identical equivalent circuit models; however, the VS–MX100–CNT5 electrode demonstrates a notably lower internal resistance, indicating that the incorporation of CNTs effectively enhances the electrical conductivity and facilitates faster charge transfer. This reduction in resistance directly contributes to the improved electrochemical performance of the electrode.

To further elucidate the charge storage mechanisms and the respective contributions of Faradaic (battery‐type) and non‐Faradaic processes in the VS‐MX100 and VS‐MX100‐CNT5 electrodes, power‐law and Trasatti plot analyses can be performed.[ 39 ] Figure 8c evaluates the charge storage kinetics by analyzing the relationship between current response and scan rate based on the power‐law equation (i = aνb ), where the b‐value indicates the dominant charge storage mechanism. The extracted b‐values are 0.5601 for the VS‐MX100 and 0.7212 for the VS‐MX100‐CNT5, suggesting that both systems operate via a mixed charge storage mechanism involving both diffusion‐controlled and capacitive processes. The higher b‐value of the VS‐MX100‐CNT5 suggests a greater pseudocapacitive contribution, likely facilitated by the increased accessible surface area and rapid redox activity supported by the CNT framework. Figure 8d further quantifies the capacitive and diffusive contributions across various scan rates through a kinetic deconvolution approach. At lower scan rates (e.g., 5 mV s−1), diffusion‐controlled processes dominate both systems. However, as the scan rate increases, the capacitive contribution becomes more prominent, particularly in the VS‐MX100‐CNT5, where fast surface redox reactions are favored due to enhanced electron mobility. Notably, at a high scan rate of 100 mV s−1, the capacitive contribution reaches ≈69% for the VS‐MX100‐CNT5, compared to ≈61% for the VS‐MX100, underscoring the effectiveness of CNTs in enhancing high‐rate charge storage behavior.

Overall, these results clearly demonstrate that the incorporation of CNTs as conductive additives in the VS‐MX100‐CNT5 hybrid electrode not only addresses the individual shortcomings of VS4 and MXene but also significantly enhances both capacitive and Faradaic charge storage mechanisms. This synergistic design approach offers a compelling pathway for engineering high‐performance electrodes tailored for next‐generation supercapattery applications.

Electrochemical Analysis of the Asymmetric Supercapattery Device (ASD)

Figure 9a illustrates the CV study of the fabricated ASD at various potential windows ranging from 0.6 to 1.6 V under ambient conditions. The inset plot displays CV curves recorded at scan rates from 5 to 100 mV s−1 within a fixed window of 1.6 V. A progressive increase in current response with increasing scan rate confirms the hybrid charge storage characteristics of the device.[ 40 ] Notably, the ASD achieved a specific capacity of 192. C g−1 (equivalent to 320.05 F g−1) at a scan rate of 5 mV s−1. However, this value is lower than the capacity measured in a three‐electrode configuration, primarily due to factors such as differences in cell resistance, incomplete electrode utilization, and uneven potential distribution across the two‐electrode system. In contrast, the three‐electrode setup allows precise evaluation of intrinsic material properties due to the presence of a reference electrode, minimizing interfacial losses.[ 41 ] Figure 9b presents the GCD profiles at different current densities, which exhibit quasi‐triangular shapes with mild slopes indicative of a hybrid supercapattery‐type behavior involving both surface capacitive and battery‐type intercalation processes. This dual behavior is consistent with the electrochemical nature of the VS‐MX100‐CNT5 electrode, where the combined effects of ion adsorption/desorption and intercalation/deintercalation govern the charge storage.[ 42 ]

Electrochemical evaluation of the assembled ASD employing a PVA–KOH polymer gel electrolyte. a) CV curves recorded at a scan rate of 5 mV s−1 at various potentials ranging from 0.6–1.6 V; the inset displays CV responses at various scan rates from 5 to 100 mV s−1 in a 1.6 V window. b) GCD profiles at current densities ranging from 0.3 to 0.7 A g−1. c) Nyquist plot derived from EIS, providing insight into charge‐transfer resistance and ion diffusion behavior. d) Cyclic stability of the device over 5000 charge–discharge cycles, demonstrating long‐term electrochemical durability.

The energy storage performance of the ASD is further quantified, delivering an excellent specific capacity of 148.18 C g−1 (246.97 F g−1), high energy density of 12.34 Wh kg−1, and a power density of 1856.43 W kg−1 at a current density of 0.3 A g−1. The Nyquist plot in Figure 9c reveals an internal series resistance of 0.4760 Ω, confirming low ohmic losses and favorable charge transport kinetics. Figure S8c (Supporting Information) shows the fitted data and equivalent circuits for the ASD. Long‐term cycling (Figure 9d) evaluated over 5000 continuous GCD cycles at 0.5 A g−1 shows outstanding capacitance retention of 93% and Coulombic efficiency of 98% after an initial decrease likely due to partial loss of electroactive material. This excellent stability can be attributed to the reversible ion adsorption and intercalation processes facilitated by the structurally robust MXene‐VS4‐CNT composite architecture.

The advantage of our device can be appreciated by comparing with commercial activated carbon (CAC) and recently reported similar ASD systems. For instance, CAC‐based symmetric devices in 1 M Na2SO4 have exhibited ≈990 mF (≈494 mF cm−2), an energy density of ≈13 mWh cm−3, a power density of ≈2189 mW cm−3, and 97% retention after 5000 cycles.[ 43 ] A similar CAC device in 6 M KOH, achieved an energy density of 8.47 Wh kg−1, a power density of 1000–4000 W kg−1, and 97.16% retention after 5000 cycles.[ 44 ] The optimized VS‐MX100‐CNT5 composite electrode outperforms the other electrode materials employing similar constituents, MXene and/or VS4. A VS4/MXene ASD using a PVA–K2SO4 gel electrolyte (1.3 V) has exhibited an aerial capacitance of 70.9 mF cm−2 at 5 mV s−1, with an energy density of 5.65 mWh cm−2, a power density of 290.9 mW cm−2, and 75% retention after 3000 cycles.[ 20 ] A MXene–MnO2 ASD with a PVA‐H2SO4 electrolyte (1.5 V) has delivered a capacitance of 20.5 F g−1 at 1.5 A g−1, along with 6.4 Wh kg−1 energy density, 1107.7 W kg−1 power density, and 84% retention after 3000 cycles at 2 A g−1.[ 45 ] Collectively, these results showcase the remarkable balance of energy and power density of the hybrid electrode architecture developed in this study, underscoring its strong potential for advanced energy storage applications.

Conclusion

In summary, this study reports the rationally engineered composite comprising VS4 nanosheets uniformly anchored onto 2D MXene (Ti3C2Tx) sheets through a facile solvothermal method. The composite could be further reinforced with CNTs to construct a 3D conductive network for advanced supercapattery applications. The design leverages the high redox activity of VS4, the excellent conductivity and surface functionality of MXene, and the mechanical reinforcement and electron bridging capabilities of CNTs to overcome the individual limitations of each component. Systematic variation in MXene concentration revealed that the VS‐MX100 composition offered the most favorable morphology, characterized by uniform VS4 dispersion, optimal interfacial integration, and enhanced structural cohesion, crucial parameters for efficient ion/electron transport and stable cycling behavior. Further incorporation of CNTs into the VS‐MX100 composite (resulting in the VS‐MX100‐CNT5 formulation) produced a hierarchically structured, multidimensional conductive network. This architecture not only minimized electrodes' internal resistance (≈0.15 Ω, as confirmed by EIS) but also promoted rapid ion diffusion and robust interfacial kinetics. As a result, the VS‐MX100‐CNT5 electrode exhibited an impressive specific capacity of 802.46 C g−1 (1337.44 F g−1) at 0.3 A g−1 in 6 M KOH, with 97% capacitance retention over 5000 cycles, reflecting excellent durability and electrochemical reversibility. Kinetic analyses confirmed a hybrid charge storage mechanism, with a b‐value of 0.7212, indicative of a balanced contribution from both surface‐driven pseudocapacitance and diffusion‐controlled intercalation. At high scan rates (100 mV s−1), the capacitive contribution of the CNT‐reinforced system reached ≈69%, significantly surpassing the ≈61% observed in the non‐reinforced counterpart, thus affirming the critical role of CNTs in boosting rate capability and electroactive site utilization. Moreover, the VS‐MX100‐CNT5‐based ASD demonstrated a high specific capacity of 192 C g−1 (320.05 F g−1) at 5 mV s−1, operating efficiently within a broad potential window of 1.6 V. The device delivered a notable specific energy of 12.34 Wh kg−1 and a specific power of 1856.43 W kg−1 at 0.3 A g−1, alongside excellent cyclic stability with 93% capacitance retention over 5000 cycles. These findings underline the synergistic effect of VS4, MXene, and CNTs in forming a robust and conductive hybrid architecture that supports fast charge transport, high surface utilization, and long‐term durability. The insights provided herein pave the way for the development of next‐generation supercapattery materials, offering strong potential for future scalable and high‐power energy applications.
