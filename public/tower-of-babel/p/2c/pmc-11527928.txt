The economic burden of varicella among children in France: a caregiver survey
Authors: Salome Samant, Hervé Haas, Joana Santos, David R Mink, Richard Pitman, Tanaz Petigara, Manjiri Pawaskar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo estimate the economic burden of varicella among children in France from family and societal perspectives. We conducted an online cross-sectional survey of caregivers of 185 French children (≤ 17 years) diagnosed with varicella in the previous six weeks. Data collected included varicella-related healthcare resource use, out-of-pocket costs, missed school days, and workdays missed by either parents or other caregivers. Mean and median direct and indirect costs (2022 Euros) were calculated using survey data and published literature. The annual societal burden of pediatric varicella in France was estimated. Of the 185 children, 95.1% had ≥ 1 outpatient visit, 10.3% had ≥ 1 emergency room visit, and 2.2% were hospitalized. The median [interquartile range, IQR] number of outpatient visits/child was 1.0 [1.0 – 2.0]. The median length of hospital stay among those hospitalized (n = 4) was 5.0 [3.5 – 6.5] days. Caregivers of 185 children missed a median of 2.0 [0 – 5.0] workdays; 113 families (61.1%) had ≥ 1 parent miss work. A median of 5.0 [3.0 – 7.0] school days were missed by 170 children who attended preschool or school. The median direct out-of-pocket cost to the family was €30.0 [€17.0 – €60.0]. The median [IQR] societal costs per varicella case were €455.2 [€70.5 – €1013.5]; the median [IQR] direct and indirect costs per child were €60.8 [€39.0 – €102.7] and €364.7 [€0.0 – €911.7] respectively. The annual societal burden of varicella among children in France was estimated to be €450,427,578 (95% CI: €357,144,618 – €543,710,538), with indirect costs accounting for 85%.Conclusions: A substantial economic burden is associated with pediatric varicella in France, primarily due to the productivity loss among caregivers. Introduction

Varicella-zoster virus is highly contagious and causes varicella (chickenpox) upon primary infection [1, 2]. Though it is generally a mild disease, it can sometimes lead to complications such as skin infections or pneumonia that may require hospitalization [1–3]. Most (> 90%) of the annual 550,000 - 700,000 varicella cases in France are among children below ten years of age [4–6]. Varicella is also associated with 3,500 hospitalizations and 20 deaths each year [6]. A systematic review of seroprevalence studies estimated the annual incidence of varicella in France to be 13,488 per 100,000 persons (95% C.I. 12,658–14,252) among children below five years [4], while the incidence of hospital admission is estimated to be 23 per 100,000 persons among children under 15 years of age [3, 7, 8].

A substantial decrease in varicella morbidity and mortality has been observed in countries where universal varicella vaccination (UVV) has been implemented, including Germany, Italy, and the United States (US) [3, 6, 9–13]. Despite the World Health Organization's (WHO) recommendation for varicella vaccination and substantial evidence of the effectiveness of UVV programs, several European countries, including France, have yet to include UVV as part of their national immunization programs [1, 3, 6, 11]. Varicella vaccination is recommended only for high-risk groups or those aged 11–12 years without prior varicella disease in France [14]. Although varicella is considered a mild disease, it poses a substantial burden to caregivers and society [11, 15]. There is a lack of recent data quantifying the economic and societal burden of varicella in France, which is necessary to understand both the full burden of managing varicella patients to society and the healthcare system and the potential value of the UVV program [11, 15].

This study aimed to quantify the economic burden of varicella among children in France from both family and societal perspectives.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a non-interventional, cross-sectional survey study conducted among parents or legal guardians of children aged 17 years or younger diagnosed with varicella in the previous six weeks and residing in France. Respondents were excluded if their child was vaccinated against varicella since the focus of this study was to evaluate the economic burden of varicella in unvaccinated children. Varicella in vaccinated children is rarer, milder, and shorter in duration than varicella in an unvaccinated child [1, 3, 6, 11]. The survey collected data on the sociodemographic characteristics of parents/guardians and their children, the healthcare resource utilization (HCRU), and costs associated with varicella, including doctor and emergency room (ER) visits, hospitalizations, medications, and out-of-pocket expenses incurred by the family. Information was also collected on schooldays missed by the sick child, workdays lost by the parents or other caregivers to care for the sick child, and the impact on caregiving arrangements. Respondents who had multiple sick children with varicella were asked to consider only the youngest eligible child in their responses.

The family perspective included only the direct out-of-pocket expenses incurred by the family; that is, direct out-of-pocket medical expenses (such as co-payments made by the family for a doctor’s visit or medications, and not reimbursed by social security or private insurance) and direct non-medical costs (such as payments made by the family for transportation, paid caregivers, or isolation of high-risk family members). The societal perspective included the direct medical and non-medical costs incurred by the family, the French social security system, or private insurance, and the indirect costs due to productivity losses [16, 17].

Survey development

The survey was developed after conducting a targeted literature review of instruments available to assess caregiver burden associated with varicella or other pediatric infectious diseases, along with inputs from a pediatric infectious disease expert in France. It was translated into French, verified by a native French speaker, and validated through cognitive pre-testing (See Supplementary Information 1). The study was approved by the Commission Nationale de l'Informatique et des Libertes (CNIL) and the ethics committee, Comités de Protection des Personnes Ouest IV – Nantes, France.

Participants were recruited by a third-party recruitment agency, Global Perspectives, through a multifaceted approach, including referrals of potentially eligible parents from family physicians, pediatricians, and pharmacists from across France; social media/digital outreach; advertisement through daycare, schools, and other locations; and a general population panel. Quotas were used to ensure that the study sample reflected the age distribution of varicella cases in France and the regional distribution of the French population, with restrictions put to avoid over-representation of certain groups; e.g. limiting children below one year of age (≤ 10%), 13 years or older (≤ 20%), and children requiring hospitalization (≤ 10%) [3, 18]. The final web-based survey was fielded from April 2022 to July 2022, after the end of COVID-19 lockdowns and school closures in France, to capture the burden of varicella accurately [19, 20]. After screening, eligible participants were informed about the study procedure, including the risks/benefits, and completed an electronic informed consent form before survey participation. Participants were paid an honorarium for their time. All data were anonymized. A copy of the survey in English is included in the supplementary information 2.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to analyze the demographic characteristics of parents and children, HCRU, and missed work or school days. Analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC); p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Data were summarized overall and by age strata (< 3 years, 3 to < 6 years, 6 to < 10 years, and ≥ 10 years), using means, standard deviations (SD), medians, and interquartile ranges [IQR] for continuous variables and counts and percentages for categorical ones. Mann Whitney, analysis of variance (ANOVA), and chi-square tests were conducted as appropriate. Using unit cost data from the literature (see supplementary information 1) and HCRU and productivity loss data from the study, a micro-costing approach was employed to calculate direct and indirect costs for varicella for each child [21, 22]. All costs were reported in 2022 Euros. Given that the response to questions 1 through 36 was obligatory, and surveys that were not completed in their entirety were omitted from the principal set for analysis, there was no need for imputation of any missing data.

For the direct cost calculations, we assumed that the family paid out-of-pocket for all direct medical costs not covered by the French social security system and insurance [23]. Since the exact burden to the family varies depending on the type of HCRU, income, and type of private insurance, we assumed, for the primary analysis, that the French social security system bore 70%, 80%, and 65% of the total costs associated with medical visits, hospitalizations, and medications respectively, to avoid double counting [17, 24–26]. These costs (calculated for each child using HCRU data reported in the survey and unit costs from the French health department) were added to the various out-of-pocket direct costs reported by the family to provide the mean total direct cost [24–27]. We also conducted a scenario analysis where we assumed that the family had no direct medical expenses and that the French social security system or private insurance bore 100% of the total costs associated with medical visits, hospitalizations, and medications. These were added to the out-of-pocket expenses reported by the family to provide the mean direct medical cost for the scenario analysis.

Productivity losses were calculated using a human capital approach [28]. Indirect costs were calculated by multiplying the missed workdays by parents and unpaid caregivers (e.g., grandparents or other family members and friends, but not paid caregivers) with an average daily wage of €182.3/day, derived by dividing the annual wage in France by 220 working days [29]. The societal cost was obtained by summing the direct and indirect costs. The mean societal cost per child with varicella from our study was multiplied by the annual number of pediatric varicella cases in France (525,184 cases in 2019) reported to the French health sentinel surveillance system to estimate the annual societal burden [5].

Results

Of the 224 individuals who accessed the survey, 188 (83.9%) met the eligibility criteria, 185 (82.6%) completed all mandatory questions and formed the final data set. This (n = 185) was a convenience sample (See Supplementary information 1).

Demographic characteristics

The median age of the respondents or caregivers was 34.0 [31.0 – 38.0]; most were female (82.7%). In addition, 63.8% of respondents and 73.0% of their partners were employed full-time. Almost 70% of families had both public and private insurance (Table 1). In the survey, the median age of children with varicella was 3.0 [2.0 – 6.0] years, with 40% between 3 and 6 years of age. Slightly more than half were male (54.1%). Almost two-thirds of children (64.3%) attended preschool and 26.5% attended grade school (Table 1). Respondents were recruited from across France; Ile-de-France (27.6%), Hauts-de-France (16.8%), and Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur (15.7%) were the most common regions.

Healthcare utilization and medication use

Nearly all children (95.1%) with varicella had ≥ 1 outpatient visit. The median number of outpatient visits was 1.0 [1.0 – 2.0] per child (See Supplementary Information 1). Most visits (81.6%) were to the general practitioner; 10.3% of children had at least one ER visit. Four children were hospitalized, and the median length of hospital stay was 5.0 [3.5 – 6.5] days. While 91.9% and 55.7% of all 185 children received analgesics and antihistamines, respectively, 14.6% received at least one antibiotic, and 10.3% received at least one antiviral.

Missed school days and caregiving arrangements

A median of 5.0 [3.0 – 7.0] school days were missed among 170 children usually attending school (See Supplementary Information 1). Due to varicella, approximately 40% of children could not attend daycare or have their usual paid caregiver at home for at least one day.

Productivity loss

Overall, 113 children (61.1%) had at least one parent take at least one day off to care for the sick child, resulting in 618 workdays lost (Table 2). This resulted in a median productivity loss of 2.0 [0.0 – 5.0] days across parents of all 185 children. Accounting for additional productivity losses among grandparents, family members, or friends to care for the sick child led to a total of 740 days lost; a median of 2.0 [0.0—5.0] days were lost per child for the full sample.

Economic burden of varicella

The total median [IQR] and mean (SD) direct cost per child incurred by the family was €30.0 [€17.0 – €60.0] and €53.8 (€72.2) respectively, primarily due to out-of-pocket expenses related to medical visits and non-medical services such as transportation (Table 3). In the primary analysis, the median (IQR) and mean (SD) total cost from the societal perspective was €455.2 (€70.5 – €1013.5) and €857.7 (€1232.6) per child, respectively. About 85% or €729.4 (SD €1088.9) was due to productivity losses (corresponding to a median [IQR] of €364.7 [€0.0 – €911.7]) and the remaining €128.3 (SD €289.9) due to direct costs (corresponding to a median [IQR] of €60.8 [€39.0 – €102.7]). Total mean and median societal costs were highest for children in the 6 to 10-year age group (mean (SD): €1119.8 (€1602.2), median [IQR]: €590.3 [€72.3 – €1124.6]), driven by hospitalization and indirect costs. Three of the four children hospitalized for varicella were in this age group. The median [IQR] and mean (SD) societal costs in the scenario analysis (i.e., where the French social security system or private insurance bore 100% of the total direct medical cost) were €464.3 [€79.7 – €1032.1] and €884.0 (€1269.7) respectively (Supplement 1). These costs were slightly higher than in the primary analysis due to the higher mean direct cost per child.

Discussion

This study assessed the economic burden of pediatric varicella in France from family and societal perspectives. Our study estimated the median and mean societal cost of varicella to be €455.2 and €857.7 per child, of which 85% was due to productivity losses. Out-of-pocket direct costs incurred by the family averaged €53.8 per child (median €30.0). Although varicella is generally considered a clinically mild disease, our study highlighted a substantial economic burden of pediatric varicella in France, ranging from an estimated €450 million to €464 million annually, depending on the scenario. The study also provides unique insights into the work and school days missed due to varicella.

Almost two-thirds (61%) of children had parents or their partners take at least one day off due to their sick child, with a mean of 3.3 and a median of 2.0 workdays missed by parents across all 185 children. The number of missed workdays was slightly higher (mean 4.0 days; median 2.0 days) when productivity losses among unpaid caregivers, like grandparents, family, or friends, were included. A previous study conducted in France in 1995 assessed workdays lost among 200 children under three years of age with varicella in a daycare setting, almost all with both parents employed. Comparable to our study, 52% of those children had parents or relatives take time off (4.5 work days on average) [30]. Another French study conducted in 2003 found that a much lower proportion (17%) of children < 13 years of age had parents miss work (mean 4.7 days). However, results from both these studies could be influenced by differences in the study design and changing trends in employment, including hybrid work options and participation of mothers in the workforce over the past two decades in France [31]. A recent systematic literature review found that the average number of days off work parents take to care for their sick child with varicella varied from 0.27 to 8.8 days [32].

There is limited literature on the impact of varicella on school attendance. Our study assessed the impact of varicella on children attending preschool and school. Children missed a median of 5.0 days of school, this number ranges from 4 to 5 across age groups. Schooldays missed may impact learning opportunities and quality of life, placing an additional burden on children to catch up on missed schoolwork, especially among older children [15, 33, 34].

Most children had at least one outpatient visit. The mean number of outpatient visits reported in our study (1.5 visits/child) is comparable to previous studies in the UK, France, Hungary, and Poland [31, 35, 36]. A recent global systematic literature review found that the mean number of outpatient visits for varicella patients ranged from 0.04 to 2.2 per case [32]. Only four children in our study were hospitalized, so the interpretability of that data is limited.

The mean societal cost of €857.7 per child under the primary analysis is a conservative estimate since we assumed that the families paid all the costs not covered by the French social security system and that the French social security system bore only 70%, 80%, and 65% of the total costs associated with medical visits, hospitalizations, and medications respectively. However, this may be an underestimation of the direct costs since approximately 70% of families in our study had some supplementary private insurance, and the French healthcare system bears 100% of the costs for families with financial hardships [23, 37]. In a scenario analysis, we assumed that the French social security system or private insurance covered all direct medical costs, leading to an increase in the mean (€857.7 to €884.0) and the median societal cost (€455.2 to €464.3) per child. The national burden also increased from €450 million to €464 million annually with the scenario analysis. The primary driver of varicella-related costs was productivity losses among parents and caregivers. These costs accounted for 85% of total costs, consistent with other studies reporting a much higher burden associated with indirect than direct costs [3, 32, 35]. We included productivity loss by either parents or unpaid care provided by family and friends. Mean direct costs were highest among children 6 to 9 years old, primarily driven by the four hospitalized children in this age group.

When reviewing the economic burden of varicella in European countries without UVV, a study found that France and Germany (before UVV implementation in Germany) accounted for almost half of the economic burden of varicella in Europe, primarily driven by larger populations and higher healthcare costs [38]. Since implementing a 2-dose UVV program, Germany has substantially reduced varicella incidence (> 70%) [3, 6]. France is estimated to have 550,000–750,000 varicella cases annually, with 3,500 hospitalizations and 20 deaths that could be largely prevented by UVV [3, 6, 7]. Our study further provides evidence of the economic and caregiver burden of varicella in France.

Limitations

This was a cross-sectional survey study with all the limitations inherent to the survey design, including selection bias, recall bias, inaccurate comprehension of survey questions by respondents, and limited generalizability of results from our small study to all pediatric varicella cases across France. This was a convenience sample with a limited number of children in each age category and may not be representative of all French children and their caregivers. The data collected in our survey, including the varicella diagnosis and medication history, were self-reported and not confirmed by medical records. There may be some recall bias, which we tried to minimize by limiting the study to caregivers of children who experienced varicella in the past six weeks. We did not capture the employment status of grandparents who provided child care. Days contributed by grandparents were assumed to add to productivity loss, even if unemployed, as their time has value. Though we used published mean healthcare costs from government sources, we made some assumptions that might have influenced the cost analysis, e.g., the proportion of cost borne by the family or using the price of the most common drug in that class (see footnote of Table 3 and supplementary information 1 for details on assumptions). The actual disease burden may be higher than the estimates from sentinel surveillance networks (525,184 cases of pediatric varicella reported in France in 2019) [5], which capture only those seeking medical consultation from general physicians, leading to possible underestimation of the national economic burden in our study. It is also possible that some parents may refrain from seeking any medical care for their sick children, leading to no direct medical cost for such patients. Similarly, temporary relaxation in work-from-home policies post-pandemic may have reduced the missed workdays reported, potentially underestimating the societal burden in our study [19, 20]. However, to minimize this impact, the survey data was collected after the end of lockdowns associated with the COVID-19 pandemic.

Conclusion

A substantial economic burden is associated with pediatric varicella in France, primarily due to high productivity losses among parents and caregivers. Our study findings may be of interest to policymakers in France and other European countries who want to evaluate the benefits of UVV programs.
