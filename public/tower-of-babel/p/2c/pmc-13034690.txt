Giant cervical facet joint osteophyte causing myelopathy: illustrative case
Authors: Brian Hall, Madison Michels, Hilary Ellen Jordan, Stepan Capek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Osteophytic degeneration of the cervical spine is pervasive, with spondylosis being the most common progressive disorder of the cervical spine.1 This is a natural process of aging; osteophyte proliferation is a compensatory change to support load-bearing and limit pathological movement of a degenerative spinal segment.2 These growths, however, pose significant risk to the spinal cord, and compressive myelopathy is a frequent occurrence in the setting of cervical spondylosis and osteophyte proliferation. Most cord compression caused by osteophytes results from growth from the posterior aspect of the vertebral body or ossification of the ligamentous structures that line the central canal, namely the posterior longitudinal ligament and ligamentum flavum.3 When degenerative spinal changes result in osteophytic proliferation of the cervical facet joints, these growths tend to project posterolaterally, causing neural stenosis or vertebral artery compression rather than encroaching on cord territory.4 To our knowledge, myelopathy due to a facet joint osteophyte occupying the central canal in the cervical spine has never been described. Herein, we present a case of a giant cervical facet joint osteophyte presenting with myelopathy and propose a theory describing the pathogenesis of this unique clinical entity.

Illustrative Case

A 57-year-old right-handed male presented to the outpatient neurosurgery clinic for symptoms of progressive weakness in both upper and lower extremities along with hand numbness and tingling that had worsened over the past few years. The patient’s past medical history was significant for aortic stenosis and atrial fibrillation managed with apixaban and aspirin; his social history was significant for occupation as a waiter as well as alcohol use disorder and tobacco use. Specifically, the patient reported holding his right arm elevated for extended periods of time. The patient’s family history was noncontributory.

Initial neurological examination revealed intact cranial nerves and 5/5 motor strength. Sensory findings were decreased sensation in distal upper and lower extremities. Reflex findings were +3 in upper extremities, +4 in lower extremities, positive Hoffmann’s sign bilaterally, and positive clonus bilaterally. MRI revealed a 2-cm extradural lesion without significant enhancement around the C5 level in the central canal showing evidence of spinal cord compression (Fig. 1). CT confirmed a calcified extradural mass at the right C5–6 joint (Fig. 2). Differential diagnosis included a calcified peripheral nerve sheath tumor as schwannoma or primary bone tumor of the spine as osteochondroma. Given the imaging appearance, malignant lesions were considered less likely.

Axial (A) and sagittal (B) T2-weighted MR images demonstrating an osseous lesion (arrows) with spinal cord compression (asterisk).

Axial (A), sagittal (B), and coronal (C) CT images demonstrating an osseous space-occupying lesion (arrows) compromising the central canal, suggestive of an osteophyte arising from the right C5–6 facet joint (arrow in C).

Given the patient’s grossly myelopathic symptoms at presentation and diagnosis of a calcified extradural mass, he was indicated for cervical laminectomy and resection in addition to short-segment posterolateral fusion. The patient underwent uncomplicated navigation-assisted complete laminectomy at C4 and C5 as well as partial laminectomy of C6, which revealed a large bony growth projecting superomedially from the right C5–6 facet joint and causing significant compression of the thecal sac and displacement of its contents to the left of the central canal, with the right C5 and C6 nerve roots stretched around the lesion. The growth was drilled off the facet joint with the residual removed en bloc, carefully preserving stretched nerves that were adherent to the mass. Intraoperatively, the growth was found to arise from the facet joint and consisted of 2 pieces connected with a cartilaginous joint-like structure confirming this to be an osteophyte. The thecal sac as well as the nerves were free of compression and assumed their natural position. After resection, we proceeded with instrumented fusion in the form of lateral mass screws at C4–6 with decellularized bone matrix and morselized bone autograft. The wound was closed uneventfully. Neuronavigation was utilized to aid with lateral mass screw placement, but proved to be very valuable in delineation of the mass under stretched spinal nerves. Neuromonitoring was utilized and showed a brief decline in right hand motor evoked potentials, which returned to baseline by conclusion of the surgery. Pathology showed normal bony overgrowth of the facet joint without atypia, consistent with an osteophyte. The patient experienced an uncomplicated postsurgical course before being discharged with a hard cervical collar. Preoperative and postoperative upright radiographs showed stable spinal alignment (Fig. 3).

Preoperative anteroposterior (A) and lateral (B) upright cervical spine radiographs. Postoperative anteroposterior (C) and lateral (D) cervical spine radiographs demonstrating stable spinal alignment.

At the 1-month follow-up, the patient exhibited remarkable recovery, tolerating activities of daily living and exercise and reporting only mild balance problems. He denied neck pain or sensory deficits and exhibited full range of motion on removal of his cervical collar. Deep tendon reflexes were +2 in all extremities and clonus was absent, although Hoffmann’s sign was still present bilaterally.

Discussion

Observations

Giant facet joint osteophytes represent a seldom-described entity. Projections into the spinal canal from the posterior elements have been described, but these cases do not represent the pathology detailed above.5 One case report documented a facet osteophyte causing myelopathy in the thoracic spine; however, this lesion was much smaller and more focal than the osteophyte seen in our patient.6 Another case detailed a large osteophyte arising from the neural arch and causing myelopathy, but this growth was partially cartilaginous, a histological finding unique from that in our patient that led to the diagnosis of solitary exostosis. Other giant osteophytes seem to arise from ligamentous structures, either projecting anteriorly from an ossified anterior longitudinal ligament or commonly posteriorly forming a bridging spur over an immobilized disc space in the absence of facet arthropathy.3,5 In the absence of an understood process underlying our patient’s presentation, we propose a theory describing a cyclic interaction of asymmetric joint stress and bone proliferation.

It has been established that sagittally oriented facet joints in the lumbar spine are more predisposed to degenerative changes.7,8 Similarly, an asymmetrically oriented facet that forms through anomalous development, trauma, or, as in the case of our right-handed patient, the repetitive stress of carrying objects overhead with his right arm during his lifelong career as a waiter may have a high likelihood of degeneration.9 It then follows that osteophyte proliferation within the facet joint would serve to stabilize and counteract asymmetric load and coronal imbalance. These osteophytes would then project horizontally by a similar mechanism to the traction osteophytes seen in degenerative vertebral bodies.10 As bone forms on the medial aspect of the facet joint and the direction of stress gradually changes, a vicious cycle of osteophyte proliferation and joint remodeling ensues, resulting in a large, hyperproliferative osteophyte that develops medially from the facet joint and progressively occludes the central canal.

Lessons

This case report describes a rare instance of central canal stenosis and myelopathy due to a giant facet osteophyte in the cervical spine, an instance not previously documented in the literature. This novel presentation of cervical spondylotic myelopathy reinforces that facet joint osteophytes, although rare, can be a cause of central canal stenosis and cord compression in the cervical spine. The proposed mechanism of asymmetric joint stress and osteophyte proliferation, potentially exacerbated by the patient’s occupational repetitive stress, offers a novel hypothesis for the pathogenesis of such lesions.
