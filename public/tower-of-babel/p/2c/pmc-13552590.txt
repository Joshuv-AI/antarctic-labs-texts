In Vitro Performance of Valved Holding Chambers in Paediatric Asthma Controller Therapy
Authors: Laura Ojanperä, Lauri Lehtimäki, Péter Csonka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimResearch on the drug delivery of inhaled corticosteroids and combination medications via valved holding chambers (VHCs) with children's breathing patterns is limited. We aimed to evaluate the in vitro performance of commercially available VHCs without facemasks under preschooler breathing conditions, focusing on total delivered dose and fine particle dose (FPD).MethodsWe evaluated the FPD in 1–5 μm range of fluticasone/salmeterol combination inhaler, fluticasone propionate mono‐inhaler, and ciclesonide delivered through three VHCs: AeroChamber Plus Flow‐Vu, EasyChamber, and OptiChamber Diamond. Drug delivery was measured using a Next Generation Impactor with simulated breathing representative of a 4‐year‐old child.ResultsThe total delivered dose compared to the label claim was highest for ciclesonide with all the VHCs. The median FPD in 1–5 μm range was significantly higher with EasyChamber compared to AeroChamber and OptiChamber for salmeterol and fluticasone from combination‐inhaler and ciclesonide. Overall, relative throat deposition was lowest for ciclesonide and highest for fluticasone mono‐inhaler. OptiChamber yielded lowest relative throat depositions for ciclesonide and fluticasone mono‐inhaler, while EasyChamber gave lowest relative throat depositions for salmeterol and fluticasone from combination inhaler.ConclusionThere were notable differences between the tested VHC's drug delivery. Ciclesonide exhibited favourable aerodynamic properties than fluticasone.Keywords: ciclesonide, drug delivery, fluticasone, salmeterol, valved holding chambers

Introduction

Asthma is a leading cause of childhood morbidity and the most common chronic illness requiring daily medication [1, 2]. According to the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA), the preferred device for controller therapy in preschool children is a pressurised metered‐dose inhaler (pMDI) used with a valved holding chamber (VHC) and mouthpiece [3]. However, limited co‐operation of young children and variability in respiratory rate (RR) and tidal volume (Vt) challenge efficient drug delivery in this age group, particularly during exacerbations and acute wheezing episodes.

Chronic airway inflammation is primarily treated with inhaled corticosteroids (ICS). Fluticasone propionate (hereafter fluticasone) is recommended as the primary choice for children requiring ICS therapy via a pMDI [3]. For the most severe cases, the optimal treatment strategy remains uncertain. Combination therapy with an ICS and a long‐acting β2‐agonist (LABA), specifically fluticasone/salmeterol fixed combination‐inhaler, has been proposed as an option. However, due to insufficient safety and efficacy data in children under five years of age, its use remains off‐label in this population [3]. Ciclesonide has shown a favourable safety profile in children [4] and comparable efficacy to other ICS used in preschooler asthma treatment, yet data remain insufficient to fully support its use in this age group since in a clinical trial comparing ciclesonide to placebo failed to meet their primary endpoint time to first severe asthma exacerbation [5].

The aerodynamic particle size distribution (APSD) is a key determinant of drug delivery to therapeutically relevant regions of the respiratory tract. Optimal lung deposition occurs when particles fall within the respirable range of 1–5 μm, whereas in children an even narrower range of 1–3 μm has been proposed as ideal [6, 7]. VHCs differ in volume, material, electrostatic properties, and valve design, all of which may influence APSD and subsequent drug deposition [8, 9]. In our previous in vitro study of fluticasone, VHCs showed significant variability in drug delivery under simulated paediatric breathing conditions [10]. However, no published data describe how different VHCs affect the in vitro drug delivery of fluticasone/salmeterol combination inhaler and ciclesonide under simulated paediatric tidal breathing.

This study aimed to evaluate fine particle delivery, total delivered dose (DD), and throat deposition of fluticasone mono‐inhaler, fluticasone/salmeterol combination inhaler, and ciclesonide with three small‐volume VHCs using a simulated 4‐year‐old breathing profile.

Materials and Methods

In accordance with international standards for in vitro testing of inhaled drugs, we used a Next Generation Impactor (NGI; Copley Scientific Limited, Nottingham, UK) equipped with a paediatric throat model (Child Alberta Idealized Throat, Copley Scientific Limited, Nottingham, UK). This model represents the upper airway anatomy of children aged 6–14 years; a validated model for younger children is currently unavailable. A breathing simulator (BRS 3100; Copley Scientific Limited, Nottingham, UK) generated a predefined sinusoidal tidal breathing pattern intended to represent calm breathing in a typical 4‐year‐old child: respiratory rate (RR) 24 breaths/min, tidal volume (Vt) 150 mL, inspiration/expiration (I/E) ratio 1.0 s/1.5 s. We used Finnish growth curves to determine the average height of a 4‐year‐old child [11], and used this height‐based estimate to select RR and Vt from published reference equations/values [12]. Each VHC was attached to the throat model using a silicone adapter without a facemask, as children aged four years and older are typically capable of using VHCs without a mask [3, 9]. The assembled system (Figure S1) was checked for leaks before each test run.

The VHCs evaluated were AeroChamber Plus Flow‐Vu (AC), EasyChamber (EC) and OptiChamber Diamond (OD). Three pMDIs were used to deliver the study molecules: Seretide Evohaler fluticasone 125 μg/actuation and salmeterol 25 μg/actuation (GlaxoSmithKline Inc., Evreux, France) for fluticasone/salmeterol combination, Alvesco 160 μg/actuation (AstraZeneca AB, Södertälje, Sweden) for ciclesonide, and Flixotide Evohaler 125 μg/actuation (GlaxoSmithKline Inc., Evreux, France) for fluticasone mono‐inhaler. Multiple canisters were used, ensuring that all experimental actuations were drawn from well within the labelled actuation range.

The pMDIs were primed before use. The pMDI was shaken immediately before each actuation, and the medication was delivered one puff at a time into the test system. After each puff, the breathing simulator was manually triggered to perform five breathing cycles to draw that dose through the spacer. This sequence was then repeated for the second puff. After completion of both puffs and their corresponding breathing cycles, samples were collected from the eight NGI stage cups and the throat model.

For each VHC type, one unit from three different manufacturing lots was tested, and sample collection was repeated four times per VHC unit. After four test runs the VHC's were soaked in lukewarm water with mild household detergent and airdried thoroughly.

The test system was prepared by coating the NGI stage cups with a fixation solution (Brij/ethanol/glycerol) to minimise particle bounce. After each test run, deposited drug was recovered by adding solvent to the throat model and each NGI stage cup. Ethanol was used as a solvent for fluticasone/salmeterol combination and 50/50 ethanol/water mixture for fluticasone and ciclesonide. The resulting samples were transferred to individual vials for analysis. Drug quantification was performed by high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) at Emmace Consulting AB (Lund, Sweden). The HPLC setups are described in detail in the Supporting Information.

The total DD was defined as the sum of the dose recovered from the throat model and from all NGI stages. Fine particle doses (FPD) within the NGI were analysed for both 1–5 μm and 1–3 μm ranges to get FPD1–5 μm and FPD1–3 μm, respectively. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 29 (IBM Corp., NY, USA). As the data were not normally distributed, differences in DDs among the VHCs were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis test. To control for Type I error due to multiple comparisons and to maintain a familywise error rate below 0.05, Bonferroni correction was applied, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05 (two‐sided). Fisher's Exact test was used for comparisons of categorical variables between two groups.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the in vitro delivery of fine particles from a fluticasone/salmeterol combination inhaler and for ciclesonide using simulated paediatric tidal breathing pattern. Our findings show statistically significant differences between VHCs with similar external appearances and demonstrate that the fine particle delivery profiles differ between drug molecules, even when delivered through the same device model under identical conditions.

EC provided higher FPDs than OD and AC for salmeterol and for fluticasone delivered from the combination inhaler, in both FPD1–5 μm and FPD1–3 μm. For ciclesonide, EC also delivered higher FPDs compared with AC and OD in both size ranges. Although OD showed slightly lower relative throat deposition than EC for ciclesonide, the absolute difference (0.6 percentage points) was small. AC delivered higher FPD1–5 μm and FPD1–3 μm than OD for salmeterol and for fluticasone from the combination inhaler, whereas no statistically significant differences in FPD were observed between VHCs for fluticasone mono‐inhaler.

For fluticasone, EC and AC delivered higher median FPD1–3 μm from the combination inhaler than from the mono‐inhaler, whereas OD delivered a higher FPD1–3 μm from the mono‐inhaler than from the combination inhaler. All these differences were statistically significant, with the absolute difference being most pronounced for EC. For the fluticasone mono‐inhaler, relative throat deposition was higher than for the combination inhaler with both AC and EC. These observations suggest that the formulation of the combination inhaler may promote the emission of smaller particles from the VHC, while larger particles are more likely to deposit in the throat [13].

Additionally, the total DD of fluticasone from the combination inhaler was higher with EC than from the mono‐inhaler, further supporting the notion that formulation characteristics influence both the amount and size distribution of emitted particles. This finding does not in itself establish a clinical advantage, but it suggests that formulation–device interactions may have a meaningful impact on the dose available for lung deposition.

Among the studied molecules, ciclesonide showed the highest total DD relative to label claim and the lowest relative throat deposition across all VHCs. While the FPD profiles were broadly similar among VHCs for each molecule, the FPD profile of ciclesonide was clearly shifted toward smaller particle sizes compared with fluticasone and salmeterol. These aerodynamic properties appear to facilitate greater drug delivery and reduced oropharyngeal deposition for ciclesonide compared to fluticasone. Clinical studies in children have reported low rates of oropharyngeal effects with ciclesonide, which is consistent with our in vitro findings [4]. Our results also underline that the aerodynamic behaviour of ICSs is molecule‐specific, and that findings from one ICS should not be extrapolated to others. Because throat deposition is linked to local adverse effects, differences in oropharyngeal deposition are not merely of technical interest but also clinically relevant when selecting an ICS and VHCs.

Our methods adhere to international standards for in vitro testing of inhaled medication, and the breathing parameters used in our study were chosen to approximate calm breathing of a 4‐year‐old child. Children's RR and Vt change with age, with higher RRs and lower Vt in younger children and a gradual shift toward lower rates and higher volumes during growth [14, 15, 16]. The use of a sinusoidal breathing profile with a RR of 24 breaths per minute and a Vt of 150 mL is therefore physiologically plausible for this age group and provides a more realistic representation of actual inhalation patterns than steady‐flow conditions. The use of multiple pMDI canister units with prospective replacement before depletion ensured that all experimental actuations were obtained outside the tail‐off zone, avoiding the dose variability that characterises the final actuations of HFA suspension pMDIs (fluticasone and fluticasone/salmeterol) [17, 18].

Previous in vitro studies conducted under steady‐flow conditions have reported no significant differences in throat deposition for salmeterol/fluticasone between certain VHCs (Vortex, AeroChamber Plus or AbleSpacer) when using a standard induction port as a throat model. However, overall drug delivery with AbleSpacer was significantly lower than with the other spacers [19]. Another steady‐flow study similarly found no differences in fluticasone/salmeterol delivery between Volumatic and AeroChamber Plus [20]. In contrast, our study, using simulated paediatric tidal breathing and a more realistic paediatric throat model, found significant differences in fine particle delivery and, for some molecule–device combinations, in throat deposition. These findings suggest that steady‐flow experiments alone may fail to detect potentially clinically relevant differences between VHCs, which become apparent under more realistic paediatric breathing conditions.

In our previous studies with salbutamol and fluticasone, we found that some VHCs exhibited substantially poorer drug delivery performance under simulated children's tidal breathing compared to other available VHCs [10, 21]. Because the aim of the present study was not to re‐demonstrate extreme underperformance but to compare VHCs that are capable of providing clinically acceptable delivery, we chose not to include VHCs that had repeatedly shown substantially inferior performance in earlier studies. This approach reduces direct comparability with all devices currently in use but enabled a more informative and clinically relevant comparison between VHCs that are more likely to be considered viable options in practice.

To date, no published in vitro data exist on the impact of VHCs on ciclesonide delivery using paediatric breathing patterns and cascade impactor analysis. Our results show that molecule‐specific differences are substantial, and that the relative ranking of VHCs can vary between molecules. This emphasises the importance of evaluating each clinically relevant drug–device combination individually, rather than assuming that performance data from one molecule or VHC can be generalised to others. Selecting an efficient VHC is therefore a key component of optimising inhaled therapy, but robust, clinically relevant, and financially independent research is needed to inform such choices. Unlike short‐acting bronchodilators such as salbutamol, ICS dosing cannot be adjusted based solely on rapid symptomatic response, and oropharyngeal deposition is particularly relevant because of the associated risk of local adverse effects.

Our study provides novel evidence on fine particle delivery and throat deposition of both combination therapy and ciclesonide in such settings. Improved fine particle delivery and reduced throat deposition are expected to support more consistent lung dosing and fewer local side effects. Previous work with dry powder inhalers has demonstrated a strong correlation between FPD and lung bioavailability [22], supporting the relevance of FPD as surrogate for pulmonary exposure. Moreover, clinical data suggest that fluticasone propionate bioavailability can vary depending on the VHC used [23], which aligns with our findings that VHC choice influences fine particle delivery.

This study has several limitations. We used a single paediatric breathing pattern representing calm breathing in a 4‐year‐old child. While physiologically reasonable, this approach does not capture the variability in respiratory patterns across the broader paediatric population or under conditions of distress or poor cooperation. Additionally, the currently validated paediatric throat model represents children aged 6–14 years. Its use in combination with a 4‐year‐old breathing profile may introduce some mismatch between airway geometry and breathing pattern, thereby limiting generalizability to younger children. Facemasks were not used, as children from around four years of age are generally able to use VHCs without a mask, but this may limit applicability to situations in which facemasks are used. Finally, as an in vitro study, our work cannot account for patient‐related factors such as inhalation technique, cooperation or adherence. Although in vitro studies cannot directly guide clinical recommendations, our findings clearly demonstrate that performance differences exist among VHCs, and that these differences are molecule specific.

In conclusion, differences in fine particle delivery and throat deposition should be taken into account when selecting VHCs for paediatric patients. Similarly, the aerodynamic properties of different ICSs should be considered in treatment selection, especially when local adverse effects limit the use of fluticasone propionate. Establishing stricter regulatory requirements for pre‐market testing of VHCs, particularly under simulated breathing conditions representative of the intended patient group, would likely enhance device quality and clinical reliability. Future research should extend testing across multiple paediatric age groups and include in vivo studies to determine the clinical relevance of the in vitro differences observed.
