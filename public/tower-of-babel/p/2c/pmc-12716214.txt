Electrostatic Defrosting
Authors: Venkata Yashasvi Lolla, Hongwei Zhang, Beckett Z. Socha, Rui Qiao, Jonathan B. Boreyko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectrification of ice has been studied for over half a century, mostly in the context of atmospheric science. Here, the polarizability and natural thermovoltage of a substrate‐bound frost sheet are exploited for frost removal by placing an actively charged electrode overhead. This new technique, which we term electrostatic defrosting (EDF), can remove up to 75% of the frost's mass from its substrate over a time scale of only minutes. A one‐dimensional numerical model is developed to rationalize the effective electrostatic force exerted by the electrode on the warm end of the frost sheet. Experimentally, the effectiveness of EDF is shown to depend on the applied voltage, relative humidity of the ambient air, the gap height between the frosted substrate and the electrode plate, and the type of substrate. Although EDF primarily removes the dendritic frost structures rather than the underlying frozen condensate, this selective removal can still offer significant advantages for applications requiring improved visibility or reduced surface roughness. EDF can effectively remove frost without the application of heat, chemicals, or mechanical forces, rendering it a promising new construct for defrosting.

Introduction

Icing and frosting phenomena affect a broad range of engineered systems ranging from power transmission lines,[ 1 ] aviation,[ 2 , 3 ] roadways,[ 4 , 5 ] and energy‐related infrastructure.[ 6 , 7 ] Ice accretion directly impacts both the overall energy efficiency and mechanical integrity of various systems.[ 8 , 9 , 10 ] For example, icing on planes leads to increased drag and reduced lift, sometimes even resulting in accidents and loss of life.[ 11 , 12 ] Significant frost accumulation has been attributed as the single biggest reason for the decrease in efficiency of heat exchangers.[ 6 , 13 ] As a result, a significant amount of financial resources are spent every year to overcome the challenges posed by icing and frosting.[ 14 ]

In an attempt to minimize the challenges and threats posed by frost formation, recent research has focused on better understanding frost growth under a variety of conditions.[ 15 , 16 ] The ulterior motive of many of these studies was to use this enhanced understanding of incipient frost formation and surface adhesion to develop advanced anti‐frosting or defrosting strategies.[ 17 , 18 , 19 ] There are two modes of incipient frost formation on dry substrates chilled beneath the dew point: desublimation frosting (vapor‐to‐solid) or condensation frosting (vapor‐to‐liquid‐to‐solid). Desublimation frosting is rare, only occurring on surfaces exhibiting an ultra‐high affinity to ice (ex: silver iodide) or at ultra‐low temperatures.[ 20 , 21 ] Condensation frosting is far more common, where supercooled condensate initially forms, which eventually freezes over into frost.[ 22 , 23 ]

Current defrosting strategies include thermal, chemical, and mechanical methods as well as low‐adhesion surface coatings.[ 24 ] Thermal defrosting requires a heated gas flow,[ 25 ] embedding resistive heating elements,[ 26 , 27 ] or applying photothermal coatings.[ 28 , 29 ] In any case, overcoming the latent heat of melting is energy‐intensive, and it is not always practical to include air flows or heating elements into systems. The use of anti‐icing and de‐icing chemicals, for example on aircraft and roadways, is both expensive and toxic to the environment.[ 2 , 3 , 30 , 31 ] Mechanical methods, like vibration,[ 32 ] pneumatic inflation,[ 33 ] or scraping,[ 34 ] are either manually intensive or require highly specified installations. The adhesion strength of frost can be reduced using surface coatings that are superhydrophobic[ 35 , 36 ] or oil‐impregnated;[ 37 , 38 ] however, these surfaces are notorious for their poor long‐term durability under outdoor frosting conditions.[ 39 , 40 ] There is an emerging trend of reducing ice adhesion with polymer coatings exhibiting low stiffness,[ 41 , 42 ] low interfacial toughness,[ 43 , 44 ] or tethered quasi‐liquid surfaces.[ 45 , 46 ] Whether these pagophobic polymer coatings will prove cost‐effective and durable for practical outdoor applications remains to be seen.

In the context of atmospheric clouds, frost growth on aerosol particles is partially responsible for thunderstorms.[ 47 , 48 ] This is due to the spontaneous electrification of ice in the presence of an internal temperature gradient, which can discharge during the collision of ice particles in clouds.[ 48 , 49 , 50 ] The thermo‐electrification of ice was first reported in the 1960s by imposing a temperature differential across a cylinder of ice and measuring the resulting electric potential.[ 51 ] A more recent numerical study indicated that thermo‐electrification occurs in two stages: a fast stage, dominated by the mismatched diffusive migration of Bjerrum defects, and a slow stage, governed by ionic defects.[ 52 ] The first stage results in a pseudo‐steady‐state where the warmer side of the ice is positively charged, followed by a switched polarity to the warmer side being negatively charged after the second stage. Experimentally, it was found that by introducing a polar liquid in the vicinity of frost, micro‐scale dendrites detached and jumped into the liquid.[ 53 , 54 ] This is due to the spontaneous generation of an electric field between the negative thermovoltage at the top of the frost and positive charges assembling at the interface of the polarizable liquid. Both the fracture threshold and jumping speed of frost dendrites were recently shown to be enhanced by actively applying a voltage to complement the frost's thermovoltage.[ 54 , 55 ] To date, these reports have exclusively focused on the fundamental physics of the charge‐induced fracture and jumping of individual micro‐dendrites. Whether this phenomenon can facilitate the appreciable defrosting of macroscopic frost sheets remains unknown.

In this work, we use a scalable, actively charged plate electrode to dramatically enhance the amount of frost that is ripped from an opposing substrate, thus introducing a new construct for practical frost removal that we term electrostatic defrosting (EDF). Dynamic mass measurements are used to characterize the efficacy of EDF. We found that EDF could remove at least 1/3, 1/2, or 3/4 of a mature frost sheet's mass from untreated glass, untreated copper, or superhydrophobic surfaces, respectively. For the untreated substrates, a non‐monotonic trend was obtained for the applied voltage versus the percent mass loss of frost. This is in contrast to a monotonic result predicted by our numerical model and demonstrated for the air‐trapping superhydrophobic surfaces, suggesting that charge leakage is important. These results show that EDF can effectively remove large quantities of frost without having to use mechanical force or chemicals.

Materials and Methods

Principle of Operation

Figure 1a illustrates the operating principles behind electrostatic defrosting (EDF). First, a frost sheet diffusively grows on a chilled substrate in a humid ambient via condensation frosting. The temperature gradient across the frost results in charge separation, with a negative charge at the warmer tips and a positive charge at the colder base. The concept of EDF is to then introduce an applied positive charge above the frost, which both interacts with the already negatively charged dendrites and induces further polarization. These collective effects result in an electric force sufficiently strong to cause frost dendrites to fracture off and jump toward the electrode (Figure 1b).

Proof‐of‐concept for electrostatic defrosting (EDF). a) Schematic representation of frost growth on a substrate due to condensation frosting. The frost structure is comprised of a basal layer of frozen supercooled dew droplets and an overlying layer of dendritic outgrowths. The inset depicts the naturally occurring charge separation that occurs across the dendrites due to mismatches in ion mobility along the temperature gradient. This results in the tips of the frost being negatively charged. b) Upon introducing a positively charged overhanging electrode plate, we expect the frost dendrites to fracture and jump toward the electrode plate. We term this process electrostatic defrosting. c) Experimental visual confirmation of EDF. With a gap height of H = 2.5 cm, an almost instantaneous jumping of the frost dendrites toward the electrode plate occurred upon applying 120 V to the electrode plate.

An experimental proof‐of‐concept of EDF is shown in Figure 1c, using high‐speed imaging. The first panel shows a mature frost sheet immediately before a voltage is applied to the overhanging copper plate electrode, with ambient conditions of T ∞ = 23.3 °C and RH = 60%. The substrate temperature was always T s = − 8 °C such that supercooled condensation first formed, eventually freezing via inter‐droplet ice bridging, followed by out‐of‐plane dendritic frost growth on the frozen droplets. A mere two milliseconds later (second panel), the applied voltage of 120 V is already resulting in multiple dendrites detaching from the frost sheet and jumping across the 2.5 cm gap. Another millisecond has passed in the third frame, with the already‐detached dendrites having moved further across the gap while two additional dendrites have fractured off. The jumping of the detached dendrites exhibits a characteristic speed of ≈ 6 m s−1 (obtained by manually calculating frost velocities from the Supporting Information video). The characteristic Reynolds number was found to be 30, where the characteristic length of the frost dendrite was taken at 0.1 mm, density of air taken as 1.2 kg m−3, and the dynamic viscosity corresponds to air at room temperature. For context, Mukherjee et al. reported a Reynolds number of 1.2 when suspending water over the frost rather than an electrode.[ 53 ] The stark difference in the Reynolds number is not only due to the speed at which the frost is jumping toward the electrode but also the size of the jumping frost (an order magnitude higher than previously reported). Upon detachment, the majority of frost dendrites were observed to move upward and adhere to the overhanging electrode plate, rather than falling back down to the substrate under gravity. This indicates that the applied electric field is sufficient not only to fracture the frost dendrites but also to dominate their subsequent motion. A more detailed study of the dynamics and capture efficiency of jumping frost crystals will be an interesting direction for future work.

Experimental Setup

Figure 2 represents a schematic figure for an experimental setup of EDF. Condensation frosting was accomplished by thermally bonding the test substrate to a Peltier stage (Linkam Scientific, PE120) using a thin coating of thermal paste (Aavid Thermalloy, 251G). The temperature of the Peltier stage was set to T P = −10 °C. A thermocouple was used to measure the temperature of the test substrate attached to the Peltier stage, which was T s = − 8 °C for the copper substrates and T s = − 3.5 °C for the glass substrates. The warm and humid ambient exhibited a room temperature of T ∞ ≈ 23 ± 1 °C; the humidity naturally varied from RH ≈ 20% up to RH ≈ 60% by conducting experiments during different seasons of the year. After a mature frost sheet was diffusively grown on the test substrate, a copper plate electrode was translated to become suspended directly over the frost using a linear translational stage (ThorLabs, NRT100) at a controlled speed of 2 cm s−1. The height between this suspended plate and the test substrate was varied to be either H = 1.5, 2.5, or 5 cm depending on the trial. To commence EDF, a DC voltage power supply (ESD‐EMC, ES813‐D40.1) was connected to the copper plate electrode. The temperature of the electrode plate during EDF was still roughly equivalent to T ∞, as the current flow is negligible (i.e., ≲ 0.1 mA) resulting in no appreciable resistive heating. The test substrate was electrically grounded during all experiments to prevent charge accumulation and ensure a consistent electrostatic environment.

Schematic of the experimental setup for electrostatic defrosting. Frost is grown on a substrate attached to a Peltier stage, which in turn is placed on a mass balance to record the frost growth and loss. An electrode plate is connected to a high‐voltage DC power supply. A linear translational stage was used to automate the introduction of the electrode plate directly over the frosted substrate.

Substrate Preparation

Frost was grown on four different types of 2.54 cm × 2.54 cm substrates: untreated copper, untreated glass, and copper or glass featuring a superhydrophobic surface coating. When using untreated copper or glass, the substrates were degreased by submerging them in acetone, isopropyl alcohol, and deionized water for 15 min each, followed by drying with a nitrogen gun. For the superhydrophobic surfaces, untreated copper or glass were degreased in the same manner as before, followed by two separate spray coatings of Glaco nanoparticles (Soft99, Glaco Mirror Coat Zero). Immediately after coating, the superhydrophobic substrates were placed in a thermal oven for 45 min at 250 °C followed by 30 min at 150 °C.[ 56 ] The thickness of the Glaco nanoparticle coating is expected to be ≈ 1–5 µm, consistent with previous studies of spray‐applied hydrophobic coatings.[ 56 ] Characterizing wettability for all three types of substrates was done on a contact angle goniometer (ramé‐hart, Model 590) using the droplet swell‐shrink method to measure the advancing and receding apparent contact angles.

Numerical Methods

The response of the ice slab to an applied voltage in Figure 3a is modeled by considering the polarization of ice and the transport of free charge carriers in it. The ice is taken as a leaky dielectric featuring H3O+ and OH− ions. The transport and conservation law of the H3O+ and OH− ions are given by: (1) ∂ρ+∂t=∂∂zD+∂ρ+∂z−μ+ρ+E+ρ0,+(T)τ±−ρ+ρ−ρ0,+(T)τ± (2) ∂ρ−∂t=∂∂zD−∂ρ−∂z+μ−ρ−E+ρ0,−(T)τ±−ρ+ρ−ρ0,−(T)τ± where ρ+ and ρ− are the density of H3O+ and OH− ions, respectively. The second and third terms in Equations (1) and (2) denote the generation of H3O+ and OH− ions by H2O dissociation and their annihilation by recombination governed by the mass action law, respectively. The equilibrium density of H3O+ and OH− ions at temperature T are given by ρ0, +(ρ0, −), and τ± = 2 × 10−5 s is the recombination time constant for the two ions.[ 57 ] The equilibrium densities ρ0, +(T) and ρ0, −(T) are given by ρ0, + = ρ0, −(T) = ρ0(T) = Ne−Φe/(2kBT), where N = 3 × 1028 m−3 and the activation energy of the ions is Φ e ≈ 1.08 eV. The Debye length defined by the H3O+/OH− ion's bulk density is λ± = (ɛk B T/(2ρ0 e 2)1/2), where ɛ is the dielectric permittivity of ice and k B T the thermal energy. Using the temperature and bulk ion density at the center of the ice slab, λ± is found to be 5.3 µm. The diffusion coefficient of each ion is related to its mobility by D ± = k B Tµ±/e. The mobilities of H3O+ and OH− ions are 1 × 10−7 and 3 × 10−8 m−3/(V·s), respectively.[ 58 ] The temperature of ice drops from 273 K at z = 0 to 265 K at z = 3 mm. Because the air gap has no charge, the electrical potential drops linearly in it. Therefore, the air gap is not explicitly modeled. Instead, its effect is modeled using the boundary condition for electrical potential, as widely practiced in the electrokinetic literature:[ 59 ] (4) ∂Φ∂zz=0=εr,aεr,iΦ(0)−ΔΦLa;Φ(Li)=0 where ɛr, a = 1 is air's dielectric constant, L a is the thickness of the air gap, and ΔΦ is the applied voltage. Equations (1)–(4) are solved simultaneously and marched to the steady state using a finite element software COMSOL. The mesh is refined near z = 0 and z = L i to resolve the sharp variation of ion densities in their vicinity.

The electrostatic force acting on the ice slab is obtained by integrating the Maxwell stress tensor: (5) Fe=∮ε0εrn^·E⃗E⃗−12E⃗·E⃗n^dS where ϵr is the dielectric constant, E⃗ is the local electrical field, and n^ is the unit normal vector of surfaces. To compute the force acting on a portion of the ice slab, we define a box enclosing this portion. Using the fact that the electrical field in the air gap is uniform (E a), the force acting on the portion of the ice slab extending from its top surface by a thickness of h, can be simplified as F e(h) = (ɛa E a 2 − ɛi E i(h)2)/2, where E i(h) is the electrical field inside the ice slab at z = h and a force pointing toward the air gap is defined as positive.

Results and Discussion

Numerical Results

Using electrostatic forces for ice removal has a relatively short history. When metals and high‐dielectric liquids are held above a frost layer, individual dendrites can detach and jump away from the frost layer by polarization‐induced electrostatic attraction.[ 53 ] However, because the electrical fields in such a setup are weak, ice removal is ineffective (i.e., only a minute fraction of frost dendrites are removed). Such a limitation can be alleviated by applying an external voltage. To quantitatively assess this strategy, we numerically study the electrical force on a 3 mm thick ice slab when a voltage ΔΦ is imposed between a pair of electrodes sandwiched by the ice slab and a 2.5 cm thick air gap (Figure 3a). The temperature is assumed to drop linearly from 0 °C at the air–ice interface to −8 °C at the ice–electrode interface.

The electrification and polarization of the ice slab shown in Figure 3a are modeled by considering the transport of free charge carriers (H3O+ and OH− ions) and the polarizability of the ice (see Numerical Methods). Figure 3b shows the non‐dimensionalized steady‐state density profiles of the H3O+ and OH− ions at ΔΦ = 1100 V. An excess of OH− (H3O+) ions is evident near the ice slab's warmer (colder) end. These charge separations originate from the transport of H3O+ and OH− ions. Specifically, H3O+ and OH− ions exist in ice due to the spontaneous dissociation of H2O molecules. Here, the temperature gradient between the air–ice and ice–electrode interfaces causes a density gradient of these ions across the ice slab, which is also maintained by the local generation and annihilation of ions through the mass action law.[ 57 ] Since H3O+ ions are 3.3 times more mobile than OH− ions, the diffusion (driven by density gradient) and migration (driven by the applied voltage) of H3O+ ions toward the bottom electrode are faster than those of OH− ions.[ 58 ] Consequently, OH− (H3O+) ions are enriched near the ice slab's warmer (colder) end. As expected for interfacial systems,[ 60 ] the charge separation is confined within several Debye lengths defined by H3O+/OH− ions' bulk density (λ±= 5.3 µm, see Numerical Methods). It follows that the electrical field across the ice slab, E i , is essentially uniform, except for a few λ± from the ice slab's two ends (see Figure S1, Supporting Information).

The electrostatic force pulling a portion of the ice slab extending from its top surface by a thickness of h is Fe(h)=(εaEa2−εiEi(h)2)/2, where ɛa(ɛi) is the permittivity of air (ice), and E a and E i(h) are the electrical fields in the air gap and ice slab at z = h, respectively (see Numerical Methods). At ΔΦ = 1, 100 V, F e(h) is 11.1 mN m−2 at h = 2λ± = 10.6 µm and remains at 11.1 mN m−2 as h increases to 1.5 mm (Figure S1, Supporting Information). The negligible change of F e(h) for h > 2λ± is due to the near‐uniform nature of E i except in the vicinity of the ice–air interface. This result suggests that, under an applied voltage, the ice slab can potentially break anywhere along its thickness direction. Hereafter, F e(h = 1.5 mm) is taken as the electrostatic force acting on the ice slab. We note that, if the free charge carriers (H3O+ and OH− ions) inside the ice slab are neglected and only the polarization of the ice slab by the applied voltage is considered, the electrostatic force is 10.9 mN m−2 (Numerical Methods). This suggests that the contribution of free ions due to spontaneous charge separation is secondary to the net force, which is attributed to the large dielectric permittivity and consequently weak electrical field in the ice slab compared to the air gap above it.

Figure 3c shows that the electrostatic force acting on the ice slab increases quadratically with ΔΦ. Such a scaling is consistent with that for electrostatic forces dominated by polarization effects and highlights that electrostatic deicing can be enhanced by elevating the applied voltage. The numerical results reveal that the dominant contribution to the electrostatic force arises from the polarization of the ice slab under the applied external electric field, rather than from internal thermovoltage generation. As a result, a strong temperature gradient across the frost is not a necessary condition for EDF to be effective. Finally, while the numerical model employs a simplified slab geometry rather than replicating the complex dendritic structure of frost, it effectively captures the primary physics underlying EDF. Specifically, the results show that polarizability of the ice dominates the electrostatic force, with charge separation acting as a secondary effect, and that the electrostatic force increases monotonically with applied voltage. Although incorporating a dendritic geometry could alter the magnitude of the computed forces, the qualitative trends identified here are expected to remain unchanged. Having established the underlying electrostatic mechanisms with numerical modeling, we now turn to experimental investigations of how various parameters such as applied voltage, gap height, humidity, and substrate type influence the effectiveness of EDF.

Experimental Results

For experimental validation, the following parameters were varied: i) voltage applied to the electrode plate (ΔΦ = 0 , 120, 550, 1100, 2500, or 5500 V); ii) gap height between the substrate and the overhanging electrode plate (H = 1.5, 2.5, or 5 cm); iii) relative humidity of the ambient air (RH ≈ 20%, 40%, or 60%); iv) substrate type (untreated copper, SHPB copper, untreated glass, and SHPB glass). The voltage applied was always below 6000 V to avoid corona discharge, as the mass balance readings became inaccurate due to electrical interference. In all experiments, the electric field was applied once the accumulated frost mass reached approximately 2.5 g, rather than at a fixed time. This ensured a consistent frost layer mass and thickness across experiments, although the frosting time required to reach this mass could vary slightly depending on ambient conditions or substrate properties.

The sample was the only actively cooled surface; the sample is surrounded by a rubber gasket which has low thermal conductivity. The overlying electrode plate did not extend beyond the actively cooled frosted sample. Consequently, frost growth and defrosting via EDF were both localized to the sample, and any off‐sample frost on the mass balance contributed negligibly to the recorded mass. All graphs depicted in the main manuscript depict a single (best‐case) trial of EDF for the given set of conditions. However, Figures S2–S6 (Supporting Information) show all three trials conducted for each of the five voltage choices for the most standard case of untreated copper, H = 2.5 cm, and RH ≈ 60%. Figure S7 (Supporting Information) then plots the average EDF performance across the three trials, with statistical error bars that capture trial‐to‐trial variability. The error bars across trials were especially large for the higher voltages, most likely due to the amplification of messy charge leakage issues.

Figure 4b graphs the percentage of frost that is detached due to EDF as a function of the positive voltage applied to the electrode plate. The effectiveness of EDF is defined as the percentage of frost mass removed relative to the initial frost mass, where the initial frost mass was grown to approximately 2.5 g prior to applying the electric field. Even an uncharged plate, ΔΦ = 0, removed about η ≈ 15% of the frost mass from its substrate. This is due to the polarizability of copper in the presence of charge separated frost, analogous to the results already reported for suspending water films over frost.[ 53 ] For a given trial, the amount of frost removed increases with the choice of applied voltage, with η ≈ 40% at 120 V and ≈ 50% at 550 V (Figure S8, Supporting Information). However, this trend reverses for choices of applied voltage beyond 550 V. For example, for 1100, 2500, or 5,500 V, the defrosting continually diminishes to η ≈ 30%, 25%, or 20%, respectively (see Figure S9, Supporting Information, for visual representation). This non‐monotonic trend is counter‐intuitive and runs contrary to what is predicted by the preceding numerical model. Therefore, we tentatively hypothesize an experimental reality of charge “leakage” at higher voltage, for example where charge is transported from the polarized frost into the underlying substrate and heat sink to dampen the extent of charge separation. It is also possible that the higher voltages are interfering in some way with the continual thermal activation and/or migration of the ions in the first place.

Figure 4c shows the effect of height on the amount of frost that is lost due to EDF process. The electric field strength should be inversely proportional to the square of the distance between two plates.[ 61 ] The EDF performance for H = 1.5 and 2.5 cm are comparable, with the latter being slightly higher performing at lower voltages and the former slightly better at higher voltages. It is unclear what leads to the larger gap height being somewhat better at the lower voltages; one possible explanation is the slight warming of the frost tips due to the nearby presence of the plate. However, direct temperature measurements confirmed that the electrode plate temperature remained approximately constant at room temperature during EDF, with negligible resistive heating (<0.1 mA current). Therefore, any differences in EDF performance between gap heights are unlikely to be due to thermal effects from the electrode, and are more likely attributable to variations in local electric field strength or surface charge dynamics. As expected, the EDF performance was consistently and substantively worse for a much larger gap height of H = 5 cm due to the greatly weakened strength of the electric field. The non‐monotonic trend of η with ΔΦ is observed for all three gap heights, giving confidence that this trend was not an aberration. See Figure S10 (Supporting Information) for graphs of how the frost mass evolves in time for H = 1.5 and 5 cm for ΔΦ = 120 V, complementing the graph for H = 2.5 cm shown in Figure 4a.

Figure 4d highlights the surprisingly strong effect of relative humidity on EDF (using H = 2.5 cm). For all applied voltages, η was substantially larger with increasing ambient humidity. Using the peak performance at ΔΦ = 550 V as an example, the effectiveness of EDF triples when comparing a relative humidity of RH = 20% (η ≈ 15%) to RH = 60% (η ≈ 49%). We suspect this is due to the important role that relative humidity (and by extension the supersaturation) plays in determining the geometric structure of the frost sheet. At higher supersaturations, the nucleation density of the initial supercooled droplet population is increased. Further, after these droplets freeze over, longer networks of daughter dendrites will be able to grow from the tops of the droplets before the vapor at the frost tips becomes saturated to terminate growth. The end result is having more plentiful dendrites that are also long and slender, which will facilitate more fracture events for a given electric field strength. Refer to Figure S11 (Supporting Information) for graphs of the time evolution of frost mass for RH = 20% and 40% for ΔΦ = 120 V, while RH = 60% is shown in Figure 4a. Here, it is imperative to point out that, in our experiments, frost removal was demonstrated under conditions with significant ambient‐to‐surface temperature differences (ΔT ≈ 30 °C). Although these conditions naturally promote internal charge separation within the frost, the numerical model indicates that EDF could still operate under much smaller ΔT, suggesting a promising avenue for future experimental validation.

For fixed conditions of H = 2.5 cm and RH ≈ 58%, the importance of substrate choice on EDF over the full range of applied voltages is graphed in Figure 5b. Time variant graphs of frost mass are shown in Figure 4a for uncoated copper and in Figure S13 (Supporting Information) for glass and SHPB copper for ΔΦ = 120 V. The glass substrate exhibits the same non‐monotonic trend of η versus ΔΦ as the uncoated copper substrate, with two important differences. First, the values of η are substantively smaller with the glass substrate at lower voltages; for example, at ΔΦ = 120 V, the effectiveness of EDF is roughly half that of the copper substrate. Second, the electrically insulating glass substrate exhibits a much weaker decrease in η beyond the peak performance at ΔΦ = 550 V, such that it actually outperforms the copper by ΔΦ = 5500 V. This strongly supports our hypothesis that charge leakage into the substrate at higher voltages is at least partially responsible for the non‐monotonic trend and can be mitigated with insulating substrates. Interestingly, the low‐adhesion superhydrophobic substrate exhibited inferior η values compared to untreated copper up to ΔΦ = 120 V. We attribute this to the very dilute and small droplet population that forms on a superhydrophobic condenser due to the jumping‐droplet effect. Upon freezing, this dilute droplet population would grow weaker dendritic networks compared to the hydrophilic substrates, resulting in inferior EDF. However, the superhydrophobic substrate is the only one that does not feature the non‐monotonic trend, such that it far outperforms the other surfaces at ΔΦ = 2500 V (η ≈ 40%) and especially at ΔΦ = 5500 V (η ≈ 65%). This is almost certainly due to the air‐trapping Cassie state of the frost minimizing charge leakage into the underlying copper substrate. Although the Cassie state simultaneously decreases the ice adhesion strength of the frozen mother droplets, we do not expect this to directly contribute to the increase in η, considering that it is the daughter dendrites that are primarily being removed with EDF.

Using only the superhydrophobic copper substrate, Figure 5c varies the gap height for a fixed RH ≈ 58%. The monotonic trend of continually increasing η with ΔΦ is captured for all three choices in H, validating that the air pockets are able to mitigate charge leakage. There was also a consistent trend of increasing η with decreasing H. Using the smallest gap height of H = 1.5 cm and maximal applied voltage of ΔΦ = 5500 V, we achieved a record high EDF performance of η ≈ 75% (Figure 5d). In Figure 5e, this excellent EDF performance is shown visually with before‐and‐after photographs of the SHPB copper. Prior to EDF, a rich network of dendritic frost coats the SHPB surface, such that the copper itself is almost entirely obscured from view. After EDF is performed, the majority of the copper's surface area is now clearly visible, as most of the daughter dendrites have been removed leaving only a dilute population of frozen condensate.

The higher effectiveness of EDF for substrates with superhydrophobic coatings extended to glass substrates (Figure 6a), where η ≈ 72% was achieved on Glaco‐coated glass. By painting a Virginia Tech logo onto the top face of the glass, it can be seen that the initial frost sheet almost completely obscures the substrate, whereas the logo is readily visible after performing EDF (Figure 6b). This shows that, when the conditions are properly designed (Figure S14, Supporting Information), EDF can remove a large majority of frost from a substrate without the input of any chemicals, heat, or mechanical forces.

ΔΦ of 5500 V was applied, which removed a maximum of 72% of the frost in approximately 20 min. b) Visual representation of (a), where before‐and‐after photography confirms that EDF removed the majority of frost from the SHPB glass substrate.

Although EDF requires the application of high voltages (up to 5500 V), it operates under extremely low current conditions (≲ 0.1 mA), resulting in minimal power consumption. Even at the maximum applied voltage of 5500 V, the instantaneous electrical power during EDF is only P ≲ 0.55 W. The energy consumption for a typical 10‐min EDF cycle is around E ≲ 330 J. Now compare this with the energy requirement for the thermal defrosting of the same 2.5 g of frost: given that the latent heat of melting is L m = 334 kJ kg−1, it would require E = 835 J of thermal energy to melt the frost (over 2.5 times more). This enhancement in energy efficiency is very conservative, as we expect that future improvements to EDF, such as reduced charge leakage rates and faster defrosting times, could dramatically reduce its energy requirement.

Conclusion

We have demonstrated that applying voltage to an electrode plate can dramatically expel frost from an opposing chilled substrate. Electrostatic defrosting (EDF) does not require any chemical, mechanical, or thermal input and was shown to be effective with uncoated and hydrophilic substrates. The maximum percentage of frost removed was about η = 50% for hydrophilic substrates and η ≈ 75% for superhydrophobic substrates. Photographic imaging suggests that EDF is primarily removing daughter dendrites, while the underlying frozen mother dew droplets virtually always remained adhered to their substrate. Future studies should attempt to further boost the electrostatic force or reduce the substrate's ice adhesion strength, to determine whether complete defrosting via EDF is possible.

The enabling mechanism for EDF is the electrostatic force acting upon the frost. Our numerical model reveals that this electrostatic force is primarily due to the polarizability of ice and only secondarily due to the charge separation that naturally occurs when ice/frost exhibits a temperature gradient. The experimental results cannot directly validate the model, as the latter assumed a continuous ice slab rather than a three‐dimensional dendritic network. Regardless, the model was valuable in terms of clarifying that the primary mechanism for EDF is the applied voltage polarizing the ice, as opposed to thermovoltage that requires a temperature gradient. Further, the model's monotonic relationship between the applied voltage and the resulting electrostatic force clarified the issue of charge leakage for the non‐monotonic experimental results with copper. This model‐induced epiphany led to our use of insulating glass and air‐trapping superhydrophobic coatings to mitigate the leakage issue; for example, the superhydrophobic copper sample yielded the monotonic trend predicted by the model. Although the present experiments were conducted under conditions with substantial surface‐to‐ambient temperature differences, the primary mechanism of field‐induced polarization suggests that the technique could also be effective under minimal thermal gradients. Future work will aim to experimentally validate EDF performance in near‐isothermal environments.

Although future work is needed to enhance EDF for full removal of both daughter and mother frost, the selective removal of dendritic structures can already offer practical benefits. For example, we showed that clearing dendritic frost from glass can dramatically improve its transparency, even if a dilute population of frozen condensate persists. Identifying specific applications where dendritic removal alone provides meaningful functional improvements, such as for optical sensors or windows, is a direction for future investigation. Overall, EDF represents a novel and promising strategy for frost management, offering a chemical‐free, mechanical‐free, and low‐energy approach to mitigate frost accumulation under cold conditions. Further testing and optimization of electric field strength, electrode positioning, substrate choice, pre‐EDF frost thickness, and performance across multiple frosting/defrosting cycles will be important steps toward expanding its applicability across a wider range of real‐world systems. Installation challenges also needed to be addressed, for example, when to use continuous versus mesh‐based electrodes, whether to permanently install the electrode(s) versus utilizing them dynamically (to avoid obstructing the surface), and when to use EDF continually versus intermittently.
