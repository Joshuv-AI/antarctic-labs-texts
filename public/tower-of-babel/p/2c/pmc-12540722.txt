Analysis of the correlation between PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations and the epidemiology of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae in the PICU
Authors: Yufan Yang, Hengyun He, Wang Chen, Xinping Zhang, Jiaotian Huang, Xiulan Lu, Zhenghui Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to analyze the epidemiological characteristics of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae in the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) of Hunan Children’s Hospital from 2020 to 2023. This study investigated the epidemiological patterns related to different years, seasons, ages, and sexes, as well as the correlations of PM2.5, PM10, and NO2 concentrations, temperature, and humidity with severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae. The goal is to increase awareness of the prevention and control of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae in the Changsha region, thereby improving clinical outcomes, reducing mortality rates among patients with severe cases, and providing evidence-based guidance for clinical practice. A retrospective analysis was conducted on children diagnosed with severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae in the PICU of Hunan Children’s Hospital from January 2020 to December 2023. The study compared the positivity rates of Mycoplasma infections across different years, seasons, age groups, and sexes. The correlations between PM2.5 (µg/m3), PM10 (µg/m3), NO2 (µg/m3), average temperature (°C), and average relative humidity (%) and the number of cases of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae were also analyzed. Among 2,047 children with severe pneumonia, the overall MP-IgM positivity rate was 9.5%. Annual positivity rates increased from 6.3% in 2020 to 15.7% in 2023. Rates were highest in summer (15.8%), and preschool children (3–7 years) showed the greatest positivity (30.0%). At pollutant concentrations above thresholds (PM2.5 ≥35 µg/m3, PM10 ≥ 50 µg/m3, NO2 ≥ 25 µg/m3), moderate positive correlations with severe cases were found, with risk ratios of 1.28, 1.31, and 2.04 per 10 µg/m3 increase, respectively. The positivity rate and number of cases of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae in the PICU tended to increase from 2020 to 2023. The condition was most prevalent in summer and among preschool-aged children. When the PM2.5 concentration was ≥ 35 µg/m3 or the PM10 concentration was ≥ 50 µg/m3, moderate positive correlations were observed between the PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations and the number of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae cases.

Introduction

Mycoplasma pneumoniae, the smallest prokaryotic pathogen lacking a cell wall, causes atypical acute respiratory infections in children. It is transmitted through respiratory droplets and has an incubation period of up to 1–3 weeks1,2. Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection is one of the most common causes of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), accounting for 10–40% of CAP cases. Its epidemiology exhibits a cyclical pattern, with outbreaks occurring every 3–7 years and each outbreak lasting 1–2 years3–5. Severe pneumonia is a leading cause of death in children younger than five years6. Studies have shown that Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia accounts for approximately one-third of severe CAP cases in pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) settings7.

The clinical manifestations of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia include respiratory symptoms such as fever, wheezing, and difficulty breathing, along with numerous complications. Pulmonary complications include plastic bronchitis, pulmonary embolism, lung consolidation or necrosis, and pleural effusion. Extrapulmonary complications include skin and mucosal damage8. The primary mechanisms by which Mycoplasma pneumoniae causes these symptoms include direct damage, indirect damage, and immune evasion9. Children with severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia are more prone to develop both pulmonary and extrapulmonary complications as well as coinfections with viruses or bacteria10.

In studies exploring the health impacts of air pollution, particulate matter (PM), particularly fine particulate matter with a diameter ≤ 2.5 μm (PM2.5), has been reported to have unique biological consequences. PM2.5 can enter the body through the respiratory system and cause severe damage to multiple systems, particularly in growing children11. Recent pediatric evidence indicates that short-term elevations in PM2.5 and NO2 increase hospitalizations for Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection, supporting a pollution-infection link12,13. Meteorological factors, also modulate Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia incidence13. Mechanistically, PM2.5-associated metals (e.g., cadmium, lead, zinc) may enhance M. pneumoniae adherence and epithelial injury14. Furthermore, higher levels of PM2.5, PM10, and NO2 are broadly associated with increased pediatric hospitalizations for pneumonia and asthma15. Together, these data highlight the importance of evaluating air pollutants and meteorological conditions in children and provide the rationale for the present study.

However, research on the epidemiological characteristics of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia in the PICU and its correlation with atmospheric pollutants is limited. To understand the epidemiological status of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia in the PICU of Hunan Children’s Hospital, this study retrospectively analyzed MP-IgM positivity rates and epidemiological characteristics across different seasons, age groups, and sexes among children admitted to the PICU from 2020 to 2023. This study summarized and analyzed the epidemiological patterns of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia in the Hunan region and the correlations between PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations and this disease. The aim was to increase awareness of Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection prevention and control in the region, thereby improving clinical outcomes, reducing mortality rates among patients with severe cases, and providing evidence-based guidance for clinical practice.

Considering that air pollutants may cause potential lung damage through mechanisms such as airway inflammation, oxidative stress, and epigenetic modifications, this study also explored the correlation between certain atmospheric pollutants and severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia.

This study aimed to describe the epidemiology of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia in a tertiary PICU (2020–2023) and to explore its associations with air pollutants and meteorological factors. We hypothesized that higher pollutant levels would be associated with an increased risk of severe M. pneumoniae pneumonia.

Subjects and methods

Study subjects

This study collected clinical data from children who were hospitalized in the PICU of Hunan Children’s Hospital from January 1, 2020, to December 31, 2023, and were diagnosed with severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia. A retrospective analysis was conducted on 2,047 children who underwent Mycoplasma antibody testing from 2020 to 2023, including 1,327 males and 720 females. The age distribution was as follows: 1,168 patients under 1 year of age, 433 patients aged 1–3 years, 253 patients aged 3–7 years, and 193 patients aged 7 years or older. All the clinical data of the patients who tested positive and were included in the study met the following standards based on the “Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Mycoplasma Pneumoniae Pneumonia in Children (2023 Edition)” issued by the National Health Commission16:

Patients included in this study were diagnosed with severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia according to the 2023 Chinese guidelines, which require the presence of at least one laboratory criterion such as an elevated MP antibody titer or positive MP-DNA or RNA, combined with clinical symptoms and imaging findings consistent with severe disease.

Reagents and methods

Passive agglutination (Serodia-MYCO II diagnostic reagent kit, Zhuhai Lizhu Reagent Co., Ltd.) was used to detect Mycoplasma pneumoniae IgM antibodies. A Mycoplasma pneumoniae IgM titer ≥ 1:160 was considered positive.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed via SPSS 25.0 and R(4.4.3). Categorical data are expressed as proportions or percentages (%), and group comparisons were conducted via the χ2 test. Linear correlation analyses were used to evaluate the relationships between meteorological data and the number of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae cases. Linear regression models were applied to estimate regression coefficients (β) and 95% confidence intervals (CI), and to calculate risk ratios (RR) per 10 µg/m3 increase in pollutant concentration. To account for potential nonlinear associations and the simultaneous effects of multiple pollutants, generalized additive models (GAM) were employed. In these models, the number of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia cases was modeled as a function of PM2.5, PM10, and NO2 concentrations, using smoothing splines to capture potential nonlinear dose-response relationships. The significance level was set at α = 0.05, with P < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Meteorological data

Meteorological data for Changsha, including the average temperature (°C) and relative humidity (%), were obtained from World Weather Online. The monthly average concentrations of atmospheric pollutants, including PM2.5 (µg/m3), PM10 (µg/m3), and NO2 (µg/m3), were obtained from the Changsha Ecological Environment Bureau. Air pollutant concentrations were categorized according to established environmental-health thresholds. Specifically, PM2.5 ≥ 35 µg/m3 and PM10 ≥ 50 µg/m3 were defined based on the Grade I 24-hour limits of the Chinese National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS, GB 3095 − 2012)17, which are commonly applied in epidemiological research. For NO2, a cut-off of 25 µg/m3 was selected according to the World Health Organization (WHO) 2021 Air Quality Guideline (24-hour mean)18.

Results

Comparison of MP-IgM positivity rates across different years

Among the 2,047 serum samples collected from children with severe pneumonia tested for MP-IgM antibodies, 195 were positive, with a positivity rate of 9.5% (195/2,047). Specifically, the MP-IgM positivity rates were as follows: 6.3% (35/554) in 2020, 6.5% (37/573) in 2021, 11.4% (57/500) in 2022, and 15.7% (66/420) in 2023 (Table 1). The differences in positivity rates from 2020 to 2023 were statistically significant (P < 0.05). Pairwise comparisons indicated no significant difference between 2020 and 2021, and between 2022 and 2023 (P > 0.05). Other annual comparisons were statistically significant (P < 0.05).

Multivariable analysis of air pollutants

In the generalized additive model including PM2.5, PM10, and NO2 simultaneously, the smooth term for PM10 was statistically significant (edf = 9.000, χ2=32.253, P < 0.05), as was NO2 (edf = 8.396, χ2 = 35.796, P < 0.05). In contrast, PM2.5 did not reach statistical significance (edf = 1.903, χ2 = 4.218, P > 0.05). The corresponding exposure-response curves showed nonlinear associations for PM10 and NO2, while the curve for PM2.5 remained relatively flat with wide confidence intervals (Fig. 7). Concurvity analysis revealed substantial collinearity among PM2.5, PM10, and NO2, with worst values of 0.99, 0.99, and 0.98, respectively.

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed the epidemiological characteristics of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia in the PICU of Hunan Children’s Hospital from January 2020 to December 2023. The results revealed that the MP detection rate was 9.5% among children with severe pneumonia in the PICU from 2020 to 2023, which is lower than the Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia detection rate of 15.72% in severe pneumonia cases reported by Chen et al.19 in a certain region of China. The detection rate of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia reported in this study was also significantly lower than the detection rate of 30.5% in Shanghai from January 2017 to December 20197.

However, it is noteworthy that the positivity rate and number of cases of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia in the PICU showed a yearly increasing trend from 2020 to 2023. Li et al.20 reported similar trends among children with nonsevere Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia. The temporal variation in severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia from 2020 to 2023 may have been influenced by the COVID-19 pandemic and related public-health measures. The notably low detection rate in 2021 coincided with strict infection-control measures, including mask wearing, school closures, social distancing, and mobility restrictions, which likely reduced the circulation of Mycoplasma pneumoniae2. With the gradual relaxation of restrictions and resumption of social activities in 2022 and 2023, transmission opportunities increased, contributing to the rebound in severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia positivity20. The differences in positivity rates and changes in the prevalence trends of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia across different regions are influenced by multiple factors, including external factors such as climate, diagnostic methods, and public health policies, as well as socioeconomic background, population mobility, immune levels, antibiotic use, and Mycoplasma resistance.

Seasonal variations are influenced by various factors, including regional population structure, school and holiday schedules, population mobility, and public health interventions. From 2020 to 2023, the MP-IgM positivity rate among children with severe pneumonia showed significant seasonal variation, with the highest positivity rate observed in summer. This may be related to humid climates and increased large gatherings during summer vacations. In contrast, Chen et al.19 reported that the Mycoplasma detection rate was highest in autumn, followed by winter and spring, in a certain region of China, which may be associated with temperature changes and the resumption of school activities.

When we analyzed the age distribution of among children with severe pneumonia and MP-IgM positivity, we found significant differences across age groups. The highest positivity rate of 30.0% was observed in the preschool group (ages 3–7 years), whereas the lowest rate of 1.6% was observed in the infant group (< 1 year). Zhou reported that severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia mainly occurs in children under 6 years of age7. These differences among children in this age range may be related to the maturity of the immune system, opportunities for exposure to pathogens, and group living environments (e.g., kindergarten), all of which may increase the risk of Mycoplasma infection.

Some studies have shown that patients with severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia tend to be younger than those with nonsevere Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia4. This suggests that as children grow older, the risk of infection decreases, likely due to further maturation of the immune system and the immunity acquired from prior infections.

This study revealed that although no statistically significant difference was found between sexes, a trend toward higher the severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia positivity rate in females children was observed. No other studies have yet discussed the epidemiological characteristics of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia in terms of sex. However, some researchers have reported that in hospitalized children with Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia in general pediatric units, the MP-IgM positivity rate is higher in females than in males5,21,22. This finding indicates that there is no significant difference in sex susceptibility between patients with severe and nonsevere Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia.

The data in this study indicate that when the PM2.5 concentration is ≥ 35 µg/m3, the PM10 concentration is ≥ 50 µg/m3, and the NO2 concentration is ≥ 25 µg/m3, PM2.5, PM10, and NO2 are moderately positively correlated with the number of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae cases. This may be because air pollutants directly stimulate the respiratory tract, leading to weakened immune function and enhanced pulmonary inflammatory responses, thereby creating favorable conditions for the onset of Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia. Similar findings have been reported in other studies, such as those by Shen et al.23, who proposed that exposure to PM2.5 is closely related to airway inflammation, characterized by increased infiltration of inflammatory cells, an imbalance of T-helper cell subpopulations (increased Th17 proportions and decreased Treg proportions), and increased expression of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR).

Additionally, the metallic components in PM2.5, such as calcium, cadmium, lead, strontium, tin, vanadium, and zinc, can induce oxidative stress by increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and depleting antioxidant reserves. This leads to lipid peroxidation, DNA mutations, and protein (enzyme) oxidative damage, thereby adversely affecting respiratory function24,25. NO2 can also impair lung function through mechanisms such as epigenetic changes, including reduced methylation of the NOS2A gene and increased methylation of the ARG2 gene, as well as exacerbation of airway inflammation26.

To further account for potential collinearity among air pollutants, an exploratory generalized additive model including PM2.5, PM10, and NO2 was performed. In this multivariable framework, PM10 and NO2 remained significant predictors, whereas PM2.5 did not show an independent effect. These results suggest that the contribution of PM2.5 may largely reflect its correlation with other pollutants, while PM10 and NO2 appear to play a more direct role. However, concurvity diagnostics revealed strong collinearity among PM2.5, PM10, and NO2. Given the limited sample size (n = 48 months), the multi-pollutant GAM estimates may therefore be unstable and susceptible to overfitting. Accordingly, the overall associations between air pollution and severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia should be interpreted with caution and require confirmation in larger, multicenter studies.

When the average temperature was < 20 ℃, a moderate negative correlation was observed with the number of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae cases. At temperatures ≥ 20 ℃, a moderate positive correlation was noted, although this did not reach statistical significance. This suggests that temperature alone cannot fully explain the seasonal variation in severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae incidence. Case distribution demonstrated a clear summer peak, followed by a smaller peak in winter. In the Chinese context, several behavioral and social factors may have contributed to these patterns. During the summer months, the long school vacation results in children spending more time in close contact with peers in tutoring classes, summer camps, or family gatherings. In addition, the extensive use of air conditioning and prolonged stays in enclosed indoor environments may facilitate respiratory pathogen transmission despite the hot climate. By contrast, in winter, low outdoor activity levels and increased time spent indoors in poorly ventilated spaces, combined with weakened immune defense at lower temperatures, may predispose children to infection. School reopening after summer and winter breaks also creates opportunities for clustered outbreaks, as large numbers of children congregate in classrooms and dormitories.

This study did not reveal a significant correlation between humidity and the number of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae cases, suggesting that humidity may have a minimal impact on the occurrence of Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia. While this study revealed a positive correlation between PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations and the number of cases of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia, Zhang et al. reported a negative correlation between the occurrence of Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia and PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations21. This apparent inconsistency may be explained by several contextual differences. First, pollutant composition varies geographically: In Chengdu, basin meteorology promotes PM2.5 rich in secondary inorganic and organic aerosols, and frequent stagnant weather together with soil dust contributes to higher PM10 levels. In Changsha, pollution is more influenced by traffic and industrial emissions, with combustion-derived components dominating PM2.5 and PM10, and NO2 concentrations also elevated due to dense vehicular exhaust. These differences indicate that pollutant composition, in addition to concentration, may influence the epidemiological patterns of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia. Second, Zhang’s findings may reflect the fact that under good air quality conditions (i.e., PM2.5 <35 µg/m3 and PM10 < 50 µg/m3), particulate matter is unlikely to be the primary factor influencing the occurrence of severe M. pneumoniae pneumonia. Instead, other determinants such as immune dysfunction or deficiency, preceding viral or bacterial infections, and underlying health conditions may play more significant roles. Third, the study populations were not identical. Zhang et al. included all pediatric respiratory tract infections, while the present study concentrated on severe cases admitted to the PICU, who may have heightened susceptibility to pollutant-induced immune and inflammatory responses.

Although the present study used outdoor monitoring data, which may not fully capture children’s actual exposure, existing evidence supports their representativeness. Studies in different regions have shown that indoor concentrations of particulate matter are generally lower than, but closely correlated with, outdoor levels, and that temporal variations indoors largely follow outdoor trends27. Investigations in industrial clusters further indicate that indoor particulate matter and black carbon are strongly influenced by surrounding outdoor pollution sources, contributing to cumulative health risks28. Moreover, research in urban residential settings has demonstrated that children’s respiratory outcomes are closely associated with outdoor pollutants such as PM2.5, PM10, and NO2, while indoor environments interact with these exposures29. Taken together, these findings suggest that although differences exist between indoor and outdoor microenvironments, outdoor monitoring data provide a reliable and scientifically valid proxy for assessing children’s exposure in epidemiological studies.

Furthermore, Xu et al.30 reported that for every 1 °C increase in temperature, the incidence of Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia increased by 0.83%. This result further supports the positive impact of temperature on the incidence of Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia, and the lack of a significant correlation with humidity aligns with the conclusions of this study.

Conclusion

The positivity rate and number of cases of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia in the PICU showed a yearly increasing trend from 2020 to 2023. Severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia is most prevalent in summer and is most common among preschool-aged children. When the PM2.5 concentration is ≥ 35 µg/m3 and the PM10 concentration is ≥ 50 µg/m3, PM2.5 and PM10 were moderately positively correlated with severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia.
