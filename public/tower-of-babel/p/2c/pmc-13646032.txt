Clinical and genetic features of pediatric hereditary polyposis syndromes in Israel: A nationwide multicenter cohort
Authors: Shlomi Cohen, Tal Cozacov, Ilana Weintraub, Ron Shaoul, Oren Ledder, Dotan Yogev, Mordechai Slae, Ranya Egbarieh, Galina Ling, Anat Yerushalmy‐Feler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesHereditary polyposis syndromes in children are rare, heterogeneous disorders associated with significant morbidity and long‐term risk of malignancy. Current data on their presentation, management, and outcomes in pediatric populations remain limited.MethodsWe conducted a nationwide, multicenter, retrospective study that included children diagnosed with polyposis syndromes in eight pediatric gastroenterology centers in Israel. Demographic, genetic, clinical, endoscopic, and outcome data were collected.ResultsA total of 105 children were included: 30 with juvenile polyposis syndrome (JPS), 26 with familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP), 19 with Peutz–Jeghers syndrome, 16 with phosphatase and tensin homolog‐hamartoma tumor syndrome, 8 with constitutional mismatch repair deficiency syndrome, 5 with hereditary mixed polyposis syndrome, and 1 with Lynch syndrome. The median age at diagnosis was 8 years (interquartile range 4.1–11.5). Overall, 43 (41%) were of Ashkenazi Jewish origin, and a family history of polyposis was present in 54 (51%). Genetic testing was performed in 91 (87%), identifying pathogenic variants in 77 (73%) of them. Gastrointestinal symptoms were reported in 60 (57%) and extra‐intestinal manifestations in 48 (46%). Polyposis‐related surgery was required in 22 (21%) children. Malignancy in eight children included two cases of colorectal carcinoma and glioblastoma multiforme, T cell lymphoma with Wilm's tumor, medulloblastoma, ganglioneuroblastoma, and abdominal adenocarcinoma. Three mortality events were recorded.ConclusionIsraeli children with hereditary polyposis syndromes present with diverse clinical and genetic features. Despite increasing use of genetic testing, morbidity and malignancy risks remain substantial, highlighting the need for early recognition, surveillance, and multidisciplinary care.

INTRODUCTION

Hereditary polyposis syndromes comprise a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by the development of polyps throughout the gastrointestinal (GI) tract and an increased lifetime risk of malignancy. Although rare, these conditions collectively represent an important cause of morbidity in pediatric populations due to GI bleeding, anemia, intussusception, protein‐losing enteropathy, burden of repeated endoscopic surveillance, and risk of early‐onset malignancies. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4

The most ubiquitous pediatric GI polyposis syndromes include juvenile polyposis syndrome (JPS), familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP), Peutz–Jeghers syndrome (PJS), phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN)‐hamartoma tumor syndrome, and constitutional mismatch repair deficiency (CMMRD). These conditions differ in genetic background, polyp burden, location and histology, extracolonic manifestations, and risk of malignancy. Pathogenic disease‐causing variants (DCVs) in genes, such as adenomatous polyposis coli (APC), SMAD4, bone morphogenetic protein receptor type 1A (BMPR1A), serine/threonine kinase 11 (STK11), PTEN, and mismatch repair genes underlie most of these syndromes. 2 , 5 , 6 , 7 Advances in germline genetic testing have substantially improved diagnostic precision and facilitated cascade screening of at‐risk relatives. However, genotype–phenotype correlations remain inconsistent, particularly in pediatric cohorts. 5 , 8 , 9 Additional diagnostic challenges include heterogeneous clinical presentation, differences in genetic testing practices, and the paucity of multicenter pediatric data needed to refine surveillance strategies and prognostic assessment.

To address these challenges, the European Society for Paediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (ESPGHAN) has published position papers providing recommendations for the management of JPS, PJS, and FAP in children, emphasizing early genetic evaluation, risk‐adapted endoscopic surveillance, and multidisciplinary care. 10 , 11 , 12 Nevertheless, pediatric‐specific, multicenter data, particularly those addressing presentation, genetic yield, management strategies, and outcomes across multiple polyposis syndromes, remain limited. Therefore, our aim in this study was to conduct a nationwide multicenter study to comprehensively characterize the demographic, genetic, clinical, and outcome profiles of children with GI polyposis syndromes in Israel.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Tel Aviv Sourasky Medical Center (TLV‐23‐0212) and equivalent committees of the contributing centers. Written informed consent was waived in accordance with the ethical committee's approval, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, given the retrospective nature of the study. The data retrieved from the medical records were anonymized for research purposes, and the researchers were blind to the names of the patients and their parents.

Study design

We conducted a nationwide, multicenter, retrospective cohort study involving 8 tertiary pediatric gastroenterology centers in Israel (Table S1). The study included children diagnosed with GI polyposis syndromes between 2010 and 2025.

Study population

All children and adolescents (age <18 years) with a confirmed diagnosis of a GI polyposis syndrome were included. Diagnosis was established based upon clinical criteria and/or genetic confirmation according to international guidelines. 10 , 11 , 12 Patients with isolated sporadic polyps without a defined hereditary syndrome were excluded.

Data collection

Children were identified through local institutional registries maintained at the participating centers. Data were retrospectively extracted from electronic medical records at all participating centers by means of a standardized case report form. Demographic data included age at diagnosis, sex, ethnicity, parental consanguinity, and family history of a known hereditary polyposis syndrome in first‐ or second‐degree relatives. We recorded results of germline genetic testing, including identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants. Genotype–phenotype correlations were explored within the various syndromes according to previous reports in children and adults. 9 , 13 Children with FAP were classified according to the APC mutation loci and the expected disease severity as cited in the adult literature 13 : mild FAP: APC 0–178 or APC > 1550, classical FAP: APC 179–1249, and severe FAP: APC 1250–1549. Clinical data included GI symptoms and extra‐intestinal manifestations (EIMs). Laboratory measures included serum hemoglobin and albumin levels. Anemia was defined according to the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria. 14 Endoscopic findings established the location and histology of polyps in both the upper and lower GI tracts. Evaluation of the small bowel was performed using additional modalities, including video capsule endoscopy (n = 19), magnetic resonance enterography (MRE) (n = 3), and device‐assisted enteroscopy (n = 8). Outcome measures included the need for surgical intervention, hospitalizations (excluding elective cases), development of malignancy, and mortality during follow‐up.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) or mean ± standard deviation (SD), as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Categorical variables were compared by the Chi‐square test or the Fisher exact test, while continuous variables were compared with the Mann–Whitney test. All statistical tests were two‐tailed. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. SPSS software (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, ver. 29, IBM Corp., 2023) was used for all statistical analyses.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

A total of 105 children from eight centers diagnosed with a GI polyposis syndrome were included in the study: 30 with JPS, 26 with FAP, 19 with PJS, 16 with PTEN‐hamartoma tumor syndrome, eight with CMMRD syndrome, five with GREM1‐positive DCV hereditary mixed polyposis syndrome (HMPS), and one with Lynch syndrome (Table 1). The median age at diagnosis was 8 years (IQR 4.1–11.5) and the median follow‐up duration was 6 (4–11) years. Fifty‐seven (54%) children were males and 43 (41%) were of Ashkenazi Jewish origin. A family history of polyposis was present in 54 (51%) children, of whom 37 (69%) involved a first‐degree relative. Genetic testing was performed in 91 (87%) children, identifying DCVs in 77 (73%, range 53%–100% across syndromes).

Symptoms were reported to be present in 84 (80%) children, specifically, GI symptoms in 60 (57%) (Table 2) and EIM in 48 (46%) (Table 3). The most frequent GI symptoms were abdominal pain (n = 32) and rectal bleeding (n = 31). The remaining asymptomatic children (20%) were identified following recognition of a known DCV/syndrome within the family, which prompted cascade genetic testing and subsequent endoscopic evaluation. Colonic polyps were detected in 70% (100% in JPS), evenly distributed throughout the segments of the colon across syndromes (Table 2). Upper GI endoscopy was performed in 96 (91%) children, and polyps were detected in 34 (32%) of them, with the highest rate in cases of PJS (68%). Polyps were detected in the small bowel in 13 (12%) children, mostly in those with PJS (37%).

Genotype‐phenotype correlation

Among the children with JPS who completed a genetic evaluation, those with DCVs negative (n = 6, 27%) were younger at diagnosis than those with DCVs positive (n = 16, 73%) (3.5 [2.3–4.8] vs. 8 [7.5–9.5] years, p = 0.005). A family history of polyposis was more frequent in the DCVs‐positive group (88% vs. 17%, respectively, p = 0.001). All other demographic, clinical, laboratory, and endoscopic findings were comparable between the groups. Outcomes were also similar between the two groups.

Six of the 16 children with JPS and a positive DCVs (38%) carried a SMAD4 mutation and 10 (62%) carried a BMPR1A mutation. Clinical characteristics and outcomes were similar between patients with SMAD4 and BMPR1A mutations. However, while three children with a SMAD4 mutation required colectomy, no comparable need for surgery was reported in the BMPR1A mutation group.

Mutations in the FAP group were as follows: nine children had APC > 1550 mutations, eight had APC 179‐1249 mutations, and two had APC 1250–1549 mutations. None had mutations in APC 0–178, and the mutation data were not available for two children. The clinical characteristics and outcomes were comparable across genotypes. Similarly, no significant genotype‐phenotype correlation was observed in children with PJS.

Outcomes

Thirteen children were hospitalized at a median age of 8 (4.5–12.5) years. Five children had >1 hospitalization. The indications of hospitalizations were anemia (n = 7), intussusception (n = 7), rectal bleeding (n = 3), and small bowel obstruction (n = 1).

Polyposis‐related surgery was required in 22 (21%) children at a median age of 10 (8–14) years. The surgeries included colectomy (n = 13), small bowel resection (n = 8), and pancreaticoduodenectomy (n = 3). The main indications for surgery were intussusception (n = 6), dysplasia (n = 5), severe anemia (n = 3), high burden of polyps (n = 2), malignancy (n = 2), uncontrolled bleeding (n = 1), and small bowel obstruction (n = 1).

Eight patients were diagnosed as having malignancy: colorectal carcinoma in two 16‐year‐old males (one with CMMRD and one with Lynch syndrome), glioblastoma multiforme in two children with CMMRD (a 7‐year‐old female and a 10‐year‐old male), T‐cell lymphoma with Wilm's tumor in 1 child with CMMRD, a medulloblastoma in another child with CMMRD, a ganglioneuroblastoma in one child with PTEN, and abdominal adenocarcinoma in a 20‐year‐old male with JPS. The clinical presentation of the patient with Lynch syndrome included rectal bleeding, and colonoscopy revealed colorectal carcinoma without associated polyposis. Both patients with glioblastoma multiforme died within 1 and 3 years from diagnosis, as did the patient with abdominal adenocarcinoma.

DISCUSSION

In this nationwide multicenter cohort, we described the clinical characteristics, genetic findings, and long‐term outcomes of children diagnosed with GI polyposis syndromes in Israel. To the best of our knowledge, this represents one of the largest pediatric cohorts of hereditary polyposis syndromes reported at a national level.

The distribution of the various polyposis syndromes in our cohort is broadly consistent with previously reported pediatric series. 15 JPS and FAP together accounted for more than one‐half of the cases, similar to reports from large epidemiological cohorts and registry‐based studies. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 Furthermore, our cohort also included a relatively large proportion of children with rare syndromes, such as PJS, PTEN hamartoma tumor syndrome, and CMMRD. This likely reflects the nationwide design of the study and the inclusion of multiple large referral centers with expertise in hereditary GI disorders.

An additional aspect explored in our study was the potential association between ethnicity and the distribution of polyposis syndromes. Israel is characterized by a highly heterogeneous population composed of multiple Jewish and non‐Jewish ethnic groups, the former including individuals of Ashkenazi, Sephardi, and mixed ancestry. In our cohort, 41% of patients were of Ashkenazi Jewish origin, representing the largest ethnic group, followed by patients of Sephardi (31%), and mixed ethnic backgrounds (24%). However, the distribution of ethnic groups was similar across the different polyposis syndromes, and no significant differences were identified. This observation is notable given that several hereditary polyposis disorders in Israel (e.g., JPS) have been associated with founder mutations in specific adult ethnic populations. 23 , 24 The lack of a clear ethnic predominance in our cohort may reflect the increasing genetic and social heterogeneity of the Israeli pediatric population, with growing intermarriage between historically distinct communities over recent decades. Consequently, ethnicity‐based differences may be more difficult to detect, particularly in rare hereditary conditions, such as polyposis syndromes.

Genetic testing was performed in the majority of patients and it identified pathogenic variants in three‐quarters of those tested. This detection rate is comparable to previously reported cohorts of hereditary polyposis syndromes. 19 , 25 , 26 In agreement with our previous study in children with JPS, we observed that patients without identifiable DCVs were diagnosed at a younger age than those with confirmed mutations. 9 These findings support the notion that children with DCVs‐negative JPS have early‐onset and more aggressive disease with early symptomatic presentation compared to those with DCVs‐positive patients. In contrast, a positive family history was strongly associated with the presence of pathogenic DCVs, supporting the role of inherited mutations in familial cases. No major clinical differences were observed between children carrying SMAD4 or BMPR1A mutations among those with DCVs‐positive JPS, although surgical intervention occurred only in patients with SMAD4 variants. Due to the limited sample size, these observations should be interpreted cautiously. Notably, we did not observe increased upper GI involvement in either group. This contrasts with the findings reported by Katz et al., in which increased gastric involvement was observed among BMPR1A mutation carriers, largely driven by a founder mutation in the Israeli population. 23 Overall, genotype–phenotype correlations in our cohort were limited, supporting the known variability in clinical expression of pediatric JPS.

While genotype–phenotype correlations have been described in JPS, we did not identify a similar association between genetic variants and clinical presentation in children with FAP. Previous studies have suggested associations between the location of mutations in the APC gene and disease severity or extracolonic manifestations, but such relationships are often difficult to demonstrate in relatively small pediatric cohorts. 13 , 27 , 28 Larger international datasets may be required to better define these associations in pediatric populations.

The endoscopic findings in our cohort reflected the characteristic patterns of the various syndromes. Colonic polyps were identified in most of our patients and were universally present in children with JPS, while upper GI involvement was less frequent overall and predominated in patients with PJS. The detection of small bowel polyps in a substantial proportion of children with PJS further supports the importance of dedicated small bowel evaluation, including capsule endoscopy or enterography in these patients.

EIMs were common in our cohort and reflected the characteristic phenotypes of the various polyposis syndromes. Findings, such as osteomas, dental anomalies, and congenital hypertrophy of the retinal pigment epithelium, were observed in children with FAP, while mucocutaneous pigmentation was typical in patients with PJS. Macrocephaly, lipomas, and neurodevelopmental disorders were prominent features in PTEN hamartoma tumor syndrome. Importantly, we observed that several of these manifestations may precede GI symptoms and can provide important diagnostic clues, emphasizing the need for clinical awareness and multidisciplinary evaluation in children with suspected hereditary polyposis syndromes.

Although most children were managed conservatively with endoscopic surveillance and polypectomies, a significant minority required hospitalization or surgery. Approximately one‐fifth of our cohort underwent polyposis‐related surgical intervention, mostly colectomy or small bowel resection. The indications for surgery were diverse and included intussusception, dysplasia, severe anemia, and high polyp burden. Intussusception was a particularly important complication in patients with PJS, reflecting the well‐established risk of small bowel obstruction in that syndrome. 11

Malignancy was rare but occurred primarily in patients with mismatch repair–related conditions. These findings highlight the aggressive cancer predisposition associated mostly with CMMRD and the need for intensive surveillance in affected individuals. Although malignancy was uncommon in the overall cohort, its occurrence in childhood or adolescence reinforces the critical role of early recognition and long‐term monitoring of these syndromes.

Our study has several strengths. The nationwide design and inclusion of multiple tertiary referral pediatric centers allowed the collection of a relatively large cohort of children. In addition, the long study period allowed assessment of clinically meaningful outcomes, such as the need for and performance of surgical interventions, the presence of malignancies, and the extent of mortality during childhood. Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design may have resulted in incomplete data capture. Second, although this represents a large pediatric cohort, the number of patients within individual syndromes remained relatively small, limiting the ability to detect genotype–phenotype correlations. Third, follow‐up duration during childhood may not fully reflect the long‐term risk of malignancy in these syndromes, many of which manifest cancer risk primarily in adulthood.

CONCLUSION

This nationwide pediatric cohort provides a comprehensive overview of polyposis syndromes in Israeli children. Genetic testing identified pathogenic variants in the majority of patients and should be considered an essential component of the diagnostic evaluation. Although malignancy was uncommon, a substantial number of children required hospitalization or surgery. These findings emphasize the role of early diagnosis, multidisciplinary management, and structured surveillance programs for children with hereditary polyposis syndromes.
