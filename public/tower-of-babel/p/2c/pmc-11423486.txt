Urothelial carcinoma associated with a long‐term indwelling cystostomy component of a subcutaneous ureteral bypass device in a domestic shorthair cat
Authors: Samantha Lackeyram‐Owen, Erin Gibson, Jennifer Reetz, Taylor Chan, Charles‐Antoine Assenmacher, Dana L Clarke
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrothelial carcinoma (UC) occurs uncommonly in cats and no association has previously been observed with long‐term indwelling urinary implants. An 18‐year‐old male castrated domestic shorthair cat initially was presented for hematuria, leading to the diagnosis of a right‐sided ureterolithiasis and severe pyelectasia on ultrasound examination, prompting right‐sided subcutaneous ureteral bypass (SUB) device placement. The cat subsequently had intermittent hematuria and dysuria, without ultrasonographic abnormality of the bladder or positive urine culture. Thirteen months later the patient developed refractory lower urinary tract signs, azotemia, a proliferative mass in the region of the cystostomy tube component of the SUB device and evidence of left ureteral obstruction. Cystostomy tube revision and left‐sided SUB device placement were performed, as well as a partial cystectomy for removal of the mass. Upon histopathology, the mass was diagnosed as a UC. To our knowledge, UC associated with a long‐term indwelling cystostomy catheter component of a SUB device has not been reported in veterinary medicine.Keywords: feline, interventional radiology, oncology, radiology and diagnostic imaging, renal/urinary tract, ureteral obstruction, ureters

INTRODUCTION

Urothelial carcinoma (UC) is the most common neoplasm occurring in the lower urinary tract of cats. Although this disease in cats has been described, 1 , 2 limited information is available on environmental factors that may predispose cats to malignant transformation of the lower urinary tract. Comparatively, dogs and people have well‐documented environmental exposures and conditions that are strongly associated with increased risk of UC, including long‐term indwelling urethral catheters in people. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 We describe the diagnosis of UC in a cat after long‐term sterile cystitis with an indwelling cystostomy tube after subcutaneous ureteral bypass (SUB) device placement for benign ureteral obstruction.

CASE DESCRIPTION

History

An 18‐year‐old, male castrated domestic shorthair cat was presented to the Emergency Service of the Matthew J. Ryan Veterinary Hospital at the University of Pennsylvania for a 4‐day history of vomiting. Before presentation, the cat had a history of intermittent chronic upper respiratory infections. Physical examination was within normal limits except for mild serous discharge from the left nostril.

Diagnostic findings and interpretation

After CBC and a serum biochemistry panel, clinically relevant findings consisted of mild azotemia (blood urea nitrogen [BUN], 37 mg/dL, reference interval [RI]: 15‐32 mg/dL; serum creatinine concentration, 2.3 mg/dL, RI: 1‐2 mg/dL). On urinalysis, proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (4+) were present, and abdominal ultrasound examination disclosed obstructive right‐sided ureterolithiasis with severe pyelectasia, bilateral renal degenerative changes and normal bladder wall. Urine aerobic culture was negative.

Treatment and outcome

Three days of medical management failed to resolve the obstruction, prompting placement of a right‐sided SUB device under fluoroscopic guidance using previously described techniques. 1

After ventral midline incision, the right kidney was identified and its caudal pole cleared of retroperitoneal attachments. A pyelocentesis urine sample was obtained and a contrast pyelogram subsequently performed identifying moderate dilatation of the renal pelvis and scant passage of contrast through the native ureter, consistent with partial to almost complete obstruction. Nephrostomy and cystostomy tubes were placed. The kidney and bladder were unremarkable at the time of device placement. The subcutaneous port was placed on the right ventral body wall, and after final attachment of all sections of the SUB device a contrast study was performed to confirm patency and assess for leakage, and was unremarkable. The pyelocentesis sample was submitted for aerobic culture, which was negative.

The patient was discharged 2 days postoperatively with improved renal function test results (BUN concentration, 22 mg/dL; serum creatinine concentration, 1.7 mg/dL).

One month after discharge, the cat was re‐presented for pollakiuria and vocalizing when urinating. Laboratory test results indicated mild azotemia (BUN concentration, 33 mg/dL; serum creatinine concentration, 2.3 mg/dL) and moderate hematuria (4+) and proteinuria (3+) were present on urinalysis. The SUB device was aspirated and a sample submitted for culture, which was negative. The SUB irrigation indicated obstruction of the cystostomy tube and an unremarkable bladder wall was observed on ultrasound examination. The next day, fluoroscopic evaluation confirmed a kink in the cystostomy tubing (Figure 1). The patient underwent open revision of the cystostomy tube and was discharged the next day with improved laboratory test results (BUN concentration, 23 mg/dL; serum creatinine concentration, 1.7 mg/dL). The bladder was unremarkable at surgical evaluation. However, 5 days later, the owners reported persistent signs of dysuria and hematuria. Consequently, medications to treat lower urinary tract pain were altered to include prednisolone (0.72 mg/kg PO q 24 h) to treat inflammation and dosages of buprenorphine (0.02 mg/kg from 0.01 mg/kg PO q 8 h) and gabapentin (14.4 mg/kg from 7.2 mg/kg PO q 8 h) were increased.

Throughout the subsequent year, the patient was seen intermittently for persistent hematuria and lower urinary tract signs. Repeat diagnostics throughout this time included 8 aerobic cultures performed on samples aspirated from the SUB device that were all negative for bacterial growth, consistent with persistent sterile cystitis. Sterile cystitis subsequently was treated using a variety of medication combinations including prednisolone, buprenorphine, prazosin, and gabapentin, with variable but overall inconsistent response.

Thirteen months from the date of the right‐sided SUB revision surgery, the cat was presented for progressive lower urinary tract signs. On repeat laboratory testing, a mildly increased serum creatinine concentration (2.1 mg/dL) was detected, and both hematuria (4+) and proteinuria (3+) were noted on urinalysis. On abdominal ultrasound examination, a new markedly irregular thickening of the luminal bladder wall was noted encompassing the cystostomy tube of the right‐sided SUB device measuring 2.2 cm in width and protruding 1 cm into the lumen of the bladder, with irregular thickening extending to the region of the left ureterovesical junction and trigone (Figure 2). Differential diagnosis included inflammatory cystitis or neoplasia (UC). Progressive chronic left renal degenerative changes were identified along with shadowing debris within the distal aspect of the left ureter and associated mild ureteral dilatation. Left‐sided ureteral obstruction was suspected secondary to mineral debris or thickening or both of that region that was identified on ultrasound examination. Surgical revision of the right‐sided cystostomy tube and placement of a left‐sided SUB device was recommended, along with biopsy of the bladder wall thickening. The owners elected to proceed with revision 2 months later. At the time of the procedure, the cat was receiving lactulose (1 mL PO q12), prazosin (0.14 mg/kg PO q12), gabapentin (7.4 mg/kg PO q8), and prednisone (0.3 mg/kg PO q24). Preoperative laboratory testing and a focal ultrasound examination identified moderate progressive azotemia (BUN concentration, 44 mg/dL; serum creatinine concentration, 4.0 mg/dL) and progressive left‐sided hydronephrosis, renal cysts, and ureteroliths with static bladder wall thickening that previously had been identified. The next day, the patient underwent 3‐way PantsPort SUB device placement on the left side, the right‐sided cystostomy tube was replaced, and a biopsy of the mass in the bladder was taken. Externally, the bladder was palpably thickened in the region of the mass without overt extravesicular lesions. Focal cystotomy to view the region of the thickened wall identified an intravesicular light pink irregularly proliferative lesion surrounding the cystostomy tube and extending laterally toward the trigone. Histopathologic analysis identified UC with extensive infiltration into the smooth muscle wall, vascular invasion, and carcinomatosis. Although the patient's azotemia progressively improved and plateaued 4 days postoperatively, the cat became acutely anuric with progressive azotemia (BUN concentration, 35 mg/dL; serum creatinine concentration, 6.0 mg/dL) on the 5th day. New onset of clinically relevant peritoneal effusion was identified on abdominal ultrasound examination and found to be consistent with uroabdomen (effusion potassium concentration, 5.3 mmol/L; serum potassium concentration, 5.2 mmol/L; effusion creatinine concentration, 8.6 mg/dL; serum creatinine concentration, 6.0 mg/dL). Because these findings indicated a poor prognosis, the cat's owners elected euthanasia. Necropsy (Figure 3) disclosed urinary bladder and ureteral UC with focal subacute full‐thickness rupture and secondary uroperitoneum.

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, UC developing after placement of a long‐term indwelling cystostomy tube as a part of a SUB device has not been reported previously in a cat. Well‐documented recurrent and persistent sterile cystitis after cystostomy tube placement in this cat, and frequent ultrasonographic evaluations enabled temporal association of UC formation after cystostomy tube placement. Although the cause of malignant transformation in this cat cannot be conclusively determined, chronic inflammation leading to urothelial dysplasia and malignant transformation is the presumed method of carcinogenesis.

Although reports of UC in cats are uncommon, UC is reported more commonly in older cats within the ventral, dorsal, or apical regions of the bladder rather than in the trigone. 1 , 2 At the time of necropsy, UC was discovered in the body and trigone of the bladder in the cat described here. Etiology and risk factors associated with development of UC in cats are not well‐described. In dogs, exposure to pesticides, topical insecticides, and cyclophosphamide are associated with development of UC. 3 Other factors in dogs such as female sex, obesity, and Scottish terrier breed, also have been associated with increased risk of UC development.

In people, development of UC is attributed to carcinogens in the environment or exposures such as cigarette smoking, aromatic amines or polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons in various chemical products, ionizing radiation, and chronic inflammation in up to 50% of cases. 4 , 5 , 8 Pertinent to this report is a strong association in people between chronic indwelling urinary catheters for spinal cord injury management and an increased risk of bladder cancer. 7 , 9 , 10 , 11 Etiology is unknown but chronic inflammation is the presumed carcinogenic stimulus. An earlier histopathological evaluation of the bladder after variable duration of urethral catheterization in people for urethral obstruction from benign prostate hyperplasia has been reported. 6 Findings included evidence of urothelial dysplasia (6% of cases) in addition to more common findings such as mucosal inflammatory response or urothelial hyperplasia. More advanced urothelial changes were correlated with longer duration of catheterization. The authors considered urothelial dysplasia an important change and potential precursor to irreversible neoplastic change. 6 In cats, it is unknown how the duration of an implant in the bladder may affect susceptibility to neoplastic transformation of those tissues. Cystostomy tubes as components of SUB devices are lifelong implants in most cats in which they are placed, indicating that these implants can be well tolerated over long periods of time. Interestingly, the cat described here had poor tolerance of the cystostomy tube for the entire duration that it was in place. It is possible that chronic inflammation influenced development of UC in the bladder, although it is unknown if this tumor initially formed at the site of cystostomy tube insertion and spread to the region of the trigone, or vice versa. Additionally, it is possible that other unidentified risk factors could have existed in this cat and contributed to development of UC. Ultimately, chronic inflammation secondary to long‐term sterile or infectious cystitis, such as what may be seen with indwelling urinary catheters in people, can lead to malignant transformation of lower urinary tract tissues. 4 , 5 , 8

Subcutaneous ureteral bypass devices in cats have a high rate of success in resolving benign ureteral obstruction, but they can be associated with complications such as mineralization, obstruction, displacement, chronic urinary tract infection, perirenal effusion or hematomas, and kinking as well as chronic sterile cystitis. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Of the long‐term complications reported, malignant transformation of the tissues of the lower urinary tract in association with a SUB device has not been previously reported. A long‐term indwelling cystostomy tube likely would mimic the irritation and inflammatory conditions that may drive malignant urothelial transformation in people with indwelling urethral catheters. Long‐term lower urinary tract inflammation in association with the indwelling cystostomy tube was well documented in the cat described here. Close monitoring enabled identification of UC long after the cystostomy tube was placed in this cat. Although it is possible that microscopic UC not identified on ultrasound examination predated cystostomy tube placement in this cat and may have formed spontaneously, an association with the placement of cystostomy tube and subsequent development of UC is plausible and possible.

The normal appearance of the bladder on frequent repeat ultrasound evaluations and operative procedures, along with the acute change in the appearance of the bladder and mass‐like formation after cystostomy tube placement suggest that UC formed after cystostomy tube placement and may have been associated with the irritation and inflammation associated with the device. Urothelial carcinoma should be considered as a differential diagnosis in cases with a similar clinical presentation.
