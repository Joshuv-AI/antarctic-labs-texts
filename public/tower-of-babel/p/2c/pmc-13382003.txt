Efficacy and safety of dual antiplatelet treatment up to 72 hours after mild ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack in patients with established cardiovascular disease
Authors: Zhang Xia, Ying Gao, Weiqi Chen, S. Claiborne Johnston, Pierre Amarenco, Philip M. Bath, Xuan Wang, Peiyuan Gao, Hongyi Yan, Tingting Wang, Yingying Yang, Yanli Zhang, Yongjun Wang, Yilong Wang, Yuesong Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patients with established cardiovascular disease (CVD) may have a higher risk of recurrent stroke and a higher prevalence of antiplatelet resistance than those without CVD. This study investigated the efficacy and safety of dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) with clopidogrel plus aspirin in patients with established CVD. This is a secondary post hoc analysis of the Intensive Statin and Antiplatelet Therapy for Acute High-risk Intracranial or Extracranial Atherosclerosis (INSPIRES) trial. Patients with mild ischemic stroke (IS) or high-risk transient ischemic attack (TIA) of presumed atherosclerotic cause were randomized to receive either DAPT or aspirin alone within 72 h after symptom onset. Established CVD was defined as a prior diagnosis of IS, coronary artery disease, TIA, or peripheral arterial disease. The primary efficacy and safety outcomes were the occurrence of new stroke and moderate-to-severe bleeding within 90 days. This study included 6100 patients, with a mean age of 63.7 ± 9.6 years and 35.8% of female. DAPT was associated with a reduced risk of new stroke (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.67, 95%CI: 0.54–0.85, P < 0.001) in non-CVD patients but not in those with established CVD (HR = 0.97, 95%CI: 0.73–1.28, P = 0.82), compared with aspirin (P for interaction = 0.04). Moderate-to-severe bleeding did not differ by antiplatelet treatments across CVD subgroups (P for interaction = 0.32). In the INSPIRES trial, patients with established CVD may benefit less from clopidogrel–aspirin in reducing the risk of recurrent stroke within 90 days than those without CVD after mild IS or high-risk TIA.Trial registrationURL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique identifier: NCT03635749.

Introduction

Patients with acute ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA) commonly have a pre-existing history of cardiovascular disease (CVD), with prevalences of 29.7% for prior ischemic stroke, 13.9% for coronary heart disease, and 6.5% for atrial fibrillation [1,2]. Meanwhile, patients with CVD often share more stroke risk factors and face higher risk of recurrent stroke than those without CVD [3,4], suggesting that more intensive antiplatelet therapy may be warranted for stroke prevention in this population.

However, resistance to antiplatelet agents is common during treatment: aspirin resistance affects 5.5%–75% of patients with CVD, and clopidogrel resistance occurs in approximately 40%, largely attributable to factors such as inadequate dosing, poor adherence, increased platelet reactivity and platelet turnover [[5], [6], [7]], indicating that patients with CVD likely benefit less from the dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) of clopidogrel plus aspirin than those free of CVD. A study has shown that DAPT reduced the risk of myocardial infarction (MI) and ischemic stroke but increased bleeding risk compared with aspirin alone among patients with CVD after a median follow-up of 12 months [8]. Cardiovascular mortality and all-cause mortality did not differ between the treatment groups in the same population [8]. Notably, the highest risk period for stroke recurrence is within 90 days after minor stroke or TIA, during which the cases account for approximately 70% of cases within 1 year [9]. Although DAPT is recommended for secondary stroke prevention within 90 days [3], it remains unclear whether patients with established CVD require more intensive DAPT management and whether they could benefit from the DAPT within 90 days, given their relatively higher prevalence of stroke risk factors and antiplatelet resistance [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. Addressing this issue could provide important and urgently needed evidence on the applicability of DAPT for patients with new-onset stroke and pre-existing CVD.

In this exploratory post hoc subgroup analysis of the Intensive Statin and Antiplatelet Therapy for Acute High-Risk Intracranial or Extracranial Atherosclerosis (INSPIRES) trial, we investigated the efficacy and safety of clopidogrel–aspirin therapy initiated within 72 h after symptom onset of mild ischemic stroke or high-risk TIA in patients with established CVD.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

The data were derived from the INSPIRES trial. Details of the rationale, design, and main results of the INSPIRES trial have been published elsewhere [1,10]. Briefly, the INSPIRES trial was a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicenter, 2 ✕ 2 factorial trial conducted at 222 centers in China from September 17, 2018 to October 15, 2022. The trial evaluated the efficacy and safety of clopidogrel–aspirin therapy initiated within 72 h after symptom onset in patients with mild ischemic stroke or high-risk TIA of presumed atherosclerotic cause. Eligible participants were patients aged 35–80 years and who had mild ischemic stroke (National Institute Health Stroke Scale [NIHSS] score ≤5) or high-risk TIA (ABCD2 score ≥4) between 24 and 72 h after onset, or ischemic stroke (NIHSS score 4–5) within 24 h of ictus. In addition, participants had to meet at least one of the following imaging criteria: at least 50% stenosis of a major intracranial or extracranial artery, or acute new multiple infarctions of presumed large-artery atherosclerosis origin. Exclusion criteria included stroke or TIA due to cardiac embolism or other definite non-atherothrombotic etiology; contraindications to clopidogrel, aspirin, or atorvastatin; serious illnesses such as non-vascular neurological disorders; receipt of intravenous thrombolysis, endovascular or antithrombotic therapy after onset, or dual antiplatelet or intensive statin therapy within 14 days before randomization; and hemorrhagic transformation or history of intracranial hemorrhage or subarachnoid hemorrhage. Detailed information on inclusion and exclusion criteria was provided in the study protocol [10].

In the INSPIRES trial, eligible participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive either clopidogrel (300 mg on day 1 and 75 mg daily on days 2–90) plus aspirin (100–300 mg on day 1 and 100 mg daily on days 2–21 and then a matching aspirin placebo for days 22–90), or matching clopidogrel placebo for 90 days plus aspirin (100–300 mg on day 1 and 100 mg daily on days 2–90) within 72 h after symptom onset. The trial demonstrated that DAPT reduced the risk of new stroke at day 90 but was associated with a higher risk of moderate-to-severe bleeding as compared with aspirin monotherapy [1]. The INSPIRES trial was approved by the ethics committees of Beijing Tiantan Hospital (No. KY2017-065-02) and all other participating centers. Patients or their representatives provided written informed consent before enrollment. This study was a post hoc analysis of the INSPIRES trial and included all participants from the parent trial.

Assessment of established cardiovascular disease

Trained research coordinators collected information of past medical history through face-to-face interviews using standardized questionnaires. Past medical history of ischemic stroke, coronary artery disease (including MI and angina), TIA, and peripheral arterial disease was ascertained through self-report and further validated by diagnostic records from specialist physicians. Established CVD was defined as the presence of any of the following past medical conditions: ischemic stroke, coronary artery disease (CAD), TIA, and peripheral arterial disease [11,12]. In addition, for sensitivity analyses, established CVD was redefined based on more severe cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events, including the presence of ischemic stroke, CAD, or TIA, and the presence of ischemic stroke or MI.

Efficacy and safety outcomes

The primary efficacy outcome was the occurrence of new stroke, including ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke within 90 days. The secondary efficacy outcomes included a composite cardiovascular event, ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, TIA, MI, death from cardiovascular causes, and poor functional outcome (modified Rankin scale score of 2–6, with higher scores indicating greater disability and a score of 6 indicating death). The composite cardiovascular event consisted of stroke, MI, or death from cardiovascular causes. The primary safety outcome was moderate-to-severe bleeding within 90 days defined by the Global Utilization of Streptokinase and Tissue Plasminogen Activator for Occluded Coronary Arteries criteria [13]. The secondary safety outcomes included death from any cause, any bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, and mild bleeding. Severe bleeding was defined as fatal or intracranial hemorrhage or bleeding resulting in haemodynamic changes requiring blood or fluid transfusions, cardiac stress therapy, ventricular assist devices, surgery, or cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Moderate bleeding was defined as bleeding requiring transfusion therapy but not resulting in hemodynamic changes that require intervention. Mild bleeding was defined as bleeding that does not require transfusion or fluid therapy and does not result in hemodynamic changes. This includes subcutaneous bleeding, small hematomas or bruises at the puncture site. If the patient's bleeding events were consistent with multiple classifications, it was classified as the most severe one. The definition of efficacy and safety outcomes was the same as that in the parent trial [10]. Participants were evaluated at their last follow-up after experiencing a clinical event, at the end of the trial, at the time of withdrawal from the trial, or at the last visit if the primary outcomes were missing. The time to the first event was used if there were multiple events of the same type.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentage. Differences in baseline characteristics between groups were compared using the Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables and the Chi-square test or Fisher's exact probability method for categorical variables. Efficacy and safety analyses were performed in accordance with the intention-to-treat principle and included all randomized patients from the parent trial. The cumulative incidence of new stroke and moderate-to-severe bleeding events between groups was estimated by the Kaplan–Meier method and compared by the log-rank test. Differences between groups in the incidence of stroke, composite cardiovascular events, TIA, MI, death, and bleeding events were estimated by the Cox proportional hazards model, with hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) reported. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed for the primary efficacy outcome by testing the interaction of groups by log-transformed time in models. The antiplatelet treatment groups satisfied the proportional hazards assumption (P > 0.05), while the CVD subgroups did not satisfy the proportional hazards assumption (P < 0.05), thus a time-dependent Cox proportional hazards model was fitted. Differences between groups in the proportion of poor functional outcome were estimated by the generalized linear model, with relative risk (RR) and 95% CI reported. All models were adjusted for age, sex, smoking state, NIHSS on admission, time to randomization, in-hospital antihypertensive therapy, lipid-lowering therapy before admission, in-hospital hypoglycemic therapy, antiplatelet therapy before admission, and statin therapy assignment (immediate or delayed intensive statin). The interaction term of antiplatelet treatment groups and CVD subgroups was included into models to assess interaction effects. Sensitivity analyses were performed in participants with different definitions of established CVD and in participants with previous ischemic stroke. To better balance baseline characteristics between the treatment groups, another sensitivity analysis was performed using the inverse probability weighting (IPW) method. Baseline characteristics between groups were adjusted using stabilized weights calculated from the propensity score. The propensity score was calculated using a logistic regression model based on following characteristics: age, sex, medical history (hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia), current smoking, use of agents before qualifying event (antiplatelet agent, aspirin, and clopidogrel), 50% symptomatic stenosis, time to randomization after onset of symptoms, NIHSS score in qualifying ischemic stroke, and ABCD2 score in qualifying TIA. Balance was assessed using the absolute standardized mean difference (SMD), with an SMD of <0.1 considered as a negligible level of differences. Weighted Cox proportional hazards model and weighted generalized linear model were used to estimate HR, RR, and 95% CI.

The post hoc analysis was exploratory and hypothesis-generating. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of 6100 patients, 2292 (37.6%) had established CVD at baseline (Fig. 1). The mean age and proportion of female were 62.6 ± 10.0 years and 35.2% in patients without CVD and 65.4 ± 8.8 years and 36.8% in patients with established CVD, respectively. Patients with established CVD were older and had higher proportion of use of agents, ≥50% symptomatic stenosis, NIHSS score of 4 or 5, and ABCD2 score greater than 5 compared to those without established CVD (Table S1). In patients without CVD, all baseline characteristics were not statistically significant between the DAPT group and the aspirin-alone group. In patients with established CVD, the DAPT group had lower proportion of female than aspirin-alone group. Association of established cardiovascular disease with stroke outcome

Within 90 days, the incidence of new stroke in patients with established CVD was numerically higher than that in patients without CVD (Fig. 2a). After adjustment of potential confounders, including antiplatelet therapy assignment (aspirin or clopidogrel plus aspirin), established CVD was not associated with an increased risk of new stroke, composite cardiovascular events, ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, TIA, MI, or death from cardiovascular causes. Nevertheless, established CVD was associated with a higher risk of poor functional outcome (adjusted RR = 1.39, 95%CI: 1.20–1.61, P < 0.001) within 90 days (Table 2). Among safety outcomes, the incidence of moderate-to-severe bleeding events was not significantly different between patients with and without established CVD (Fig. 2b). Established CVD was not associated with an increased risk of moderate-to-severe bleeding, any bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, or mild bleeding. Efficacy outcomes of clopidogrel–aspirin treatment in patients with and without established cardiovascular disease

In patients without established CVD, the incidence of new stroke in the DAPT group was lower than that in the aspirin group (Fig. 2c). DAPT was associated with a reduced risk of new stroke (adjusted HR = 0.67, 95%CI: 0.54–0.85, P < 0.001). In contrast, in patients with established CVD, the incidence of new stroke was similar between the DAPT and aspirin group (Fig. 2c). DAPT was not associated with a reduced risk of new stroke (adjusted HR = 0.97, 95%CI: 0.73–1.28, P = 0.82). The association between antiplatelet treatment and new stroke was significantly different in patients with and without established CVD (P for interaction = 0.04). Safety outcomes of clopidogrel–aspirin treatment in patients with and without established cardiovascular disease

The incidence of moderate-to-severe bleeding events was numerically highest in the DAPT group in patients with established CVD, followed by the DAPT and aspirin groups in without CVD, with the numerically lowest in the aspirin group with established CVD (Fig. 2d). After adjustment of potential confounders, there were no interaction effects of antiplatelet treatment by CVD subgroups for the risk of moderate-to-severe bleeding events, death from any cause, or intracranial hemorrhage. However, DAPT was associated with an increased risk of any bleeding and mild bleeding among patients with established CVD, but not among those without CVD (P for interaction<0.05) (Table 3). Sensitivity analyses showed similar results (Table S2–S5).

Discussion

In this post hoc analysis of the INSPIRES trial, patients with established CVD had a higher risk of poor functional outcome and death from any cause within 90 days compared to those without CVD. More importantly, DAPT was associated with a significant reduction in new stroke, composite vascular events, and ischemic stroke but not with increased risk of any bleeding and mild bleeding among patients without CVD. Conversely, among patients with established CVD, DAPT was not associated with a reduced risk of new stroke, composite vascular events, or ischemic stroke but was associated with an increased risk of any bleeding and mild bleeding within 90 days. These findings suggest that patients with established CVD may benefit less from clopidogrel plus aspirin DAPT but suffered from a higher risk of bleeding as compared to those without CVD.

Patients with established CVD are known to have a higher risk of recurrent stroke and other cardiovascular events including non-fatal MI, heart failure, cardiovascular death, and all-cause mortality, compared to those without CVD [[14], [15], [16]]. Nevertheless, our study found that established CVD was not significantly associated with an increased risk of subsequent stroke, TIA, MI, or death from cardiovascular causes. Two reasons may explain the inconsistent findings. First, the follow-up time of this study was 90 days, substantially shorter than studies investigating the prognosis of CVD after many years [14,15]. The current study is not likely to observe enough cardiovascular events and deaths in such a short period of time. In addition, all participants in this study received short-term and well-compliant antiplatelet therapy, which may have further reduced the incidence of atherothrombotic cardiovascular events and deaths associated with established CVD [17]. Thus, our study did not find a noticeable difference in the occurrence of stroke, cardiovascular events, or death from cardiovascular causes between patients with and without established CVD. However, this study demonstrated that established CVD was associated with a higher risk of poor functional outcome and all-cause mortality within 90 days. This suggests that CVD patients need more attention in clinical practice to prevent poor functional outcome and mortality after the onset of mild ischemic stroke or high-risk TIA.

A meta-analysis study found that the DAPT of clopidogrel with aspirin reduced the risk of MI and stroke but increased bleeding risk compared to aspirin alone in patients with established CVD [18]. However, the Clopidogrel for High Atherothrombotic Risk and Ischemic Stabilization, Management, and Avoidance trial showed that DAPT was not significantly more effective than aspirin alone in reducing the risk of MI, ischemic stroke, or death from cardiovascular causes but increased moderate bleeding risk in patients with symptomatic atherothrombosis [19]. In line with these studies [18,19], our study supported that the DAPT increased bleeding risk within 90 days among CVD patients. However, unlike the previous study [18], DAPT did not reduce the risk of recurrent stroke and composite cardiovascular events within 90 days among CVD patients. Several explanations have been proposed for these findings. First, patients with established CVD have more vascular risk factors related to stroke and higher thrombosis risk due to an elevated level of endothelial microparticles, platelet microparticles, thrombin generation, and more severe vascular inflammation and endothelial dysfunction as compared to those without CVD [3,[20], [21], [22], [23]]. Thus, it is more difficult for such a population to prevent recurrent stroke despite receiving DAPT. Second, the proportion of use of antiplatelet agents before qualifying events was significantly higher among CVD than those without CVD. The lack of a washout period for the previous antiplatelet treatment and the presence of antiplatelet resistance may result in the unapparent benefit of the DAPT [[5], [6], [7],24]. Third, both heterozygotes and homozygotes carriers of loss-of-function (LOF) CYP2C19 alleles have lower levels of active clopidogrel metabolite and diminished platelet inhibition compared with noncarriers [25]. Clinical data have shown that the frequency of LOF CYP2C19 alleles (∗2, ∗3) is 38.1% in patients with ischemic stroke, 36.9% in CAD, 35.4% in cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases [[26], [27], [28]], compared to 25.8%–27.9% in healthy population [29,30]. This implies that patients with established CVD likely benefit less from clopidogrel therapy due to the relatively higher frequency of LOF CYP2C19 alleles.

This study has important implications. We demonstrated that established CVD was associated with a higher risk of poor functional outcome and death from any cause within 90 days, supporting that CVD is a vital prognostic factor for patients with mild ischemic stroke or high-risk TIA. This population needs careful monitoring to avoid poor prognosis and death despite receiving DAPT. Furthermore, guidelines recommend early use of DAPT in minor ischemic stroke [3,4]. However, our study indicates that the efficacy of current DAPT was reduced in patients with established CVD after the onset of mild ischemic stroke or high-risk TIA. Thus, this population requires more attention in clinical practice. Antiplatelet therapy strategies may need further optimization to prevent recurrent stroke among patients with established CVD.

There are some limitations. First, this study is a post hoc analysis of the INSPIRES trial. The statistical power may be insufficient in CVD subgroups, especially in sensitivity analyses. Further studies with larger sample size are warranted to verify our findings. Second, it cannot rule out the influence of potential confounders because the randomization was not preserved in subgroups, although most of baseline characteristics were balanced between antiplatelet treatment groups and confounding factors were adjusted in the models. Third, established CVD was determined by the combination of self-report and diagnostic records from specialist physicians. Misclassification bias may exist because of recall bias and missing diagnostic records. Fourth, all patients in the INSPIRES trial were Chinese. It should be cautious to extrapolate our findings to White and Black populations. Finally, the short time of follow-up and the study design make it impossible to draw definitive conclusions. A randomized trial that includes clopidogrel resistance analysis and longer follow-up is needed.

In conclusion, established CVD was associated with an increased risk of poor functional outcome and death from any cause within 90 days among patients with new-onset mild ischemic stroke or high-risk TIA. Patients with established CVD may be not at a reduced risk of recurrent stroke and composite cardiovascular events but at an increased risk of any bleeding and mild bleeding after the treatment with DAPT of clopidogrel-aspirin initiated within 72 h after symptom onset. The efficacy of DAPT might be reduced in patients with established CVD.
