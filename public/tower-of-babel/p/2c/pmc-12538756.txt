Virtual reality for relaxation in intensive care unit patients: feasibility, acceptability and intervention adaptation
Authors: Ann Louise Hanifa, Pia Dreyer, Søren Aagaard, Anna Holm
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLess sedation has become the standard regimen in patients admitted to an intensive care unit. Consequently, conscious patients experience discomfort and anxiety during their admission. Virtual reality is used in other hospital settings to distract patients and reduce discomfort and anxiety; however, its acceptability in intensive care has yet to be explored. This study aimed to identify and adapt a virtual reality intervention for relaxation and distraction to meet the specific needs of patients admitted to an intensive care unit, focusing on patients’ and nurses’ acceptability of the intervention.MethodsThis exploratory study was guided by the Medical Research Council’s framework for developing or identifying, evaluating and implementing complex interventions. In the initial phase, combined knowledge from existing literature and experts in virtual reality for healthcare guided the preliminary identification process. Findings from think-aloud interviews with stakeholders guided the final adaptations. The data was analysed using the framework method.ResultsThe combined findings from the literature, stakeholder audit trails and interviews showed consensus that nature films with auditory stimuli were acceptable for inducing relaxation and distraction in critically ill patients, with few or no adverse effects. A key uncertainty regarding virtual reality’s effect on delirium or its potential role in delirium development affected nurses’ acceptability of virtual reality, highlighting a need to consider this risk.ConclusionsThis intervention identification, adaptation, acceptability and feasibility study concludes that virtual reality for relaxation and distraction is an acceptable intervention in the intensive care unit setting. The identified and adapted virtual reality software intervention was a 10-min 360-degree natural beach film combined with nature sounds and calming music. The identified hardware intervention was a headset connected to a tablet, enabling nurses to control the software. A subsequent study should examine the preliminary short effectiveness and adapt the intervention according to the further findings.Trial registrationRetrospectively registered in Open Science Framework 14 Feb 2024. Background

Over the past decades, sedation practices in intensive care units (ICU) have undergone significant changes due to the pharmacological side effects of sedatives [1, 2]. Traditionally, sedation was used to keep patients comfortable; however, conscious patients often experienced discomfort, thirst, blurred memory, loss of control, sleep disturbances and challenges in communication [3–5]. Patients describe feeling imprisoned and constrained by medical equipment in an unfamiliar environment [6], with their critical illness experiences persisting for years [7]. Following an ICU admission, patients frequently struggle with physical, cognitive and mental complications and decreased quality of life [8, 9]. Collectively, these complications are referred to as post-intensive care syndrome (PICS) [10], which manifests psychologically as anxiety, depression and post-traumatic stress disorder [11, 12].

Developing non-pharmacological interventions to alleviate patients’ discomfort and reduce perceived stressors has become an emerging focus in ICU research, particularly in response to evolving sedation practices [1, 13]. Exposure to natural environments, such as having a view of the outside world from the bedside or projecting nature images onto walls, has been proposed as a strategy to reduce stress in ICUs. However, implementing nature-based interventions is often challenging due to reliance on technical equipment and the architectural constraints of many ICUs [14, 15]. Exposure to nature-based interventions may reduce stressors in patients admitted to ICUs [14] and is well-documented to improve mental and physical health in other populations [16]. Notably, similar effects have been observed with exposure to natural environments through virtual reality (VR) [17].

VR refers to an immersive and multisensory computer-generated environment that effectively provides real experiences, giving the user a sense of being in that environment [18]. Within hospital settings, VR has been used to protect patients from the environment, promote well-being and reduce stress and anxiety in various populations [19]. VR is an emerging field within ICU research [20]. Implementing VR in ICUs requires a thorough assessment of its impacts on critically ill patients with impaired consciousness and cognition [21] and at risk of delirium [22]. However, existing knowledge supports that VR is both safe and feasible for use in the ICU [23–25] and may reduce discomfort, anxiety and stress [26]. Presently, the growing interest of VR companies in developing solutions for hospital use presents numerous opportunities. However, to identify and adapt an existing VR intervention for ICU patients, it is essential to evaluate its feasibility, acceptability and suitability for this specific clinical context.

Aim

This study aims to identify and adapt an existing VR intervention for relaxation and distraction to meet the specific needs of patients admitted to an ICU, focusing on patients’ and nurses’ acceptability of the intervention.

Methods

This explorative feasibility study was guided by the Medical Research Council's (MRC) framework for developing or identifying, evaluating and implementing complex interventions [27]. The MRC framework is iterative and has four phases. Research may begin in any phase, depending on key uncertainties and existing evidence and experience related to the intervention. Six core elements guide decisions made in the four phases [27]. In this study, the research began by identifying an intervention and concludes with an evaluation of the feasibility of the intervention design in an ICU setting. The core elements in focus are the context, key uncertainties and stakeholder involvement.

Context

The context is the circumstances under which the intervention is implemented and refers to active and unique features that may influence, facilitate, or constrain the intervention [27, 28]. In this study, the context was the complex setting of the ICU.

Key uncertainties

Key uncertainties refer to gaps in knowledge that persist despite existing research [27]. A key uncertainty in using VR in the ICU is user acceptability. The theoretical framework for acceptability (TFA) describes acceptability as a necessary condition for an intervention’s effectiveness, as it determines whether the intervention will be used. Patients and healthcare professionals will be more likely to use or deliver an intervention they consider acceptable [29]. The TFA has seven component constructs [29]; we focused on three: (1) affective attitude to investigate whether VR would be used by patients and nurses in the ICU if available; (2) perceived effectiveness to clarify if end-users perceived VR as a likely intervention for relaxation and distraction; (3) ethicality to include considerations related to exposing vulnerable patients to a virtual environment as if it were real, as doing so may not fit with individual patients’ and nurses’ value systems.

Stakeholder involvement

Stakeholder involvement is a core element in the MRC Framework and the ADAPT guidance [27, 30]. Stakeholders were identified as end-users (patients and nurses in an ICU), VR experts (providers or users of VR in patient care) and research team members. VR experts guided the initial stages of identifying existing interventions and adapting a VR intervention for ICU use. This study was centred on the target population, ensuring that the selection and adaptation of the intervention were informed by the perspectives and experiences of end-users [31].

Adaptation

When adapting interventions to new contexts, it is essential to determine whether existing interventions are suitable and to consider if adaptations alter their underlying mechanisms or introduce unintended consequences. These considerations should be addressed before formal evaluation and implementation [30]. In this study, adaptations were designed to be acceptable and feasible for use in clinical practice from an end-user perspective and were agreed upon by the research team. The team comprised four members with extensive clinical and research experience in the ICU, representing a critical care nurse, two clinical nurse specialists and a medical doctor/ICU Consultant.

Identifying the knowledge base

Data collection was conducted to identify, include and exclude potential VR providers and make a preliminary assessment of suitable content for the VR intervention.

Literature overview

A targeted literature overview was conducted to inform intervention adaptation. We did not perform a systematic review as reviews on VR interventions had recently been published. Literature on VR interventions for relaxation and distraction aimed at patients admitted to an ICU was identified in medical databases (PubMed and CINAHL), supplemented with internet searches. Search concepts included the following: patient (adult, ICU, critical illness), intervention (virtual reality, VR/exposure therapy, augmented reality), context (intensive care units, critical illness, critical care) and outcomes (delirium, sleep, quality of life, anxiety, depression, traumatic stress). Literature was included if it addressed interventions for relaxation or distraction for patients in ICUs, focusing on feasibility and outcomes related to stress, anxiety, relaxation and distraction.

Identifying existing VR

The identification of existing VR interventions aimed at relaxation and adaptable to the ICU setting was based on a mapping of companies providing VR in Denmark [32] and insights from a network of VR experts in the hospital region.

Experiences of VR experts

An audit trail, inspired by Carcary’s methodological guidance [33], was used to document and justify decisions throughout the intervention identification and adaptation process. This trail consisted of ad hoc information from meetings with VR experts and reflections from the research team.

Theoretic perspectives

The intervention development was informed by Galvin and Todres’ Caring and Well-being life world approach [34]. This theoretical perspective aligns with the growing interest in humanising ICU treatment and care to improve patient outcomes in the ICU [35]. Well-being is an important phenomenon in caring science and serves as a key approach to humanising healthcare [36].

Participants and study setting

The primary target population for the VR intervention consisted of patients admitted to an ICU, with nurses as the secondary target group.

Qualitative data was collected across two university hospitals and five medical and surgical ICUs to capture perspectives from different organisational and clinical contexts. Collectively, these ICUs provide 68 beds and admit approximately 4000 patients annually. Patients were recruited from three ICUs at one hospital, while nurses were recruited from four ICUs spanning both hospitals. Data was collected between June and November 2023. Data collection was ceased when data provided insights across participants aligning with knowledge drawn from existing literature and expert input. Patients and nurses were recruited through nurse coordinators at the different units and sites. Patients were recruited based on the clinical nurses’ ethical decision regarding VR appropriateness. Eligible patients were contacted by the primary researcher to provide information, assess inclusion and obtain consent. One patient declined participation.

Think-aloud interviews

Individual and paired think-aloud interviews were conducted with patients and nurses to investigate the end-user perspectives on acceptability, ease of use and ethical considerations, guided by the MRC framework and the TFA [27, 29, 37].

The think-aloud method combines observations with participants’ real-time reflections as they perform a task and a brief exit interview to triangulate data [38]. The think-aloud approach is in line with the recommendations for data collection when developing interventions [39].

In this study, the think-aloud approach was adapted to accommodate participants using the VR headset, as they occasionally required verbal prompting due to their inability to see the interviewer. Exit interviews were executed immediately after the VR sessions.

Analysis method of interview data

Data from the interviews was analysed using the Framework Method for management and analysis of qualitative data. This approach was selected because it facilitates collaborative analysis and enables the integration of insights from different data sources [40].

The Framework Method has seven stages of analysis. (1) Audio recordings were transcribed into verbatim text. (2) Researchers familiarised themselves with the entire data set across all sources. (3) Data was coded and paraphrased, emphasising important passages. (4) Preliminary codes were grouped into categories to develop a working analytical framework. (5) The analytical framework was applied to the existing interviews. 6) Summarised data and quotations were chartered into the framework matrix, ensuring a balance between reducing data and retaining the original meaning. (7) The analysis of the dataset involved exploring and interpreting the data, generating themes that described and explained the categories through comparison between and within the cases (40). The first and last authors independently coded the patient transcripts line by line in stage 3. A preliminary coding framework was then developed and iteratively refined within the research team before being applied to the existing interviews in stage 5. Manual coding was chosen over qualitative software, as the structured stages of the Framework Method supported systematic organisation of the data without software assistance.

Ethical considerations

The study was registered at the Regional Data Protection Agency (no. 1-16-02-180-23). Under Danish law, interview studies do not require ethical approval. All interview participants provided informed verbal and written consent, and the study was approved by leading staff in the ICU department. Participants were given time to consider their participation and could withdraw consent without consequences, as outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [41]. The study is registered in the Open Science Framework [42].

Findings

The findings presented below integrate insights from the existing literature on VR interventions in the ICU setting, expert knowledge from VR providers and users and findings from end-user interviews (patients and nurses). The demographic data of the interview participants is outlined in Table 1. The patients and nurses varied in age, length of ICU stay and years of work experience at the time of data collection. A total of seven patients participated, completing 11 VR sessions, while 11 nurses were interviewed after testing two VR solutions and participating in two or more VR sessions. The patient interview duration ranged from 5 to 35 min (mean ≈ approximately 21 min), reflecting variations in participants’ clinical conditions and tolerance for prolonged conversation. The nurse interviews ranged from 25 to 45 min (mean ≈ approximately 32 min). Findings from the literature and knowledge from VR experts

Findings from the existing literature suggested combining visual and auditory stimuli, fitting the scenes to enhance the feeling of immersion in VR [48].

The length of VR sessions varied across studies, ranging from 5 min [43, 51], 15 min [44–47, 51] to 20 min [49]. One study did not predefine session length, allowing participants to choose durations between 5 and 20 min [50]. Most participants completed the planned VR sessions, while non-completion was attributed to technical errors, subjectively expressed discomfort or unwillingness to continue [46], claustrophobia and displacement of the headset [47], fatigue, feeling overwhelmed, or unawareness that sessions were intended to last 20 min [49].

All of the above studies tested 360-degree natural or computer-generated landscapes in combination with ambient nature sounds, breathing exercises, hypnotic speech, or relaxing music. However, the outcome measures and test methods varied across studies.

Findings from the audit trail of VR users highlighted the importance of considering cost impact and perspectives for other uses. Users independently recommended subscription-based software due to the high costs of VR programmes and the rapid pace of technological advancements. Furthermore, they recommended keeping the intervention simple and user-friendly and using short films for optimal usability and engagement. Users with experience in using relaxing and distracting VR for psychiatric disorders and dementia reported no adverse effects of VR in these vulnerable patient groups and hence expressed no concerns about using VR for patients at risk of delirium. Based on their knowledge from other healthcare settings, they suggested three potential VR providers for collaboration within the ICU setting. Following this assessment, two VR interventions were chosen for acceptability interviews with ICU end-users. The VR providers suggested using natural environments for relaxation and distraction for patients in ICUs. They underlined the potency of immersion in the VR experience. One company had experience with exposure therapy, relaxation and distraction for children and adults during surgical procedures and patients with psychiatric disorders. The other company had experience with pain management in children during procedures, virtual ICU follow-up and physical and cognitive rehabilitation. Findings from the end-user interviews

Findings from the interviews produced three main themes: the experience, the content and the functionality related to the TFA components of affective attitude, ethicality and perceived effectiveness. The themes are presented below and in Table 2.

The experience

This theme captured patients’ and nurses’ experiences of immersion in the virtual world, and the degree to which VR distracted them from the ICU environment.

The patients experienced distraction from the ICU environment differently. Several patients described being transported away from the ICU and immersed in the natural scenes, forgetting their surroundings, with one reflecting that “I didn’t think about anything but watching the waves and the blue sky” (P3). Nurses were often surprised by the realism: “It surprises me that it is this realistic. It is like being in a forest… I felt like being in another world” (N5). They described VR as a sensory “escape” or shield from the ICU environment (N1, N3, N6 + 7, N8 + 9). However, concerns were raised about how immersive experiences might affect patients with delirium or at risk of developing delirium.

Not all patients experienced full detachment. One remained aware of the ICU noises despite feeling immersed in the beach scene (P6). Another found the experience overstimulating (P4). The reflection was supported by a patient who enjoyed the session but worried that it might have been overstimulating earlier on (P2). These accounts highlight that patients’ tolerance for immersion may vary depending on illness stage.

The content

The theme included the visual and auditory content of the VR interventions. All patients and nurses agreed that nature scenes were ideal for relaxation, and most suggested that VR sessions should last between 5 and 15 min, depending on the situation (P5, P6, P7, N1, N2, N3, N8 + 9). Familiar environments were especially valued: as one nurse described, “I choose a forest because I live next to a forest, and it is lovely being there because everything is completely peaceful” (N3). Similarly, other participants highlighted the calming effect of places they associated with personal meaning, such as the sea or a field in summer. Nature scenes that allowed for gazing were preferred, and combining the visual and auditory stimulation underpinned the sense of immersion. Three nurses expressed concerns about incorporating relaxing music into the sessions, as music may evoke personal associations that vary among individuals (N1, N6 + 7). However, observations suggested the combination of relaxing music and nature sounds enhanced the calming feeling of the VR, potentially by further masking environmental noises.

It was evident in the observations that participants spent time re-orientating themselves when scenes changed in the films. One patient verbalised how this counteracted the calming experience (P7). No patients reported symptoms consistent with cybersickness, but three nurses were concerned about causing cybersickness in patients.

However, some patients were also interested in being entertained and suggested VR for uses other than relaxation (P1, P3, P6). Two patients found the nature films boring after a while, although they made them relax (P3, P6). Nurses similarly speculated on alternative uses of the VR. This indicated the potential for broader applications within the ICU, should the current intervention prove functionally feasible.

The functionality

The theme encompassed findings related to practical functionality, perceived effectiveness and expectations regarding the intervention’s ability to fulfil its intended purpose. Most findings were derived from the nurses’ perspectives.

Patients and nurses found the headset comfortable and wearable. If necessary, glasses could be worn underneath the headset, although this was not always needed.

All patients and nurses regarded VR's potential to induce relaxation and distraction in the ICU context positively. VR sessions could function as alternatives to TVs and radios, shield patients from ICU noises and serve as a non-pharmacological intervention to reduce anxiety. Furthermore, it could serve as an activity that did not involve physical activity or leaving the ICU.

In the ICU setting, the technology was easy to use and control via the tablet. Nurses emphasised the importance of knowing exactly what the patient was watching due to challenges in communication. As one nurse stated: “It’s difficult to control what the patient sees, especially if the patient can’t tell me” (N6 + 7). One solution showed icons on the screen, i.e. volume or options, which the nurses found disturbing as most patients could not navigate the menus anyway. Hence, a VR solution with a control tablet was preferred.

Final intervention: The identified and adapted VR software intervention was a 10-min 360-degree natural beach film combined with nature sounds and calming music. The identified hardware intervention was a VR headset connected to a control tablet. The adaptation was an increased length of the existing film. Details of the intervention and its adaptations are outlined in the template for intervention description and replication [52] in Additional file 2.

Discussion

This exploratory feasibility study aimed to identify and adapt an existing VR intervention to the ICU context with a particular focus on end-user perspectives.

The combined findings showed that nature-based films with auditory stimuli were broadly acceptable and perceived as calming, offering a potential non-pharmacological tool for relaxation and distraction in critically ill patients, with few or no adverse effects. In line with our findings, a study of healthy participants exposed to an ICU environment while receiving a VR intervention reported that the combination of auditory and visual stimuli was more effective in distracting from the surroundings than visual stimulation alone [53]. Natural environments are known to benefit well-being and reduce stress [54], a phenomenon that also applies to the ICU setting [55]. However, access to real nature is restricted in ICUs [56], suggesting that VR-based nature exposure may represent a feasible and low-cost way to deliver these benefits [57].

In our interview study, patients found the VR sessions relaxing and distracting, and nurses regarded VR's potential to shield patients from the ICU environment positively. Yet, the literature overview highlighted the challenges of measuring constructs such as anxiety and comfort in ICU research, although anxiety is the most frequently self-reported symptom [58]. This highlights the importance of testing whether VR can provide measurable relief within future trials and investigating the relevance of tests, assessment methods and outcome measures before commencing the subsequent trial.

In the findings, nurses expressed concerns that VR could trigger or exacerbate delirium by altering the patients’ sense of location. Supporting this, a study exploring patients’ recall of ICU delirium found that confusion regarding time and space was a central aspect of their experience [59]. However, evidence from other VR experts in different contexts and evidence from ICUs suggests that VR does not increase delusions and hallucinations [25]. Furthermore, findings from the nurses’ interviews raised concerns regarding VR’s potential to cause cybersickness, although not voiced by the patients. This may reflect the static, calming and relatively brief nature of the VR content, which differs from more dynamic or immersive VR applications. Future trials should monitor cybersickness, delirium and the impact of VR on ICU memories, and possibly exclude patients with delirium until we have a better understanding of VR’s effect on non-delirious ICU patients.

Findings revealed that the length of the VR sessions varied across studies, with end-users suggesting that sessions of approximately 10 min were the most acceptable, though tolerance varied. Currently, studies indicate varying effects of VR on anxiety depending on session length, with trends observed in sessions varying from 5 to 15 min [43, 44, 51]. Another study suggests a potential increase in effectiveness with longer sessions of up to 30 min. Nevertheless, few participants were able to complete 30-min sessions due to fatigue, feeling overwhelmed and/or experiencing discomfort [49]. A spectrum of sensory inputs has been reported to negatively affect patients' outcomes; constant exposure to light, noise and people may result in sensory overload, while a lack of varied sensory stimuli may lead to sensory deprivation [60]. Conclusively, we decided to create a 10-min film for this intervention. In subsequent studies, the dose–response relationship warrants further investigation, and VR should be administered flexibly to prevent overstimulation in vulnerable patients.

Findings from the existing literature showed that some VR sessions were discontinued due to technical issues; however, none of the studies have explicitly focused on the technological aspects of using and applying the VR hardware and software. In contrast, the nurses in our study found the headset-tablet combination acceptable and easy to use. This arrangement enabled real-time monitoring, which may help overcome concerns about safety and patient isolation [61]. This approach may help address uncertainties regarding the use of VR for vulnerable patients by enabling healthcare providers to participate in the experience, even if only as observers.

This study highlights several barriers and issues that need to be monitored in a subsequent trial, e.g. feasibility of outcome measures, delirium, cybersickness and any adverse experiences. Structured training, technical support and clear protocols could address barriers for uptake, given that technical competence has been identified as a barrier in other settings. Concerns regarding the appropriateness of VR should be addressed by developing inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure a safe selection of patients.

Strengths and limitations

The explorative design of this intervention acceptability, feasibility and adaptation study has several limitations. The limited number of participants and lack of preliminary quantitative data limit the ability to assess potential effectiveness and generalise the findings. Future feasibility and definitive trials will need to include larger and more diverse samples to capture a broader range of experiences. As the study was conducted across five ICUs and two hospital settings in Denmark, the intervention content may not be applicable in other ICU contexts, especially as end-users preferred films associated with personal meaning.

In this study, the patients had longer ICU admissions (median 45 days) than the Danish ICU average of 77 h [62]. Thus, the findings and the final intervention may not be suitable for patients with shorter ICU admissions. Patient recruitment was based on the nurses’ assessments of suitability for VR exposure, and most nurses and patients had no prior experience with VR. This provided insights into acceptability and potential barriers to introducing VR in the ICU, and may likewise reflect that some healthcare professionals protect vulnerable patients when acting as gatekeepers. Hence, this may introduce bias into patient selection research inclusion [63]. Consequently, in a subsequent effectiveness study, recruitment should follow clearly defined inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure a diverse patient population. Furthermore, given that concern about delirium influenced nurses’ acceptance of VR, future studies should carefully consider and address this factor in patient selection.

A strength of the study was the triangulation of findings from literature and experts and end-user perspectives that confirmed and expanded the knowledge of acceptability of VR interventions in ICUs. Using the MRC framework and ADAPT guidelines during the intervention identification and adaptation processes allowed us to address the core elements of context, key uncertainties and stakeholder involvement. However, not all aspects of the MRC framework were addressed in this study. Future research should focus on evaluation, implementation and further adaptations. The VR intervention was adapted based on current ICU patients’ experiences of the intervention, a novel approach that has not previously been seen in VR intervention literature within the ICU setting.

Conclusion

This explorative intervention identification, adaptation, acceptability and feasibility study concludes that VR for relaxation and distraction is an acceptable intervention in the ICU setting. Based on the findings from the literature and input from VR experts and end-users, the identified and adapted VR software intervention consisted of a 10-min 360-degree natural beach film combined with nature sounds and calming music. The identified hardware intervention was a VR headset connected to a tablet, enabling ICU nurses to control the software and follow the patient's engagement in the VR. This combination reassured nurses that patients were safe using the intervention.

Future evaluation studies should examine the preliminary short- and long-term effectiveness of the intervention as well as relevant outcome measures for assessing anxiety, stress and relaxation in ICU patients. A key uncertainty regarding VR’s effect on delirium or its potential role in delirium development affected nurses’ acceptability of VR, highlighting the need to consider this risk. Furthermore, using a mixed methods approach, where qualitative data help interpret quantitative findings, can nuance patient experiences. The VR intervention should be adapted according to further findings.
