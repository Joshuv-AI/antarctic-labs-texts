Development and validation of a novel score to predict adverse outcomes in paediatric congenital mitral regurgitation (PRIMARY): a multicentre cohort study
Authors: Han Zhang, Chunhu Gu, Jin Shentu, Gang Li, Tingting Xiao, Zhongqun Zhu, Yuehu Han, Tao Wang, Hui Zhang, Tianli Zhao, Hongbo Luo, Hao Zhang, Xinxin Chen, Xiangming Fan, Qiang Wang, Guocheng Shi, Huiwen Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundPaediatric patients who underwent surgery for mitral regurgitation (MR) have a high risk of recurrence or death; however, no prediction tool has been developed to risk-stratify this challenging subpopulation.MethodsIn this multicentre cohort study, paediatric patients undergoing surgery for congenital MR in Shanghai Children's Medical Center in January 1st, 2009–December 31st, 2022 were included for analysis while those had a combination with infective endocarditis, anomalous left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery, rheumatic valvular disease, connective tissue disease, or single ventricle were excluded. A Cox regression model predictive of the primary outcome (a composite of mortality or mitral valve [MV] re-operation) was derived and converted to a point-based risk score. This score was externally validated in a cohort of patients undergoing MR surgeries between January 1st, 2009 and December, 31st, 2022 in eight independent hospitals in China. The Harrell's c index and Hosmer–Lemeshow test was used to quantify the discrimination and calibration of the risk score.FindingsIn total, 2640 patients (female: 57% [n = 1505]) with a median age of 0.99 (IQR, 0.47–2.60) years were included. The primary outcome occurred in 262 patients (16.6%) over a median of 5.7-year follow-up in the derivation cohort (n = 1581; median age of 1.03 years [IQR, 0.47–2.67]) and in 130 (12.3%) over a median of 7.1-year follow-up in the validation cohort (n = 1059; median age of 0.93 years [IQR, 0.46–2.51]). The score variables included preoperative variables (age, the presence of primary MR, N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide, left ventricular ejection fraction, and left atrium z score), perioperative changes in z scores of LA and left ventricular end-diastolic dimension, and the procedural variable (use of MV annuloplasty or not). This risk score, ranging from −22 to 10, yielded strong discrimination (Harrell's c index: 0.85, 95% CI, 0.83–0.87) and exhibited good calibration of postoperative 10-year primary outcome (Hosmer–Lemeshow; χ2 = 9.85; P = 0.33) in the derivation cohort, which maintained in the validation cohort (Harrell's c index: 0.86, 95% CI, 0.83–0.89; Hosmer–Lemeshow; χ2 = 23.80; P = 0.64). In addition, a simplified stratification of the score model (low-risk: −22 to −5, intermediate risk: −4 to 0, high risk: >0) showed good performances in predicting the primary outcome in both derivation and validation (Ps < 0.001).InterpretationThe scoring system represents a crucial step towards personalised management care for paediatric MR. However, this model has not been applied in clinical practice and require validation in large and diverse cohorts of patients. Further work should aim to incorporate other novel multi-modality metrics to optimise the PRIMARY score.FundingChinese National Natural Science Foundation of China, Science and Technology Commission of Shanghai Municipality, Science and Technology Project of Guizhou Province.Keywords: Mitral regurgitation, Congenital heart disease, Paediatric, Risk score

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed and Web of Science for articles published in English using the keywords “MR”, “paediatric”, “risk factors”, “MV surgery” from January 1, 2004 to January 31, 2024. 44 studies explored risk factors in paediatric congenital MR, among which, 10 were multi-centred. These studies mainly focused on different etiologies, surgical techniques or clinical features, revealing younger age at surgery, emergency surgery, left ventricular outflow obstruction, genetic syndromes, lower ejection fraction, and concomitant surgery, etc. were associated with higher risks of post-operative mortality or recurrence of MR needing re-intervention of MV, while none of them carried out an integrated score system with good discrimination power in predicting surgical outcomes in such population. Therefore, the extent to which variables could be conducted in risk stratification in children undergoing MR surgery remains unclear.

Added value of this study

We have developed and validated the first prediction model in the paediatric MR setting, to our knowledge, that facilitated shared decision-making and allowed for individualised treatment pathway. Contemporarily, freedom from death or reoperation for recurrent MR in paediatric patients was 79.6% at postoperative 10 years. This point-based risk score incorporating 8 clinical, echocardiographic, and laboratory parameters was superior to the grading of MR severity by echocardiogram, which demonstrated better discrimination for outcomes after surgery for paediatric MR.

Implications of all the available evidence

The novel scoring system is useful in optimising risk stratification, supporting shared decision-making process, and tailoring counselling in the paediatric patients with MR. Further evidence from large prospective studies or registries is needed to confirm our findings.

Introduction

Recurrent significant mitral incompetence after surgical repair of paediatric mitral regurgitation (MR) is not negligible, with approximately 50% of the patients requiring reoperations within 10 years, which accumulates to an underlying burden and participating in increased morbidity and cardiovascular risks.1,2 Identifying patients who are at high risk of developing recurrent MR is therefore crucial in tailoring management and may allow timely intervention to prevent irreversible cardiac remodelling.3 Congenital MR, either in isolation or combined with other congenital heart defects, is the most common aetiology of paediatric MR.4 Individualised risk estimation in this subpopulation is challenging due on the one hand to the wide range of pathologies affecting leaflets and subvalvar elements of the MV, and on the other hand to the weak stratification capabilities of MR severity graded by echocardiogram.5

Optimisation of risk assessment has been recommended in guidelines6,7 as a fundamental step in the clinical-decision process in the MR setting. However, data specifically focused on the paediatric patients is very scarce largely because of the disease rarity and lack of studies involving enough sample size to explore the relevant prognostic factors. As such, this subpopulation is underrepresented in the current guidelines. There have been few attempts to develop a specific risk score model for paediatric MR but the model is derived from a single-center study and does not differentiate between mitral stenosis and regurgitation.1 Moreover, it has not been validated in a separate and independent cohort, precluding the clinical applicability to a broader patient population.

We designed this study to try to overcome the aforementioned limitations. The primary aim of this study was to explore the predictors of mortality and MV re-operation, in a nationwide multicenter cohort of paediatric patients with congenital MR, and further quantify their impacts to propose a risk score to enhance stratification, whilst assessing whether posterior annuloplasty is an effective method to improve repair durability.

Methods

Study design

PaediatRIc Mitral vAlve Regurgitation registrY (PRIMARY) was initiated by Shanghai Children's Medical Center (SCMC) in March 2018. This is an ongoing prospective, multicenter, observational registry conducted in China, aiming to provide contemporary data on the clinical profiles and prognosis of patients (<18 years of age) with a diagnosis of MR who were referred for surgery.

Participant and setting

For the present study, outcomes were analysed for patients with congenital MR referred for surgery at 9 tertiary hospitals between January 2009 and December 2022. Prospective inclusion of patients has commenced since March 2018, and cases were retrospectively collected before that time point. We specifically exclude patients who had MR in combination with infective endocarditis, anomalous left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery (ALCAPA), rheumatic valvular disease, connective tissue disease, or single ventricle because these patients may develop adverse outcomes resulting from other comorbidities besides MR. For example, there is a likelihood that postoperative death in patients with ALCAPA may result from the severe myocardial infarction/ischemia.

This study adhered to the TRIPOD guideline8 wherein an external validation should use participant data collected by other investigators in other hospitals (geographically different from the included hospital in derivation cohort). Thus, the data from SCMC defined the derivation cohort in this analysis and data from the other 8 sites (Supplemental Table S1) was evaluated as an external validation cohort and not included in the process of risk score development. Ethical approval from the local ethics committees were obtained from all participating centres prior to joining the registry. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient's parents or caregivers. Ethics approval was obtained for this study from Shanghai Children's Medical Centre review board (SCMCIRB-K2021096-1).

Data collection and MR aetiology

Data were collected independently by each of the participating centres using their local electronic databases, including patient demographic information (age, sex, body surface area), preoperative N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), associated congenital heart defects (CHDs), surgical notes, perioperative information (rhythm disturbance, complete atrioventricular block, haemorrhage, low cardiac output, length of hospital stay), echocardiographic parameters at the index hospitalisation (preoperative and predischarge) as well as at the last follow-up. NT-proBNP was routinely tested in a structured way using Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany.9 SCMC served as the data coordinating center. To ensure the completeness and accuracy of the data, the department of clinical research in SCMC kept regular telephone contact with the investigators and performed centralised verification of the electronic case report forms. In addition, comprehensive data audits were performed in randomly selected sites.

Congenital MR was subcategorized into type-I: valvar (annular or leaflet abnormality), type-II: subvalvar (chordae tendineae or papillary muscle abnormality), and type-III: mixed aetiology (a combination of valvar, leaflet, and subvalvar abnormality) based on a combined evaluation of preoperative echocardiography and operative inspection.10 Specifically, atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD) was considered as type-I MR considering the deformed left component of the atrioventricular valve as well as the leaflet abnormality (e.g., cleft and bridging leaflet).

Echocardiographic measurements

All of the echocardiographic imaging was transmitted to the core laboratory of SCMC, and were reanalysed by a single expert investigator (W.L.P) who was blinded to the clinical outcomes.

Severity of MR was graded as mild, moderate, and severe using an integrative approach including quantitative, semi-quantitative, and qualitative measurements.11 Baseline evaluation of MR included quantitative (effective regurgitant orifice area and regurgitant volume by proximal isovelocity surface area method) and semi-quantitative (vena contracta width). Left ventricular (LV) size and function were described by LV end-diastolic dimension (LVEDD; parasternal view), LV end-systolic dimension (LVESD; parasternal view), and LV ejection fraction (LVEF). Left atrial (LA) size was described by LA diameter (LAD; major-axis length from apical 4-chamer view at ventricular end-systole). Evaluation of the atrioventricular valves included lateral mitral and tricuspid valve diameter (apical 4-chamber view at cardiac diastole), mitral inflow velocity, and tricuspid regurgitation jet peak velocity.

Outcomes and follow-up

The primary composite outcome was mortality or mitral valve (MV) re-operation. We separately examined the outcomes of death, MV re-operation (including re-repair and MV replacement [MVR]), and subsequent MVR after failure of the index repair. Follow-up started on the date of the initial operation and ended at the time of primary endpoints or January, 2024, whichever occurred first. Data from those who lost follow-up were censored at their last follow-up visit.

Statistics

R version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) was used for statistical analysis and figures construction. All tests were two-tailed, and significant level was set as P < 0.05.

Categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages) and were compared between groups using the χ2 test. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed data were presented as mean (standard deviation, SD) and compared using the student's t test while non-normally distributed data were presented as median and interquartile range (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum test. All echocardiographic 2D diameters of cardiac structures were transformed into z score adjusted by body-surface area relative to the normal population, and summarised as mean (SD).12 z score of +2 or −2 represents a measurement value that is two SDs above or below the mean value, usually recognised as the thresholds for normal in a paediatric population.13 The Harrell's c index and time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) were used to assess the predictive performance of the model. A Harrell's c index of >0.7 was considered acceptable for a sufficient model fit. The calibration of the model was evaluated by the calibration curve14 at different time points (postoperative 1, 5, and 10 years) and Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. The primary outcome of MV reoperation or death was plotted on Kaplan–Meier curves and between-group comparisons were made using the log-rank test. Considering that death has a competing risk with MR re-operation (including replacement), Nelson–Aalon cumulative risk curve analyses were used to estimate incidence of MR re-operation as well as mitral valve replacement, and between-group comparisons were made using Gray's test. Short-term prediction of the primary outcome was made within a prespecified time window at the landmark time point (postoperative 3 months).15 A stratified bootstrapping method16 with 1000 iterations was used to partially address the issue regarding heterogeneity in hospital sizes among the participating centres. A combination of the derivation and validation cohort was used to further assess the discrimination and calibration of the final risk score model; meanwhile, we evaluated the incidence of primary outcomes in the entire cohort of patients stratified by the presence of AVSD and age <1 year vs. ≥1 year. Missing data of NT-pro-BNP was found in 2.7% of the patients in earlier era and multiple imputation was used.

Model derivation

Univariable Cox regression analysis was used to identify the predictors of mortality or reoperation. The candidate variables included in the univariable analysis were derived from a review of the literatures17, 18, 19 or regarded as clinically relevant. Variable with P < 0.1 in univariable analysis were included in the multivariable analysis using Cox backward stepwise multiple modelling. All hazard ratios (HRs) for the continuous variables are for 1 unit increase in the predictor. Thus, a model (model 1) was generated using variables with multivariable P < 0.05. In addition, the other two models were developed by including all candidate predictors (model 2, full model) and the individual variables whose c-statistics were >0.6 (model 3). We then made comparisons among the 3 models and chose the final model.

Risk score

To further support clinical use of the final model, we created a simple, easy-to-use, integer risk score using the method described by Sullivan and colleagues.20 Detailed process was shown in the Supplemental Method Material. Briefly, continuous variables were converted into category variables wherein several categories were used to create ordinal variables for the continuous linear predictors while restricted cubic spline with five knots at the 5th, 27.5th, 50th, 72.5th, and 95th percentiles was used to determine the optimal categorisation thresholds in continuous non-linear predictors. Thus, for each predictor, a point of 0 was assigned to the reference category and the regression coefficients of other categories were then scaled and rounded to the nearest integer. Subsequently, a risk score for each patient can be calculated by summing the risk points of all predictors. Thereafter, patients were stratified into quartiles on the basis of their risk scores, and risk categories were then defined as low-risk (quartile 1), intermediate-risk (quartile 2–3), and high-risk groups (quartile 4).21 Moreover, to optimise the scoring system, we compared the risk score derived from data-driven cut points versus clinically meaningful cut points.

Results

Demographics and procedural results

During 2009–22, a total of 2640 patients (57% female) with a median age at surgery of 0.99 (IQR, 0.47–2.60) years were included (Fig. 1), among whom MR was graded moderate in 55.4% (n = 1463) and severe in 20.6% (n = 545) (Table 1). Clinically, 613 patients presented with feeding difficulties (23.2%), 961 failure to thrive (36.4%), and 637 with dyspnoea (24.1%), while thirty-five percent of the patients (n = 927) were asymptomatic at the initial surgery. Approximately, 90% of the patients with MR had coexisting cardiac lesions wherein isolated ventricular septal defect (n = 920; 34.8%) was the most prevalent, followed by AVSD (n = 610; 23.1%), isolated atrial septal defect (n = 590; 22.3%), and patent ductus arteriosus (n = 83; 3.1%). Of the 632 patients with mild MR, 449 (71%) had a diagnosis of AVSD. The presence of AVSD had a strong correlation with preoperative LA z score (r = 0.14, P < 0.001) and increased preoperative NT-proBNP (r = 0.75, P < 0.001). Of the 1358 patients, whose age was less than 1 year, 1186 had secondary MR and the majority (76.9%; 912/1186) had a combination with VSD, ASD, and/or PDA. Distribution of MR severity was similar between primary (moderate in 56.8%; severe in 21.6%) and non-primary MR (moderate in 53.9%; severe in 18.9%), while patients with primary MR tended to undergo the index MV surgery at older age (median age of 2.74 [IQR, 1.48–5.36] years vs. 0.97 [IQR, 0.45–2.50] years; P < 0.0001), had higher level of preoperative NT-proBNP (2.83 [IQR, 0.99–8.56] vs. 1.40 [IQR, 0.35–4.04] ng/mL; P = 0.018), and had longer cardiac care unit duration (6.0 [IQR, 4.0–8.3] vs. 3.0 [IQR, 2.0–6.0] days; P < 0.0001). The most prevalent MR aetiology was type-I lesion (Supplemental Figure S1). On average, the patients had a median LVEF of 63.6% (IQR, 60.5–67.7%; most were >50%) with a wide range of left heart enlargement (mean LVEDD z score 3.98, LVESD z score 2.94, and LA z score 2.56) preoperatively, and 1687 patients (63.9%) had mitral annulus dilation. Congestion-related biomarker, serum NT-proBNP concentration, was elevated in 2106 patients (79.8%), defined as the level >0.45 ng/ml.

Majority of the patients (71.8%) underwent MV repair at the index operation with a wide technique spectrum including posterior annuloplasty in 1417 (74.8%), cleft closure in 532 (28.0%), leaflet augmentation/resection/plication in 558 (29.5%), chordal or papillary muscle shortening/splitting/mobilization in 479 (25.2%), and neochordae in 215 (11.4%). Exploration of the predictors for the primary outcome

There were 1581 patients (median age of 1.03 [IQR, 0.47–2.67] years) in the derivation cohort; death occurred in 97 cases (6.1%) and MV re-operation occurred in 198 (12.5%) during a median of 5.7 (IQR, 3.9–8.2) years. Exploratory univariable and multivariable Cox regression analysis for the primary endpoint (Table 2) yielded 8 potential covariates (P < 0.05) of 17 tested variables including clinical, echocardiographic, laboratory, and procedural covariates. We then developed three multivariable cox regression models (Table 3). Model 1 was generated with the 8 variables independently associated with the primary outcome. Model 2 was the full model including all 17 candidate variables. Model 3 comprised 5 predictive variables (Harrell's c index > 0.6). Among the three models, model 1 showed better discrimination than model 2 (Harrell's c index: 0.93, 95% CI, 0.91–0.94 vs. 0.92, 95% CI, 0.90–0.93; P = 0.0042) and model 3 (Harrell's c index: 0.93, 95% CI, 0.91–0.94 vs. 0.80, 95% CI, 0.78–0.83; P < 0.001). The eight variables in model 1 included six continuous variables, among which preoperative LA z score had a non-linear relationship with the outcomes while the other 5 (age at operation, preoperative NT-proBNP and LVEF, perioperative changes in z scores of the LVEDD [ΔLVEDD z score], and perioperative changes in z scores of the [ΔLA z score]) had linear relationship with the outcomes (Supplemental Figure S2).

Optimisation and assessment of the risk score

The eight variables in model 1 were used to generate the risk score (Table 4). Considering that clinically meaningful cutpoints of NT-proBNP (>0.45 ng/ml) and LVEF (<50%) have been well established in the current guidelines,6,7 we subsequently developed two risk scores derived from data-driven cut points versus clinically meaningful cutpoints. The risk score derived from data-driven cut points showed a satisfactory prognostic value in predicting the primary outcome during the follow-up period (Fig. 2), outperforming that derived from the clinically meaningful cut points (Harrell's c index: 0.85 [95% CI, 0.83–0.87] vs. 0.74 [95% CI, 0.70–0.78]; Supplemental Figure S3). Calibration curves demonstrated adequate agreement between actual and predicted probabilities of primary outcomes at postoperative 1, 5, and 10 years (Supplemental Figure S4). Based on the distribution of patients' risk score, the scoring system was categorised into three groups: low-risk group (−22 to −5 points), intermediate-risk group (−4 to 0 points), and high-risk group (>0 points). There were significant between-group differences in the primary outcome when stratified by different risk levels (low, intermediate, high; Supplemental Figure S5).

External validation

An external validation was performed in an independent cohort of 1059 patients (median age of 0.93 [IQR, 0.46–2.51] years), wherein 65 (6.1%) death and 109 (10.3%) MV re-operation were recorded during a median follow-up of 7.1 (IQR, 3.7–7.7) years. In the validation cohort, the data-driven risk score also demonstrated good discrimination for the primary outcome (Harrell's c index: 0.86, 95% CI, 0.83–0.89; Supplemental Figure S6). Also, the Kaplan–Meier curve showed significant differences among the low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups (Supplemental Figure S7). The calibration curve (Supplemental Figure S8) demonstrated a satisfactory alignment between the risk score and the observed risk.

Comparison between derivation and validation cohort

The derivation and validation cohort were consisted of 1581 and 1059 patients, respectively. The distribution of demographic and procedural characteristics was similar between the 2 cohorts except for larger preoperative LVEDD (P < 0.0001), and a higher prevalence of primary MR (P = 0.0051) in the validation cohort (Table 1). There was no difference in the primary outcomes in the patients with primary MR between the derivation and validation cohort (Supplemental Figure S9).

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

We combined derivation and validation cohorts to further increase the sample size in order to facilitate additional analyses. In the entire cohort, a total of 392 patients (death: 162; MV reoperation: 307) had primary outcome, among whom 77 patients died after MV re-operation. Time-to-event primary outcome as well as each secondary outcome was plotted in Kaplan–Meier or Nelson–Aalon curve as appropriate (Supplemental Figure S10). The scoring system had superiority in predicting death or MV reoperation compared to MR severity (Supplemental Figure S11). Landmark analysis showed no difference in the primary outcome within 3 months post index operation among the low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups (Supplemental Figure S12). There was no difference in the primary outcomes in patients stratified by the presence of AVSD or not and age < 1 year vs. ≥ 1 year (Supplemental Figure S13).

Our scoring system also showed good discrimination for secondary outcomes of death, MV re-operation, and MVR after failure of the index repair (Supplemental Figure S14). Subgroup analysis was performed according to the adopted repair techniques, AVSD vs. non-AVSD, and primary vs. non-primary MR, and the risk score continued to exhibit high predictive performance within each subgroup (Supplemental Figures S15–S17).

Discussion

To our knowledge, PRIMARY is the largest multicenter study of paediatric patients receiving MR surgery. The present work documents follow-up outcomes up to 14 years and is a necessary first step towards developing a paediatric-specific score system to predict adverse outcomes. Our findings highlight that reoperation for recurrent significant MR is not uncommon and increases with time. We demonstrate that trough integration of clinical, echocardiographic, and laboratory features, it is possible to precisely provide prognostic information regarding MV reoperations and mortality, which may be useful for optimisation of individualised risk evaluation.

One strength of this study is that we first developed and externally validated an integrated risk score to predicate MR re-operation or mortality. Despite that recent guidelines for MR in general recommend optimising risk assessments for improved prognostication, there has been no attempt at deriving and validating a risk score in the paediatric setting probably because the availability of such a prediction score warrants large cohorts of patients with comprehensive clinical data. The present work overcomes this limitation and relies on multi-institutional collaboration to obtain separate cohorts for the derivation and validation process. Moreover, development of this data-driven scoring model corresponds to the congenital heart community that underscores the priority to find suitable criteria for paediatric MR assessment because some clinically meaningful cut points described in the guidelines are largely derived from the adult MR population and whether they are suitable for the paediatric patients remains unclear.5 However, we should clarify that this scoring model is a useful method in stratifying patients at risk of the primary outcomes during a relatively late follow-up period (<1 year postoperatively) but not at very early postoperative period (<3 months postoperatively).

Knowledge gaps remain in predicting occurrence of adverse complications after MR intervention as cardiac remodelling response to volume overload can vary significantly among individuals, which has an impact on outcomes and can be further complicated by patient age, mechanism of MR, and associated comorbidities.1,22,23 This risk score model, which integrated 8 clinical, echocardiographic, and laboratory parameters, allowed for an improved discrimination and calibration, outperforming MR severity based on echocardiography. Our scoring system helps to describe complex disease patterns with simple explanations and appears to be a useful mean to transfer and interpret clinical data, which has implication for providing information to surgeons, cardiologists, and patients regarding personalised risk prediction and tailoring management, and facilitating the shared decision-making process which has been increasingly regarded as a critical component of high-quality and value-based care.24

Plausible speculations for the significant difference in the primary outcome between the derivation and validation cohort warrants discussion. First, only one hospital constituted the derivation cohort while other 8 hospitals constituted the validation hospital. And the patient number of each hospital in the validation cohort was far less than that of the hospital in the derivation cohort. Hence, there may be a case-by-case bias because paediatric MR surgery is complicated and requires individualised strategy. Second, there may be variation in management protocols and surgical techniques in different hospitals. Third, it should be clarified that the significant differences in baseline characteristics (preoperative LVEDD and primary MR) between the 2 cohorts might less contribute to the outcome difference because (1) preoperative LVEDD was not identified as a risk factor, and this may be attributed to the fact that reverse LV remodelling could occur after operation25; (2) a comparative analysis in patients with primary MR between the two cohorts revealed no difference in the mortality or MV reoperation. On the other hand, we acknowledge that such discrepancy in outcome between the derivation and validation cohort may result in an overestimation of the predictive performance of the scoring model.

In general opinion, patients with AVSD are known to have an increased risk of reoperation for left atrioventricular valve and this rate probably increase with time.26, 27, 28 Although AVSD is not identified as a risk factor for the primary outcome, we still cannot rule out the possibility that presence of AVSD potentially contribute to the adverse outcomes. A plausible explanation for the failure to identify the AVSD phenotype as a risk factor may be that it could have collinearity with other variables (e.g., LA size, NT-proBNP). Age of 0–2 year was considered to be the highest risk age category, which helps to support that notion that younger age is associated with poorer outcomes. Interestingly, the failure to identify age of <1 year as a risk factor may be due to the fact that there were a considerable number of patients with age <1 year had secondary MR in combination with VSD, ASD, and/or PDA wherein better prognosis can usually be expected.

Some may argue the appropriateness of surgery in this series because nearly one-third of the patients were asymptomatic at the initial surgery and twenty-four percent of the patients had only mild MR. Several clarifications should be made. First, this study trended towards younger patients. Notably, these patients may not accurately report the clinical symptoms by themselves. Instead, the information was materially obtained from their parents/caregivers. Thus, there may be a risk of reporting bias due to parental interpretation, especially when the symptoms were based on subjective judgements (e.g., feeding difficulties, growth restriction). Therefore, we did not merely rely on the symptoms for decision-making. Second, a majority of the patients with mild MR had AVSD wherein valvuloplasty (e.g., cleft closure) was routinely performed. The remaining patient with mild MR had elevated NT-proBNP level, enlarged left heart which may suggest impaired cardiac function and cardiac remodelling. The surgeries were performed after a thorough communication with their parents. Notably, no significant difference in the primary outcomes when stratified by MR degree in this study also partially supports the notion that a combination of clinical symptoms, echocardiographic parameters, and blood biomarker should be taken into account for decision-making.

Despite that bootstrap method was used to mitigate the issue of heterogeneity in terms of the sample size between the derivation and validation cohort, such discrepancy still potentially influenced the robustness of the risk score model. Further studies are definitively required to assess the generalisability of this model. On the other hand, such heterogeneity in sample size may also reflect a real-world situation. For example, paediatric MR is a rare and complex congenital heart disease that usually requiring individualised treatment.29,30 Plausibly, there is a high likelihood that regionalisation of the treatment care may occur in this clinical setting and these patients were more willing to receive treatment in high-volume centres.

This risk score model also allows for prognostication of MVR after failure of valve repair. As a general principle, valve surgery in paediatric MR focuses on the possibility of repair before eventually committing to replacement given the disadvantages of prosthetic valves, i.e., obligatory lifelong anticoagulation, need for subsequent repeated replacements, and increased mortality risk (10%–36%).30,31 In this regard, our findings have implications in two aspects: first, the heart team should comprehensively take LVEF, preoperative LA diameter, and NT-proBNP into account to ensure an optimal timing of surgery to decrease the incidence of subsequent MVR. Secondly, structured surveillance should be warranted in patients who are at high risk of repair failure and require inevitable valve replacement.

The present study supports that posterior suture annuloplasty should be included as an essential component to the repair strategy across all risk categories, which appears to increase the repair durability. Plausible advantages of annuloplasty include (1) stabilisation of the annulus and restoration of its physiological systolic relationships and (2) optimising annular dimension/shape to facilitate effective coaptation and decrease the stress on the leaflets.32,33 Nevertheless, we cannot exclude the possibility that those who did not receive annuloplasty may have intrinsic unfavourable anatomic features and at high risk of repair failure.

Our findings should be interpreted with caution. First, although parameters derived from standard two-dimensional echocardiography can be easy to obtain and appears to be useful, novel imaging metrics reflecting cardiac remodelling derived from speckle tracking echocardiography or cardiac magnetic resonance may offer better prognostic as well as diagnostic value in MR setting.28 Second, the data in this study originates from tertiary centres where patients may be referred for MV surgery following local, site-specific policies, introducing selection bias. Prior to prospective validation, a broader validation in diverse patient demographics is needed to assess the performance of our models, and is crucial to mitigate the risk of systemic biases. Third, although important variables were included in the model, it may not be applicable to paediatric patients with MR of other etiologies (i.e., rheumatic heart disease, endocarditis, connective tissue disorders) because calibration and precision could vary by phenotype. Fourth, surgeon level of expertise in MV surgery varies among different institutions and may influence the results. Lastly, discrepancy in sample size between the derivation and validation cohort may be due to the selection bias. In addition, other residual and unknown confounding will always be a threat to observational studies, precluding drawing causal conclusions.

The novel scoring system appears to be a useful tool in facilitating the identification of paediatric patients with MR at high risk for mortality and need for reoperation. Seemingly, posterior annuloplasty is an integral component in the optimal therapy to improve repair durability.
