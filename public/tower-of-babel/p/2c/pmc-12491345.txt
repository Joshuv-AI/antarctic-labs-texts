Optimizing pediatric loop colostomy closure: a comparative study of linear stapler and hand-sewn anastomosis
Authors: Raashid Hamid, Ubayer Nabi, Akshat Sudhanshu, Vivek Sharma, Sabina Nisar, Nisar A Bhat, Ajaz A Baba, Gowhar N Mufti, Waseem Jan Shah, Mir Faheem ul Hassan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConventional hand suture techniques for intestinal anastomosis have been standard practice for decades, but the development of staplers has significantly impacted surgical procedures. Staplers, designed to simplify surgery, have been increasingly used in various gastrointestinal surgeries, including pediatric procedures. Colostomy takedown in infants is a commonly performed surgical procedure in pediatric surgery, yet the optimal technique remains debated. This study aims to compare the outcomes of linear stapler anastomosis and hand-sewn anastomosis for elective closure of loop colostomies in children. The hypothesis is that linear stapler anastomosis offers advantages over hand-sewn anastomosis in terms of operative time, recovery, and hospital stay.AimTo compare the outcomes of linear stapler and hand-sewn anastomosis in the elective closure of pediatric loop colostomies.MethodsThis prospective, randomized controlled study was conducted at Sher-i-Kashmir Institute of Medical Sciences (SKIMS), Soura, J&K, India, between 2021 and 2023. A total of 70 infants were enrolled, randomly assigned to two groups: Group A (35 infants) underwent SA (Stapled Anastomosis), and Group B (35 infants) underwent HS (Hand-Sewn Anastomosis). Both groups were matched for clinical characteristics. The primary outcomes included operative time and time to return of bowel movements. Secondary outcomes included anastomotic leaks, wound infections, and length of hospital stay. Data analysis was performed using appropriate statistical methods including t-tests and chi-square tests.ResultsThe mean age of patients was 5.79 ± 3.23 months in Group A and 4.21 ± 3.13 months in Group B. The mean time to return of bowel movements was significantly shorter in Group A (24.82 ± 6.34 h) compared to Group B (47.56 ± 5.65 h, p = 0.05). Oral feeding was commenced earlier in Group A (2.18 ± 0.39 days) than in Group B (3.16 ± 0.37 days, p < 0.001). Both groups had a follow-up of 1.81 ± 0.98 years, with no cases of anastomotic leakage or small bowel obstruction in either group. Hospital stay was shorter in Group A (53.82 ± 6.34 h) compared to Group B (79.56 ± 15.65 h, p < 0.0003).Keywords: Pediatric surgery, Loop colostomy, Stapled anastomosis, Hand-sewn anastomosis, Gastrointestinal anastomosis, Operative time, Postoperative recovery, Bowel movements, Hospital stay, Anastomotic leaks, Surgical outcomes, Pediatric colostomy, Infant surgery, Surgical techniques, Colorectal surgery

Introduction

Before the nineteenth century, intestinal surgeries were mostly limited to creating a stoma or closing simple lacerations. In 1826, Lembert introduced the seromuscular suture technique, and Senn later recommended a two-layer closure method. Kocher's approach involved a two-layer anastomosis with catgut and silk sutures [1]. Temporary colostomy is used to manage congenital anorectal anomalies, distal obstructions like Hirschsprung’s disease, injuries, severe inflammation, or to protect a distal anastomosis [2].

Since Steichen and Ravitch introduced stapling techniques, stapled intestinal anastomoses have become common in adults and older infants, but literature on their use in infants is limited. Talbert et al. first applied the linear stapler in pediatric patients during the Duhamel procedure. Despite its accuracy, use in neonates is limited by the small size of their intestines [3]. Today, reliable anastomosis is essential for restoring intestinal continuity after enterectomy or colectomy, with various techniques like end-to-end, side-to-side, or end-to-side approaches, ensuring the submucosal layer is included for proper healing [4].

Linear cutter GI staplers simultaneously cut and staple the bowel, eliminating the need for clamping. Although staplers are more expensive, the cost is justified by the significant reduction in intraoperative time [5]. Stapled GI anastomosis in adults is well-documented for its safety and efficacy, minimizing tissue trauma, reducing complications, and promoting faster recovery [6]. A potential drawback of stapled GI anastomosis is the cost and the size mismatch between infant bowel lumens and the instrument. Since Steichen and Ravitch introduced stapling techniques, stapled intestinal anastomoses have become widely used across all age groups [7].

However, despite being considered the conventional standard for decades, the hand-sewn technique has not been subjected to rigorous comparative evaluation in the pediatric age group. There remains a paucity of high-quality evidence regarding its short-term and long-term outcomes, particularly in contrast to stapled techniques. Most published studies have focused on adult or mixed-age cohorts, leaving a significant gap in pediatric-specific surgical literature. This study compares the outcomes of hand-sewn versus stapled anastomosis in elective loop transverse colostomy closure in children.

Methods

Patients

This prospective, randomized case–control study was conducted at the Sher-i-Kashmir Institute of Medical Sciences (SKIMS), a tertiary institution located in North India, from 2021 to December 2023. A power analysis was performed to determine the appropriate sample size. With a significance level of 0.05 and 80% power, a sample size of 70 was deemed sufficient to minimize the risk of type II error and ensure reliable results.

The study included infants under the age of 1 year who underwent colostomy closure due to anorectal malformation. The study excluded patients who underwent esophageal anastomosis, laparoscopic procedures, surgeries for small bowel atresias, stomas created after bowel perforation, and surgeries performed outside the institution. The exclusion criteria aimed to ensure a homogenous study population for evaluating the impact of the two surgical techniques being compared. The primary endpoint used for the sample size calculation was “time to return of bowel movements,” a clinically meaningful measure of postoperative gastrointestinal recovery. Based on previous institutional data and literature values, we anticipated a mean difference of approximately 20 h between the two groups, with a standard deviation of 25 h. Using these estimates, and aiming for a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a power (1–β) of 80%, the calculated total sample size was 70 infants, i.e., 35 patients in each group (Group A: Stapled Anastomosis, Group B: Hand-Sewn Anastomosis).

Ethical aspects

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) at SKIMS, under Order No. SIMS/IEC-SKIMS/2023–317. All study participants'parents or guardians provided informed consent prior to enrollment. This consent was obtained in accordance with institutional and national ethical standards to ensure patient privacy, safety, and the integrity of the research process. The study adhered to all relevant ethical guidelines in handling patient data and performing the surgical procedures.

Study design

The study enrolled 70 patients, who were randomly assigned into two groups:

Group A: 35 patients who underwent linear stapler anastomosis.

Group A: 35 patients who underwent linear stapler anastomosis.

Group B: 35 patients who underwent hand-sewn anastomosis.

Group B: 35 patients who underwent hand-sewn anastomosis.

The main focus of the study was on infants with loop transverse colostomy. Upon admission, clinical details, medical history, diagnostic investigations, and the planned surgical procedures were documented after obtaining informed consent. Broad-spectrum antibiotics were administered based on the patient's weight.

Preoperative investigations were performed to ensure that patients were fit for surgery, and surgeries were conducted by consultant surgeons to maintain consistency across procedures. In Group B, hand-sewn anastomosis was performed using delayed absorbable sutures in an end-to-end, interrupted single-layer fashion. In Group A, side-to-side GI anastomosis was performed with a 55 mm or 60 mm linear cutting stapler, which offered a side-to-side anatomical configuration but functionally resembled an end-to-end anastomosis.

A total of 188 infants were initially assessed for eligibility, and after applying the exclusion criteria, 70 infants were included in the study. These infants were randomly allocated to two groups: 35 in Group A (Linear Stapler Anastomosis) and 35 in Group B (Hand-Sewn Anastomosis). The study utilized computer-generated random allocation to ensure unbiased group assignment. Notably, there were no losses to follow-up, and both groups underwent full analysis, ensuring a robust comparison of the two surgical techniques. (Fig. 1).

To minimize bias and control for confounding factors, propensity score matching was applied to balance key variables such as age, sex, birth weight, and initial treatment approach between the two groups. Additionally, stratification by age groups (0–12 months, 13–24 months, and 25–47 months) was conducted to adjust for potential age-related confounders.

Surgical technique, linear stapler description and postoperative management

The patient is positioned supine under general anesthesia, with a sterile field and standard monitoring applied. A small abdominal incision is made to access the colostomy site, and the colostomy is carefully mobilized with the edges of the colonic stoma identified. Adequate exposure is achieved by dissecting the surrounding tissues to clearly visualize the bowel edges. The colonic edges are trimmed to ensure fresh, healthy tissue for anastomosis, and stay sutures are placed to hold the bowel in position and facilitate stapler placement. A 55 mm or 60 mm linear cutting stapler is prepared and inserted to create a side-to-side anastomosis by positioning the bowel edges between the stapler blades. A waiting time of one minute is allowed to compress and thin the bowel tissues, reduce edema, and ensure optimal staple approximation. The stapler is then fired to create the anastomosis while simultaneously dividing the bowel tissue. A second stapler cartridge is used to close the common enterotomy created by the initial stapling. The final functional anastomosis length is approximately 4–4.5 cm, as 2–2.5 cm of bowel tissue is resected and divided during the second stapler firing. The anastomosis is inspected to confirm there are no signs of tension, leakage, or misfiring of staples, and any bleeding or irregularities are addressed before proceeding. This sequential stapling technique prioritizes precision, minimizes tissue handling, and reduces the risk of iatrogenic injury, ensuring a secure, functional anastomosis with a low risk of complications.

This sequential stapling technique, while differing from the conventional triangular orifice method, was selected to accommodate the smaller bowel caliber in infants and prioritize leak prevention. Postoperatively, patients were kept nil per os (NPO), and no nasogastric tubes were inserted. Vital parameters such as body temperature, heart rate, and respiratory rate were closely monitored, and patients received intravenous (IV) fluids tailored to their age and electrolyte requirements. Potassium supplementation was introduced on the third postoperative day. IV antibiotics, including ceftriaxone and metronidazole, were administered to prevent infection. The restoration of bowel function was determined by the passage of flatus and the return of bowel sounds, which were the primary indicators of gastrointestinal recovery. Once these signs were observed, we initiated oral feeding. Test feeding was initiated, followed by the gradual reintroduction of oral feeding. All patients were closely monitored for complications, and the study parameters, including the primary and secondary outcomes, were compared between the two groups. Infants were discharged after oral feeds were successfully established and bowel function normalized.

Follow-up was performed to assess functional recovery and detect any complications post-surgery. Parameters such as time to return of bowel sounds, time to initiation of oral feeding, complications (including anastomotic leaks and surgical site infections), and overall recovery were documented. All study participants were followed up regularly to monitor recovery and detect any complications. Infants were initially seen in the outpatient department (OPD) one week after surgery, where skin sutures were removed. Follow-up continued at week 4 and then monthly for the next 6 months. After the first 6 months, follow-up visits were spaced to quarterly intervals for the next 2 years. During these follow-ups, assessments included wound healing, functional recovery, and detection of long-term complications such as anastomotic leaks, strictures, or obstruction. This regular monitoring allowed for early intervention if any postoperative issues arose.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 22.0. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize continuous variables, which were expressed as means ± standard deviation, and categorical variables were expressed as percentages. Independent t-tests were used for comparisons of continuous variables, and chi-square tests were used for categorical variables. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A power analysis determined that a sample size of 70 patients, with 35 patients in each group, would provide 80% power to detect significant differences with a significance level of 0.05. Propensity score matching was used to adjust for key confounders such as age, sex, birth weight, and treatment approach. Additionally, age stratification was applied to account for age-related differences and improve the accuracy of the comparison between the two groups.

Results

The mean age of patients was 5.79 ± 3.23 months for the linear stapler group and 4.21 ± 3.13 months for the hand-sewn group, with no significant difference. The mean weight of infants was 6.72 ± 2.37 kg in Group A and 7.01 ± 1.88 kg in Group B. The male-to-female ratio was 1.3:1 in the stapled group and 1.2:1 in the hand-sewn group.

The duration of anastomosis was significantly lower in the stapled group (15.82 ± 0.91 min) versus the hand-sewn group (39.72 ± 9.39 min; p < 0.001). Overall operative time was also significantly shorter (35.71 ± 11.79 min vs. 59.89 ± 12.85 min). Mean time to return of bowel movements was 24.82 ± 6.34 h in Group A and 47.56 ± 5.65 h in Group B (p = 0.05). Oral feeding was commenced earlier in Group A (2.18 ± 0.39 days) compared to Group B (3.16 ± 0.37 days; p < 0.001).

The mean hospital stay was significantly shorter in Group A (53.82 ± 6.34 h) compared to Group B (79.56 ± 15.65 h; p < 0.0003). Cost was comparable (Group A: INR 3200 ± 10.25; Group B: INR 3000 ± 260), and was not analyzed as a primary outcome.

Anastomotic leakage occurred in 2.86% of Group A and 8.57% of Group B (not statistically significant). Wound infections were observed in 8.57% and 14.29% of Group A and B, respectively. In Group A, one patient required re-exploration and another recovered conservatively; in Group B, three patients underwent re-exploration for leaks. No patients in either group developed anastomotic stricture during follow-up. (Table 1 and Table 2).

Discussion

Surgical stapling devices, first introduced by Hultl and Hummer in Budapest in 1908, have gained widespread usage in the past three decades due to advancements in disposable stapling instruments [8]. Initially, hand-sewn techniques were preferred, with staplers reserved for cases where stapling was not feasible due to anatomical or technical limitations [1]. Today, the choice between stapled and hand-sewn anastomosis depends on surgical expertise, patient-specific factors, and available resources. A Cochrane review of seven trials reported fewer leaks with stapled anastomosis, with no significant difference in operative time, stricture formation, or wound infection [9]. Similarly, a meta-analysis of 13 trials found no difference in mortality or leakage rates between the techniques [10].

Our department previously used the hand-sewn method exclusively for colostomy closure. However, we recently adopted the stapled technique and observed favorable outcomes, such as early discharge and reduced operative time. In the current study, both groups were comparable in age and weight, consistent with data from Kozlov et al. [11], Hintz et al. [12], and Sato et al., who reported variations in patient demographics but supported the safety of stapling in younger children.

The linear stapler technique used here employed sequential stapling—first creating a side-to-side anastomosis, followed by closure of the common enterotomy with a second stapler. This method minimizes tissue manipulation and optimizes luminal patency, even in infants with small-caliber intestines. Functionally, it mimics an end-to-end anastomosis without requiring a large triangular lumen. A secure seromuscular seal is achieved, lowering the risk of leakage, especially in fragile bowel segments.

Our findings align with Sato et al. [13] and Kozlov et al. [11], who found stapled techniques to be safe and effective in pediatric patients. In our study, anastomotic time was significantly shorter in the stapled group (15.82 ± 0.91 min vs. 39.72 ± 9.39 min, p < 0.001), supporting reports by Hintz et al. [12], Sato et al. [13], and Kozlov et al. [11].

Oral feeding was initiated significantly earlier in the stapled group (2.18 ± 0.39 days vs. 3.16 ± 0.37 days, p < 0.001). This contrasts with Mitra et al. [6], who began nasogastric feeding on day 4.61 for the stapled group and 6.07 for the hand-sewn group. Kozlov et al. [11] reported similar time to full enteral feeds (6.7 days) in both groups, though their patient population and protocols differed. Similarly, Shrivastava et al. [14] noted faster feeding in stapled cases (3.18 ± 0.39 days vs. 4.16 ± 0.37 days, p ≤ 0.001).

Stapled anastomosis facilitates early bowel function recovery by reducing operative trauma and duration, leading to faster restoration of peristalsis. The technique allows uniform approximation with less inflammation and edema, promoting better healing and minimizing the risk of complications.

Anastomotic leaks and wound infections are key postoperative concerns. In our series, leak rates were lower in the stapled group (2.86% vs. 8.57%), although not statistically significant, aligning with previous literature [6, 15, 16]. No anastomotic strictures occurred in either group. Hintz et al. [12] observed 20 anastomotic complications, including higher stricture rates in the hand-sewn group (9 vs. 2 cases). Their findings also emphasized that stapled anastomosis reduced operative time, complications, and hospital stay.

A key concern with pediatric anastomoses is long-term growth of the bowel. Anastomotic segments in infants grow proportionately, maintaining function over time. Ordorica-Flores et al. reported no stenosis in follow-up up to 36 months, regardless of whether one or two suture layers were used. Similarly, data from pediatric intestinal transplant follow-ups show preserved bowel growth over 15 years. These observations are reinforced by Raghu VK et al. [16] and Ordorica-Flores et al. [17], affirming that stapled anastomoses in infancy do not hinder intestinal development.

Our study found no statistical difference in surgical site infections (SSIs), observed in 8.57% of Group A and 14.29% of Group B. These rates are comparable to institutional norms and existing literature. The observed reduction in postoperative complications with stapling, though not significant, supports the safety of the method. This study highlights significant advantages of the stapled technique over hand-sewn anastomosis, particularly in terms of the primary outcomes—namely, a marked reduction in anastomosis time and earlier return of bowel function. These outcomes are crucial in pediatric patients, where minimizing anesthesia exposure and promoting faster recovery are paramount. Secondary outcomes, including the time to initiation of oral feeds, hospital stay, and complication rates such as anastomotic leaks and wound infections, also favored the stapled group, though not all differences were statistically significant. Notably, no anastomotic strictures were observed in either group during follow-up, underscoring the safety and functional reliability of both methods. Together, these findings affirm the clinical utility of stapled anastomosis in infant colostomy closure and support its broader adoption in suitable pediatric surgical settings.

Despite promising outcomes, our study has limitations. It was conducted in a single tertiary center, possibly limiting generalizability. The sample size, while adequate for comparing primary outcomes, may not capture rare complications. The follow-up period, although over a year, may still be insufficient to detect late-onset sequelae like strictures or bowel dysfunction. Larger multicenter trials with longer follow-up are warranted to confirm these findings and support wider adoption of the stapled technique in pediatric colostomy closure.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that linear stapler anastomosis is superior to traditional hand-sewn techniques in pediatric loop colostomy closure. It significantly reduces operative time, accelerates gastrointestinal recovery, shortens hospital stay, and promotes earlier initiation of oral feeding, despite comparable overall procedural costs. The technique maintains an excellent safety profile, with fewer anastomotic leaks and no cases of stenosis or strictures noted during follow-up. Our findings advocate wider adoption of stapled anastomosis as a standard pediatric surgical practice, suggesting further training and cost-reduction measures to enhance its accessibility and implementation.
