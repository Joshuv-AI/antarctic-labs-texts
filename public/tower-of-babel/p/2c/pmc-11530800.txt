British Cardiovascular Society survey of the provision and structure of cardiology multidisciplinary meetings in England
Authors: B. Liu, R.A. Archbold, J.P. Greenwood, S. Ray, S.S. Hothi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Multidisciplinary meetings (MDMs) are central to clinical decision-making in many areas of cardiology. This study assessed current provision and structure of cardiology MDMs in England in comparison with national guidelines.British Cardiovascular Society (BCS) members were surveyed regarding frequency, core attendees, and organisational aspects of cardiology MDMs for myocardial revascularisation, endocarditis, heart failure, aortic valve, mitral and tricuspid valve MDMs, whether local, regional or outside of the region. Access to electrophysiology (EP), inherited cardiac conditions, and adult congenital heart disease (ACHD) MDMs was also assessed.Survey responses were received from 64 hospitals across England, of which 40 (62%) were secondary care centres and 24 (38%) were tertiary care centres. All units had access to revascularisation MDMs, although 6% of them (all in secondary care centres) lacked any surgical representation. Heart failure MDMs were available in 94% of centres, but 7% reported no attendance by a cardiologist with specialist interest in heart failure, and 23% reported no attendance by a device specialist. 61% of centres had access to dedicated endocarditis MDMs; however, 11% were not attended by a microbiologist or infectious disease physician, and 22% were not attended by a surgeon. Aortic valve MDMs were available in 69% of centres, while mitral and tricuspid valve MDMs were available in 56% of centres. One quarter of centres reported no access to EP, and one third of centres reported no access to ICC or ACHD MDMs.Substantial improvements in provision and structure of cardiology MDMs in England are needed in order to meet national guidance.

Introduction

In recent years, multidisciplinary meetings (MDMs) have become central to clinical decision-making in many areas of cardiology.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 In an MDM, the multidisciplinary heart team reviews clinical cases and aims to reach consensus regarding the best management advice to be offered to the relevant patients. In doing so, the full range of treatment options are considered so that the appropriate ones are made available to the patient, biased decision-making is reduced, and patient choice is facilitated. MDMs are of particular value when there is uncertain clinical diagnosis, indeterminate or conflicting diagnostic results, symptomatology discrepant from objective data, more than one possible management approach, high patient risk from invasive procedures, and the option for use of a new treatment strategy which has a limited evidence base and/or lies outside of contemporary clinical practice guidelines.

The central role of MDMs in cardiology care pathways was highlighted in the specialty's Getting It Right First Time (GIRFT) report.6 However, significant variation in access to regular, quorate meetings was also noted by the report's authors. GIRFT recommended that all patients should have network-level access to MDMs for heart failure (HF) and cardiac device implantation, myocardial revascularisation, aortic valve disease, mitral and tricuspid valve disease, endocarditis, and electrophysiology (EP), and that pathways are available for patients to access external MDMs in inherited cardiac conditions (ICC), adult congenital heart disease (ACHD), advanced heart failure, and low volume interventions, if these are not provided within the network. It is important that, in addition to the provision and staffing of an MDM, the meeting must be effective in its aims and outputs, have clear terms of reference to guide case listing, and be time-efficient. The roles of the chair and coordinator are vital in providing structure and flow, bearing in mind the combined cost of all professionals attending the meetings.

Implicit to effective functioning of MDMs are the following prerequisites: (1) a professional arena conducive to reasoned debate and decision-making, (2) a chair who facilitates discussion and allows all voices to be heard, (3) clinical expertise in relevant fields of cardiology and cardiac surgery, (4) expertise in other areas such as microbiology and/or care of older people medicine, depending upon the clinical issue, (5) involvement of allied healthcare professionals, which might include specialist nurses, pharmacists, psychologists, and/or physiologists, and (6) access to tertiary/quaternary care and low-volume interventions, irrespective of local service provision. Acknowledging these core elements for the underpinning of effective MDM delivery, both GIRFT and a multi-professional society working group chaired by the British Cardiovascular Society (BCS) have produced guidance regarding the structure and function of cardiology MDMs.6,7 In addition, to subspecialty areas which should be covered, these guidelines made specific recommendations regarding frequency, core attendees and organisational aspects of cardiology MDMs. In this study, we assessed the provision and structure of cardiology MDMs in England in comparison with this national guidance.

The survey was disseminated electronically to BCS members via the BCS newsletter email, including 3,000 of the society's ordinary, associated and affiliated members, between December 2022 and February 2023. A follow-up email was circulated in the final survey window month. Survey responses were analysed according to each user's cardiac centre, with responses from centres in England analysed. If multiple responders from the same centre provided incongruent responses to binary variables, then a ‘best case scenario’ approach was adopted. For example, if one responder stated that an MDM was accessible virtually while another responded unavailability, then the centre's MDM was labelled as possessing a virtual access option. Where multiple responders from the same centre provided different responses to a question with a discrete numerical value, the mean was calculated. Continuous variables were assessed for normality via a histogram. Skewed data variables are presented as median, interquartile range (IQR) and range. The Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparison of non-parametric data between two groups. Fisher's exact test was used for assessing differences between binary data within two groups. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v29.0 (IBM Corp., New York).

Results

Survey responses

Survey responses were received from 64 hospitals, of which 40 (62%) were secondary care centres and 24 (38%) were tertiary care centres. The 24 tertiary care units represent 83% of all cardiac surgical units in England. 48 of the 64 hospitals were percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) units, which represented 55% of all NHS PCI centres within England as per National Institute for Cardiovascular Outcomes Research (NICOR) data.8 The remaining 16 hospitals were from hospitals without a PCI service. Overall, responses were received from 51% of all hospitals within England that possessed a cardiology unit.9 79% of responders were consultants with the remainder associate specialists and specialty trainees. Responders identified their cardiology subspecialty as intervention (28%), imaging (26%), heart failure (17%), devices (11%), EP (6%), ACHD (2%), ICC (3%) and general cardiology (5%).

Revascularisation MDMs

All centres had access to revascularisation MDMs (Table 2), either locally (77%) and/or regionally (41%). Revascularisation MDMs had a median occurrence of once per week (weekly frequency IQR 1–2, range once monthly to daily), with 55% of centres having a dedicated single referral route for all patients with coronary artery disease, and 63% of centres having access to ad hoc same-day MDM discussions (Supplementary Table 1). 80% of MDMs were accessible virtually, 76% featured an MDM coordinator, and 70% featured a dedicated person to record outcomes and feedback to the referrer (Supplementary Fig. 1). Endocarditis MDMs

61% of centres (19 secondary, 20 tertiary units) had access to a dedicated endocarditis MDM, either locally (44%) and/or regionally (30%) (Supplementary Table 1). Tertiary cardiology units reported better access to endocarditis MDMs than secondary cardiology units (75% v 50%; P = 0.037; Table 2). Of the 19 secondary care units, 63% reported accessibility locally, with the remaining 37% accessing the MDM via a regional pathway.

In centres with access to dedicated endocarditis MDMs, the median frequency was once per week, 80% of centres offered virtual access, 67% featured an MDM coordinator, and 67% featured a dedicated person to record outcomes and feedback to the referrer (Supplementary Fig. 2). 11% of centres with a dedicated endocarditis MDM did not feature either a microbiologist or infectious disease specialist (while 47% attended >⅔ of the time, and 11% attended <⅓ of the time) (Fig. 1), 22% did not feature any surgeons (whilst 47% attended >⅔ of the time, and 19% attended <⅓ of the time), and 81% did not feature an anaesthetist/intensivist. Attendance frequency of these clinical personnel did not differ between secondary and tertiary units (Table 3).

Heart failure MDMs

94% of centres had access to a dedicated HF MDM, either locally (86%) and/or regionally (14%). In centres with HF MDMs, the median weekly frequency was once per week (IQR 0.84–1.0), with 75% of centres offering virtual access (Supplementary Table 1). Of the centres with HF MDMs, 95% featured a consultant cardiologist with a specialist interest in HF (of which 52% attended >⅔ of the time, and 14% attended <⅓ of the time) and 77% featured a consultant cardiologist with a specialist interest in implantable cardiac devices (of which 39% attended >⅔ of the time, and 25% attended <⅓ of the time) (Supplementary Fig. 3). 95% of MDMs were attended by hospital-based, and 79% by community-based, nurse specialists, although variably so (Supplementary Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 3). Only 19% of centres had specified access routes to advanced HF services for mechanical circulatory support or transplantation.

Aortic valve MDMs

69% of centres had access to a dedicated aortic valve MDM on either a local (41%) and/or regional (50%) basis. In centres with dedicated aortic valve MDMs, the median weekly frequency was once per week (IQR 0.67-1.00/week), with 80% offering a virtual access option (Supplementary Table 1). Within centres with dedicated aortic valve MDMs, survey responses identified variations in clinical personnel makeup. 86% of MDMs featured a dedicated TAVI coordinator, 95% featured a TAVI consultant operator (of which 71% attended >⅔ of the time, and 8% attended <⅓ of the time), 68% featured an anaesthetist, and all MDMs featured a surgeon (of which 74% attended >⅔ of the time, and 26% attended between ⅓ and ⅔ of the time), although the frequency of attendance was variable (Supplementary Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 3). 97% of MDMs benefited from a consultant cardiologist with either a specialist interest in valve disease or imaging (of which 74% attended >⅔ of the time, and 3% attended <⅓ of the time) (Fig. 1). Comparison of aortic valve MDMs between secondary and tertiary care units showed that tertiary care units held MDMs with higher frequency (Supplementary Table 2), but attendance by key clinical personnel occurred with similar frequency (Table 3).

Mitral and tricuspid valve MDMs

Mitral and/or tricuspid valve MDMs were available in 56% of centres, either through local (44%) and/or regional (30%) network provision. Virtual access was available in 94% of centres which held dedicated mitral and tricuspid valve MDMs. All MDMs featured a cardiac surgeon (of which 62% attended >⅔ of the time, and 15% attended <⅓ of the time), 97% featured an interventional cardiologist (of which 62% attended >⅔ of the time, and 15% attended <⅓ of the time), but 43% did not feature an anaesthetist or intensivist (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Access to electrophysiology, inherited cardiac conditions and adult congenital heart disease MDMs

Local access to EP MDMs was available in 56% of centres, and network access was available in 19% of centres; the remaining 25% of centres did not report any access to EP MDMs. ICC MDMs were available locally in 34% of centres, and through a regional network in 27% of centres; 36% of centres reported no access to ICC MDMs. For ACHD, 30% of centres had local access and 34% had network access to an MDM, while 36% of centres reported no access to ACHD MDMs.

Discussion

Multidisciplinary working is an essential part of contemporary clinical practice, which was quickly embedded by high-performing centres into their models of care. One of the most well-recognised examples of multidisciplinary working is the MDM. This forum is of particular value in many areas of cardiological practice, in which the advent of advanced cardiac imaging and new therapeutic technologies have contributed to increased subspecialisation and more complex clinical decision-making.

In this study of 64 cardiac units in England, we found that dedicated MDMs for myocardial revascularisation, endocarditis, HF, aortic valve disease, and mitral and tricuspid valve disease were available in the majority of units, whether at a local, regional or extra-regional level. Of these MDMs, provision was greatest for revascularisation, for which all units reported access, and least for mitral and tricuspid valve disease, for which there was a 56% access rate. One quarter of units reported no access to EP MDMs and more than one third of units reported that had no access to ICC and/or ACHD MDMs, even on a network basis. Access to MDMs varied by type of hospital, secondary units having poorer access than tertiary units to endocarditis, aortic valve, and mitral and tricuspid valve MDMs. Where provided, revascularisation, endocarditis, aortic valve, mitral and tricuspid MDMs took place more frequently at tertiary units than at secondary units. All types of MDM suffered from deficiencies in attendance by ‘core’ practitioners. Our approach to assess MDM quoracy was not binary, but instead explored the frequency of attendance of each core members in tertiles, to account for variable attendance, whatever the reasons might be, recognising potential impacts of on-call commitments, annual or professional leave, and competing clinical commitments.

Twenty percent of secondary units reported no surgical representation in their revascularisation MDMs more than half of the time. Sixty-one percent of centres had access to a dedicated endocarditis MDM, but 11% of these were not attended by a microbiologist or infectious disease physician, and 22% were not attended by a surgeon. Similarly, while the availability of HF MDMs was high, occurring in 94% of centres, 7% of them were not attended by a consultant cardiologist with a specialist interest in HF. These meetings cannot be considered to be quorate. Furthermore, 23% of HF MDMs were not attended by a cardiologist with a specialist interest in implantable cardiac devices and only in approximately one third of MDMs was there device specialist attendance more than two thirds of the time. Interestingly, attendance by a device consultant was no higher in tertiary units than in secondary units and regular attendance by community HF nurse specialists was less common in tertiary units than in secondary units. Tertiary units are often organisationally remote from community services; these findings highlight the requirement for clear links between tertiary units, secondary care and the community, where the uptitration of prognostically beneficial medical therapy generally takes place.

The GIRFT report concluded that the main reasons for variation in MDM provision were lack of availability of multidisciplinary team members and lack of access to appropriate technology. Guidance from a joint working group of the BCS, British Cardiovascular Intervention Society (BCIS), the Society for Cardiothoracic Surgery (SCTS), the Association for Cardiac Anaesthesia and Critical Care (ACTACC) and the British Heart Valve Society highlighted that not only are MDMs a conduit for collective decision-making, they also create an environment which fosters collective ownership of the arising management decisions and clinical outcomes.7 To achieve this, it is imperative that participation in MDMs is agreed during job planning so that there are no competing clinical or other commitments which prevent attendance by ‘core’ participants, and that attendance at the MDM is recorded and audited. Core participants should prioritise attendance even when they do not have a patient who is directly under their care to discuss. By default, the MDM provider should offer a virtual option in order to facilitate equal access to referring centres.6 We found that virtual access was available for approximately 80% of all MDMs. The highest rate (94%) was for mitral and tricuspid valve disease MDMs, probably reflecting its highly subspecialist nature and MDM provision through regional networks led by tertiary cardiac units.

In addition to attendance by core clinical members of the multidisciplinary team, the presence of a dedicated coordinator is crucial for the smooth running of the MDM. Considerable administrative effort is required in order to collate referrals and the data relevant to each case discussion. The data can take many forms, including blood test results, ECGs, non-invasive imaging, cardiac catheterisation data, GP records, hospital case notes, and letters from other specialties that have seen the patient. All relevant clinical data are required in order to support appropriate clinical decision making. Despite this, we found that a coordinator was by no means universal in cardiology MDMs. MDM outcomes should be documented in the medical records and distributed to the responsible teams. The presence of a respected chair is also required in order to facilitate orderly and efficient discussion.

Limitations

The data in this study were self-reported by cardiologists and, therefore, subject to reporting bias and lacked more diverse views from allied health professional such as specialist nurses. However, there was strong engagement from cardiology centres in England, with representation from both tertiary units and secondary hospitals. Our study focused on MDMs which should routinely involve the attendance of at least one cardiac surgeon; it did not include cardio-oncology, cardio-obstetric or aortovascular MDMs. Finally, the purpose of the study was to assess current provision of MDMs; the data do not indicate the effectiveness of the meetings or their impact on clinical outcomes.

This study has provided a unique, contemporary assessment of the provision and composition of cardiology MDMs in England, which we hope will stimulate critical appraisal of MDMs by local service providers. Where there is evidence of inadequate provision of MDMs or of non-quorate meetings, modifications to local practice will be needed. Improvements in MDM provision and their operation will require clinical leadership from within local departments, healthcare providers and regional and national networks, bearing in mind the complex logistic arrangements for the organisation of cross-specialty scheduling, and demand for adequate information technology systems and internet bandwidth to support effective virtual meetings. Assessments of cardiology service provision, whether led by the relevant department's own hospital, by commissioners or by the CQC, should include metrics regarding MDMs. As things stand, there is considerable room for improvements in the provision and structure of cardiology MDMs in England more than 1 year after the publication of the cardiology GIRFT report and the joint British society recommendations in this area.1,2

Conclusions

In this study of 64 hospitals in England, there was almost universal access to myocardial revascularisation and HF MDMs. Access to dedicated MDMs for endocarditis, valve disease, EP, ICC, and ACHD was less robust. Where MDMs did occur, they were often deficient in key attendees who are required for genuine multidisciplinary decision making.

Ethics and consent

As a survey of clinicians with no access to or inclusion of patient data, research ethical approval is not required. Similarly, as there was no access to patients or to patient data, patient consent was not required.
