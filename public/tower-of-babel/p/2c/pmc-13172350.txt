Inhibiting glucocorticoid receptors enhances adult spinal cord neural stem cell activity and improves outcomes in spinal cord injury
Authors: Xuefeng Zhang, Siyuan Zhou, Shengyu Tang, Xiaotong Hou, Yonghui Cai, Changlong Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The spinal cord injury (SCI) microenvironment undergoes significant biochemical changes that influence the behavior of endogenous neural stem/progenitor cells (NSPCs), including elevated glucocorticoid levels. Here, we investigate the effects of glucocorticoids on NSPCs in the adult mouse spinal cord. Adult spinal cord NSPCs express glucocorticoid receptors but not mineralocorticoid receptors. Glucocorticoid exposure significantly inhibits NSPC proliferation, neurosphere formation, and differentiation through glucocorticoid receptor activation. In a traumatic SCI mouse model, treatment with the glucocorticoid receptor inhibitor CORT125281 improves motor function and increases the number of NSPCs at the injury site. Flow cytometry and RNA sequencing analyses show that glucocorticoids induce NSPC G0/G1 cell-cycle arrest through activation of the p53 signaling pathway, accompanied by increased expression of the cell-cycle regulators p15, p18, and p27. These findings suggest that glucocorticoid elevation after SCI suppresses endogenous NSPC proliferation via glucocorticoid receptor activation, and targeting this pathway may represent a potential therapeutic strategy to enhance recovery after SCI.

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a complex and severe neurological condition, typically resulting from traumatic events such as traffic accidents and falls. This condition often leads to debilitating neurological deficits, including loss of sensation, bowel and bladder dysfunction, paraplegia, and tetraplegia1. Despite extensive research efforts, effective neurorestorative treatments for SCI remain elusive, with current interventions focusing primarily on symptomatic management and physical rehabilitation, offering limited functional recovery. Stem cell transplantation has been extensively explored as a potential therapeutic approach for SCI2,3. In addition to stem cell transplantation, there is growing evidence supporting the activation of endogenous neural stem/progenitor cells (NSPCs) as a promising strategy for SCI repair4,5. Adult endogenous spinal cord NSPCs, derived from the ependymal layer of the central canal, play a crucial role in promoting functional recovery post-SCI6. Various approaches have been explored to stimulate endogenous NSPCs, including transplanting biomaterials that replicate spinal cord mechanics and using functional biomaterials for sustained drug release, among others5. Regardless of whether exogenous stem cells or endogenous NSPCs are chosen as the repair strategy, both approaches must contend with the significant changes in the microenvironment following SCI.

The spinal cord’s microenvironment, composed of various cells, extracellular matrix (ECM), and signaling molecules, undergoes dramatic changes following injury, which can either promote or hinder the recovery process7. Understanding these microenvironmental changes after SCI is crucial for developing therapeutic strategies that can modulate the post-injury environment to promote regeneration and improve functional outcomes. Following SCI, a maladaptive sympathetic–neuroendocrine adrenal reflex rapidly elevates systemic glucocorticoid levels, even in the presence of suppressed ACTH, rather than through classical HPA-axis activation8. In rodent models, plasma glucocorticoid concentrations typically peak around 24 h after injury and then gradually decline, returning to baseline within approximately 3 days in some studies, while in others the elevation persists longer depending on injury severity and experimental conditions9–12. In patients with SCI, serum cortisol concentrations rise markedly compared with control levels, particularly within the first 96 h after injury (median 19 h, interquartile range 4.25–33.5 h)8, indicating a comparable acute stress response. Elevated glucocorticoid following SCI disrupts normal circadian hormone rhythms10, impairs tissue repair processes11, and may suppress immune function9,12. Together, these findings highlight that glucocorticoid elevation is a critical determinant of post-injury pathophysiology and recovery. Glucocorticoids have been shown to inhibit the proliferation of both mouse and rat embryonic NSPCs13,14. These studies suggest that glucocorticoids may also inhibit adult endogenous spinal cord NSPCs and impair SCI repair.

In present study, we investigated the impact of glucocorticoids on endogenous NSPCs in the adult mouse spinal cord. Our data showed that glucocorticoids elevation following SCI suppresses the proliferation of endogenous NSPCs via glucocorticoid receptor activation, and that the glucocorticoid receptor inhibitor CORT125281 enhanced motor function in a mouse model of traumatic SCI. Targeting glucocorticoid receptors with specific inhibitors may represent a novel therapeutic strategy to promote recovery after spinal cord injury.

Results

Glucocorticoids suppress the proliferation of adult mouse spinal cord NSPCs

To investigate the effect of glucocorticoids on the proliferation of spinal cord NSPCs, we employed a neurosphere assay. Since cortisol is the predominant glucocorticoid in humans and corticosterone in rodents, we assessed the effects of both. Both glucocorticoids significantly reduced neurosphere formation in a concentration-dependent manner after 7 days of treatment (Fig. 1A–D). To confirm this inhibitory effect, CCK-8 assays revealed that both cortisol and corticosterone decreased NSPC viability (Fig. 1E, F). Since 1 μM is a pathologically relevant concentration in both spinal cord injury (SCI) patients and rodent models8, this concentration was selected for subsequent experiments. Furthermore, EdU incorporation assays showed a significant reduction in EdU-positive NSPCs upon treatment with either 1 μM cortisol (Fig. 1G, I) or 1 μM corticosterone (Supplementary Fig. 1). To determine whether reduced proliferation after glucocorticoid treatment was associated with cytotoxicity, we performed Calcein-AM/Propidium Iodide (PI) staining after 7 days of 1 μM cortisol exposure. The results showed no increase in PI-positive (dead) cells, indicating that cortisol treatment did not induce cytotoxicity under these conditions (Fig. 1H, J). To determine whether glucocorticoid-induced growth suppression was associated with apoptosis at either early or prolonged stages of treatment, Annexin V–FITC/PI staining was performed. Glucocorticoid receptors mediate the inhibitory effect of glucocorticoids on NSPC proliferation

Glucocorticoid effects can be mediated through glucocorticoid or mineralocorticoid receptors. Previous studies have shown that embryonic neural stem cells express both receptors15. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that cultured adult spinal cord NSPCs express glucocorticoid receptors but do not show detectable mineralocorticoid receptor expression (Fig. 2A). To confirm the specificity of the mineralocorticoid receptor antibody, HeLa cells, which express mineralocorticoid receptors16, were used as a positive control (Fig. 2B). Adult endogenous spinal cord NSPCs originate from the ependymal layer of the central canal of the spinal cord6,17. To investigate whether the glucocorticoid receptor mediates the effects of glucocorticoids, we used the specific receptor blocker CORT12528118. Both the neurosphere assay and CCK-8 experiments demonstrated that pre-incubation with the glucocorticoid receptor-specific antagonist, CORT125281(500 nM) for 30 min, abolished the inhibitory effects of cortisol on NSPC proliferation (Fig. 2D–F). These results indicate that glucocorticoids inhibit NSPC proliferation through the glucocorticoid receptor. We next investigated the effects of glucocorticoids on NSPC differentiation.

Glucocorticoids impair the differentiation of adult spinal cord NSPCs

To assess the role of glucocorticoids in NSPC differentiation, cells were cultured in differentiation medium with or without 1 μM cortisol for 7 days. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that cortisol inhibited NSPC differentiation into glial cells, an effect that was partially reversed by the glucocorticoid receptor antagonist CORT125281 (Fig. 3A, B). Cortisol also suppressed neuronal differentiation, although CORT125281 did not affect this inhibition (Fig. 3C, D). Furthermore, glucocorticoids influenced the morphology of neurons. Cortisol not only reduced the number of neurites, an effect that could not be blocked by CORT125281, but also significantly decreased neurite length. This reduction in neurite length was partially reversed by CORT125281 (Fig. 3E–H). Moreover, CORT125281 treatment led to marked improvements in motor function in the traumatic SCI mouse model. Mice receiving a placebo exhibited initial recovery of hip joint movement during the first 2 weeks post-surgery, with occasional foot-planting by the third week, but little further improvement in motor function thereafter. In contrast, mice treated with CORT125281 exhibited faster early recovery of hindlimb function. By the third week, they could consistently stand on their feet and showed continuous improvement over the following month. By weeks 6–7, some toe function had returned, and by week 9, some mice were able to walk with stable body movement (Videos 1 and 2, Fig. 5A, B). The BMS19 was used to assess recovery after SCI. The BMS score of CORT125281-treated mice was significantly higher than that of the vehicle control mice (Fig. 5C). Additionally, in the grid-walking test, mice treated with the glucocorticoid receptor antagonist were able to place their hind paws correctly on the grid lines, exhibiting coordinated movement between forelimbs and hindlimbs. Glucocorticoids induce NSPC arrest in the G0/G1 phase via glucocorticoid receptor-mediated regulation of cell cycle-related genes

Therefore, we examined the effects of glucocorticoids on the activity of ERK and Akt in NSPCs at multiple time points, including 10, 30, and 60 min as well as 1, 4, and 7 days following 1 μM cortisol treatment. Hes1 and Hes5 genes play essential roles in the maintenance and proliferation of neural stem cells23–25. We also assessed the effect of cortisol on these genes. Real-time PCR results showed that 1 μM cortisol significantly decreased the mRNA expression of Hes5, while it did not alter the expression of Hes1 (Fig. 8C).

NG2 glia show limited glucocorticoid receptor expression during early stages after SCI

A previous study showed that NG2 glia proliferate after SCI and possess latent neurogenic potential that requires forced reprogramming with ectopic Sox2 expression26. We therefore examined whether NG2 glia express GR and might respond to glucocorticoid signaling. In sham controls, NG2⁺ cells were sparsely distributed, consistent with previous reports27, and approximately 10% of NG2⁺ cells expressed GR (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B). At 7 days post-injury (dpi), NG2⁺ cells increased markedly at the lesion border, accompanied by a modest increase in GR expression (Supplementary Fig. 3C). In addition, we examined whether glucocorticoid signaling regulates Sox2 expression in NSPCs and observed no detectable changes in Sox2 expression following glucocorticoid treatment (Supplementary Fig. 3D).

Discussion

Previous studies have shown that glucocorticoid levels significantly increase following SCI in both animal models and human patients8, but their effects on adult spinal cord NSPCs remains poorly understood. In this study, we demonstrate that glucocorticoids suppress the proliferation of adult spinal cord NSPCs through glucocorticoid receptors. Glucocorticoids induce NSPC arrest in the G0/G1 phase via glucocorticoid receptor-mediated regulation of cell cycle-related genes. Moreover, treatment with the glucocorticoid receptor inhibitor CORT125281 significantly enhanced motor function in a traumatic SCI mouse model. These results suggest that targeting glucocorticoid receptors with specific inhibitors may offer a novel therapeutic strategy to promote recovery after SCI.

Endogenous glucocorticoids bind to two receptors: the mineralocorticoid receptor, which has a high affinity, and the glucocorticoid receptor, which is activated at higher glucocorticoid levels28. Both receptors are expressed in embryonic NSPCs. Typically, glucocorticoids inhibit the proliferation of embryonic NSPCs via glucocorticoid receptors29. Anacker et al. reported that a low concentration of cortisol (100 nM) enhanced the proliferation of human hippocampal progenitor cells via the mineralocorticoid receptor, whereas a high concentration of cortisol (100 μM) reduced proliferation by activating glucocorticoid receptors30. In contrast, we found that adult mouse spinal cord NSPCs express only the glucocorticoid receptor, and glucocorticoids inhibit their proliferation in a dose-dependent manner via this receptor. A previous study reported that CORT125281 exhibits poor blood–brain barrier (BBB) penetration under physiological conditions31. However, accumulating evidence indicates that the blood–spinal cord barrier (BSCB) is more permeable than the BBB32. Moreover, SCI is known to disrupt the BSCB, markedly increasing its permeability to circulating compounds; this increase begins within minutes after injury and can persist for up to 28 days33–35. Such injury-induced leakage likely enabled CORT125281 to access the lesion site. Nevertheless, incomplete or heterogeneous barrier breakdown may have limited compound distribution in some regions, potentially contributing to the modest neural effects observed in vivo.

Previous studies have shown that melatonin promotes the proliferation of adult spinal cord NSPCs through the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway36. Similarly, Lu et al. reported that icariin sustains the proliferation of Aβ-treated hippocampal neural stem cells via the ERK/Akt signaling pathway37. However, we found that glucocorticoid treatment did not significantly alter ERK or Akt phosphorylation. This may reflect the transient and tightly regulated dynamics of ERK activation38 or feedback inhibition mediated by glucocorticoid-induced MAPK phosphatase-139. It is also possible that compensatory signaling mechanisms maintain basal ERK activity while proliferation is suppressed through alternative pathways such as p53-dependent cell-cycle arrest. RNA sequencing analysis further revealed that the p53 signaling pathway is involved in the glucocorticoid-mediated suppression of adult spinal cord NSPC proliferation.

p53 is known as a key regulator of cell growth and division, and its activation leads to G1 arrest40. Previous studies have demonstrated that glucocorticoid receptors and p53 interact both physically and functionally, with such interactions contributing to various physiological and pathological outcomes41,42. Dexamethasone has been shown to induce G0/G1 arrest and increase p21 expression in MC3T3-E1 cells through aberrant glucocorticoid receptor activation, leading to subsequent p53 activation43. In addition, dexamethasone induces G1-phase arrest and increases p27 expression in hippocampal progenitor cells44. Similarly, dexamethasone upregulates cell-cycle regulatory genes such as p16 and p21 in rat embryonic neural stem cells13. In this study, we observed that glucocorticoids induce G1-phase arrest and increase the expression of cell-cycle regulators p15, p18, and p27 in adult spinal cord NSPCs. The effects of glucocorticoids on the differentiation of adult spinal cord NSPCs are complex. Our findings show that glucocorticoid receptor antagonists can partially block glucocorticoid-induced inhibition of glial cell differentiation but have no effect on the suppression of neuronal differentiation. These results suggest that additional mechanisms beyond the classical glucocorticoid receptor pathway may mediate the effects of glucocorticoids on the neuronal differentiation of adult spinal cord NSPCs.

Previous studies have shown that NG2 glia proliferate after SCI and possess latent neurogenic potential that can be unlocked by ectopic Sox2 expression26. However, given that CORT125281 was administered during 0–2 dpi—before the major NG2 glial expansion at 3–7 dpi27—NG2 glia are unlikely to be the primary cellular targets of treatment in our paradigm. Although NG2 glia can generate neurons when ectopic Sox2 is introduced26, our data do not support a role for glucocorticoid signaling in regulating Sox2 expression under the conditions examined. In contrast, GR signaling is known to strongly influence the proliferative and regenerative behavior of ependymal-lineage NSPC45. Thus, while a contribution of NG2 glia cannot be fully excluded, current evidence supports ependymal-derived NSPCs as the main cellular targets of glucocorticoid inhibition in our study. The potential involvement of NG2 glia nevertheless merits further investigation.

CORT125281 enhances endogenous repair after SCI by promoting NSPC proliferation and neurite growth. This effect may mirror stem cell transplantation, where progenitors support circuit reconstruction, remyelination, and neurotrophic signaling2,46. By activating resident NSPCs, CORT125281 can generate new neurons and glia, improve the injury microenvironment, and support structural and functional remodeling after SCI, including enhanced 5-HT fiber presence, which is critical for locomotor recovery. The accompanying neurite extension indicates enhanced neuronal differentiation and connectivity, providing a structural basis for functional improvement. However, 5-HT immunoreactivity alone cannot distinguish between axonal regeneration, spared fibers, or neuroprotective effects. Future studies employing regeneration-specific markers or axonal tracing approaches will be required to define the precise nature of these changes. These findings suggest that pharmacological activation of endogenous NSPCs can partially recapitulate transplantation benefits, offering a promising, less invasive approach for spinal cord repair.

Changes in the microenvironment following SCI impact the balance between inhibitory and growth-promoting factors7. For example, after SCI, the expression of myelin-associated inhibitors, such as Nogo-A, increases, further impeding axonal regeneration47. In contrast, growth-promoting molecules like neurotrophin-3 may be downregulated, limiting their availability to injured neurons48. Researchers are exploring strategies such as targeting inflammatory pathways, degrading inhibitory molecules, and enhancing the availability of growth-promoting factors to create a more favorable microenvironment for recovery49. High-dose methylprednisolone (a synthetic glucocorticoid) is commonly administered in clinical settings for the treatment of acute SCI. However, an increasing body of evidence suggests that methylprednisolone fails to improve motor function or neurological outcomes, while also causing notable adverse effects50–52. Furthermore, studies have indicated that clinically relevant doses of methylprednisolone may inhibit the proliferation of endogenous spinal cord NSPCs following SCI53,54. Recent studies have shown that glucocorticoid levels are significantly elevated in both murine and human SCI models8. Nelson et al. reported that glucocorticoid receptors are highly expressed in spinal cord ependymal cells in both zebrafish and rats, and that exogenous glucocorticoids (10 μM dexamethasone) inhibit functional recovery after SCI by targeting ependymal glia45. Their study demonstrated that glucocorticoid receptor activation blocks the formation of trans-lesional glial bridges, axon regeneration, and ependymal glial proliferation. Consistent with these findings, our results show that elevated endogenous glucocorticoid levels following injury suppress the proliferation of adult spinal cord NSPCs through glucocorticoid receptor activation, thereby impairing recovery after SCI.

Combinatorial strategies are increasingly recognized as essential for promoting functional recovery after SCI55,56. Our findings suggest that glucocorticoid-receptor inhibition enhances the endogenous regenerative response by promoting NSPC proliferation and neurite growth. This mechanism could complement other therapeutic approaches—for example, combining glucocorticoid inhibition with biomaterial scaffolds or cell transplantation may improve the survival, differentiation, and integration of NSPC-derived cells by providing a more permissive microenvironment.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that glucocorticoids suppress the proliferation and differentiation of endogenous adult spinal cord NSPCs, and that pharmacological blockade of glucocorticoid receptors improves motor function in a traumatic SCI model in mice. These findings provide new insight into the inhibitory role of glucocorticoids in the injured spinal cord and suggest that targeting glucocorticoid receptors may represent a promising strategy to promote functional recovery after SCI.

Methods

Reagents and chemicals

Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM)/F12, B27, N2, and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were obtained from Gibco (Grand Island, NY, USA). Accutase, protease inhibitor cocktail, and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail 2 were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Munich, Germany). Basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) and epidermal growth factor (EGF) were sourced from Peprotech (Rocky Hill, NJ, USA). Corticosterone and cortisol were purchased from Sigma (St. Louis, MO, USA). CORT125281 was obtained from MCE (#HY-117880, Brooklyn, NY, USA). CORT125281, corticosterone, and cortisol were first dissolved in DMSO to prepare 50 mM stock solutions, which were then diluted to the desired test concentrations with culture medium. The final DMSO concentration did not exceed 0.2% in any cell experiments.

Animals and ethics statement

The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. Female C57BL/6J mice (16–20 g, 6 weeks of age) were employed for this study. All mice were procured from Slack (Shanghai, China), and accommodated in specific pathogen-free Fudan University facilities under controlled conditions of constant temperature (21 ± 3 °C) and humidity (50% ± 5%) with a 12-h light/dark cycle. Access to food and water was provided ad libitum. For primary culture of adult mouse spinal cord NSPCs, mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation prior to spinal cord isolation. For immunofluorescence analyses, mice were deeply anesthetized with pentobarbital and subsequently subjected to transcardial perfusion before tissue collection. All procedures were performed to minimize animal suffering, and were approved by the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments of School of Life Sciences, Fudan University (No.2021JS0090). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Cell culture

Primary culture of adult mouse spinal cord stem/progenitor cells. Six-week-old female C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Slack (Shanghai, China). Adult spinal cord NSPCs were isolated as previously described36. The cells were cultured in non-tissue culture (non-TC) treated T-25 flasks (Cat: 169900, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) with proliferation medium consisting of DMEM/F12 supplemented with 1% B27, 1% N2, 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin, 10 ng/mL EGF, and 20 ng/mL bFGF. NSPCs at passages one to four were used for the experiments. For differentiation assays, the cells were cultured in tissue culture (TC)-treated 6-well plates (Cat: 140675, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) at 2 × 105 per well with differentiation medium containing DMEM/F12 supplemented with 1% B27, 1% N2, 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin, and 1% FBS.

HeLa cell lines were initially obtained from the America Type Culture Collection (ATCC; USA) and cultured in DMEM with 10% (vol/vol) FBS. The HeLa cell line was used to validate the efficacy of MR antibodies.

Neurosphere assay

Cultured NSPCs (4000 cells/well) were seeded in non–tissue-culture-treated 96-well plates (Cat. No. 3370; Corning, USA) and treated with proliferation medium containing various concentrations of cortisol or corticosterone (0.1–50 μM) for 7 days. The treatment was administered at the time of seeding, and the medium was not replaced during the culture period to allow neurospheres to form without mechanical disturbance, after which the number of neurospheres was counted. Neurospheres were considered to be of standard size when measuring 70 µm in diameter. The number of neurospheres was quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, MD, USA).

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) following the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Briefly, neurospheres were dissociated into single cells using Accutase and seeded at 4000 cells/well in non-TC-treated 96-well plates with proliferation medium. The medium was supplemented with corticosterone or cortisol and maintained for 7 days without replacement. Four concentrations (0.1–50 μM) and a DMSO vehicle control were tested per experiment. For antagonist studies, cells were pre-incubated with 500 nM CORT125281 for 30 min prior to the addition of 1 µM cortisol, followed by a 7-day culture without medium change. Subsequently, cells were incubated with CCK-8 solution for 4 h at 37 °C. The optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm to determine cell viability.

Calcein-AM/Propidium Iodide (PI) staining assay

The spinal cord NSPCs were treated with 1 µM cortisol for 7 days. Then the neurospheres were digested into single cells using Accutase, and the cells were stained according to the instructions with the Calcein-AM/Propidium Iodide (PI) Double Staining Kit (Beyotime, China). After staining, the cells were transferred to a 12-well plate and photographed using a fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems) for detection. Live (Calcein-positive) and dead (PI-positive) cells were quantified in three random fields per well using ImageJ (https://fiji.sc/).

5-Ethynyl-2-Dexoyuridine (EdU) incorporation assay

The neuropheres were cultured in proliferation medium with or without cortisol for 24 h, then dissociated into single cells and plated onto 0.1 mg/ml poly-L-ornithine-coated glass slides. After an 8-h incubation in proliferation medium containing EdU, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature for 30 min. The PFA was then removed, and the cells were incubated with a glycine solution (2 mg/ml) for 6 min. After two washes with PBS, the cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. EdU staining was performed using the BeyoClick™ EdU-647 Cell Proliferation Kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. The ratio of EdU-positive cells to DAPI-positive cells was calculated under fluorescence microscopy.

Annexin V-FITC /PI staining assay

NSPCs were treated with 1 μM cortisol for 36 h or 7 days. Cells were then dissociated into single-cell suspensions using Accutase, washed twice with PBS, and stained using the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cell pellets were resuspended in 195 μL of 1× binding buffer, followed by the addition of 5 μL Annexin V–fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) and 10 μL propidium iodide (PI). The samples were gently mixed and incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Subsequently, 300 μL of 1× binding buffer was added, and samples were analyzed by flow cytometry within 1 h.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Spinal cord NSPCs were cultured in proliferation medium with or without cortisol for 5 days. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (TIANGEN, Beijing, China), and reverse transcribed (2 μg RNA) using the FastKing RT Kit (with gDNase) (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). qRT-PCR was performed as previously described57. Briefly, qRT-PCR was conducted in 20 μL reactions containing 2 μL of cDNA template, 0.4 μmol/L of each primer pair, and SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: 94 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 8 min. Results were normalized to β-actin mRNA. Data were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method and are expressed as fold change relative to the control. Western blot

Spinal cord NSPCs were cultured in proliferation medium and treated with cortisol for 10, 30, and 60 min. Following treatment, total cellular protein was extracted using ice-cold RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Sigma, USA) to preserve protein integrity. Protein concentration was measured with a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China). Protein samples (10 μg per lane) were resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes in transfer buffer (25 mM Tris, 192 mM glycine, 20% methanol, v/v) at a constant current of 230 mA for 2 h at 4 °C. Subsequently, the membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in TBST (Tris-buffered saline containing 0.05% Tween 20) for 1 h at room temperature, and were immunoblotted with primary antibodies against phospho-ERK (p-ERK, 1:1000, #4370, Cell Signaling Technology), phospho-AKT (p-AKT, 1:1000, #4060, Cell Signaling Technology), total ERK (1:1000, #4695, Cell Signaling Technology), total AKT (1:1000, #9272, Cell Signaling Technology), and β-tubulin (1:1000, #AF2835, Beyotime) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TBST three times, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (goat anti-mouse, 1:1000, #P0946, Beyotime; goat anti-rabbit, 1:1000, #P0948, Beyotime) for 1 h at room temperature. The blots were developed using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents and imaged using the ChemiDoc XRS+ imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) with the manufacturer’s software. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (https://fiji.sc/). Phosphorylation levels of ERK and AKT were normalized to their corresponding total protein levels.

Immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence analysis of NSPC differentiation, poly-L-ornithine–coated slides were placed in 6-well tissue culture plates. Neurospheres were dissociated into single cells using Accutase, and the cells were seeded onto the coated slides at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per well. The cells were first pre-incubated with 500 nM CORT125281 for 30 min. Subsequently, 1 μM cortisol was added, and the cultures were maintained for 7 days without medium change. For immunofluorescence analysis of glucocorticoid receptors, neurospheres cultured in suspension for 6 days were seeded onto poly-L-ornithine-coated slides and incubated for an additional 24 h. Neurospheres or single cells seeded on poly-L-ornithine-coated slides were fixed with 4% PFA for 30 min at room temperature, followed by three washes with PBS. Cells were then incubated with a blocking solution containing 10% horse serum and 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. After three PBS washes, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-GR (1:1000, #3660, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-MR (1:500, ab64457, Abcam), anti-GFAP (1:1000, ab7260, Abcam), anti-Tuj1 (1:1000, ab18207, Abcam), anti-nestin (1:1000, ab6142, Abcam), anti-5-HT (1:500, #20079, IMMUNOSTAR), anti-NG2 (1:50, MA524247, Invitrogen). Following three washes with PBST (0.025% Triton X-100 in PBS), cells were incubated with secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor® 488-conjugated donkey anti-mouse IgG, 1:500, 715-545-150, Jackson; Cy5-conjugated donkey anti-goat IgG, 1:500, 705-175-147, Jackson; Alexa Fluor® 647- conjugated goat anti-rabbit, 1:500, #A0468, beyotime) in 1% horse serum and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS three times, cells were counterstained with DAPI.

On days 7 and 63 post-SCI, spinal cord segments encompassing the lesion site (centered at the T8 level) were collected for analysis of the organization and distribution of neural stem cells (NSCs) in response to SCI. Mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital and subsequently transcardially perfused with ice-cold 4% PFA in PBS. The spinal cord was then carefully excised and post-fixed in the same fixative at 4 °C for 4–8 h. After fixation, the tissue was equilibrated in 30% (w/v) sucrose in PBS overnight at 4 °C, embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound, and frozen for sectioning. The tissue was sectioned at a thickness of 25 μm and stained using the immunofluorescence protocol described above.

Microscopy and image analysis

Immunofluorescence images were acquired using a Nikon A1 inverted laser scanning confocal microscope controlled by NIS-Elements software. Low-magnification tiled images were collected and stitched to cover the entire spinal cord section. All images within each experiment were acquired using identical laser power, gain, and exposure settings. Quantitative image analysis was performed using ImageJ/Fiji. Fluorescence intensity and axon density analyses were conducted on raw images using identical analysis parameters across experimental groups. Background subtraction and signal normalization were applied uniformly to all images. For fluorescence intensity analysis, mean signal intensity was measured at sequential 500 μm intervals rostral and caudal to the lesion epicenter (defined as 0). Background intensity was defined as the average signal from the rostralmost and caudalmost 250 μm regions within the same section and subtracted from each measurement. For axon density analysis, the percentage area occupied by immunoreactive fibers was quantified within a 3000 μm caudal region. To normalize for inter-section variability, density values from the caudal region were normalized to the average density measured in the homologous rostral region of the same section, which was set to 100%.

Flow cytometry analysis

The neuropheres were cultured in proliferation medium with or without cortisol for 36 h, then dissociated into single cells. Cells were collected, washed with PBS, and fixed overnight at 4 °C with 70% ice‑cold ethanol. Prior to staining, cells were washed and resuspended in 500 µL of cell‑cycle staining solution from the Cell Cycle Detection Kit (Beyotime, China), incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. Flow cytometry was performed on a BD (Becton, Dickinson and Company) LSRFortessa™ cytometer using a 488 nm laser and PI channel (FL2) for DNA content, acquiring ~10,000 single‑cell events per sample. Debris and doublets were excluded by SSC-A/FSC-A gating and FSC‑H/FSC‑A gating. Gating and cell-cycle distribution (G0/G1, S, G2/M) analysis were performed with FlowJo v10.7 software (TreeStar Inc.). All assays were performed on three independent biological replicates, and data are presented as mean ± SEM.

SCI model

To model clinical compression injury of the spinal cord, a crush-injured SCI model was established as previously described59. Adult female C57BL/6J mice (16–20 g, 6 weeks of age) were selected to ensure uniform spinal cord size. To minimize lesion variability due to differences in surgical experience, all SCI procedures were performed by the same surgeon. Mice were randomly assigned to three groups: sham control (n = 10), SCI + Vehicle (n = 20), SCI + CORT125281 (n = 19). Randomization sequences were generated using the =RAND() function in Microsoft Excel. Briefly, animals were anesthetized with 2% pentobarbital (30 mg/kg), and a ~2 cm incision was made along the midline of the back. After performing a laminectomy at the T8 level, the spinal cord was laterally compressed for 15 s using a pair of specialized forceps with a 0.4 mm spacer, producing a precise moderate crush injury. Sham control animals only received laminectomy.

Following surgery, mice were returned to their home cages and received manual bladder expression twice daily until reflexive bladder control was restored. The animals received oral administration of either CORT125281 or vehicle. CORT125281 was administered at a dose of 30 mg/kg on days 0, 1, and 2 following spinal cord injury. The compound was dissolved in a vehicle consisting of 2% DMSO, 40% PEG 300, and 2% Tween 80 in saline. Animals that suffered accidental death were excluded from analysis. Depending on the experiment, mice were euthanized at 7 or 63 days post-injury for immunofluorescence analysis.

Assessment of locomotor performance

Hindlimb function in mice was assessed weekly post-surgery using the Basso Mouse Scale (BMS) open-field locomotor test19 and the inclined-grid climbing test60. The BMS test quantitatively evaluates voluntary movement and the ability to support body weight. The inclined-grid climbing test assesses the percentage of mice that regained the ability to place their hindlimbs correctly and recover proprioceptive responses. Mice with SCI were randomly assigned to control or treatment groups. The animals received oral administrations of either CORT125281 or the vehicle as described above. Drug administration and hindlimb functional assessments were carried out in a double-blind fashion.

RNA sequencing

Spinal cord NSPCs were collected after treatment with or without 1μM cortisol for 3 days. Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Kit (Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted RNA was used to construct RNA sequencing libraries, which underwent quality control analysis before sequencing on the DNBSEQ platform. Clean reads were mapped to the reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.0.4). Bowtie2 (v2.2.5) was employed to align the clean reads to the reference coding gene set, and gene expression levels were calculated using RSEM (v1.2.12). Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.4.5). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted using Phyper, based on the Hypergeometric test, with statistical significance corrected by the Bonferroni method at a stringent threshold (Q value ≤ 0.05). The raw sequencing data included in this study have been deposited in the SRA database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra) under the accession code PRJNA1208143.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v9.4, GraphPad Software Inc, USA). The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The two-tailed paired or unpaired t-test was used to compare two groups, and one-way or two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s post hoc test was employed for comparisons involving multiple groups. Data are presented as mean ± SEM, with “n” representing the number of independent experiments or animals unless otherwise specified. A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
