How enterprise social media use affects employee well-being
Authors: Da Eun Song, Seongcheol Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe widespread adoption of enterprise social media (ESM) in hybrid and remote work settings has transformed workplace collaboration by enabling seamless connectivity and real-time interaction. Yet it also requires employees to remain continuously responsive and digitally present. These conditions increase communication overload and blur work–personal boundaries, reflecting the phenomenon of techno-invasion. Such disruptions may heighten psychological strain and diminish well-being. Guided by the Job Demands–Resources (JD–R) model, this study examines how distinct ESM-induced job demands−communication overload and boundary management stress−are associated with employee exhaustion and well-being. We collected survey data from 200 Korean office workers (mean age = 44.38, range = 27–64; 56% male) using validated self-report scales and analyzed the data using partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM). The results indicate that intensified ESM use is linked to greater communication overload and boundary management stress, which are also associated with increased exhaustion and reduced well-being. By elucidating how ESM-driven connectivity can undermine employee well-being, this research enriches the technostress literature. Practically, it highlights the need for organizations to implement supportive initiatives that alleviate excessive job demands and foster a sustainable digital work environment that supports employee psychological health.Keywords: Enterprise social media, Job demands–resources model, Boundary theory, Communication overload, Exhaustion, Well-beingSubject terms: Business and management, Business and management, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Advancements in digital technologies have significantly expanded the adoption of management information systems (MIS), and communication-focused tools now play a central role because they shape information flow, workplace relationships, and performance. Computer-mediated communication (CMC), encompassing instant messaging, email, and corporate blogs, facilitates interpersonal and mass communication. However, research indicates mixed outcomes: these tools enhance efficiency, yet they also create information overload and stress. The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic accelerated the global shift to remote work in 2020 and established digital work as a core operational mode. Consequently, organizations increasingly adopted enterprise social media (ESM) as a primary tool to maintain productivity and foster employee connections despite physical separation1.

Unlike traditional CMC, ESM integrates information sharing, communication, and collaboration within a single platform and provides affordances such as organization-wide visibility and persistent content. These features enable employees to learn from others’ interactions and strengthen interaction, socialization, and teamwork, which drives widespread adoption in modern organizations2. Numerous organizations have implemented ESM, and empirical studies report that it improves productivity, communication, collaboration, engagement, and innovation3. Employees leverage ESM for activities ranging from team communication to task execution, which enhances their autonomy and competence. Research also demonstrates that ESM improves work-related outcomes, including employee performance, knowledge sharing, and creativity4–7.

Earlier studies primarily highlighted the positive impact of ESM use, but more recent research examines its negative consequences. A growing body of literature now shows that ESM increases digital interruptions and workplace stress5,8,9. Although ESM offers substantial benefits, it also brings unintended drawbacks. The same features that make ESM valuable can contribute to its problematic effects. In the post-pandemic era, the expansion of hybrid work increased organizational dependence on digital workplace tools and heightened the need to understand how job demands impact employee productivity and well-being. For instance, ESM enables constant connectivity across time and space, which improves access to information and communication; however, this same connectivity can be also associated with information overload, which corresponds with negative psychological and behavioral outcomes.

This study explores the adverse effects of digital workplace technologies, with a focus on individual-level challenges that employees experience. Grounded in the Job Demands−Resources (JD–R) model, this research focuses on the demand-side associations—specifically communication overload and boundary management stress—and evaluates how these demands correspond with employees’ psychological strain. Boundary theory complements this framework by explaining how ESM’s persistent visibility and asynchronous affordances blur work–non-work boundaries and intensify boundary management stress.

Although the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, this study integrates these perspectives to extend technostress research by identifying two distinguishable demand dimensions with different associative patterns. In doing so, it provides a more nuanced understanding of how technological advantages of digital work settings can unintentionally disrupt resource dynamics. The study clarifies the statistical associations among these variables and provides an empirical foundation for developing evidence-based approaches that support employee well-being in technology-mediated workplaces.

Job demands–resources model

The JD–R model10 classifies all job characteristics into job demands and job resources and explains how these factors jointly influence work-related outcomes. Job demands are the material, psychological, or organizational demands of work that require sustained effort, such as excessive workload and role conflict, and typically trigger negative psychological and physiological responses11. In contrast, job resources refer to the psychological, social, or organizational resources available in the work environment, such as social support, job engagement, and rewards, that motivate employees by satisfying basic human needs. These resources help reduce the costs associated with job demands and foster employee growth and development10,12.

According to the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, individuals actively seek, preserve, and protect resources they value, including tangible objects, personal traits such as self-esteem, energy, and other resources that hold intrinsic value or help secure additional resources13. Individuals are particularly sensitive to resource loss and invest efforts to prevent depletion. However, chronic exposure to high job demands (e.g., excessive workload and emotional labor) is associated with depleted psychological and physical resources and with exhaustion14.

The rise of ESM has reshaped workplace communication by promoting a culture of constant connectivity and technical availability, which often leads employees to feel a persistent need to stay psychologically engaged with digital interactions, even outside standard work hours15. The extent of this “connectedness mindset” varies among individuals, but those with a stronger sense of constant engagement experience higher levels of overload and psychological strain, particularly in digital work environments16. From a JD–R perspective, the technological connectivity that ESM enforces cultivates this mindset by encouraging employees to remain continuously accessible and responsive, thereby corresponding with excessive job demands and reports of depleted cognitive and emotional resources.

Communication overload and boundary management stress

Lee et al.17 highlight that digital technologies can impose behavioral and psychological burdens on individuals through mechanisms such as involuntary communication, expanded social networks, and information saturation. In the context of ESM, where interactions frequently transcend temporal and spatial constraints, employees face heightened susceptibility to various forms of overload.

Constantly accessible communication channels increase the likelihood of frequent interruptions, redundant interactions, and excessive messaging18. Communication or social overload arises when employees perceive that they expend excessive social resources on others through ESM, often due to intensified social demands19. Unlike information overload, which stems from processing large volumes of content, communication overload emerges from the interactive nature of workplace exchanges. Organizations require employees to receive information and respond, coordinate, and maintain an active social presence. These reciprocal demands strain cognitive resources and time management.

Prior research identifies overload as a key precursor to role conflict20, work exhaustion8, ESM-specific exhaustion5, and stress21,22. Similarly, studies across various information systems contexts show that overload contributes to fatigue, reduces psychological well-being, and lowers intentions to continue system use23,24. Notably, the same processes—communication and coordination—that ESM aims to enhance also generate these adverse outcomes, turning the tool’s primary strength into its most significant drawback.

Excessive engagement with ESM can lead to communication overload, which differs from information overload by focusing on frequent, unplanned interactions initiated by others. As ESM expands users’ social networks, individuals may feel obligated to provide communication and social support to a growing number of contacts23. Continuous communication across multiple connections exposes users to persistent demands and requires additional, often excessive, effort to meet expected levels of support24.

The connectivity affordance amplifies the frequency and immediacy of interactions and suggests that communication overload may statistically mediate the association between ESM usage and negative psychological or behavioral outcomes. Identifying communication-related factors that intensify overload can guide the design of ESM platforms and policies that preserve collaboration while reducing negative effects.

Based on this, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1a. Employees’ use of enterprise social media (ESM) is positively associated with communication overload.

Recent research explores psychological responses to digital work environments. For instance, Keightley et al.25 identify two key responses to active ESM use: blurred boundaries between work and personal life and increased job demands. These responses are associated with depleted psychological resources, such as autonomy, flexibility, and negative cognitive, emotional, and behavioral outcomes26. Boundary theory explains that individuals define boundaries between life domains differently and perceive them as integrating or impermeable27,28. While the JD–R model explains the association between excessive demands and depleted employees’ resources with negative outcomes, boundary theory clarifies how ESM’s affordances such as constant connectivity erode the separation between work and non-work domains and relate to boundary management stress as a distinct demand.

Boundary management involves multiple dimensions, including cognitive confusion about role salience, emotional responses such as anxiety and guilt from boundary violations, and behavioral efforts to create and maintain work–non-work separation27. However, since individuals co-construct these boundaries with others, mismatches can occur between personal expectations and others’ interpretations, which can create conflict28.

Active ESM engagement often erodes the distinction between professional and personal spheres and increases the likelihood that others will violate individuals’ preferred boundaries. Boundary violation occurs when excessive intrusion blurs the separation between domains, resulting in conflict29. ESM’s constant temporal and spatial connectivity further amplifies boundary management stress in digital work environments. In flexible settings such as remote or hybrid work, rapid ESM adoption has transformed these platforms from efficiency tools into sources of stress30. Prior studies also show that remote work reduces job autonomy, increases work pressure, and heightens perceived obligations, all of which represent essential personal resources for managing job demands25.

Based on this reasoning, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1b. Employees’ use of enterprise social media (ESM) is positively associated with boundary management stress.

Outcomes of communication overload and boundary management stress

Communication overload poses a significant challenge in employees’ use of ESM within organizational settings23. Although ESM enhances connectivity across workplace networks, it frequently requires employees to respond continuously to work-related and non-work-related demands, which can lead to adverse psychological effects. This study examines these effects, with a particular focus on exhaustion and employee well-being.

The JD–R model and technostress research show that excessive job demands deplete employees’ energy and lead to outcomes such as exhaustion. Sustained exposure to demanding work environments causes exhaustion, which manifests as emotional and physical fatigue and represents a primary psychological consequence of ESM use24. This exhaustion reduces energy and contributes to lower motivation, engagement, and dedication8. When communication or social demands surpass available resources, exhaustion becomes more likely. Yu et al.24 demonstrate that excessive ESM engagement, concurrent with communication overload, is associated with higher levels of emotional exhaustion.

Employee well-being represents a crucial outcome associated with the adverse effects of ESM use, reflects individuals’ subjective assessments of their personal lives and is vital for organizational growth and sustainability31. Researchers conceptualize well-being through two perspectives: a hedonic perspective, which equates well-being with subjective happiness (subjective well-being), and a eudaimonic perspective, which views well-being as the outcome of personal growth and self-fulfillment (psychological well-being)32. Effective measurement of employee well-being requires general indicators, such as life satisfaction, happiness, self-acceptance, and autonomy, and work-specific metrics, such as job satisfaction and workplace-related affect32.

In organizational settings, Marsh et al.21 found that digital workplace overload significantly undermines employee well-being, highlighting the importance of mitigating negative emotional experiences to support organizational resilience. Adverse psychological responses weaken emotional resilience, hinder recovery from stress, and further erode employees’ ability to maintain well-being25.

The autonomy paradox refers to the dual nature of digital technologies: they offer flexibility for remote work while simultaneously creating expectations of constant availability, which constrains employees’ autonomy33. This paradox becomes especially evident in ESM contexts, where flexibility often comes with implicit expectations for continuous communication, blurring boundaries between work and personal life and exacerbating communication overload.

In flexible work arrangements, employees must constantly manage competing demands across work and non-work domains, which requires them to reallocate personal resources such as time and energy. Lutz et al.34 find that in environments characterized by constant technological accessibility, the pressure to remain available negatively impacts employees during work and personal time, ultimately reducing well-being. Diminished well-being increases turnover intentions and absenteeism35 and weakens organizations’ capacity for innovation and sustained performance36, which makes the management of ESM-related overload a critical strategic priority.

Based on these insights, we propose the following hypotheses:

H2. Communication overload is positively associated with (a) exhaustion and negatively associated with (b) well-being.

H3: Boundary management stress is positively associated with (a) exhaustion and negatively associated with (b) well-being.

In this study, communication overload and boundary management stress function as job demands that deplete employees’ cognitive and emotional resources. Increased use of ESM amplifies these demands instead of reducing work strain. From a JD–R perspective, the effect of job demands on employee outcomes−such as exhaustion and diminished well-being−operate indirectly through the depletion of psychological resources37. Accordingly, communication overload and boundary management stress are examined as statistical mediators in the associations between ESM use and employees’ psychological states.

Based on this reasoning, we propose the following hypotheses:

H4. Communication overload statistically mediates the association between enterprise social media (ESM) usage and (a) exhaustion and (b) well-being.

H5: Boundary management stress statistically mediates the association between enterprise social media (ESM) usage and (a) exhaustion and (b) well-being.

Results

We used Smart PLS 3.0 to test the measurement and structural models. Given that this research adopts an exploratory approach to examine how ESM-induced job demands influence employees’ exhaustion and well-being, we selected partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) for its prediction-oriented nature and its ability to handle complex models with multiple latent variables38,39. PLS-SEM offers methodological strengths such as robustness with smaller sample sizes and flexibility in handling non-normal data distributions, which makes it well-suited for exploratory research40. Accordingly, we selected PLS-SEM as the analytical framework for this study.

Measurement model

In PLS-SEM, researchers evaluate the measurement model using reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity. All constructs were specified as reflective measurement models, in which the indicators are assumed to reflect their underlying latent variables39. We assessed convergent validity using average variance extracted (AVE) and factor loadings. AVE values should exceed 0.5, and factor loadings should exceed 0.7. Among the control variables, job position was significantly positively associated with well-being. Average daily working hours were significantly correlated with higher exhaustion and lower well-being. However, age and gender show no significant effects on communication overload, boundary management stress, exhaustion, or well-being. The type of ESM platform used as a control variable also showed no significant effects.

We evaluated the R-square statistics for the dependent variables to assess how well the model explains and predicts the data (Fig. 2). These values reflect the strength of the relationships and the substantive effects of the associated latent variables. The model explained 41.5% of the variance in communication overload, 42% in boundary management stress, 48% in exhaustion, and 24.1% in well-being.

The results indicate significant positive associations between ESM use and communication overload (β = 0.657, p < .001), and boundary management stress (β = 0.619, p < .001), which supports H1. The results supported H2: communication overload significantly linked to higher exhaustion (β = 0.222, p = .001) and reduced well-being (β = − 0.197, p = .009). Similarly, the results support H3, as boundary management stress corresponded with significantly higher employee exhaustion (β = 0.494, p < .001) and reduced well-being (β = − 0.215, p = .008).

The mediation analyses further show that communication overload and boundary management stress statistically mediated the association between ESM use and exhaustion and well-being. Specifically, ESM use showed significant indirect associations with exhaustion via communication overload (β = 0.146, p = .001) and boundary management stress (β = 0.306, p < .001), which supports H4a and H5a. In contrast, ESM use showed significant indirect associations with well-being via communication overload (β = − 0.130, p = .008) and boundary management stress (β = − 0.133, p = .009), which supports H4b and H5b.

Summary and interpretation

Our analysis examined demand-related associations of ESM use in organizational contexts, with particular attention to how communication overload and boundary management stress correspond with employees’ psychological strain. Consistent with H1a and H1b, intensive engagement with ESM platforms was significantly associated with both communication overload and boundary management stress, which also coincided with higher levels of psychological strain in organizational settings. Communication overload imposes excessive burdens from managing incoming communications, including frequent notifications, urgent messages, and pressure to manage simultaneous interactions with multiple coworkers.

Boundary management stress, in turn, captures employees’ multidimensional responses to boundary erosion, including difficulty disconnecting, a persistent sense that work never ends, and feelings of guilt or anxiety. These results are consistent with prior studies that highlight that ESM use depletes employees’ cognitive and emotional resources5,8. The JD–R model explains that higher job demands reduce personal and psychological resources, which lowers employees’ capacity to sustain well-being14. For example, employees may struggle to balance the demands of responding to requests with completing core job responsibilities, corresponding to a sense of cognitive overload.

Consistent with H2a, H2b, H3a, and H3b, communication overload and boundary management stress were significantly associated with exhaustion and well-being. Communication overload was significantly linked to higher exhaustion and reduced well-being, which supports a growing body of research linking excessive communication demands to heightened stress and lower job satisfaction21,23,44. Therefore, ESM use is associated with demands that can overwhelm employees, extending beyond its intended role as a coordination tool. Similarly, boundary management stress, defined in this study as employees’ responses to the erosion of work–life boundaries related to the pervasive nature of ESM, was significantly associated with higher exhaustion while corresponded to lower well-being.

The mediation analyses supported H4 and H5. Consistent with the JD–R framework, communication overload and boundary management stress, both representing job demands, statistically mediated the association between ESM use and exhaustion and well-being. Notably, boundary management stress was more strongly associated with exhaustion (β = 0.494) than communication overload (β = 0.222), which suggests that failure to achieve psychological detachment depletes resources more than message volume alone. However, alternative explanations remain possible. For instance, employees who already experience exhaustion may perceive greater overload and boundary stress, and unmeasured variables such as neuroticism or organizational communication norms may also influence these patterns.

Boundary-related strain reflects techno-invasion, wherein ESM’s constant connectivity erodes work–personal boundaries45. Although ESM platforms enable greater flexibility in terms of work location and timing, they simultaneously correspond to expectations of continuous availability across multiple communication channels, such as group chats, direct messages, and notifications46. These expectations foster a sense of obligation to remain continuously responsive, a phenomenon described by some scholars as an “electronic leash” that binds employees to their work responsibilities even during non-work hours47.

Persistent connectivity prevents psychological detachment, which employees need for emotional recovery, stress reduction, and sustained work engagement48. In these environments, employees must rely on personal resources—particularly time management and boundary regulation—to manage excessive job demands and maintain detachment49. In this sense, ESM creates a paradox: it offers flexibility while simultaneously imposing expectations of constant availability, which reflects the autonomy paradox and contributes to higher communication overload and eroded work–life boundaries33.

Implications

This study advances the theoretical understanding of technostress by integrating the JD–R framework to explore the interrelationships between communication overload, boundary management stress, and employees’ psychological outcomes in the context of ESM use. By leveraging the JD–R model, we show that excessive ESM engagement is associated with higher job demands, which are also correlated with adverse outcomes like exhaustion and diminished well-being. We also identify the important role of boundary management stress, related to the pervasive and intrusive nature of ESM features, in amplifying these effects. This approach deepens understanding of the “dark side” of ESM use and strengthens the broader discussion on how digital tools are associated with employee psychological health. Our integrative model offers a comprehensive framework for examining job demands and their association with individual-level outcomes.

Our findings also offer several practical implications. First, ESM platform developers should design features that promote psychological disengagement and allow employees to disconnect during non-work hours. Tools such as “quiet hours,” “focus mode,” and scheduled notification pauses can help employees prioritize tasks, regulate engagement, and reduce the mental health toll of constant connectivity, thereby fostering healthier work practices.

Organizations should also address the negative effects of excessive ESM use by establishing shared norms that respect employees’ work−life boundaries. Boundary management stress showed a stronger association with exhaustion than communication overload, which suggests that employees’ inability to detach psychologically from work depletes resources more than message volume alone. This insight shifts managerial attention from reducing notification frequency to protecting employees’ psychological recovery. Since employees co-construct and socially reinforce boundaries, collective expectations often shape perceptions of acceptable boundary crossings more than individual preferences50.

Organizations should implement policies such as after-hour communication guidelines to encourage boundary-respecting behaviors and mitigate overload. Although ESM facilitates collaboration and operational efficiency, unchecked demands for constant responsiveness reduce employee well-being and threaten long-term organizational sustainability23. Therefore, managers should promote technology use that strengthens collaboration while prioritizing employee mental health and fostering a supportive work environment.

Policymakers should also adapt labor regulations to reflect the realities of digital work. They can encourage organizations to adopt “right to disconnect” policies or require digital rest periods, which support psychological recovery and help build sustainable work systems that safeguard employee well-being.

Limitations and future research

This study offers meaningful implications but also has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits the study’s ability to establish temporal precedence or rule out reciprocal relationships. Although the JD–R model guides the framework, the design does not allow for causal inference. For instance, employees who experience higher exhaustion may report greater overload and boundary stress, reversing the assumed direction. In addition, unmeasured variables such as boundary preferences or organizational communication norms may confound the observed relationships. Future research should employ longitudinal or experimental methods, such as multi-wave panels or experience-sampling methods, to track changes in ESM use, job demands, and well-being over time and provide stronger evidence on directionality.

Second, the modest R2 for well-being reflects the study’s focus on demand-related variables. The model intentionally excludes individual differences, such as boundary management preferences and technology self-efficacy, as well as organizational communication norms identified in previous research21,23,51 to isolate ESM-specific demand effects. However, these omitted variables may still influence well-being outcomes. Future research should incorporate these factors to explain a greater share of variance in well-being.

Third, the study’s reliance on a South Korean sample may limit the generalizability of the findings. South Korea’s work culture, characterized by extended working hours, hierarchical organizational structures, and advanced digital infrastructure, likely shapes patterns of ESM use and their psychological impacts. Cultural factors, including collectivism and high-context communication norms, may further influence how employees experience ESM. Future studies should adopt cross-cultural designs that compare ESM use and its effects across diverse national contexts to clarify the role of cultural dynamics in technostress.

Fourth, the study collected data from employees across multiple organizations in two phases, without modeling the nested structure of the data. Organizational and team-level factors such as responsiveness norms and after-hour communication expectations remain unmodeled. Although the analysis included platform type, job position, and daily working hours as control variables to account for cross-organizational differences, these controls do not replace multilevel modeling. Future research should employ multilevel approaches to capture the hierarchical structure of employees within teams and organizations more rigorously.

Finally, this study differentiated among ESM platforms, such as mobile and desktop systems. In real-world contexts, platform-specific characteristics, such as notification frequency, response immediacy, and device portability, shape user experience. Moreover, employees often use multiple technologies simultaneously, including mobile devices and personal social media, which collectively contribute to communication overload and boundary management stress. Future research should investigate the distinct and cumulative effects of these platforms and channels to gain deeper insights into their impact on employee well-being and exhaustion.

Methodology

Sample and data collection

We conducted an online survey from June 10–13, 2025, in collaboration with Macromil Embrain, a professional survey agency in South Korea. The study targeted office workers familiar with commonly used ESM platforms in Korean corporate settings, including Slack, Microsoft Teams, Naver Works, JANDI, Swit, and Flow. These platforms share core features, such as real-time communication through topic- and team-based chat channels, file-sharing across formats (e.g., images), and integration of multiple work-related services into a unified platform.

To ensure workplace relevance, we restricted eligibility to individuals who used these tools officially for work within their organizations. Initial recruitment through Macromil Embrain yielded 150 participants, which fell short of the target sample size of 200. To close this gap, we recruited an additional 50 participants using snowball sampling, bringing the total to 200. We conducted the second phase of data collection from June 10–20, 2025. An a priori power analysis using G*Power (version 3.1.9.6) showed that the study required at least 146 participants to test the hypotheses adequately. The final sample of 200 valid responses—150 from Macromil Embrain and 50 from snowball sampling—exceeded this requirement.

The researchers’ university waived ethical review and approval for this study under the Bioethics Law of Korea (Chap. 16, Enforcement Regulation Chap. 13), which exempts studies that use public information or do not collect personally identifiable data. We conducted all procedures involving human participants in accordance with the ethical standards of Korea University (Seoul, Korea) and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. We obtained informed consent from all participants at the start of the online survey. To reduce social desirability bias, we instructed participants to respond to all survey items anonymously52.

The survey began with questions about work-related details, including the primary ESM platform used, average daily working hours, and job position. Participants then reported their ESM usage and answered questions measuring communication overload, boundary management stress, exhaustion, and well-being. Measurements

The study drew all constructs from established and validated scales and adapted them to reflect recent research on ESM usage and employee well-being. We collected responses using a seven-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (Strongly disagree or Do not use at all) to 7 (Strongly agree or Use very frequently). We adapted the measurement items for ESM usage were adapted from Luqman et al.5 and Zhong et al.54, and items for communication overload from Chen and Wei19 and Karr–Wisniewski and Lu55, the items for boundary management stress from Keightley et al.25, the items for exhaustion from Moore56, the items for well-being from Zheng et al.32.

Following prior studies8,26,57–59, we included several control variables. We included gender and age as common demographic controls because prior studies show that these variables influence technology usage behaviors and relate to inter-role conflict and perceptions of the work environment8,59. We also included average daily working hours and job position because workload intensity and managerial responsibility can influence employees’ exposure to job demands, exhaustion, and well-being.

Because the sample included employees from different organizations who used different ESM platforms, we anticipated potential sample heterogeneity. To address this concern, we included ESM platform type as a control variable to capture platform- and organization-related differences that may shape the extent and impact of ESM use.
