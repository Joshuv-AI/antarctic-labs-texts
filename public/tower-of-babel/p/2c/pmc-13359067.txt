Resection of C3 osteochondroma in a 20-year-old male with the combination of virtual reality and 3D printing for preoperative planning: illustrative case
Authors: Aazad Abbas, Luke Reda, Husain Shakil, Nitish Agarwal, Andres Rodriguez-Buitrago, Yuyang Tony Jiao, Zachary Fishman, Michael Hardisty, Antoine Eskander, Jetan H Badhiwala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDWhile osteochondromas are the most common benign bone tumors, spinal involvement remains uncommon. Anterior vertebral body involvement is unusual, and anterior C3 osteochondroma with parapharyngeal extension in an adult has not been previously reported in the literature. Osteochondromas represent the most common benign bone tumors, yet their involvement in the spine remains exceptionally rare.1–4 The majority of spinal osteochondromas involve the posterior elements of the cervical spine, with anterior body involvement being unusual.1,4 The involvement of the C3 vertebral body with anterior extension into the retropharyngeal and parapharyngeal spaces represents an extraordinarily rare presentation, with only sporadic case reports of anterior cervical osteochondromas in the literature.5,6

Hereditary multiple exostosis (HME), an autosomal dominant condition caused by mutations in the exostosin-1 and exostosin-2 genes, predisposes patients to the development of multiple osteochondromas throughout the skeleton.7 While some form of spinal involvement occurs in up to 68% of HME patients, the majority manifest as posterior element lesions.3,4 Anterior vertebral body involvement with symptomatic parapharyngeal extension has been sporadically described in the context of HME in a young adult.8 Resection of these lesions presents a unique challenge, due to the proximity of vital structures such as the carotid vessels, esophagus, and pharynx.

The management of complex spinal tumors has been revolutionized by the integration of 3D printing and virtual reality (VR) technologies into surgical planning workflows.9,10 While 3D printing has been increasingly adopted for preoperative planning in complex spine surgery, the combination of patient-specific 3D models with immersive VR planning for anterior cervical tumor resection remains uncommon.9,10 The integration of these technologies with intraoperative volumetric navigation to guide resection margins in proximity to critical neurovascular structures represents an emerging application with limited documentation in the literature.

As such, this report describes the first case of anterior C3 osteochondroma with parapharyngeal extension managed with integrated 3D printing and VR-assisted surgical planning, combined with intraoperative volumetric navigation.

Illustrative Case

Clinical History

A 20-year-old male with a known diagnosis of HME presented with a 1.5-year history of progressive dysphagia. Initial head and neck surgery evaluation revealed a firm posterior pharyngeal lesion, prompting cervical CT imaging and spine referral. Prior to presentation, he could tolerate solid and liquid food without episodes of choking, although he noted gradual difficulty with swallowing that was progressively limiting his quality of life and causing progressive weight loss. There was no hemoptysis, but he had a progressively muffled voice. He did not report any neurological symptoms.

Preoperative cervical CT and MR images had shown signs of a benign-appearing retropharyngeal lesion extending from the C3 vertebral body (Fig. 1), most likely in keeping with an HME lesion. Because of the size and location of the lesion, the patient was counseled regarding the possibility of corpectomy with the potential need for bone grafting and cervical fusion, although definitive reconstruction needs would be determined intraoperatively based on the extent of resection and spinal stability assessment.

Postoperative lateral (G) and anteroposterior (H) radiographs of the cervical spine. The images demonstrate complete removal of C3 vertebral osteochondroma.

Preoperative Planning

Initial imaging evaluation included high-resolution CT and MR images (with contrast) of the cervical spine with thin-section (2.0-mm cut) imaging to delineate the bony anatomy. MRI revealed the relationship to the spinal cord, trachea, and esophagus, demonstrating significant mass effect on the pharynx and parapharyngeal space (Fig. 1).

Based on the CT scans, patient-specific 3D-printed models were created using stereolithographic modeling. These reproduced the complex tumor anatomy, including its relationship to the C2–3 and C3–4 disc spaces, vertebral bodies, transverse processes, and vertebral arteries. The normal anatomy was segmented in 3D Slicer (slicer.org) using the machine learning–based TotalSegmentator tool for the vertebrae, veins, and arteries. The tumor was segmented by setting an intensity threshold and was manually refined to ensure accurate margins. The segmentation for the spinal cord was added and enlarged to maintain the positioning between the vertebrae. These segmented structures were then exported into Meshmixer (Autodesk) for remeshing, cleaning, and incorporation of a baseplate to fix the positions of the vertebrae and adjacent vessels, after which the resulting meshes were prepared in PrusaSlicer and printed on a Prusa MK3.5 platform (prusa3d.com) to generate a physical model for operative planning.

The 3D model was imported into an immersive VR simulation software within the SieVRt DICOM viewing platform (Luxsonic.ca) using an HTC Vive Pro VR headset. This allowed the surgical team to manipulate the anatomy from all angles, simulate the planned retropharyngeal and parapharyngeal approach, and rehearse the sequence of tumor resection while visualizing critical structures such as the vertebral arteries, carotid artery, internal jugular vein, and pharyngeal constrictors in relation to the lesion. This helped inform the decision for a combined head and neck surgery and spine approach, the optimal inferior-to-superior dissection strategy, and anticipated areas of technical difficulty. Operative Technique

The patient was positioned supine on a flat Jackson table and endotracheally intubated. His head was secured with a Mayfield head clamp, with a bolster placed under his head and neck for support. He was sterilely draped and his skin was prepared with betadine-based surgical preparation (Fig. 3). Bilateral horizontal neck crease incisions (two fingerbreadths below the angle of the mandible) were marked given the bilaterality of the lesion. A left longitudinal incision was made along the sternocleidomastoid for a retropharyngeal approach to the prevertebral space in conjunction with the head and neck surgery team.

Photographs showing the positioning (A and B) and surgical preparation/draping (C and D) of the patient from the top (A and C) and side (B and D) views.

Surgical Exposure

Subplatysmal flaps were elevated after the skin incision (Fig. 4). The sternocleidomastoid muscle was skeletonized superiorly to the mastoid tip. The posterior belly of the digastric muscle was identified and skeletonized after the common facial vein was divided. This approach avoided exposure of and injury to the marginal mandibular branch of the facial nerve. It also allowed for elevation of the submandibular gland and identification and preservation of the hypoglossal nerve. The hypoglossal nerve was skeletonized and retracted superiorly. Dissection then proceeded medial to the carotid sheath including the carotid artery, internal jugular vein, and vagus nerve, which allowed for identification of the hyoid bone and the superolateral aspect of the thyroid cartilage. Given the location of the tumor, the superior laryngeal artery, vein, and nerve were identified on entering the thyrohyoid membrane and divided. This allowed for the development of a medial tunnel down to the prevertebral fascia and the lesion.

Photographs showing stages of tumor exposure (A–C), removal of main body of tumor (D), removal of of tumor base (E), final surface of the vertebral body with the anterior longitudinal ligament intact (F), and piecemeal components of the tumor (G).

Tumor Exposure and Initial Resection

Working from inferior to superior as planned during VR rehearsal, the prevertebral fascia was incised and the pharyngeal constrictors carefully elevated off the tumor mass using an elevator. The tumor was found to be encapsulated within multiple lobules, as anticipated from preoperative 3D modeling. Sharp dissection with a no. 15 blade and progressive elevation of the soft tissues allowed circumferential exposure of the tumor on its superior, ventral, lateral, and inferior margins. A detailed dissection was required to avoid injuring the constrictor muscles and to avoid perforation into the upper aerodigestive tract. The mucosa, submucosa, and constrictor muscle layers were extremely splayed (Fig. 1A).

Continued Resection and Intraoperative Navigation

Once the tumor was mobilized extraosseously, the spine surgical team utilized Leksell rongeurs and an ultrasonic knife (Sonopet iQ, Stryker) to address the bony attachment at the C3 vertebral body in a piecemeal fashion. Critical to safe resection was the use of intraoperative fluoroscopic volumetric navigation (Ziehm Vision RFD 3D system) to guide osteotomy cuts (Fig. 5). Given the tumor’s proximity to the vertebral artery in the transverse foramen and lateral aspect of the vertebral body, real-time volumetric imaging (0.31-mm slices at 5123 voxels) allowed precise identification of the tumor-bone interface and guidance of resection instruments to avoid vascular injury. The 3D navigation system provided real-time feedback regarding the location of resection relative to critical structures, allowing the team to confidently approach the tumor margins.

Intraoperative Ziehm volumetric navigation images demonstrating tumor attachment to the lateral C3 body—inferior extent (A), superior extent (B), and midpoint (C)—with proximity to the vertebral artery. Image (D) showing the complete resection plane compared to the pre-resection scan.

After circumferential dissection, navigated osteotomy was performed using the Sonopet across the pedicle of the tumor attachment. All ventral tumor was resected, and minimal corpectomy of the involved C3 vertebral body was completed, with care taken to preserve the anterior longitudinal ligament and maintain the C2–3 and C3–4 disc spaces. A confirmatory volumetric navigation scan was obtained to verify complete resection and exclude residual tumor. All abnormal tissue was removed and the surgical bed was copiously irrigated (Figs. 4 and 5).

Reconstruction

Given the limited extent of corpectomy and intact lateral uncovertebral joints and disc endplates at C2–3 and C3–4, the multidisciplinary team determined that spinal instrumentation was not necessary to maintain stability. This assessment was informed by the detailed preoperative 3D and VR planning, which had allowed the team to anticipate the extent of resection needed and its impact on spinal stability. The additional risks of spinal instrumentation and hardware on a very thin pharyngeal mucosal layer were also considered, particularly given the patient’s young age and the risk of perforation/extrusion of the hardware through the pharyngeal mucosal over time.

Closure and Hemostasis

Meticulous hemostasis was achieved with bipolar cautery and bone wax. A 7-Fr Jackson-Pratt drain was placed in the surgical cavity. The platysma was closed with 2-0 vicryl suture, the skin with monocryl suture, and steri-strips were applied. The patient was transferred to the postanesthesia care unit intubated for slow awakening and remained hemodynamically stable throughout.

Postoperative Imaging and Clinical Course

Immediate postoperative cervical spine radiographs and CT scans confirmed complete resection of the tumor with no residual disease (Fig. 1E–H). The patient experienced complete resolution of his preoperative dysphagia; however, his recovery did require careful swallowing rehabilitation through speech and language pathology given the expected sacrifice of the internal branch of the superior laryngeal nerve. Given his young age, his rehabilitation was quick (< 5 days) and he was able to tolerate oral intake with removal of his nasogastric tube. Cranial nerves VII, XI, and XII were unaffected and his voice was normal (cranial nerve X) without evidence of Horner’s syndrome. He did not develop first bite syndrome. There were no complications related to the approach or neurological deficits, and he was discharged to a rehabilitation facility on postoperative day 5. At the latest follow-up at 3 months, the patient remains asymptomatic with intact swallowing function and no imaging evidence of recurrence (Fig. 1G and H).

Discussion

This case represents the first reported instance of anterior C3 vertebral body osteochondroma with retroparapharyngeal space extension, treated with patient-specific 3D printing and immersive VR-assisted surgical planning in conjunction with intraoperative volumetric navigation.

Observations

Rarity of Anterior Cervical Osteochondroma

Spinal osteochondromas comprise only a small percentage of spinal tumors, with posterior elements accounting for approximately 80% of cases.1,2,4 While sporadic anterior cervical osteochondromas affecting the C1 anterior arch or isolated vertebral bodies have been reported, none have documented significant parapharyngeal space involvement with symptomatic dysphagia in a young adult HME patient.3 The combination of anatomical location, HME context, and clinical presentation makes this an exceptionally rare case warranting documentation in the medical literature.5

Innovation in Surgical Planning: 3D Printing and VR Integration

While 3D printing has been increasingly adopted for complex surgical planning, reducing operative time and improving accuracy, the integration with immersive VR represents a novel approach with limited precedent for anterior cervical tumor resection.11 The physical 3D-printed model enabled physical manipulation of tumor anatomy, understanding of its multilobulated architecture, and direct visualization of proximities to critical neurovascular structures. This tangible model facilitated communication between spine surgery and head and neck surgery specialties by providing a concrete real-world anatomical reference.

The VR environment enabled surgical rehearsal in simulated 3D space, allowing visualization of the planned retropharyngeal approach, anticipation of technical challenges (lateral tumor extension near the vertebral artery), and virtual practice of dissection sequences. This preoperative rehearsal directly informed the multidisciplinary team’s strategy for safe and efficient tumor resection. Such combined 3D printing and VR planning represents an emerging application with significant clinical utility for rare and complex pathology.

Intraoperative Volumetric Navigation for Precision Tumor Resection

Intraoperative volumetric navigation was used to guide precise osteotomy cuts in proximity to the vertebral artery. While volumetric navigation has primarily been used for pedicle screw placement accuracy, its application to complex tumor resection near critical neurovascular structures represents an emerging valuable use.12 Real-time volumetric imaging allowed precise identification of the tumor-bone interface and confirmed complete resection objectively, helping to reduce recurrence risk.

Multidisciplinary Team Approach to Complex Anterior Cervical Pathology

The seamless collaboration between the spine surgery and head and neck surgery teams enabled safe exposure and resection of this complex lesion. The combined retropharyngeal and parapharyngeal approach allowed safe mobilization of pharyngeal constrictors and critical neurovascular dissection that would have been technically challenging through a single anterior cervical approach. Integration of preoperative 3D and VR planning into multidisciplinary team preparation enhanced collaborative outcomes by improving shared communication and visualization between specialties.

Lessons

Advanced Technologies Enhance Safety in Rare Complex Pathology

Rare and anatomically complex pathology benefits substantially from detailed preoperative planning using 3D printing and VR. These technologies enable surgical teams to develop accurate 3D mental models of complex anatomy and rehearse approaches before entering the operating room. For rare lesions with critical spatial relationships to vital structures, investment in advanced preoperative planning is justified by improved surgical efficiency, better anticipation of challenges, and enhanced safety.9,10 The physical manipulation of a 3D-printed model provides tactile and visual understanding that traditional 2D/cross-sectional imaging cannot convey, allowing surgeons to appreciate nuances of complex anatomy and plan precise dissection sequences. Immersive VR rehearsal enhances preparedness by enabling mental practice in a risk-free environment, improving procedural confidence. Institutions without access to these technologies should consider developing partnerships or protocols to ensure complex pathology is appropriately planned before intervention.

Volumetric Navigation Extends Beyond Instrumentation Guidance

While intraoperative volumetric navigation has been well established for improving the accuracy of pedicle screw placement, its application to complex tumor resection represents an emerging and underutilized capability.12,13 Intraoperative volumetric navigation provides valuable real-time feedback for tumor resection near critical structures, allowing surgeons to advance resection with confidence while maintaining safety margins around vital neurovascular structures. This technology enables objective documentation of complete tumor removal through confirmatory imaging, reducing the risk of recurrence from residual disease. In this case, real-time volumetric feedback was instrumental in safely approaching the tumor margins while maintaining a protective distance from the vertebral artery, a level of precision that would be more difficult to achieve with fluoroscopic guidance alone. As spine surgeons become increasingly familiar with volumetric navigation systems through their use in instrumentation and other procedures, their application to complex tumor resection should become standard practice.

Multidisciplinary Collaboration Enables Management of Complex Pathology

Lesions extending significantly into parapharyngeal or retropharyngeal spaces present surgical challenges that may exceed the technical scope of a single surgical specialty. The spine surgery team brings deep expertise in spinal anatomy, tumor resection principles, and management of neurovascular complications, while the head and neck surgery team provides specialized knowledge of neck dissection, parapharyngeal space anatomy, and identification and protection of lower cranial nerves. The complementary skills of both specialties combined enable safe and complete tumor resection. Preoperative 3D and VR planning facilitated this collaboration by providing all team members a shared anatomical understanding and coordinated strategy, reducing intraoperative miscommunication, and enabling seamless transitions between the head and neck surgery and spine surgery components of the procedure. Future complex anterior cervical cases should routinely incorporate multidisciplinary planning from the outset, with both surgical teams participating in preoperative conferences and technological planning sessions.

Complete Resection Remains Essential for Preventing Recurrence

This case exemplifies the well-established principle that complete resection, including the cartilaginous cap and perichondrium, is essential for preventing osteochondroma recurrence. The literature on osteochondroma surgical outcomes demonstrates recurrence rates of less than 2% with complete resection versus 11%–33% with incomplete resection.3,14 The combination of 3D printing, VR planning, and volumetric navigation–guided resection facilitated achievement of complete tumor removal in this case while maintaining safety and avoiding damage to adjacent critical structures. Even in technically demanding cases in which complete resection might seem prohibitively risky, the substantial morbidity associated with recurrent disease necessitates maximal safe resection at the initial operation. Surgeons should view complete resection as the standard of care whenever technically feasible, recognizing that the short-term operative complexity is justified by the long-term benefit of preventing recurrence.

Limitations and Relevance

This is a single case report of a rare pathology managed with advanced technologies, which inherently limits the generalizability of our findings. Without larger comparative case series, we cannot definitively establish whether the benefits observed in this case extend to all anterior cervical osteochondromas or represent isolated favorable outcomes. Follow-up is currently limited to 3 months; longer-term surveillance extending to years is essential to exclude late recurrence, which has been documented up to 15 years after initial resection in osteochondroma cases.14 The accessibility, preparation time, and technical expertise of 3D printing and VR technologies may restrict widespread adoption of this approach, particularly in resource-limited settings, underscoring the need for cost-effective solutions that lower the complexity of surgical 3D planning either in VR or with 3D printing technology. Additionally, the absence of a comparison group managed with traditional preoperative planning methods prevents quantification of the specific benefits of advanced planning technologies in terms of planning time, operative time reduction, blood loss, complication rates, and long-term functional outcomes.

Despite limitations, this case documents a novel presentation, demonstrates practical integration of emerging technologies with multidisciplinary collaboration, and highlights successful management of extremely complex cervical pathology. The achievement of complete resection without complications provides reassurance that even rare anatomically complex osteochondromas can be managed successfully with appropriate planning and technique. This case exemplifies how advanced preoperative planning technologies can facilitate effective multidisciplinary collaboration applicable to other complex surgical pathology.

The successful management of this anterior C3 osteochondroma with retropharyngeal extension highlights that rare and complex spinal pathology warrants investment in detailed preoperative planning using 3D printing and VR. Multidisciplinary surgical collaboration, supported by advanced preoperative planning, enables safe management of pathology that single surgical specialties might find prohibitively risky. As spine surgery evolves and advanced technologies become increasingly accessible, their integration into standard practice for complex and rare pathologies should be considered. Future prospective studies comparing traditional versus advanced planning approaches would help establish evidence bases for these emerging technologies.
